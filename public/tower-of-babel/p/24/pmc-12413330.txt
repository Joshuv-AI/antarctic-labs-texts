Invasive woodlands and intruding dogs shape the structure of a mesocarnivore guild
Authors: Chetan Misher, Karani Singh Bithoo, Abi T Vanak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhilst the impacts of individual invasive species are relatively well studied, the combined effects of both plant and animal invasive species on multispecies assemblages are poorly understood. We studied the impact of two invasive species—the mesquite tree, Neltuma juliflora, and free-ranging dog, Canis familiaris, on a guild of native mesocarnivores in the human-dominated grasslands of the Thar desert. We found that the mesquite had varying effects on the mesocarnivore guild, benefiting generalist species such as the golden jackal Canis aureus and jungle cat Felis chaus, while negatively affecting open habitat specialist species such as Indian desert fox Vulpes v. pusilla, Indian fox Vulpes bengalensis, and desert cat Felis lybica ornata. All mesocarnivores strongly avoided dogs along the spatial or temporal niche axis, likely to evade interference competition. The high prevalence of dogs in these landscapes could lead to the competitive exclusion of smaller native species, such as the Indian fox, resulting in a local decline in their population. Our study reveals significant impacts of both plant and animal invasive species on native mesocarnivores, altering their distribution and activity patterns, with potential consequences for long-term population persistence. We suggest control of mesquite in areas prioritised for conservation of open habitat specialists such as desert fox, Indian fox, and desert cat, and removal or restriction on the movement of dogs in sensitive wildlife habitats.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10530-025-03659-5.Keywords: Invasive species, Free-ranging dogs, Grasslands, Savanna ecosystems, Mesocarnivore, Thar desert

Background

Globally, grassland ecosystems are facing severe threats of degradation due to a variety of factors such as overgrazing, cropland expansion, afforestation, climate change, and ecological invasion, among others (Bardgett et al. 2021). Encroachment by invasive species has been recognized as one of the major drivers of grassland degradation (Ravi et al. 2021; Bardgett et al. 2021). Invasive species, native or non-native, show competitive superiority over other functionally similar species in the community, leading to the restructuring of the trophic chain and ecosystem functioning (Valéry et al. 2008). However, plant-invasive and animal-invasive species can differ in the way they shape local faunal species assemblage.

Plant invasive species can directly alter landscape-wide vegetation composition and the physical structure of habitats. These structural changes can substantially influence the diversity, distribution (Bateman et al. 2008; Pike et al. 2011), activity (Jayadevan et al. 2018; Bachen et al. 2018), and abundance (Kluever et al. 2019) of one or more species in native faunal assemblages. For example, predators like coyotes and bobcats avoid habitats covered by a dense monotypic stand of invasive Arundo donax, and show a preference for native vegetation (Hardesty-Moore et al. 2020). Encroachment by invasive plants does not evenly influence all species in a community and may show differential impacts. For example, invasive woodlands of mesquite tree Neltuma juliflora support a high abundance of generalist rodents like Indian gerbil Tatera indica, while making the invaded erstwhile open habitat unsuitable for open habitat specialists such as Indian desert gerbil Meriones hurrianae (Misher et al. 2022).

Structural changes in habitat can also trigger a change in species interaction by influencing species differentially. For example, invasive spotted knapweed Centaurea maculosa, in North America, provides a substantially higher availability of webbing substrata to Dictyna spiders compared to what is provided by native grass cover. Invasive knapweed enables the spider to make larger webs and trap more prey, resulting in significantly lower prey abundance under its cover (Pearson 2009). Another study on spiders showed that the replacement of native vegetation cover by invasive Japanese stilt-grass causes a significant decrease in the spider population due to a lack of web substrate that is often provided by native vegetation. The decline in the spider population resulted in an almost 70% increase in the invertebrate prey population under invasive cover (Simao et al. 2010). These examples show that plant invasive species can affect multiple trophic levels and show species-specific impacts based on their ecological requirements.

Animal invasive species can show direct or indirect competition through predation or interference competition, often resulting in a deleterious impact on native species. For example, the invasive red foxes in Australia are a major driver of the population decline of the endangered dusky hopping mouse Notomys fuscus through direct predation (Letnic and Dworjanyn 2011). In the absence of a larger predator, animal invasive species can establish themselves at the top of the food chain, altering species interactions through a cascading top-down effect. Invasive Burmese python Python molurus bivittatus functions as an apex predator in the Everglades ecosystem and suppresses the population of smaller predators, which indirectly supports turtle nest survival (Willson 2017). Animal invasive species can also sometimes eliminate the top predators in a system, thus releasing predation pressure from their prey community. For example, the invasive cane toad Rhinella marina in Australia has caused the elimination of apex reptilian predators—Varanid lizards and Elapid snakes due to their toxicity, which ultimately increases the fledgling success of species such as the crimson finch Neochmia phaeton (Doody et al. 2015).

A species’ habitat association and its interaction with other sympatric species are major drivers of faunal species assemblages in an ecosystem (Gompper et al. 2016). The arrival of invasive plants and animals can substantially alter these relationships, potentially leading to the reorganization of species assemblages (Riley et al. 2008; Polo-Cavia et al. 2009). Studies focusing on ecological invasion often look for the impact of either a plant or an animal invasive species on the faunal assemblage. The combined effect of both plant and animal invasive species on multispecies assemblages in a system is relatively less understood. In this study, we aim to understand the impact of two invasive species—mesquite tree Neltuma juliflora, and free-ranging dog Canis familiaris on a native mesocarnivore community in the dry grasslands of the Thar desert (hereafter Thar) in Western India.

Dry grasslands of the Thar are among the most densely populated arid ecosystems in the world (Sharma and Mehra 2009). A mosaic of grasslands and anthropogenic land covers, such as agriculture, rangelands, and plantations, has historically been managed by traditional land management institutions, such as common grazing lands, fallows, and sacred groves (Robbins 1998; Pandey et al. 2019; Kher and Dutta 2021). These are socio-ecological systems where human activities consistently interact with ecological processes in shaping the structure and functioning of these ecosystems. The spread of invasive species in these landscapes has traditionally been managed by local people based on the utilization value of a species. These socio-ecological systems are also a stronghold of native biodiversity and are categorized as potential conservation priority areas, particularly for a unique guild of medium-sized carnivores (Bhattacharjee et al. 2018; Srivathsa et al. 2020). Understanding the mechanisms enabling human-wildlife co-existence in these systems is crucial to inform land management policies to achieve socio-ecologically responsible conservation. Here, we examine how a plant and animal invasive species shape the landscape of animal interactions in the Thar.

Neltuma juliflora (hereafter mesquite), also known as the mesquite tree, is a native South American species belonging to the Fabaceae family. Under favourable conditions, it can grow as a tree reaching heights of up to 20 m. However, due to continuous lopping, it takes the shape of a medium-sized woody shrub. It was systematically planted across the dry landscapes of India during the 1960s to ostensibly control desertification and to provide fodder and fuelwood for local communities. Although the plant contributed to human well-being by providing an important source of income to several communities (Ravi and Krishnan 2024) it has also raised conservation concerns. Over the last few decades, the expansion of mesquite has converted large patches of semi-arid grasslands into thorny woodlands. Locals call it “Gandho Bawar” or “Bawaliya”, which translates to “crazy plant”, a name given owing to its fast-growing nature. Previous studies in one part of this landscape have shown that the expansion of mesquite woodland reduces suitable habitat for desert foxes, thus reducing their occupancy (Misher and Vanak 2021). Similarly, these woodlands have also been reported to affect the foraging efficiency (Jayadevan et al. 2018), activity, and diversity (Misher et al. 2022) of the nocturnal small mammal community.

Free-ranging dogs are the most prominent animal invasive species in these dry landscapes (Home et al. 2018). These dogs are dependent on human subsidies for food and shelter, and roam across these landscapes, directly interacting with native wildlife species at multiple levels (Gompper and Vanak 2008; Home et al. 2018).

Free-ranging dogs in natural habitats can affect native wildlife through competition (Vanak and Gompper 2009a), predation (Ritchie et al. 2013; Wierzbowska et al. 2016), and disease transmission (Vanak et al. 2007; Acosta-Jamett et al. 2010; Belsare and Gompper 2015). Dogs, as the larger predators of the landscape, can assert a top-down impact on the smaller native carnivores through interference competition, altering their movement patterns and habitat use (Vanak and Gompper 2010a). Direct predation and chasing or harassment by dogs have been a major cause of reported wildlife mortality in the Thar Desert (Zapata-Ríos and Branch 2016; Bhardwaj et al. 2018).

In this study, we investigated the spatio-temporal activity of the mesocarnivore guild in the desert landscape in response to bush encroachment by mesquite tree and the presence of free-ranging dogs. An increase in bush encroachment may impact how a group of mesocarnivores uses their space differently. Species such as jackal and jungle cats are known to be habitat generalists associated with a range of habitat types, like forests, scrublands, and agricultural land, across their distribution range (Katna et al. 2022). Our first hypothesis was that the expansion of mesquite woodlands would provide more suitable habitats to these habitat generalist species, resulting in their increased occupancy in the landscape. We assumed a negative influence of invasive woodland on species specialists in open brush and grassland habitats, such as the Indian fox, desert fox, and desert cat (Fig. 1).

Predicted effect of habitat types—invasive mesquite woodlands, open native vegetation, and an invasive predator (free-ranging dogs) on native mesocarnivore guild and inter-specific interaction within the guild

A guild of predators often displays a size-based hierarchy where larger species dominate smaller species through interference interactions. Such interference interaction sometimes may lead to intraguild predation. Dogs are the largest predators in the guild. Our second hypothesis was that the increased interference interaction of dogs can cause a spatiotemporal shift in native mesocarnivore activity, thus influencing their association with habitat types. Size-based hierarchy within the native guild can also determine the activity of smaller species. Therefore, we assumed that the larger size of jackals and dogs may carry a higher risk of intra-guild predation or interference competition for desert and Indian foxes, and negatively influence their occupancy. Similarly, we assumed a negative effect of dog activities on golden jackal occupancy. The larger jackal may also benefit the smallest Indian fox by limiting the activity of its competitor—the desert fox through direct interference. In the feline guild, we assumed that larger jungle cats can have a negative influence on smaller desert cat occupancy.

Even though the impact of both N. juliflora and free-ranging dogs occurs through different pathways, the effect may be comparable at the scale of the biogeographical region, resulting in either habitat or interference interaction-mediated spatiotemporal segregation.

Human activity also plays an important role in shaping the spatio-temporal activity of the mesocarnivore guild. Therefore, anthropogenic variables such as livestock grazing, proximity to human settlements, and roads can mediate the effect of mesquite presence and dog activities. Livestock grazing is the most widely occurring among all anthropogenic variables in our study landscape. Our third hypothesis was that grazing and proximity to human settlements can have a negative influence on the activity of the mesocarnivore guild. We did not include the effect of roads, as we avoided placing our camera traps near roads to prevent theft.

Methodology

Study area

The Thar is one of the smallest deserts in the world, extending over an area of 446,000 km2 in western India and southern Pakistan. In India, the desert encompasses an area of 208,110 km2 in parts of Western Rajasthan and the Kutch region of Gujarat. To cover a sampling area that is representative of the topographic variation of the Thar, our study was conducted at two different sites—(i) Banni grasslands of Kutch, Gujarat (23° 40′ 1.21″ N, 69° 36′ 22.14″ E), and (ii) Bikaner region of western Rajasthan (27° 57′ 8.54″ N, 73° 23′ 59.02″ E). Both sites are part of the desert biogeographic region of India (Champion and Seth 1968) with a mean annual rainfall ranging between 300 and 350 mm.

The Banni landscape is ~ 2500 sq. km of grassland. Its geography consists of a mix of vast salt plains, fringed by sand embankments, and a mosaic of mesquite woodland, grasslands, and brushlands of salt-tolerant vegetation. Banni is an open grazing system supporting around 21 different communities of previously nomadic pastoralists (semi-sedentary now) and livestock herders. Banni represents the part of the Thar that is severely affected by the encroachment of mesquite woodlands, as more than 50% of the landscape is occupied by a dense or sparse cover of mesquite (Vaibhav et al. 2012). The landscape is predominantly shaped by a system of day-night livestock grazing and widespread lopping and regrowth cycle of mesquite woodlands regulated by local communities.

The Bikaner landscape consists of multiple patches of connected grasslands that cover a total area of ~ 180 sq. km of grassy plains and stabilized dunes. It is predominantly an agro-pastoralist system where large areas are occupied by seasonal agriculture fields (monsoon dependent) along with large community grazing lands and protected grasslands. These areas fall under various management regimes. First, government protected area includes the Jorbeer conservation reserve and Dungargarh territorial land, and second, common property regimes include Nathania community grazing land and Deshnoke Oran. Orans are sacred groves explicitly managed as mixed shrub-grass systems protected in the name of the local deity. Due to continuous human interventions and expanding agricultural fields, Mesquite woodlands in the Bikaner landscape are small patches scattered between grasslands covering less than 13% of the area. Both areas together represent the socio-ecological and topographic diversity of the larger Thar biogeographic region.

The native vegetation structure of both landscapes is primarily made of drought-resistant grass species from the genera Dichanthium, Cenchrus, Lasiurus, and Dactyloctenium, and shrub species such as Ziziphus nummularia, and Capparis decidua. Low-lying saline patches of Banni also support the halophytic brush of Suaeda fruticosa, and grass species like Aeluropus lagopoides. Owing to their similar climate and vegetation structure, faunal communities of both landscapes are composed of similar species such as Indian gazelle Gazella bennettii, Desert cat Felis silvestris ornata, Desert fox Vulpes vulpes pusilla, Jungle cat Felis chaus, Black-napped hare Lepus nigricollis, Wild pig Sus scrofa cristatus, golden jackal Canis aureus indicus, and Desert gerbils Meriones hurrianae in addition to several other unique desert-adapted small mammals, birds, and herpetofauna.

Data collection

Field data collection

Data collection took place between December 2020 and March 2021 in the Banni landscape and between December 2021 and March 2022 in the Bikaner landscape. Data collection at both sites was done during winter and early summer by placing a network of motion-triggered infrared trail cameras. The Banni grasslands were divided into grids of 4 × 4 km in size. The size of the grid was defined based on the home range of the largest species in the study group—the golden jackal (Katna et al. 2022). The selection of a grid for sampling was decided using a checkerboard design (every alternate grid in each row). Each sampling grid was further divided into 2 × 2 km sub-grid and one camera trap was placed near the centre of each sub-grid keeping a minimum of 2 km distance between two camera trap points. Traps were deployed at each site to minimize theft and maximize detection of a species whilst avoiding keeping in mind the sensibilities of the local communities. Each camera trap site was considered as one sampling unit, with a total of 225 sites sampled across the Banni grasslands. Similarly, in Bikaner, we sampled a total of 44 such camera trap sites. Across both the sites the minimum distance between two trap points was kept at ~ 2 km to standardize sampling between both sites. A camera trap was deployed for five consecutive days at a sampling site as temporal replicates of the survey at each site. The camera was installed at a height of 70 to 75 cm from the ground using a t-shaped metal stand hammered into the ground. Each camera was set up to record a 15-s-long video whenever triggered to confirm the presence of a species. A total of 1345 camera trap days (Banni = 225 × 5 = 1125, and Bikaner = 44 × 5 = 220) were sampled to record species presence. To maximize detection, we used a drop of lure (a mix of cross-breed food lure, Kishel’s, USA, and/or locally made food lure from the viscera remains of a goat) for every camera trap. It is likely that this may influence the detection probability of the carnivore species. We used Eyeleaf camera traps REAP IR Model: RP008 for sampling.

The sampling design was uneven between the two sites due to variations in their landscape configuration and socio-economic characteristics. The Banni landscape is a continuous grassland under an open grazing regime, which allowed us to sample almost the entire area. The Bikaner landscape is predominantly comprised of privately owned land, limiting our sampling to smaller patches of open grasslands. There were notable differences in dominant land cover types. Banni was primarily covered by mesquite woodland, whereas Bikaner was dominated by agricultural land.

Habitat composition

Information regarding the proportion of each habitat type was extracted from multispectral high-resolution satellite images (Fig. 2). To differentiate grasslands more accurately within the complex landscape matrix, we used a combination of images obtained from Synthetic Aperture Radar of Sentinel-1 and multi-spectral high-resolution (10 m) optical bands of Sentinel-2 to generate land use and land cover classification of study sites in Banni and Bikaner as per (Samrat et al. 2021). Land use classes were divided into open natural vegetation, invasive woodland, agricultural land, water bodies, and built-up areas. Built-up areas were excluded from further analysis due to their minuscule presence within our sampling boundary (0.6%). We used a support vector machine classifier to generate the land use land cover layer. The classification output was compared against ground truth points using ‘errorMatrix’ function, and a confusion matrix was generated (KappaBanni = 0.92, KappaBikaner = 0.98) (Gorelick et al. 2017). The confusion matrix, also referred to as an error matrix, was generated by comparing the predicted class labels from the classification model with the corresponding ground truth points. Each cell in the matrix represents the number of instances where a specific class was predicted relative to its actual class. This matrix helps identify errors of omission (false negatives) and commission (false positives), thereby offering insights into the classification accuracy and the reliability of individual classes. Accuracy assessment of classification output was done using the confusion matrix shown in the supplementary Tables 4 and 5. The entire classification and analysis were done in the Google Earth Engine environment.

Study area map showing land-use land-cover classes, and camera trap sampling points in the Bikaner (top) and Banni (below) landscape

The classification output was overlaid with camera trap points in the QGIS platform (QGIS Development Team. 2024). QGIS is an open-source platform used to analyze and visualize geospatial datasets. For each camera trap point, we created three circular buffers of sizes 100 m, 500 m, and 1000 m, representing small-scale to large-scale habitat use by a species. We utilized the zonal histogram tool to obtain the proportion of each land cover class within each buffer for each camera trap point. These proportions were subsequently used as predictor variables in occupancy modelling.

Analysis

To understand the species occupancy data, we first conducted a descriptive analysis. We estimated naive occupancy as the number of sites occupied by each species and examined the co-occurrence patterns of different species using simple paired t-tests. Additionally, we compared the detection of each species between the Banni and Bikaner sites. Our analysis was divided into two parts: (i) occupancy modelling to determine spatial occurrence patterns, and (ii) time analysis to observe the temporal activity of species.

Occupancy modelling

Occupancy modelling is a widely used technique to understand species-habitat association and their interaction with co-occurring species at a landscape level (MacKenzie et al. 2017). These models use presence-absence data to generate parameters such as the probability of detecting a species p on a given occasion and the probability of a site being occupied by the species Ψ. A range of occupancy models is available from single-species single-season to multi-season and multispecies occupancy models. Our sampling was restricted to the winter season only, and detection histories showed a very low number of spatial co-occurrences between pairs of different species (Supplementary Table 3). The detection of an individual at a site is also often imperfect and varies with species and sites sampled. Therefore, we used single-species occupancy models (MacKenzie et al. 2017) for each species separately to determine the detectability and habitat factors driving a species' distribution.

We used a multi-staged modelling approach (Gompper et al. 2016) to fit occupancy models with detection histories of Indian fox, desert fox, golden jackal, jungle cat, and desert cat. At the first stage, we ran a null model for each species, adding no effect of any predictor on the detection probability and occupancy of a species.

At the next step, we only modelled the probability of a species being detected at any given occasion ‘p’ with detection covariates by keeping the occupancy probability ‘Ψ’ as constant. We hypothesized (Table 1) that the activity of dogs and livestock grazing can influence the probability of a species being detected by a camera on a given occasion at a site. Models were ranked based on Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) values and model weight to select the best-fit model.

The top detection model for each species was later used to fit habitat models in the third stage. For the habitat model, we used the proportion of each habitat type (open native vegetation, invasive woodland, and agricultural land) and distance from the nearest human habitation as a predictor variable. Our predictor variables are the proportion of habitat available at a sampling site; therefore, we did not use more than one habitat type together in a single model to avoid autocorrelation among predictors. We fitted 3 models with habitat type (one for each habitat type) with the addition of distance from human habitation as predictors, and 3 models with only habitat type as a predictor by removing distance from human habitation. These 6 models were repeated at three different habitat scales (100 m, 500 m, and 1 km) to compare the effect of scale, thus having a total of 18 habitat models for each species (Supplementary Table 1). We selected the top habitat model at each of the habitat scales for the fourth stage where we added the relative occurrence of potential competing species to see if the model performance increases. For the desert fox, we added the relative occurrence of golden jackals and dogs as potential competitors in the model. We added the relative occurrence of dogs as a predictor in occupancy models for the Indian fox. The analyses were done using the package ‘unmarked’ in R statistical programming (R Core Team 2020). Predicted site-specific occupancy probability for each species derived from the best model was plotted against the different predictors to visualize the effect size of the predictor relationship with species occupancy.

Temporal activity

All detection records of a species within a one-minute time frame were taken as one independent detection. We used non-parametric kernel density estimation to determine the overlap of temporal activity distribution between a pair of species. The estimate provides an overlap coefficient Δ1, when the sample size is less than 50 detections for any species in a pair, while Δ4 when the sample size is more than 50 detections for each species in pairwise analysis. The value of the coefficient ranges between 0, showing no overlap, to 1, showing a complete overlap of activity between a pair of species. The precision of the overlap coefficient was obtained by a 95% confidence interval generated through 999 bootstrap samples (Ridout and Linkie 2009). We estimated activity overlap between each pair of wild mesocarnivore species, along with the activity of dog and livestock grazing. We also compared the daily activity of each species between the Banni and Bikaner sampling sites. However, the between-sites comparison was restricted to only desert foxes as other species had a very low number of detections (< 10) at sampling sites in Bikaner.

We performed the Mardia-Watson-Wheeler (MWW) test to see if the overlap of diel activity distribution between the two species is significantly different from each other (Mori et al. 2021). This test combined the samples of both species and arranged them in increasing order of angle around the diel cycle and provided a uniform score. If there is any significant difference in sample distribution, the value of the W test statistic will be large, and the resulting p-value will show the level of significance. We also performed Rayleigh's test to determine whether the diel activity of species is uniformly distributed or clustered around specific hours throughout the diel cycle (Humphreys and Ruxton 2017). The analysis was performed using packages ‘overlap’ and ‘circular’, and ‘CircStats’ in the R statistical programming environment.

Result

A total of 7985 videos were generated through our camera trap sampling. Our trap detected 13 mammalian species (naïve occupancy—the minimum proportion of sites occupied): wild pig Sus scrofa (9.67%), domestic dog Canis familiaris (12.64%), golden jackal Canis aureus (24.54%), desert fox Vulpes Vulpes pusilla (17.47%), Indian fox Vulpes bengalensis (5.95%), jungle cat Felis chaus (20.07%), desert cat Felis lybica ornata (8.55%), Indian crested porcupine Hystrix indica (0.37%), small Indian mongoose Urva auropunctata (2.60%), long-eared hedgehog Hemiechinus auritus (1.49), Indian gazelle Gazella bennettii (6.32%), Nilgai Boselaphus tragocamelus (2.23%), and Indian hare Lepus nigricollis (1.86%).

Based on the raw number of species co-occurrence at a site (Table 3), we observed species-specific avoidance and co-occurrence behavior. Indian foxes showed avoidance of sites that are used either by desert foxes or free-ranging dogs. The highest number of co-occurrences at a site was detected between Indian fox and golden jackal (N = 7, 43% of Indian fox’s occurrences), followed by jungle cat and jackal (N = 27, 40% of jackal’s total occurrences), desert fox and dogs (N = 17, 36% of desert fox’s occurrences).

Spatial activity pattern

Detection models

The null model for all the species performed poorly. Detection probability and site occupancy varied across species. The desert fox had the highest detection probability among all native species, followed by the jungle cat, Indian fox, jackal, and desert cat (Table 2). The detection models for the Indian fox and the desert cat did not show any significant effect of incorporating dog occurrence or grazing as detection covariates. For the rest of the species, models with the relative occurrence of dogs at an occasion ranked as the top model, though dog activity did not influence the detection probability of jackal (βDog = − 0.10 ± 0.24, P = 0.69) and jungle cat (βDog = 0.59 ± 0.38, P = 0.12). Contrary to our hypothesis desert fox was detected more often on occasions when relative dog occurrence was high based on the top-ranking model (βDog = 0.23 ± 0.12, P = 0.05).

Occupancy models

The model with open native vegetation and distance from the nearest human habitation performed well in predicting site occupancy of desert foxes at all three habitat scales. The cross-scale comparison showed the best prediction of the desert fox’s occupancy at a 500 m habitat scale. Total dog occurrence at each site showed a substantial but unexpectedly positive association with site occupancy of desert fox (βdog occurrence/site = 1.18, S.E. = 0.56, p < 0.036). Temporal activity patterns

Rayleigh's test showed that the daily activity pattern of all mesocarnivore species was highly heterogeneous and directed toward specific hours throughout the circadian cycle (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). The temporal activity of species also varied between sites sampled in Banni and Bikaner. Mardia-Watson-Wheller test showed that the activity of the desert fox was nocturnal at both sites and significantly different from the activity of dogs; Banni (Δ1 = 0.353, CI = 0.096–0.553, p < 0.002) and Bikaner (Δ4 = 0.586, CI = 0.447–0.651, p < 0.001). The diel activity patterns of other species did not have any significant influence from free-ranging dog activity patterns. Desert fox activity peaked in hours when golden jackal’s activity reduces (Δ1 = 0.569, CI = 0.395–0.742, p = 0.0008) in the Banni landscape. Similarly, the Indian fox also avoided the peak activity time of the desert fox (Δ1 = 0.575, CI = 0.409–0.784, p = 0.002). Desert fox activity patterns significantly differed between Banni and Bikaner, showing a wider distribution of activity throughout the circadian cycle in the latter (Δ1 = 0.531, CI = 0.340–0.694, p = 0.0005). The highest overlap of activities among canid species was seen between the Indian fox and golden jackal (Δ1 = 0.752, CI = 0.617–0.869, p = 0.35).

The activity time of livestock was significantly avoided by all the mesocarnivore species; desert fox (Δ1-Banni = 0.330, CI = 0.133–0.444, p < 0.001 & Δ4-Bikaner = 0.408, CI = 0.298–0.448, p < 0.001), golden jackal (Δ4 = 0.669, CI = 0.592–0.741, p < 0.001), Indian fox (Δ1 = 0.504, CI = 0.335–0.590, p < 0.001), jungle cat (Δ4 = 0.555, CI = 0.442–0.602, p < 0.001), and desert cat (Δ1 = 0.652, CI = 0.512–0.780, p = 0.005).

Discussion

The spatiotemporal habitat association of the mesocarnivore guild in the Thar was highly variable and driven by multiple factors. Species such as the desert fox V. v. pusilla showed a high level of habitat specificity by selecting open native vegetation over invasive woodlands. The golden jackal C. aureus—the largest wild canid in the guild showed habitat generalist behavior at the landscape level. The smallest species among canids—Indian fox V. bengalensis was highly responsive towards interspecific competition with desert foxes rather than vegetation structure. Sympatric jungle cat F. chaus and desert cat F. l. ornata did not show spatiotemporal avoidance of each other while selecting different types of habitats. Interestingly, the occurrence of jungle cats was positively associated with the golden jackal. Invasive mesquite woodland, activity of free-ranging dogs, and livestock grazing substantially shaped the activity behavior and habitat selection of mesocarnivore species in the Thar landscape (Table 3).

Role of invasive woodlands

The conversion of open grasslands into mesquite woodlands favoured a select group of habitat generalist species—golden jackal and jungle cat. At the home-range scale, golden jackals were habitat generalists occurring across habitat types; however, at moderate (500 m) and smaller (100 m) scales, they showed a substantial avoidance of open grass and brushlands. On the other hand, the habitat specialist desert foxes showed a strong avoidance of mesquite woodlands at all habitat scales. Given the negative effect of jackal occurrence on desert foxes as our result shows, further expansion of these woodlands may lead to an increase in interference between both species. The expansion of woodlands along with agricultural habitats, also directly reduces open native vegetation for desert foxes. Unexpectedly, desert foxes were highly associated with proximity to human habitation. This could be due to dense human settlements among the patches of scattered open grasslands in Bikaner, where desert fox occurrence was higher compared to sparsely populated Banni grasslands. We observed a higher proportion of open native vegetation at all habitat scales at the sites of Indian fox occurrence, but occupancy modelling did not show any significant influence of habitat structure on its occupancy (Supplementary Fig. 2). The occupancy of Indian foxes was rather driven by interspecific interaction substantially.

Role of free-ranging dogs and interspecific interactions

Allometric-driven interspecific interaction plays a significant role in shaping the hierarchy among mammalian carnivores (Palomares and Caro 1999). Species that had a lower difference in body weight and had a higher dietary niche overlap showed higher spatiotemporal segregation in habitat use. In our study, sites occupied by the largest native species—the golden jackal observed lower activity of desert foxes, which are intermediate in size between jackals and Indian foxes. The co-occurrence of both species was reported at less than 2% of sampling sites. The interference interaction between both species is evident as desert foxes had their lowest activity during the time of high jackal activity across the diel cycle (Supplementary Fig. 1a).

Desert foxes share their habitat with sympatric Indian foxes. Both foxes are open habitat specialists and have similar dietary composition (Vanak and Gompper 2009b; Misher and Vanak 2021). Due to its relatively larger size, the desert fox potentially outcompetes smaller Indian foxes through interference competition. We found that Indian foxes avoided sites occupied by desert foxes, showing absolute spatial niche segregation (Supplementary Table 3). The temporal activity of both fox species also showed a substantial difference (Fig. 1a). On the other hand, both the temporal activity and site occurrence of the smaller Indian fox overlapped significantly with jackals. This is unusual since Indian foxes and golden jackals show strong habitat separation in other ecosystems where only these species co-occur (Carricondo-Sanchez et al. 2019; Katna et al. 2022).

The spatiotemporal interaction between two sympatric canids can be determined by the position of a species in the trophic chain. In a system of two sympatric canids, the larger species suppresses the activity of the smaller species through intraguild predation or interference competition (Loveridge and Macdonald 2002; Vieira and Port 2007). While in a three-canid system, species at the top of the trophic level may suppress the activity of subordinate species, thereby releasing competitive pressure on the smallest species (Prugh et al. 2009; Kamler et al. 2012, 2013; Fowler et al. 2021).

In our case, high jackal activity creates a landscape of fear for desert foxes thus expanding foraging opportunities for the Indian fox by supporting their activity for a relatively long time. While in a similar human-dominated semi-arid grassland system where intermediate canid—the desert fox was absent, Indian foxes showed a decreasing trend with the increasing presence of golden jackals (Carricondo-Sanchez et al. 2019).

These patterns of the size-based hierarchy have also been observed among several different assemblages of carnivores from other ecosystems. In the North American carnivore guild, grey wolves showed a positive influence on the red fox population by suppressing the activity of intermediate coyotes through intra-guild predation (Levi and Wilmers 2012; Newsome and Ripple 2015). In a South American carnivore guild, Puma has also been observed to benefit the smallest South American grey foxes by altering the spatial activity of culpeo foxes (Curras et al. 2021). Similarly, observed dog activity reduced the foraging of raccoons, resulting in a high abundance of its intertidal prey in the Gulf Islands (Suraci et al. 2016). Dogs are known to be a major intraguild competitor negatively influencing the activity of native canid species such as the Indian foxes (Vanak and Gompper 2010b).

Dogs are considered native invasive mammals as they are subsidized by humans, and can occur at densities far greater than native predators. Free-ranging dogs, therefore, add additional competitive pressure on native carnivores that may further lead to the exclusion of the smaller species in the guild, as has been seen in previous studies (Morin et al. 2022).

In our study system, both species of foxes avoided the activity of free-ranging dogs, but the mechanism of avoidance differed between sampling landscapes. In the Banni, both species of foxes refrained from using sites occupied by free-ranging dogs. Relatively lower occupancy of dogs and large patches of habitats in Banni provide enough space for native species to avoid free-ranging dogs spatially. While in Bikaner, small patches of grasslands with a high occurrence of free-ranging dogs make it almost impossible to have habitat patches devoid of dog presence. The relative occurrence of dogs in Bikaner was 48 times higher as compared to Banni. Therefore, desert foxes in Bikaner showed temporal avoidance of free-ranging dog activity by remaining strictly nocturnal. Grey foxes in southern Illinois have also been observed showing similar temporal niche partitioning to avoid direct interaction with dogs (Morin et al. 2022).

Surprisingly Indian fox was absent in the Bikaner landscape, where a previous study had reported an encounter rate of 8 individuals/100 km transect (Bithoo et al. 2017). High activity of free-ranging dogs throughout the day and night time dominated by the desert fox—another intraguild competitor, reduces the suitable foraging time and space for the smallest Indian fox. This may have potentially resulted in the competitive exclusion of the Indian fox in the Bikaner landscape.

Role of anthropogenic drivers such as livestock grazing

Livestock grazing was another important factor that was found to affect the carnivore guild across both our study sites. Grazing activities varied between sampling areas. In Banni, grazing occurs during both day and night time while in Bikaner, it is restricted to daytime. Broadly, mesocarnivores in both areas showed significant temporal avoidance of grazing.

The day-night grazing regime in the Banni and the widely dispersed activity of competitor species (jackal) across the diel cycle result in an extremely small time window available for smaller species such as the desert fox and Indian fox. While the absence of a night grazing regime and competitors’ (dogs) activity restricted to daylight hours provides longer hours to desert fox to remain active, as seen in the Bikaner landscape.

Response of the feline guild to invasive woodland and free-ranging ranging dogs

The feline community, Jungle cat F. chaus, and desert cat F. l. ornate, showed a differential response to the conversion of grasslands into woodland. Studies elsewhere have shown that jungle cats are habitat generalist species that are frequently found close to human habitation and well-wooded landscapes (Chatterjee et al. 2020; Katna et al. 2022). Therefore, we expected the jungle cats to show an increase in site occupancy with the expansion of invasive woodlands. Our findings were consistent with this expectation. At all habitat scales, jungle cats avoided native open habitats, preferring invasive woodlands. Open dryland might not provide a suitable habitat for a species typically found in irrigated agriculture fields or forested landscapes (Katna et al. 2022).

On the other hand, occupancy models for desert cats did not show any considerable effect of habitat variables or competitive interaction with any co-occurring species. This could be an artifact of small sample size, given its extremely low probability of detection (P = 0.04 ± 0.03), indicating a highly elusive behaviour of the species. However, based on preliminary analysis we found that the proportion of availability of open native vegetation at the sites of desert cat occurrence was substantially higher compared to sites where the species was absent or not detected (t = − 5.0218, p < 0.001).

The spatial occurrence pattern between the two felines shows that co-occurrence of both species was observed at less than 2% of total sampling sites only. Both species showed substantial overlap in their diel activity—the highest among the entire mesocarnivore guild in the study landscape. A similar mechanism of coexistence has also been seen between the jungle cat and the leopard cat in Cambodia (Rostro-García et al. 2021), and jungle cat and rusty spotted cat in Tadoba-Andhari landscape in Maharashtra (Chatterjee et al. 2020).

Interaction between canids and felids

We found a considerably high spatiotemporal association between the activity of the jungle cat and the golden jackal. Both species differ in their morphology and ecological requirements and therefore show no evidence of interference competition as expected. The observed high association of these species can be a result of their preference for similar kinds of habitat- mesquite woodlands. A study in the semi-arid grasslands of Maharashtra also observed that both jungle cats and jackals selected human-modified land cover similarly (Katna et al 2022).

The differential spatiotemporal activity pattern shown by the mesocarnivore guild in our study landscape considerably mimics the habitat selection pattern of their prey community. Mesquite woodland has been observed to support a significantly high abundance of generalist rodents compared to native brushlands (Misher et al. 2022). The rodent composition of open brushlands was predominantly composed of open habitat specialists, including the diurnal desert jird. Arguably, an expansion of mesquite woodland can exclude species such as foxes from patches of high rodent abundance through two pathways—one, through structural changes in the habitat, and secondly, through providing suitable habitat for intraguild competitors. Furthermore, the restricted nocturnal activity of foxes due to interference from competitors further limits their ability to take advantage of diurnal prey such as the abundant desert jird. This missed opportunity by desert foxes is reflected in the minuscule occurrence of rodents in their diet in the Banni landscape (Misher and Vanak 2021).

Conclusion

Our study shows that the differential habitat uses and changes in spatiotemporal activities allow for the co-existence of a guild of mesocarnivore species at the landscape level. Our findings have implications for the management of invasive species for biodiversity conservation and ecological restoration.

First, at the habitat scale, continuous expansion of mesquite can permanently alter the carnivore community assemblage from being composed of specialist species—Indian fox, desert fox, and desert cat to more generalist species—Jackal and jungle cat. However, at a landscape level, a mosaic of mesquite woodlands and open grasslands supports a species composition made of a high relative abundance of both generalist and specialist carnivores, as seen in Banni compared to the homogenous open grasslands of Bikaner.

Therefore, the removal of invasive mesquite from patches prioritized for the conservation of specialist species such as desert fox and desert cat can mitigate the impact of invasion. At the same time, managing mesquite through controlled harvesting and regrowth cycles in designated zones can support local livelihoods, as the species is also an important source of fuelwood and charcoal, now a key economic activity in the region. Inarguably, maintaining the landscape structure as a mosaic of mesquite woodland and native grasslands at a larger spatial scale can have conservation benefits as well as community well-being.

Second, the increased intrusion of free-ranging dogs in these habitats showed a deleterious impact on the activity of the native species. All the carnivore species showed a substantial level of either spatial or temporal avoidance of dog presence in the Thar. Therefore, removing dogs from conservation priority areas and preventing their incursion is crucial to ensure the long-term survival of native faunal assemblages (Vanak and Gompper 2010b; Home et al. 2018).

Dogs not only increase the risk of wildlife predation but also of the potential spillover of zoonotic disease on native carnivores. A high prevalence of canine distemper virus (CDV) induced mortality among Indian foxes has been reported from the semi-arid grasslands of central India due to increased contact with dogs (Belsare et al. 2014; Belsare and Gompper 2015). Therefore, a spillover of CDV cannot be neglected as a potential cause of the local decline of Indian foxes in the Bikaner region. A detailed study is required to understand the extent of zoonotic virus prevalence in the population of wild carnivores in the Thar.
