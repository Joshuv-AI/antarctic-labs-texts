Bioprinting for craniofacial reconstruction: A review of advancements, clinical use, and challenges
Authors: Niayesh Najafi, Kevin Babakhan Vartanian, Tony Eskandar, Kevin Ghookas, Edgmin Rostomian, Devendra K Agrawal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCraniofacial reconstructive surgery faces significant challenges due to complex anatomy, intricate vascularization requirements, and the limitations of traditional grafts. Recent advancements in three-dimensional (3D) bioprinting technology present a promising paradigm shift, offering precise, patient-specific solutions for repairing critical zygomatic, orbital, nasal, and mandibular defects. This review systematically examines the latest breakthroughs in bioprinting methods, including extrusion-based, laser-assisted, stereolithography, magnetic bioprinting, spheroid rapid printing, and artificial intelligence integration. Through detailed analysis of clinical applications, we highlight successful case studies demonstrating enhanced surgical outcomes and patient satisfaction, particularly in complex zygomatic reconstructions and delicate orbital floor repairs. Furthermore, we explore the novel bioink formulations, emphasizing hydrogel composites and biofunctional materials optimized for craniofacial tissue regeneration, while addressing persistent technical and biological challenges such as vascularization, immune compatibility, and regulatory hurdles. Finally, we outline strategic pathways for advancing clinical translation, advocating for interdisciplinary collaboration, standardization, and innovative integration of artificial intelligence-driven optimization to accelerate the adoption of bio-printed constructs into clinical practice. Overall, 3D bioprinting represents a transformative frontier in craniofacial reconstruction, poised to significantly improve patient outcomes and reshape surgical approaches to complex head and neck defects.Keywords: 3D bioprinting, Artificial intelligence, Bioinks, Clinical application, Craniofacial reconstruction, Mandibular defects, Nasal cartilage, Orbital fracture, Vascularization, Zygomatic bone

Introduction

Three-dimensional (3D) printing technologies, such as additive manufacturing (AM), have changed how we create customized physical objects. This “bottom-up” process layers materials to produce tailored devices and products that meet specific needs (Mirsky et al., 2024). Building on this concept, 3D bioprinting takes things a step further by combining living cells, biomaterials, and biomolecules into a printable substance known as bioink, allowing the creation of tissue structures that closely mimic real tissues (Mirsky et al., 2024).

In reconstructive surgery, bioprinting has already shown its potential in the development of skin grafts and complex tissue structures for both aesthetic and functional repairs (Mirsky et al., 2024). However, while the technology is remarkable, it is far from perfect. Current problems and hurdles that remain include the long-term survival of bioprinted tissues, creation of vascular networks to supply blood, and integration of printed constructs into a patient’s body (Mirsky et al., 2024). Addressing these issues is crucial to unlocking the full potential of bioprinting in medicine (Santoni et al., 2022).

One of the biggest opportunities for 3D bioprinting lies in craniofacial reconstruction, one of the most complicated fields of surgery. Replicating the intricate, multi-layered arrangements of craniofacial tissues presents a notable challenge (Mirsky et al., 2024). This is where introducing bioprinting could make a marked difference. For example, cartilage bioprinting using adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) is being explored for rhinoplasty and other nasal repairs (Zhang et al., 2023). Similarly, 3D bioprinting is being used to tackle large mandibular defects, which typically require tissue grafts that can result in additional surgeries and donor site complications (Park et al., 2022).

Reconstructing bones such as the zygomatic bone—a key part of facial symmetry—or repairing orbital floor fractures highlight the need for solutions that go beyond traditional methods. These cases require high precision to restore facial aesthetics and reduce complications, and bioprinting could play a significant role in solving these challenges (Moiduddin et al., 2023; Mukai et al., 2023).

Advances in 3D bioprinting are improving outcomes in complex reconstructive surgeries such as rhinoplasty, mandibular, and zygomatic repairs. Patient-specific 3D-printed implants enhance surgical accuracy and reduce complications. In a case series involving nine patients with internal orbital fractures, surgeons used office-based 3D printing to pre-shape implants based on patient-specific CT data. The process took only 1.5 h for image processing and 3 h for printing, and post-op results showed a mean orbital volume difference of just −0.2 ± 0.4 mL compared to the unaffected side, demonstrating excellent reconstruction accuracy (Yoon et al., 2024).

Advancements in 3D bioprinting have also shown potential in enhancing wound healing and recovery. For instance, a recent study explored the use of laser-assisted bioprinting (LAB) to pattern fibroblasts on collagen gels, aiming to replicate the dynamic remodeling observed during skin repair. The researchers found that LAB-enabled precise cell patterning led to organized matrix formations, which are crucial for effective wound healing. This approach provides a versatile tool to study dermal organization and remodeling processes, potentially informing new treatment strategies for skin repair (Douillet et al., 2022).

A meta-analysis of 3D printing applications in orthopedic surgeries indicated significant improvements in operating time, blood loss, and functional outcomes while showing no substantial differences in complication rates compared to traditional methods (Wong et al., 2021). While concerns have been raised about potential complications associated with 3D printing in reconstructive surgeries, recent publications suggest that, with proper implementation, 3D bioprinting can enhance surgical precision and allow for patient-specific customization resulting in improved clinical outcomes (Kalidindi, 2024). These advancements highlight the transformative potential of 3D bioprinting in reconstructive surgery, providing innovative solutions for complex anatomical challenges and enhancing patient care.

This critical review focuses on the use of 3D bioprinting in reconstructive surgery, with more attention to craniofacial bones and cartilage, highlighting the critical role of bioprinting in refining craniofacial, head and neck reconstruction, and its potential to improve patient outcomes, particularly in zygomatic, mandibular, nasal, and orbital reconstruction surgeries. The goal is to explore how this technology is being applied to address surgical challenges in skull-based and craniofacial procedures, including mandibular reconstruction, zygomatic bone repair, and rhinology, in addition to surgeries involving orbital bones. Cartilage reconstruction, especially in procedures like rhinoplasty and reconstructive surgery. We present our findings on an in-depth examination of the evolution of bioprinting technology, highlighting significant innovations in hardware and bioink formulations, applications in craniofacial reconstruction, discussing hurdles that need to be addressed, including vascularization, and clinical integration, and future directions to integrate bioprinting into routine clinical practice.

Methodology

Search strategy

The literature search concentrated on peer-reviewed published articles to ensure that the information is both credible and reflects the latest advancements. The databases PubMed, EMBASE, and Web of Science were indexed. The search terms included: “3D bioprinting in head and neck reconstruction”; “Vascularized bio-printed constructs”; “Bioinks for craniofacial reconstruction”; “3D printing in maxillofacial surgery”; “Patient-specific implants for zygomatic and mandibular defects”. Advanced search filters in these databases were utilized to refine results (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram showing the inclusion and exclusion criteria in the literature search and the selection of the articles for critical review and evaluation in this article.

The inclusion criteria included: (i) studies published in peer-reviewed journals mostly between 2019 and 2024, (ii) papers focusing on human or clinically relevant models, (iii) systematic reviews, (iv) randomized controlled trials (RCTs), (v) meta-analyses, and (vi) innovative case studies highlighting the application of 3D bioprinting in reconstructive surgery. The exclusion criteria included: (i) publications not available in full text or open access, (ii) non-English publications, and (iii) studies that focus exclusively on theoretical models or animal applications without clear human relevance.

The selection process involved multiple stages to ensure the quality and relevance of the included studies. In the initial screening, abstracts and titles were reviewed to identify articles that directly addressed 3D bioprinting applications in craniofacial surgery. Irrelevant studies, such as those focusing on unrelated medical fields or general 3D printing without bioprinting, were excluded. Selected papers underwent a detailed full-text review to assess their methodology, sample size, and outcomes. Studies and case studies were prioritized if they presented new findings, innovative techniques, or clinically validated results. Articles were sourced from trustworthy, peer-reviewed journals. Systematic reviews, large cohort studies, and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were prioritized to build a solid foundation of evidence. Where relevant, case reports were also included, especially if they highlighted innovative or clinically significant uses for 3D bioprinting (Fig. 1).

Each paper was evaluated based on how well it aligned with the focus of this review article, advancing 3D bioprinting techniques in reconstructive surgery concerning craniofacial bones and cartilage. Studies that offered practical solutions for surgeries, like nasal procedures, mandibular reconstructions, and repairs of the zygomatic bone, were prioritized.

This strategy ensured that the review captures the latest and most credible advancements in 3D bioprinting, based on high-quality data from the past five years. For instance, studies such as by Mirsky et al. (2024) provided foundational insights into bioinks and tissue integration, while others highlighted challenges like vascularization and surgical precision (Santoni et al., 2022; Mirsky et al., 2024).

Breakthroughs in 3D bioprinting technology

There are several recent breakthroughs in 3D bioprinting that offer precise control to print tissue structures fully functional and complex tissue fabrication for repair and replacement. These are summarized in Fig. 2 and discussed below.

The major types of 3D bioprinting in surgery. (A) Laser-assisted bioprinting deposits cells in high resolution and allows for precise control over cell placement. (B) Stereolithography utilizes UV light to generate biocompatible scaffolds from bioink vats that cells can expand into during culture. (C) Extrusion bioprinting deposits cells in the form of bioink, forming clusters and working much like a traditional 3D printer. (D) Magnetic 3D bioprinting utilizes a metal nanoparticles coating on cells, allowing a magnet to form cell spheroids. (E) Rapid bioprinting of spheroids utilizes suction to organize spheroids in a needle scaffold to vertically generate a structure. (F) Bioprinting with artificial intelligence allows tissue engineers to continuously modify the printing process, bioink formulations, and scaffold designs to improve the manufacturing process.

Extrusion-based bioprinting

Extrusion-based bioprinting, which was introduced in the early 2000s, is one of the most prevalent techniques in 3D bioprinting. This method works by creating bioink-based layers through a nozzle to build 3D structures. Its versatility and ability to use a wide range of bioinks makes it an effective technique for craniofacial reconstructive surgery applications (Fig. 2). Existing biomaterials used in extrusion based bioprinting include hyaluronic acid collagen, alginate, and gelatin. They provide a scaffold structure for cell growth, along with synthetic polymers like polycaprolactone (PCL), which add strength and stability (Petkov et al., 2014). Existing applications include mandibular reconstruction, where scaffolds fabricated with this technique support bone regeneration in large defects (Park et al., 2022), and nasal cartilage engineering, where bioprinted constructs provide promising alternatives to common cartilage grafts, particularly in revision rhinoplasty (Zhang et al., 2023). Furthermore, this method is increasingly being used to create custom zygomatic implants for fracture stabilization and cosmetic restoration. Remaining challenges include ensuring mechanical strength and vascularization of larger constructs, which are important for successful integration and function (Santoni et al., 2022).

Laser-assisted bioprinting (LAB)

Laser assisted bioprinting (LAB) was also developed in the 2000s, and is a precise method adapted from laser-induced forward transfer technology (Fig. 2). LAB uses laser pulses to propel bioink droplets made of hyaluronic acid, chondrocytes, osteoblasts, growth factors and nanoparticles onto a substrate, enabling the creation of intricate tissue patterns (Wang et al., 2011). LAB is particularly effective for reconstructing the zygomatic bone, due to its accurate deposition of osteogenic cells and biomaterials which is critical for restoring facial symmetry (Santoni et al., 2022). Moreover, this technique has been used in orbital floor reconstruction, where the thin and complex implants produced through LAB reduce postoperative complications (Mukai et al., 2023). LAB is also gaining traction in cartilage tissue engineering for nasal reconstruction, offering a precise alternative for secondary or revision rhinoplasty procedures (Zhang et al., 2023). Despite its precision, LAB carries challenges including possible cell damage due to laser exposure and limitations in the types of bioinks that can be used, which may affect the viability and functionality of the bioprinted tissues (Moiduddin et al., 2023).

Stereolithography (SLA)

Stereolithography (SLA), one of the earliest 3D printing technologies developed, was introduced in the 1980s and later adapted for bioprinting to meet the demands of tissue engineering (Li et al., 2023). SLA is an additive manufacturing process that uses light to harden photo-sensitive resins in a controlled manner, enabling the creation of highly accurate printed objects (Fig. 2). The most frequently used materials are photopolymerizable resins such as polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA) and gelatin-methacrylate (GelMA), often combined with calcium phosphate-based fillers for bone tissue engineering (Mirsky et al., 2024). In craniofacial reconstruction, SLA has been employed to fabricate scaffolds for reconstructing zygomatic and mandibular bones with high precision (Moiduddin et al., 2023). Additionally, it has been applied in the production of facial prostheses of various sizes and shapes, as well as nasal implants, which are being considered as potential replacements for autografts in septal reconstruction (Santoni et al., 2022). The ability of SLA to generate high-resolution scaffolds tailored to the patient’s anatomy makes it a valuable tool in treating craniofacial deformities. However, concerns about the biocompatibility and potential toxicity of residual photo initiators in SLA-fabricated constructs necessitate further research to ensure patient safety (Zhang et al., 2023).

Magnetic 3D bioprinting

Magnetic 3D bioprinting, introduced in the 2010s, offers a novel, scaffold-free approach to tissue engineering. This technique uses magnetic nanoparticles to guide and assemble cells into complex 3D structures, avoiding the need for traditional scaffolding (Xiao et al., 2010). The cells are labeled with biocompatible iron oxide nanoparticles and self-organize into spheroids under magnetic fields (Fig. 2). This method has been successfully applied in soft tissue reconstruction, particularly for vascularized tissues in facial surgeries such as skin, lip, and nasal soft tissue reconstruction (Zhang et al., 2023). Furthermore, it is showing promising results in cartilage tissue engineering for nasal reconstruction, providing a scaffold-free alternative for revision rhinoplasty (Mirsky et al., 2024). Moreover, magnetic 3D bioprinting has been explored for hybrid constructs that combine soft and hard tissues, such as those required for zygomatic or orbital repairs (Mukai et al., 2023). Despite its advantages, there is limited long-term data on the durability and in-vivo integration of magnetic bio-printed tissues, necessitating further studies to validate its clinical reliability (Santoni et al., 2022).

Rapid bioprinting of spheroids

The rapid bioprinting of spheroids is a cutting-edge technology introduced in the late 2010s, designed to accelerate the assembly of tissue construction. Spheroids are three-dimensional aggregates of living cells that self-assemble into spherical structures mimicking organization and function of native tissues. Printing preformed cell spheroids at high speeds significantly reduces the time required to create functional tissues (Shani-Adir et al., 2009). The spheroids, often composed of chondrocytes or osteoblasts, are encapsulated in supportive hydrogels like alginate or fibrin to maintain structural integrity during the printing process. This technique is currently being used in bone reconstruction particularly for mandibular and maxillary defects, where the rapid creation of dense cellular constructs is crucial for regeneration (Park et al., 2022). It has also been applied in cartilage repair, particularly for nasal defects, offering a faster and more efficient alternative to traditional grafts (Zhang et al., 2023). Additionally, the rapid bioprinting of spheroids is being explored for reconstructing complex orbital and zygomatic defects, where precision and speed are essential (Mukai et al., 2023). However, the technique faces several challenges, such as ensuring uniform integration of spheroids and preventing fusion defects potentially caused by variability in the bioink or cell distribution. This can lead to gaps or weak points, affecting the structural and functional integrity of the final construct (Mirsky et al., 2024).

Bioprinting with machine learning and artificial intelligence

Bioprinting with machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) integrates both advancements to enhance the precision and efficiency of bioprinting processes. AI algorithms are used to automate medical image reconstruction, enabling accurate segmentation and modeling of patient-specific anatomical structures, which improves the fidelity of bioprinted tissues (Fig. 2). ML also plays a crucial role in bioink selection by predicting optimal formulations for printability and biocompatibility, leading to reduced trial-and-error in material development. Furthermore, AI facilitates real-time monitoring and adjustment of printing parameters, ensuring that the final constructs meet functional and structural standards (Freeman et al., 2022). While promising, concerns such as data standardization, algorithm transparency, and integration into existing clinical workflows remain unresolved. Yet, there are no published examples of clinical application of this form of bioprinting.

Bioink formulations

Hydrogel and composite bioinks

The hydrogel and composite formulation of bioinks span a diverse range of formulations tailored to craniofacial applications. Natural polymer inks like collagen, gelatin, and alginate provide a cytocompatible matrix for cell encapsulation, with in vitro viability typically well above 90 % (Sears et al., 2020). Such hydrogels inherently mimic extracellular matrix and thus show excellent biocompatibility – collagen-based bioinks, for example, combine high cell adhesivity with minimal immunogenicity (Debnath et al., 2025). To meet ISO 10993 guidelines, these base materials have been validated in standard cytotoxicity assays (ISO 10993-5) with no toxic leachates (Kosowska et al., 2024), and they produce negligible irritation or sensitization responses due to their natural origin. Importantly, printability and mechanical integrity can be tuned by modifying the formulation (e.g. blending polymers or adding crosslinkers) without compromising biocompatibility (Sears et al., 2020; Debnath et al., 2025). This versatility allows hydrogel bioinks to be adapted as required for either load-bearing bone or delicate soft tissue, while still adhering to the ISO-based safety framework (Al-Zyoud et al., 2023; Badhe et al., 2023).

Bone-regenerative bioinks

These bioinks (for mandibular or zygomatic defects) often incorporate ceramic or mineral components to enhance stiffness and osteo-conductivity. A collagen–β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) composite bioink, for instance, yielded a stable scaffold with significantly elevated osteogenic gene expression in vitro (Badhe et al., 2023). Similarly, alginate-based hydrogels reinforced with hydroxyapatite (HA) or bioactive glass have demonstrated robust bone cell responses: an alginate–PVA–HA formulation supported high viability of encapsulated pre-osteoblasts and maintained its structure for weeks (Bendtsen et al., 2017). These mineralized inks promote native bone formation in vivo; the incorporation of bone-like fillers (e.g. calcium phosphates) not only improves mechanical properties but also stimulates vascularized mineral deposition at defect sites (Debnath et al., 2025). Notably, their degradation products are benign (releasing bioresorbable calcium/phosphate ions), so no acute systemic toxicity has been observed in animal models (Ojansivu et al., 2019). All reported cytocompatibility tests for such composites show cell survival well above the ISO 10993 threshold (often ~85–95 % viability) (Sears et al., 2020; Kosowska et al., 2024), and short-term implantation studies indicate a normal foreign-body response (fibrous encapsulation and inflammation comparable to clinically used bone grafts) with no chronic toxicity (Ojansivu et al., 2019; Debnath et al., 2025). These findings underscore that composite bioinks can meet stringent biocompatibility requirements while providing the rigidity and osteoconductive cues crucial for mandibular and zygomatic bone repair.

Soft tissue-focused bioinks

These are engineered for flexibility and bioactivity in orbital and nasal reconstructions. Pure hydrogels based on collagen or gelatin methacrylate (often blended with glycosaminoglycans like hyaluronic acid) excel in supporting cell viability and matrix production in cartilaginous tissues (Badhe et al., 2023). For example, a gelatin–alginate–hyaluronic acid bioink showed enhanced fibroblast adhesion and proliferation (advancing tissue maturation) despite its lower stiffness (Badhe et al., 2023). In an in vivo craniofacial model, a hyaluronan–collagen scaffold permitted cell infiltration and neovascularization without eliciting significant inflammation or irritation (Badhe et al., 2023), reflecting good compliance with ISO 10993-10 (sensitization and intracutaneous reactivity) standards. Long-term in vitro culture further attests to their safety: a composite gelatin–alginate hydrogel fortified with aldehyde-functionalized hyaluronic acid and N-carboxymethyl chitosan maintained ~91 % cell viability at 4 weeks (Chen et al., 2021), indicating no deleterious degradation byproducts or delayed cytotoxicity. These hydrogels are essentially biomimetic, so they provoke minimal immune reaction; studies have found collagen-based constructs to be non-immunogenic and non-pyrogenic in implantation, given collagen’s ubiquity in the human extracellular matrix (Badhe et al., 2023; Debnath et al., 2025). Collectively, such soft bioinks satisfy the biocompatibility criteria for orbital soft tissue fillers or nasal cartilage grafts by combining pro-regenerative cell support with proven absence of acute or subacute toxicity.

Biofunctionalized bioinks

This formulation of bioinks adds therapeutic cues (growth factors, peptides, or plasma components) to further drive tissue regeneration in craniofacial sites, all while maintaining biocompatibility. Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogels enriched with bone morphogenetic protein-2 have shown markedly increased mineralization by dental stem cells (mimicking bone induction) with no loss of cell viability compared to plain GelMA (Badhe et al., 2023). Likewise, incorporating autologous platelet-rich plasma (PRP) into a silk fibroin–gelatin–β-TCP scaffold provides a rich cocktail of cytokines that significantly accelerates bone defect healing in vivo (Grzelak et al., 2024). These bioinks still adhere to ISO 10993 guidelines: the added bioactive agents are derived from the body, so they tend not to trigger sensitization or systemic toxicity (e.g. PRP-laden constructs were shown to reduce inflammatory cytokine release and improved wound healing in clinical studies) (Grzelak et al., 2024). Moreover, advanced nanocomposites such as the “NICE” bioink (GelMA reinforced with kappa-carrageenan and nano silicate clay) highlight that mechanical enhancement and osteo-inductive capacity can be achieved without introducing genotoxic or carcinogenic effects (Chimene et al., 2018; Sears et al., 2020; Debnath et al., 2025). In fact, the NICE scaffolds supported >95 % cell viability post-printing and exhibited only a normal physiological immune response upon implantation (Sears et al., 2020). Thus, whether by tethering growth factors or integrating bioactive nanoparticles, these functionalized bioinks meet biocompatibility benchmarks (acute and sub-chronic) while offering specialized benefits for craniofacial regeneration – for example, improved osteointegration in jawbone or enhanced cartilage formation in the nose – without compromising safety.

Across all 13 bioinks, the ISO 10993 biocompatibility framework serves as a critical checkpoint ensuring clinical safety. Recent studies consistently report no evidence of cytotoxicity (ISO 10993-5) for these materials, and implantations in animal models have revealed minimal adverse responses, fulfilling the requirements for irritation, sensitization, and acute systemic toxicity (Grzelak et al., 2024). Any transient inflammation observed is within expected limits for wound healing and is often mitigated by the bioactive components (e.g. platelet-rich plasma or anti-inflammatory cytokines) present in the bioinks (Grzelak et al., 2024). Genotoxicity and carcinogenicity risks are considered low – the polymers (gelatin, alginate, etc.) biodegrade into natural metabolites, and inorganic additives like HA or silica are known to be non-mutagenic in vivo (Debnath et al., 2025). While long-term chronic toxicity studies are still emerging, short- and mid-term evaluations (up to several months) show stable integration with host tissue and no tumorigenic changes (Grzelak et al., 2024; Debnath et al., 2025). Ultimately, matching each bioink to its surgical niche is key: softer hydrogels have the compliance and cell-friendly interface suited for orbital soft tissues and nasal cartilage, whereas stiffer, mineral-loaded inks provide the strength and osteo-conductivity needed for zygomatic and mandibular bone reconstructions (Badhe et al., 2023). By leveraging material-specific advantages and adhering to the ISO-based biocompatibility criteria, researchers have demonstrated that the hydrogel, composite, and biofunctional bioinks can be applied in craniofacial surgery (orbit, midface, nose, jaw) with promising efficacy and an acceptable safety profile (Ojansivu et al., 2019; Debnath et al., 2025). The performance of each bioink, thus far, regarding high cell viability, appropriate host response, and lack of systemic toxicity underscores its suitability for future clinical translation in craniofacial tissue engineering.

Advancements in vascularization strategies

Large craniofacial grafts (e.g. zygomatic, orbital, nasal, and mandibular implants) face a fundamental challenge: insufficient vascularization of the engineered tissue. The clinical translation of most 3D-printed bone scaffolds is limited by inadequate angiogenesis, which hinders graft integration and bone regeneration (X. Sun et al., 2023). To address this, recent studies (2020–2024) have explored bioactive approaches to pre-vascularize bioprinted grafts or stimulate rapid vessel ingrowth. A common strategy is the incorporation of pro-angiogenic growth factors (like VEGF) or osteogenic factors (BMP-2) into the bioink. However, these factors alone are often unstable and short-lived, and high doses can cause adverse effects (e.g. ectopic bone or tumorigenesis) (Cao et al., 2023). Emerging biofabrication techniques therefore emphasize spatiotemporal control of growth factor delivery. For example, one approach bioprinted distinct regions of a bone graft with micro-encapsulated BMP-2 and VEGF, positioning each factor in specific zones of the construct (Gharacheh and Guvendiren, 2023). This spatial delivery guided localized osteogenesis vs. angiogenesis from a single stem cell source (adipose-derived stem cells), and after perfusion culture the construct showed region-specific expression of bone (osteopontin) and endothelial cell (CD31) markers (Gharacheh and Guvendiren, 2023), confirming that controlled dual-factor release can produce a pre-vascularized bone tissue. Additionally, because natural bone healing requires angiogenic signaling over weeks (VEGF peaks ~1-week post-injury, BMP-2 ~3 weeks) (Cao et al., 2023), bioprinted scaffolds are being engineered for sustained release rather than a burst.

As an alternative to single recombinant factors, autologous platelet-rich plasma (PRP) – a concentrate of platelets that releases a cocktail of growth factors upon activation – has gained traction as a more physiological angiogenic stimulant (Cao et al., 2023). Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) provides multiple growth factors in native proportions for synergistic healing, but unmodified PRP releases them all at once, a short burst that is suboptimal for bone repair (Cao et al., 2023). To overcome this, researchers have developed composite bioinks that prolong the release of factors from PRP. For instance, a 3D-bioprinted bone scaffold combining PRP with a gelatin/alginate hydrogel and nano-clay showed sustained release of PRP-derived cytokines for up to 2 weeks (Cao et al., 2023). In vitro, this PRP-laden bioink significantly enhanced the proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) and even accelerated the formation of capillary-like networks by endothelial cells (Cao et al., 2023). Similarly, another group incorporated PRP into gelatin microspheres within a PCL/β-TCP scaffold; this system achieved controlled growth factor release for ~3 weeks and led to notably higher MSC survival and angiogenic activity in vitro compared to scaffolds without PRP (Liu et al., 2022). When implanted in vivo, these PRP-enriched scaffolds demonstrated markedly improved vascular ingrowth and bone regeneration at defect sites. The PRP–nanoclay scaffolds, for example, promoted dense blood vessel invasion and healing of rat femoral defects, while the PRP–microsphere constructs showed superior repair of critical bone injuries (e.g. large calvarial defects) relative to controls (Liu et al., 2022; Cao et al., 2023). These results underscore that sustained delivery of endogenous growth factors (via PRP or similar) can significantly improve graft vascularization and survival in craniofacial bone models.

Beyond acellular additives, cell-based strategies are advancing vascularization in bioprinting. Co-culturing supportive stromal cells with endothelial cells can establish preformed microvessels within the construct. For example, incorporating human adipose-derived stem cells (hADSCs) along with endothelial cell spheroids in a bioprinted bone construct yielded dramatically greater vascularization than printing hADSCs alone (Kim et al., 2022). The spheroid-laden bioprinted graft exhibited “outstanding” angiogenic and osteogenic activity in vitro, owing to crosstalk between the stem and endothelial cells (Kim et al., 2022). This translated in vivo: in a craniofacial (mastoid) defect model, the multi-cell construct formed significantly more new bone and blood vessels compared to a conventional single-cell construct (Kim et al., 2022). The endothelial spheroids effectively acted as vascular nuclei, releasing signals that boosted host vessel invasion and bone remodeling (Kim et al., 2022). Likewise, Shen et al. (2022) bioprinted a scaffold with built-in perfusable channels, then directly printed endothelial cells inside those channels (“in situ” bioprinting of vessels). The endothelial cells uniformly lined the inner surfaces of the tubular channels (Shen et al., 2022), forming a functional microvascular network in vitro through cell migration and tube formation (Shen et al., 2022). This in situ pre-vascularized bone construct induced coupled angiogenesis–osteogenesis (upregulating vascular and bone gene expression), and in a rat large-defect model it significantly increased new bone formation versus non-vascularized scaffolds (Shen et al., 2022).

Another cell-free but biologically active strategy uses extracellular vesicles: for instance, exosomes from MSCs or endothelial progenitors can be loaded into bioprinted biomaterials to stimulate host vessel growth. In a 2023 study, human MSC-derived exosomes incorporated into a 3D printed collagen/hydroxyapatite scaffold markedly promoted endothelial cell migration and capillary tube formation in vitro (X. Sun et al., 2023). When implanted into rat jaw defects, the exosome-functionalized scaffold achieved robust vascularization and bone regeneration, outperforming scaffolds alone (X. Sun et al., 2023). Kong et al. (2024) similarly reported that a bioprinted bioactive ceramic scaffold coated with endothelial progenitor cell exosomes created a pro-angiogenic microenvironment that accelerated vascularized bone regeneration, by concurrently releasing angiogenic exosomes and mineral ions into the defect site. Notably, many of these tissue-engineered grafts are cultured in perfusion bioreactors prior to implantation to ensure cell viability in their core. Dynamic perfusion provides convective nutrient transport, preventing the central necrosis that plagues thick constructs under static culture. For example, one group showed that after 14 days of perfusion culture, a cell-laden 3D bone scaffold had uniform cell survival throughout, as evidenced by an MTT viability assay (live cells present even at construct centers) (Gensler et al., 2024). Such preconditioning can also foster more mature vessel formation inside the graft before it ever sees the surgical site, essentially “pre-vascularizing” the tissue in vitro. Upon implantation, these preformed microvessels can rapidly anastomose with the host circulation, speeding up perfusion of the graft. Indeed, the strategies above – from co-culture and spheroids to exosome supplementation and perfusion bioreactors – are often combined, aiming to present transplanted tissues that immediately recruit blood flow and integrate with minimal delay.

Translational studies in craniofacial models underscore the importance of vascularization for graft success. In orbital floor reconstruction, for instance, researchers have tested bioactive composite scaffolds (e.g. polyurethane hydroxyapatite) designed to encourage angiogenesis. The orbital floor is a highly vascularized bone, and a blowout fracture can severely impair healing by disrupting local blood supply (AL-Hamoudi et al., 2022). Encouragingly, a 2022 orbital floor scaffold study showed excellent biocompatibility and pro-angiogenic performance: osteoblast-like cells remained viable on the scaffold (MG63 viability assays confirmed cytocompatibility) and a chick CAM assay demonstrated robust new vessel formation into the material (AL-Hamoudi et al., 2022). The addition of nano-HA in that scaffold significantly enhanced vascularization in the CAM test, correlating with greater VEGF expression, indicating that material cues can modulate angiogenesis in the graft (AL-Hamoudi et al., 2022). These vascularized constructs are poised to improve orbital floor defect repair by restoring the blood supply needed for bone regeneration in vivo.

Likewise for nasal reconstruction, while the engineered nasal cartilage itself is an avascular tissue, ensuring rapid perfusion in the surrounding tissues is critical for graft integration. Recent work has validated the in vivo stability of bioprinted human nasal cartilage implants: 3D-bioprinted nasal cartilage constructs (made of chondrocytes in collagen hydrogel) remained structurally intact and even out-performed traditional graft controls after implantation in mice (Lan et al., 2022). The printed cartilage matured with a robust extracellular matrix in vitro, and upon implantation it showed no evidence of deleterious resorption or calcification (Lan et al., 2022). This success, while not explicitly an angiogenic outcome, was contingent on the graft being thin enough and placed such that diffusion from the well-vascularized recipient bed could sustain the chondrocytes. It highlights a practical design principle: avascular tissues like cartilage can be bioprinted for craniofacial use but must be combined with a richly vascularized environment (e.g. periosteum, fascia, or well-vascularized scaffold underneath) to ensure long-term viability.

Across these studies, cell viability and vascularization metrics are key endpoints guiding progress. Bioprinted grafts are routinely assessed with assays like MTT, Live/Dead staining, and metabolic activity (CCK-8) to confirm that cells remain alive and functional throughout the construct. For example, the use of PRP or growth factor-releasing hydrogels often correlates with higher viable cell numbers in the interior of the scaffold (as measured by metabolic assays) relative to growth factor–free controls (Liu et al., 2022; Cao et al., 2023). The formation of vessel-like networks is confirmed in vitro by endothelial tube formation assays, and in vivo by histology and CD31 immunostaining or angiography. In vivo efficacy is ultimately demonstrated in animal models by improved graft perfusion, more rapid mineralization, and mechanical integrity of the healed bone. While significant advances have been made, there are still limitations and challenges being actively addressed. Achieving stable, long-term release of pro-angiogenic signals remains difficult – many constructs risk an initial burst release (e.g. when PRP or growth factor gels are not adequately crosslinked), which can lead to spatially unstable vascularization or simply waste the factors early (Cao et al., 2023). Ensuring sufficient perfusion in larger or thicker tissues is another hurdle; even with microscale channel designs, very large volume constructs may require vascular graft anastomosis or perfusion pumps to maintain oxygenation. There is also careful consideration of the immune response to any added biologics or biomaterials. While autologous components like PRP and patient-derived cells minimize immunogenicity, the use of growth factor proteins, synthetic nanoparticles (e.g. nanoclays), or allogeneic exosomes must be vetted for biocompatibility. Unwanted inflammation can impede vessel formation or cause fibrosis; indeed, supraphysiologic BMP-2 doses have been linked to inflammatory complications clinically (Cao et al., 2023). To mitigate this, many new scaffolds incorporate immunomodulatory elements (for example, the PRP–nanoclay system incidentally promoted healing-friendly M2 macrophage polarization (Cao et al., 2023).

In summary, vascularization strategies have rapidly evolved to enhance the survival and integration of 3D-bioprinted craniofacial grafts. Controlled delivery of growth factors (including via PRP and exosomes), co-printing of vascular or supportive stromal cells, and advanced perfusion culture techniques have all demonstrated improved angiogenic ingrowth and graft viability in recent bone and cartilage engineering studies. These approaches have yielded tangible improvements in graft outcomes – such as increased blood vessel density and more robust bone formation – in relevant models of orbital floor and nasal reconstruction. Continued refinement of sustained factor release vehicles, pre-vascularized bioinks, and immuno-compatible enhancers will further bridge the gap from laboratory to clinic. With each incremental advance, bioprinted craniofacial constructs are coming closer to achieving the immediate vascularization and integration needed for successful reconstruction in patients (AL-Hamoudi et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2022).

Case studies: clinical applications in head and neck reconstruction

Zygomatic bone reconstruction

The integral nature of the zygomatic bones to facial contour, symmetry, and aesthetics greatly impacts perceptions of attractiveness. Furthermore, the contributions of these structures to the lateral walls and floors of the orbits also emphasize their functional roles, providing shape and stability to the midface (Wang and Dechow, 2016). Thus, bioprinting has emerged as a promising technique for reconstructing zygomatic bone defects, offering patient-specific solutions that enhance both functional and aesthetic outcomes for these integral facial structures (Fig. 3).

3D-printing a custom implant for zygomatic bone repair. The process begins by creating a custom implant designed based on diagnostic imaging of the patient (step 1). The 3D model is printed and revised over several iterations until design criteria is met based (step 2). The final implant is then produced and implemented into surgery (step 3).

In a study of eight patients with zygomatic defects by Lee et al., CaOSiO2-P2O5-B2O3 (BGS-7) bioceramic implants showed 100 % bone fusion on CT after six months, with an average fusion rate of 76.97 %. Patients reported high satisfaction with regards to aesthetics and function, with no adverse events observed for the duration of the study (Lee et al., 2020). A separate study followed 7 patients with complex zygomatic bone defects reconstructed using patient-specific titanium implants, designed via CT-based mirroring and fabricated using Electron Beam Melting (EBM). The comparisons of reconstructed vs. normal zygomatic bone reflected a high degree of anatomical match, with no impairment of eye movement, mastication, or speech. Postoperative imaging showed stable osseointegration with no cases of implant loosening or infection during the follow-up period of 6–12 months (Moiduddin et al., 2019). Another article studying titanium implants created with 3D modeling examined 12 patients with previously mistreated complex zygomatico-maxillary fractures. Post-operative results reflected significant aesthetic improvement, restored midfacial volume, and high patient satisfaction, with no complications (Krasovsky et al., 2024). Regardless, challenges remain due to the structural complexity and vascular demand of the zygomatic bone, which often leads to postoperative complications such as infections, particularly with titanium implants. A 2020 study noted that out of 308 craniofacial mini plates implanted in 153 patients, 32 required removals due to infectious complications within four years (Jindal et al., 2021). One reason for this finding is the higher elastic modulus of titanium compared to human cortical bone, which can result in stress shielding, where the implant absorbs stress that would normally stimulate bone maintenance. This can potentially lead to bone resorption and implant loosening over time, requiring surgical intervention to repair (Niinomi and Nakai, 2011). The future directions for zygomatic bone reconstruction are focusing on new implant materials such as bioceramic, porous titanium implants as well as polyether ketone and different derivatives (Kuroda and Okido, 2012; Johansson et al., 2015).

Orbital floor and eye socket reconstruction

Like the zygomatic bones, the orbital floor and eye socket are crucial for eye functionality, protection, and aesthetic appeal. Reconstructing the orbital floor and eye socket presents significant challenges in trauma cases, due to the complex three-dimensional structure and limited surgical access (Kallaverja et al., 2024). Three-dimensional bioprinting has revolutionized orbital reconstruction by enabling the creation of patient-specific implants (PSIs) to circumvent the challenges (Fig. 4). One study showed that using 3D-printed anatomical models in orbital reconstruction improved implant accuracy, reduced surgical time (average of 28 min shorter), and better aesthetic and functional outcomes in a 17-patient sample. Decreases in postoperative complications such as enophthalmos and diplopia were also observed in this study. The implants were made of titanium mesh and 3D-printed anatomical models were used to pre-bend and shape the titanium mesh prior to surgery (Michelutti et al., 2024). Another study in 2018 showed that low-cost, 3D-printed orbital implant templates effectively restored orbital volume and globe position after exenteration (Dave et al., 2018). Over a 21-month follow-up, patients had no complications and maintained centered prostheses with high rates of cosmetic satisfaction. The approach was also cost-efficient and suitable for low-resource settings. This low cost was achieved using open-source software (like Meshmixer and Blender) and in-house 3D printing with polylactic acid (PLA) filament rather than outsourcing or using commercial-grade medical models. The authors reported that the entire process cost approximately US$2 per template, compared to US$300–$500 for commercially fabricated orbital models – a cost reduction of over 99 % compared to the standard practice (Dave et al., 2018). This paper was able to overcome one of the biggest challenges in 3D modeling of facial structures – cost and effectiveness in a low resource setting. More studies like this with larger sample sizes will be crucial in advancing facial reconstruction globally. A 2022 study demonstrated that 3D-printed patient-specific implants significantly improved clinical outcomes in orbital wall reconstruction. Patients showed restored orbital volume with postoperative scans confirming anatomical symmetry (Murray-Douglass et al., 2022). No complications were observed during follow-up. These studies highlight the capabilities of 3D-printed implants and their ability to offer a precise, reliable, and safe method for treating complex orbital fractures, setting a new standard for personalized and effective surgical care (Fig. 4).

Nasal and cartilage reconstruction

Recent advancements in 3D bioprinting have significantly enhanced nasal cartilage reconstruction, particularly in rhinoplasty procedures (Fig. 5). Due to the cartilaginous nature of the nasal region, adipose derived stem cells (ADSCs) have been of interest due to their compatibility with bioinks and chondrogenic potential (Nonaka et al., 2025). In a pilot clinical case, a 3D-bioprinted nasal cartilage graft using autologous ADSCs with GelMA or HA-based bioinks successfully implanted, maintaining shape and flexibility for over a year with no complications. CT scans confirmed structural integrity, and the patient reported high satisfaction with both function and appearance (Zhang et al., 2023). A different study reviewed recent innovations in bioprinted nasal cartilage, highlighting the success of cartilage-mimicking scaffolds in restoring structure and elasticity. Early clinical and preclinical outcomes showed stable nasal shape and biocompatibility with minimal immune response, alongside improvements in cell viability and mechanical strength (Zhou et al., 2023). A systematic review analyzed preclinical and limited early clinical data on 3D-bioprinted scaffolds for nasal cartilage repair. While most studies were animal-based, early human applications showed appropriate implant integration, maintenance of nasal contour, and no major complications (Chiesa-Estomba et al., 2021). Human nasoseptal chondrocytes were 3D-bioprinted using a collagen-based bioink. After six weeks in vitro and implantation in mice, the constructs maintained their shape and showed cartilage-like structure, including lacunae and type II collagen. Glycosaminoglycan levels were comparable to native nasal cartilage, suggesting strong potential for clinical use. Overall, there is immense potential of bioprinted constructs using ADSCs and chondrocytes, but the need for larger clinical trials of human populations remains to validate long-term viability (Lan et al., 2022).

Mandibular reconstruction

Reconstructing large mandibular defects presents significant challenges due to the complex anatomy and functional roles of the mandible. Traditional autografts, while commonly used, have notable limitations, including limited tissue availability, risk of donor site morbidity, and complications such as poor blood supply leading to graft failure (Ismail and Darwich, 2022). New advancements in 3D technology have provided promising results for mandible reconstruction (Fig. 6). Zhang et al. compared 10 patients who received 3D-printed, patient-specific titanium plates to 10 who received conventional plates for mandibular reconstruction (W.-B. Zhang et al., 2024). The 3D-printed group showed better accuracy (1.5 ± 0.5 mm vs. 2.1 ± 0.7 mm deviation) and more efficient surgeries, with 1.5 h less pre-op planning and 40 min shorter operating time, which can be attributed to improved fit and reduced adjustments (W.-B. Zhang et al., 2024). In a feasibility study involving 8 patients, 3D-printed patient-specific miniplates were used for fibula free flap fixation in mandibular reconstruction. The average deviation between the virtual surgical plan and the postoperative result was 3.64 ± 1.18 mm, demonstrating high accuracy. All patients achieved osseous union in the intersegmental gaps within 10 ± 2 months, with no instances of plate fracture, dislocation, or exposure reported during follow-up (Kreutzer et al., 2022). Hydrogels have also become a promising candidate in aiding reconstruction. Gelatin methacrylate (GelMA)-based hydrogels demonstrated over 90 % cell viability after printing, demonstrating strong biocompatibility. In rat mandibular defect models, hydrogel constructs combined with stem cells and growth factors achieved up to 85–95 % bone regeneration within 8–12 weeks. Additionally, composite hydrogels enhanced with bioactive ceramics showed superior bone formation and integration compared to hydrogel-only controls. These findings suggest strong translational potential for hydrogel-based systems in future clinical applications for mandibular reconstruction (Guo et al., 2023). A different study found that patients receiving 3D-printed orbital implants had better cosmetic outcomes than those with conventional meshes. Orbital volume deviation was lower (0.27 cm3 vs. 0.46 cm3), and satisfaction scores were higher (4.73 vs. 3.93 out of 5), with no major complications. While more research is necessary, current results suggest 3D-printed implants may offer improved accuracy and aesthetic outcomes in orbital reconstruction (Yang et al., 2025). With regards to cosmetic application, research shows that 3D bioprinting improves cosmetic outcomes in mandibular reconstruction by enabling patient-specific constructs that better match natural facial contours. It highlights improved symmetry and soft tissue support, with reduced need for intraoperative adjustments, contributing to more accurate and aesthetic facial restoration. These studies collectively demonstrate that 3D-printed, patient-specific implants offer significant advantages in mandibular reconstruction, including enhanced surgical accuracy, reduced operative times, and improved functional and aesthetic outcomes (Yang et al., 2023). Future research on 3D printing and hydrogels utilizing larger sample sizes will be crucial to improving the previously stated innovations in mandibular reconstruction.

Orthopedic surgery applications

3D bioprinting is also transforming orthopedic surgery by enabling the development of patient-specific implants and scaffolds that closely replicate native anatomical structures, significantly improving surgical precision and postoperative outcomes. In revision total knee arthroplasty, 3D-printed porous titanium cones have been used to manage complex bone defects, preserving residual bone stock and simplifying the surgical process through precise anatomical matching (Vaziri et al., 2025). In spinal surgery, customized 3D-printed implants have been applied to treat complex spine pathologies, with positive clinical outcomes and improved fit and stability, although long-term efficacy data are still under evaluation (Burnard et al., 2020). Orthopedic oncology has also benefited from this technology, where 3D bioprinting enables the production of complex metal implants tailored to a patient’s specific tumor resection site. These implants promote both osseointegration and angiogenesis, which are essential for long-term bone regeneration and stability (Meng et al., 2023). Beyond structural support, ongoing advancements in bioactive materials are pushing 3D bioprinting toward creating living constructs capable of osteoinduction and vascularization. Despite challenges, such as the need for standardized manufacturing protocols, mechanical reliability of bioprinted materials, and regulatory hurdles, 3D bioprinting remains a promising frontier in orthopedic surgery, offering highly personalized solutions that are reshaping surgical planning, execution, and recovery.

Challenges and limitations

Technical limitations

3D bioprinting for head and neck reconstruction faces several engineering constraints. Print resolution is the primary concern as most bioprinters can only deposit biomaterials with precision in the order of tens to hundreds of micrometers, falling short of recapitulating fine native tissue microarchitecture (Wu et al., 2023). This limited resolution impedes the precise reproduction of intricate structures like vascular networks and neural pathways. Printing speed is another bottleneck: building large, clinically sized tissues is exceedingly slow, often taking many hours. Prolonged print times can jeopardize cell viability, as living cells may undergo stress or die during the process (Vijayavenkataraman, 2023). As one analysis notes, bioprinting is inherently a “slow process”, and scaling up to human-sized constructs introduces the challenge of maintaining cell health over long print durations (Vijayavenkataraman, 2023). Recent advances like tomographic volumetric bioprinting and freeform suspension (FRESH) techniques aim to drastically cut printing time and support cells during fabrication, but these innovations remain in early development (Vijayavenkataraman, 2023). Scalability of the technology is thus constrained, with current bioprinters being well-suited for small constructs or tissue patches, but insufficient in printing larger structures, such as entire mandible or larynx replacements due to size and throughput limitations (McMillan et al., 2023). In sum, today’s bioprinting hardware and processes struggle to combine high resolution, high speed, and large volume production in a single platform.

Beyond the printer itself, material and mechanical limitations of bioprinted constructs pose challenges. Most 3D bioprinting in tissue engineering relies on soft hydrogel-based “bioinks” that are cell-compatible but mechanically weak (McMillan et al., 2023). Printed cartilage or bone substitutes must withstand physiological forces, yet scaffolds made of purely hydrogel inks often lack sufficient stiffness and durability. Achieving stronger biomimetic implants requires integrating stiffer polymers or support materials, which can be difficult to print with cells. Indeed, a major technical deficiency within the field is the inadequate mechanical properties of current bioprinted constructs (McMillan et al., 2023). Multi-material printing strategies are being explored to address this – for instance, reinforcing a cell-laden hydrogel with a printed thermoplastic or polymer network that can improve strength (McMillan et al., 2023). However, balancing mechanical stability with biocompatibility and printability is an ongoing challenge. Likewise, controlling the cellular architecture within prints is limited; extrusion bioprinters have relatively coarse control, making it difficult to position multiple cell types in complex arrangements or to achieve the microscopic cell organization of native tissue (Wu et al., 2023). In summary, current bioprinting technologies face technical limitations including insufficient resolution, slow fabrication speeds, limited scalability, suboptimal mechanical strength of prints, and imperfect control over cell placement. These challenges must be overcome to reliably fabricate large and multifunctional head and neck tissues.

Biological barriers

In addition to engineering issues, a host of biological barriers must be addressed to ensure bioprinted grafts survive and preserve their function long-term. A fundamental challenge is maintaining cell viability and tissue vascularization in printed constructs. Engineered tissues thicker than a few hundred microns require a blood vessel network to supply nutrients; without it, the core of the construct becomes ischemic, and cells rapidly perish. To date, bioprinted tissues often rely on the host to vascularize the graft after implantation, which is a slow and inefficient process. For example, early attempts at bioprinting a full circumferential tracheal replacement in animals failed due to lack of a vascularized mucosal layer, leading to failure of the implant to integrate with the surrounding vessels and epithelia, thus causing obstruction and scarring (McMillan et al., 2023). This illustrates the importance of printing multi-tissue structures (e.g. including an epithelial lining for airways) and pre-vascularizing constructs. Without built-in vasculature and proper tissue interfaces, even anatomically correct grafts may not integrate or may trigger adverse reactions. Ensuring long-term graft viability thus remains a major hurdle: bioprinted cartilage, bone or muscle must not only take root, but it must also remodel and mature with the host own tissue over the following months or years. Achieving this will likely require advances in pre-print cell conditioning (such as maturation in bioreactors) and incorporation of pro-angiogenic factors or vessel-forming cells into the bioink (McMillan et al., 2023).

Immune compatibility is another critical barrier. The ideal scenario for transplantation is to use the patient’s own (autologous) cells in the bioink, thereby eliminating the risk of immune rejection (McMillan et al., 2023). Bioprinting does enable personalized, patient-specific grafts using autologous stem cells or chondrocytes but obtaining and expanding those cells is arduous and expensive. Harvesting cells requires extra invasive procedures (e.g. a cartilage biopsy or bone marrow draw) and expanding them to the quantities needed for printing can be time-consuming and contain the possibility to alter cell phenotype (McMillan et al., 2023). On the other hand, using allogeneic cells from a donor or a universal cell line comes with the risk of immune rejection and would likely necessitate immunosuppressive therapy (McMillan et al., 2023). Researchers are investigating hypoimmunogenic or “universal” cell lines, but this is still experimental. Even with autologous cells, there is a graft integration challenge: the printed tissue must communicate and connect with the host’s systems. This includes inosculation of blood vessels but also potentially neural integration (for muscle or sensory organs) or mechanical integration with surrounding bone or cartilage. Achieving full functional integration is complex – for instance, a bioprinted skeletal muscle flap in the head and neck would need to be re-innervated by host nerves to restore motion or sensation, and a bioprinted larynx would need to integrate with airway mucosa and cartilage framework. Such multi-faceted integration issues remain largely unsolved. As one commentary on bioprinted organs notes, even if we can fabricate a whole organ, “how to ensure that the body can accept the 3D printed [tissue], how to keep [it] alive for a longer period of time, and how to make it effectively connect with other organ systems in the body” are open questions (Huang et al., 2024). Thus, biological barriers – including ensuring vascularization, avoiding immune rejection, and promoting seamless integration with host tissues – are significant obstacles on the path to clinically viable bioprinted head and neck grafts.

Regulatory and ethical considerations

The regulatory landscape for 3D bioprinting in medicine is still in its infancy. Regulatory agencies like the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) have not yet established specific guidelines or approval pathways for bioprinted organs or tissues (Ricci et al., 2023). Traditional medical device regulations do not directly apply to living, patient-specific tissue grafts, and while there are frameworks for cell-based therapies and tissue-engineered products, bioprinting occupies a complex middle ground. The FDA has been proactive in approving 3D-printed medical devices such as implants and surgical guides, with dozens cleared for clinical use, but these are acellular products (Ricci et al., 2023). Bioprinted tissues, which combine living cells with biomaterial scaffolds, fall under the category of Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products (ATMPs) in Europe or analogous categories in the US, but no bioprinted construct has reached clinical approval as a therapy. As of 2023, not a single 3D-bioprinted tissue or organ has been FDA approved, and no clinical trials of bioprinted tissues are registered, underscoring the early stage of clinical translation (McMillan et al., 2023). Regulators are cautious due to the need for novel standards of safety and efficacy since a bioprinted graft is neither a device nor a drug, but a combination of both. This necessitates developing new testing protocols, for example, durability testing of a living implant, and ensuring quality control at every step of the bioprinting process (Huang et al., 2024). The lack of clear regulations poses uncertainty for researchers and companies: without defined approval criteria, bringing a bioprinted head/neck tissue to market is a daunting prospect.

Ethical considerations are deeply intertwined with regulation. One concern is how to equitably allocate personalized bioprinted grafts if they become clinically available. Unlike donor organs which are scarce by nature, bioprinted organs could, in theory, be manufactured on demand – but in practice, resource limitations (specialized printers, skilled personnel, expensive reagents) may make them initially available only to a few. This raises questions of fairness and access: Who “gets” a bioprinted larynx if only a handful can be produced, and how do we ensure treatments are not limited to wealthy institutions or countries? There is also the question of cost: early bioprinted therapies are likely to be extremely costly to develop and manufacture, potentially exacerbating healthcare disparities. Ethical frameworks will need to address how these therapies can be delivered justly and who will bear the costs. Additionally, the very act of bioprinting human tissues brings bioethical questions about the source of cells and materials. Cells may come from the patient or donors; in the latter case, issues of consent and donor rights arise, especially if cells are used to create tissues for use in another person. The use of embryonic stem cells or induced pluripotent stem cells for bioprinting has its own ethical debates, although induced pluripotent cells (reprogrammed from adult cells) mitigate some controversy by avoiding embryonic tissue (McMillan et al., 2023). Another grey area is the creation of hybrid or novel tissues such as printing a composite organ that includes both human and animal cells, or cells with genetically modified components. Such possibilities challenge existing definitions in ethics and law. Regulatory bodies will likely involve ethics committees to evaluate first-in-human bioprinted products, weighing risks and societal implications. As one recent review emphasized, ethical and legal guidelines must evolve in parallel with the science, addressing issues from animal testing of bioprinted organs to the moral status of lab-made tissues (Huang et al., 2024).

In summary, regulatory and ethical considerations currently lag behind the technology: no clear approval pathways exist yet for bioprinted head and neck tissues, and pressing ethical questions – regarding safety, consent, equity, and resource allocation – remain to be fully resolved as the field progresses.

Future directions

Looking ahead, several emerging technological innovations promise to push 3D bioprinting beyond its current limitations. One area of intense research is the development of next generation bioprinters with improved capabilities. This includes printers that offer higher resolution patterning (for example, using two-photon polymerization or other microfabrication techniques to create finer features) as well as faster “volumetric” printing methods. A breakthrough in this domain is volumetric bioprinting, which projects patterns of light or ultrasound into a cell-laden resin to solidify an entire 3D construct almost instantaneously, rather than layer-by-layer. Early demonstrations of tomographic volumetric bioprinting have shown the ability to fabricate centimeter-scale cell-laden structures in under a minute (Vijayavenkataraman, 2023) – a drastic speed improvement that could make printing large implants far more practical. Meanwhile, innovations like freeform reversible embedding of suspended hydrogels (FRESH) allow the printing of soft, complex shapes by extruding bioink into a supportive bath that simulates an in vivo-like environment (Vijayavenkataraman, 2023). This approach has enabled the creation of intricately shaped cartilage and cardiac tissues that maintain high cell viability. We can also expect multi-material and multi-cell printing to advance. Future bioprinters may incorporate multiple print heads or modalities (extrusion, droplet, laser, etc.) in one platform, enabling the deposition of various cell types and biomaterials in a single construct. Such capability is crucial for head and neck applications, as many implants require complex combinations of cartilaginous and bone analogs to interface with. Emerging systems are exploring multi-nozzle setups that can switch between bioinks seamlessly, as well as coaxial nozzles that print a core-shell structure (useful for printing vessels or nerves with an outer sheath). The 4D bioprinting is another innovation on the horizon. This concept involves printing structures that can later change shape or properties in response to stimuli (e.g. temperature or chemical signals). In head and neck reconstruction, 4D bioprinted implants might be designed to gradually unfold or biodegrade to encourage tissue remodeling, or to respond to the patient’s movements. Although in its infancy, 4D bioprinting integrates smart materials into constructs and could lead to more dynamic, functional grafts.

Parallel to hardware advances, significant progress is expected in bioink development. Future bioinks will need to be biologically richer and more functional. Researchers are exploring composite bioinks that include not just one or two components but a cocktail of extracellular matrix proteins, growth factors, and even gene-delivery vectors to kickstart tissue maturation. For example, including decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM) powders specific to a target tissue (such as cartilage dECM for printing a new ear) can provide tissue-specific cues that enhance cell differentiation and integration (McMillan et al., 2023). Vascularized bioinks are another frontier, which would contain vascular endothelial cells and perhaps supporting stromal cells, pre-organized in a way that they can self-assemble into capillary networks after printing. There is also interest in spheroid- or organoid-based bioprinting, rather than single-cell suspensions, tiny multicellular spheroids could be used as the building blocks in printing, better mimicking native architecture. This approach might improve the overall organization and function of the printed tissue. In sum, future technological innovations – from faster, higher-resolution printers to smarter bioinks loaded with cells and signals – are expected to greatly enhance the fidelity and functionality of bioprinted head and neck tissues. These advancements aim to print tissues that are larger, more complex (multi-tissue), and more immediately functional, edging closer to true “organ printing.”

Research gaps

Despite rapid progress, there remain conspicuous gaps in current research that future studies need to address, especially for head and neck applications. One such gap is nerve regeneration and integration. While considerable effort has gone into bioprinting bone and cartilage for craniofacial reconstruction, there is relatively little work on printing neural elements that could restore sensation or motor function (e.g. facial nerve repair, or laryngeal nerve regeneration for vocal cord function). Peripheral nerve bioprinting is still an emerging field; recent advances have shown that 3D printed scaffolds can guide nerve growth in animal models (Liao et al., 2023), but applying this to complex head and neck nerves is largely unexplored. Developing strategies for bioprinting nerves or at least conduits that facilitate nerve ingrowth would fill a crucial need for truly functional tissue-engineered grafts in the head and neck. Similarly, muscle tissue bioprinting for applications like tongue or pharyngeal muscle replacement is in its infancy. These tissues have unique requirements, such as alignment of muscle fibers, innervation, etc., that have yet to be met by current bioprinting technologies and research.

Another underexplored area is the bioprinting of glandular tissues such as salivary glands or thyroid tissue, which are sometimes lost in head and neck cancer surgeries. Reconstructing a salivary gland, for instance, would require printing branching duct structures and secretory units and ensuring they can produce saliva – a complex challenge not yet achieved. To date, most head and neck bioprinting research has focused on structural and supportive tissues (bone, cartilage, soft tissue filler) rather than functional organs or glands. Multi-tissue interface engineering is also a gap. The head and neck region contains many junctions between different tissue types (bone-cartilage in the jaw, cartilagemuscle in the larynx, soft tissue-bone in the tongue attachment, etc.). Printing a single tissue type is difficult enough; printing a seamless interface between two tissue types, along with gradients of stiffness, cell type, and extracellular matrix composition, remains largely uncharted territory. Future research must address how to engineer these interfaces so that bioprinted constructs behave more like natural composites rather than single homogeneous blocks.

Importantly, there is also a translational research gap: bridging the in vitro and animal studies to human clinical trials. As noted, no clinical trial in tissue-regenerative bioprinting has been launched yet (McMillan et al., 2023). Many published studies demonstrate feasibility in laboratory or small animal models, but often these are short-term proofs of concept. Long-term outcome data is scarce, and studies measuring how bioprinted implants fare years after implantation in terms of function, stability, or integration are going to be crucial for adoption of these technologies. Current studies tend to report data for periods of only weeks to months of implantation in rodents or rabbits (McMillan et al., 2023). To move forward, larger animal models and longer-term evaluations will be needed to assess durability and safety over time. Standardized assessment protocols constitute another gap: different research groups measure success differently (some focus on mechanical strength, others on cell viability or gene expression), making it hard to compare results or identify best practices. The field would benefit from agreed-upon benchmarks for a “successful” bioprinted tissue, for instance, percentage of volume vascularized after X weeks in vivo, or load-bearing capacity for bioprinted bone. Addressing these research gaps – nerve and muscle bioprinting, glandular tissue printing, multi-tissue interfaces, and rigorous long-term in vivo testing – will be critical for the next wave of advances. By filling these gaps, researchers can ensure that 3D bioprinting realizes its full spectrum of applications in head and neck reconstruction, beyond the relatively narrow set of tissues that have been the focus so far (McMillan et al., 2023).

Clinical application

Moving 3D bioprinting from the laboratory bench to routine clinical use will require coordinated efforts on multiple fronts. A key step is the standardization of bioprinting processes and products. For regulatory agencies to approve a bioprinted graft, the fabrication process must be reliable and reproducible. This means developing standardized protocols for every stage: cell harvesting and expansion, bioink preparation, printing parameters, and post-print maturation. Right now, protocols vary widely between labs; establishing consensus “good manufacturing practice” (GMP) guidelines for bioprinting will help ensure that a tissue printed in one facility is comparable in quality to that printed in another (Huang et al., 2024). Efforts are underway to define quality control metrics. Metrics like viability thresholds and sterility tests for printed cells could become required aspects of regulatory submissions. Automated, closed bioprinting systems may be advantageous here, as they reduce user-to-user variation. In the future, one could envision dedicated bioprinting centers with clean-room environments and robotic handling of printing and incubation, to consistently produce implants under strict standards.

Another critical step is conducting multicenter preclinical and clinical trials once a prototype product is ready. Because this technology is so new, initial clinical trials might be small “first-in-human” case studies. As confidence grows, larger trials comparing bioprinted grafts to current standards, like allografts or autologous tissue flaps, will be needed. These trials will have to monitor not only the traditional clinical outcomes like healing or functionality, but also integration and long-term safety. Collaboration across centers will accelerate learning; if multiple hospitals can use a similar bioprinting approach, data on patients can be pooled to evaluate efficacy. Importantly, surgeons, bioengineers, and regulatory experts will need to work closely in designing these studies. A challenge here is that surgeons must be trained in the use of bioprinted grafts, and commonly used surgical techniques may need adaptation to accommodate for the complexity of bioprinted grafts. Thus, part of clinical translation is training and education, ensuring that the surgical community is prepared to implement bioprinted solutions.

Cost and scalability considerations also come into play for clinical adoption. Currently, bespoke bioprinted constructs are expensive to fabricate. For effective cost reduction, the future improvements in printing speed will help, as will economies of scale if bioprinting moves from one-off research projects to more industrialized production. The involvement of biotech companies and possibly large medical device manufacturers could drive costs down through process optimization. Some analysts note that heavy competition in the bioprinter market is already pushing prices of printers and bioinks downward, which bodes well for future affordability (Vijayavenkataraman, 2023). Nonetheless, demonstrating cost-effectiveness in a healthcare context will be important – a bioprinted solution will need to show either improved outcomes or reduced overall costs to justify its use. Health economics studies may be required as part of late-stage clinical trials.

Finally, navigating the regulatory approval process is an essential step toward widespread adoption. Early engagement with regulatory bodies is advisable; researchers and companies are encouraged to seek feedback from the FDA/EMA via regenerative medicine advanced therapy (RMAT) designations or similar mechanisms, to clarify what data is expected. Regulators will likely require robust evidence of safety and efficacy, as well as possibilities of adverse tumorigenic and immunogenic effects. They may also require long-term post-market surveillance if approvals are granted, to track outcomes. Achieving the first approvals for a bioprinted product, starting with something as simple as a cartilage implant, will be a watershed moment, creating a precedent that can guide subsequent applications. In summary, the path to clinical translation demands standardization, scaled-up trials, cost mitigation, and proactive regulatory strategy. Interdisciplinary cooperation will be vital at every step – bioengineers to refine the product, clinicians to apply and evaluate it, and regulators to provide oversight – so that 3D bioprinting ultimately makes the leap into everyday surgical practice (McMillan et al., 2023).

Optimization

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) into 3D bioprinting is poised to significantly enhance design and fabrication optimization. AI algorithms can tackle the complexity of bioprinting by discovering patterns and solutions that might elude human intuition. One promising application is design optimization of scaffolds and structures. By using computational models, ML can iterate through thousands of design permutations and predict which designs will best meet desired properties. Aspects of bioprinted products, such as tensile strength or optimal nutrient diffusion can be selected for and tailored to the needs of a specific implant’s functionality. For example, machine learning has been used to optimize scaffold porosity and architecture in conventional 3D printing, and similar approaches are being extended to bioprinting to achieve constructs with precise mechanical and biological performance (J. Sun et al., 2023). AI can also assist in materials optimization – given a database of bioink formulations and their printability or cell survival outcomes, ML models can predict which new compositions might yield better results, guiding bioink development more efficiently than trial-and-error experiments.

During the printing process itself, AI can provide real-time monitoring and control. Vision systems and sensors can be mounted on bioprinters to observe each layer as it is deposited. Using ML-based image analysis, the printer could detect defects or deviations (like a strand printed in the wrong location or a droplet that misfired) and adjust on the fly, thus improving fidelity. Such in situ correction is already explored in industrial 3D printing and is beginning to be applied in bioprinting (Vijayavenkataraman, 2023). Similarly, AI-driven feedback loops might adjust parameters like extrusion speed or crosslinking intensity to ensure the printed construct matches the digital design as closely as possible. Another arena is post-print analysis: AI can be used to analyze microscopy or imaging data of printed tissues to assess cell distribution, identify any regions of poor cell health, or predict mechanical weak points, thereby informing refinements in the next print iteration.

Perhaps the most transformative role of AI/ML will be in managing the enormous complexity when all variables are considered together. The numerous parameters, as discussed above, involved in bioprinting create a high-dimensional design space, and machine learning excels at rapid analysis of such data. Recent literature shows that both traditional ML and deep learning algorithms have been applied to optimize bioprinting process–material–performance relationships, yielding more stable processes and improved outcomes (J. Sun et al., 2023). For example, one study used a neural network to predict cell viability based on printing parameters, allowing the system to find a sweet spot that maximized survival without sacrificing print resolution (C. Zhang et al., 2024). By integrating these approaches, future bioprinters could come with intelligent software that automatically suggests optimal print settings for a given tissue geometry and cell type, lowering the expertise barrier and improving consistency.

AI can also facilitate the personalized medicine aspects of bioprinting. Given a patient’s imaging data (say a CT scan of a bone defect or an MRI of a cartilage lesion), algorithms could generate a custom implant design that fits perfectly and has internal micro-architecture tuned for that patient’s needs. This design might then be fed into the bioprinter with ML ensuring the print executes correctly. The convergence of AI and bioprinting thus enables a vision of “smart bioprinting”, where machines not only carry out instructions but actively assist in designing and fine-tuning tissue constructs. In the long term, this could dramatically accelerate development cycles and yield bioprinted grafts with superior functionality. As one perspective summarized, machine learning can be used to optimize the bioprinting process, improve dimensional accuracy, detect defects, and aid material design (Yu and Jiang, 2020). In practice, this means future bioprinted head and neck tissues could benefit from AI-optimized architectures and printing protocols optimized for cell health, bringing us closer to implants that function similarly to native tissues.

Conclusion

The field of 3D bioprinting in head and neck reconstruction has made remarkable strides in recent years, and the sections above have highlighted both the progress and remaining challenges. Key advancements include the successful fabrication of patient-specific constructs such as ear cartilage, bone grafts, and airway scaffolds, demonstrating the feasibility of replacing complex craniofacial structures with bioprinted alternatives. For instance, a landmark case was the implantation of a bioprinted outer ear (AuriNovo) derived from a patient’s own cells, which achieved a normal ear shape in a patient with microtia (Huang et al., 2024). This and other pilot studies underscore the potential of bioprinting to provide personalized solutions that traditional methods cannot easily offer. We have also seen significant improvements in bioprinting technology and materials – from multi-nozzle printers that can co-deposit bone and cartilage in a single construct, to novel bioinks that better support cell viability and function. These developments have been coupled with encouraging in vivo results in preclinical models: bioprinted grafts for tracheal segments, mandible replacements, and nasal cartilage have all shown proof-of-concept integration and functionality in animals (McMillan et al., 2023). Such achievements, often reported in the last few years, validate the concept that 3D bioprinting can produce anatomically precise, patient-tailored grafts for head and neck reconstruction.

At the same time, this review has emphasized that the full revolutionary potential of 3D bioprinting in head and neck surgery is yet to be realized. The technology stands on the cusp of transforming surgical practice by offering on-demand, patient-specific tissues, a paradigm shift from using autologous grafts or alloplastic implants. If the remaining challenges are overcome, surgeons could one day bioprint replacements for virtually any tissue lost to trauma or cancer: a new mandible that perfectly matches the patient’s original jaw, or a section of trachea engineered with both cartilage rings and lining mucosa. Patients would no longer suffer morbidity from donor site surgeries, since grafts would be printed rather than harvested. Craniofacial deformities and functional deficits could be addressed with constructs that not only restore anatomy but also integrate and grow with the patient’s body, something static titanium implants cannot do. Furthermore, because bioprinting can utilize the patient’s own cells, the risk of rejection would be minimal, and long-term immunosuppressive therapy may be avoided (McMillan et al., 2023). In essence, 3D bioprinting offers a pathway to regenerate head and neck tissues in situ, potentially achieving repairs that are indistinguishable from the original tissue in form and function. This could revolutionize reconstructive surgery, bringing us into an era where losing a body part no longer means permanent disability or deformity – instead, a new one can be printed and implanted with bespoke precision.

Realizing this vision will require concerted efforts and is in many ways a call to action for the scientific and medical community. Interdisciplinary collaboration is paramount going forward. Engineers, materials scientists, and biologists must continue to work together with clinicians to design constructs that meet real clinical needs and to develop surgical techniques for implementation (McMillan et al., 2023). Likewise, early and frequent dialogue with regulatory agencies is needed to pave a smooth pathway for approvals – regulators should be considered partners in innovation, helping define the standards that will ensure safety and efficacy of bioprinted products (Huang et al., 2024). There is also a role for ethicists, health economists, and policymakers to anticipate the societal implications and to strive for equitable adoption of these technologies. Funding, resources, and support must also be sustained and expanded. Bioprinting research is resource-intensive, often requiring specialized equipment and multidisciplinary expertise; increased funding from government, industry, and academic sources will accelerate vital research and development efforts. Importantly, translational research should be prioritized: initiatives such as multi-center consortia or public-private partnerships can help drive bioprinting from laboratory prototypes to clinically deployable therapies by pooling resources and knowledge. As highlighted, the first human trials will be a critical milestone, and stakeholders should prioritize the studies and preparatory work needed to launch these trials (McMillan et al., 2023).

In conclusion, 3D bioprinting holds the transformative promise of rebuilding parts of the head and neck with living, patient-specific tissues, potentially restoring both appearance and function in ways not previously possible. The journey from concept to clinic is underway, marked by impressive achievements but also significant hurdles that demand further research. By summarizing the current challenges, we underscore where innovation is still required – in printer technology, in biology, in regulatory frameworks. By outlining future directions, we illuminate the path forward – harnessing new technologies like AI, exploring underdeveloped research areas, and methodically translating findings into practice. With collaborative, interdisciplinary effort and a strong focus on translational outcomes, 3D bioprinting is poised to move from an experimental technique to a cornerstone of head and neck reconstructive surgery. The community of scientists, engineers, clinicians, and regulators must jointly rise to the challenge, ensuring that this groundbreaking technology fulfills its potential to improve patients’ lives. Each successful bioprinted implant, each clinical trial, each regulatory guideline will be a step toward a new era of regenerative reconstruction – one in which the phrase “tissue replacement” truly means printing a new part for the patient. Such a future, though ambitious, is within reach, and it is one that we should actively strive to achieve through continued innovation, rigorous research, and compassionate clinical application.
