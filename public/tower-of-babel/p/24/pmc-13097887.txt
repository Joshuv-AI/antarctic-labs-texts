CT-based radiomics for modeling surgical decision-making in young and middle-aged patients with ARCO stage III osteonecrosis of the femoral head: an age-stratified retrospective study
Authors: Xinran Zhang, Pei Qiu, Guoji Shi, Tianwei Xia, Nan Zhang, Tong Xu, Yun Li, Zhitao Wang, Jirong Shen, Ting Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) may progress to collapse and disability. While total hip arthroplasty (THA) is standard in older patients, surgical decision-making in younger adults remains heterogeneous. CT provides high spatial resolution for evaluating late-stage ONFH, and radiomics may offer quantitative support.ObjectiveTo investigate whether the predictive value of CT-based radiomics for surgical choice under routine practice is age-dependent and whether clinical variables add value across age subgroups.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 160 patients (201 hips) with ARCO stage III ONFH who underwent hip-preserving surgery or THA. Radiomics features were extracted from preoperative CT images and used to build radiomics-only and fusion models with four classifiers. Repeated k-fold cross-validation and bootstrap validation were applied. Three-level age stratification (< 65 years, < 50 years, and 20–35 vs. 36–50 years) and SHAP analysis were used.ResultsFusion models outperformed radiomics-only models in patients younger than 65 years. In younger subgroups, radiomics-only models achieved comparable or superior performance, while the contribution of clinical variables decreased. SHAP analysis showed diminishing importance of age and disease duration, with texture-based radiomics features dominating in younger patients.ConclusionCT-based radiomics, combined with interpretable machine learning, may provide quantitative decision support for modeling and supporting routine surgical decision-making between hip-preserving procedures and THA, particularly in younger patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09670-8.

Introduction

Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) is a progressive bone disorder caused by disruption of blood supply, which may ultimately lead to structural collapse and functional impairment [1, 2]. Clinically, patients requiring surgical intervention are primarily treated with either hip-preserving procedures or total hip arthroplasty (THA), with treatment selection largely guided by patient age, disease stage, and radiographic characteristics of the femoral head. For patients older than 65 years, ONFH is often diagnosed at an advanced stage, with pronounced imaging features such as femoral head collapse and sclerotic band formation. In this setting, THA is widely regarded as the standard treatment to restore function and improve prognosis [3]. In contrast, THA is less desirable for younger patients because of the limited lifespan of prosthetic joints, higher activity levels, and longer life expectancy, all of which increase the risk of revision surgery. Consequently, hip-preserving procedures are generally preferred in younger individuals to delay or avoid joint replacement.

Computed tomography (CT) is considered the most reliable modality for evaluating mid- to late-stage ONFH, enabling detailed assessment of structural abnormalities such as collapse, cystic changes, trabecular disruption, and sclerotic rim formation. Owing to its superior spatial resolution [4, 5], CT is widely used for ARCO staging of ONFH. Moreover, CT imaging is highly standardized and reproducible [6] and is routinely performed during preoperative hip evaluation without additional patient burden [7, 8]. However, CT-based staging remains dependent on radiologist experience, and inter-observer variability in stage assessment has been reported, contributing to subjectivity and uncertainty in surgical decision-making. These limitations highlight the need for quantitative and reproducible imaging-based tools capable of capturing subvisual disease characteristics.

Radiomics integrates medical imaging with machine learning to extract high-dimensional quantitative features, including intensity, texture, and shape descriptors, from routine imaging data [9]. As a quantitative approach for characterizing lesion heterogeneity, radiomics has demonstrated promising performance across a range of clinical applications, including musculoskeletal tumors [10], chondrosarcomas [11], infectious diseases such as COVID-19 and influenza pneumonia [12], and other bone disorders [13]. In ONFH, previous studies have shown that radiomics features derived from CT images can aid in predicting surgical outcomes and treatment response. Liu et al. developed a decision-tree model combining clinical and CT radiomics features to evaluate hip-preserving surgery efficacy [14]. Xue et al. further integrated imaging and clinical variables to predict outcomes after allogeneic fibula transplantation [15], while Chen et al. demonstrated the independent predictive value of radiomics for avascular bone grafting outcomes [16].

Despite these advances, most existing radiomics studies rely on a single age cutoff, potentially overlooking the nonlinear effects and heterogeneity of age, particularly among younger patients. Age influences bone quality, regenerative capacity, disease progression, and surgical outcomes [17–19]. Therefore, the aim of this study was to compare the relative contributions of clinical and radiomics features in modeling surgical choice between hip-preserving and hip-replacement strategies using a three-tier age stratification framework. By integrating multiple machine-learning classifiers with SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) analysis, we sought to elucidate age-dependent shifts in feature importance and to provide an interpretable, quantitative decision support for individualized surgical decision-making in younger ONFH patients.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. 2021NL-193-02) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. As this was a retrospective study using existing medical records, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee.

Patient selection

This retrospective study included 160 patients (201 hips) diagnosed with ONFH who underwent surgery and had at least 2 years of follow-up between February 2019 and April 2023 at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine.

Inclusion criteria: (1) ONFH confirmed by intraoperative pathology; (2) available preoperative CT images with evident signs of ONFH, such as large necrotic areas and cystic changes; (3) no prior hip surgeries before the index operation; (4) patients aged under 65; (5) ARCO stage III.

Exclusion criteria: (1) poor-quality CT images unsuitable for radiomics analysis; (2) other diseases such as malignant tumors, hip tumors, or bone tuberculosis; (3) collapse and conversion to THA within 2 years after initial hip-preserving surgery; (4) preference-driven discordant decisions, defined as cases in which the recorded rationale indicated that the final procedure was primarily determined by explicit patient refusal/insistence and was clearly inconsistent with the treating team’s preoperative imaging/clinical assessment.

The outcome label was the procedure performed (hip-preserving surgery or THA). All cases were managed by a single orthopedic team (one chief surgeon with > 25 years’ experience and 3–4 associate attending/attending surgeons); surgical plans were determined in routine preoperative conferences following routine practice based on ARCO staging and imaging/clinical assessment, with final adjudication by the chief surgeon, and documented for every patient. We reviewed each patient’s preoperative conference record to confirm the stated rationale and to identify preference-driven discordant decisions for exclusion.

Clinical and imaging data

Clinical data included demographic variables (age, sex, affected side, course of disease, BMI, etiologies) and laboratory values (D-dimer, alkaline phosphatase ALP, white blood cell count WBC, neutrophil percentage N, α-L-fucosidase AFU).

All patients underwent standard hip CT scans using either a 128-slice Philips Brilliance CT or a 64-slice GE LightSpeed VCT scanner. Images were obtained in DICOM format and processed in 3D Slicer (v5.0.2), with voxel size normalized to 1 × 1 × 1 mm and grayscale levels resampled to 64. The region of interest (ROI) was manually delineated on preoperative CT images to include the osteonecrotic lesion and surrounding abnormal-density regions within the femoral head. These regions were identified based on characteristic CT findings of osteonecrosis, including sclerosis, cystic degeneration, trabecular disruption, and subchondral changes. The entire femoral head was not segmented; instead, the ROI was confined to the lesion-centered abnormal bone area while excluding the femoral neck and acetabulum. This standardized lesion-oriented delineation strategy was applied consistently across all patients to ensure comparability. All ROIs were manually delineated by a radiologist with over 3 years of experience in musculoskeletal imaging. To ensure segmentation consistency, all delineations were subsequently reviewed by a senior radiologist. Any disagreements were resolved through consensus discussion before feature extraction.

Radiomics feature extraction

After Z-score normalization and gray-level discretization (binWidth = 25), radiomics features were extracted using the open-source Pyradiomics package. A total of 1,130 features were obtained, including first-order statistics, shape descriptors, and texture features from GLCM, GLSZM, GLRLM, NGTDM, and GLDM matrices. All features were standardized for downstream modeling.

To mitigate potential inter-scanner variability, ComBat harmonization—an empirical Bayes–based approach—was applied to the standardized radiomics feature matrix, with scanner manufacturer specified as the batch variable. Harmonization was implemented using the neuroCombat Python package, and all subsequent feature selection and model construction were performed on the harmonized features.

Feature selection

To avoid overfitting, features were first filtered using ANOVA, retaining 699 with high variance. LASSO (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) regression with 10-fold cross-validation further reduced dimensions. To eliminate potential collinearity interference from the age variable, Spearman rank correlation analysis was performed on the feature set retained by LASSO, and significantly correlated variables were removed from subse- quent models to ensure that the results of the “image model” were not confounded by age factors. In terms of clinical characteristics, univariate logistic regression was used for screening, and the final variables included in the screening were used for modeling.

Clinical feature selection was performed using univariate logistic regression. The final model used age, course of disease, and D-dimer as clinical variables.

Model construction, validation, and performance

Key features were input into four classifiers—random forest (RF), logistic regression (LR), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), and support vector machine (SVC)—to construct both “radiomics-only” and “clinical + radiomics” models.

A 10-fold repeated cross-validation (5 repeats) was used to ensure model stability and generalizability. A fixed random seed (Seed = 42) was set for each division to ensure reproducibility. Additionally, to address the imbalance caused by small sample sizes in the two subgroups under 50 years old, the SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) method was used to balance the category distribution in the dataset.

Model performance was evaluated by ROC curves, AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and F1. Bootstrap validation assessed internal consistency. The DeLong test compared model AUCs. To improve model interpretability, the SHAP analysis was introduced to perform a visual analysis of the global and local contributions of each variable and assess their changing trends in different age groups and classifiers to explore the key feature dominance mechanism and classifier selection criteria. Based on age stratification, patients were grouped into 20–35 years (46 patients, 66 hips) and 36–50 years (51 patients, 68 hips). Model performance across age groups

Models in the < 65 age group (Entire Cohort)

In patients younger than 65, all four classifiers (RF, LR, SVC, KNN) performed well (Table 3; Fig. 2). The clinical + radiomics fusion model consistently outperformed the radiomics-only model. For example, the RF fusion model outperformed the radiomics-only model (AUC 0.92 vs. 0.85). Similar improvements were observed for SVC and LR. Bootstrap validation confirmed model stability (AUC/F1/AP SD < 0.02), and DeLong tests showed statistically significant AUC improvements for fusion models. As shown in Fig. 3, the calibration curves of the Random Forest models and the SVC models show good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities across age groups. The decision curves of the Random Forest and SVC models in patients younger than 65 years demonstrated that the clinical–radiomics fusion models consistently provided higher net benefit than the radiomics-only models across most threshold probability ranges. Similar trends were observed across other age groups (see Supplementary Figure).

Models in the < 50 age group

In patients under 50, fusion models still outperformed radiomics-only models, but with smaller margins (Table 3; Fig. 4). RF showed an AUC increase from 0.84 to 0.85, and AP from 0.84 to 0.87. SHAP analysis revealed a marked drop in the importance of age as a predictive feature, indicating increasing dominance of radiomics features in younger patients.

Models in the 20—35 and 36—50 subgroups

To eliminate age interference, patients under 50 were further divided into 20–35 and 36–50 subgroups (Table 3). In these highly homogeneous cohorts, fusion models did not outperform radiomics-only models. For instance, in the 36–50 age group, the AUC value of the fusion model showed a certain degree of difference only in SVC.

In the 20–35 group, RF test AUC was 0.76 (Radiomics) vs. 0.73(Clinical+Radiomics), with no improvement in F1 or AP. The performance of feature fusion in several models, including random forest, is decreased. SHAP results showed that clinical variables contributed minimally, while radiomics features (e.g., original firstorder Kurtosis) dominated model output, confirming their predictive value in young, homogeneous populations. The representative ROC and SHAP results of the Random Forest and SVC models are shown in the main text. Additional performance metrics, ROC curves, and SHAP visualizations for all classifiers and age subgroups can be found in the appendix.

SHAP interpretability analysis and feature importance

SHAP analysis results

As illustrated in Fig. 5, taking the SVC model as an example, SHAP values revealed that age stood as one of the most influential features in < 65 models—its prominent position is evident across SVC, KNN, LogisticRegression, and RandomForest models in this subgroup. Course of disease also contributed notably to the RandomForest and LogisticRegression models within the < 65 group.

In the < 50 group, the contribution of age diminished in most models: particularly in the RandomForest model of this subgroup, age’s influence receded sharply, while radiomics features (e.g., diagnostics_Image-interpolated_Mean) gained more prominence.

In the 20–35 subgroup, age was no longer a dominant factor; instead, radiomics features like original_firstorder_Kurtosis and wavelet-derived indices emerged as primary driving factors, further indicating the increasing importance of radiomics features in younger patient populations.

SHAP comparison across classifiers

Different classifiers showed varying sensitivity to feature types. KNN and LR were more influenced by linear clinical features (age, course of disease), while RF and SVC responded to both clinical and radiomics features. This suggests potential for classifier- specific applications: RF may be suited for complex, high-dimensional fusion tasks in young patients, while LR and KNN may aid rapid risk stratification in older cohorts. These findings underscore the importance of tailoring classifier selection to specific clinical scenarios.

Discussion

The main finding of this study is that the age-stratified radiomics framework revealed clear age-dependent differences in the determinants of hip-preserving versus hip-replacement surgery, with CT radiomics features progressively outweighing clinical variables in younger patients. At the conventional threshold of 65 years, clinical variables—particularly age—provided complementary value to radiomics models, and fusion models significantly outperformed radiomics-only models, consistent with prior evidence highlighting age and disease duration as key risk factors in advanced ONFH.

As the cohort was restricted to patients younger than 50 years, the predictive contribution of clinical variables markedly declined, while radiomics features became dominant. Although fusion models retained a slight performance advantage, SHAP analysis demonstrated a sharp reduction in the importance of age, indicating that texture-based radiomics features had become the principal drivers of prediction. This finding suggests that in middle-aged patients, conventional clinical indicators such as age may lose discriminative power.

In the further refined subgroups (20–35 vs. 36–50 years), age-related effects were largely eliminated, enabling amore isolated evaluation of imaging-derived heterogeneity. In these homogeneous populations, radiomics-only models matched or outperformed fusion models, and SHAP analysis confirmed minimal contributions from clinical variables. Together, this layered analysis—from broad (age < 65) to fine (age 20–35)—clarifies when and why radiomics dominates prediction and underscores the need for transparent, image-based decision support in younger ONFH patients, where traditional age-based heuristics are insufficient.

The age-dependent dominance of radiomics features may have a biological basis. In younger patients with osteonecrosis of the femoral head, bone microarchitecture and reparative capacity are relatively preserved, and disease progression is often characterized by subtle and heterogeneous microstructural alterations rather than overt collapse or extensive sclerosis. Such changes— including trabecular disruption, cystic degeneration, and localized sclerosis—are difficult to quantify using conventional clinical variables but can be captured by texture-based radiomics features derived from CT images.

In contrast, in older patients, clinical variables and advanced radiographic manifestations tend to play a more prominent role in surgical decision-making, thereby reducing the incremental value of radiomics. Although non-biological explanations such as imaging protocol variability and segmentation subjectivity cannot be fully excluded, standardized acquisition protocols, uniform lesion-oriented segmentation, and senior radiologist review were implemented to mitigate these effects. Residual confounding may persist and should be addressed in future multicenter and prospective studies.

At the feature level, several selected radiomics features demonstrated plausible biological relevance. For example, GLSZM_ZoneEntropy reflects the spatial complexity and heterogeneity of cystic and sclerotic bone marrow lesions, while GLDM_LargeDependenceHighGrayLevelEmphasis captures clusters of dense sclerotic tissue indicative of trabecular collapse. These features may reflect variations in necrotic density and fibrotic encapsulation relevant to surgical outcomes, consistent with prior studies [16, 20, 21]. Compared with MRI-based approaches that primarily capture early marrow signal changes, CT-based radiomics provides superior spatial resolution for identifying structural failure, such as trabecular rupture and subchondral collapse, reinforcing its value in late-stage ONFH. Future studies integrating histopathology and biomechanical validation are warranted to strengthen biological interpretability.

Classifier-specific SHAP analysis further revealed differential sensitivity to feature types. Random forest and support vector classifiers responded to both radiomics and clinical features, whereas k-nearest neighbors and logistic regression were more influenced by clinical variables. These findings suggest that RF may be better suited for capturing complex, nonlinear imaging patterns in younger patients, while simpler models may remain useful for rapid stratification in older populations.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be noted. First, the outcome label reflected the procedure performed, which may not represent the biologically “correct” strategy. Second, this single-center retrospective study had relatively small subgroup sizes (especially < 35 years) and lacked external validation, limiting generalizability and introducing potential bias from local practice patterns and imaging protocols. Third, the ONFH etiology was not stratified, and histopathologic correlation was unavailable. Finally, real-world integration remains untested because key contextual factors (e.g., surgeon preference, functional status, and quality-of-life measures) were not modeled, and preference-driven cases were under-represented due to our exclusion strategy. Future work should include multicenter prospective validation and multimodal imaging (e.g., CT + MRI) with clinically relevant covariates.

Conclusion

By integrating age stratification with interpretable radiomics analysis, our study clarified when and how imaging and clinical factors influence surgical choice. The results highlight the age-dependent transition from clinical to imaging dominance and underscore the value of CT-based radiomics as an interpretable, quantitative decision-support approach for modeling and supporting surgical decision-making, especially in younger patients.
