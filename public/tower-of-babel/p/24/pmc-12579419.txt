Inflammation associated microglial expansion disrupts hippocampal glial network communication, driving postoperative neurocognitive impairment
Authors: Yuxiang Zheng, Wenjie Xu, Zizheng Suo, Yinyin Qu, Lina Lin, Enze Zhang, Dengyang Han, Meikui Wu, Hongyi LI, Hui Zheng, Ting Xiao, Cheng Ni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative neurocognitive impairment (PNCI) significantly affects the recovery and long-term outcomes of elderly patients, with central nervous system (CNS) inflammation serving as the key pathogenic driver of its development. As the resident immune cells of the CNS, microglia play a crucial role in regulating perioperative inflammation and maintaining homeostasis. However, the contribution, phenotypic heterogeneity, and communication network of perioperative microglia in the development of PNCI remain insufficiently characterized.Methods18-month-old mice underwent surgery and developed PNCI. The hippocampi of mice in both control and surgery groups were dissected 24 h postoperatively for single-cell RNA sequencing. Immunofluorescence coupled with fluorescence in situ hybridization was used to verify the number of subpopulations and the expression levels of target RNA transcripts in the hippocampus. Cognitive function tests were conducted to evaluate the effects of the TNF inhibitor etanercept in PNCI model.ResultsEight microglial subpopulations and six astrocytic subpopulations were identified in the hippocampus. Postoperatively, the percentage of microglial subpopulations underwent dramatic changes, with inflammation associated microglia (IAM) increasing more than 14-fold and transition state microglia (TSM) increasing more than 33-fold. These alterations were accompanied by a marked enhancement of intercellular communications among glial cells, particularly driven by the activation of TNF signaling pathway in IAM. This pathway, along with its associated regulatory network, critically modulated the function of astrocytes and endothelial cells, thereby playing a pivotal role in CNS inflammation and the subsequent development of PNCI. Notably, administration of TNF inhibitor etanercept attenuated IAM activation and glial network communication in the hippocampus, which was associated with improved cognitive performance in PNCI mice.ConclusionThese results highlight the pivotal role of TNF signaling activation in IAM and its associated glial cell communication as the fundamental mechanism driving hippocampal CNS inflammation, which subsequently contributes to the onset and progression of PNCI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-025-03566-5.

Introduction

With the aging global population and the development of surgical techniques, an increasing number of elderly individuals undergo surgical interventions [1, 2]. Perioperative factors such as surgical trauma and anesthesia render this population particularly vulnerable to postoperative neurocognitive impairment (PNCI), a complication that hampers recovery and long-term quality of life [3, 4]. Despite its clinical importance, the pathogenesis of PNCI remains incompletely elucidated, and effective targeted treatments are lacking. The hippocampus, a key region involved in learning and memory, has been widely implicated in cognitive impairments observed after surgery [5, 6]. Our studies showed that aged mice undergoing exploratory laparotomy exhibit impairments in hippocampus-dependent associative memory, suggesting that perioperative changes in this region play a key role in PNCI pathophysiology [7].

Microglia are the primary contributors to postoperative central nervous system (CNS) inflammation and cognitive impairment [8]. Under multiple pathological conditions, excessive activation of microglia leads to the release of neurotoxic pro-inflammatory cytokines, exacerbating the inflammatory response [9]. Notably, microglia are highly heterogeneous, and the dynamic balance and transition between phenotypic states at distinct time points may critically influence the progression of neurodegenerative disorders [10]. The traditional dichotomous classification of microglia into M1 (neurotoxic and pro-inflammatory) and M2 (neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory) phenotypes no longer fully captures the complexity of microglial phenotypic heterogeneity [11]. A specific subpopulation of microglia in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) models, termed disease associated microglia (DAM), is characterized by high expression of genes related to lipid metabolism and phagocytosis [12]. These microglia may interact with neurons, astrocytes, and other immune cells, contributing to AD pathology [13, 14]. In addition, other microglial subpopulations with neuroprotective and tissue-repairing functions were also identified. These microglia exhibit transcriptional signatures indicative of anti-inflammatory activity and regenerative potential, suggesting a modulatory role in maintaining CNS homeostasis under pathological conditions [15, 16]. However, the role of specific microglial subpopulations in the perioperative hippocampus, as well as their phenotypic heterogeneity during activation, remains poorly understood. Filling these gaps will enhance the understanding of PNCI mechanisms and inform the development of therapeutic strategies.

Under physiological conditions, the blood-brain barrier (BBB) preserves CNS immune homeostasis by limiting peripheral immune infiltration [17, 18]. However, perioperative factors can compromise BBB integrity, leading to glial cell activation and neuroinflammation [19, 20]. In particular, the dynamic crosstalk between hippocampal microglia, astrocytes, and endothelial cells (ECs) played critical roles in initiating BBB damage and CNS inflammation [21–23]. Therefore, this study investigated the phenotypic heterogeneity of hippocampal microglia in the context of PNCI, and identified a distinct subpopulation closely associated with inflammation, termed inflammation associated microglia (IAM). Further analyses revealed that IAM engaged in network communication with glial cells and ECs via a TNF-mediated signaling axis. Pharmacological inhibition of TNF using etanercept effectively attenuated IAM-driven inflammation and cognitive impairment. These findings suggest that targeting hippocampal IAM may offer a promising therapeutic strategy for PNCI.

Characterization of glial and vascular cell populations in perioperative hippocampus

The hippocampi of mice in both control and surgery groups were dissected 24 h postoperatively for single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq, Fig. 1A). Sequencing yielded a total of 20,684 hippocampal cells. Following classification, 10 cell populations were identified including microglia, astrocytes, ECs, neurons, and others (Fig. 1B). Given their essential biological functions within BBB damage, CNS inflammation and homeostasis, as well as their observed transcriptional alterations, we focused on two glial cell populations and one vascular cell population: microglia (marked by C1qc, 3,814 and 4,776 cells in control and surgery groups, Fig. 1C), astrocytes (marked by Aqp4, 1,183 and 1,072 cells, Fig. 1D), and ECs (marked by Flt, 150 and 236 cells, Fig. 1E). Heatmap displayed the top 10 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each cell type (Fig. 1F). For microglia, top DEGs such as C1qc and Ccl4 are involved in the classical complement cascade and chemokine-mediated immune recruitment, contributing to microglial inflammatory activation [24], while Cx3cr1 mediates neuron-microglia signaling and synaptic maintenance. For astrocytes, DEG Slc1a2 regulates glutamate clearance to prevent excitotoxicity, Aldoc participates in glycolysis, and Plpp3 modulates phospholipid metabolism and astrocyte development [25]. Perioperative inflammation associated microglia expanded over 14-fold

The initiation of CNS inflammation is closely associated with the activation of hippocampal microglia, which serve as the resident immune cells of the brain [27]. To further investigate the heterogeneity of microglial activation in this context, a secondary clustering analysis was performed on 8,590 hippocampal microglia, identifying eight transcriptionally distinct subpopulations with divergent activation states and transcriptional profiles. Among them, 42% were homeostatic microglia (HM). 27% of microglia expressed both immediate early genes (IEGs, e.g. Fos, Jun, Egr1) and inflammatory genes (e.g. Irf1, Icam1), representing a transcriptional state intermediate between homeostasis and full activation. They were annotated as transition state microglia (TSM). One subpopulation (accounting for 12%) expressed a significant number of inflammation related genes and was defined as inflammation associated microglia (IAM), and another subpopulation (6%) expressed a set of neurodegenerative disease related genes and was defined as disease associated microglia (DAM). Three subpopulations with anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective gene expression patterns were identified as inflammation inhibitory microglia (IIM, 6%), metabolism associated inhibitory microglia (MIM, 2%), and disease associated inhibitory microglia (DIM, 1%). The remaining 4% of cells were macrophages (MΦ, Fig. 2A, B). Heatmap illustrated the top 10 DEGs in each microglial subpopulation, highlighting the distinct transcriptional profiles (Fig. 2C). Representative functional genes were selected from each subpopulation to show their specificity. In HM, DEG P2ry12 maintained microglial homeostasis and supported synaptic pruning, while Glul encoded glutamine synthetase, essential for ammonia detoxification and neurotransmitter recycling [28]. In TSM, DEG Egr1 and Jun were IEGs responsive to neuronal stimuli and immune signals, facilitating the transcription of inflammation-related pathways. In IAM, Tnf was specifically upregulated (Fig. 2D, scaled average expression: IAM = 1.99, IIM=−1.15, other subpopulations < 0.28), indicating its key role in proinflammatory signaling; Lgals3bp, another highly expressed gene, promoted microglial activation and was associated with neuroinflammatory progression [29]. In DAM, DEG Malat1 contributed to RNA metabolism and was implicated in neurodegeneration, while Plp1, encoding myelin proteolipid protein, reflected alterations in myelin integrity. The variation of microglial subpopulations between control group (preoperative period) and surgery group (postoperative period) was further analyzed. In control group, microglia were in a homeostatic state, and HM accounted for 81% of total microglia. TSM were scarcely detected (1.4%). Notably, IAM increased from preoperative 1.3% to 21% (increased by 1474%, or over 14-fold), while changes in other subpopulations were relatively insignificant (Fig. 3A). Given the significant alterations of microglial subpopulations, their functions were further explored (Fig. 3D). For HM, enrichment of terms included regulation of neurogenesis and positive regulation of cellular catabolic process. For TSM, enriched terms included myeloid leukocyte differentiation and mononuclear cell differentiation. IAM exhibited significant enrichment in regulation of inflammatory response, and cytokine-mediated signaling pathways. In contrast, IIM showed downregulation of inflammation-related pathways. DAM exhibited enrichment in autophagy and regulation of protein stability, while DIM exhibited reduced enrichment in disease-related pathways. Lastly, MIM exhibited enrichment in metabolism-associated pathways and downregulation of inflammation-related terms. In DAM, elevated Apod expression reflected lipid dysregulation and oxidative imbalance, while Irf1 was a transcriptional regulator of autophagy and immune response.

Shift in microglial gene expression patterns from HM to IAM and IIM

As shown in dot plot (Fig. 4A), the expression of homeostatic genes (Fcrls, P2ry12, etc.) was higher in HM and TSM (p < 0.0001). Gene co-expression network analysis was employed to identify key regulatory genes responsible for the functions of HM, TSM, IAM and IIM. Modules with the highest number of co-expressed genes and hub genes were identified as the representatives for these subpopulations (Fig. 4C). HM hub genes P2ry12, Itm2b and Sparc were involved in maintaining microglial persistence and homeostatic function [28, 30]. Co-expressed genes, such as Cx3cr1 and Picalm, were also involved in the regulation of neurogenesis [31, 32]. The hub genes Jun and Ier5 identified in the TSM module were associated with rapid responses to stimuli, inflammatory responses, and CNS functions, particularly those involved in neuronal plasticity [33]. Co-expressed genes, such as Cebpb and Hhex, played roles in the development, differentiation, and proliferation of microglia [34]. IAM hub gene Ddx5 influenced the inflammation balance by inhibiting IL-10 secretion [35]. Junb and Egr1 promoted the production of various pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby facilitating the propagation of CNS inflammation [36]. Numerous co-expressed inflammatory genes (e.g., Nfkbia, Nlrp3, Tnfrsf1a) defined the proinflammatory signature of IAM, shaping the postoperative hippocampal inflammatory milieu. IIM hub gene Tyrobp interacted with immune receptors and was linked to AD development [37]. The downregulation of Tyrobp in IIM, along with complement system genes C1qa and C1qb, suggested neuroprotective effects. Analysis of the four co-expression modules revealed enriched terms that characterize the perioperative functional reprogramming of microglia, suggesting a gradual shift from a homeostatic state to an inflammation-related state.

TNF signaling activation in postoperative hippocampal IAM

Based on the perioperative changes in microglial subpopulations, microglial activation-related signaling pathways were further established (Fig. 5A). TSM, originating from HM, could transform into M1 microglia via IEG pathway (Egr1, Jun and Fos) or into M2 microglia via TGF-β pathway and related mechanisms. In IAM, both Tnf and its receptor Tnfrsf1a were upregulated. As a central regulator of immune responses, Tnf contributed to the pathogenesis of various diseases, such as sepsis and multiple sclerosis, by triggering pro-inflammatory signaling cascades and promoting programmed cell death [38]. The inflammatory cytokine Il1b was upregulated in IAM, possibly via TNF and NF-κB pathway, leading to a pro-inflammatory reaction. Ccl2 and Tspo were upregulated in IAM, while Hk2 and Prkcd were elevated in DAM. These genes were associated with immune signaling, mitochondrial activity, and metabolic processes [39, 40]. In IIM and DIM subpopulations, genes such as Nlrp3, Il1b, and Casp8 were downregulated, indicating suppressed inflammasome and apoptosis-related pathways. However, the proportional changes of these regulatory subpopulations were relatively limited. A significant increase of Tnf + IBA1 + cells (IAM) in the hippocampus of surgery group was confirmed by IF/FISH double staining (p = 0.0074, Fig. 5B). As inflammatory marker genes of IAM, the fluorescence intensities of Tnf, Tnfrsf1a, and Il1b mRNA were significantly elevated in hippocampal microglia of surgery group. As chemokine and mitochondrial marker genes of IAM, the fluorescence intensities of Ccl2 and Tspo mRNA were also elevated in hippocampal microglia of surgery group (Fig. 5C). Perioperative astrocytic subpopulations remained relatively stable

Microglia have been shown to influence astrocytic function through the release of specific cytokines or direct cell-cell interactions [41]. Given the role of astrocytes in maintaining CNS homeostasis and coordinating inflammatory responses, we next explored astrocytic heterogeneity in the perioperative hippocampus. Clustering analysis identified six distinct astrocytic subpopulations (Fig. 6A, B). 36% were homeostatic astrocytes (HA). A small subpopulation (accounting for 3%) expressed genes related to brain blood flow regulation and was defined as angiogenesis associated astrocytes (AAA). Another small subpopulation (accounting for 4%) expressed a significant number of metabolism related genes and was defined as metabolism associated astrocytes (MAA). 32% expressed both neurodegeneration and metabolism related genes and were defined as disease and metabolism associated astrocytes (DMA). The remaining two clusters exhibited neuroprotective functions and were defined as disease associated inhibitory astrocytes (DIA, 9%) and metabolism associated inhibitory astrocytes (MIA, 16%). Heatmap illustrated the top 10 DEGs (Fig. 6C), and dot plot showed the representative functional genes of each subpopulation (Fig. 6D). In HA, DEG Htra1 encoded a serine protease that modulated extracellular matrix remodeling and TGF-β signaling, potentially affecting astrocyte reactivity, while Hacd2 was involved in very long-chain fatty acid elongation, supporting membrane lipid balance [42]. In AAA, DEG Agt played a central role in the renin-angiotensin system, influencing cerebral perfusion and vascular tone [43]. In MAA and DMA, DEG Dlk1 functioned in the regulation of adipocyte differentiation and neurodevelopment, and Aldoc, a glycolytic enzyme, contributed to astrocytic energy metabolism and was involved in maintaining redox homeostasis under stress conditions [44]. In DIA and MIA, the top DEGs included Kif5a, Son, and others. IAM dominated postoperative glial network communication

Given the roles of microglia and astrocytes in amplifying or modulating neuroinflammation [45], the communications between glial cells and ECs after surgery were explored. CellChat analysis showed increased intercellular communications after surgery, with significantly enhanced signaling from microglia to microglia, astrocytes to astrocytes, and astrocytes to microglia. Their interaction numbers increased by 8.5%, 26.8%, and 84.0%, and interaction strengths rose by 30.6%, 15.6%, and 17.5%, respectively (Fig. 8A). In microglial subpopulations, IAM and TSM exhibited the highest total differential outgoing and incoming interaction numbers (IAM = 91, TSM = 85) and interaction strengths (both IAM and TSM = 2.7). In astrocytic subpopulations, HA and MAA exhibited the highest total differential outgoing and incoming interaction numbers (HA = 135, MAA = 111) and strengths (HA = 4.4, MAA = 4.6). Additionally, ECs also exhibited increased differential interaction numbers (60) and strengths (2.4, Fig. 8B, C). Given the perioperative changes of microglia, the signaling pathway alterations between control and surgery groups of microglia were further compared. Stacked bar plots (Fig. 8D) showed that the majority of signaling pathways were upregulated in surgery group. Notably, the signaling pathways related to extracellular matrix interaction (LAMININ), immune regulation (CD22, CD25), and vascular remodeling (ANGPTL) exhibited increased activity. This upregulation was observed in both signal-emitting and signal-receiving cells, indicating a bidirectional amplification of glial cell communication. The major signaling pathways upregulated in microglia of surgery group were further analyzed. For TNF signaling pathway, chord diagram (Fig. 9A) showed that the interaction signals existed exclusively between microglial subpopulations. In control group, several subpopulations including HM, TSM, and IAM participated with moderate signaling activity. However, after surgery, IAM became the principal regulator of TNF signaling, functioning as the primary source of TNF ligands, the main recipient of TNF-related signals, and a key intermediary in signal propagation (red box, Fig. 9B). This shift suggested a central role of IAM in postoperative inflammatory response. Compared to control group, Tnf-Tnfrsf1a ligand-receptor (L-R) pair communication increased significantly in the IAM of surgery group (red box, Fig. 9C). Microglia-drived Tnf interacted with its receptor Tnfrsf1a, mediating various biological processes, including neuroinflammation and BBB damage [46, 47]. For BAFF pathway, chord diagram (Fig. 9D) showed that in control group, IAM primarily served as signaling initiators. After surgery, IAM displayed enhanced involvement by simultaneously producing BAFF ligands, responding to BAFF-related cues, and participating in signal relay within the microglial network (Fig. 9E). Compared to control group, Tnfrsf13b-Tnfrsf13b L-R pair communication increased in the IAM-dominated subpopulations of surgery group (red box, Fig. 9F), suggesting the involvement of other TNF-related signaling in IAM crosstalk. CHEMERIN is an adipokine with immunomodulatory properties. For the CHEMERIN pathway (Fig. 9G-H), astrocytes (HA, AAA, and MAA) acted as major signal emitters in control group, primarily targeting HM and TSM. Discussion

In this study, a substantial expansion of hippocampal IAM was observed in PNCI mice, with 14-fold increase in IAM and 33-fold increase in transitional microglia preceding inflammatory activation. Transcriptomic analyses revealed marked upregulation of TNF signaling within IAMs, suggesting its potential role in postoperative hippocampal inflammation. These findings were further supported by fluorescent labeling. In contrast, astrocytic activation appeared relatively limited. Communication analysis showed bidirectional interplays between microglia-astrocytes and microglia-ECs, mediated by TNF, CSF1, ICAM1 and associated signaling. Notably, TNF inhibitor ameliorated aberrant changes in key genes of glial communication and improved PNCI, highlighting the pivotal role of TNF-mediated signaling in initiating glial network communication and inflammatory responses, and suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy for PNCI.

Known as the first responders to CNS inflammation, microglia exhibit significant heterogeneity [50, 51]. In neurodegenerative disease models, various microglial subpopulations with distinct functions have been identified, including DAM [12] and terminally inflammatory microglia (TIM) [52] in AD, IFN-I-responsive microglia (IRMs) [16] in the partial whisker deprivation model, and lipid-droplet-accumulating microglia (LDAM) [53] in systemic inflammation models. This study addressed the issue in perioperative setting. Homeostatic microglia were activated and transitioned into distinct subpopulations, including IAM, which exhibited a more than 14-fold increase following surgery. TSM, transcriptionally distinct from traditional categories, were identified with a gene expression profile intermediate between homeostatic and activated states. This subpopulation was characterized by high expression of IEGs and enrichment in functions related to cellular differentiation. Notably, TSM were sparse in control group but increased over 33-fold after surgery, suggesting the microglial responses to local environmental changes. This phenomenon mirrored intermediate phenotypes previously observed in AD and aging models [15, 54]. Despite their gene signatures suggestive of distinct biological functions, these microglial subpopulations could broadly be categorized into two main functional groups: pro-inflammatory M1-like microglia, including IAM and DAM, and anti-inflammatory M2-like microglia, including IIM, MIM and DIM. This classification provided a clearer framework for understanding microglial dynamics and their dual roles in PNCI.

Among microglial subpopulations, the greatest number of DEGs between control and surgery groups were observed within IAM and MIM. IAM exhibited both substantial expansion (over 14-fold) and strong upregulation of pro-inflammatory genes such as Tnf, Il1b, and Ccl2, while MIM exhibited the highest numerical DEG. Astrocytes and ECs remained relatively stable in both abundance and overall transcriptomic changes, though key genes such as Icam1 and Ifitm3 were upregulated in surgery group. These findings showed distinct transcriptomic alterations and functional signatures at subpopulation level of microglia. Thus, dissecting cell heterogeneity at the subpopulation level could be critical to uncovering key cellular contributors to PNCI.

Hippocampal IAM and its TNF signaling played a vital role in glial network communication. TNF-mediated pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, enhanced chemotaxis, and mitochondrial dysfunction drove IAM to exacerbate hippocampal inflammation. Chemokine Ccl2 (upregulated in IAM) recruited leukocytes through damaged BBB. Mitochondrial membrane protein Tspo (upregulated in IAM) affected the activity of mitochondrial permeability transition pore, resulting in mitochondria dysfunction and increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [55]. Distinct from the chronic activation observed in neurodegenerative diseases such as AD, microglial activation during the perioperative period was transient and intense [56], and IAM increase was observed 24 h after surgery. The acute microglial activation observed at this time point could also initiate a prolonged neuroinflammation and progressive neuronal dysfunction, contributing to long-term cognitive impairment. Additionally, TNF-driven inflammation induces an excessive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and ROS, which causes neuronal injury through apoptotic and necrotic pathways. The resulting neuronal injury triggers the release of damage-associated molecular patterns, which amplifies microglial activation and perpetuates a self-sustaining inflammatory loop, exacerbating neurodegeneration and cognitive dysfunction [57, 58].

Microglia, astrocytes, and ECs together constitute the glial-vascular unit (GVU), which plays an indispensable role in regulating blood-brain barrier (BBB) function and CNS homeostasis during the progression of neurodegenerative diseases [59–61]. Within GVU, microglia and astrocytes maintain close spatial proximity to cerebral vasculature [62]. This study showed that the postoperative expansion of IAM was associated with enhanced communication with astrocytes and ECs. IAM induces a reactive astrocyte phenotype, further shaping the inflammatory microenvironment [41]. Bidirectional interactions between microglia and astrocytes, along with endothelial dysfunction, form a pathological loop that sustains neuroinflammation [63–65]. ECs respond secondary to postoperative IAM-derived inflammatory signals. Icam1 contributes to endothelial inflammation and, in combination with TJ disruption, exacerbated barrier dysfunction [66]. Icam1, along with chemokine Ccl2 derived from activated microglia, facilitates the recruitment of peripheral leukocytes into CNS, further amplifying neuroinflammation [67]. Ifitm3 is implicated in promoting endothelial amyloid-β deposition through enhanced γ-secretase activity, potentially triggering endothelial inflammation and apoptosis [68]. These findings suggested that IAM-driven GVU dysfunction played a pivotal role in amplifying postoperative CNS inflammation and vulnerability.

Controlling microglial activation and mitigating CNS inflammation have been identified as promising therapeutic strategies for AD 15, ischemic stroke [69], and traumatic brain injury [70]. Our previous study showed the role of disrupted glia-neuron interaction in CNS inflammation and PNCI, indicating the therapeutic potential of targeted dexmedetomidine intervention [7]. Interventions such as CSF1R inhibitors, NADPH oxidase inhibitors, and clinically used glucocorticoids have been proposed to ameliorate postoperative neuroinflammation and PNCI [71], but their broad mechanistic targets have resulted in highly heterogeneous therapeutic outcomes. Microglia replacement therapies have shown promising results in the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases [72, 73], and these approaches have been linked to potential toxicities or adverse effects, such as cytokine storms and astrocytic gliosis [74, 75]. These complications could result from the non-selective depletion of microglia, disrupting CNS homeostasis or microglia polarization. This study showed the role of postoperative hippocampal glial network communication in PNCI, and highlighted the therapeutic potential of IAM inhibition through TNF signaling intervention. Clinical trials also showed the safety, tolerability, and potential cognitive benefits of TNF inhibitors in AD patients [76]. However, this intervention remains off-label, and the use of TNF inhibitors in PNCI and neurodegenerative disease patients still requires further investigation.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides a single-cell atlas of the hippocampal glial and vascular cell populations in PNCI mice, revealing eight microglial subpopulations. Among them, expanded IAM exhibits enhanced network communication with glial cells and ECs, contributing to hippocampal inflammation through TNF-mediated signaling. Furthermore, TNF inhibition attenuates postoperative microglial activation and cognitive impairment. These findings highlight hippocampal IAM as a critical pathogenic driver and promising therapeutic target for the prevention and treatment of PNCI.

Methods

Animals

Animal experiments in this study were carried out in conformity with approved ethical protocols and guidelines from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Cancer Hospital Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (No. NCC2021A040). Male C57BL/6 mice, 18-month-old, weighing between 25 and 34 g were used. The mice were housed in cages and maintained on a standard housing condition with food and water ad libitum for 2 weeks.

Study protocol

In the scRNA-seq experiment, mice were randomly assigned to two groups: control group and surgery group (n = 3 per group). The mice in surgery group received anesthesia and laparotomy, while control group received 50% oxygen for 30 min. All mice were sacrificed by decapitation 24 h after procedure. The brain tissue was rapidly removed, and the hippocampi were dissected out and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Postoperative 24 h was selected as the sampling time point because it was both the key period for memory formation during behavior test including MWM and fear conditioning, and the period when major pathophysiological changes are most pronounced [77, 78].

In the following intervention experiment, mice were divided into four groups: control, surgery, surgery + ETN, and ETN. In control group, mice received subcutaneous injections of normal saline (6.7 ml/kg) 36 and 12 h prior to oxygen exposure. In surgery group, mice received normal saline (6.7 ml/kg) 36 and 12 h prior to the surgical procedure. In surgery + ETN group, mice received subcutaneous injections of etanercept (ETN, 6.7 mg/kg, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) 36 and 12 h prior to the surgical procedure. In ETN group, mice received etanercept (6.7 mg/kg) 36 and 12 h prior to oxygen exposure.

Surgery and anesthesia

The mice in surgery groups received 2.5% sevoflurane in 50% oxygen for 30 min through breathing masks, and control group received 50% oxygen for 30 min. The mice breathed spontaneously, and the sevoflurane concentration was monitored continuously with an anesthetic monitor (Datex, Tewksbury, MA, USA). The surgical procedure (exploratory laparotomy) was modified from our previous studies [79]. A longitudinal midline incision was made from xiphoid to 0.5 cm proximal pubic symphysis on the skin. The abdominal muscles and peritoneum, then approximately 10 cm of the intestine were exteriorized. The bowel loops remained outside the abdominal cavity for 1 min and then replaced into the abdominal cavity. The incision was finally sutured layer by layer with 5 − 0 Vicryl thread. After surgery, 2.5% lidocaine cream was applied topically to the incision site for analgesia. The rectal temperature was maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C using a heating pad, and this surgical protocol has been shown not to significantly alter values of blood pressure and SpO2 in the preliminary studies. Following anesthesia, mice were transferred to a recovery chamber with 50% oxygen until 10 min after regaining consciousness, then returned to their home cages with free access to food and water.

Tissue dissociation for single-cell RNA sequencing

One day after the surgery, mice were euthanized, the brains were harvested, and the bilateral hippocampi were dissected on ice. Hippocampal cells were dissociated using the Adult Brain Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The tissue was enzymatically digested, filtered through a MACS SmartStrainer, and centrifuged to obtain a cell suspension. To remove blood contamination and residual erythrocytes, a Red Blood Cell Lysis Buffer was applied according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A Debris Removal Solution was applied to improve cell quality, and the final suspension was prepared for downstream scRNA-seq.

Single cell barcoding and library Preparation

scRNA-seq libraries were prepared using the Single Cell 3’ Reagent Kits v2 (10x Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Single cells were encapsulated with reagents and uniquely barcoded beads within oil droplets to form Gel Beads in Emulsion (GEMs). Reverse transcription was conducted within GEMs to generate cDNA, each tagged with a cell-specific barcode. After GEM disruption, the cDNA was pooled, amplified, and processed for library construction, enabling downstream sequencing.

ScRNA-sequencing and data pre-processing

The library concentration and fragment length were evaluated using Qubit Fluorometric Quantitation (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tustin, CA, USA) and Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA), respectively. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq2500 following 10x Genomics’ guidelines. Quality control ensured > 90% cell viability, 2 nM library concentration (qPCR), and removal of low-quality reads, adapters, and reads with > 10% unknown bases. Over 95% alignment rates to the reference genome were achieved, and low-quality cells (e.g., empty droplets, doublets) were filtered based on unique gene counts and mitochondrial gene content. Data processing utilized Seurat v4.0.4 in R (4.3.1). Cells with a minimum of 200 features and 3 cells per gene were retained. Data normalization, selection of 2000 highly variable features, and scaling were performed. Principal component analysis (PCA) identified 20 significant components, which were used for clustering via Seurat’s FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions. Visualization was achieved through t-SNE for dimensionality reduction. Microglia and astrocytes were further analyzed by secondary clustering meeting the same filtering criteria. PCA was performed with 12 principal components, and clustering used resolutions of 0.25 for microglia and 0.4 for astrocytes.

Cell type classification and DEGs identification

The expression levels of cell type-specific markers across clusters were analyzed, and distinct populations were revealed including neurons, microglia, astrocytes, etc. Marker genes for major hippocampal cell types were identified based on previously published references [80–82]. For microglial and astrocytic subpopulations, the names were assigned primarily based on the functional roles of the highly expressed marker genes identified within each cluster and the recent advances in the field. Clusters with similar gene expression patterns and GO enrichment profiles were merged into unified subpopulations to reflect biologically relevant cellular states. Clusters with distinct gene expression patterns were retained as individual subpopulations to preserve their potential biological relevance. For example, in astrocytic subpopulations, Cluster 4 expressed genes related to metabolic processes (MAA), including the top 2 DEGs Hmgcs2, Etnppl, and Agpat5. Cluster 5 expressed angiogenesis-related genes (AAA), notably Agt, the top1 DEG, a core regulator of vascular function. After quality control preprocessing and classification of cellular identities, DEG analysis was performed using FindMarkers function in Seurat to find marker genes of each cluster. The software generated averaged log2 (Fold Change) of gene expression, the percentage of cells expressing the genes in each group (pct.1 and pct.2), P value, and adjusted P value using a Wilcoxon method. Min.pct = 0.25 and logFC threshold = 0.25 were used in finding DEGs.

Heatmap, violin plots, Dot plots, and GO analysis

All DEGs in specific cell types were used as background. Seurat and ggplot2 (3.5.0) packages in R were used to produce heatmaps, violin plots, and dot plots. Org.Mm.eg.db (3.20.0), clusterProfiler (4.14.4), and ggplot2 packages in R were used to produce Lollipop plot of GO terms.

Pseudotime analysis and cell-cell gene co-expression analysis

Monocle package (2.30.0) in R was used to analyze and visualize the differentiation of microglia subclasses from homeostatic to inflammation-related activation states, based on previous classifications and subclass-specific DEGs. MEGENA package (1.3.7) in R was used to analyze gene co-expression in microglia subtypes. Next, Cytoscape (3.10.3) was used to visualize the results. One of the most representative GO terms was selected for each of the four types of microglia. The genes contained in this term and the overlapping genes in co-expression were connected with black dotted lines.

Cell-cell communication analysis

Cell-cell communication networks were inferred using the CellChat R package (1.6.1). The interactions were analyzed among all microglial subpopulations, astrocytic subpopulations, and ECs based on ligand-receptor expression profiles. The CellChatDB.mouse database was used, including the categories of “Secreted Signaling”, “Cell-Cell Contact”, and “ECM-Receptor” interactions. Gene expression matrices were normalized using Seurat, and cell labels derived from clustering analysis were used as cell identities in CellChat. We constructed communication networks separately for control and surgery groups. Communication probability was computed using the computeCommunProb and computeCommunProbPathway functions, and the interactions with p < 0.05 were retained. Differential analysis between groups was then performed using compareInteractions() and rankNet() functions to identify upregulated and downregulated signaling pathways. The strength and pattern of signaling changes were visualized using plots generated by CellChat’s built-in functions.

Immunofluorescence (IF) coupled fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assay

Freshly collected brain tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 10 μm using a sliding microtome (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tustin, CA, USA). Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized, followed by antigen retrieval using microwave heating in a citric acid-based solution (Elabscience, Wuhan, China). Sections were blocked with 5% goat serum (Elabscience, Wuhan, China) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including IBA1 (1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), GFAP (1:200, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and CD31 (1:200, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). After washing, sections were incubated with goat anti-rabbit CY3-conjugated secondary antibody (1:50, Elabscience, Wuhan, China). FISH was performed using a Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization Kit for RNA (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Sections underwent acetylation and hybridization with pre-incubated probes (SAINTBIO, Beijing, China) at 37 °C for 12 h. Post-hybridization washes were completed, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Sections were mounted with anti-fade medium and imaged using a Nikon Eclipse Ti confocal fluorescence microscope. For imaging analysis, one field per mouse was randomly selected under high magnification from anatomically defined hippocampal Cornu Ammonis 1 (CA1) or dentate gyrus (DG) regions. For target gene quantification, FIJI software was used to define cell-type-specific ROIs (e.g., IBA1+, GFAP+, CD31 + areas) and measure the fluorescence intensity of target markers within these ROIs after background subtraction. Quantified intensities were normalized to the control group average to calculate fold change. The data, including vendor, catalog number, dilution ratio, and probe sequences, are presented in Table S1 and S2.

Transmission electron microscopy

The brains were harvested 24 h after surgery. Tissue blocks (1 mm³) from the DG region of the hippocampus were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide. Tissues were then dehydrated through a graded acetone series, embedded in epoxy resin, and sectioned into ultrathin slices (~ 70 nm) using an ultramicrotome. The sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Ultrastructural changes in ECs, BM, TJ, and perivascular microglia were examined using a JEM-1400 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Hippocampal tissues were collected 24 h post-surgery and homogenized in cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) using a tissue grinder. The homogenates were then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for analysis. TNF-α levels in the mouse hippocampus were quantified using ELISA kits (Elabscience, Wuhan, China). All samples were assayed in duplicate, and the average optical density (OD) value from each pair of wells was used for analysis. Samples were incubated in wells pre-coated with a capture antibody, followed by addition of biotinylated detection antibody and Avidin-HRP conjugate. A substrate solution was then added, and color development was terminated with stop solution. The optical density was measured spectrophotometrically. TNF-α concentrations (pg/ml) were determined based on a standard curve generated using recombinant standards provided in the kit.

Morris water maze and fear conditioning test

The FCT (Xeye CPP, MacroAmbition S&T Development, Beijing, China) was used to assess the cognitive function of mice after surgery as described in previous studies [83]. The training was performed at 3 h post-surgery, and the tests were conducted on postoperative days 2 and 7. During training, mice were placed in a conditioning chamber with black-and-white striped walls for 180 s to acclimate, followed by a 60-second 2 Hz pulsed tone co-terminated with a 0.8 mA foot shock. To assess hippocampus-dependent contextual memory, mice were returned to the same chamber, and freezing behavior was recorded for 180 s. For hippocampus-independent cued memory, mice were placed in a modified chamber with altered cues, and after 180 s of free exploration, the same auditory cue was presented for 60 s, followed by freezing assessment. However, since the training was conducted at 3 h post-surgery, residual effects of anesthesia or postoperative pain could still be present and potentially influence behavioral performance, representing a limitation of this approach.

The MWM test (Sunny Instruments Co. Ltd., Beijing, China) was used to assess the spatial learning and memory. The protocol consisted of two phases: a training phase (place navigation test) and a probe test. In the training phase, the mice were placed in one quadrant facing the wall of the maze and allowed to explore for the hidden platform for 90 s in each trial (four trials per day with an intertrial interval of 10 min). The time to locate the submerged plat form was recorded (escape latency). If the platform was not found within 90 s, the mice were guided to the platform, where they stayed for 30 s. Mice underwent daily testing in the water maze from day 1 to day 5 after surgery. On postoperative day 6, the submerged platform was removed from the water maze and a spatial probe test was performed for 90 s to assess the spatial memory. The swimming speed, escape latency, times of platform crossing, and the time spent in target quadrant were recorded by a video camera.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 7.0 software (GraphPad software, La Jolla, CA, USA) was used to analyze the data. Data were presented as means ± SEM. Based on the preliminary data, assuming a two-sided Student’s t-test, samples of six for biochemistry studies, and 12 for FCT and MWM test would lead to 90% power and 5% significance. Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests were applied for comparisons between two groups. Two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test was used for comparisons in ETN intervention experiment (Control, Surgery, Surgery + ETN, and Control + ETN groups) when data were normally distributed. Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post hoc test was used for analyzing escape latency in the MWM across multiple time points. The normality of data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (p > 0.1 was considered indicative of a normal distribution). The value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
