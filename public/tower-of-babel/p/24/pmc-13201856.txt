Transformer augmented hybrid deep learning for explainable multi class pest classification
Authors: Vivek Kumar Verma, Ashish Kumar, Varda Pareek, Yogesh Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Agricultural pests continue to impose serious threats to global food security by causing major yield losses across diverse cropping systems, making early and accurate identification vital for effective pest management. With the growing integration of digital technologies in modern agriculture, deep-learning–based pest recognition has become a promising approach to surpass the limitations of manual scouting and conventional monitoring practices. This work presents a comprehensive experimental evaluation of multiple deep-learning architectures for multi-class pest classification via image-level classification covering 19 pest species. The study investigates classical CNNs (MobileNetV2, VGG16), compound-scaled models (EfficientNetB0/B3, EfficientNetV2-B0), residual architectures (ResNet50), automated NAS models (Xception, NASNetLarge), and novel hybrid CNN–Transformer designs including Hybrid InceptionResNetV2, Hybrid ResNet50 + CBAM, Hybrid EffNet-Transformer, and Hybrid EfficientNetV2-S + Transformer. To enhance foreground isolation and reduce background complexity in field images, segmentation-driven preprocessing is employed using GrabCut, Watershed, SLIC, and Felzenszwalb, generating structure-refined image representations for downstream classification. Results show that attention-augmented hybrid models consistently outperform standalone CNNs, with the Hybrid EfficientNetV2-S + Transformer achieving the highest performance with 0.8800 validation accuracy, 0.849 macro-F1, and 0.4560 validation loss. These findings highlight the effectiveness of combining convolutional feature hierarchies with global self-attention for reliable multi-species pest classification and offer meaningful guidance for developing intelligent precision agriculture systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41248-1.

Introduction

Pests are one of the main factors that endanger agriculture all over the planet and they cause such losses that they negatively influence food security and economies on the global scale. According to the forecasts for the years 2024–2025, pests alone will account for about 20–40% of the total annual crop yield that will be lost, which would mean that these pests have an economic impact of $220 billion to $290 billion every year on the global economy1. The huge loss of yield here consists of the damage that insects, weeds, and plant diseases have done, with insect pests being solely responsible for about 38% of these lost crops. The losses might be even bigger if there were no crop protection in place, as some major food crops could suffer losses of up to 70%. Besides, the damage done to crops has a dual effect on the market - less food available and hence its price goes up, and at the same time, it becomes harder for farmers to survive because of the high production costs2. The agricultural pest control market is expanding rapidly, with a value of over $8.5 billion and a future growth expectation due to the rise in food demand and the increasing pest problems caused by climate change. The size of the market signifies an urgent need for pest management through improved and sustainable methods3. In any case, traditional pest control methods, particularly those that use chemical pesticides, have the disadvantages of pest resistance, pollution, and the destruction of non-target beneficial species. Consequently, the practice of integrated pest management and the development of biopesticides that are less harmful to the environment have gained even more popularity4.

Earlier, pest control methods were mainly based on manual inspection, followed by the extensive spraying of chemical pesticides that took over large areas. Although these methods have been around for decades and are still widely used, they have many drawbacks. Some of the issues caused by these methods are late classification of pests, air quality deterioration, decreased pesticide efficacy, and accidental killing of beneficial insects. Besides, climate change and pest migration have made it even harder to achieve sustainable pest control5. Still, in the recent past, AI has been one of the promising solutions to this problem. The pest classification systems powered by AI are using computer vision, deep learning and advanced sensor technologies, to locate the pests quicker, at a higher level of accuracy and in real-time6. The presence of large datasets and the deployment of powerful CNN algorithms often enable these systems to attain precision levels that are significantly superior to manual inspection. The benefits of using automated classification along with constant monitoring are the implementation of targeted treatments which results in reduced chemical use and minimal adverse environmental impact. In addition, the partnership of drones, satellite imagery and IoT sensors provides new insights on pest locations, populations and their interactions with crops, thus making pest management decisions eco-friendlier and more informed7.

In the recent years, AI technology has emerged as a promising approach to address these limitations. These AI-centric pest classification units implement various methods such as computer vision, deep learning and sensor technology. This makes the process of classification less complex and continuous. Next, the system is working on a huge volume of data and a very complex algorithm such as Convolutional Neural Networks that can attain precision in the range of 85 to 95%, which is way higher compared to manual processes8. Another advantage of this technology is that it automatically recognizes and instantly monitors the appeasement of the pest towards precision pest management in the infested areas. This reduces the volume of chemical used, thus saving the pest from contamination. Combining drone and satellite analysis for crop monitoring along with IoT sensor real-time data would create a foundation for the sustainable pest surveillance in terms of the pest population and the environment. The development of a balanced dataset; the application of advanced image segmentation techniques; and optimization must come followed by the changing of the metaheuristic algorithms that will be enhancing the accuracy and adaptability of the classification to the different practical agricultural environments9. By the integration of real-time variables of soil moisture and crop pans, AI can provide very accurate pest control management through direct intervention and, thus, deterring high levels of damage. The use of AI has brought a change in the direction of pest control to a more sustainable and efficient future that is also environmentally responsible and promotes the health of both the ecosystem and the crop. This approach offers potential benefits for agricultural applications. Hence the aim of the paper is to identify the most effective deep-learning architecture for multi-class pest recognition by evaluating CNNs, modern compound-scaled networks, automated NAS-based models, and hybrid CNN–Transformer designs on a large field-based pest dataset.

Related work

Recent advances in computer vision have led to significant progress in automated pest classification, with many studies exploring deep learning models for classification, classification, and field-level monitoring (Table 1).

Pest-YOLO by Wen et al. (2022)10 achieved a mean-AP of 69.59% and mRecall of 77.71% when evaluated on the Pest24 benchmark (approximately 20k images, around 190k annotations). Qi et al. (2023)11 improved Pest-YOLO by enhancing classification accuracy (mAP increased from 71.5% to 74.1%) along with increased throughput (79.4 → 104.2 FPS) and decreased FLOPs/model size in their experiments. The Forestry Pest Dataset assembled by Liu et al. (2022)12 provided a massive and carefully assembled set of pest images in the order of several tens of thousands with well-established class divisions, and although the problem of class imbalance persists, it has turned into a frequently applied reference point for classification research. The Hybrid CNN and Transformer-based classifiers which were introduced by Peng & Wang (2022)13 showed to be better in terms of performance on IP102 and related datasets. Moreover, the authors had reported classification accuracies varying from 74.9% to 75.6% depending on the different resolutions of inputs. By their results, the necessity of the Transformer heads to capture the global context cues, which were very important for fine-grained recognition of pests, has been proved. Karar et al.14 managed to accomplish in-field accuracy of the highest level for certain pest targets, e.g., red palm weevil classification. Guan et al. (2023)15 found an optimal combination of the model size, the speed of inference, and the accuracy at the field level. Dise-Efficient had shown that it is able to perform well on the PlantVillage dataset, which was designed for controlled experiments, and reach only moderate accuracy on the much more demanding IP102 dataset after applying transfer learning. LLA-R-CNN (2024)16, which was based on a MobileNetV3 backbone and is equipped with locality and channel attention modules, has claimed to have very good performances versus standard detectors yet to consume significantly less FLOPs and total model size. Meanwhile, GBiDC-PEST, the YOLO-based mobile-friendly method that includes GhostNet, BiFPN, and CBAM, possesses a very compact model with an efficient real-time performance and is highly suitable for mobile use. Furthermore, Yuan et al.’s ESA-ResNet34 (2024)17 and Venkateswara and Padmanabhan (2025)18 made new contributions to the field of attention-enhanced classification models and augmentation-driven improvements on the IP102 dataset, respectively.

Despite substantial progress in deep-learning–based pest recognition, the existing literature consistently identifies several open challenges that hinder reliable deployment in real agricultural environments. A key issue is robustness to real-field variability, as pest images captured under natural conditions exhibit significant variation in illumination, background clutter, pest pose, scale, and life stage, often accompanied by complex foliage textures, shadows, partial occlusions, and color similarity between pests and host plants, all of which introduce noise and reduce feature consistency. Closely related to this is the challenge of fine-grained discrimination among visually similar pest species, where subtle morphological differences such as wing venation, body segmentation, or minor color gradients must be distinguished despite high intra-class variability and limited visual prominence when pests occupy small or partially obscured regions. In parallel, the literature reflects an unresolved trade-off between classification accuracy and computational efficiency: lightweight and mobile-oriented models are well suited for real-time and edge deployment but frequently lack sufficient representational capacity for complex field imagery, whereas larger and more expressive architectures improve recognition performance at the cost of increased computational demand, memory usage, and susceptibility to overfitting. Furthermore, many existing approaches rely predominantly on local receptive fields, resulting in limited modeling of global contextual information, even though spatial relationships between pests and surrounding plant structures often provide critical cues for accurate identification when local features are ambiguous. Finally, most prior studies evaluate narrowly focused model families or task-specific optimizations, offering limited insight into how architectural design choices such as depth, width, scaling strategies, and attention integration interact under unified experimental settings, thereby restricting consensus on the most effective principles for robust, scalable, and generalizable multi-class pest classification in realistic agricultural scenarios10–18.

Dataset description

The research employed a well-structured multi-class pest image dataset consisting of 34,200 images, comprising 28,500 images for training and 2,850 images each for validation and testing. The dataset is sourced from a publicly available Kaggle repository (PestAI Dataset v2) and contains real field-acquired images representing 19 significant agricultural pest species, including aphids, whiteflies, mites, thrips, beetles, weevils, cutworms, cabbage loopers, and various hemipteran and lepidopteran insects (Fig. 2). Each pest category contains a total of 1,800 images, with approximately 1,440 images allocated to the training set and around 180 images each assigned to the validation and test sets19.

The images were captured under real agricultural field conditions using heterogeneous consumer-grade imaging devices, resulting in substantial variation in background clutter, illumination, pose, and pest life stages. While the dataset documentation confirms real-field acquisition across multiple crop types and environments, detailed metadata such as exact geographic location, capture time span, crop variety, or camera specifications are not consistently provided. Prior to model training, the dataset was inspected for exact duplicate samples using filename consistency and hash-based comparisons, and no identical images were retained across the training, validation, and test splits. The dataset was partitioned using random stratified sampling at the image level to preserve balanced class distributions across all splits. Since the dataset lacks location and capture-session details, some visually similar images from the same plant or scene may appear in both the training and test sets. This can lead to a potential data leakage issue, which is commonly observed in open field-image datasets. As a result, the results indicate image-level generalization rather than generalization across different fields or times. Nevertheless, the dataset still offers a realistic and challenging setting for assessing how effectively deep-learning models can separate similar pest species and operate under practical agricultural conditions.

Data segmentation

After performing the preprocessing techniques on image data, it undergoes various segmentation techniques20–23. All segmentation techniques such as Canny, GrabCut, Otsu thresholding, K-means clustering, Watershed, SLIC, and Felzenszwalb were applied as preprocessing to the dataset prior to training the classification models. Each method was used to generate segmented versions of the pest images, ensuring that the models were trained on inputs with enhanced boundary localization and foreground–background separation. These segmentation outputs provided multiple representations of each pest instance, enabling the classifiers to learn from boundary-refined, background-reduced, and structure-enhanced variants of the images.

To avoid ambiguity between experimental settings, it is important to clarify that models trained on segmentation-enhanced images operate under a different input regime than those trained on raw RGB images. A consistent training protocol was adopted across all architectures; however, input resolution was adjusted according to architectural capacity and common practice, with lightweight CNNs trained at 224 × 224 and higher-capacity and hybrid models trained at increased resolutions to preserve fine-grained pest details. Consequently, performance differences reflect both architectural design and input-resolution effects. All models used identical dataset splits, optimizer, loss function, learning-rate scheduling, regularization settings, and early stopping criteria unless explicitly stated otherwise. Although Table 3 lists ranges for certain hyperparameters, these values were not freely tuned on a per-model basis. MobileNetV2 makes use of lightweight inverted-residual blocks with linear bottlenecks, which leads to a significant reduction in the parameter count and computational width. The design of MobileNetV2 allows the preservation of important edge and texture information even with the limit of a small receptive field, thus it is suitable for pests that have simple shapes or are in strong color contrast. Conversely, an insufficient channel limit and intermediate feature maps with a narrowness restrict the modeling capacity for complex spatial interactions. Therefore, MobileNetV2 (Fig. 5) has a harder time in a variety of scenarios-for instance, overlapping pests, high-frequency background clutter, or subtle interspecies differences with requiring greater contextual integration. From its deep yet identical stacks of 3 × 3 convolutions input, VGG16 represents radically different architectural biases. Although a very deep network without skip connections provides for representing more complex features, it also promotes background relationship memorizing. With refusal to use architectural regularization mechanisms like identity shortcuts, squeeze-and-excitation modules, and attention layers, the low-level textures present in the background of an image such as leaf venation, soil granularity, or shadow gradients-go deep into the hierarchy of features. This, in turn, adversely affects the capacity of VGG16 to maintain interclass separations under visually similar objects, and its bias towards background invariance weakens.

The architecture of EfficientNetB0 (Fig. 6) is a very well-thought and classified scaling procedure of depth, width, and input resolution, which permits increased diversification of feature learning while keeping computation costs and memory down. This scaling, based fully on compound scaling, acts to stretch the receptive field effectively at one hand while increasing discriminative capability of mid-level features at the other hand, so that species discrimination can be carried out even through very small adjustments across a pest species. This gets stretched gradually by EfficientNetB3, which goes even deeper and wider through the same compound scaling principle, yet providing more detailed anatomy such as antenna segmentation, thorax marking, and wing venation with accuracy. Larger architecture allows stronger connections among multi-scale features, crucial in differentiating species where the distinctions are based on very tiny, subtle-folded morphological characters.

Architecture of EfficientNetB0.

The ResNet50’s identity-based residual connections, as the name suggests, not only stabilize the gradient flow over the entire depth of the network but also preserve the low-level spatial information continuity. Thus, in case of the Network, it can detect pests hidden in very noisy biological substrates like, for instance, leaves with colors resembling the ones of the pests, which are considered as a type of camouflage. On the other hand, its downsampling strategy and large feature-map strides result in a very strong spatial smoothing. A great benefit here for the network would be the robustness, but this smoothing might also eliminate some very fine discriminative cues, such as minute color gradients, micro-patterning, or small appendage structures, leading to the occasional misclassification when the class boundaries depend on tiny, localized differences.

EfficientNetV2-B0 features a combination of improved training-aware scaling, fused-MBConv blocks, and optimized progressive learning which not only expands the stability across varying imaging conditions but also maintains the feature consistency. These features are effective in preserving the features even under different lighting, motion blur, mismatched angles and in the presence of cluttered backgrounds. The improved capacity-to-compute ratio is a major factor in helping the model generalize better when pest samples are taken under non-standard or noisy field conditions.

Xception and NASNetLarge are examples of another dimension of architectural diversity. By applying depthwise separable convolutions (SepConv), the model design of Xception (Fig. 7) has good boundary sensitivity that is good for post-processing. Despite that, the parameter count remains relatively low. This means that we can focus more to the fine boundaries of objects and hence the model can precisely tell pest outlines apart even if they are well sketched against complex leaf structures. On the other hand, NASNetLarge, which is the result of neural architecture search, utilizes cell-based motifs with wider filter sizes and bold multi-branch design. Although the model is very expressive, the occupation of high-frequency texture channels can lead to overfitting of the dominant background textures, thus causing confusion when pests are on visually intricate plant surfaces.

Architecture of Xception.

The hybrid architectures Hybrid InceptionResNetV2, Hybrid ResNet50 + CBAM, and Hybrid EffNet_Transformer are the ones who got their gain from the merging of convolutional backbones and attention-enhancing modules. Such models utilize still or moving architectural variables like channel-attention weighting, spatial-attention refinement, and multi-head global reasoning shift to divert focus from the environment around the pest to its body. They also improve the selectivity of the features, suppress the noise coming from the background, and assist in the localization of the target by making it more discriminative.

Inception-ResNet-v2, as shown in Fig. 8, is a robust deep convolutional neural network that can perform pest classification with great accuracy. Its training stability along with learning rich and multi-scale visual features are its major advantages. The network, which is a combination of residual connections and Inception modules, can capture the fine-grained features of the pests such as texture, shape, and their structural patterns which are necessary for the distinction of visually similar pest species. The reduction blocks have the effect of increasingly deeper features and at the same time, they compress the spatial information; this enables the hierarchical abstraction from low-level visual cues to high-level semantic representations. Residual learning also contributes to faster convergence and better generalization, which is especially valuable when dealing with limited or imbalanced agricultural datasets. Global average pooling and a softmax classifier are then employed alongside Inception-ResNet-v2, which not only provides but also requires robust and discriminative feature representations; thus, it is a strong backbone for accurate multi-class pest identification in real-time agricultural monitoring systems.

The Hybrid EffNetV2-S + Transformer configuration is achieved the strongest overall performance for offering such a high level of performance because of the mutual benefit of its architectural components. The EfficientNetV2-S backbone is the one that, thanks to the increased depth–width balance and fused-MBConv layers, can capture small-scale color transitions, texture irregularities, and micro-structural features with high resolution. To avoid the situation where the model only sees the local area, the Transformer provides a global receptive field where the long-range relational modeling can take place. This modeling is not possible through convolution alone. The resulting combination is such that even when the pests are obscured, out-of-focus, fragmented in space, or very similar to neighboring classes, the model’s classification remains true.

The differences in performance that were noted are from the mutual influence of the architectural variables such as depth, width, scaling of the receptive field, type of convolution, number of parameters, attention mechanisms, and the strategies applied for feature aggregation. These factors play a fundamental role in determining each model’s ability to pick up details of small objects, its tolerance to background variability, and its capability to separate pests with minor morphological differences.

While MobileNetV2 and VGG16 tend to show weaker generalization due to smaller representational power, as they can hardly capture fine-grained morphology details, and so they are more sensitive to noisy backgrounds that may come up with domain shifts in field imagery. On the reverse side, modern families such as EfficientNet, ResNet, Xception, and NASNet stand out owing to deeper integration with depthwise feature hierarchies; for instance, extreme scaling strategy, good convolutional blocks making better spatial patterns, etc. The main direction pursued for these architectures is to balance depth and efficiency, which also enables the modeling of the shape, color patterns, and structural variations of pests more robustly.

Indeed, hybrid architectures combining deep CNN backbones with attention mechanisms or Transformer modules have taken global contextual modeling to yet higher levels. Global models have the unique advantage of modeling all long-range dependencies, as required when a pest occupies a small region, or when discrimination of features depends on some larger context present in the leaf structure. Some of the hybrid models are sporting a symptom of overfitting, failing to generalize to validation data: their model capacity is simply too large. This too has the potential to make them remember the training data over the generalization, particularly if the dataset is imbalanced or limited in representations among the pest classes. Adding CBAM or Transformer layers usually results in models that are more interpretable with greater emphasis on regions of interest, resulting in detecting obstacles in complex environments. Taking this into account, the ones that strike the best balance between model capacity and regularization are generally more generalizable. In conclusion, the overall performance follows a progression: simpler CNNs are not very good at handling fine differences in pest data, strong modern CNNs do this very well, and hybrid attention-driven models would excel, provided they maintain the correct balance between expressiveness and generalization.

On the other hand, NASNetLarge, which is still performing well, shows a bit more scattered misclassifications, this is especially true for pests that have overlapping visual traits or similar color patterns. The ResNet50 + CBAM model is the one that most frequently misclassifies, and the confusion is spread across pest types in several clusters. The use of CBAM in the network enables it to pay attention to the regions that are most important, but the backbone corresponding to it is not as successful as the modern or hybrid architectures in capturing the fine-grained differences. This is in line with its average performance on the macro scale and the train–validation gaps noted earlier.

In summary, these confusion matrices serve to strengthen the explanation interpreted by the learning curves and macro metrics: the models with balanced scaling or careful integration of attention (especially EfficientNetB3 and the well-regularized hybrids) are the ones that generalize more reliably among the different pest species, while the architectures that are either prone to overfitting or limited in representational depth produce errors that are more scattered and class-specific.

Confidence-based reliability assessment

The per-class confidence value in Fig. 11 describes two complementary aspects of EfficientNetB3 behaviour for each pest: how often the model is correct (class accuracy) and how confident it is when it predicts (mean and spread of confidence for true predictions and for the top-1 prediction regardless of correctness). Reading these together reveals whether the model is (a) reliable and well-calibrated for a class, (b) underconfident, or (c) overconfident and brittle and those patterns tie directly back to the learning-curves and confusion matrices.

Per-class accuracy and confidence across pest categories using EfficientNetB3 model.

First, a few classes show high accuracy (green color) together with high mean true confidence (blue color) and low true-confidence variance (light blue color). For these classes, the model both knows and knows that it knows: predictions are correct, consistently confident, and rarely scattered to other labels in the confusion matrices. This indicates clean, distinctive visual features (or plentiful representative examples) that the feature extractor learned robustly. Second, another set of classes have moderate accuracy but still display reasonable mean true confidence (blue), while their top-1 confidence (orange) is noticeably higher than the mean confidence on true predictions. That gap is a hallmark of overconfidence on incorrect predictions: the model often makes high-confidence mistakes for these classes. In the confusion matrices, these classes appear as sources or sinks of off-diagonal errors (confusions with visually similar pests). Practically, this pattern means the model forms sharp decision boundaries around noisy or overlapping feature regions which means it is decisive, but sometimes decisively wrong. Third, several classes have low accuracy (green), low mean true confidence (blue), and high top-1 and true-confidence variability (red and light blue). These classes are both uncertain and inconsistent: the model is frequently wrong, and when it is correct its confidence varies substantially. In the confusion matrices, these classes tend to have diffuse error patterns (confused with many other labels), and in the learning curves they align with cases where validation instability or class-specific variance is observed. Contributing factors include limited or low-diversity training samples, high intra-class variability (pose, background, life-stage differences), or strong visual overlap with other pest species.

These “highest-confidence scores” offer a visual explanation for the quantitative patterns observed earlier across accuracy, confusion matrices, and per-class confidence statistics (Fig. 12).

For both models i.e. EffNetV2-S + Transformer and EfficientNetV2-B0, the correctly classified, high-confidence images share a set of clear visual properties: strong contrast between the pest and its background, distinctive morphological cues (body shape, color patterning, segmentation, wing structure), and minimal occlusion. When such cues are present, both architectures respond with near-perfect certainty, which aligns with the very high true-class confidence values shown in the per-class confidence table for classes like snail, Mediterranean fruit fly, cutworm, or cabbage looper. The consistency of these high-confidence examples also helps explain why certain classes repeatedly ranked as top performers in macro precision/recall, why their confusion-matrix diagonals were nearly perfect, and why their confidence distributions had low variance. During these classes, the model gets to look at examples of the species that are the “canonical” visual forms, thus, the deep learning backbone can distinguish even small differences in texture and shapes while the Transformer or the improved feature-scaling layers are working to maintain a global consistency across the picture. This teamwork is mainly noticed in classes with very strong outline identity e.g. snail, earwig, weevil where the two models have stable and clear predictions. Meanwhile, these pictures are also revealing the reason why some classes were more difficult: the examples of the highest confidence are surprisingly clean in comparison with those of the classes that have lower accuracy and confidence variance. Insects such as beetles, thrips, mites, or aphids, for instance, usually have their dataset samples mixed up with a lot of other things, have individuals of various sizes overlapping each other, or have very low contrast. The best cases of these insects are still confidently detected, but they make it very clear what the model means by “looking for” highly visible structure and minimum background noise which is a condition that is not always the case in real field imagery. This observation is directly linked to the previous finding that some classes have their model confidence overestimated on the hard or noisy samples or have their validation performance fluctuating.

In summary, these exemplary images are qualitatively confirming the numerically observed trends: classes with clean, distinctive visual signatures consistently driving high confidence and high accuracy across the models, while visually ambiguous classes challenging even advanced architectures. The visuals for practical improvement reveal the necessity of diversifying training data to include more difficult, field-realistic cases and of applying targeted augmentation or calibration techniques to ensure that the model’s confidence better reflects its uncertainty in less ideal conditions.

Discussion

The comparison of classical CNNs, modern deep architectures, and hybrid CNN–Transformer models in this study brings out the fact that the choice of architecture has a very significant influence on the capacity of a model to recognize pests that are very small, overlapped or partly hidden in the real environment of agriculture30. The strengths of VGG16 and MobileNetV2 have even been more clearly seen in that they effectively reveal the limitations of older CNN designs that are deprived of modern regularization and attention mechanisms and also limited in terms of depth and channel capacity. Consequently, such models usually have a hard time ensnaring subtle morphological features and are more prone to noise from the background and slight variations between classes. On the other hand, contemporary compound-scaled architectures like EfficientNetB0/B3 and EfficientNetV2-B0 provide good generalization and stability via the adjustment of depth, width, and input resolution just this way balancing. Likewise, networks such as ResNet50, Xception, and NASNetLarge demonstrate that residual connections, depthwise separable convolutions, and architecture search can significantly enhance multi-scale feature learning.

The best performance is achieved by hybrid models that combine convolutional backbones with attention mechanisms or transformer components. CNNs enable highly accurate local feature extraction, while transformers effectively capture global contextual information, leading to improved discrimination among visually similar pest species. This advantage is clearly demonstrated by the Hybrid EfficientNetV2-S + Transformer model, which achieves the highest classification accuracy and exhibits superior robustness across pest categories. Furthermore, segmentation analysis reinforces these findings, showing that architectures enhanced with attention mechanisms can more effectively focus on pest regions while reducing the influence of complex background noise. Recent CNN–Transformer hybrid frameworks have shown strong performance in fine-grained visual recognition tasks. Investigating additional CNN–Transformer architectures on pest datasets therefore represents a promising direction for future research. However, due to computational constraints and the scope of the present study, such experiments are deferred to future work.

The advantageous findings nevertheless accompanied limitations that somewhat restricted their general applicability. The dataset is diverse but lacks consistency in visual quality. In cases of some pest species, their presence is denoted by clear, high-contrast images while for others, their capturing is done in poor lighting, under heavy occlusion, or amid cluttered backgrounds. Such inconsistencies make certain autotrophic classes difficult to identify, which leads to a noticeable decline in accuracy and a rise in confidence variability, especially for pests like thrips, mites, beetles, and earwigs. Although their performance is second to none, high-capacity hybrid models might eventually get overfitted if trained on little or unbalanced data samples. Besides, the models are still prone to domain shifts like changing regions, different camera types, or varying crop environments, which might lead to a decrease in performance when used in the field unless domain adaptation is employed. The segmentation techniques used in this research were applied as a preprocessing step to generate segmented versions of the images, and the classifiers were trained using these segmentation-enhanced images. Lastly, the study emphasizes single-image classification, overlooks temporal information and multi-view perspectives, both of which could be crucial in real-time pest monitoring and more detailed field diagnostics. The above-mentioned drawbacks also put forth several attractive areas for future investigations. One of the ways to reduce the models’ sensitivity to domain shifts and to support their better generalization is by making the dataset more diverse through the inclusion of images from various parts of the world, various seasons, different crop types, and various camera devices. Data-centric methods such as targeted augmentation, class rebalancing, generative creation of rare pest samples, and curriculum learning can still be used to enhance performance, especially for hard classes. On the architectural side, future research might investigate fully integrated segmentation-classification pipelines, Transformer-based detectors, or multi-stage networks that simultaneously capture fine morphological details and broader ecological context. Knowledge distillation, among others, might help to generate lightweight versions of the high-performing hybrid models for drone, smartphone, and low-power IoT device deployment in the field. It is possible through multi-sensors data fusion to achieve an improved interpretation of pest behavior by combining RGB images with hyperspectral measurements, pheromone traps, or environmental sensors. Besides, the integration of these models into farmers’ decision-support systems that consider the weather patterns, crop growth stages, and pest life-cycle information would not only make pest management predictive and proactive but also enhance the effectiveness of precision agriculture.

Although the segmentation outputs helped visualize boundary structures and improve interpretability, in the present study the segmentation techniques were also applied as preprocessing to generate multiple transformed versions of the dataset prior to model training. Therefore, segmentation functioned as a dataset-level enhancement rather than a post-hoc visualization tool. However, since no controlled ablation experiment was performed to compare raw versus segmented training regimes, the precise performance contribution of segmentation cannot be quantified within this study and will be addressed in future work.

Conclusion

In this research, various deep-learning architecture for multi-class pest classification was evaluated extensively and the impact of various computational designs on the performance of the real agricultural images was clearly shown. The results of comparing the classical CNNs, modern compound-scaled networks, residual architectures, NAS-based models, and attention-enhanced hybrid frameworks revealed that effective pest classification is dependent on both the strength of the feature extractor and the model’s skill in uniting fine details with broader contextual information. The results underline that lightweight traditional networks are often unable to differentiate subtle characteristics among similar pest species while modern and hybrid architectures extract richer multi-scale features to be far better at the task of differentiating between pests with closely related visual characteristics.

Out of all the models tested, the Hybrid EfficientNetV2-S + Transformer gave the best and most reliable results. The model was able to classify pests correctly in a wide range of images, thanks to the combination of hierarchical convolutional features and the global relationship capturing ability of the Transformer. Different methods of evaluation classification metrics, confusion matrices, confidence analyses, and segmentation-based visual checks were all in agreement that the integration of convolutional efficiency with attention-based adaptability resulted in the best-performing models. The results suggest that the combination of CNN and Transformer components in a balanced architecture is a reliable method for the identification of pests with high accuracy. This, in turn, lays the groundwork for future agricultural monitoring systems that can be eco-friendly by supporting the sustainable protection of crops, reducing the use of chemicals and improving decision-making in the field of precision agriculture.
