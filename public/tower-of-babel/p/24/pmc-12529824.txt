Development and validation of a dynamic nomogram for predicting in-hospital mortality in acute massive cerebral infarction: a retrospective study in a Chinese population
Authors: Xuhui Liu, Xujie Wang, Rongfei Xie, Zhaohui Liu, Shasha Liang, Jinyin Bai, Chunjian Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMassive cerebral infarction (MCI) is a severe form of ischemic stroke that can result in adverse outcomes, including death. This study aimed to identify the independent risk factors associated with MCI mortality by developing a multivariate model using stepwise logistic regression analysis.MethodsThis retrospective study included 159 hospitalized patients between January 15, 2022, and October 20, 2023. The diagnosis of MCI was based on clinical symptoms, the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and brain MRI. Potential mortality-related predictors were identified by analyzing patient histories, coagulation profiles, renal function, and serum biochemical indicators such as fasting blood glucose (FBG), homocysteine (HCY), and hemoglobin (Hb).ResultsAmong the 159 patients, optimized multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that smoking (OR = 10.48, 95% CI 2.85–42.80), FBG (OR = 1.97, 95% CI 1.45–2.82), HCY (OR = 8.62, 95% CI 1.29–76.21), Hb (OR = 0.96, 95% CI 0.94–0.99), and GCS score (OR = 0.67, 95% CI 0.52–0.83) were significantly associated with in-hospital mortality (all P < 0.05). The model showed good discrimination (AUC = 0.943, 95% CI 0.903–0.982), with a marginal R-squared (R2M) of 0.660. Calibration and decision curve analyses suggested good predictive performance and potential clinical utility of the nomogram.ConclusionSmoking, elevated FBG and HCY, low Hb, and lower GCS scores were identified as independent predictors of mortality in MCI patients. Managing these factors may help reduce the risk of death.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03300-0.Keywords: Massive cerebral infarction (MCI), Risk factors, Mortality, Nomogram, Logistic regression

Introduction

Stroke is currently recognized as the world’s second-leading cause of mortality and the primary cause of acquired disability among adults [1, 2]. Predominantly, ischemic stroke constitutes the majority of stroke cases, contributing to 70–80% of incidents. This type occurs due to the blockage of cerebral arteries [3]. Particularly, Massive cerebral infarction (MCI) is characterized as a significant space-occupying lesion within the middle cerebral artery (MCA) territory, the entire anterior cerebral circulation, or the cerebellum [4, 5]. These malignant stroke variants, comprising 10–20% of ischemic strokes, are historically known as “massive strokes” and are linked to elevated mortality rates [6]. MCI usually worsens clinically in the first 1–7 days, and the mortality rate rises sharply to 53–78%, likely due to our broader inclusion criteria that encompassed all patients with infarction involving at least two-thirds of the MCA territory, not only those with malignant progression [7, 8]. In the realm of massive stroke management, not every patient experiences a progression toward fatal outcomes. It is essential to identify sensitive indicators that can accurately forecast which factors might precipitate a lethal MCI. This enables a tailored approach in treating and preventing high-risk cases, ultimately reducing mortality rates associated with this condition.

In an investigation aimed at identifying predictors of mortality in MCI and examining the efficacy of decompressive hemicraniectomy, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores were employed as prognostic indicators for fatal outcomes in malignant MCA cases [9]. Similarly, the study highlighted the significance of high National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores as critical evaluative tools in large cerebral infarctions [10]. Building on these insights, this retrospective study was conducted to analyze the clinical characteristics and risk factors in patients with massive cerebral infarction (MCI) who either succumbed to or survived the condition.

While numerous studies have highlighted conventional risk factors for cerebral infarction [11, 12], the role of anemia as a vascular risk factor remains under-recognized. The correlation between declining hemoglobin (Hb) levels and cerebral infarction is established, but its exact nature remains elusive [13]. The clinical significance of anemia in stroke patients has received increasing attention in recent years. The impact of anemia in stroke patients has garnered increasing scrutiny recently, especially concerning its ability to predict adverse outcomes. [14] Research by Hao et al. [15] indicates that anemia prevalence in acute stroke patients varies between 15 and 29%, with anemia at admission being an independent predictor of mortality at discharge and within a 12-month period. Similarly, findings by Li et al. [16] suggest higher mortality rates in stroke patients with anemia compared to those without. Additionally, Milionis et al.[16] have demonstrated that anemia not only correlates with the severity of a stroke, but also independently forecasts both short-term and long-term mortality post-stroke. Protective roles of homocysteine (HCY) have been reported, particularly in shielding the brain from ischemic damage, with exogenous HCY administration shown to reduce infarct volume.[17] Significantly, abnormally high HCY levels are strongly linked to the onset and progression of cerebral infarction and other cerebrovascular diseases [18, 19]. Hence, the study aimed to develop a predictive model based on these risk factors, intending to enhance the clinical decision-making process in diagnosis and treatment strategies.

Method

Study cohort and route

This retrospective cohort study was executed at the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University, a leading 3500-bed national tertiary healthcare institution. The research encompassed a group of 159 patients, admitted from January 15, 2022, to October 20, 2023, specifically for MCI management. Diagnosis upon admission was carried out by an experienced neurologist, adhering to the established treatment guidelines for hemispheric infarcts. The study’s inclusion criteria were: (1) patient age ranging from 18 to 80 years; (2) unilateral MCI covering at least two-thirds of the middle cerebral artery territory, as confirmed through either computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI); (3) MCI onset within 7 days prior to hospital admission. Exclusion criteria included: (1) patients undergoing decompressive surgical treatment (e.g., hemicraniectomy); (2) MCI accompanied by intracranial hematomas; (3) patients who died of severe systemic illnesses (such as multiple organ failure or critical infections), or who had significant pre-stroke disability (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] ≥ 3), or who had a history of malignant tumors; (4) patients in whom the infarct size did not involve at least two-thirds of the MCA territory, as confirmed by imaging. It should be noted that patients who received intravenous thrombolysis and/or mechanical thrombectomy were not excluded, provided that the final infarct size still met the imaging definition of MCI. These cases were included in the cohort and analyzed in the “Results” section. Participants were divided into two categories based on their outcomes: those who succumbed to brain herniation and those who survived within a week of MCI onset. Brain herniation was identified through specific clinical signs and imaging findings, including pronounced cerebral edema, ventricular compression, and midline shift, as observed in cranial CT or MRI scans. Flowchart of the case selection process

Upon admission, comprehensive baseline data were meticulously documented for each patient. This included demographic details such as gender, age, smoking status, and alcohol consumption habits, along with pertinent medical history encompassing prior stroke occurrences, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and atrial fibrillation. Clinical parameters were also carefully recorded, including HB levels, coagulation parameters, renal function, lipid levels, blood glucose, and glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) values. For all patients, a standardized neurological assessment was performed between 1 and 4 days after symptom onset, including assessment of neurological function using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) and Glasgow Coma Score (GCS). The research protocol received full approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University, ensuring adherence to ethical standards in medical research (Approval No: 2024A-1390).

Observation of indexes

NIHSS: Including the current NIHSS score on admission, the NIHSS score includes consciousness, thinking, visual field, upper limb movement, lower limb movement, ataxia, sensation, speech, dysarthria, and unilateral neglect, with a total score of 30. The higher the NIHSS score, the more severe the neurological impairment [20].

GCS score: Containing scores on the day of admission or discharge, this scale assesses the patient based on three responses: eye opening, movement, and verbal response. The scale ranges from a maximum of 15 to a minimum of 3, with lower scores indicating a worse prognosis [21].

Each score on the above scale was assessed by three attending neurological ICU physicians or above in our hospital, and the final score was obtained by taking the average of the three scores.

In addition, variables such as platelet count, serum albumin, and renal function were collected at admission; however, because most values fell within the normal range (platelet count 100–300 × 10⁹/L; albumin > 35 g/L in more than 95% of patients; all patients with normal renal function and no hemodialysis), these parameters were not included in the prognostic model. Infarct volume, cerebral edema, and brain herniation were used as outcome-defining criteria and were therefore not analyzed as independent predictors; instead, their clinical impact was indirectly captured by objective scoring systems such as GCS and NIHSS. Additionally, we recorded the presence of large infarction, cerebral edema, and brain herniation based on imaging findings during hospitalization. These indicators were used to describe the severity of the infarction but were not included as predictors in the multivariate model due to potential collinearity with NIHSS and GCS scores. In this research, the criteria for diagnosing diabetes mellitus were stringent. It involved either the use of antidiabetic medication or multiple pathological blood tests indicating fasting blood glucose levels at or above 7 mmol/l (126 mg/dl), or postprandial glucose levels reaching or exceeding 11.1 mmol/l 2 h following an oral glucose challenge. Hypertension was identified based on one of two criteria: either blood pressure readings equal to or greater than 140/90 mmHg on three separate occasions after the current admission or a prior diagnosis of hypertension with ongoing antihypertensive therapy. A history of stroke was determined through a documented past cerebrovascular event that had led to physical or speech impairments, corroborated by relevant imaging findings. A significant smoking history was defined as an average consumption of 10 cigarettes per day over a period of 20 years or more.

Measurement of various clinical indicators

For this study, venous blood samples were meticulously obtained from patients diagnosed with MCI on the second day post-ischemic event, using EDTA-anticoagulated Vacutainer tubes. These samples were promptly stored at 4 °C for standard laboratory analysis. Each patient’s blood (2 × 4 ml per patient) was rapidly centrifuged at 200 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant plasma was carefully separated and preserved for subsequent biochemical assays. To guarantee the integrity and reliability of the laboratory results, meticulous steps were followed in blood sample collection, processing, storage, and quality control. These procedures were strictly aligned with the laboratory’s quality control standards. The process involved using specialized equipment like a blood collector and centrifuge, along with a biochemistry analyzer. The reagents necessary for testing various biochemical markers were precisely chosen, adhering closely to established guideline indicators. The biochemistry analyzer employed in this research was sourced from Olympus, Japan. Key biochemical indices analyzed included low-density lipoprotein (LDL), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and homocysteine (Hcy), each measured with high precision and accuracy.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± sd) or median (IQR). Categorical variables are expressed as percentages [n (%)]. Continuous variables were tested and analyzed for normality using a t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were performed using the Chi-square test or two-tailed Fisher’s exact test. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to analyze the risk factors of MCI mortality. To optimize the results, we gradually removed meaningless variables until the P-value of all factors was less than or equal to 0.05. The nomogram for predicting MCI mortality was established based on optimized multivariate logistic regression. The nomogram-based prediction model was verified based on bootstrapped calibration curves (1000 times) and marginal R-squared (R2M). This study used the ROC (receiver operating characteristic) curve approach to test the optimized multivariate logistic regression model’s discrimination abilities. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the clinical benefit of the optimized multivariate logistic regression model. In order to further assess potential confounding factors, we additionally analyzed treatment strategies and pre-stroke therapies in relation to in-hospital mortality. Treatment strategies were categorized into three groups: 0 = refusal of both thrombolysis and thrombectomy, 1 = thrombolysis followed by thrombectomy, and 2 = thrombolysis only with refusal of thrombectomy. Furthermore, pre-stroke anticoagulant and antiplatelet therapies were recorded. Chi-squared tests were applied to evaluate associations between these categorical variables and mortality outcomes.

Results

Basic characteristics

The final safety set included 159 patients; 124 (77.99%) patients were included in the survive group, and 35 (22.01%) patients were included in the mortality group (Table 1). In the survive group and mortality group, most of the patients were female (66.04%) and had no hyperlipidemia (76.10%), no diabetes (83.02%), no atherosclerotic heart disease (78.62%), and no drinking (90.57%). In hyperlipidemia, hypertension, diabetes, atherosclerotic heart disease, history of atrial fibrillation, TC, TG, body mass index (BMI), D-dimer (D-d), fibrinogen (FB), urea nitrogen (UN), uric acid (UA), Cr, and LDL, no significant difference was detected between these two groups. The study found that the mortality group had a higher male percentage (51.43% vs 29.03%), history of stroke (42.86% vs 0.00%), smoking (60.00% vs 8.87%), drinking (20.00% vs 6.45%), and age (68.91 vs 63.14) than the survive group (all P < 0.05). In clinical characteristics, FBG of 7.54 ± 1.61 mmol/l, Cr of 1.16 ± 0.45 umol/l, HCY of 13.17 ± 2.98 umol/l, decreased HB of 28.20 ± 23.29 g/l, and NIHSS score of 20.69 ± 5.43 in the mortality group were higher than in the survive group (FBG of 5.45 ± 1.25 mmol/l, Cr of 79.70 ± 36.59 umol/l, CyC of 0.92 ± 0.24 umol/l, decreased HB of 19.32 ± 14.60 g/l, and NIHSS score of 17.69 ± 5.19); a statistically significant difference was detected between these two groups (P < 0.05). Hb and GCS of the group of score were lower than in the survive group (P < 0.05). To further describe cohort severity, we summarized the distribution of large infarction, cerebral edema, and brain herniation across both groups. Among patients who survived, 83.9% (n = 104) exhibited large infarctions, whereas this proportion was higher (77.1%, n = 27) among those who died. Similarly, 94.0% (n = 117) of the survivors had no signs of brain herniation or cerebral edema, while 71.0% (n = 25) of patients in the mortality group exhibited these complications. These findings are illustrated in Supplementary Figure S3.

Treatment strategies and pre-stroke therapies

We further evaluated the potential impact of treatment strategies and pre-stroke therapies on in-hospital mortality. Regarding treatment strategies, patients were classified as 0 = refusal of both thrombolysis and thrombectomy, 1 = thrombolysis followed by thrombectomy, and 2 = thrombolysis only with refusal of thrombectomy. Chi-squared analysis revealed no significant differences in mortality across these three groups (χ2 test, P = 0.799). In addition, neither prior anticoagulant use (χ2 test, P = 0.277) nor antiplatelet therapy (χ2 test, P = 0.183) was significantly associated with mortality. These findings are presented in Supplementary Figure S1 (A–C).

As can be seen in Fig. 4, the prognostic nomogram included all significant independent factors of MCI mortality in the final safety set. Interactive dynamic nomogram for mortality risk prediction

A dynamic web-based nomogram was constructed to estimate individualized in-hospital mortality risk among patients with acute massive cerebral infarction (MCI). The model integrated five predictors—hemoglobin (Hb), smoking history, fasting blood glucose (FBG), homocysteine (HCY), and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score—into a logistic regression framework and was deployed as an online application (available at: https://nomoiuxuhui.shinyapps.io/dynnomapp/). Figure 6A shows the interactive input interface. Users can enter patient-specific values for Hb, smoking status, FBG, HCY, and GCS to obtain an individualized prediction of in-hospital mortality risk. Figure 6B displays the Numerical Summary output generated by the application. This table provides the predicted probabilities together with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For example, when the covariates were set as Hb = 126 g/L, smoking = Yes, FBG = 5.6 mmol/L, and HCY = 15 μmol/L, the model estimated mortality risks of 0.906 (95% CI, 0.663–0.979) for GCS = 12, 0.973 (95% CI, 0.812–0.997) for GCS = 6, and 0.969 (95% CI, 0.794–0.996) for GCS = 3. These results indicate that lower GCS scores were generally associated with higher predicted mortality risk. However, the estimates for GCS = 6 and GCS = 3 were very close, with overlapping confidence intervals, suggesting that the difference in predicted risk between these two levels of neurologic impairment may not be substantial. The application also provided automatic high-risk alerts when the predicted probability exceeded a pre-specified threshold, such as 0.75, which facilitates rapid identification of patients at elevated risk. Figure 6C summarizes the logistic regression coefficients (β) and their significance for each of the five predictors included in the model. Smoking (β = 2.7207, P < 0.001), FBG (β = 0.8062, P < 0.001), and GCS score (β = 0.4431, P < 0.001) were positively associated with mortality. Hemoglobin was inversely associated with mortality (β = − 0.0348, P = 0.0235), indicating that higher Hb levels were linked to a lower probability of death. Although HCY showed a positive trend (β = 0.0545, P = 0.1651), its association did not reach statistical significance in this cohort. The model performance appeared robust, with a residual deviance of 97.778 on 153 degrees of freedom, an Akaike information criterion (AIC) of 197.78, and successful convergence after six Fisher scoring iterations. The dispersion parameter of 1.0 suggested no evidence of overdispersion. To our knowledge, this web-based nomogram is among the first to dynamically integrate neurologic function and metabolic biomarkers into a visualized, patient-specific prediction platform for MCI. By enabling rapid individualized risk estimation and generating automated alerts for high-risk patients, this model may support timely clinical decision-making and align with the principles of precision medicine in acute stroke care.

Discussion

In this study, we utilized a multivariate logistic regression model to identify and quantify the risk factors of MCI mortality. Our findings indicated a significantly heightened risk of mortality among individuals with an extensive history of smoking when compared to their non-smoking counterparts, even accounting for duration of exposure. Furthermore, the analysis revealed a marked increase in mortality risk associated with specific clinical parameters: notably, elevated levels of FBG and HCY, alongside decreased HB levels and lower scores on the GCS. This integrative approach, encompassing both lifestyle factors and clinical biomarkers, affords a more nuanced and precise assessment of mortality risk in MCI, potentially guiding targeted interventions and management strategies. Importantly, our study population also differed from previous reports in terms of patient composition. Specifically, we included individuals who initially presented with mild symptoms at admission but later experienced in-hospital neurological deterioration that met the imaging criteria for MCI. Such progressive stroke cases are often excluded from studies focusing solely on early malignant infarction. This broader inclusion criterion, combined with timely rescue treatments such as tirofiban administration during deterioration, may have contributed to the relatively lower mortality rate observed in our cohort. These distinctions should be considered when interpreting the generalizability of our findings. Our study shares methodological similarities with previous research, as both employed logistic regression models validated through ROC, calibration, and DCA analyses to predict in-hospital mortality in patients with massive cerebral infarction. However, while earlier studies primarily emphasized imaging features and disease severity indicators [27], our work highlights the independent prognostic value of metabolic and hematologic factors, underscoring the complementary nature of these approaches.

MCI typically results from blockages in the internal carotid artery or the principal segment of the middle cerebral artery [28]. The significance of early diagnosis and intervention, ideally before hospital admission, cannot be overstated, as it plays a crucial role in mitigating severe risks, including mortality associated with MCI [29]. While imaging techniques are dependable for detecting vascular occlusions, they are frequently not feasible before hospitalization. Consequently, identifying suitable blood biomarkers becomes essential for the early detection of MCI, offering a potential avenue for prompt and effective medical response.

Previous studies had compared patients with MCI who developed ischemic stroke to healthy individuals without MCI. It was found that patients with MCI had a worse assessed prognosis and death [30]. It has been shown that the inclusion of the NIHSS in assessment protocols has shown promising results in studies focusing on the treatment of MCI [31]. In our study, GCS and NIHSS were included as assessment tools; however, in the study, the final prediction of death from cerebral infarction by NIHSS score was not optimistic, which may be related to the severity of the cases we included. Previous studies have used hypothermia as a therapeutic approach to enhance neurologic function and reduce stress in patients with acute MCI [32], and results showed that elevated GCS scores were associated with treatment efficacy. Nevertheless, many studies have not demonstrated a direct association between mortality in large cerebral infarction and a lower GCS score. Our study, which suggests a significant association between GCS score and mortality in cases of MCI, may provide a new perspective in this area of research and offer additional insight into the prognostic value of this scoring system.

In this study, we found a significant association between decreased Hb levels and the occurrence of large cerebral infarcts and high mortality. The brain relies critically on a constant supply of oxygen for cellular oxidative metabolism, which is not only dependent on cerebral blood flow but also on arterial blood oxygen content, largely influenced by Hb levels [33]. However, the role of Hb levels during hospitalization as a predictor of risk for occurrence or death in large cerebral infarct patients has been insufficiently studied. Young et al.[34] indicated that lower Hb ranges, rather than higher, are linked to poorer functional outcomes three months post-stroke. In addition, Kellert et al. [35] observed that both anemia or a nadir in Hb within the first five days of stroke onset, and a reduction in Hb or erythrocyte pressure volume, correlated with mortality three months after stroke onset. The hypothesis is that a decrease in Hb during hospitalization might lead to secondary damage in vulnerable ischemic areas by diminishing cerebral oxygen delivery. [36, 37] This can adversely impact the function of the heart, brain, and other organs. [38, 39] Some studies had found that while anemia was associated with mortality in patients with mild to moderate strokes (NIHSS < 10), the association not held in cases of more severe strokes[40, 41] This finding contrasts with our study, where Hb levels are linked not only to the occurrence of large cerebral infarcts but also to the likelihood of mortality from these events. Arata et al. [42] found an independent correlation between a decline in Hb during hospitalization and poor prognosis at discharge in acute stroke patients. However, these studies did not specifically focus on large cerebral infarcts, a gap our study aims to address. From a post-hospital management perspective, our findings suggest that correction of anemia and periodic Hb monitoring during follow-up could help optimize neurological recovery and reduce recurrence risk, aligning with prior recommendations on secondary prevention after stroke [43]. Furthermore, upon admission, patients generally received standard evidence-based management including antiplatelet therapy, statins, blood pressure and glucose control, and, where indicated, intravenous thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy, followed by intensive care monitoring, which provides a necessary therapeutic context for interpreting our findings [16].

HCY. Recent research indicates that HCY can mitigate oxidative damage. [44] However, elevated serum HCY levels are increasingly recognized as a predictor of heightened stroke risk and poorer prognostic outcomes [45]. Its influence extends to the development and initiation of cerebrovascular diseases, systemic inflammatory responses, and atherosclerosis. Elevated HCY levels may directly impact vascular wall remodeling by altering the balance between protein hydrolysis and anti-protein hydrolysis activities [46]. Moreover, HCY is implicated in having direct toxic effects that contribute to stroke and other cardiovascular conditions. [47] In ischemic conditions, HCY is released in substantial quantities [48]. Observations reveal that serum HCY levels are markedly elevated in both severe and moderate cerebral infarction groups compared to the mild infarction group. Furthermore, there is a correlation between higher HCY levels, larger cerebral infarction areas, and more severe clinical neurological deficits. [49] However, the association between HCY levels and the occurrence or mortality in MCI has been less frequently explored in the literature. Our study delves into this area, finding that higher levels of HCY are indicative of MCI occurrences that culminate in death, thereby adding to the understanding of HCY’s role in cerebrovascular pathologies. In terms of post-discharge management, our results suggest that reducing HCY levels through lifestyle modifications (e.g., smoking cessation, diet adjustment) and vitamin supplementation (e.g., folate, vitamin B6, B12) may help improve long-term prognosis, which has been supported by interventional studies linking HCY reduction to lower vascular event risk [50]. In addition, during hospitalization, patients with markedly elevated HCY levels were routinely managed with vascular protective strategies and metabolic regulation in addition to standard acute stroke therapy, underscoring the importance of integrating biochemical monitoring into clinical care [51].

A study from Germany identified key factors associated with MCI, noting a high prevalence among males (66.2%), smokers (37.6%), and individuals who consume alcohol (14.7%) [52]. However, data from Asian populations were limited, and few articles had explored the risk factors of MCI mortality. In our study, smoking emerged as a significant risk factor leading to mortality from MCI, whereas gender and drinking did not demonstrate notable differences. While diabetes was not typically considered an independent risk factor for massive strokes, which might be attributed to the fact that diabetes-induced vascular changes usually affect small and medium-sized vessels. Nonetheless, our study found a significant disparity in elevated fasting blood glucose levels between the survival and mortality groups in MCI cases. This could be explained by a shift in the body’s sugar metabolism from aerobic to anaerobic oxidation in severe cerebral infarcts, leading to the production of acids like lactic acid, which exacerbate the condition. Zhang et al. analyzed the risk factors between large artery atherosclerotic stenotic cerebral infarction and small artery occlusive cerebral infarction, identifying diabetes mellitus and age as independent risk factors for the former. [53] However, the specific relationship between blood glucose levels and mortality in MCI cases remains underexplored and warrants further investigation.

This study was characterized by the analysis of the risk factors leading to death in MCI cases, aiming to prevent adverse outcomes in MCI patients. However, there were a few limitations in our research. Firstly, a small sample size restricted a more comprehensive examination of MCI cases, particularly due to the limited number of deaths observed. Additionally, geographical limitations could potentially introduce biases into the results. To overcome limitations and enhance the reliability of findings, future research should be prospective in design and encompass a larger, more diverse patient cohort, ideally involving multiple centers to address issues more comprehensively.

Conclusion

In this study, we used a comprehensive multivariate logistic model to predict the risk factors of MCI mortality. This study suggests that smoking, high FBG, high HCY, low Hb, and low GCS score may be major risk factors. Strict management and control of these identified risk factors may play an important role in reducing the likelihood of mortality associated with MCI.
