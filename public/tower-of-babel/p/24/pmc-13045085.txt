The association between perceived discrimination and Chinese adolescent loneliness: the roles of school bullying, suicide self-injury ideation, and teacher-student relationship
Authors: Ting Wang, Lingwei Jiang, Chaocao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerceived discrimination is a source of social stress that has been widely proven to negatively impact individuals’ mental health, especially among adolescents, potentially leading to emotional problems such as anxiety, depression, and loneliness. The current study constructed a chain mediation model and a moderated mediation model to explore whether perceived discrimination is associated with loneliness among adolescents and to understand the underlying mechanisms.MethodsWe surveyed 1,732 middle school students (886 were male [51.15%] and 846 were female [48.85%]) from Jiangxi Province, China, using the perceived discrimination scale for adolescents, the UCLA loneliness scale, the school bullying questionnaire, the suicide self-injury ideation questionnaire, and the teacher-student relationship scale.ResultsThe findings indicate that there is a significant association between perceived discrimination and loneliness, with school bullying and suicide self-injury ideation serving as statistical mediators. Additionally, the teacher-student relationship score moderates the impact of perceived discrimination on suicide self-injury ideation, showing that a high-quality teacher-student relationship can mitigate the negative effects of perceived discrimination, thereby reducing loneliness.ConclusionThe current study shows that perceived discrimination is an important predictor of loneliness in adolescents. School bullying and suicide self-injury ideation serve as mediators in this relationship, and the teacher-student relationship moderates the pathway between perceived discrimination and suicide self-injury ideation. These findings underscore the importance of fostering positive teacher-student relationships as a protective factor, suggesting that such relationships can help mitigate the adverse mental health effects of perceived discrimination.Keywords: Discrimination perception, Loneliness, School bullying, Suicide self-injury ideation, Teacher-student relationship

Introduction

Perceived discrimination refers to an individual’s awareness of negative evaluations or unfair treatment directed towards themselves or their group by external sources [1]. As a source of social stress, perceived discrimination has a detrimental impact on an individual’s social adaptation [2]. According to the stress and coping theory [3], once individuals perceive discrimination, their defense systems are activated by this danger signal, putting the body in a state of stress. The maintenance of this state is accompanied by various stress responses, such as loneliness, anxiety, and depression [4].

Perceived discrimination is a significant risk factor for individual developmental adaptation and is closely related to the internalization and externalization of problems [5]. The mental health status of Chinese adolescents may have worsened due to social changes, especially the increase in social problems. The negative indicators of this group’s mental health, such as mental issues, anxiety, and depression, have risen significantly, while positive indicators such as self-esteem, self-efficacy, and psychological resilience have shown a downward trend. Previous studies indicate that the occurrence of extreme levels of psychological and behavioral problems in adolescents is becoming increasingly serious. When adolescents in a highly sensitive state of discrimination experience school bullying [6]– [7], it increases the risk of developing physical and mental health issues [8], and may even lead to self-harm and suicide. A deficiency or absence of core family functions in adolescents is likely to lead to them becoming psychologically vulnerable. In the absence of parental care and support, they are more susceptible to experiencing loneliness. Once they encounter neglect or even discrimination from external sources, adolescents in a psychologically vulnerable state may develop perceived discrimination, which may increase the likelihood of experiencing school bullying [6]. Furthermore, school bullying increases the risk of loneliness in individuals [9]. However, a positive teacher-student relationship has a protective role in adolescent mental health to some extent. This relationship suggests that a good teacher-student relationship may reduce the likelihood of adolescents experiencing loneliness [10].

In summary, how to alleviate the psychosocial problems of adolescents (such as perceived discrimination, loneliness, and suicidal/self-harming thoughts), reduce the probability and severity of adolescents encountering adverse environments (such as school bullying) through positive social relationships (teacher-student relationships), and propose effective alleviation strategies to ensure their mental health development, has become the focus of this study. At the same time, this study will also provide feasible suggestions to promote the social adaptation of the adolescent group and play a role in preventing the occurrence and development of problematic behaviors, with considerable theoretical and practical significance.

Perceived discrimination and loneliness

Adolescents are in a critical period of physical and mental growth and development. Loneliness is an important indicator of adolescents’ social adaptability [11]. It not only leads to a low sense of social belonging, decreased self-esteem, and emotional dysregulation among adolescents [12], but it may also have a lasting impact on their healthy development [13]. According to the stress and coping theory, the human body strives to maintain a state of physical and mental balance. When faced with stress, the body responds both physiologically and psychologically. When experiencing low to moderate levels of stress, individuals may become more sensitive to their environment and improve their problem-solving abilities without prolonged negative emotional experiences. However, when experiencing high levels of stress, individuals may suffer disruptions in their balance, leading to decreased problem-solving abilities and the emergence of negative emotions such as depression and anxiety. The stress process model posits that discrimination, as a chronic stressor, can keep individuals in a state of stress, depleting their psychosocial resources and making them prone to experiencing negative emotions such as loneliness. This maladaptive response can further harm adolescents’ physical and mental health [14].

Previous studies have shown that perceived discrimination has a significant predictive effect on loneliness [15, 16], but these studies have primarily focused on groups with specific conditions such as disability or poverty. However, as an externally imposed stressor, discrimination exists not only among adolescents with special conditions but also among those without such conditions. Additionally, research has found that the higher the level of perceived discrimination among left-behind children, the higher the negative emotions, including loneliness, while positive emotions are relatively low [17]. Moreover, a study in Japan on the general population found a strong correlation between discrimination and loneliness, which is also associated with a deterioration in mental health [18]. A study in Poland, which examined the relationship between self-reported discrimination experiences, self-esteem, seeking support, and loneliness among 110 patients with mental disorders, demonstrated that experiences of discrimination directly or indirectly increase loneliness [19]. Based on these findings, this study proposes Hypothesis 1:

Hypothesis 1

(H1): Perceived discrimination can predict the level of loneliness.

The chain mediating role of school bullying and suicide self-injury ideation

School bullying can be categorized based on the roles participants play, including perpetrators, victims, and bystanders. The current study focuses on the perspective of victims of school bullying. School bullying involves one or more students deliberately and repeatedly bullying or assaulting another student, causing varying degrees of physical and psychological harm to the victim [17]. School bullying is characterized by its imbalance of power, repetitiveness, and harmfulness, and it includes both direct forms (e.g., physical and verbal attacks) and indirect forms (e.g., exclusion and isolation) [20]. School bullying can have severe adverse effects on adolescents’ physical and mental health, academic development, and social adaptation, leading to irreversible consequences for their long-term development [20]. Research indicates that students who are victims of school bullying are more likely to experience psychological symptoms such as anxiety, depression, loneliness, and suicidal or self-injurious ideation compared to their peers who have not been bullied [21].

Adolescents experience various forms of discrimination, including verbal, physical, conceptual, and avoidant [22]. All of these types of discrimination convey negative and harmful messages [1]. The forms of discrimination are not only manifested as overt physical attacks but can also be more subtle psychological harm, such as through social exclusion or labeling, which further exacerbates adolescents’ self-doubt and emotional distress [20], When discrimination manifests as tangible harm within the school environment, it transforms into school bullying, which can easily lead to emotional problems such as anxiety, depression, and loneliness among adolescents [20]. Long-term experiences of school bullying can trigger a sense of helplessness, making adolescents less capable of coping with difficulties in life [23], this may even result in suicidal or self-injurious thoughts and behaviors. Numerous studies have shown that school bullying is a significant risk factor for suicide self-injury ideation and attempts among adolescents [24–26]. Those who are frequently bullied are not only at a higher risk of suicide self-injury ideation and attempts within the year following the bullying but may also continue to experience these issues years later [27].

There is a potential link between suicide self-injury ideation and loneliness [28]. Suárez-Relinque et al. [28] found that students with suicide self-injury ideation often experience profound loneliness and that there is a significant positive correlation between suicidal ideation, suicidal planning, suicidal attempts, and loneliness. In summary, perceived discrimination may lead to school bullying, which in turn may result in suicide self-injury ideation, ultimately contributing to loneliness. This process reveals the profound impact of perceived discrimination on adolescent mental health through multiple pathways. School bullying is not only a catalyst for emotional distress but may also contribute to the development of more severe psychological issues by affecting adolescents’ emotions and cognition. The moderating role of teacher-student relationship

Research has shown that adolescents who receive effective social support exhibit fewer problem behaviors. For adolescents in adverse environmental conditions, social support is a crucial coping resource [29]. It can improve their self-evaluation, enhance their psychological ability to cope with negative environments, and directly buffer the negative impacts of external stressors, thereby playing a protective role in psychological and behavioral adaptation [30]. Ecosystem development theory posits that schools are significant microsystems influencing adolescent development, with teachers playing a crucial role in this process. In the absence of a positive teacher-student relationship, individuals may perceive more discrimination, and poor teacher-student relationships themselves may be perceived as a form of discrimination [31]. Conversely, a good teacher-student relationship can have significant positive implications, protecting students from issues such as depression, loneliness, and suicidal or self-injurious behaviors [32]. Additionally, research suggests that protective factors can mitigate the negative impacts of stressful events on the physical and mental development of children and adolescents [33]. This finding indicates that when adolescents feel devalued and hurt due to discrimination, a positive teacher-student relationship may provide a safe environment and help alleviate some of the harm caused [34]. Furthermore, studies show that low-quality teacher-student relationships can exacerbate the negative consequences of discrimination, as misunderstandings and interpersonal conflicts with teachers may place additional burdens on students [35, 36]. For many Chinese adolescents who live in boarding schools, their primary interactions and emotional exchanges are with teachers rather than parents. Therefore, the teacher-student relationship becomes a critical protective factor during this period [36]. A high-quality teacher-student relationship may help high-risk adolescents cope with and manage the negative emotions and behavioral responses resulting from perceived discrimination due to stressful events. Based on these findings, the following hypothesis is proposed:

Overall, the current study constructed a chain mediation model and a moderated mediation model to explore whether perceived discrimination among adolescents is associated with the occurrence of loneliness and to understand the statistical pathways underlying this association. Additionally, it seeks to determine whether there are factors that positively moderate this relationship. Addressing these questions will help suggest effective strategies for alleviating adolescents’ psychosocial issues (such as perceived discrimination, loneliness, and suicide self-injury ideation) and ensure their psychological well-being.

Materials and methods

Participants

The current study utilized convenience sampling to randomly distribute questionnaires. To ensure data quality, lie detection questions were included and responses were screened based on the following criteria: completion time less than 5 min, random answering patterns, and selecting the same option for all questions. The survey was conducted from June 2025 to July 2025. A total of 2,085 online questionnaires were distributed. At the designated testing sites, the schools uniformly arranged for students to complete the surveys, with all data collected in the school computer rooms. All participants were informed of the purpose and content of our study and agreed to participate. A total of 1,906 valid questionnaires were retrieved. After applying these filters, a total of 1,732 (valid response rate: 83.07%) middle and high school students from Jiangxi Province, China, including vocational high school students, were recruited. Among them, 886 were male (51.15%) and 846 were female (48.85%). Detailed demographics are presented in Table 1.

Measuring tools

Perceived discrimination

This study used the Perceived Discrimination Scale developed by Shen et al. [37] to measure the level of discrimination perceived by adolescents. The scale is comprised of 6 items, such as “I feel I have been treated unfairly,” rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (“completely disagree”) to 5 (“completely agree”). The total score is calculated by summing the scores of all items, with higher scores indicating a higher level of perceived discrimination. The reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s alpha) for this scale in the current study is 0.883.

Loneliness

This study employed the UCLA Loneliness Scale developed by Deol et al. [38]. This scale contains 20 items rated on a 4-point scale, where 1 indicates “never,” 2 indicates “rarely,” 3 indicates “sometimes,” and 4 indicates “often.” Nine items are reverse-scored. The total score is the sum of all item scores, with higher scores indicating greater loneliness. The reliability coefficient for this scale in the current study is 0.827.

Suicide self-injury ideation

This study designed a single question based on a review of numerous tools and the WMH Composite International Diagnostic Interview (CIDI) 3.0 [39]. The question is: “Do you frequently have thoughts of suicide or self-harm?” Response options are rated from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“severe”), with scores of 3 and above indicating a certain level of self-injury ideation. The assessment focuses on the participants’ self-reported levels. The assessment focuses on the participants’ self-reported levels. To improve the reliability of the measurement, we randomly recruited 353 adolescents from the participant pool to retake the single-item question. The results showed a retest coefficient of 0.73, indicating good measurement reliability for this item.

School bullying

This study used the revised Bear’s Delaware Bullying Victimization Scale by Xie et al. [40]. The scale employs a 6-point Likert scale, where 1 to 6 represent different frequencies of bullying from “never” to “every day.” One of the items, item 13, “I have been bullied at this school,” is not included in the dimensional scores but is used to identify if the respondent has been bullied. The original scale includes a separate dimension for cyberbullying, treated similarly in the Chinese version; hence, the actual scale used includes verbal bullying, physical bullying, and social/relational bullying [41]. This study combined the scores from all dimensions into a total score, reflecting the overall frequency and impact of school bullying. The reliability coefficient for this scale in the current study is 0.913.

Teacher-student relationship

Finally, this study used the Teacher-Student Relationship subscale from the “My Class” questionnaire developed by Jiang et al. [42]. Widely used in educational psychology, this subscale consists of 8 items rated on a 5-point scale (1 = “completely disagree” to 5 = “completely agree”). Higher total scores indicate better teacher-student relationships. The reliability coefficient for this scale in the current study is 0.952.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was conducted using SPSS 25.0. Some items in the questionnaire were reverse-scored and then converted before analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic information and the means and standard deviations of the study variables. Pearson correlation coefficients were employed to explore the relationships between perceived discrimination, school bullying, suicide self-injury ideation, teacher-student relationship, and loneliness. The SPSS PROCESS macro was used for mediation analysis [43]. Model 6 was utilized to examine the chain mediation role of suicide self-injury ideation and school bullying between the perceived discrimination and the loneliness. Model 7 was used to test the moderating role of the teacher-student relationship [44].

Results

Common method bias test

To control for common method bias (CMB), this study employed procedural measures such as anonymous responses and reverse-scoring of some items. The collected data were tested for CMB using Harman’s single-factor test. The analysis revealed that seven factors had eigenvalues greater than 1, with the largest factor accounting for 30.130% of the variance (which is less than the 40% threshold). Therefore, the results indicate that there is no serious issue of CMB in this study. Given the limitations of Harman’s single-factor test, we further conducted an unmeasured latent method factor (ULMC) analysis based on confirmatory factor analysis (CFA): a method factor orthogonal to the substantive latent variables was added to the original measurement model, with it loading equally on all items from the same source. This model was then compared with the baseline model without the method factor. The fit indices improved from the baseline model’s CFI = 0.89, TLI = 0.87, RMSEA = 0.061, SRMR = 0.073, to the ULMC model’s CFI = 0.93, TLI = 0.91, RMSEA = 0.049, SRMR = 0.055, indicating an improvement in fit. Based on the evidence from both CMB and ULMC, it can be concluded that common method bias did not dominate the primary effects in this study.

The moderated mediation role diagram

To further illustrate the moderating role of the teacher-student relationship between perceived discrimination and suicide self-injury ideation, the teacher-student relationship was divided into high and low groups based on one standard deviation above and below the mean. A simple slope test was conducted, and a simple effects analysis plot was created. Details are shown in Fig. 5. The results indicate that under conditions of a low-quality teacher-student relationship (M-1SD), perceived discrimination has a significant positive predictive role in suicide self-injury ideation (simple slope = 0.08, t = 18.45, p < 0.001). For individuals with a high-quality teacher-student relationship (M + 1SD), perceived discrimination is also significantly associated with suicide self-injury ideation (simple slope = 0.030, t = 5.49, p < 0.001). However, a high-quality teacher-student relationship more effectively mitigates the impact of perceived discrimination on suicide self-injury ideation compared to a low-quality teacher-student relationship. This result suggests that as the quality of the teacher-student relationship improves, the positive predictive role of perceived discrimination on adolescent suicide self-injury ideation gradually weakens. To further illustrate this moderating effect, we also used Johnson–Neyman (J–N) analysis to plot a continuous moderation graph (see Fig. 6). As shown in Fig. 6, the boundary point for the teacher-student relationship is approximately 1.56 (with the x-axis representing standardized, centered scores). In the range of x < 1.56, the gray shaded area indicates the 95% confidence interval for the slope, which does not include 0, suggesting that the conditional slope for “perceived discrimination → suicide self-injury ideation” is significantly positive. When the teacher-student relationship is ≥ 1.56, the confidence interval begins to touch/cross 0, and the effect is no longer significant. This means that by improving the teacher-student relationship to about 1.5 standard deviations above the mean, the immediate impact of discrimination on suicide self-injury ideation can be reduced to statistically insignificant levels. From a practical perspective, enhancing the quality of the teacher-student relationship is a key intervention target to reduce this risk pathway.

Discussion

Using a chain mediation model and a moderated mediation model, this study reveals how perceived discrimination leads to loneliness. The findings indicate that school bullying and suicide self-injury ideation serve as chain mediators in the relationship between perceived discrimination and loneliness (see Fig. 3). Furthermore, the teacher-student relationship moderates the first half of the mediation pathway in which perceived discrimination is associated with loneliness through suicide self-injury ideation (see Fig. 4). This result suggests that a high-quality teacher-student relationship is more effective in mitigating the association between perceived discrimination and suicide self-injury ideation compared to a low-quality teacher-student relationship (see Fig. 5).

The chain mediation effect diagram

This study reveals that the impact of perceived discrimination on loneliness among Chinese adolescents can be explained by the presence of school bullying, suicide self-injury ideation, and their interaction. Specifically, when adolescents perceive discrimination from others, they are more likely to become victims of school bullying, which mediates the development of loneliness. Additionally, perceived discrimination may lead to suicidal and self-injurious thoughts, which also mediate loneliness. Importantly, school bullying and suicide self-injury ideation do not predict loneliness in isolation but interact in complex ways. This interaction suggests that perceived discrimination not only directly affects loneliness but also indirectly amplifies the degree of loneliness through psychological mechanisms such as social exclusion and identity devaluation [6, 29]. As adolescents accumulate experiences of discrimination and bullying, they may develop a persistent negative self-concept, further intensifying their psychological isolation and emotional disconnection [31]. This finding aligns partially with Patra et al. [41], who found that school bullying is significantly associated with loneliness levels in adolescents, as is suicide self-injury ideation. Research has found that adolescents’ cognitive and attitudinal responses to perceived discrimination and school bullying could mediate the impact of such adverse stressors and environmental threats, with suicide self-injury ideation being a negative factor leading to maladaptive responses like loneliness [45]. This validates the application of the stress and coping theory, which suggests that perceived discrimination, as a chronic stressor, effectively triggers adolescents’ stress responses. These stress responses not only affect their emotional state but also interfere with their social adaptation and interpersonal relationships [3, 9]. Long-term experiences of school bullying can trigger a sense of helplessness, making adolescents less capable of coping with difficulties in life. This negative emotional experience may, in turn, intensify suicidal or self-injurious thoughts and behaviors. This cycle of negative emotions strengthens the cumulative effect of discrimination, where the intertwining of discrimination, bullying, and suicide self-injury ideation continuously exacerbates the deterioration of adolescent mental health. Numerous studies have shown that school bullying is a significant risk factor for suicide self-injury ideation and attempts among adolescents [24–26]. There may be a potential link between suicide self-injury ideation and loneliness. Suárez-Relinque et al. [28] found that students with suicide self-injury ideation often experience profound loneliness and that there is a significant positive correlation between suicidal ideation, suicidal planning, suicidal attempts, and loneliness. Loneliness, as one of the core manifestations of psychological distress in adolescents, is often closely related to their social adaptability. Adolescents experiencing perceived discrimination may feel more emotionally and cognitively isolated and rejected, making them more prone to negative emotions such as loneliness, which may ultimately develop into suicidal or self-harming behaviors [27, 46, 47]. Therefore, the findings of this study not only demonstrate the direct effects of perceived discrimination, school bullying, and suicide self-injury ideation but also reveal their interactive effects. This suggests that perceived discrimination, as an external stressor, triggers adolescents’ mistrust and rejection of their surrounding social environment, further affecting their mental health. School bullying and suicide self-injury ideation do not exist in isolation but influence each other through a complex mediating mechanism, intensifying loneliness. This aligns with previous research, confirming that perceived discrimination is not only a direct predictor of loneliness but also amplifies the experience of loneliness through multiple psychological mechanisms and social environmental factors [7].

The moderation role test shows that both high-quality and low-quality teacher-student relationship scores can moderate the impact of perceived discrimination on suicide self-injury ideation. As the quality of the teacher-student relationship improves, the predictive role of perceived discrimination on suicide self-injury ideation weakens, indicating that a high-quality teacher-student relationship more effectively mitigates the impact of perceived discrimination on suicide self-injury ideation. This result suggests that a good teacher-student relationship can serve as a protective factor against adolescent suicidal and self-injurious behaviors. A low-quality teacher-student relationship often lacks effective communication and understanding, which can exacerbate adolescents’ psychological distress and increase the likelihood of suicidal and self-injurious thoughts in the face of discrimination and exclusion. Conversely, a high-quality teacher-student relationship, built on mutual respect, trust, and understanding, creates a more positive and healthy environment. This type of relationship provides emotional support and psychological comfort, especially during times of stress and adversity. In such an environment, even if adolescents experience discrimination and harm, they can receive sufficient support and help from teachers to effectively alleviate negative emotions and reduce the risk of suicidal and self-injurious behaviors. This finding is consistent with that of Han et al. [48], who found that the teacher-student relationship, as a key social relationship established during school years, can significantly reduce psychological maladjustment or distress, such as distrust, shame, fear, embarrassment, and shyness. This result also highlights the potential role of teachers and other school staff who interact with students daily in identifying, supporting, and responding to adolescent mental health issues. Therefore, it is crucial to emphasize the importance of building high-quality teacher-student relationships to provide robust support for the healthy growth of adolescents.

In addition, a good teacher-student relationship can effectively mitigate the negative psychological effects caused by perceived discrimination, particularly concerning self-injury ideation and suicide risk. Therefore, we focused primarily on the pathway through which perceived discrimination affects adolescent mental health via self-injury ideation, exploring the protective role of the teacher-student relationship. The quality of the teacher-student relationship also appears to buffer the relationship between perceived discrimination and loneliness; however, in this study, we aimed to examine whether the teacher-student relationship moderates the relationship between perceived discrimination and suicide self-injury ideation. Moreover, the emergence of loneliness is influenced by multiple interacting factors; for instance, the impact of perceived discrimination on loneliness is more likely to occur through mediating pathways (such as school bullying and self-injury ideation). Compared to the moderating effect of the teacher-student relationship on perceived discrimination and loneliness, its moderating effect on perceived discrimination and suicide self-injury ideation may be more meaningful, as it can directly mitigate suicide self-injury ideation [49, 50]. This is particularly significant for preventing adolescents from experiencing direct physical harm, such as suicidal behavior [51]. Although loneliness is the outcome variable in this study, the practical significance of the moderating effect of the teacher-student relationship may not be as substantial as it is for suicide self-injury ideation. Therefore, the direct moderating effect of the teacher-student relationship on the association between perceived discrimination and loneliness did not emerge in our model.

This study reveals the complex relationships between perceived discrimination, school bullying, suicide self-injury ideation, and loneliness as well as highlights the moderating role of the teacher-student relationship. However, there are some limitations. First, the sample is limited to Chinese middle and high school students, who may not fully represent adolescents worldwide. Regional, cultural, and economic differences may predict the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the proportion of only children in the sample (9.8%) is significantly lower than the national average, which may lead to potential sample bias. The proportion of only children is higher among Chinese adolescents nationwide, and this difference could affect the research results, particularly the interaction between “only-child status” and “perceived discrimination.” Future research could further explore the relationship between this variable and core pathways by ensuring a more representative sample in terms of the proportion of only children. Second, the cross-sectional design of the study makes it difficult to establish causal relationships. Future research should consider a longitudinal design to more accurately reveal the causal relationships between variables. Third, the data mainly rely on participants’ self-reports, which may be subject to social desirability bias and recall bias. Future research should combine multiple data collection methods, such as observation, interviews, and experiments, to increase the reliability and validity of the data. Fourth, the study focuses only on school bullying, suicide self-injury ideation, and the teacher-student relationship without considering other potential moderating and mediating variables such as family support, peer relationships, and individual personality traits, which may also significantly impact adolescent mental health. Fifth, the current study did not control for the influence of family-level heterogeneity. However, study suggests that the association between perceived discrimination and psychological health is partially confounded by unobserved family-level heterogeneity [52]. Lastly, individuals with a stronger sense of loneliness are more likely to perceive discrimination. In existing research, we have primarily focused on the predictive role of perceived discrimination on loneliness. However, we also recognize the existence of a reverse causal relationship: lonely individuals may be more sensitive to negative evaluations or unfair treatment from others due to their internal psychological state [37]. Therefore, the relationship between loneliness and perceived discrimination may be interactive rather than merely unidirectional. Future research can adopt a longitudinal design to track the mental health development of adolescents at different time points, in order to verify the causal sequence between perceived discrimination, school bullying, suicide self-injury ideation, and loneliness. Longitudinal data will help reveal the causal relationships between these variables, thereby providing stronger evidence for the development of effective intervention strategies.

Based on these limitations, future researchers can improve and expand in the following areas. First, expand the sample to cover more regions and cultural backgrounds, thereby enhancing the generalizability and external validity of the findings. Second, adopt longitudinal research designs to track changes in adolescent mental health development over time and further reveal the causal relationships between perceived discrimination, school bullying, suicide self-injury ideation, and loneliness. Third, combine multiple data collection methods to reduce social desirability and recall biases, increasing the reliability and validity of the data. Additionally, future researchers should consider more potential moderating and mediating variables, such as family support, peer relationships, and individual personality traits, to further explore the statistical pathways in the relationship between perceived discrimination and loneliness. Moreover, specific intervention measures, such as training programs to improve the quality of teacher-student relationships and anti-bullying programs, should be designed and implemented. These measures should then be evaluated to verify their effectiveness in reducing adolescent loneliness. By addressing these improvements and expansions, future research can more comprehensively understand and explain the complex relationships between perceived discrimination, school bullying, suicide self-injury ideation, and loneliness, providing a stronger theoretical foundation and practical guidance for developing effective mental health intervention strategies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that perceived discrimination has a significant positive predictive role in loneliness among adolescents. School bullying partially mediates the effect of perceived discrimination on adolescent loneliness. Suicide self-injury ideation also partially mediates the role of perceived discrimination on adolescent loneliness. In conjunction, school bullying and suicide self-injury ideation form a chain mediation role in the relationship between perceived discrimination and adolescent loneliness. The teacher-student relationship score moderates the first half of the mediation pathway, in which perceived discrimination is associated with loneliness through suicide self-injury ideation. This moderation indicates that the quality of the teacher-student relationship may influence the extent to which perceived discrimination leads to suicide self-injury ideation and subsequently loneliness. These findings highlight the complex interplay between perceived discrimination, school bullying, suicide self-injury ideation, and loneliness, underscoring the importance of fostering high-quality teacher-student relationships to mitigate the negative impacts of discrimination on adolescent mental health.
