Tumours in children and adolescents with Turner syndrome and Klinefelter syndrome: a retrospective Chinese cohort study
Authors: Gaowei Wang, Meiye Wang, Jin Wang, Zhenhua Zhang, Karel Allegaert, Yaodong Zhang, Shuying Luo, Yang Fang, Yongxing Chen, Haiyan Wei, Dongxiao Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo analyse the incidence and characteristics of tumours diagnosed during childhood or adolescence in patients with Turner syndrome (TS) and Klinefelter syndrome (KS).MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on clinical information of children diagnosed with TS and KS between 2010 and mid-2024. Meanwhile, the number of children with concomitant tumours was determined, and the incidence was analysed.ResultA total of 23 individuals (median age 10 years, range: after birth to 16 years) with tumours were identified among the 657 children with TS and KS (median age=9 years, range: birth to 18 years), resulting in a tumour incidence of 3.50%. Within a cohort of 560 TS cases (median age 9 years, range: birth to18 years), 20 (3.57 %) were diagnosed with tumours. In a cohort of 97 KS cases (median age 4.3 years, range: 1–16 years), three (3.09 %) cases were diagnosed with tumours. The most common tumours in TS were osteochondroma (n=7) and haemangioma (n=6), with an estimated incidence of 1.25% and 1.07%, followed by gonadoblastoma (n=2), with an incidence of 0.36% in all TS and 4.44% in TS presenting with Y-chromosome material. In patients with KS, haemangioma (n=1), acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (n=1) and germ cell tumours (n=1) were observed. The co-occurrence of hepatic haemangiomas, mixed-phenotype acute leukaemia in TS, and the karyotypes (48,XXY,+21) of patient with intracranial germ cell tumours have not been previously reported.ConclusionEarly-diagnosed TS and KS present with a variety of tumours in affected children, with osteochondromas representing the most prevalent in early diagnosed TS.Keywords: Endocrinology, Adolescent Health, Genetics

Introduction

Turner syndrome (TS) and Klinefelter syndrome (KS) are the most commonly diagnosed sex chromosome abnormalities (SCAs) in humans. These conditions are caused by the absence (TS) or gain (KS) of an X chromosome and affect 50 in 100 000 females and 152 in 100 000 males.1 Individuals with TS may present with a reduction in stature and premature/primary ovarian insufficiency, as well as involvement of multiple organs, including cardiac malformations or renal disease.2 3 The clinical phenotypes of patients with KS are diverse, typically manifesting as small testes and azoospermia. Additionally, multisystem disorders such as neurological and cardiovascular issues were also reported.4 KS diagnosed in early childhood is more frequently associated with severe external genital anomalies.5

Congenital aneuploidies have been associated with an increased risk of specific tumours. These include SCAs such as monosomy X (TS), triple X, polymorphism Y and XXY (KS).6 7 In individuals with TS, an increased risk of melanoma and central nervous system (CNS) tumours has been reported.6 The incidence of germ cell tumours in individuals with KS, as well as in TS patients with Y chromosome material, has been found to be higher than that in the general population.8 9 Research has shown that patients diagnosed with KS have an elevated risk of breast cancer, while conversely, the incidence of breast cancer seems to be reduced in those with TS.3 4

A multidisciplinary approach is increasingly recognised as essential for the diagnosis and management of TS and KS. This comprehensive strategy encompasses the utilisation of growth hormone (GH) therapy and hormone replacement therapy (HRT), primarily aimed at achieving optimal height outcomes and mitigating the adverse effects associated with oestrogen deficiency.3 10 However, the long-term administration of GH and HRT has been associated with various potential complications, including an elevated risk of tumour development.11 Despite extensive research, the scientific community has not yet reached a consensus regarding this potential association.1 11 12

The increased susceptibility to tumourigenesis in TS patients has been attributed to multiple factors, including the absence of X chromosome material and the potential impact of HRT in female patients.13 14 Furthermore, tumours in TS patients exhibit diverse phenotypic manifestations and demonstrate variable incidence rates.15 In contrast to TS, KS often remains undiagnosed, particularly during childhood, resulting in a paucity of data regarding tumour incidence in this population.4 The precise mechanisms underlying tumour development in KS patients remain incompletely understood, with current hypotheses implicating several contributing factors, including hormonal imbalances, developmental abnormalities and impaired DNA repair mechanisms.15

Certain tumours may demonstrate higher incidence in TS and KS. However, considering that the majority of existing studies predominantly focus on adult populations with tumours in TS, and given that KS is less frequently diagnosed during childhood,4 16 there remains a significant gap in our understanding of tumour distribution patterns in childhood and adolescent populations with these conditions. The current study was designed to investigate the pathogenetic characteristics of tumours in children and adolescents diagnosed with TS and KS during their early developmental stages. Recognising that tumour development in TS and KS represents a lifelong risk, we anticipate that our research will provide valuable insights into the tumour profiles associated with these two forms of SCAs during the initial stages of diagnosis.

Materials and methods

The study population comprised children with a genetic diagnosis of TS and KS, obtained between 2010 and mid-2024. The patients with a genetic diagnosis were identified through the utilisation of sex chromosome testing conducted at the time of admission. Furthermore, individuals were referred to our hospital for consultation and received a diagnosis at an external medical facility. Clinical and genetic data were collected through a retrospective review of both paper and electronic patient records by our team for children with a genetic diagnosis of TS and KS. The aim of this review was to identify patients with concomitant tumours. The following data were recorded for the patients with tumours: genetic results, age at diagnosis of TS and KS, age at diagnosis of tumours, tumours type and descriptions and comorbidities during diagnosis. A statistical analysis was conducted to determine the respective incidences of the different types of tumours, with the data presented in terms of the percentage within each group.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Henan Children’s Hospital (2022-K-L020). The study was conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The Ethics Committee of Henan Children’s Hospital has waived the requirement for informed consent.

Patient and public involvement

This study was done without specific participation of patients or parents.

Results

A total of 701 children were diagnosed with a sex chromosome number or structural abnormality. Through retrospective analysis of both paper-based and electronic medical records, complete clinical documentation was obtained for 657 patients, encompassing both outpatient and inpatient records (table 1). The age at diagnosis ranged from birth to 18 years, with a median age at genetic diagnosis of 9 years old. Tumour characteristics and distribution patterns were systematically analysed across two distinct patient groups: 560 TS patients (median age, 9 years; range, birth to 18 years) and 97 KS patients (median age, 4.3 years; range, birth to 16 years). Within the TS cohort, cytogenetic analysis revealed mosaicism in 8% of patients (45 cases), with detectable Y-chromosome material. Furthermore, 15 TS patients underwent surgical gonadal exploration followed by comprehensive histopathological examination.

We identified 23 patients (3.50%) with concomitant tumours among the 657 children with SCAs (table 1). The tumour distribution showed distinct patterns between the two groups: 20 patients (20/560, 3.57%) were identified in the TS cohort, while three patients (3/97, 3.09%) were found in the KS group. A comprehensive database documenting each tumour patient was systematically compiled in an Excel spreadsheet, including detailed classification of tumour types and associated clinical data.

Tables2 3 provide a comprehensive overview of the clinical characteristics of patients with TS and KS, along with detailed information regarding concomitant tumour presentations. The analysis revealed distinct tumour incidence patterns, with osteochondromas (7/560, 1.25%) and haemangiomas (6/560, 1.07%) demonstrating the highest incidence. Gonadoblastoma (GB) showed an overall incidence of 0.36% (2/560) in the TS cohort, with a markedly higher incidence of 4.44% (2/45) in TS patients presenting with Y-chromosome material. The remaining patients with tumour in the TS group (n=5) exhibited diverse pathological manifestations, including single cases of lymphangioma, pilomatricoma, ovarian teratoma, pancreatic tumour and mixed-phenotype acute leukaemia (MPAL), each demonstrating an incidence of 0.18%. In the KS cohort, tumour presentations were identified in three patients, comprising haemangioma, acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) and germ cell tumours, corresponding to an incidence of 1.03%.

Discussion

The development of diverse tumour types in patients with TS and KS can manifest as early as childhood. To elucidate the tumour characteristics in patients with these two SCAs diagnosed at our institution over a 15-year period, we conducted a comprehensive systematic analysis. Our findings indicate that osteochondromas and haemangiomas were the most common tumours observed in the TS cohort. A previous study in Belgium involving 105 patients with TS (median age, 29 years; range, 2–69 years) indicated that these patients were at an increased risk of developing tumours in the CNS and on the skin.17 Our investigation identified several other tumours, including germ cell tumours (specifically GBs and ovarian teratomas), leukaemias and various other tumour conditions. Advances in our team’s understanding of such diseases and multidisciplinary diagnosis and treatment allow for more comprehensive identification of comorbidities in these patients. This systematic analysis was designed to characterise the early-onset tumour patterns in TS and KS patients and to establish a foundational framework for a more comprehensive understanding of tumourigenesis in individuals with these two SCAs.

Our findings indicate that the incidence of haemangiomas among patients in the TS group is 1.07% (7/560), and most of them were found after birth. They were observed to occur on various parts of the body, including the face, shoulder, foot, intracranially and the liver. Haemangioma is a benign tumour that is prevalent in infants and was defined in terms of infantile haemangiomas (IHs), which occur in as many as 5%–10% of infants.18 Previous review studies have indicated that children with TS may present with haemangiomas.19 20 Case report studies have demonstrated that these lesions can occur in various locations, including the feet, the dorsal metacarpal area and the left parotid gland area.21,23 Apart from occurring on the skin, lesions also occur in areas other than skin, such as the liver, parotid gland and larynx and very rarely the CNS.24 A review of the literature revealed no previous studies on liver haemangiomatous lesions in children with TS. Our case reports the detection of a liver haemangioma in a child during a medical visit for a diagnosis of TS due to short stature at 12 years of age. A comparison of the incidence of IHs revealed a significantly lower incidence in the TS group. This may be attributed to the distinctive characteristics of the selected cohort, which is predominantly composed of children and adolescents. The majority of IHs are small, innocuous, self-resolving, typically appearing within a few weeks after birth, with the tumour rapidly growing during infancy and then naturally regressing over several years.18 25 Thus, parents may overlook or underappreciate the minor lesions during the neonatal period. The cases we have observed and reported in this study are those diagnosed in infancy with extensive lesions that have not regressed or in children and adolescents with larger lesions. A comprehensive understanding of the changes in skin lesion characteristics from birth until TS diagnosis in children would be highly valuable. Such insight would contribute significantly to elucidating the incidence of haemangiomas in TS patients. In patients with KS, haemangiomas are rarely discussed in the medical literature. A review of the extant literature revealed only previous case reports describing.26 27 A haemangioma was identified in a single child in the XXY group, with an incidence of 1.03%. The lesion was located on the right shoulder.

In addition to the above tumours presenting as skin lesions, there are four other visible tumours lesions: pilomatricomas, cystic lymphangioma and superficial osteochondroma in the TS group. Pilomatricomas are the second most common skin tumours in children, accounting for 10%.28 They are most often observed in patients under the age of 20 years, with a predilection for the head, neck and upper extremities.29 The reported incidence of the pilomatrixoma was between 0.001% and 0.0031% of all dermatohistopathologic materials submitted for examination, according to a number of studies.30 A single case of pilomatricoma was identified in the course of our investigation (0.18%, 1/560), affecting the face of an individual aged 10 years and 9 months. The sex chromosomes were identified as X38%/XY27%/XYY35% through FISH testing. There is a paucity of reports concerning children with TS and pilomatricoma. A multicentre study from the USA indicated that the incidence of pilomatricoma in TS was 2.6% (8/311), with a mean age of pilomatricoma diagnosis of 7 years (age range: 3–15 years). The cohort included six patients of white race/ethnicity and 2 of Hispanic race/ethnicity.29 This phenomenon, at least in part, mirrors presence, but with variations in the incidence of pilomatricoma among patients with TS across diverse geographical and ethnic groups. Currently, only nine children with TS have been documented to have this lesion.31 This lesion is not a typical clinical manifestation of TS and is diagnosed concurrently with the diagnosis of TS. The diagnosis of this lesion may frequently be delayed in children with TS, with a median age of diagnosis of 15.5 years.31 On the basis of the evidence available, it may be possible to hypothesise that there is a potential association between pilomatricoma and TS. Besides, it is possible that there were some cases of underdiagnosis in the context of routine diagnostic evaluations. The incidence of pilomatricoma in patient with TS has not been sufficiently examined, and thus warrants further investigation in future clinical diagnoses. Cystic lymphangioma, also known as cystic hydatid, is an uncommon, benign congenital malformation of the lymphatic system, affecting 1 in 6000 newborns at birth and 1 in 750 spontaneous abortions.32 33 In previous studies, the majority of cystic lymphangiomas were identified during prenatal ultrasound examinations of pregnant women. Following birth, some children were subsequently diagnosed with TS following genetic diagnosis, and there is a suggestion that cystic lymphangioma is associated with TS.32 In our case under examination, one child (0.18%, 1/560) was diagnosed with cystic lymphangioma affecting the left lower limb at the age of 10 years, and TS was diagnosed 1 year later, with a karyotype of 45,X. The lesion manifested with pronounced oedema of the lower two-thirds of the left lower limb extending from the lower calf to the foot, with no sunken appearance and a localised smoothness to the skin surface that did not respond significantly to elevation.

In our cohort, seven patients (7/560, 1.25%) with TS were diagnosed with osteochondroma. Of these, six cases had lesions located in the tibia, and one had a lesion located in the sternum. Osteochondroma is defined as a benign bone lesion, which is a projection on the external surface of the bone. It may present with varying sizes and locations, with the knee, femur or tibia, ilium and scapula being the most common sites.34 The long bones represent their more frequent location, and the incidence of lesions in the proximal tibia was 15%–20%, while those in the sternum were rarer, occurring in less than 5% of cases.35 The prevalence of osteochondroma varies across different racial and regional populations, with an overall estimated occurrence rate of 0.44% in the general population.36 37 A comprehensive literature review identified one case of osteochondroma previously reported in TS and with an incidence of 0.95%.17 The median age at diagnosis of TS (median=11.1 years) is equivalent to the median age at diagnosis of osteochondroma (median=11.1 years) in our study. Overall, our study report more individuals with osteochondroma and a slightly higher incidence rate. Which indicates that patients with TS who present with tibial masses or pain require special attention. These patients should undergo enhanced imaging investigations to exclude the presence of osteochondroma. Patients with TS and a 45,X/46,XY karyotype are considered to be at potential risk of developing GB, with an incidence of 0%–100% and a 30% risk of malignant metastasis for GB, as evidenced by previous studies.8 38 The most recent guidelines for TS recommend that the optimal timing for gonadectomy should be determined by weighing the risk of developing GB against the potential benefits to gonadal function and fertility.3 In our cohort, 45 patients with TS were found to be accompanied by the presence of one or two Y-chromosome material. Of these, two had a diagnosis of GB, with an incidence of 4.44% (2/45). In comparison to previous studies,8 16 we report a relatively low incidence. This may be attributed to most patients with the X/XY sex chromosome in our study being identified via FISH testing. Among the 45 patients with TS, 41 were diagnosed by FISH and four by karyotype. The FISH technique proved more advantageous in detecting low proportions of mosaicism and potential Y chromosome material.2 39 A review of the data according to the criteria for surgical gonadal exploration revealed a positivity rate of 13.33% (2/15), which suggests a slightly higher incidence of GB. In conjunction with the aforementioned analyses, the utilisation of the FISH technique has led to the identification of a greater number of Y-containing mosaicism in TS, in addition to the continued necessity for the intensification of surgical gonadal exploration in children with Y-chromosome-containing TS. In our study of SCAs, in addition to the presence of GB, two other types of germ cell tumours were identified: an ovarian teratoma with X17%/XX83% sex chromosomes and an intracranial germ cell tumours with a karyotype of 48,XXY,+21. Ovarian teratoma is also classified within the category of germ cell tumours based on its aetiology and presents in a variety of forms.40 Ovarian teratomas can be classified into three main categories: mature teratomas (1.2–14.2/100 000), immature teratomas (0.05/100 000) and monodermal teratomas.41 The medical literature on the incidence of ovarian teratomas in patients with TS was limited. One case report described a rare left ovarian mature cystic teratoma in a patient with the 45,X (6)/46,XX(64) karyotype.42

A previous study estimated that the risk of developing a germ cell tumour for males with KS is 0.025%.43 Our case report describes one patient (1/97, 1.03%) with KS harbouring the rare 48,XXY,+21 karyotype, who was subsequently diagnosed with an intracranial germ cell tumour. A systematic review of PubMed literature revealed no reported cases of intracranial germ cell tumours in patient with 48,XXY,+21 karyotype. It is hypothesised that genes escaping inactivation might play a role in contributing to the KS phenotype and the elevated risk of germ cell tumours.15 43

Previous studies have documented the incidence of leukaemia in patients with TS and KS.44 45 The available literature on the incidence of leukaemia in patient with TS predominantly concerns acute myelocytic leukaemia and ALL.45,47 There have been no documented cases of patients diagnosed with TS developing MPAL. Our study presents a case of a 14-year and 7-month-old patient diagnosed with TS, exhibiting a mosaic karyotype of 45,X21 /47,XXX[69], who was subsequently diagnosed with MPAL approximately 2 years following the initial TS diagnosis. This case represents the sole instance of leukaemia in our TS cohort, corresponding to an incidence rate of 0.18% (1/560). Flow cytometric analysis of the patient’s bone marrow revealed a substantial infiltration of abnormal haematopoietic cells, with 70.8% demonstrating T-lymphoid lineage markers and 7.7% exhibiting myeloid differentiation. This distinct immunophenotypic profile, characterised by the co-expression of both lymphoid and myeloid markers in significant proportions, confirmed the diagnosis of MPAL according to WHO classification criteria for acute leukaemias. At present, there was no conclusive evidence from empirical studies to confirm a causal relationship between TS and specific types of leukaemia or to determine if patients with TS are more susceptible. Further investigation on this topic is warranted.

Studies revealed a significantly elevated risk of haematological malignancies in patients with KS compared with those with TS.6 While existing literature suggests no established association between ALL and KS,48 with an estimated incidence of approximately 0.11%.49 We identified one case (1/97, 1.03%) of ALL in a 4-year and 8-month-old patient with a 47,XXY karyotype who presented with fever and joint pain. Comprehensive molecular characterisation of the ALL case indicated: FISH analysis demonstrated 89% positivity for the TEL-AML1 (EVT6-RUNX1) fusion gene, while flow cytometric analysis identified 44.1% abnormal cells of B-cell lineage origin. RT-PCR quantification confirmed a TEL-AML1/ABL ratio of 86.59%, leading to a definitive diagnosis of B-cell ALL.

The incidence of pancreatic tumour varies across regions, genders and age groups, with a rate of 4.4 per 100 000 population in China.50 Our study also identified one rare case (1/560, 0.18%) of pancreatic tumour within the TS group. The case involves an 11-year-old patient diagnosed with TS following evaluation for short stature, who was concurrently found to have a pancreatic tumour. A comprehensive review of existing literature revealed extreme rarity of pancreatic tumours in paediatric TS patients, with only one previously documented case.51 Although pancreatic tumours do occur in TS, no conclusive link has been established. Long-term follow-up studies in future clinical practice are needed to gain a more comprehensive understanding.

In our study, we present a relatively large cohort to examine the characteristics of tumourigenesis in children diagnosed with SCAs at an early age, but there are still some limitations in our study. The cohort was derived from a single centre, which limits the generalisability of the findings. The omission of genetic data in this study was due to the fact that some of the genetic data had already been presented in previous studies.

Conclusions

The present study offers a more comprehensive account of the characteristics of tumours in patients with TS and KS who were diagnosed under 18 years of age. A higher number of osteochondromas and haemangiomas has been observed in TS. Rare prior studies have investigated the co-occurrence of osteochondromas in patients with TS. Additionally, the 48,XXY,+21 karyotypes of patient with intracranial germ cell tumours, as well as MPAL and hepatic haemangiomas with TS, have not been described. In conclusion, we present the more commonly occurring tumours that have previously been observed in TS and KS, including germ cell tumours and leukaemia, and also identified several rare tumour associations. In our subsequent study, we aim to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the characteristics associated with tumour occurrence in patients with TS and KS by extending the long-term follow-up of the cohort of patients we have acquired.
