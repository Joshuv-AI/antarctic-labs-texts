Effect of growth-promoting implants and revaccination timing on stress physiology and growth performance in male beef calves
Authors: S Lasso-Ramirez, M Londono-Mendez, V Ramirez-Sepulveda, A E M Silva, A Macias Franco, H A Lardner, G M Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHormonal growth-promoting implants are known to enhance growth performance in beef cattle, but their influence on vaccine response has not been studied extensively. Herein, we evaluated the growth performance and immunity of beef steers under different hormonal implant treatments and timing of booster vaccination. Fifty-nine crossbred beef calves were stratified by body weight (BW) and age (109 ± 18.9 kg and 70 ± 8 d of age) and randomly distributed into a completely randomized design with a 3 × 2 factorial arrangement to one of three implant treatments: (i) no implant (CON); (ii) short-term payout period implant (Synovex C; ST); or (iii) long-term payout period implant (Synovex One Grower; LT), and to one of the booster vaccination groups; at weaning (WV) or 7 days post-weaning (PWV). From implantation, and initial vaccination (d 0) until weaning (d 114), calves were raised with their dams in a single pasture. After weaning, calves were allocated to a single pen until d 63 post-weaning (d 177). Body weight was recorded, and blood samples were collected to analyze insulin-like growth factor, cortisol, β-hydroxybutyric acid, non-esterified fatty acids, leptin, serum antibody titers, and complete blood cell count. Peripheral blood lymphocyte concentration was greater in CON compared with ST and LT (P < 0.01), and greater plasma leptin (P = 0.01) in ST compared to LT, while CON was intermediate. A treatment × timing of booster vaccination (P < 0.01) was observed with greater peripheral blood monocyte counts in LT-PWV compared to ST-PWV and CON-PWV. A timing of booster vaccination × day interaction (P = 0.01) was observed for plasma cortisol with smaller concentration in WV compared with PWV at booster vaccination and d 28 post-booster vaccination. No main effects of treatments or treatment interactions were observed for serum antibody titers (P > 0.20). Implant treatment did not affect weaning weights; however, calves implanted with LT were the heaviest on d 142 (P = 0.03) and tended to have greater final body weights at the end of the study (P = 0.07). In summary, implant strategy and booster vaccination timing affected metabolic and immune parameters in beef calves but did not influence serum antibody titers. Growth performance was not affected at weaning; however, long-term-implanted calves were heavier post-weaning and tended to have greater final BW, indicating improved growth without compromising antibody-mediated immune responses.Keywords: bovine respiratory disease, cattle, livestock, vaccine, weaning

Introduction

In the pursuit of producing one calf per cow annually, a regular practice in the beef industry is to wean the calves approximately 180 d of age (Enríquez et al. 2011). The weaning process is associated to the introduction of many stressors, including dam separation, diet changes, exposure to different animals, new environments, vaccination, and other handling procedures (Weary et al. 2008; Richeson et al. 2019). The stress associated with those husbandry practices has been linked with a negative cascade of hormonal and, consequently, physiological disruptions (O’Loughlin et al. 2014). An increase in plasma cortisol and acute-phase proteins has been observed in calves following abrupt weaning (Hickey et al. 2003; Silva et al. 2018). Additionally, Lynch et al. (2010) reported that abrupt weaning in calves resulted in an increased neutrophil count and impaired their operational and phagocytic function, suggesting a reduction in immune homeostasis. Also, Snowder (2009) reported that reducing the phagocytic function of neutrophils increases the likelihood of bovine respiratory disease (BRD).

It has been observed that vaccine efficacy against BRD is reduced when animals are under elevated stress conditions (Blecha et al. 1984; Silva et al. 2018). Additionally, vaccination against BRD pathogens may cause a temporary reduction in total dry matter intake, thereby impairing growth performance (Rodrigues et al. 2015; Silva et al. 2018). Several strategies and technologies have been implemented to mitigate the adverse effects of weaning calves, aiming to reduce BRD incidence and improve their performance. Schumaher et al. (2019) concluded that altering the timing of vaccination and revaccination improved the serum antibody responses, reducing the BRD occurrence in feedlot cattle. Likewise, Silva et al. (2018) observed an improved humoral response when calves were vaccinated post-weaning compared with those vaccinated pre-weaning.

In addition to vaccination strategies, the use of growth-promoting implants during pre-weaning has been shown to improve growth performance during the suckling phase until weaning (Brad Jones et al. 2016; dos Reis et al. 2025). This might be partially explained by the counteracting effect of steroid hormones competing with glucocorticoids for binding sites in organs, thereby reducing glucocorticoid secretion and enhancing the production of insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1; Bryant et al. 2010). A review mostly compiled from mice, rats, and some human studies previously showed the potential for hormonal implants to stimulate antibody production due to T-lymphocyte suppression (Grossman 1984). For example, increased estrogen concentration has been associated with changes in the immune capacity of peripartum cows (Burton et al. 1993). Richeson et al. (2015) previously explored the interaction between growth performance, health, and vaccine response to different hormonal implanting timing; however, in their study, delayed vaccination and comparison between short and long-term payout implants were not investigated. As recommendations for hormonal implant use and reimplantation programs have evolved, implants with different payout periods have gained popularity because they provide greater management flexibility while reducing the labor associated with reimplantation. These hormonal implanting recommendations should be investigated for their potential to alter immune response to vaccines, and to the knowledge of the authors, their comparative association to immune response under vaccination at weaning and delayed vaccination has not been evaluated.

Even though the benefits of implanting suckling calves between around 30 to 80 days of age have shown improvements in growth performance (Mader et al. 1985; Brad Jones et al. 2016; dos Reis et al. 2025), limited research has evaluated whether implants can alleviate weaning stress or interact with different booster vaccination schedules to influence immune responses. Due to the difference in payout duration between the implants used in the current study, they were selected to evaluate their effects during the post-weaning period. Therefore, the objectives of this study were to evaluate whether the use of growth-promoting implants administered during the pre-weaning phase would interfere with physiological responses that improve stress resilience and growth performance at weaning and 60-d post-weaning. We also compared booster vaccination timing (at weaning vs. 7 days post-weaning) on vaccine response and assessed the interaction between implant treatment and vaccination timing on physiological and growth performance outcomes. It was hypothesized that administering growth-promoting implants and delaying the booster vaccination against BRD pathogens by 7 d post-weaning would mitigate the negative effects of weaning stress, thereby enhancing vaccine response, growth performance, and physiological status in calves.

Material and methods

The animals used in this study were managed in accordance with the guidelines established by the Canadian Council on Animal Care. The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Alberta (protocol #AUP00004301) approved all procedures for the present study.

Animals and management

Fifty-nine crossbred nursing male beef calves that had been castrated within the first days of life (body weight [BW]: 109 ± 18.9 kg; age on day 0: 70 ± 8 d) were stratified by BW and age in a completely randomized design with a 3 × 2 factorial arrangement of treatments. The two treatment factors were the implants; (i) where the control was not provided with a hormonal implant (CON; n = 20); (ii) a short-term payout period implant (Synovex C; ST n = 20; 100 mg progesterone/10 mg estradiol benzoate; up to 100 days; Zoetis Inc. Parsippany, NJ); (iii) and a long-term payout period implant with extended-release controlled by a pore-forming polymer coating (Synovex One Grower; LT; n = 19; 150 mg trenbolone acetate [TBA] and 21 mg estradiol benzoate; up to 200 days; Zoetis Inc. Parsippany, NJ), and the timing of booster vaccination (TBV), either at experimental d 114 (weaning; WV; n = 29; 10 from ST, 10 from CON, and 9 from LT), or at experimental d 121 (d 7 post-weaning; PWV; n = 30; 10 from ST, 10 from CON, and 10 from LT; Fig. 1). The two treatment factors were randomly assigned in a combination to experimental units (calves), resulting in six treatment combinations.

Experimental timeline from the beginning (d 0) to the last experimental day (d 177) where calves were sampled on d 0, 114, and 177. In addition the timeline shows the blood sampling relative to timing of booster vaccination (TBV), with groups receiving either the booster vaccine at weaning (WV) or at d 7 post-weaning (PWV), that were respectively sampled at d 28 after booster vaccine, WV (d 142) and PWV (d 149).

On d 0, all calves received their assigned implant treatment subcutaneously administered in the middle third of the back of the ear following the manufacturer’s recommendations. The calves also received the vaccination protocol, composed of a 2 mL dose of a modified live vaccine against Infectious Bovine Rhinotracheitis (IBR), Bovine Viral Diarrhea (BVD) types 1 and 2, Bovine Parainfluenza-3 Virus (PI-3), Bovine Respiratory Syncytial Virus (BRSV), and M. haemolytica Type A1 (Bovi-Shield Gold One Shot; Zoetis Inc. Parsippany, NJ), and a 5 mL dose of a vaccine against Clostridium chauvoei, Cl. septicum, Cl. novyi-sordellii, Cl. perfringens types B, C and D (Ultrabac 7; Zoetis Inc. Parsippany, NJ). Both vaccines were administered subcutaneously in the neck region according to label instructions. At booster time, the same vaccination protocol was administered to each subgroup on the respective day, in addition to treatment for internal and external parasites (Solmectin Pour-on; Solvet, Calgary, Alberta, Canada).

During the pre-weaning phase, cow-calf pairs were treated alike and grazed on a single pasture (∼ 150 hectares) comprised mostly of Kentucky bluegrass (Poa pratensis), smooth brome (Bromus inermis Leyss), and Western porcupine grass (Hesperostipa curtiseta), with free-choice access to water and mineral supplementation. On d 114 (∼ d 184 of age), calves were separated (weaned) from their dams and split into two groups (calves receiving vaccine at weaning or 7 d post-weaning) to assess the interaction between implants and timing of vaccine booster. Then, at weaning were housed in a single pen (∼ 1560 m2 total pen area) until d 177 of the study. During the post-weaning phase, calves were provided ad libitum access to a mix of alfalfa (Medicago sativa) and bromegrass (Bromus madritensis) hay, which was delivered in round hay feeders, providing adequate access space for the 59 steers. Hay samples were randomly collected from the bales on d 114 and d 121 and composited for chemical composition analysis by a commercial laboratory (Down To Earth Labs, Lethbridge, Canada). Sample collection and analysis

Blood samples were collected, and BW was recorded on d 0, 114 (weaning), 121 (d 7 post-weaning; PWV subgroup only), 142 (d 28 post-TBV; WV subgroup only), 149 (d 28 post-TBV; PWV subgroup only), and 177 (d 60 post-weaning; Fig. 1). Due to experimental conditions and herd management during the experiment, blood collection could not happen more regularly to better monitor the potential acute stress window during weaning (eg d 3 post-weaning) – the authors formally acknowledge this as a study limitation in interpreting the biomarkers. The final full BW was calculated as the average of BW on d 177 and d 178. Calves were weighed on two consecutive days at the end of the study to account for the absence of shrunk body weight measurements. Full double body weight is also commonly used in trials to replace shrunk BW. Subsequently, the ADG between all the timeline points was calculated. Blood samples were collected with the jugular venipuncture technique, using 20-gauge Becton Dickson Vacutainer needles. Three different tubes were collected as follows: 10.0 mL BD Vacutainer Lithium heparin tube for plasma, 2.0 mL BD Vacutainer K2EDTA tube for whole blood, and a 10.0 mL BD Vacutainer containing no additives for serum (Becton, Dickinson, and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Immediately following sample collection, lithium heparin and K2EDTA tubes were carefully mixed by inverting the tubes several times and then placed on ice. Subsequently, two plasma aliquots were obtained by centrifugation at 2050 × g for 20 min at 4°C, while whole blood was used for a complete blood cell count analysis. The serum tubes were kept at room temperature (∼ 60 min) to allow clot formation, and two serum aliquots were obtained by centrifugation at 2050 × g for 20 min at 18°C. Serum and plasma aliquots were frozen (−80°C) until further analyses.

Colorimetric ELISA assays were performed following commercial manufacturer instructions to analyze plasma concentrations of cortisol (E-EL-0158; Bovine/Sheep Elabscience Biotechnology Inc. Kampenhout, Belgium), leptin (EK760144; AFG Bioscience Northbrook, IL, USA), non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA; 999–34691; NEFA-HR, Fujifilm Corporation—Wako Chemicals, USA), bovine haptoglobin (E-10HPT; ICL, Inc., Portland, OR), bovine beta-hydroxybutyric acid (BHBA; CSB-E10056b; Cusabio, Wuhan, China), and insulin growth factor-1 (IGF-1; DG100B; IGF-1; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN). Inter and intra-assay coefficients of variation were ≤ 10.0%.

A complete blood cell count was performed as previously reported by Steyrer et al. (2022), in the fully automated Abaxis Vetscan HM5b Hematology Analyzer (Zoetis, Leatherhead, U.K.). Frozen serum samples were sent to a commercial veterinary diagnostic laboratory (Prairie Diagnostic Services, Saskatoon, Saskatchewan), and serum antibody titers against BVDV-1 were determined by a virus neutralization assay, reported as the greatest dilution of serum that exhibited optimal neutralization effects (ie the least and greatest tested dilutions). Antibody titers for BRSV and IBR were analyzed through ELISA and reported as negative (IBR: 0–2; BRSV: 10 or less), suspicious (IBR: 3–13; BRSV: 11–13), low antibody (IBR: 14–40; BRSV: 14–50), moderate antibody (IBR: 41–80; BRSV: 51–100) or high antibody (IBR: >80; BRSV: >100).

Blood titers were explored due to their correlative ability to common BRD detectable pathogens (O’Donoghue et al. 2025). For BVDV-1 seroconversion analysis, the serum titers were reported as the log2 of the greatest serum dilution that exerted a neutralizing effect. Thus, the samples with a virus neutralization value < 4 were considered negative and assigned a value of 0, while values ≥ 4 were considered positive and assigned a value of 1. These values (0 or 1) were used to calculate the percentage of calves with positive seroconversion (Artioli et al. 2015; Moriel et al. 2015; Silva et al. 2018). For IBR and BRSV seroconversion detection, the values reported as units by the ELISA immunoassay were used to calculate the increase in units from one time point to another. Accordingly, a difference of < 20 units was considered negative, whereas a difference of ≥ 20 units was considered positive seroconversion.

At weaning, each steer was fitted with an IceQube pedometer (Peacock Technology [formerly IceRobotics], Stirling, U.K.), attached in a vertical position on the left hind leg with a hook and loop strap to track activity behavior. At a frequency of 4 Hz, the pedometer continuously captured the activity of each steer for 28 d (from weaning to d 28 post-weaning), storing the data in their onboard memory. The activity behavior was determined using an internal sensor with a 3-axis accelerometer, which monitored three behaviors to ascertain the activity: standing time (when the sensor position was vertical), lying time (when the sensor position was horizontal), and step count (based on the number of times the calf lifts its leg, and the amount of force used). Once the pedometers were removed, the data was downloaded from the IceQube desktop application. A final report was generated, detailing the start day (year-month-day), start time (hh: mm: ss), and number of steps (n) within a 15-minute interval, which were used to calculate activity behavior (transition, standing, and lying times) per hour and day.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using a completely randomized design with a 3 × 2 factorial arrangement of treatments and tested for fixed effects of growth-promoting implants, TBV, and their resulting interactions, employing the GLIMMIX procedure in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). The individual steer was the experimental unit for all analyses, and steers within treatment were used as the random effect, and the BW at d 0 was included as a covariate to account for initial differences in BW. The normality of the data was tested through Shapiro-Wilk tests using the Univariate procedure in SAS. The BVDV type 1 titers were transformed using log base 2 to create logarithmic means before statistical analysis. For seroconversion, a binary (1 = positive, 0 = negative) logistic regression model was conducted using PROC LOGISTIC in SAS to evaluate the effect of treatment on the probability of seroconversion. Least squares means (LSMEANS) were estimated and back-transformed using the ILINK option, and pairwise comparisons of least-squares means were made with a Wald chi-square test using the DIFF and LINES options. Log odds for regression estimates and odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported for all comparisons.

Analyses from plasma samples, antibody titers, and behavioral measurements were analyzed as repeated measures and tested for fixed effects of growth-promoting implants, TBV, day (included as a fixed effect to evaluate temporal responses at defined sampling points), and all resulting interactions. Data from blood samples (plasma, serum, and whole blood) were analyzed relative to TBV (ie comparison between d 114 and d 142/149 [d 28 post-booster vaccine administration]), and relative to implantation day (ID; ie d 0 [implantation], 114 [weaning], and 177 [end of the experiment]). The covariance structure used for the repeated-measures analyses was selected based on the smallest Akaike information criterion. Data were separated using PDIFF if a significant preliminary F-test was detected. Post-hoc analyses were performed using Tukey’s test to evaluate pairwise differences between groups following significant ANOVA results. Significance was set at P < 0.05, and tendencies were considered if P > 0.05 and < 0.10.

Results

Metabolic stress and energy balance

No main effects or interactions were detected for plasma BHBA relative to the TBV (P > 0.14; Table 2). When analyzing data relative to ID, an implant treatment × day interaction was observed for plasma NEFA, with greater concentration on TBV in ST compared with CON, while the LT being intermediate (0.43, 0.31, and 0.29 ± 0.024 mEq/l, respectively; P = 0.04; Fig. 2a). A significant treatment × day interaction was detected for BHBA (P = 0.02; Fig. 2b), with greater concentrations in ST than in CON, with LT intermediate. An effect of day was also observed for NEFA (P < 0.01), with the least concentration on d 0, compared with d 114 and d 177, and for plasma haptoglobin (P < 0.01) which was greater on d 114 compared to d 177 and d 0 (Table 3). Plasma haptoglobin was also affected by TBV (P = 0.04; Fig. 2c), with greater concentrations in the PWV group compared to WV.

Serum antibody titers and seroconversion

No effects of treatment, day, or their interaction were detected for serum antibody titers against BVDV, IBR or BRSV (P > 0.20; Table 3), but a day effect was observed for IBR and BRSV (P < 0.01), with greater titers at d 28 post-TBV compared to the TBV. When data was analyzed relative to ID, there was no effect of treatment or treatment × day interaction for BVDV, IBR, or BRSV (P > 0.43; Table 3), but they differed across days (P < 0.01; Table 3). Antibody titers varied over time; BVDV and IBR titers were greatest on d 0, whereas BRSV titers peaked on d 177 (P < 0.01; Table 3). No significant treatment effects in seroconversion were detected for BVDV, IBR, or BRSV (95% confidence interval included; Table 4) either from weaning to d 60 post-weaning or from TBV to d 28 post-TBV.

Complete blood cell count

In the peripheral whole blood analysis, an implant × time of booster vaccination interaction was detected for monocytes (MON; P < 0.01; Fig. 3a), with greater concentrations in LT-PWV than in ST-PWV and CON-PWV. A similar interaction was observed for monocyte percentage (MON%; P < 0.01; Fig. 3b), which was less in LT-WV than in ST-WV and greater in LT-PWV than in the other PWV groups. For mean platelet volume (MPV; P = 0.04; Fig. 3c), values were greater in ST-WV and both LT-PWV and ST-PWV compared to their respective controls. A time of booster vaccination × day interaction was observed for mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC; P < 0.01; Fig. 3d), with greater values in WV than PWV at TBV. Platelet counts (PLT; P = 0.04; Fig. 3e) and procalcitonin (PCT; P = 0.04; Fig. 3f) were greater in PWV than WV at TBV. At d 28 post-TBV, mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH; P = 0.03; Fig. 3g) and hemoglobin (HGB; P < 0.01; Fig. 3h) were also greater in PWV than WV.

Discussion

The present study reveals that implant strategy and booster vaccination timing influenced metabolic and immune parameters in beef calves without altering antibody-mediated responses, indicating that growth-promoting implants did not compromise vaccine efficacy under the conditions evaluated. However, two main limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the statistical power to detect significant differences was limited by the animals available for this experiment, and therefore, additional research is warranted to further investigate the responses observed herein, and to determine if some of the tendencies become statistically different (ie 13–15 kg differences between LT and CON on d 121–177). Second, some of the tendencies should be interpreted carefully, our animals received a hay diet that did not meet the metabolizable protein requirements at weaning and therefore could serve as an explanation for the lack of differences observed. Nonetheless, we discuss other similar findings that report findings like ours which highlight that the findings we report and discuss are biologically meaningful. Richeson et al. (2015), reported a metabolic snapshot that we expand with additional biomarkers. Additionally, that manuscript compares implanting time, herein we explore different implanting treatments and a delayed vaccination, and therefore expand on previously explored published work that, to the knowledge of the authors, hadn’t yet been explored for Western Canadian environments.

These findings are particularly relevant within the context of weaning, a critical management practice in cow-calf operations. Weaning represents a complex physiological and behavioral transition, and both proper and improper management during this period can have detrimental effects on calf health and performance (Enríquez et al. 2011; Taylor et al. 2020). Consequently, optimizing calf performance and immune function is essential for the success of weaning programs and overall production efficiency. Despite its importance, weaning remains a challenging period due to the multiple stressors calves experience, including exposure to novel environments, abrupt dietary changes, and commingling with unfamiliar animals (Murray et al. 2016).

In addition, from birth to weaning, calves are subjected to physiological, management, and environmental stressors that can compromise health (Hulbert and Moisá 2016). To mitigate these effects, routine management strategies such as vaccination of cows and calves have been widely implemented to reduce disease risk and the need for treatment (Stokka 2010). However, the effectiveness of vaccination is influenced by several factors, including the animal’s stress status. Previous research has demonstrated that stress can impair vaccine responses. For example, Silva et al. (2018) reported that calves with elevated plasma cortisol concentrations immediately following initial vaccination against BRD exhibited reduced antibody production against bovine PI-3. This response is likely mediated by cortisol-induced immunosuppression, including inhibition of cytokine secretion by CD4 + T helper cells, which play a central role in antibody production (Salak-Johnson and McGlone 2007). Therefore, reducing stress during critical periods such as vaccination is essential to improve vaccine efficacy.

Some studies have shown that delaying vaccination after weaning can enhance the antibody response against BRD pathogens (Poe et al. 2013; Lippolis et al. 2016). Mediators of stress and immune functions that last up to 7 d after weaning (Lynch et al. 2010) may explain the positive effect of delaying vaccination. However, in the current study, it was found that TBV, set 7 d after weaning, did not affect antibody titers for BVDV, IBR, or BRSV, nor were they impacted by growth-promoting implants. Similar results were reported by Richeson et al. (2015), who reported that growth implant regime did not affect vaccine response in beef calves when implanted at feedlot arrival or d 14 or 28 after arrival. This appears to highlight that regardless of the delay (in booster vaccination or time of implanting), immune response might be governed by biological mechanisms that remain unaffected by timing of implanting or booster vaccination. As noted by Callan (2001), a new environment, changes in diet, management, and limitations in disease prevention during the two weeks after vaccination may reduce the development of effective immunity. Our findings may indicate that delaying the booster vaccination affected other biological parameters, suggesting that the timing of the booster relative to weaning can influence overall calf health.

Although seroconversion has been used as a reliable measure to evidence the immune activation against multiple viruses, there is insufficient information summarizing the timing from exposure to effective immune activation and time to concentrated seroconversion against BRD (Grissett et al. 2015). However, this is still used to measure vaccine effectiveness; for instance, Kirkpatrick et al. (2008) found a greater seroconversion in calves vaccinated against BRD pathogens at 67 and 190 or at 167 and 190 days of age when compared with non-vaccinated animals, which, in contrast, had a greater mortality. Also, Richeson et al. (2008) found that delaying vaccination by 14 d after the feedlot receive, the seroconversion was greater for IBR, suggesting an improvement in acquired immune response.

In this study, neither the treatments nor the booster vaccination time showed a difference in seroconversion improvement. At the same time, all the steers that seroconverted were equally distributed among the treatments and in both booster vaccination groups. However, our findings should further be evaluated in future studies to ensure consistent results with greater sample size. In contrast to other studies, in this research, all steers received the vaccine, and when the vaccine was delayed, it appears that 7 d are not enough to improve the immune activation measure by seroconversion. Similar to our results, Richeson et al. (2009) did not find a difference in the number of positive seroconverted animals, BW, or morbidity when vaccination was delayed. Also, Snyder et al. (2019) in a systematic review, evaluating 5 different modified live vaccines and delaying the time of vaccination 7 d or more after feedlot arrival, concluded there is no advantage or disadvantage to delaying the vaccination without increasing morbidity and mortality.

Although haptoglobin concentrations were greater in the PWV group than in the WV group, the difference remained within the normal physiological range (∼ 0 – 110.00 ng/ml), and did not indicate acute or chronic inflammation (Tourlomoussis et al. 2004). In contrast to our results, Richeson et al. (2009) reported no differences in haptoglobin levels when booster vaccination was delayed. Nevertheless, consistent with our observations, they noted an increase in haptoglobin concentrations during the first 14 d post-vaccination, without evidence of immune suppression. The temporal increases of cortisol in PWV indicate that booster vaccination 7 d post-weaning may be detrimental to vaccination efficacy. Similarly, the antagonistic effect of IGF-1 on glucocorticoids may explain the greater IGF-1 concentrations and reduced cortisol levels observed in the WV group on d 0 (Sacheck et al. 2004). Supporting this, Mccarthy et al. (1990) found that elevated cortisol levels negatively influenced pathways involved in IGF-1 synthesis. While growth implants are known to increase IGF-I concentrations (Johnson et al. 1998; Mader and Kreikemeier 2006; Smith et al. 2018), this effect is thought to result from estradiol (E2), a component of the implants. Estradiol influences the production of growth hormone-releasing hormone in the hypothalamus and enlarges somatotrophs in the anterior pituitary, which synthesize growth hormone, thereby increasing both growth hormone secretion and hepatic IGF-1 production (Smith 2010). However, in our study, implants did not affect circulating IGF-1 levels. The absence of detectable differences may have been influenced by the timing of blood collection, potentially limiting the ability to detect transient treatment effects. Nevertheless, greater IGF-1 concentrations were observed in the WV group on d 0. It was reported that IGF-1 supports anabolic processes and lymphocyte maturation, which are important for antibody production and immune response (Smith 2010). In addition, Smith and Johnson (2020) observed that IGF-1 reduced inflammatory markers and stress by reducing cortisol levels in steers implanted with TBA. These results are consistent with our findings, which may suggest that calves in WV group at TBV had a greater potential to trigger a greater immune response and to reduce cortisol levels, it was indeed observed in WV compared to PWV at TBV and at d 28 post-TBV.

Leptin is mainly associated with feed intake and metabolism, but it may also be classified as a proinflammatory cytokine, linking energy balance to immune function (Rodrigues et al. 2015). In our study, greater plasma leptin concentration was observed in ST calves and the WV group at TBV and at d 28 post-TBV. Houseknecht et al. (2000) highlighted the importance of the growth hormone-IGF-1 axis and leptin correlation in evaluating the nutritional and metabolic status of cattle, regulating growth and development. Growth-promoting implants, which enhance feed efficiency and ADG (Mader 1998; Brad Jones et al. 2016; Reichhardt et al. 2021), may therefore indirectly affect leptin dynamics through their impact on the growth hormone–IGF-1 axis. Supporting this, McMahon et al. (1998) noted that implants can help counteract the catabolic effects of proinflammatory cytokines during periods of stress or infection, further suggesting a link between hormonal regulation, immune modulation, and leptin levels. However, feed intake and fat deposition were not measured in this study and therefore warrant future investigation, as both can directly influence leptin concentrations.

Increased concentration of circulating NEFA occurs under a negative energy balance when a greater demand for nutrients stimulates fat mobilization (Waterman and Butler 2010). This increment in NEFA concentration has consequently been positively correlated with adipose tissue catabolism (Smith et al. 2018). Bryant et al. (2010) found that circulating NEFA was greater in heifers implanted with TBA and estradiol-17β. The increased concentration of NEFA observed in calves implanted with ST may be correlated with the presence of estradiol benzoate in this implant. In contrast, several authors have not found a difference in circulating NEFA under steroidal and trenbolone implants in steers (Enright et al. 1990; Parr et al. 2014; Smith et al. 2018). Non-esterified fatty acids and BHBA concentration have a positive correlation (Ribeiro et al. 2013). In the present study, LT calves exhibited greater BHBA concentrations early in the experiment (d 0); however, this difference did not persist throughout the experimental period and was not associated with implant treatment, as no significant differences were observed on subsequent sampling days. Therefore, the initial elevation in BHBA appears to reflect a transient metabolic variation rather than a treatment effect.

An elevated WBC counts may indicate the occurrence of pathogenic infection or improved ability to develop an innate or adaptive immune response against a foreign pathogen (Richeson et al. 2009). In the present study, calves receiving either ST or LT implant treatment had greater NEU% compared with CON. The NEU: LYM ratio (N: L) has been reported as an indicator of stress (Gross and Siegel 1983; McGlone et al. 1993). This relationship may be explained because NEU is the first line of defense against pathogens, while LYM are essential in cell-mediated and humoral immunity (Lynch et al. 2010). Although elevated NEU can be associated with an improved immune response, in this study, they did not translate into increased antibody titers, likely because the NEU elevation did not result in a disrupted N: L ratio that would indicate stress-induced immunosuppression.

Increases in monocyte concentration have been reported as indicators of stress, particularly during weaning (Van Engen et al. 2014). In contrast, in the current study, the MON concentration and proportion of WBC were greater in the PWV group, suggesting an elevated stress response compared to animals vaccinated at weaning. This may be due to the increased frequency of handling, as those animals passed through the squeeze chute more often, prolonging their exposure to stressors. Additionally, during the pre and post-weaning phases, no cases of BRD were diagnosed, and no animals were treated or removed due to health issues.

Although there were no differences in growth performance at weaning, starting d 7 after weaning and throughout the post-weaning phase, calves implanted with LT showed improved growth performance, resulting in greater BW. The mean difference expressed at the end of the experimental period (d 177) by LT was 17 kg more than CON and 7 kg more than ST. Although ST also had greater BW during the post-weaning phase, there was no significant difference with CON. Similar to our results, Gill et al. (1986) found that calf performance improved between 4.3 to 10% with a greater growth rate in calves (138 steers and 155 heifers; BW = ∼ 66.9 kg) implanted once or reimplanted with ST vs. no implanted calves. Twenty-four predominantly Angus steers (BW = ∼ 434.5 kg) receiving a combined trenbolone acetate and estradiol benzoate implant (same hormones as used in LT implants) showed increased skeletal maturity, greater carcass weight, and larger longissimus muscle areas (Smith et al. 2019). Likewise, improved ADG, greater dry matter intake, and enhanced gain efficiency in 240 crossbred yearling beef steers (BW = ∼ 365 kg) implanted with anabolic implants containing trenbolone acetate and estradiol benzoate (Smerchek and Smith 2020).

In agreement with our results, Silva et al. (2018) observed reduced calf growth performance in the post-weaning phase when calves were vaccinated 7 and 21 d post-weaning, coupled with a reduced frequency of energy supplementation. In contrast, Richeson et al. (2009) found no effect on ADG when vaccinating beef calves at stocker arrival or 14 d later. In line with this, Kirkpatrick et al. (2008) did not find differences in feedlot performance or carcass value when calves were vaccinated in the age range of 67 and 190, 167 and 190, or when they were not vaccinated. These contrasting results suggest that the effects of vaccination timing may depend on additional factors such as nutritional management, environmental conditions, and stress levels during the weaning transition. Though the lack of differences could be associated with some of the previously discussed limitations, the evidence of similar findings from previous studies indicates that management and weaning stress are drivers in animal welfare, health, and growth (Guasco et al. 2025). Additionally, the hormonal kinetics of the treatments studied contrast the delivery of a moderate and early growth promotion implant treatment and a prolonged-slow released delivery of androgenic effects through trenbolone acetate (Zoetis Inc. n.d.; Ball et al. 2022), which collectively should result in detectable performance differences. Our WV appeared to have a smaller detectable stress response, which likely explains their greater ADG than PWV animals over the d 121–142 period.

Weaning, combined with sudden changes in social, environmental, and physiological conditions, can significantly impact activity behavior. This period is often characterized by increased standing and walking times and reduced lying time in the first few days post-weaning, which may temporarily impair immune function (Enríquez et al. 2011). In our study, consistent with previous findings and regardless of the treatment, all steers were observed with increased activity, spending more time standing. However, activity declined progressively, with lying time returning toward pre-weaning levels, and on d 4 post-weaning, calves had resumed a normal activity pattern typical of healthy cattle (Robért et al. 2011).

The establishment of a regular activity budget was not correlated with the effects of growth-promoting implants, the TBV, or the time spent standing or walking during the first two days post-weaning. In addition, differences observed in blood parameters associated with the stress response were not reflected in changes in activity budgets during the first days after weaning. This may be due to the timing of blood collection (d 7), when the acute stress responses typically observed during the initial days post-weaning had likely already been regulated.

Conclusion

In this study, there was no evidence that growth-promoting implants influence vaccine antibody titers, even when the booster vaccination was delayed by seven days post-weaning. However, implants significantly altered several hematological and metabolic parameters, which could trigger multiple changes associated with immune response and physiological condition. Though limited by collection time points, which possibly missed the acute-stress peak for some biomarkers, and lacking experimental units to have greater power, our findings support that metabolic changes detectable in serum biomarkers occur in response to hormonal implants without affecting humoral immune response to vaccination. Growth performance was not affected at weaning; however, long-term-implanted calves were heavier post-weaning and tended to have greater final BW, indicating improved growth without compromising antibody-mediated immune responses.
