An Atypical “Hot-Phase” ARVC in a PKP2 Carrier
Authors: Cedrick Mutebi, Marius Chukwurah, Merije Toya Chukumerije, Oluwakorede Akele, Ranvir Raman Bhatia, Aubrey J Grant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC) is a leading inherited cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes. Rarely, ARVC may present with acute myocardial injury and troponin elevation, mimicking myocarditis and delaying diagnosis.Case SummaryA 20-year-old competitive female cross-country runner presented with exertional presyncope and emesis during a race and was found to have marked troponin elevation. History of Presentation

A previously healthy 20-year-old woman presented to urgent care after a near-syncopal episode during a competitive cross-country race. Approximately 2 miles into the event, she developed profound fatigue and was forced to stop running. While being escorted to the medical tent, she experienced a presyncopal episode accompanied by emesis, prompting administration of intravenous fluids. She subsequently presented to a local urgent care for further evaluation.

On arrival, she was hemodynamically stable with blood pressure of 102/67 mm Hg and heart rate of 62 beats/min, breathing comfortably on room air. A physical examination revealed intermittent premature ventricular contractions on cardiac auscultation with a normal S1 and S2 without any murmurs, rubs, or gallops. Electrocardiogram demonstrated normal sinus rhythm with a rate of 67 beats/min with multiple premature ventricular contractions, normal axis, and normal intervals. T-wave inversions were present in leads V1 through V3, with poor R-wave progression across the precordium (Figure 1). Initial laboratory evaluation was notable for a markedly elevated high-sensitivity troponin of 6,000 ng/L and an elevated D-dimer. Given concern for acute coronary syndrome and pulmonary embolism, she was started on intravenous heparin and transferred to our institution for further evaluation.

Twelve-Lead Electrocardiogram at Presentation

Twelve-lead electrocardiogram demonstrating sinus rhythm with intermittent premature ventricular complexes, T-wave inversions in the right precordial leads (V1-V3), and poor R-wave progression, consistent with underlying arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy in the inflammatory “hot phase.”

Past Medical History

The patient reported a prior episode of syncope earlier in the year that occurred during a running competition and was attributed at the time to inadequate caloric intake preceding the event. She had no known chronic medical conditions. There was no family history of unexplained sudden cardiac death.

Differential Diagnosis

In a young endurance athlete presenting with exertional near-syncope, ventricular ectopy, anterior precordial T-wave inversions, and marked troponin elevation, the principal diagnostic considerations included arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC), acute myocarditis, anomalous coronary artery-related ischemia, and idiopathic right ventricular outflow tract–mediated ventricular arrhythmias.

ARVC was strongly considered given the presence of exertional presyncope, premature ventricular contractions, and T-wave inversions in leads V1 through V3 beyond adolescence, a recognized repolarization abnormality associated with right ventricular cardiomyopathic remodeling. The endurance exercise context further heightened suspicion, as sustained high-intensity exercise is known to accelerate phenotypic expression and arrhythmic risk in ARVC.

Acute myocarditis was considered given the profound troponin elevation in the absence of ischemic ST-segment changes. However, the absence of antecedent viral symptoms and the presence of localized right precordial repolarization abnormalities raised concern for an underlying structural arrhythmogenic substrate rather than isolated inflammatory myocardial injury.

Investigations

Upon arrival at our institution, computed tomographic angiography of the chest was performed and demonstrated no evidence of aortic dissection, aortic aneurysm, or pulmonary embolism. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a normal left ventricular size and wall thickness, with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 55%. The right ventricle was mildly dilated with mild reduction in systolic function.

Given persistent clinical concern, the patient underwent right and left heart catheterization. Hemodynamics demonstrated a right ventricular pressure of 24/8 mm Hg, pulmonary artery pressure of 21/9 mm Hg, and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 10 mm Hg. Cardiac output and index by thermodilution were 3.85 L/min and 2.31 L/min/m2, respectively. Coronary angiography revealed normal epicardial coronary arteries without evidence of dissection, myocardial bridging, or obstructive disease (Figure 2). The high-sensitivity troponin levels subsequently peaked at 8,100 ng/L, and intravenous heparin was discontinued.

Coronary Angiography

(Left to Right) Selective coronary angiography of the right and left coronary systems demonstrating angiographically normal coronary arteries without evidence of atherosclerotic disease, coronary dissection, myocardial bridging, or anomalous origin.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) demonstrated a mildly reduced left ventricular ejection fraction of 49% with normal left ventricular wall thickness. The right ventricle was severely dilated with a markedly reduced right ventricular ejection fraction of 27%. Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) was present throughout significant portions of the right ventricular free wall, with absence of enhancement in the left ventricle, a pattern consistent with ARVC (Figure 3, Video 1). T2 mapping demonstrated a native T2 relaxation time of 47 ms, which was within normal limits. The absence of elevated T2 signal despite significant troponin elevation may reflect either resolution of acute myocardial edema by the time of imaging or a noninflammatory mechanism of myocyte injury related to mechanical stress in the setting of desmosomal dysfunction.

Cardiac Magnetic Resonance Images

(A) Short-axis and (B) 4-chamber late gadolinium enhancement images demonstrate severe right ventricular dilation with patchy subepicardial and midmyocardial late gadolinium enhancement of the right ventricular free wall (arrows), consistent with arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy. The left ventricle shows no enhancement.

Management

After CMR findings consistent with ARVC, the advanced heart failure and electrophysiology services were consulted. Given the patient's exertional presyncope, severe right ventricular dysfunction, and high arrhythmic risk profile, she underwent subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement prior to discharge, without complication (Figure 4). She was initiated on metoprolol succinate 25 mg daily for ventricular ectopy suppression and arrhythmic risk mitigation.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient subsequently established care in the advanced heart failure and sports cardiology clinics. Genetic testing confirmed a pathogenic plakophilin-2 (PKP2) variant (c.2437-1G>C). At the 3-month follow-up, she reported no recurrent syncope, presyncope, or dyspnea, and no ICD therapies had been delivered.

Cardiopulmonary exercise testing was performed to guide exercise prescription. Testing demonstrated preserved aerobic capacity, with a peak Vo2 of 40.2 mL/kg/min (109% predicted) and peak respiratory exchange ratio of 1.24, confirming maximal effort. The ventilatory anaerobic threshold occurred at a heart rate of 159 beats/min. Notably, the VE/Vco2 slope was elevated at 28.0, suggesting subclinical ventilation-perfusion mismatch related to right ventricular dysfunction. Postexercise monitoring revealed premature ventricular contraction couplets and bigeminy, underscoring ongoing arrhythmic substrate. Based on these findings, the patient was prescribed a target exercise heart rate zone of 120 to 150 beats/min, maintained at or below her anaerobic threshold. She was instructed to use a chest-strap heart rate monitor during all physical activity. Light resistance training (<50% one-repetition maximum) was permitted, with avoidance of Valsalva maneuvers. Competitive athletics and high-intensity endurance exercise were discontinued.

A personalized emergency action plan was developed that followed contemporary recommendations for athletes with genetic heart disease.1 Key elements included 1) exercise only in locations with accessible automated external defibrillators, 2) education of family members and exercise partners in cardiopulmonary resuscitation and automated external defibrillator operation, 3) establishment of a communication plan for ICD alerts or concerning symptoms, and 4) clear instructions to cease activity immediately for presyncope, palpitations, or chest discomfort.

Cascade screening of first-degree relatives was strongly recommended; however, formal genetic and clinical evaluation of family members has not yet been completed. Notably, there was no family history of sudden cardiac death, cardiomyopathy, or unexplained syncope. Surveillance CMR is planned at 6-month intervals to monitor for disease progression.

Discussion

Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy is a leading inherited cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes, with ARVC representing the most common subtype.2,3 ARVC is characterized by progressive fibrofatty replacement of right ventricular myocardium, creating a structurally and electrically unstable substrate for malignant ventricular arrhythmias. Although the estimated prevalence ranges from 1 in 2,000 to 1 in 5,000, penetrance is highly variable, and diagnosis often requires a high index of suspicion.2

The fundamental pathophysiology of ARVC reflects genetically mediated desmosomal dysfunction, resulting in impaired mechanical coupling between cardiomyocytes, myocyte detachment, and cell death under mechanical stress. Mutations in desmosomal genes, most commonly PKP2, disrupt Wnt/beta-catenin signaling, promoting adipogenic and fibrogenic transcription and progressive myocardial replacement.4,5 This patient was found to have the PKP2 pathological variant, the most commonly mutated gene, accounting for up to 45% of cases.6

ARVC classically presents with palpitations, ventricular arrhythmias, or exertional syncope. T-wave inversions in the right precordial leads are the most common electrocardiographic abnormality, whereas epsilon waves, though highly specific, are infrequently observed.6 Elevated troponin is uncommon in ARVC but has been described in “hot-phase” clinical presentations, which mimic acute myocarditis with myocardial injury in the absence of coronary disease.7 Hot-phase ARVC is a rare phenomenon: A large cohort study of 560 patients with ARVC found a 5% incidence rate of hot-phase presentation, with all patients presenting with chest pain.8

Genotype-phenotype correlations in hot-phase arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy have important diagnostic and prognostic implications. Hot-phase presentations are most commonly associated with desmoplakin (DSP) variants, with DSP accounting for approximately 69% of cases in large cohort studies.8,9 Nondesmosomal genes, including filamin C (FLNC) and phospholamban (PLN), have also been implicated in inflammatory arrhythmogenic phenotypes.9,10 Critically, left ventricular or biventricular involvement predominates in DSP- and FLNC-associated disease, whereas isolated right ventricular involvement is uncommon in hot-phase presentations.8,10 Our patient's presentation—PKP2 genotype with isolated right ventricular LGE and absence of chest pain—represents an atypical hot-phase phenotype and underscores the phenotypic heterogeneity that can occur even within established genotype-phenotype associations.

The diagnosis of ARVC remains anchored to the 2010 Modified ARVC Task Force Criteria.11 This patient satisfied 3 major criteria: severe right ventricular dysfunction, transmural right ventricular LGE, and pathologic precordial T-wave inversions beyond puberty. Although anterior T-wave inversions may be seen in healthy athletes, their presence in a symptomatic postpubertal endurance athlete warrants comprehensive cardiomyopathic evaluation.12

Once ARVC is identified, prevention of sudden cardiac death becomes paramount. High-intensity endurance exercise is a powerful disease modifier that accelerates structural progression of ARVC and increases malignant arrhythmic risk. Accordingly, contemporary guidelines endorse primary prevention ICD implantation through shared decision-making in high-risk individuals and strongly discourage competitive athletics in affected patients.13

Conclusions

This case describes an atypical presentation of PKP2-associated ARVC in a young female competitive endurance athlete presenting with exertional presyncope and marked troponin elevation in the absence of chest pain. The PKP2 genotype and isolated right ventricular involvement distinguish this case from the more commonly reported DSP-associated hot-phase phenotype with left-dominant disease. CMR proved essential in distinguishing ARVC from myocarditis. This case underscores the importance of multimodality imaging, genetic testing, comprehensive exercise prescription guided by cardiopulmonary exercise testing, and development of personalized emergency action plans in athletes diagnosed with genetic cardiomyopathy.
