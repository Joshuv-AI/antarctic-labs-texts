The Outcome of Tuberculosis Treatment in the Presence of SARS-CoV-2 Anti-RBD Antibody
Authors: Heni Muflihah, Fajar Awalia Yulianto, Winni Maharani, Anis Rapiq Hanipan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVaccination or infection with severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) can induce specific antibodies. The level of anti-spike receptor-binding (RBD) antibody may last long and interact with the immune response to tuberculosis (TB). We aimed to investigate the impact of the anti-RBD antibody level at baseline on the outcome of the intensive phase of TB treatment.Patients and MethodsThis cohort study recruited newly diagnosed TB patients from September 2022 to May 2023. Observation was conducted at baseline and after two months of TB treatment. Interviews and physical examinations were performed for patients with a history of Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) vaccination or infection and anthropometric data, respectively. Peripheral blood was collected for baseline assessment of anti-RBD antibodies, an interferon gamma release assay (IGRA), and complete blood count. The clinical outcomes of TB treatment include sputum conversion, hematology parameters and body mass index (BMI).ResultsAmong the 63 participants, the majority were vaccinated for COVID-19 (73,02%), underweight (58.73%), and IGRA positive (79,03%), increased median ESR (71.5 (IQR 18–111) mm/hour), and slightly low median lymphocyte proportion (19.5 (IQR 7–33) %) at baseline. The median titer of anti-RBD antibodies at baseline was 1,584.2 (IQR 95.6–34,379.3) AU/mL/. At two months of TB treatment, all the returned participants had sputum conversion and improved BMI and normal hematology parameters. The anti-RBD antibody level was associated with the baseline BMI (β =0.00019, 95% CI: 0.0001–0.0003, p=0.00) and post treatment lymphocyte count (β=0.0003 (95% CI: 0.0001–0.001, p=0.02).ConclusionOur study revealed that susceptible TB patients had favorable outcomes after 2 months of TB treatment when baseline anti-RBD antibody was high at baseline. We suggest that larger studies are needed to confirm the relationship.Keywords: anti-RBD antibody, COVID-19, interferon gamma, SARS-CoV-2 antibody, tuberculosis, treatment

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) has become the leading cause of death among infectious diseases since the Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. In 2023, Indonesia accounted for 10% of all TB cases and was the second highest contributor to the incidence of TB worldwide.1 Syndemic interactions between TB and COVID-19 have an impact on TB control and TB disease. As health care has been restored, some studies have suggested that the immune response to the COVID-19 pandemic may affect TB outcomes. The impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on TB diagnosis has been investigated.2 A study revealed that favorable TB treatment outcomes from 2020 to 2022 decreased by 49.8% in the area with the highest TB burden in Mexico.3 The disruption of TB services caused by the COVID-19 pandemic has contributed to this phenomenon.3 Sociodemographic factors such as living in a municipality with an indigenous population and low education level were associated with a lower rate of successful treatment in Mexico.4 A recent study revealed that those infected with TB and COVID-19 had a shifting predominance of genetic clusters of genotype strains of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), which affects the effectiveness of TB treatment.5 However, little is known about the impact of the immune response to COVID-19 on the outcome of TB treatment.

The outcome of TB treatment is assessed bacterially. The Indonesian standard uses the acid-fast bacilli (AFB) staining method to determine bacterial conversion at two months and six months after treatment. Clinically, improvements in chronic infection and inflammation can be assessed based on symptoms, body mass index (BMI), or hematology. Compared with healthy donors, active TB patients have lower lymphocyte counts and greater numbers of white blood cells and neutrophils.6 Delayed bacterial conversion after intensive-phase TB treatment was associated with a high pretreatment neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and low BMI.7 This study revealed that an impaired cellular immune response prior to treatment contributed to the outcome of TB treatment. The specific immune response to TB infection is measured via an interferon gamma release assay (IGRA), which measures the production of interferon gamma by lymphocytes in response to the TB antigen. There is limited information on whether the immune response to TB infection and the outcome of TB treatment are affected by the immune response to COVID-19.

The immune response following COVID-19 infection or vaccination is determined mainly by detecting the level of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) anti-spike receptor binding domain (RBD) antibody. The level of antibody might last long and remain detectable 180 days after the second dose of vaccination among health workers in Indonesia.8 The interaction effects of the immune response to TB or chronic respiratory infection on the response to COVID-19 vaccination have been investigated.9,10 These studies revealed that people with underlying TB or chronic respiratory disease had a lower antibody response following vaccination with inactivated COVID-19 vaccines than healthy controls did. Therefore, these populations should be prioritized for booster vaccination.10 Despite the evidence that pre-existing TB impaired immune response to COVID-19 vaccination, we have a lack of evidence that pre-existing immune response to COVID-19 alters the response to the TB treatment.

Compared with those of unvaccinated patients, the peripheral blood mononuclear cells of active TB patients who had previously received COVID-19 vaccination had a lower proportion of CD8+ T cells expressing activation markers in response to the SARS-CoV-2 peptide.11 This study suggested that COVID-19 vaccination might prevent excessive T-cell responses associated with unfavorable outcomes during reinfection. The high level of anti-RBD antibodies may lead to a shift in the capacity of host immune response towards B cell response and may impair T cell response that is critical for controlling TB infection. This could lead to a delayed bacterial clearance. We hypothesized that a high level of anti-RBD antibody prior to TB treatment would be associated with an unfavorable outcome of TB treatment. Deciphering the impact of previous COVID-19 vaccination or infection on TB should contribute to the decision of whether a particular intervention is required for post-pandemic TB control.

We aimed to explore the impact of previous immune response to COVID-19 on the outcome of TB treatment. The first objective was to assess whether the clinical outcome improved after two months of treatment in TB patients who had an antibody response to COVID-19. The second objective was to investigate the correlation between the level of anti-RBD antibody at baseline and the outcome after two months of TB treatment.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Patients

This was a prospective cohort study conducted in seven public health centers located in Bandung District, Indonesia. Newly diagnosed TB patients were recruited for this study. Observation was performed at baseline and two months after treatment. We defined baseline as an early TB diagnosis when patients had not initiated TB treatment or had been treated for less than 2 weeks. Observations included interviews, physical examinations, and blood collection. The outcome of two-month TB treatment was defined bacterially by the conversion result of acid‒fast bacilli (AFB) staining. The outcome was also evaluated clinically by measuring body mass index (BMI) and hematology results.

Newly diagnosed pulmonary TB patients were recruited when they visited public health centers for initial drug treatment between September 2022 and May 2023. The inclusion criteria were patients who were ≥18 years old, had positive and sensitive results from rifampicin-rapid molecular tests (Xpert MTB/RIF Assay) and had not initiated or had less than 2 weeks of TB treatment. Written informed consent was provided for those who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate in the study. Patients were excluded if they were diagnosed with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV).

Interview, Physical Examination, and Clinical Data

The participants were interviewed, physically examined, and clinical data were collected at two time points of observation. Prior to TB treatment, the interview collected information concerning a history of COVID-19 infection, COVID-19 vaccination, smoking, diabetes mellitus (DM), HIV, household TB contact, a history of TB and symptoms of TB. Physical examination included measurement of body weight and body height to calculate BMI and observation of a BCG scar in the upper arm. Clinical data from medical records, including TB diagnosis, TB drug regimen, and TB comorbid diseases (HIV, DM), were obtained. Two months after treatment, the interview collected information concerning TB symptoms, COVID-19 infection and vaccination during TB treatment. BMI was remeasured, and AFB data were collected.

Blood Collection, Hematology, and Antibody Assessment

Blood collection was performed at baseline and after two months of treatment. Complete hematology was assessed at the two time points, whereas the assessment for IGRA and anti-RBD antibodies was conducted once at baseline. At baseline, the following blood samples were collected: 4 mL from a heparin tube for IGRA (1 mL per tube), 3 mL from an EDTA tube for complete hematology and 2 mL from a plain tube for anti-RBD. The anti-RBD antibody was measured via a chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassay (CMIA). Anti-RBD antibodies were detected via the SARS-CoV-2 IgG II Quant Kit 6S60 (Abbot). Fresh samples were centrifuged and incubated in the presence of SARS-CoV-2 antigen-coated paramagnetic microparticles. The IgG antibodies to SARS-CoV-2 present in the sample bind to the SARS-CoV-2 antigen-coated microparticles. The mixture was washed, followed by incubation with an anti-human IgG acridinium-labeled conjugate. After a wash cycle, pretrigger and trigger solutions were added, and the chemiluminescent reaction was measured as a relative light unit (RLU) per the manufacturer’s manual from the ARCHITECT system. The interval analytic measurement was 21–40.0000 AU/mL, with a cutoff for a positive result of ≥50 AU/mL. Blood collection was performed at public health centers, and the blood samples were transported to the laboratory for assessment.

IGRA Test

The tuberculosis-specific immune response was assessed via an ex vivo IGRA method as previously performed.12 The assay was performed on whole blood samples from each participant via QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus (QFT-Plus QIAGEN, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the blood from the heparin tube was divided into each of the QFT-Plus blood collection tubes. The kit consists of four blood collection tubes: (i) nil tube (negative control: whole blood without antigens or mitogen); (ii) mitogen tube (positive control: whole blood with phytohemagglutinin (PHA)); (iii) TB antigen tube 1 (whole blood with peptides of ESAT-6, CFP-10 proteins simulating TB-specific antigens eliciting CD4); and (iv) TB antigen tube 2 (whole blood with peptides of ESAT-6, CFP-10 proteins simulating TB-specific antigens eliciting CD4 and CD8). The tubes were incubated at 37 °C for 16–24 hours. After centrifugation at 2000–3000 × g for 15 minutes, the supernatant was collected and stored at 2–8°C for a maximum of 28 days. The concentrations of IFN-γ (IU/mL) were measured via an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit from QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus (QFT-Plus QIAGEN, USA). The results were considered positive if the concentration of IFN-γ in the antigen-stimulated well was ≥0.35 IU/mL after the level in the nil well was subtracted and negative if the IFN-γ concentration in the antigen-stimulated well was ≤0.35 IU/mL after the level in the nil well was subtracted and the concentration in the mitogen well was ≥0.35 IU/mL. A result was considered indeterminate if (1) the blood sample did not respond appropriately to either the positive or negative tube and thus could not be interpreted; (2) the concentration of IFN-γ in the antigen well minus that in the nil well was <0.35 IU/mL, but the concentration of the cytokine in the mitogen well was <0.5 IU/mL; or (3) the concentration of IFN-γ in the nil well was >0.7 IU/mL, and the concentration of the cytokine in the antigen well was >50% greater than that in the nil well.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables are expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). The normal distribution was evaluated based on the standard deviation, median and mean relationship, skewness, and Shapiro–Wilk test. Student’s t test was used for the parametric analysis, and the Wilcoxon signed rank test was used for nonparametric analysis to compare the hematological data at baseline with those at two months after treatment. The association between baseline anti-RBD antibody levels, BMI, and hematology findings were analyzed using simple linear regression. We analyzed age and sex as potential confounding factors in the linear regression analysis.

Results

A total of 63 participants who met the inclusion criteria from seven public health centers were recruited, and none were excluded. At baseline, all the participants had data on their clinical history and characteristics collected from the interview and physical examination. The laboratory data were incomplete for IGRA (1 participant) and the anti-RBD antibody titer (6 participants) because of insufficient volume or quality of the blood sample. Fourteen participants did not return to the public health centers at the second time point because of death (1 participant), referral to the hospital (1 participant), or loss to follow-up (12 participants).

Hematology Parameters Changed After Two months of Treatment

Complete hematology was assessed at baseline and two months after treatment. Table 3 shows that, based on the referred range, the majority of hematology parameters were abnormal at baseline. These included a low median Hb level (11.7 (IQR 8.8–15.2) g/dL), increased median leukocyte count (10.1 (IQR 6.1–15.6) ×103/μL), increased median erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) 71.5 (IQR 18–111) mm/hr), and slightly low median lymphocyte proportion (19.5 (IQR 7–33) %). Compared with those at baseline, all the parameters were significantly different at two months after treatment. At this time, all the parameters were within the normal range except for the ESR. These findings provide evidence that the clinical outcome of TB patients, which is based on hematology parameters, improved after two months of TB treatment.

The Level of Baseline Anti-RBD Antibody Was Associated with BMI, Lymphocytes, Segment Neutrophils and the ESR

All the subjects had negative acid‒fast bacilli (AFB) staining results at two months after treatment. We evaluated the level of baseline anti-RBD antibody as the independent variable in association with clinical and immunological parameters at baseline and two months after treatment using multivariate simple linear regression analysis (Table 4). The level of anti-RBD antibody was positively associated with baseline BMI (p=0.00) with a small effect size (β =0.00019, 95% CI: 0.0001–0.0003). This represented for every 1 unit increase in the antibody titer, the baseline increased by 0.00019 kg/m2. The baseline level of anti-RBD antibody was positively associated with the level of lymphocytes after treatment (p=0.02) with the effect size 0.0003 (95% CI: 0.0001–0.001). In contrast, the level of baseline anti-RBD antibody was negatively associated with the ESR after treatment (p=0.04) and segment (p=0.05) with the effect size β=−0.001 (95% CI: −0.001–−0.00001) and β =−0.0003 (95% CI: −0.0006–7.31E-08), respectively.

Discussion

Previous COVID-19 infection or vaccination induces antibodies that may have an impact on the pathogenesis and outcome of TB treatment. Our study revealed that the clinical outcomes of newly active TB patients improved after two months of treatment in the presence of substantial levels of anti-RBD antibodies at baseline. Our study revealed that the baseline level of anti-RBD antibody was positively associated with the number of lymphocytes after treatment.

The level of anti-RBD antibody in newly active TB patients in this study was substantially high. The data for previous infection and vaccination for COVID-19 in our study were collected based on a history of positive rapid tests or PCRs for COVID-19 reported by the participants, as other studies conducted.13,14 We found that most patients were vaccinated, but none reported infection. We suggest that the high level of RBD antibodies in this study was induced not only by vaccination but also by community infection. There was limited access to laboratory examinations in the community because the patients were unaware of their symptoms or avoided restrictions due to confirmed COVID-19. A study recruiting COVID-19-recovered individuals and vaccine recipients visiting two hospitals in Makassar, the capital city of South Sulawesi Province in Indonesia, revealed that vaccination induced similar levels of anti-RBD antibodies.14 The study measured antibodies at days 0, 30, and 90 after the second dose of vaccination and revealed similar levels of anti-RBD antibodies regardless of preexisting immunity from infection. Our study found that almost all the participants had high level of anti-RBD antibody regardless COVID-19 vaccination history. However, we did not evaluate the response of the antibody against SARS-CoV-2 antigens. This antibody response might be different to T cell response. A study found that CD8+ T cells from COVID-19-vaccinated had higher frequency of CD8 T cells expressing CD69 and TNF than anon vaccinated active TB following stimulation with SARS-CoV-2 spike and non-spike peptide.11 Thus, vaccination and community infection during the COVID-19 pandemic have resulted in high levels of RBD antibodies in Indonesian TB patients, as shown in our study.

Our study revealed that abnormal hematology parameters at baseline improved after two months of treatment. At baseline, innate and adaptive immune responses in our study were reflected by increased leukocyte numbers with high monocyte, high neutrophil, and low lymphocyte proportions. A decrease in the number of peripheral T and B lymphocytes in active TB patients compared with that in uninfected controls was observed in general samples from cohorts except those from Indonesia, where there were no changes in the number of peripheral B cells.15 Our study measured the interferon gamma produced by Mtb antigen-specific CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells using IGRA QFT Plus TB1 and TB2 antigens. Antigen-specific CD4+ T lymphocytes producing interferon gamma are associated with protection against Mtb infection in mice.16 The impaired production or function of lymphocytes leads to the progression of TB disease. Possible mechanisms proposed for lymphopenia in severe TB include the tissue redistribution of lymphocytes and the inhibition of lymphocyte proliferation by macrophages.17 Chronic infection with Mtb provides prolonged weak activation signals that induce immature myeloid cells that induce pathologic activation.18 Monocytic (M) and polymorphonuclear (PMN) myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) are increased in the peripheral blood and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of TB patients.19 The level of MDCSCs in the peripheral blood is correlated with the severity of COVID-19 and inversely correlated with the lymphocyte count during hospitalization.20 The impaired cytokine response of monocytes and dendritic cells persists three months after the COVID-19 diagnosis, when the level of MDSCs is normal.20 Compared to the previous study in SLE patient receiving immunosuppressant, our study found a similar proportion of indeterminate IGRA in total SLE and healthy control subject. The proportion was higher than the quiescent cases (3.85%) but lower than the active cases (27.27%).12 Further study was required to confirm whether this finding was due to immune response interaction or the laboratory technique. Thus, previous COVID-19 vaccination or infection may contribute to the failure of the host immune response against chronic infection with Mtb

The host immune response to infection is affected by nutrient status. Several studies have reported conflicting results regarding the correlation of anti-RBD antibody levels with BMI. Our study revealed that the RBD antibody level was associated with the median, but not the category, BMI at baseline. Categorized BMI was not associated with the level of total anti-SARS-CoV-2 RBD antibody following CoronaVac vaccination among residents in Banda Aceh, Indonesia.21 A study in China revealed that the level of anti-spike RBD antibody was significantly lower in the obese group than in the other BMI-categorized groups.22 The latest study measured BMI from a medical history questionnaire completed in response to an invitation for the study via email. In contrast, some studies have shown that the level of RBD is positively associated with BMI. A population-based longitudinal study by COVIDENCE UK investigating determinants of the antibody response to the SARS-CoV-2 vaccine (ChAdOx1, AstraZeneca) or BNT162b2 (Pfizer-BioNTech)) revealed that higher antibody titers were independently associated with South Asian ethnicity, a higher BMI category, and pre-vaccination seropositivity.13 A prospective study in healthcare workers revealed that total antibodies against the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein were positively associated with BMI after two doses of the BNT162b2 vaccine.23 The mean (±SD) BMI of the participants in that study was 24.68 kg/m3 (±4.20 kg/m2), which was higher than the mean BMI in the present study. The level of RBD was positively associated with BMI in categories and continuous data.13,23 In the context of our study, a high baseline BMI may indicate that a well-nourished TB patient has a good immune response following COVID-19 vaccination. This contributed to the immune response against TB disease and improved outcomes after TB treatment.

A previous study found that a high monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio before treatment and a low BMI were associated with delayed sputum conversion in TB patients.7 Our study revealed that despite abnormal hematology parameters at baseline, all TB patients had sputum conversion and an improved systemic immune response after the intensive phase of TB treatment. We found that the level of anti-RBD antibody at baseline was positively associated with changes in BMI, lymphocytes, neutrophils and the ESR after treatment. We confirmed that age and sex were not confounders for the association. Our study found that the underlying immune response induced by a COVID-19 vaccination or infection has a minimal interaction on the clinical outcome of TB treatment. However, the interpretation of these findings must be with caution because of the lack of control group and modest number of participants. The extreme outliers of anti-RBD antibody with a small sample size limited the amount of degree of freedom in regression analysis. Without the negative control group, the association between the level of RBD antibody with the baseline BMI could be interpreted that people with healthier nutritional status naturally have a stronger immune response to prior COVID-19 as well as to current TB treatment.

The main limitation of our study is the small number of participants, which may limit the detection of differences and association. We did not have a control group with negative RBD antibodies because the study was conducted at the end of the COVID-19 pandemic, when vaccination was compulsory. However, we confirmed that sputum conversion, the main outcome of the intensive phase of TB treatment, occurred in all returned participants.

Conclusion

This study found that the majority of newly active TB patients had a high level of anti-SARS-Cov-2 antibody and positive TB IGRA at baseline of TB treatment. The antibody level was associated with BMI. After treatment, all returned subjects had improved clinical outcome based on sputum conversion, increased BMI, and increased lymphocyte count. A future study requires a control group and larger participants to confirm our findings.
