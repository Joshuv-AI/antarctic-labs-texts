The effect of an eight-week FIFA 11+ program on the landing error scoring system following functional fatigue in adolescent female soccer players with dynamic knee valgus
Authors: Fatemeh Asadi, Mansour Sahebozamani, Saeid Bahiraei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescent female soccer players exhibit a disproportionately high risk of non-contact anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries, with dynamic knee valgus (DKV) representing one of the major modifiable biomechanical risk factors, particularly under conditions of functional fatigue. The FIFA 11+ injury prevention program has demonstrated efficacy in reducing lower extremity injury risk, yet its impact on landing mechanics under fatigued conditions in this population remains underexplored. This study aimed to investigate the effects of an 8-week FIFA 11+ program on Landing Error Scoring System (LESS) scores following a functional fatigue protocol in adolescent female soccer players with DKV.MethodsThis parallel-group randomized controlled trial included 21 adolescent female soccer players (aged 13–17 years) exhibiting DKV, allocated to an intervention group (n = 11) receiving the FIFA 11+ program or a control group (n = 10) maintaining standard warm-up routines. LESS assessments were conducted before and after a validated youth soccer fatigue simulation protocol (YoSFS⁵) at baseline and after 8 weeks. A 2 × 2 × 2 mixed-design ANOVA examined the effects of group, time, and fatigue on LESS scores.ResultsSignificant main effects were observed for time (F1,19= 4.90,p = 0.039, partial η² = 0.205) and fatigue (F1,19= 73.49, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.795). Critically, significant interactions emerged for time × group (F1,19= 13.51,p = 0.002, partial η² = 0.416), time × fatigue (F1,19= 15.95,p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.456), and time × fatigue × group (F1,19= 4.62, p = 0.045, partial η² = 0.196). The intervention group demonstrated substantial reductions in LESS scores post-intervention, particularly under fatigued conditions (-34.2% vs. +3.1% in controls).ConclusionThe 8-week FIFA 11+ program significantly improved landing mechanics and attenuated fatigue-induced deteriorations in adolescent female soccer players with DKV. These findings highlight the program’s potential to improve the maintenance of landing mechanics during high-risk fatigued states, with potential implications for improving biomechanical factors associated with ACL injury risk during fatigue.Trial registrationIranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT), IRCT20250818066901N1, registered 24/09/2025.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-01906-3.Keywords: FIFA 11+, Anterior cruciate ligament, Dynamic knee valgus, Landing error scoring system, Fatigue, Female soccer, Injury prevention

Background

Soccer (football) is the world’s most popular sport, with over 13 million female participants globally, including more than 3 million under the age of 18 [1, 2]. While participation offers substantial health benefits, it carries a significant injury risk [2]. Injury incidence rates (IIR) among female soccer players stand at 3.4 per 1000 exposure hours. For U13 to U16 female age groups, match IIR has been reported at 12.67 injuries per 1000 h [1].

Among female players, the knee joint is the most common site of severe injuries, exhibiting a markedly higher incidence than in males [3, 4]. Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries are particularly prevalent in female athletes compared to other knee injuries [4]. This susceptibility may stem from sex-specific factors, including hormonal influences such as the menstrual cycle and anatomical differences that predispose women to ACL rupture [5]. Research indicates that female players face a 2- to 8-fold higher risk of ACL injury than males, irrespective of playing level or training intensity [4]. ACL tears account for 100,000 to 200,000 injuries annually among athletes, often requiring surgical intervention and resulting in high re-rupture rates, with long-term consequences like osteoarthritis [6].

The majority of ACL injuries in female soccer players occur via non-contact mechanisms, arising from both modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors [4]. Dynamic knee valgus (DKV) has emerged as a primary biomechanical risk factor for non-contact ACL injuries in this population [7]. DKV represents a multifaceted movement pattern characterized by medial knee displacement relative to the hip and ankle, involving femoral adduction and internal rotation, tibial external rotation, and ankle eversion. This pattern signals underlying lower-limb kinematic imbalances, including increased femoral anteversion, greater tibial torsion, diminished hip abductor strength, poor lower pelvic muscle control, heightened tibial mobility, wider pelvic structure, and excessive Q-angle. The broader pelvis in females and larger Q-angles, combined with reduced hip abductor force, heighten DKV susceptibility and ACL injury risk [8, 9]. Fatigue exacerbates these biomechanical alterations by impairing physical and cognitive capacities, thereby modifying movement patterns [7].

Fatigue constitutes a substantial risk factor for ACL injuries in soccer players, impacting knee joint mechanics multifactorially. Exhausted players exhibit diminished energy absorption during jump-landing tasks, elevating ACL vulnerability [10]. Fatigue also compromises neuromuscular control of the lower limb, reducing dynamic knee stabilization and optimal alignment maintenance [7]. Athletes experiencing over 20% fatigue demonstrate substantially elevated DKV [11]. Neuromuscular fatigue induces greater performance decrements in females, amplifying non-contact ACL injury risk [12]. Peripheral fatigue protocols, such as those targeting knee flexors and extensors, yield strong evidence of increased knee internal rotation angles at peak vertical ground reaction force (vGRF) during landing (effect size: 0.24–0.68) and reduced peak vGRF (effect size: 0.12–0.32) [13]. In unanticipated landings, fatigue prompts progressive sagittal-plane knee kinematic changes, diminished attentional control, prolonged reaction times, and increased knee abduction angles [14].

In recent years, various multicomponent warm-up programs have been implemented as interventions to reduce injury rates and target modifiable risk factors in athletes [15]. Among these, the FIFA 11 + program, developed by FIFA’s medical research center, has garnered substantial empirical support. Specifically designed for soccer players, FIFA 11 + is a structured injury prevention regimen that emphasizes muscle strengthening, balance, neuromuscular control, and landing technique correction, making it a highly effective strategy for mitigating ACL injury risk [16, 17]. The program incorporates dynamic warm-ups, neuromuscular control, proprioception, plyometrics, and core stability exercises. Together, these components address critical biomechanical risk factors associated with ACL injuries, such as poor landing mechanics and knee valgus collapse [16].

Previous research indicates that implementing this program can enhance fatigue resistance and improve landing mechanics in adolescent males [10, 18]. However, the majority of existing studies have predominantly focused on male athletes, healthy populations, or non-fatigued conditions. Consequently, there is limited information regarding the program’s impact on the landing mechanics of adolescent female soccer players with dynamic knee valgus, particularly under conditions of functional fatigue. Therefore, the present study was designed to determine whether an eight-week FIFA 11 + intervention significantly affects Landing Error Scoring System (LESS) scores following functional fatigue in adolescent female soccer players with dynamic knee valgus.

Methods

This study was a parallel-group randomized controlled trial with pretest-posttest assessments with experimental (FIFA 11+ intervention) and control groups. The primary aim was to evaluate the effects of an eight-week FIFA 11+ injury prevention program on Landing Error Scoring System (LESS) scores following functional fatigue in adolescent female soccer players exhibiting dynamic knee valgus (DKV).

Participants

A priori power analysis using G*Power software (Version 3.1) was conducted for a repeated-measures ANOVA. Based on a desired power of 80%, an alpha level of 0.05, an effect size of 0.25, and a repeated measures correlation of 0.50, a minimum required sample size of n = 24 was calculated. Participant recruitment and baseline assessments were conducted between July and August 2025. The intervention was implemented between July and September 2025.

Initially, 35 adolescent female soccer players (aged 13–17 years) from a local academy in Kerman, Iran, volunteered and were screened. Twenty-five players met the inclusion criterion of exhibiting DKV. Prior to baseline assessments, 4 participants dropped out for personal reasons, resulting in a final sample of n = 21 players. The screening, enrollment, and allocation processes are illustrated in the CONSORT flow diagram (Fig. 1).

CONSORT flowchart

Inclusion criteria were: (1) female soccer players aged 13–17 years; (2) at least two years of organized soccer training experience; and (3) no history of lower-limb injury or surgery in the preceding six months [19]. Exclusion criteria included goalkeepers (due to position-specific biomechanics), individuals with neurological or musculoskeletal disorders, and those unable to complete at least 80% of training sessions (tracked via coach attendance logs; i.e., missing more than two sessions) [8, 19]. Initial screening for dynamic knee valgus (DKV) was performed using the single-leg squat test (ICC = 0.93). Participants performed three trials recorded in the sagittal and frontal planes and analyzed using Kinovea (version 0.9.5). The sagittal view was used to confirm a minimum knee flexion angle of 60° before FPPA (ICC = 0.88) was measured from the frontal plane. Participants were included if their mean FPPA was between 16.3° and 32.4°, and/or if medial displacement of the knee joint center relative to the foot-thigh line was visually observed [20, 21]. This combined quantitative and qualitative approach was used to improve the clinical identification of DKV to increase clinical diagnostic accuracy and identify high-risk movement patterns in the absence of complex laboratory tools. To ensure equivalence in cardiovascular capacity across participants, a 20-meter shuttle run test was administered and the results are presented in Table 1. Following screening and dropouts, the final 21 eligible participants were randomly allocated to the experimental (FIFA 11+; n = 11) or control (n = 10) group (Table 1). An independent researcher, who was not involved in participant recruitment, assessment, or data analysis, generated the random allocation sequence using Randomizer.org. The assessor who administered and scored the LESS test remained blinded to group allocation throughout outcome assessment.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome measures assessed jump-landing mechanics evaluated with LESS, before and after the fatigue protocol, at both pre-test and post-test.

Testing procedure

Testing was conducted over two consecutive days at baseline and repeated over two consecutive days after the eight-week intervention. To control for diurnal variations and baseline fatigue, all testing sessions (both pre- and post-intervention) were conducted in the morning at 8 a.m. (11 participants on the first day and 10 on the second day of each phase). On the days of data collection, the participants were separated from the rest of the team and were completely exempted from their regular daily training. They were instructed to refrain from any physical activity prior to the assessments to ensure they were in a fully rested state before executing the testing protocol. Each session commenced with standardized instructions on the LESS assessment and the fatigue protocol. Upon arrival, participants’ height and body mass were measured using a calibrated stadiometer and a digital scale, respectively. Following a brief dynamic warm-up (5–10 min, including light jogging and joint mobilizations), participants performed three trials of the LESS test under non-fatigued conditions. The functional fatigue protocol was initiated immediately thereafter. Upon completion of the 5-minute YoSFS fatigue protocol, participants promptly proceeded to the post-fatigue LESS test to capture their fatigued biomechanics. This sequence was replicated at the eight-week follow-up for both groups. All assessments were conducted at the Shahin Soccer Academy in Kerman, Iran, by a single trained examiner to minimize inter-rater variability and ensure scoring reliability (Fig. 2).

Instruments and measures

Landing Error Scoring System (LESS)

The LESS is a valid and reliable clinical screening tool (inter-rater intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC] = 0.84 and intra-rater [ICC] = 0.91) for identifying individuals at elevated risk of non-contact anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries through evaluation of jump-landing mechanics. It quantifies movement errors associated with biomechanical risk factors, such as inadequate knee flexion and excessive knee valgus, which predict ACL injury risk. The LESS was administered before and after the fatigue protocol at both pretest and post-test sessions. Participants performed three standardized jump-landing trials per session. They started from a 30-cm-high plywood box positioned at a distance of 50% of their standing height from a marked landing line on the floor (Fig. 3).

Landing Error Scoring System test, from both Sagittal and Frontal plane view

Instructions emphasized jumping forward with both feet leaving the box simultaneously, landing beyond the line, and immediately executing a maximal vertical jump upon landing. Practice trials [2, 3] were permitted for familiarization, and invalid trials (e.g., vertical rather than forward jumps or failure to achieve maximal jump height) were discarded and repeated. Two smartphones (positioned 3 m anteriorly for frontal view and 3 m laterally for sagittal view) recorded movements at 240 Hz to capture frontal, sagittal, and general plane perspectives. Videos were analyzed offline using the LESS protocol, which comprises 17 items scored dichotomously (0 = no error; 1 = error) across three domains: sagittal plane (e.g., knee flexion displacement), frontal plane (e.g., knee valgus), and general strategy (e.g., overall landing softness and absence of transverse plane motion) [10]. Total scores ranged from 0 to 19, with scores ≥ 5 indicating higher ACL injury risk compared to scores < 5 [10]. The average score from the three valid trials was used for analysis [23]. Detailed LESS scoring criteria are provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Youth Soccer Fatigue Simulation (YoSFS⁵)

Functional fatigue was induced using the YoSFS⁵ protocol, a validated five-minute, high-intensity interval exercise designed to simulate match-induced neuromuscular fatigue in youth soccer players while targeting cardiovascular and muscular demands. This protocol incorporates multidirectional, sport-specific movements across four stations, emphasizing accelerations, decelerations, and soccer-relevant actions (e.g., kicking, jumping, heading, and sprinting with a ball) to replicate intermittent high-intensity demands of play [10]. Participants rotated through stations continuously for five minutes. Fatigue attainment was confirmed using rating of perceived exertion RPE ≥ 17 on the Borg 6–20 scale (i.e., “very hard”). Immediately after completion of the protocol, participants reported RPE. All participants reached the predefined fatigue criterion (RPE ≥ 17) immediately after the YoSFS⁵ protocol (Fig. 4) [10].

Intervention

FIFA 11+ injury prevention program

The intervention group performed the FIFA 11+ injury prevention program as a structured warm-up before each training session over an 8-week period. The FIFA 11+ program, developed by the FIFA Medical Assessment and Research Centre (F-MARC), was selected because this evidence-based program effectively reduces lower extremity injuries in soccer players through improved neuromuscular control, strength, balance, and proper movement patterns [16]. Participants began at level 1 for Part 2 exercises and progressed to higher levels based on proper technique and tolerance, as supervised by the research team (see Supplementary Material 2 for the detailed FIFA 11+ program description and illustrations). Compliance was monitored through session logs maintained by coaches, and correct exercise execution was emphasized to ensure fidelity to the protocol [15].

Control group

The control group performed the team’s usual warm-up for the same duration as the FIFA 11+ sessions (20–25 min), consisting of light running, dynamic stretching, mobility drills, and ball work. No structured neuromuscular, balance, plyometric, or strength components were included. Warm-up duration and content were monitored weekly to minimize contamination.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality assumptions were assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test (p > 0.05 for all variables). A mixed-model repeated-measures ANOVA was employed to examine the interaction effects of time (pretest vs. posttest), fatigue state (prefatigue vs. postfatigue), and group (FIFA 11+ vs. control) on LESS scores, with partial eta squared (η²) quantifying effect sizes (small: 0.01; medium: 0.06; large: 0.14). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustments were conducted for significant interactions (p < 0.05). Mauchly’s test verified sphericity; Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied when violated. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 35 players were screened and 25 met the eligibility criteria, 4 participants dropped out for personal reasons, resulting in a final sample of n = 21 players; and were randomized to the FIFA 11+ group (n = 11) or the control group (n = 10). All participants in the experimental group attended 100% of the scheduled training sessions, with no reported absences. No adverse events, injuries, or intervention-related harms were reported during the study period. All randomized participants completed baseline and post-intervention assessments and were included in the final analysis. No outcome data were missing; therefore, no imputation procedures were required. Table 1 presents baseline demographic characteristics by group and exact p-values. The Shapiro-Wilk test indicated normal distribution for body mass, body mass index, and VO₂max (p > 0.05). Age, height, and years of playing experience showed non-normal distribution in at least one group (p < 0.05). Independent samples t-tests compared normally distributed variables, and Mann-Whitney U tests compared non-normally distributed variables. No significant differences emerged between groups for any baseline variable (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the effects of an eight-week FIFA 11 + program on Landing Error Scoring System (LESS) scores after functional fatigue in adolescent female soccer players with dynamic knee valgus. The results showed that participants in the intervention group who completed the FIFA 11 + program demonstrated significant improvements in LESS scores compared to the control group. Importantly, the program not only improved landing mechanics in non-fatigued conditions but also reduced the negative effects of fatigue on landing performance. This finding has particular clinical relevance because a considerable proportion of ACL injuries occur during moments of fatigue [24, 25], especially late in training sessions or matches [26]. However, previous studies have rarely examined the effectiveness of training programs under fatigued conditions.

The improvement in landing mechanics observed in the intervention group aligns with previous studies showing that the FIFA 11 + program can reduce biomechanical risk factors associated with non-contact ACL injuries. Dix et al. (2021) reported that implementation of the FIFA 11 + program leads to improvements in knee alignment and landing strategy among young female soccer players [17]. Akbari et al. (2019) also reported that the FIFA 11 + program had a significant effect on landing patterns in soccer players by improving the LESS scores [27]. Bukry et al. (2024) also noted significant reductions in knee valgus moments during fatigue following an eight-week period of the FIFA 11 + program [10]. The present findings are consistent with this body of evidence and extend it by showing that the benefits of the FIFA 11 + program are not limited to non-fatigued conditions but also apply when neuromuscular performance is challenged by fatigue.

The strong detrimental effects of fatigue observed at baseline are consistent with previous literature. Kernozek et al. (2008) reported that fatigue reduced knee and hip flexion and increased knee valgus, patterns that elevate ACL loading [12]. Fidai et al. (2020) similarly found that fatigue leads to impaired proprioception and delayed muscle activation, contributing to poorer landing mechanics [11]. The baseline findings of the present study support these conclusions, as participants showed substantially higher LESS scores when fatigued. However, after completing the FIFA 11 + program, the intervention group exhibited significantly attenuated fatigue-induced increases in LESS scores, indicating improved maintenance of landing mechanics under fatigued conditions.

These improvements may be attributed to several neuromuscular and biomechanical adaptations induced by the multifaceted components of the FIFA 11 + program. Previous research has demonstrated that plyometric and neuromuscular training programs incorporating strength, core stability, and movement control exercises exert beneficial effects on improving LESS scores [28]. The FIFA 11 + program similarly integrates running, strength, plyometric, and balance exercises, accompanied by cues for lower-extremity and trunk alignment [27]. Its resistance-training components are specifically designed to enhance lower-extremity muscle strength [15, 29], which has been shown to be effective in reducing knee valgus [29]. Additional exercises target gluteal muscle strengthening, which contributes to a reduction in hip internal rotation—a key factor in dynamic knee valgus and a known risk factor for ACL injuries [30].

However, although alterations in neuromuscular control are likely contributing factors, variables such as specific muscle activation patterns (e.g., gluteal activation) were not directly measured in the current study. Furthermore, while the program yielded significant improvements in LESS scores, it is crucial to emphasize that LESS is a surrogate biomechanical marker. Because actual injury rates were not tracked, these findings primarily indicate an improvement in movement quality rather than direct ACL injury prevention.

Beyond the overall reduction in LESS, the intervention may have improved sagittal-plane landing strategy, particularly by promoting greater knee and hip flexion at initial contact and during the deceleration phase, which are typically viewed as protective movement features for the ACL. This interpretation is consistent with prior work reporting increases in peak knee flexion and reductions in transverse-plane deviations following FIFA 11+ –based neuromuscular warm-up interventions [16, 30]. Mechanistically, non-contact ACL injury events are frequently characterized by limited knee flexion combined with high anterior tibial shear and unfavorable multi-planar loading, and improving neuromuscular control and landing technique may mitigate these high-risk patterns [31, 32]. Notably, knee-flexion displacement and frontal-plane knee motion are explicitly captured within the LESS scoring framework; therefore, technique-focused training with structured progression and feedback may plausibly translate into fewer observable landing errors and improved movement quality [10, 23].

Through its structured training approach, the FIFA 11 + program promotes proper jumping, landing, and cutting techniques [10]. Research has shown that trainer feedback on movement quality facilitates rapid acquisition of new motor patterns, thereby reducing knee loading and the risk of ACL injury in soccer players [15]. The observed reduction in LESS scores may also stem from increased awareness of proper postural alignment during movement.

A key finding of the present study is that the FIFA 11 + program improved LESS scores even under soccer-specific fatigue. Acute fatigue is known to impair neuromuscular control and is frequently associated with increased frontal-plane knee motion (e.g., dynamic knee valgus) and poorer landing quality in youth athletes [11, 33]. Because FIFA 11 + is a structured neuromuscular warm-up program [34] and has been shown to improve knee valgus control and joint position sense in adolescent soccer players [15], it may enhance the robustness of lower-limb alignment and landing technique when athletes are fatigued.

Supporting this interpretation, an 8-week FIFA 11 + intervention has been reported to produce favorable changes in landing biomechanics assessed with LESS during induced fatigue in youth soccer players [10]. From a motor-learning and neurocognitive perspective, repeated exposure to technique-focused drills with appropriate feedback may help automate safer landing strategies, making movement patterns more resistant to fatigue-related decrements [35].

While our results demonstrate statistically significant improvements, establishing the clinical relevance of these changes (MCID) is paramount. Previous research established a critical clinical threshold, indicating that athletes with a LESS score of ≤ 5 are at a substantially higher risk for future non-contact ACL injuries. Therefore, a reduction in the LESS score that transitions an athlete from a high-risk to a lower-risk category, or a substantial reduction in baseline movement errors, represents a clinically meaningful change [27].

In our study, participants inherently presented with high-risk biomechanics due to dynamic knee valgus, which was further exacerbated by fatigue (e.g., baseline fatigued LESS score of experimental group: 9.30). Following the 8-week FIFA 11 + intervention, the experimental group demonstrated a significant mean reduction of 3.18 points under fatigued conditions. Furthermore, consistent with previous literature indicating that athletes with greater baseline movement errors exhibit superior therapeutic responsiveness to prevention programs [27], the FIFA 11 + program effectively improved the landing kinematics of our high-risk cohort. This substantial reduction in LESS scores indicates that the FIFA 11 + program provides not only statistical improvements but also a clinically meaningful reduction in biomechanical risk factors of ACL injury for adolescent female soccer players under physically demanding conditions.

From a practical perspective, these findings have strong clinical importance. Many ACL injuries occur toward the end of training sessions or matches when athletes are fatigued. The ability of the FIFA 11+ program to reduce fatigue-related deterioration in landing mechanics indicates that it may offer protective benefits during periods when athletes are most vulnerable. Although fatigued LESS scores did not reach fully “low-risk” levels, the substantial improvements observed suggest that regular implementation of the FIFA 11+ program could meaningfully reduce biomechanical risk factors of ACL injury in adolescent female soccer players, a population known to have disproportionately high rates of ACL injury.

Another finding of the current study was the deterioration of LESS scores in the control group over the 8 weeks could be due to neuromuscular fatigue accumulated from routine training, motor coordination disorders associated with puberty (13–17 years), and the effect of the small sample size. This indicates that regular soccer training alone is not enough and that targeted programs such as FIFA 11+ are necessary.

This study has several limitations. A primary limitation of this study is the slightly reduced sample size. Although an a priori power analysis indicated a minimum requirement of n = 24 participants to achieve optimal statistical power (80%), participant dropouts prior to baseline testing resulted in a final sample of n = 21. Consequently, the study may be slightly underpowered. This increases the potential risk of Type II errors, particularly when evaluating smaller effect sizes or the complex three-way interaction effects, and the results should be interpreted with this constraint in mind. The relatively small sample size and the single-center design of this study limit generalizability, and the absence of three-dimensional biomechanical analysis restricts our ability to examine detailed joint kinematics. The lack of long-term follow-up prevents assessment of effect retention, and the study relied on a surrogate biomechanical measure rather than actual injury outcomes.

A major limitation of the current study is the lack of control for biological maturation status. Given the participants’ age range (13–17 years), individuals were likely at varying stages of puberty. Rapid growth and maturation during this period significantly impact neuromuscular control, lower limb biomechanics, and joint laxity in female adolescents, which can inherently alter movement quality and injury risk. Because maturation indicators (e.g., Tanner stages or peak height velocity) were not assessed, it is possible that biological maturation acted as a confounding variable, influencing the magnitude of biomechanical adaptations observed in both groups. Additionally, we did not control for menstrual cycle phase which may influence neuromuscular control and fatigue responses in adolescent females.

Furthermore, fatigue was verified subjectively using the Borg rating of perceived exertion (RPE ≥ 17). The lack of objective physiological markers, such as heart rate, blood lactate concentrations, or objective performance decrements, limits our ability to completely confirm the exact state of neuromuscular fatigue. Future studies should incorporate objective physiological measurements to verify fatigue protocols. Moreover, there is a potential risk of control group contamination, as participants from both groups might have interacted. Finally, training and match load outside the intervention sessions were not objectively quantified, which could have affected fatigue-related outcomes.

In conclusion, implementing the FIFA 11+ program in adolescent female soccer players with dynamic knee valgus improved jump-landing biomechanics, as evidenced by reduced LESS scores under both rested and fatigued conditions. These biomechanical adaptations suggest a reduction in movement-related risk factors for knee injuries. However, future prospective studies incorporating longitudinal injury surveillance are required to confirm the program’s actual efficacy in preventing ACL injuries in this specific population. While our analysis revealed a significant three-way interaction between time, fatigue, and group, it is important to note that the p-value was marginal (p = 0.045). Therefore, these specific interactive effects should be interpreted with caution. Considering our slightly reduced sample size, future studies with larger cohorts are necessary to robustly confirm how the FIFA 11+ program modulates the combined effects of fatigue and dynamic knee valgus over time.
