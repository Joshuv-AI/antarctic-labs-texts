Cultural evolution accelerated human range expansion by more than two orders of magnitude
Authors: Charles Perreault
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceHumans spread across nearly every terrestrial habitat in a timeframe far too short to be explained by biological evolution alone. Humans thrive in environments ranging from Arctic tundra to tropical forests and deserts. This study shows that our global distribution cannot be explained by biology alone: achieving it through biological evolution would require tens of millions of years, thousands of species, and enormous variation in body size. Instead, cultural evolution has enabled rapid adaptation to diverse environments. At the same time, cultural diversity produces local specialization, with ethnolinguistic groups occupying smaller, more finely tuned territories than biological species. These findings quantify culture’s distinctive role in driving rapid human ecological expansion.

The human range encompasses virtually every terrestrial habitat on the planet, a feature that makes it an outlier in the animal kingdom (1–4). Following an expansion in ecological niche breadth within Africa around 70 ka (5), a subset of the human population had begun moving into Eurasia c. 60 ka (6–8). This dispersal was swift and wide-ranging. By 50 ka, humans had already navigated the deep ocean channels separating Asia from Sahul—a feat matched among terrestrial mammals only by a murid rodent (9). More strikingly, this same expansion soon carried humans into a variety of biomes and climates. In addition to the grasslands, temperate woodlands, and coastal zones occupied by earlier Homo species, Homo sapiens rapidly expanded its range into more challenging environments. By 45 ka, some groups were hunting mammoths in the Siberian Arctic (10), while others were exploiting Sri Lanka’s tropical rainforests (11). By the close of the Pleistocene, human foragers had colonized every major ecosystem—arid deserts, high-altitude plateaus, savannas, temperate forests, coastal margins, tropical jungles, and polar tundra—across Africa, Eurasia, Sahul, and into the Americas (12).

How did early modern humans adapt to these ecological habitats so rapidly? To expand into new ecological areas, a population must accumulate a certain amount of adaptive evolutionary change. In humans, this can be accomplished not only through biological evolution but also, crucially, through cultural evolution.

Most animals adapt to their environment through biological evolution, i.e., a combination of natural selection and phenotypic plasticity, resulting in morphological, physiological, and behavioral adaptations. Not surprisingly, signals of genetic adaptations have been found in humans (13–18), including physiological and metabolic adaptations to high-altitude zones (19), UV radiation levels (20), pathogen loads (21, 22), and cold weather and protein-rich diet (23).

But humans can also adapt culturally to their environment. Humans can learn from others, giving rise to a secondary inheritance system (24–27). Cultural evolution is a population-level process in which social learning among individuals and improvements across generations produce technological and behavioral adaptations, including social norms that sustain large-scale cooperation. These cultural adaptations can emerge without genetic changes, since cultural evolution operates on faster timescales than genetic inheritance (28). Cultural evolution is also reinforced by frequency-dependent learning biases such as conformity, which help preserve locally adaptive traits in the face of migration or mixing (24, 29, 30). These mechanisms enable human populations to accumulate beneficial innovations rapidly. As a result, human foragers deploy a vast array of food-getting technologies, clothing, shelter, and other habitat-specific cultural adaptations (31–37), resulting in a level of behavioral diversity among human societies that far exceeds that of any other species (38).

Cultural evolution likely predates the emergence of H. sapiens. Lithic assemblages from the Middle Pleistocene show levels of technological complexity consistent with cumulative cultural evolution as early as ~800 to 600 kya (39). This may explain how archaic Homo species like heidelbergensis and neanderthalensis occupied a more diverse climatic niche than earlier hominins—though still less diverse than that of modern humans—including cold habitats at the southern edge of the boreal zone (40–44). In fact, modern humans may have acquired some of the cultural adaptations that supported their expansions through interactions with other Homo species.

A growing body of empirical evidence highlights the distinctive contribution of cultural evolution to human behavior, one that cannot be reduced to individual cognitive capacities, high levels of intelligence, or causal reasoning alone. There is no relationship between relative brain size and geographic range across mammals (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), weakening the plausibility of a scenario in which human range expansion is attributable to encephalization alone, independent of culture.

Humans can also acquire adaptive traits without understanding their functional basis—an observation supported by theoretical models (45), experimental studies (46), and ethnographic observations (47, 48). The vulnerability of small, isolated populations to the loss of beneficial technologies further illustrates the dependence of adaptation on cultural transmission (49–52). In addition, cross-cultural variation in traditional societies reflects both ecological conditions and shared cultural ancestry (53–56). Thus, while the importance of intelligence and causal reasoning in the emergence of behavioral adaptations is debated (46, 57–60), the data unequivocally support the view that culture plays a distinctive role in human adaptation.

Yet, what role did cultural evolution play in the expansion of the human range, and what might that range have looked like without it? One way to answer this question would be to rewind the tape of history and seed Africa with a small population of noncultural hominins, and track how its range evolves over time. Here, I take a more practical approach: A macroevolutionary comparison that uses the combined ranges of terrestrial-mammal clades across various taxonomic ranks as a benchmark for range expansion achieved solely through biological evolution.

First, I place the human range within the broader context of terrestrial mammal distributions. It is widely recognized that humans occupy the most extensive geographic range of any extant mammal species. But how does that span compare with the ranges of whole genera, families, or orders? In other words, how far up the taxonomic ladder must we climb before a mammalian clade approaches the footprint of H. sapiens? I compare the human range with that of other terrestrial mammals across different taxonomic ranks in terms of spatial extent and ecological diversity.

Second, I ask how much cumulative evolutionary change—measured in terms of lineage age, number of speciation events, and body-size divergence—a mammalian clade would, on average, need to accumulate to match the geographic footprint of humans.

Third, I test the prediction that the faster pace of cultural evolution (28) enables humans to adapt to environmental variation at a finer spatial grain than would be possible through biological evolution alone. Rapid cultural change allows populations to respond to local selective pressures more effectively, because beneficial practices can emerge and become fixed before they are swamped by gene flow from other regions. This capacity is reinforced by parochialism and other ingroup biases, which limit the flow of cultural information across social boundaries, even when there is migration between groups. As a result, human groups can specialize in local environments without requiring geographic isolation, even in densely populated regions. Thus, ethnolinguistic groups—defined by shared language and close social ties that maintain high-fidelity cultural transmission—should occupy smaller and therefore more ecologically specific ranges than biological species.

Terrestrial mammals (hereafter, “mammals” refers to all nonhuman mammal species) provide a counterfactual for humans without cultural evolution because two key background variables are held constant by nature. First, biology: Humans and other mammals share fundamental biological similarities—a common Bauplan, core physiology, and life-history constraints—that make them comparable subjects. Second, environment: Humans and mammals have operated within the same environmental arena. Mammalian morphology and physiology are sufficiently flexible that mammals have repeatedly evolved specialized forms, behaviors, and diets that enable them, like humans, to occupy nearly every habitat on earth (61). The crucial difference lies in their cultural capacity; although many mammal species exhibit social learning, it never yields the open-ended accumulation of adaptations that characterizes human cultural evolution (62). By comparing humans to mammals, we can more clearly isolate the distinctive effects of cultural evolution on the expansion of ecological range.

Examining clades above the species rank allows us to track range expansions that unfolded over deeper evolutionary timescales. This is because each higher-rank unit integrates the cumulative geographic histories of all its member species from the moment the lineage originated. A further advantage of this macroevolutionary perspective is that it smooths over the idiosyncratic factors that shape the range of any one species—such as geographic barriers, locomotion mode, diet, and biotic interactions (63–69). These contingencies complicate direct comparisons between the human range and that of any single species, such as chimpanzees. Similarly, the macroevolutionary approach mitigates noise from imprecise range estimates and inconsistencies in taxonomic assignments, which arise because there are no standardized criteria for defining taxonomic ranks. Moreover, by focusing on orders-of-magnitude differences rather than precise quantitative estimates, this approach is naturally robust to noise in range estimates and taxonomic inconsistencies, as small measurement errors become negligible when the pattern of interest spans multiple log units. By averaging across the entire clade of wild terrestrial mammals, we can detect a signal of the evolutionary work necessary for range expansion in terrestrial habitats.

Results

The results show that the human range is larger than that of any species (Fig. 1). The human range is ~132.4 million km2, whereas the median species range is ~165,530 km2. Notably, the human range exceeds the 98th percentile of species range area (11.9 million km2) by 1.04 orders of magnitude. The human range is also larger than all genus, family, or order ranges and similar only to the range of the entire class Mammalia (~132.5 km2).

Ecologically, the human range is also vast (Fig. 2), encompassing more ecoregions (N = 804) than any other mammal range, regardless of taxonomic rank. The median number of ecoregions for a species is 7, and the human range encompasses more ecoregions than the 97th percentile of species ranges (N = 74) by 1.04 order of magnitude. The orders with the greatest ecological diversity—Carnivora, Rodentia, and Chiroptera—encompass 681, 746, and 772 ecoregions, respectively, while the class-rank range includes 795 ecoregions.

To estimate the amount of evolutionary work required for a terrestrial mammal clade to attain a geographic range comparable to that of humans, I modeled each of three variables as a function of geographic range using regression analysis (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Text S1–S6). Because the goal is to estimate average evolutionary patterns rather than predict individual cases, I focus on the posterior distributions of the model parameters and report credible intervals. The models predict that a terrestrial mammal clade with a range the size of the human range would be, on average, ~88 My old (95% CI: 53 to 139 Ma), include 2,178 species (95% CI: 842 to 5,024), and span 3.89 orders of magnitude in body mass (95% CI: 2.22 to 6.38). To put this last figure in perspective, a 3.9-order-of-magnitude difference corresponds to the mass gap between a 60 kg human and a 7.9 g little pocket mouse (Perognathus longimembris).

Human history, of course, defies these predictions. Humans emerged approximately 300,000 years ago (70, 71) and have remained not only a single species but also one that is genetically less diverse than a single population of wild chimpanzees (72, 73). Moreover, body mass across a sample of contemporary human foraging groups spans only ~0.26 orders of magnitude (SI Appendix, Materials and Table S1). These values for humans are smaller than the mean values predicted by the models for lineage age, species richness, and body mass range by 2.44, 3.35, and 3.63 orders of magnitude, respectively.

The human range remains exceptional even when compared to extreme outliers, such as the gray wolf (Canis lupus), the widest-ranging terrestrial mammal. C. lupus arose 1 to 2.5 Mya (74–76) and has since diversified into a dozen subspecies whose body masses span 0.43 orders of magnitude—nearly twice the span in humans (SI Appendix, Materials and Table S2). Yet, the species’ present range is only about half the area occupied by humans and encompasses just one-third as many ecoregions (N = 257). This smaller range cannot be attributed only to recent human-driven extirpation, as gray wolves have always been confined to the Northern Hemisphere.

Cultural evolution also allows humans to adapt to environmental variation at much finer spatial scales than biological evolution alone. Ethnolinguistic groups occupy far smaller territories than mammal species living on the same continents. Although ecological specialization can arise within mammal species—typically through geographic isolation and the formation of subspecies—the process is fundamentally different from the evolution of ethnolinguistic groups in humans. Only 24% of mammal species are polytypic (77), and among those, the median number of subspecies is three. In contrast, there are currently more than 7,150 languages in use worldwide (78). In Papua New Guinea alone, 840 indigenous languages are spoken (78).

The median range size for mammal species is 168,000 km2 in Africa and 234,000 km2 in North and Central America, compared to just 14,500 km2 and 12,200 km2 for ethnolinguistic groups—a difference of 1.06 and 1.28 orders of magnitude, respectively (Fig. 4). Cultural groups also exhibit less variation in territory size: The span from the 5th to the 95th percentile covers 3.2 orders of magnitude for African mammals and 3.1 for American mammals, but only 1.8 and 1.9 for ethnolinguistic groups in the same regions. These smaller territories also show lower ecological diversity, as measured by the number of ecoregions present (Fig. 5). The median number of ecoregions for African and American species is 7 and 14.5, respectively, and for ethnolinguistic groups is 2 and 3. This ecological homogeneity is further highlighted by the high proportion of ethnolinguistic territories with only one ecoregion: 23.9% in Africa and 12.7% in the Americas, compared to just 3.1% and 1.5% among mammal species. Taken together, these results suggest high levels of ecological specialization at the scale of ethnolinguistic groups.

One caveat to these results is that many species have recently seen their ranges contract under anthropogenic pressure: 26% of the species in the dataset now carry an at-risk conservation status (79). Their present distributions, therefore, almost certainly underestimate their historical range. Removing those at-risk species, however, leaves the species-rank patterns above (the species-rank boxplots in Figs. 1, 2, 4, and 5) essentially unchanged (SI Appendix, Materials and Figs. S7–S10). Addressing this issue at higher taxonomic ranks is more complex. Simply removing threatened species from a genus, family, order, or class would shrink the composite range and thus amplify, rather than alleviate, the bias. In the above-species analyses (including the regression models in Fig. 3), we expect the bias to be most pronounced in the lowest ranks, because redundancy among overlapping species ranges is minimal at the genus rank but increases toward family, order, and class. This overlap would provide a buffering effect: As taxonomic breadth widens, the contraction of any individual species’ range subtracts an ever-smaller fraction from the clade’s overall geographic footprint.

Discussion

Cultural evolution is a powerful engine of ecological adaptation. From a macroevolutionary perspective, recent human history resembles a classic adaptive radiation—an expansion across geographic and ecological space driven by adaptation and diversification—but one that unfolded culturally rather than through speciation. Human societies diversified behaviorally and technologically while remaining a single species with low genetic diversity. The analyses here provide a quantitative measure of that acceleration—an adaptive radiation powered by a nongenetic inheritance system.

The evolutionary work accomplished by cultural evolution during the human range expansion around the globe is immense, equivalent to what would have required biological evolution tens of millions of years, thousands of speciation events, and over three orders of magnitude in body mass divergence. Our analyses show that it compressed the human global expansion into a geological instant, enabling humans to colonize the globe in less than 1% of the average time biological evolution alone would have required.

Cultural evolution achieves this greater speed by sidestepping several constraints that slow biological change. Biological evolution can be limited by functional and developmental trade-offs, resulting in new traits replacing or compromising earlier ones, as when cetaceans forfeited terrestrial locomotion to become efficient swimmers. Cultural adaptations, by contrast, are largely cumulative (80); adopting dugout canoes, for instance, did not cost humans the technologies that support their terrestrial locomotion, like footwear. Moreover, cultural innovations can arise from intentional modifications rather than from the rare, random mutations that drive genetic evolution. Once innovated, cultural traits diffuse not only vertically, from parents to offspring, but also horizontally, obliquely, and one-to-many, spreading in an epidemic-like manner (24). Finally, cultural learning is also continuous across an individual’s lifespan and unconstrained by generation time, enabling changes within a single generation. This allows humans to adapt to environmental change with a speed normally accessible only to species with very short generation times, yet still enjoy the benefits of long lives, large bodies, and complex brains (28). The data on ethnolinguistic groups presented here suggest that cultural evolution mitigates another classic ecological tradeoff between generalists and specialists. This is achieved by allowing the human species to function as a generalist while simultaneously enabling specific groups to live as ecological specialists. Earlier work that compared changes in archaeological and fossil sequences suggested that cultural change proceeds about 1.7 orders of magnitude faster than genetic change—roughly a fifty-fold acceleration (28). Our macroevolutionary comparison, which uses geographic range area, finds the gap between the pace of culture and biology to be even wider.

This work contributes to a broader effort to build a quantitative science of human macroevolution (81). Advances in paleoenvironmental, genetic, and cultural data now allow explicit tests of evolutionary tempo and mode at the species scale. As empirical records continue to improve, the emerging field of human macroevolution will refine such tests and assess how culture reshapes the scaling laws that govern diversification, adaptation, and niche expansion in life on Earth.

Methods

The analysis is based on 5,986 georeferenced polygon maps of the ranges of nearly all extant wild terrestrial mammal species, the union of which produced 1,185 genera, 141 families, 24 orders, and 1 class range maps (in addition to a map of the human range) (SI Appendix, Materials). Ethnolinguistic group territories were collected from ethnographic maps for two regions: Africa (N = 832) and the Americas (North + Central America) (N = 583).

A Geographic Information System (GIS) approach was used to measure the spatial and ecological extent of geographic ranges. Spatial extent is calculated as total area in square kilometers (km2), while ecological diversity is quantified by the number of unique ecoregions a range spans (SI Methods). Ecoregions are defined as large land units (N = 825), each characterized by a distinct assemblage of natural communities and species, with boundaries that approximate the original extent of these communities before significant land-use changes (82).

Three correlated proxies were used to estimate the evolutionary work underlying mammalian ranges at various taxonomic ranks: 1) clade age, 2) the number of species within a clade, and 3) the range of body mass among those species. These three macroevolutionary properties, measurable at the lineage level, reflect the cumulative evolutionary change a lineage undergoes as it expands into new habitats. Greater divergence times provide more opportunities to accumulate beneficial genetic mutations, enabling lineages to diversify and adapt to new habitats, resources, or ecological niches. (83, 84), resulting in greater species richness and broader variation in body mass (85). Body mass is one of the most critical levers available to natural selection. Many key morphometric, physiological, metabolic, and life history traits are scale-dependent, with broad ecological implications (86–89). Famously, the mammalian radiation following the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction ~65 Mya involved significant changes in body mass (90).
