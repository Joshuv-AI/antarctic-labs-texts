More Photocatalysis With Less Light in Polar Semiconductors—The Role of Pyroelectric Fields
Authors: Balasurya Senthilmurugan, Amira Becheikh, Mohammad Bakhtbidar, Aparna Kharade, Daniel Gueckelhorn, Fiorenzo Vetrone, Andreas Ruediger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPhotocatalysis is an energy conversion process, where its efficiency is expressed as the ratio of formed or broken bond energy to solar energy. While the light‐induced charge transport behind photovoltaics and photocatalysis is the same, photocatalysis includes the additional step of adsorption/desorption. For the case of wastewater remediation, the conversion efficiency from solar‐to‐chemical energy is several orders of magnitude lower than photovoltaic. This underlines that the light‐induced charge transport is not the rate‐limiting step of photocatalysis and thus its optimization has very limited potential. Here, we present the case where modulated solar irradiation removes approximately 50% of photons compared to continuous irradiation, and still achieves an increase in photocatalytic performance. This overcompensation originates from the effect of pyroelectric fields from the polar BaTiO3 near the phase transition. Interestingly, it is not the absorption of the catalyst that triggers the relevant temperature changes but the absorption of IR radiation through water. Using methyl orange and rhodamine B as model pollutants and BaTiO3 nanoparticles as a photocatalyst, we demonstrate the proof of concept and provide quantitative consistency as a function of particle size that governs the phase transition temperature, as a function of offset temperature to approach the phase transition, and through control experiments without IR contributions.

Introduction

Photocatalysis is an energy conversion process where, in most cases, solar energy is converted into chemical energy, which is expressed as either the formation or breaking of bonds. As in any energy conversion process, the overall conversion efficiency is the most critical benchmark to express the significance of the process. Generally, across the literature on photocatalysis, especially in the case of degradation processes, the overall conversion efficiency is not reported. While several review articles state recommendations for best practice [1], the difficulty of a direct comparison between references has hidden this observation for a long time. The challenge to compare results includes but is not limited to non‐standardized pollutants, different concentrations of pollutants and catalysts to the point that affects the reaction kinetics, different soaking times before illumination, different irradiation times, and even different light sources, to eventually determine a degree of decomposition of the pollutant.

The interest in photocatalysis in recent years has been driven by the need to transform products that are both chemically and photostable and which play a major role in global wastewater contamination, where a major source of contamination is due to dye molecules from the textile industry [2, 3, 4]. Recently, catalysis has gained attention to treat contaminated water bodies [5, 6], where a multitude of new materials have been suggested, while only very few of them are scalable. In addition to new materials, different beneficial physical effects were invoked, including plasmon‐assisted, piezo‐, pyro‐, sono‐, and tribo‐catalysis [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Plasmon‐assisted catalysis relies on the use of noble‐metal nanoparticles and is facing challenges of scalability for economic reasons, while piezo‐, sono‐, and tribo‐catalysis rely on the mechanical activation of the solvent, making it energetically unfavorable. Pyroelectrically assisted photocatalysis (pyro‐photocatalysis for brevity) exploits temperature fluctuations or deliberate changes to generate a local electric field on the surface of the catalyst for performance enhancement [12]. For the case of external electric heating, the scalability is poor due to the large heat capacity of water and the associated costs, similar to piezo‐, tribo, and sonocatalysis.

The concept of pyro‐photocatalysis relies on the photocatalysis generating radicals to trigger reduction and oxidation reactions through superoxide (•O2 −), hydroxide (•OH), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) formation, which promotes catalytic reactions [13, 14, 15] near the surface, while the pyroelectric effect generates a local electric field that promotes the mass transport of ionic species to and/or from the catalyst surface. The generated reactive oxygen species (ROS) can be utilized for various applications, including water treatment, water splitting, and also for antimicrobial desinfaction. Wu et al. presented the pyro‐electric effect on the multiferroic material BiFeO3, which was tested on the degradation of Rhodamine B and observed the complete decomposition after 85 relatively slow thermal cycles between 27°C and 38°C.

The importance of mass transport is not only derived from the low conversion efficiency of photocatalysis as compared to photovoltaics, but it also manifests itself through experiments like that of Westendorff et al., who reported that the use of Brønsted acid‐based catalysis boosts the catalytic efficiency by five orders of magnitude [16]. While this again is not a scalable solution for wastewater remediation, it highlights the importance of electrostatic screening near the catalyst surface through the modulation of the charge neutrality point in this case [17].

Herein, we introduce BaTiO3 as the model ferroelectric catalyst, leveraging its temperature‐ and size‐dependent phase transition to the non‐polar phase to exploit the pyroelectric effect [18]. Among the ferroelectric polar oxide materials, BaTiO3 exhibits a relatively large pyroelectric coefficient of 20 to 30 nC/(cm2K) for the bulk phase that can be further enhanced in nanoparticles or at slightly elevated temperatures [19, 20]. Previous work has mainly focused on the pyroelectric effect introduced by the external electric heating [21, 22]. We use modulated solar irradiation to heat the system, where the temperature rises under illumination and cools down when the light is shut off. This process will, however, remove a certain percentage of photons that are required for the photocatalytic process. For example, for a duty cycle of 50% illumination, 50% blocking, the amount of photons will drop by a factor of 2.

In the present work, tetragonal BaTiO3 nanoparticles were first synthesized by a microwave‐assisted hydrothermal method, and the catalytic activity was studied against methyl orange as a model pollutant. A solar simulator (1 sun) was used as a source of illumination, and in order to induce the pyroelectric effect, an optical chopper was introduced into the system for chopping frequencies between 1 and 200 Hz. Changes of concentration were detected optically via absorption measurements. Details of the procedure are as follows:

Synthesis of Iron‐Doped Barium Titanate

Fe‐doped BaTiO3 was synthesized via a microwave‐assisted hydrothermal method to achieve an approximate composition of BaTi0.97Fe0.03O3 (BaTiO3:Fe 3%) under the assumptions a) that iron heterovalently substitutes titanium on the B‐site (creating an Fe3+Ti4+ defect that is compensated by a single positively charged oxygen vacancy VO ..) and b) of a complete integration of all available iron into the crystal lattice. Moderate to strong iron doping of BaTiO3 promotes light absorption and charge carrier generation while excessive doping (above 3 percent) disproportionately causes charge carrier recombination on defect sites and reduces the tetragonality of barium titanate and consequently, the bulk photovoltaic effect that helps drive electrons and holes apart [23]. Moreover, iron is non‐toxic as compared to, for example, chromium [24]. Titanium butoxide (Ti(OBu)4 –50 mM) was dissolved in 5 mL of ethanol and mixed with 15 mL of barium hydroxide (Ba(OH)2—73.5 and 1.5 mM of Fe(NO3)3) dissolved in H2O for 10 min to attain a homogenous solution. Subsequently, 3 mL of 1 % polyethylene glycol was added to the reaction mixture, followed by the addition of 4 mL H2O2, and the mixture was stirred for 10 min. Hydrogen peroxide acts as a scavenger for excessive hydrogen that would otherwise promote ionic leakage inside the nanocrystals. The reaction mixture was then transferred to a Teflon‐lined autoclave, and the hydrothermal reaction was performed at 120 W (2.5 GHz) for 7 min. The nanoparticles were washed twice with ethanol and water, and dried at 80°C to remove organic residues. Similarly, different percentages of nominal Fe doping were obtained by concentration of Fe by 6%, 9%, and 12%, which results in the approximate composition of BaTi0.84Fe0.06O3 (BaTiO3:Fe 6%), BaTi0.91Fe0.09O3 (BaTiO3:Fe 9%), and BaTi0.88Fe0.12O3 (BaTiO3:Fe 12%). Pure BaTiO3 was synthesized without Fe in the reaction mixture.

Characterization of Nanoparticles

The XRD spectrum of the BaTiO3:Fe nanoparticles has been recorded by using Bruker D8 Advance with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) in the range of 2θ from 10° to 90°. Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) analysis of BaTiO3:Fe has been performed by using JEM‐2100PLUS at 200 kV, manufactured by JEOL and IDES, where the nanoparticles were dispersed in ethanol and drop‐casted onto a copper grid (300 mesh). Raman spectra of the as‐synthesized nanomaterial have been acquired on a Horiba iHR320 system equipped with a linearly polarized 473 nm cw laser (2.6 eV, TEM00 in backscattering geometry). The surface morphology of the nanoparticles was characterized using Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) in tapping mode, employing a Smart SPM1000 system from AIST‐NT Inc. Silicon cantilever tips (NANOSENSORS model PPP‐NCHR) were utilized, featuring a tip radius of less than 8 nm.

Photocatalytic Experiments

The photocatalytic activity of BaTiO3:Fe was determined through the decomposition rate of methyl orange as a model pollutant. For this purpose, 1 g/L nanoparticles of BaTiO3:Fe were dispersed in 5 mg/L of methyl orange, the reaction mixture was stirred for 30 min to attain adsorption/desorption equilibrium, and the reaction mixture was irradiated under 100 mW/cm2 (1 sun) solar irradiation by an AM 1.5 G solar simulator (Photo emission tech. SS50AAA). We emphasize the importance of the soaking time of approximately 30 min that is required to achieve the best catalytic performance. Previous investigations have demonstrated that the reaction kinetics slow down after the equivalence of the soaking time [25]. To determine the catalytic activity of the nanoparticles on the degradation of methyl orange, the optical absorption of the solution was measured at 405 nm after centrifugation of the reaction mixture at 5000 rpm for 5 min. This step separates the catalytic nanoparticles and removes their Mie scattering from the optical absorption spectra. The rate constant of the reaction mixture was numerically extracted from the concentration change over time to quantify the degradation efficiency of the reaction. Temperature‐dependent experiments were conducted on a PID‐controlled hotplate under persistent stirring.

Pyroelectric Contribution

To induce temperature variations that are required for the pyroelectric effect, the illumination was chopped by a quartz‐stabilized mechanical chopper (Scitec instrument). The combined pyro‐ and photocatalytic activity of BaTiO3:Fe for methyl orange degradation has been studied as a function of light modulation frequency (1 to 200 Hz), of the concentration of BaTiO3:Fe (100 mg/L to 2 g/L), and of the concentration of methyl orange (1 to 7.5 mg/L), and as a function of temperature offset. A control experiment with an IR filter (6 mm water, identical to the depth of the catalytic solution) has been conducted to confirm IR absorption of water as the dominant nature of heating.

Results

Particle Size

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) imaging of BaTiO3:Fe 3% was conducted to determine its size and morphological characteristics, as shown in Figure 1. The TEM image reveals that the particles are close to cuboid in shape with an average size of ≈20 nm. The size distribution has been independently determined by Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) height measurement (Figure S1). The Si substrate was cleaned using acetone, isopropyl alcohol, and water, then 1 mL of nanoparticles dispersed in water was spin‐coated at 3000 rpm for 30 s. The size distribution analysis of BaTiO3 and BaTiO3:Fe 3% nanoparticles yielded 26 and 18 nm mean grain size, respectively, confirming a size allowing for the tetragonal phase to still be present at room temperature, but for which a considerable reduction of the phase transition temperature as compared to bulk should be expected [26]. The proximity of a size‐induced phase transition from the polar tetragonal to the non‐polar cubic phase causes a noticeable increase in the pyroelectric coefficient, albeit at the expense of a transition from a first‐order phase transition to a second‐order phase transition, that is, the peak values of the pyroelectric coefficient will slightly decrease for smaller particle sizes [27].

(a) Transmission electron microscopy image of BaTiO3:Fe 3% and (b) higher‐resolution image of the same sample. Particles are of regular shape in a size range between 20 and 30 nm, typically.

Crystal Structure

The XRD diffraction pattern of BaTiO3 (Figure 2a) displays expected peaks at 22.26°, 31.49°, 38.88°, 45.38°, 50.97°, 55.95°, 65.75°, 70.66° and 79.47°, corresponding to the (100), (110), (111), (002), (210), (211), (200), (212) and (311) planes respectively [28] of the room temperature tetragonal phase with a 4 mm space group (JCPDS card no: 00‐005‐0626) (Figure 2a). Additional peaks at 24.08°, 34.4°, 41.87°, and 46.83° correspond to BaCO3, resulting from surface carbonate contaminations through chemisorption of ambient CO2 onto BaO‐terminated surfaces [29]. The XRD pattern of BaTiO3 confirms that the nanoparticles are in the tetragonal phase, which implies pyroelectricity, which in turn also implies piezoelectricity [26, 30, 31] (Figure 2b). The tetragonality of the synthesized BaTiO3 decreases with increasing iron‐doping from nominal 3 to 12 at%; between a nominally undoped sample and a sample with 12 at% Fe, the tetragonality drops by 50%, thus approaching the cubic, non‐polar phase (Figure 2c). For a nominal doping level of 3% iron, the tetragonality does not change substantially. However, we observe an increase in the light absorption as shown in Figure 3a and as previously reported [24, 32].

Optical Properties

While the fundamental absorption of bulk BaTiO3 occurs slightly below 400 nm for a band gap of 3.2 eV, Fe‐doped BaTiO3 shows two additional absorption bands: one centered around 530 nm (Figure 3a), associated with the presence of Fe3+ and another close to the fundamental absorption, associated with the presence of single charged oxygen vacancies near the valence band that are the charge compensation mechanism for the negatively charged Fe3+Ti4+. Upon increasing the Fe doping concentration from 3% to 12%, the intensity of the broad absorption peak centered at 530 nm increases progressively. This enhancement suggests a higher concentration of Fe‐related electronic states within the band structure, which contributes to the visible‐light absorption (Figure 3a). As a result, the optical response of BaTiO3 is significantly modified with increasing Fe content. Nevertheless, despite the increase in visible‐light absorption, the tetragonality decreases with increasing Fe doping, leading to a loss of ferroelectric properties at higher dopant concentrations, as confirmed by XRD analysis. Further losses of the catalytic performance might be associated with a disproportionate increase in recombination sites for very high iron doping.

To further determine the effect of iron‐doping on the BaTiO3 and BaTiO3:Fe 3% nanoparticles, Raman spectroscopy of the nanomaterial has been performed and is illustrated in Figure 3b. The Raman spectrum of BaTiO3 shows the expected peaks at 180, 270, 305, 515, and 720 cm−1 attribute to the vibrational stretching of A1 (TO) mode–Ba–TiO3 vibration, A1 (TO2) mode–Ti–O vibration, B1 + E (TO3 + LO2) mode–TiO6 vibration, A1 (TO) mode on Ti–O6 vibration and A1 mode–BO6 vibration respectively [33, 34]. The Raman peak at 1040 cm−1 is attributed to BaCO3 [35], corroborating the XRD data.

Photocatalytic Degradation of Methyl Orange in The Presence of Pyroelectric Contributions

Figure 4a,b illustrate the photocatalytic and combined pyro‐ and photocatalytic activity of BaTiO3 (1 g/L) on the degradation of anionic methyl orange (5 mg/L) separately under solar illumination until the complete decomposition of the pollutant. The additional pyroelectric contribution to the catalytic degradation was achieved by mechanically chopping the light irradiation at a frequency of 1 Hz. This chopping implies two things: for one, it introduces a duty cycle of illumination during which the light is modulated between 50% maximum intensity (500 ms) and 50% darkness (500 ms) with an almost rectangular profile, so we remove 50% of all photons for a given duration of the experiment. On the other side, the solar simulator provides a broad spectrum that not only caters to high‐energy photons to trigger the photocatalytic reaction, but it also provides infrared photons to heat the solvent (water) quite efficiently. Therefore, the change in infrared intensity causes changes in temperature, which are discussed in detail in the supplementary information section. Results with additional stirring are provided to assess tribocatalytic contributions.

(a) Plot of C/C0 versus time under different pyro‐ and photocatalysis condition of BaTiO3 against anionic dye methyl orange, (b) Rate constant and degradation percentage of BaTiO3 under different catalysis conditions (pyro‐ and photocatalysis), (c) Plot of C/C0 versus time of BaTiO3:Fe (0, 3, 6, 9 and 12%), and (d) Rate constant versus Fe‐doping on the pyro‐photocatalytic degradation of methyl orange.

The results of the photocatalytic degradation indicate that the catalytic process follows zero‐order kinetics, which is in agreement with relatively high concentrations of the catalyst. We determine the rate constant of photocatalysis for the given concentrations to be 11·10−3 min−1 (with an R2 larger than 0.95). A control experiment with non‐polar titanium dioxide (P25, see Figure S2) demonstrates that chopping indeed reduces the photocatalytic activity by 50% for a scenario in which the total number of available photons is the only contribution to the photocatalytic activity.

For barium titanate nanoparticles, the situation drastically changes: under conditions of chopped illumination, where we remove 50% of all photons but introduce temperature variation in exchange, the catalytic rate constant increases from 11·10 to 18·10−3 min−1, and for pyro‐photocatalysis with stirring, it increases even more to 22·10−3 min−1 (Figure 4a,b and Table S1). This is more than 60% increase compared to the conventional photocatalysis. In other words, the pyroelectric contribution has overcompensated for the loss of photons.

Further, to determine the contribution of the tribocatalytic effect through stirring, the catalytic activity of BaTiO3 and BaTiO3:Fe 3% has been performed under dark conditions with constant stirring. The results show that the catalytic efficiency under dark conditions with constant stirring provides negligible degradation of methyl orange (Figure S3). We therefore attribute the increase from 18·10 to 22·10−3 min−1 to the effect of stirring without triboelectric contribution.

At this point, it is clear that the overcompensation of the loss of high‐energy photons that triggers the photocatalytic reaction is due to the contribution of a different spectral region. To verify the contribution of the infrared spectrum that we invoke for the pyroelectric effect, we performed a control experiment in which we introduced an infrared filter, which will be discussed in the subsequent section.

Figure 4c,d illustrate the dependence of the combined pyro‐ and photocatalytic efficiency as a function of iron‐doping, and a non‐monotonous dependence again indicates the beneficial effects of moderate iron‐doping while concentrations above 3 at% reduce the performance. Moderately iron‐doped barium titanate has a moderate increase in the rate constant from 22·10−3 to 25·10−3 min−1. Specifically, the rate constants for the combined pyro‐ and photocatalytic degradation of methyl orange decrease from 25·10−3 min−1 for BaTiO3:Fe 3% to 12·10−3 min−1 for BaTiO3:Fe 6% to 6·10−3 min−1 for BaTiO3:Fe 9% to eventually 5·10−3 min−1 for BaTiO3:Fe 12%. The combined pyro‐ and photocatalytic activity of Fe‐doped BaTiO3 decreases with an increase in the Fe doping from 3% to 12% Figure 4d, which follows the change in tetragonality towards the cubic structure, as shown in Figure 2c. Nevertheless, the rate constant on the BaTiO3:Fe 3% is 1.13 times higher than for pure BaTiO3, and the degradation efficiency of BaTiO3:Fe 3% reaches of 97.1% in our experimental configuration (Table S2). In what follows, the pyro‐photocatalytic experiments have been performed by using BaTiO3:Fe 3%, as it shows the strongest catalytic activity on cationic and anodic dyes.

Following the kinetics of heat transfer from the solar simulator to the solvent as detailed in the supplementary information, we now optimize the light modulation to maximize the pyroelectric effect. At this point, we emphasize a couple of important points: (a) we do not consider absorption via the catalyst to be a dominant effect, as the thermal contact to the solvent is excellent and heat would very rapidly dissipate from the nanoparticles to the water, especially considering the relative heat capacitances. So, we consider exclusively heating of the water through IR radiation (which will be confirmed later through a control experiment), and (b) we do not consider electrical heating to accomplish large temperature changes. We shall see that the expected temperature changes through light modulation are well below 1 K and yet sufficiently strong to trigger tangible pyroelectric fields, which provides an economically viable perspective.

The pyroelectric effect is defined by the change of spontaneous polarization as a function of temperature change. This spontaneous polarization is expressed as surface charge density of bound charges (originating from dipoles) that cannot directly participate in the catalytic reaction. Through the very definition of the pyroelectric effect, it becomes large at the phase transition from the ferro‐ to the paraelectric phase (Ps = 0), which occurs close to 383 K (110°C) in the bulk phase. The phase transition in the bulk phase is first order with a pyroelectric coefficient at the phase transition temperature close to 20 nC/(cm2K), which corresponds to a very small change of roughly 0.1% of the total remanent polarization (26 mC/cm2) per Kelvin at room temperature. However, at the phase transition temperature, the pyroelectric coefficient increases by two orders of magnitude to approach 1 mC/(cm2K). Upon reduction of the particle size, barium titanate undergoes a size‐driven phase transition into the paraelectric phase at room temperature for particle sizes below approximately 10 nm, so that nanoparticles like the ones in use in this investigation are close to the respective phase transition at which, for the reasons outlined above, the pyroelectric coefficient peaks. However, the nature of the phase transition has been reported to change from first order to second order, resulting in a slight loss of pyroelectric peak value as compared to the bulk phase transition [27]. As we had previously discussed [26], changes in the size of the catalyst come with different consequences: first and foremost, the increase of surface for a given quantity of catalyst with decreasing particle size increases the reaction speed as catalysis linearly scales with area. Secondly, polar oxides exhibit the bulk photovoltaic effect, a consequence of the non‐centrosymmetry of these crystal structures that provides an additional charge separation mechanism after photoexcitation, resulting in better catalytic performance. Since this property, represented by a third rank tensor, is tightly related to the non‐centrosymmetry, it also vanishes with decreasing particle size. The pyroelectric effect, as discussed before, increases with decreasing particle size as long as the critical phase transition size has not been exceeded.

For any given pyroelectric coefficient, we have to first consider the amplitude of temperature change induced by the infrared portion of the solar irradiation. While we provide all quantitative details in the supplementary information section, we will briefly discuss the qualitative aspects that were given consideration. Approximately 50% of the solar energy is in the spectral range above 1 micron wavelength that gets efficiently absorbed in the first millimeter of water. We are stirring in our experiments and therefore transfer this heat to the entire volume of contaminated water underneath (6 mm depth). At this point, we neglect further heat dissipation through the glass walls of the reactor or through evaporation, as the temperature changes are relatively slow and small. Large temperature changes are beneficial for a large pyroelectric effect, but the water temperature only changes slowly, considering the large heat capacity of water. For 1 s of irradiation, we may expect a change of several tens of mK for the uppermost millimeter. If the light modulation rate is very low, we will reach an equilibrium temperature, causing the pyroelectric effect to saturate. In practice, since any sample exhibits internal leakage currents, the pyroelectric field will even disappear after some time due to internal compensation mechanisms. On the other hand, if the light modulation rate is too fast, the temperature changes are too small to cause noticeable effects. So, we investigated the optimum modulation rate knowing that it depends on the strength of heat‐ and charge‐dissipation mechanisms.

The light chopping frequency was varied from 1 to 400 Hz for the degradation of methyl orange (5 mg/L) using BaTiO3:Fe 3% (1 g/L) under solar irradiation for 40 min (Figure 5a,c). The rate constant observed at different frequencies were non‐monotonous 17·10−3 (at 0 Hz, continues illumination), 25·10−3 min−1 at 1 Hz, 15·10−3 min−1 at 2 Hz, 15·10−3 min−1 at 200 Hz, and 13·10−3 min−1 at 400 Hz with a maximum at 1 Hz (Figure 5c and Table S3). Higher chopping frequencies reduced the efficiency the temperature modulation becomes negligible. For experimental reasons, lower frequencies have not yet been tested. As stated above, at high frequencies, the temperature modulation should become negligible, leaving us with only the reduction of the light intensity to 50% of the value for continuous illumination. While this should lead to a rate constant of 8.5·10−3 min−1, the observed value of 13·10−3 min−1 indicates that even at this frequency, we still generate a tangible pyroelectric response. The uncertainties for these values are of the order of 10−3 min−1 for two reasons: (1) All experiments were reproduced multiple times with catalyst nanoparticles from the same batch to avoid variations in particle size distribution, even if the average particle size is reproducible. (2) The detection of concentration via the Lambert‐Beer law provides very high sensitivity. A linear change of concentration translates into an exponential change of absorbance. As a consequence, the detectable changes of absorbance allow for a highly accurate determination of residual pollutant concentration, in particular after the centrifugation step.

Combined pyroelectric and photocatalytic activity of BaTiO3:Fe 3% at (a) different chopping frequencies and (c) different temperatures, and rate constant plots as a function of (b) chopping frequency and (d) temperature.

As previously outlined, the pyroelectric effect is a function of both size and temperature, so for the given size, the phase transition temperature has been reported to be substantially below that of the bulk phase. We therefore conducted the degradation experiments at different offset temperatures in the range from 25°C to 80°C in a stabilized water bath (Figure 5b,d) for a fixed chopping frequency of 1 Hz. The rate constant on the pyro‐photocatalytic degradation of methyl orange verses BaTiO3:Fe 3% at different temperature were non‐monotonous: 25·10−3 (25°C), 28·10−3 (40°C), 33·10−3 (50°C), 34·10−3 (60°C), 15·10−3 (70°C) and 8·10−3 (60°C), the maxima was achieved at 60°C, where the phase transition for our particle size had been reported [36]. The rate constant increases from 25°C to 60°C and immediately drops at the transition to the paraelectric phase near 60°C (Figure 5d and Table S4) [37, 38]. The results are consistent with the loss of pyroelectricity in the cubic phase, which leads to a decline in catalytic enhancement.

In order to verify the predominant contribution to heating, we investigated the pyro‐photocatalytic effect of BaTiO3:Fe 3% with an infrared (IR) filter (Figure 6a). With a band gap of 3.2 eV, barium titanate, even with iron doping, does not absorb infrared radiation. So if the primary heating mechanism was about the absorption of the particles and their local heating, the pyro‐photocatalytic efficiency should be unaffected by the presence of an IR‐filter. The rate constants of the combined pyroelectric‐ and photocatalytic activity of BaTiO3:Fe 3% with and without an IR filter are 8·10 and 25·10−3 min−1, respectively (Figure 6b). The suppression of the infrared part of the solar spectrum suppresses the temperature modulation of the water and therefore suppresses the pyroelectric contribution, leaving the performance of the polar, pyroelectric catalyst at approximately 50% of the photocatalytic performance under permanent irradiation (50% of 17·10−3 min−1 yields an expectation of 8.5·10−3 min−1), just as expected considering the loss of UV photons. This confirms that it is indeed the heating effect of infrared radiation on the water, which enables the water to follow the hot and cold cycle on chopped illumination and thereby pyro‐electrically activate BaTiO3, including the conclusion that the absorption of visible light through the catalyst is not the dominant driving force for the pyroelectric effect.

As previously mentioned, the change in the polarization of the catalyst was achieved by chopping the light, inducing a thermal oscillation in the system and thereby oscillating dipoles via the pyroelectric effect, which results in a periodic modulation of the field on the surface of the catalyst. The catalytic activity of the BaTiO3:Fe 3% was studied against different environmental conditions, such as offset temperature, light modulation frequency, ionic concentration (NaCl), and pH. Upon an increase in the salt concentration in the catalytic reactor, the rate kinetic drops from 25·10 to 8·10−3 min−1 as the concentration of the NaCl increases from 0 to 1 M (Figure S4a,b). According to Debye screening theory, the Debye length is inversely proportional to the square root of the ionic strength of the surrounding medium [39]. As the ionic concentration near the crystal surface increases, the Debye length decreases, leading to enhanced electrostatic screening of the pyroelectric field by NaCl [40, 41]. Consequently, the effective spatial extent of the pyroelectric field is reduced, thereby limiting its interaction with the target pollutant, methyl orange, at the interface.

The ionic effect on the pyro‐photocatalytic activity of BaTiO3:Fe 3% without the addition of NaCl was evaluated by variation of the pH of the reaction medium, as pH changes the ionic strength of the solution. According to the previous studies, methyl orange degradation is generally more efficient under acidic conditions in conventional photocatalysis. This trend is primarily associated with the protonation of the dye and its enhanced surface interaction with the catalyst [42]. Under pyroelectric activation, however, BaTiO3:Fe 3% exhibited higher catalytic efficiency than under neutral conditions (Figure S4c,d). This difference suggests that the governing factor is no longer solely dye protonation, but rather the interfacial electrostatic environment. In acidic media, the increased ionic content compresses the Debye length and limits the spatial extent of the pyroelectric field, thereby weakening its interaction with methyl orange. By contrast, neutral conditions provide a less screened interface, allowing the pyroelectric field to extend more effectively and promote charge separation and dye degradation. These results indicate that pyroelectric activation is strongly influenced by the ionic strength of the reaction medium, which plays a decisive role in determining the catalytic performance of BaTiO3:Fe 3%.

The stability of the catalyst in the pyro‐photocatalytic activity of BaTiO3:Fe (3%) was studied by conducting the reaction for five cycles using the same catalyst. The results show that the catalytic degradation efficiency decreased from 97% to 92% after five cycles, while the rate constant changed from 25 ·10 to 24 ·10−3 min−1 (Figure S5). The change in catalytic performance was less about 5%, which confirms the stable catalytic behavior and suggests that the catalyst can be used for long‐term applications.

To further understand the ionic effect on the catalytic activity of BaTiO3:Fe 3%, photocatalytic and pyro‐photocatalytic experiments were performed using the cationic dye Rhodamine B, with a BaTiO3:Fe 3% concentration of 1 g/mL and a Rhodamine B concentration of 5 mg/L. In analogy to the experiments on methyl orange, the pyroelectric effect was activated by chopping the light at 1 Hz. The results showed an increase in the rate constant from 6.2·10 to 19 ·10−3 min−1 upon pyroelectric activation compared with photocatalysis under continuous illumination (Figure S6). That is an increase by more than 200%. In summary, pyroelectric activation enables a broader catalytic response for pollutant degradation in water treatment.

Discussion

The comparison of photocatalytic experiments requires a transferable metric for given experimental conditions (initial concentrations of catalyst and pollutant). We opt for the rate constant here, while the conversion efficiency would be equally instructive. As discussed above, we observe an increase in the effective rate constant by almost 50% as we compare the photocatalytic experiment with constant illumination to an experiment where the light was modulated at 1 Hz. This corresponds to an increased quantum efficiency by a factor of 3. Despite the reduced input energy (UV photons and infrared irradiation) by 50% for the given duration of the experiment, we observe an overcompensation of this reduction through an increase in the reaction constant.

While light modulation reduces the number of photons and thus the amount of photoexcited charge carriers by 50%, it adds bound charges (dipoles expressed as surface charge density) through the pyroelectric effect in return. These bound charges provide a slowly modulated electric field that acts on ionic species. In order to estimate the amplitude of the electric field, we start with the absorption of infrared light in the upper millimeter of the water. As a rough approximation, solar irradiation with wavelengths above 1 micron is absorbed in the upper millimeter of the water reservoir. This spectral range corresponds to approximately 50% of the energy contained in the entire spectrum. That is around 500 W/m2. With a heat capacity of water at room temperature of nearly 4.2 kJ/(kg K), it therefore takes approximately 8 s to change the temperature of this layer by 1 K (neglecting all heat losses), or we might at best expect a change of roughly 120 mK every second of illumination. Since we stir and mix with a reservoir of 6 mm depth, the temperature change across the entire volume is reduced to 20 mK under the assumptions of a) fast temperature exchange upon stirring and b) negligible heat losses.

The change in the surface potential of BaTiO3 nanoparticles is rationalized in the supplementary information section. It is important to take into consideration that the spontaneous polarization, the dielectric permittivity, as well as the pyroelectric coefficient of nanoparticles, are different from the bulk values, given the proximity to the phase transition into the paraelectric phase.

Pyroelectric Fields

A conservative estimation of the pyroelectric field across a cuboid barium titanate nanoparticle for a temperature change of 10 mK, following the above considerations, is 104 V/m. While the total voltage is low, given the small size of the particles, the local electric field is very high. Please refer to the supplementary information section for additional details.

This corresponds to a change in the surface potential of approximately 0.2 mV on the polar surfaces of barium titanate, which, in the case of a cuboid particle, represents 1/3 of the total surface area. On these surfaces, the surface potential is now modulated following the evolution of the pyroelectric field. The adsorption energy of water on BaTiO3 has been approximated to 80 kJ/mol [43] via DFT calculations, albeit without specificity on the polar nature of surfaces. Following Nernst's equation, the change in surface potential changes the adsorption energy. The change in the surface potential by 0.2 mV results in a change of adsorption energy of 19 J/mol, which is 0.02%.

Now, we estimate k (rate of adsorption/desorption) as determined by the Arrhenius equation. k=Ae−EaRT where A is 1013 (according to Transition State Theory, TST), R is the universal gas constant (8.314 J/mol K), and T is the temperature (room temperature 298 K). Without a change in the surface potential (80 kJ/mol activation energy), k is 9.47·10−2 s−1. Upon the effect of a 0.2 mV change in the surface potential, k is 9.405·10−2 s−1 (favor desorption), and for −0.2 mV change in the surface potential, k is 9.55·10−2 s−1 (adsorption desorption). A change in the surface potential from −0.2 to 0.2 mV modifies the rate constant k (governing adsorption and desorption), leading to an ∼1.5% swing in the reaction rate relative to the k (without pyroelectric contribution), which plays a major role in boosting the catalytic performance.

A schematic representation of the pyro‐photocatalytic activity of BaTiO3:Fe 2% on the degradation of methyl orange is shown in Figure 7. The spontaneous polarization of BaTiO3 is 26 µC/cm2. Light modulation induces the pyroelectric effect due to the change in temperature, so that the additional pyroelectric polarization results in a change in the surface potential of 0.2 mV. The change in the surface potential drives an oscillation of the Helmholtz layer and thereby improves the mass transportation, enhancing the catalytic efficiency on the degradation of methyl orange against the nanoparticle. Previous findings on time constants of the reaction [26] indicate that in our case, it is primarily the desorption kinetics that are affected.

Conclusion

This study highlights the influence of the pyroelectric effect on the photocatalytic degradation of methyl orange using BaTiO3. The pyroelectric polarization of BaTiO3 induces a surface potential change and an additional electric field in response to the infrared portion of a modulated illumination. This oscillation in surface potential enhances the dynamics on the surface of the nanoparticles, which collectively enhances mass transport on the catalytic surface to promote catalysis.

For this proof of concept, the total catalytic efficiency of BaTiO3:Fe 3% in pyro‐photocatalysis is already a 50% larger than that of photocatalysis alone. Remarkably, despite the reduced energy input due to chopped illumination, the catalytic efficiency overcompensates for the loss of UV photons. This significant improvement is attributed to the oscillations in the Helmholtz layer, which enhance surface interactions and pollutant ad‐ and desorption.
