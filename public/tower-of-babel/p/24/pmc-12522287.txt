Impact of dynamic changes in pain on cardiovascular disease in Chinese middle and elderly people: results from China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS)
Authors: Zengao Yang, Li Zheng, Haijing Zhao, Honghong Zhang, Yue Zhu, Yuhan Ma, Yingyue Zhang, Suyun Cai, Yuqi Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies found chronic pain was an important risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD), but only focused on baseline pain status, without exploring into the dynamic changes of pain status on CVD. This study aims to investigate the associations of dynamic changes in pain status with incident CVD.MethodThis study used data from China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) aged 45 or older. The pain status is defined as whether the individual has frequently suffered from pain since the last survey (conducted every 2 or 3 years). Changes in pain status were assessed by pain status at baseline and the second survey which was three years after the baseline. CVD was ascertained by heart disease and/or stroke. This study employed multivariable logistics models adjusting for potential confounders. Moreover, different subgroup analysis and heart disease and stroke analysis respectively, were applied to test the consistence and robustness of the investigation result.ResultEleven thousand, two hundred seventeen participants (female 50.94%, mean age 58.63 years) were included in pain status changes analyses. In comparison with stable painless participants, painless participants who progressed to pain had significantly elevated risks of CVD (Painless-Pain [OR 1.57, 95% CI 1.30–1.90]; Stable Pain [OR 2.23, 95% CI 1.79–2.77]). Further, participants were reclassified by pain status in the third survey. Compared with stable painless participants, participants who progressed to pain in the third survey had significantly elevated risks of CVD (painless-painless-pain: OR 1.51, 95% CI 1.12–2.04; painless-pain-pain: OR 2.15, 95% CI 1.66–2.79; pain-painless-pain: OR 2.26, 95% CI 1.44–3.52; stable pain: OR 2.57, 95% CI 1.90–3.48). Different subgroups analysis and outcomes analysis of CVD, heart disease and stroke had consistent results.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that progression from pain-free to chronic pain status significantly increases CVD incidence, while the pain relief status shows no increased CVD risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24552-9.Keywords: Pain, Cardiovascular disease, Heart disease, Stroke, CHARLS

Introduction

The global burden of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) among middle-aged and elderly populations represents a growing public health crisis. As the leading cause of mortality worldwide, CVDs accounted for approximately 20.5 million deaths in 2021, with nearly 80% occurring in low- and middle-income countries [1]. This burden has shown significant escalation among adults aged ≥ 55 years, with marked increases in incidence rates, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and mortality observed globally [2]. China exemplifies this concerning epidemiological transition, currently harboring an estimated 330 million CVD patients. Cardiovascular mortality dominates disease-related deaths, comprising 48.00% and 45.86% of rural and urban fatalities respectively [3]. This disproportionate burden coincides with rapid demographic aging—China's 2020 census revealed 18.30% of the population now exceeds 60 years of age [4], a trend mirroring global patterns [5, 6].

The aging phenomenon presents particular challenges as it frequently coincides with multimorbidity, including hypertension, diabetes, osteoporosis, arthritis, and chronic pain conditions [7, 8]. This clinical complexity underscores the critical need for comprehensive disease management strategies, particularly for CVD prevention and control in elderly populations [9, 10]. The intersection of demographic aging with rising CVD prevalence necessitates urgent public health interventions tailored to middle-aged and elderly populations worldwide [11, 12].

Chronic pain is a very common symptom [13–16] in middle aged and elderly people, which has been reported to increase the risks of adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Ryan studied that chronic musculoskeletal pain (CMP) increased CVD events [17]. Subsequently, two Mendelian randomization analyses [18, 19] showed a causal relationship between Multisite chronic pain (MSCP) and cardiovascular disease. In addition, some studies suggested that persistent chronic pain can disrupt the relationship between arterial pressure levels, potentially through damage to baroreceptor function and pain perception [20, 21]. Investigations have revealed that parasympathetic efferent have anti-inflammatory properties [22–24]. Obviously, chronic pain plays a negative role in cardiovascular disease. Clarifying the relationship between pain and CVD will provide new insights for the prevention and management of CVD in the elderly.

However, previous studies only focused on baseline pain status, did not consider dynamic changes in pain status during follow-up. Compared to a single assessment of pain status at baseline, dynamic changes in pain status can provide a more comprehensive reflection of biological associations, such as the associations of the progression of pain with CVD. More importantly, with appropriate intervention such as changing unhealthy lifestyle and providing psychological counseling, pain could be reversed. Assessing incident CVD risks in individuals with changes in pain status will provide crucial evidence for the necessity of implementing interventions on pain in cardiovascular practice. Therefore, it is urgent to investigate the associations of changes in pain status with incident CVD risks. In this study, the representative prospective cohorts of China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) was used to investigate the associations of changes in pain status with incident CVD risks in middle-aged and elderly people. We hypothesized that changes in pain status increase the risk of incident CVD risks.

Methods

Study design and population

Our study is a reanalysis based on data from CHARLS which had got ethical approval (IRB00001052-11015) [25]. The study encompassed 450 villages across 150 counties in 28 provinces, surveying over 17,000 individuals from approximately 10,000 households. CHARLS employs a longitudinal design with biennial to triennial surveys, utilizing a multi-stage probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling methodology. The survey waves were conducted as follows: Wave 1 in 2011, Wave 2 in 2013, Wave 3 in 2015, Wave 4 in 2018, and Wave 5 in 2020. Due to the difference in questionnaire of pain between wave 2 (2013) and the other waves survey data, we used wave 3 (2015) survey as the baseline data.

Figure 1 showed the selection process of study population. Among 20967 participants from CHARLS, we first excluded 1275 participants with missing data on the pain status at baseline. Moreover, participants with CVD at baseline, loss to follow-up or age less than 45 years old were also excluded. Finally, 12239 eligible participants were included in baseline pain status analyses and 11217 participants were included in pain status change analyses. In consideration of dynamic changes of pain status, we re-assessed pain status according to wave 5 data (2020). 11216 participants were further divided into eight groups to investigated the effect of dynamic change of pain status on risk of incident CVD.

Selection process of study population

Assessment of pain status and cardiovascular disease

In CHARLS, pain status and CVD were determined based on self-reported questionnaires. People who reported often troubled with pain since the last survey (2 or 3 years) were defined as pain. People who reported being diagnosed with heart disease (including angina, heart attack, congestive heart failure, and other heart problems) or/and stroke were considered to have CVD. The details of CVD determination were described in CHARLS questionnaire (https://charls.charlsdata.com/). Therefore, CVD includes heart disease and/or stroke. Changes in pain status was evaluated through data from wave 3 (2015) to wave 4 (2018). Participants were divided into four groups (group 1: stable painless; group 2: pain to painless; group 3: painless to pain; group 4: stable pain). According to the pain status of wave 5 (2020), participants were further divided into eight groups (group 1: stable painless; group 2: pain-painless-painless; group 3: painless-pain-painless; group 4: pain-pain-painless; group 5: painless-painless-pain; group 6: painless-pain-pain; group 7: pain-painless-pain; group 8: stable pain). In the baseline pain status analyses, follow-up was initiated from wave 3 (2015). In the pain status change analyses, follow-up was initiated from wave 4 (2018) of CHARLS. The endpoints of follow-up was the first occurrence of CVD.

Ascertainment of covariates

The covariates included age, sex, address, marital status, education, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, dyslipidemia, abnormal glucose metabolism, chronic lung disease, kidney disease, digestive disease, and arthritis. The address was divided into two categories: urban/rural areas (main urban area, urban–rural integration areas, town center, town area) and rural area (countryside, country village). Marital status was divided into two categories: current married (married with spouse present, married but temporarily not living with spouse due to work or other reasons) and other marital status (separated, divorced, unmarried, widowed, or cohabitated). Education was divided into two levels: primary school or lower and secondary school or higher. Smoking status was categorized as current smokers or not. Similarly, drinking status was categorized as current drinkers or not. Physical activity level was divided into doing activity or not.

Statistical analyses

The mean and standard deviation (SD) of continuous variables, as well as percentage of categorical variables are used to describe basic features. Continuous variables were compared by Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test, and categorical variables were compared by Chi-square test or Fisher exact probability test. Missing rates of covariates were summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The missing data of covariates were imputed using the multiple imputation method (R package “Mice”) (see Supplementary Tables S1). Reliably, the baseline characteristics of participants using data not being imputed (see Supplementary Tables S2-4) were similar with Tables 1, 2 and 3 and Supplementary Table 5.

To analyze the association between baseline pain status and the risks of incident CVD, logistics regression was used to calculate the odds ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence interval (95% CI). Three models were fitted for the logistics regression using robust participants as the reference. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted controlling for hypertension, dyslipidemia, abnormal glucose metabolism, chronic lung disease, kidney disease, digestive disease and arthritis, sleep time, depression score. Model 3 was adjusted controlling for age, sex, marital status, education, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, BMI, sleep, depression score, hypertension, dyslipidemia, abnormal glucose metabolism, chronic lung disease, kidney disease, digestive disease, and arthritis. Using similar methods, we also analyzed associations of dynamic changes in pain status with incident CVD.

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted: (i) due to unstable changes in pain, we re-assessed pain status at wave 5 survey data (2020) to further ensure the stability of pain changes; (ii) to test the consistence of analyses result of CVD, we further investigated the association of pain with heart disease and stroke respectively employing the same method; (iii) to test the robust of results, stratified analyses with adjusted covariates were performed by age, sex, marital status, address, education, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, BMI, sleep time, depression score, hypertension, abnormal glucose metabolism, chronic lung disease, kidney disease, digestive disease, and arthritis. Age, BMI and sleep time were divided into tertiles; (iv) we also investigated the interaction of pain with covariates above. The statistical significance of interaction was evaluated by the likelihood ratio test. All statistical analyses were carried out by R software (Version 4.3.0) and the significance level of statistical tests was 0.05.

Results

Association of baseline pain status with incident cardiovascular disease

The subjects’ average age was 58.62 ± 9.32 years. Individuals with higher levels of pain tended to be female (50.80%) (Table 1). Table 3 shows the association of baseline pain status with risks of incident CVD. Model 1, model 2 and model 3 had consistent results. After adjusting for age, sex, marital status, education, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, BMI, sleep, depression score, hypertension, dyslipidemia, abnormal glucose metabolism, chronic lung disease, kidney disease, digestive disease and arthritis, pain participants had significantly elevated risks of incident CVD than painless participants (OR 1.57, 95% CI 1.34–1.83). The outcomes of heart disease (OR 1.54, 95% CI 1.29–1.84) and stroke (OR 1.41, 95% CI 1.08–1.85) analysis were consistent with CVD (see Supplementary Table S6).

We also did sensitivity analyses. In different stratifies analyses, whether adjusting multiple confounding risk factors or not, the association of baseline pain status with risks of incident CVD, heart disease and stroke was robust (Supplementary Table S9-11, S18-20). It is worth noting that there is interaction between the potential predictors of education (P value for interaction = 0.028), depression score (P value for interaction = 0.031) and arthritis (P value for interaction < 0.001) in the CVD event (see Supplementary Table S18). There is interaction between the potential predictors of arthritis (P value for interaction = 0.001) in the heart disease event (see Supplementary Table S19). There is no interaction between the potential variables in the stroke event. (see Supplementary Table S20).

Association of changes in pain status with incident cardiovascular disease

Baseline characteristic of pain status change analyses had similar result with pain status (average age: 58.63 ± 9.31, emale: 50.94%) (Tables 1 and 2). Table 2 shows the number and percentage of the changes in pain status after three years of follow-up. Among baseline painless participants, 4084 (49.29%) participants progressed to pain status. Meanwhile, among baseline pain participants, 524 (17.87%) recovered to painless status.

Figure 2 shows the association between changes in pain status and risks of incident CVD, which is consistent in different models even after adjusted for age, sex, marital status, education, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, BMI, sleep, depression score, hypertension, dyslipidemia, abnormal glucose metabolism, chronic lung disease, kidney disease, digestive disease, and arthritis. In comparison with stable painless participants after adjusted covariates, pain participants who recovery to painless status and painless participants who progressed to pain status all showed significantly elevated risks of incident CVD (OR 1.52, 95% CI 1.06–2.19; OR 1.57, 95% CI 1.30–1.90, respectively, Fig. 2). These results were similar with outcome of stroke (Supplemental Table S7). However, there was no statistic difference of heart disease risk was observed between stable painless participants and pain participants who recovered to painless (OR1.43, 95% CI 0.94–2.16, P = 0.092, see Supplementary Table S7). Additionally, participants with stable pain had the largest risk of incident CVD (OR 2.23, 95% CI 1.79–2.77), heart disease (OR 2.06, 95% CI 1.61–2.63) or stroke (OR 2.05, 95% CI 1.37–3.08).

Associations of changes in pain status based on 3 consecutive survey data with incident cardiovascular disease

Figure 3 showed the association of changes in pain status (wave 3, wave 4, and wave 5) with risks of incident CVD. Due to pain status change, participants based on the pain status of wave 3, wave 4 and wave 5 was further devided into 8 groups. In comparison with group 1 (stable painless participants) after adjusted covariates, no significant increased risks of incident CVD was observed in group 2 (pain-painless-painless) (P = 0.872) and group 3 (painless-pain-painless) (P = 0.456). Notably, although significant increased risks of incident CVD was observed in group 4 (pain-pain-painless) (OR 1.70, 95% CI 1.10–2.64; P = 0.017, Fig. 3), risk of heart disease (P = 0.117, Supplementary Table S8) and stroke (P = 0.123, Supplementary Table S8) showed no significant statistics difference compared with stable painless group. Participants with pain in 2020 showed significantly elevated risks of incident CVD (group 5: OR 1.51, 95% CI 1.12–2.04; group 6: OR 2.15, 95% CI 1.66–2.79; group 7: OR 2.26, 95% CI 1.44–3.52; group 8: OR 2.57, 95% CI 1.90–3.48, Fig. 3), consistent with outcomes of heart disease and stroke (Supplementary Table S8). Participants with stable pain still had the largest elevated risks of incident CVD and heart disease. However, group 7 (pain-painless-pain) (OR 2.92, 95% CI 1.38–6.15) had the largest CVD risk among all pain status transition groups (Supplementary Table S8).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the associations of baseline pain status and changes in pain status with incident CVD risks. pain participants showed elevated risks of incident CVD than painless participants. Painless participants who progressed to pain status increased incident CVD events compared to stable painless status. However, statistic difference was not observed between 2015–2018 pain status change recovered to painless in 2020 and stable painless, which suggested pain participants who recovered to painless will decrease risks of incident heart disease and stroke. In addition, the longer the duration of pain lasts, the higher risks of CVD, including heart disease and stroke.

Pain is becoming an increasingly common condition with the rapid growth of elderly population worldwide [26–29]. Previous studies showed that there was a close association between chronic pain and cardiovascular disease. Using the UK Biobank data, Rönnegård et al. [30] found that chronic pain increased the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events. Zhu j et al. [19] also found a causal relationship between multi-site chronic pain and coronary artery disease. In our study, we also observed consistent results that participants with pain had an increased risk of CVD compared with painless participants. Laboratory tests suggested that intersecting pathways between the cardiovascular system and pain modulation system [31]. In addition, our results remain significant after adjusting for traditional cardiovascular risk factors. Our findings further gave robust evidence that pain is a risk factor for CVD [32, 33].

In addition to the baseline pain status, our study also delved into the associations of changes in pain status with incident CVD, which were not explored previously. We found that painless participants who progressed to pain status had an increased risk of incident CVD as compared with stable painless participants (Fig. 2). These results highlighted the adverse effects of pain progression on incident CVD [34–36]. Interestingly, the risk of incident heart disease and stroke was not observed in 2015–2018 pain participants who recovered to painless status in 2020 when compared with stable painless, suggesting great benefits of reversing pain on incident adverse CVD outcomes. These results demonstrated the dynamic change of pain status that pain progression was closely associated with elevated incident CVD risks, but the recovery of pain can reduce the risk of incident CVD. Based on these results, we recommend: (1) incorporating routine pain assessment into cardiovascular risk evaluation, particularly for patients with persistent pain; (2) closely monitoring patients with fluctuating pain status (pain-painless-pain transition), as they exhibit the highest stroke risk; and (3) considering pain management as part of comprehensive CVD prevention strategies. These data-driven recommendations highlight the clinical importance of integrating pain history into cardiovascular risk stratification protocols.

Our study has important clinical and public health implications. Firstly, it is necessary to integrate pain assessment into routine cardiovascular practice, especially among middle-aged and elderly people. Individuals who frequently experience pain should be considered as the primary target for prevention of adverse cardiovascular events. Even if painless individuals also need to assess the risk factors for unstable pain status in order to identify individuals with higher risk of CVD and take prevention measures to prevent pain progression. In addition, pain is a reversible status, and decreased CVD risks were observed in individuals who recovered from pain status. Therefore, more measurements are required to implement effective interventions for the reversal of pain status. For the pain recovery, we found that participants with pain recovery had higher proportion in doing physical activity. Some investigations [37, 38] demonstrated exercise and educational intervention can alleviate pain in patients with hip/knee OA. This result suggested that rehabilitation of physical function might be an important intervention target for pain. In addition, there is an interaction between pain and potential variables, including depression score, education and kidney disease, et al. The association between pain and depression has been reported in previous studies [39–41]. Moreover, CVD is also associated with depression [42], arthritis [43, 44] and kidney disease [45, 46]. One observational study showed the protective effect of education on the risk of cardiovascular outcomes [47]. Therefore, participants with pain should also reinforce the management of depression, arthritis, and kidney disease, and pay attention to education.

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the associations between dynamic changes in pain status and risks of incident CVD. In addition, this study also investigated the association of heart disease and stroke with change in pain, which were consistent with CVD, indicating the reliability of our findings. Different subgroup analyses also ensured the robustness of our results.

This study has some Limitations. Firstly, selection bias might occur in pain status change analyses because we further excluded 1022 participants from baseline pain status analyses. Although we compared the baseline characteristics of included and excluded participants, suggesting similar baseline. Secondly, although we adjusted for multiple confounders, residual confounding factors might exist, such as genetic susceptibility. Finally, the CHARLS baseline variable is based on a questionnaire survey, which has a certain degree of subjectivity and may affect the analysis of results. However, this is a common issue in all cohort studies in the real world, and many prospective studies may be needed for further consultation in the future.

Conclusions

Dynamic changes in pain status are associated with different risks of incident CVD. The progression of pain status increases incident CVD risks, while the recovery of pain status reduces the risk of incident CVD.
