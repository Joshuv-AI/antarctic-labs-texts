Efficacy of the Dual Stent Retriever Technique for Branching-Site Occlusions in Mechanical Thrombectomy
Authors: Nobuaki Yamamoto, Yuki Yamamoto, Izumi Yamaguchi, Takeshi Miyamoto, Masaaki Korai, Kenji Shimada, Yuishin Izumi, Yasushi Takagi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveMechanical thrombectomy (MT) is an established treatment for acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion. Although achieving first-pass effect (FPE) is strongly associated with clinical outcomes, branching-site occlusions remain technically challenging and are associated with lower FPE rates. The dual stent retriever (DSR) technique has been proposed to improve thrombus engagement in branching vessel anatomy. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of the DSR technique in patients with branching-site occlusions.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed consecutive patients with angiographically confirmed branching-site occlusions who underwent MT between January 2020 and April 2025. Patients were classified according to the first-line thrombectomy strategy into a DSR group or a single SR (SSR) group. We compared the FPE rate, procedural complications, and prognosis between the groups. Furthermore, an adjusted analysis was performed using Firth’s penalized logistic regression.ResultsAmong 105 eligible patients, 20 were treated with the DSR technique. The rate of FPE was significantly higher in the DSR group than in the SSR group (90.0% vs. 27.1%, p <0.001), whereas procedure time and complications did not differ between groups. After adjustment for relevant confounders, the DSR technique remained independently associated with FPE (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 38.92, 95% confidence interval [CI] 4.60–329.39, p <0.001) and favorable functional outcome (aOR 4.59, 95% CI 1.03–20.36, p = 0.045).ConclusionThe DSR technique showed an association with higher rates of first-pass reperfusion in branching-site occlusions without a clear increase in procedural risk, indicating that it may be a feasible option in selected cases, although confirmation in prospective studies is needed.

Introduction

Mechanical thrombectomy (MT) has become the standard of care for patients with acute ischemic stroke due to large vessel occlusion (LVO).1–6) Among the procedural endpoints, achieving the first-pass effect (FPE), defined as successful reperfusion with a single thrombectomy attempt, has emerged as a robust surrogate marker for procedural efficacy and favorable clinical outcome.7–10) However, branching-site occlusions, such as those involving the internal carotid artery (ICA) terminus or distal horizontal segment (M1) of the middle cerebral artery (MCA), remain challenging due to complex vascular anatomy and increased risk of thrombus fragmentation and embolism into new territories (ENTs) and/or distal embolism (DE), despite advances in devices and techniques.11,12) These lesions are characterized by complex thrombus geometry, clot extension into multiple branches, and an increased risk of thrombus fragmentation and ENT/DE, all of which may compromise first-pass reperfusion.

The dual stent retriever (DSR) technique has been proposed as a strategy to improve clot capture by simultaneously engaging thrombi extending into each branch.13–15) By sandwiching the clot between 2 retrievers, this technique may enhance retrieval efficacy and reduce DE. Nevertheless, clinical data regarding the effectiveness and safety of the DSR technique remain limited. The present study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of the DSR technique in patients with branching-site occlusions undergoing MT.

Materials and Methods

Study design and patient selection

This study was approved by the local ethics committee of Tokushima University (Approval No. 1934-7), and informed consent was obtained from all patients for using the patients’ data. This retrospective observational study included consecutive patients who underwent MT at Tokushima University Hospital between January 2020 and April 2025. Patients with angiographically confirmed branching-site occlusions involving the ICA terminus or distal M1 segment were eligible. Patients were classified according to the first-line thrombectomy strategy into 2 groups: (1) the DSR group, in which 2 SRs were simultaneously deployed into branching-site branches and (2) the single SR (SSR) group, in which an SSR was used as the initial strategy. Baseline characteristics, procedural details, and clinical outcomes were compared between the groups. The patient selection flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

Stroke etiology was classified according to the Trial of ORG 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) criteria using vascular imaging, cardiac evaluation, and brain MRI. CTA or MRA of the head and neck, electrocardiography, cardiac monitoring, and transthoracic echocardiography were routinely performed, with transesophageal echocardiography and additional investigations conducted when clinically indicated. Diabetes mellitus was defined as a hemoglobin A1c level greater than 6.4% or the current use of drugs for hyperglycemia; dyslipidemia as a total cholesterol concentration greater than 220 mg/dL or the current use of drugs for dyslipidemia; and hypertension as the current use of antihypertensive drugs.

Treatment

Intravenous thrombolysis was administered to all patients who met the eligibility criteria for thrombolytic therapy according to current guidelines issued by the Japan Stroke Society. The dosing regimen followed the standard protocol used in Japan, in which alteplase is administered at a dose of 0.6 mg/kg.16,17) MT was performed in patients with an Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS) of 6 or higher on baseline imaging. In addition, patients with a low ASPECTS (3–5) on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) were also considered eligible for MT when there was no corresponding hyperintensity on FLAIR.18–20) However, patients with ASPECTS <6 were excluded from the present analysis because extensive baseline ischemic changes could substantially confound the assessment of clinical prognosis, independent of procedural success. All procedures were performed by experienced neurointerventionalists. MT was primarily performed using a combined technique consisting of an aspiration catheter and an SR as the standard first-line strategy. The choice between single and dual SR deployment was left to the discretion of the treating physician based on vascular anatomy and occlusion morphology. The SSR technique involved deploying an SSR at the occluded site after microcatheter navigation into the target branch distal to the clot, followed by device retrieval. The DSR technique, involved simultaneously deploying 2 SRs into each branch (e.g., the A1 segment of the anterior cerebral artery and the M1 segment of the MCA) after navigation of 2 microcatheters (0.017 and/or 0.021 inch) into each target branch distal to the branching site, followed by retrieval together (Fig. 2). Because 2 microcatheters (0.017 and/or 0.021 inch) were used, SR size selection was constrained by branch diameter and catheter compatibility. Therefore, one of the devices was limited to a 3-mm SR. In most cases, SRs with diameters of 3 and 4 mm were used. Identical SR size and type were preferred when branch diameters were comparable, whereas different sizes or types were selected when branch anatomy differed. Furthermore, a combined technique was consistently employed in all cases: a medium- or large-bore aspiration catheter was paired with an SSR, whereas a large-bore aspiration catheter was exclusively used in combination with the DSR technique to accommodate 2 microcatheters.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of this study was the FPE, defined as successful reperfusion, corresponding to an expanded Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (eTICI) grade of 2c or 3,21) achieved with a single thrombectomy attempt without the need for rescue therapy. Other key outcomes included final eTICI grade; procedure metrics, such as procedural complications; and functional outcome at 90 days after onset, assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate, and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Because of separation due to the small number of outcome events, adjusted analyses were performed using Firth’s penalized logistic regression. The model included age, baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, pre-procedural ASPECTS, stroke etiology, and intravenous thrombolysis. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Results

Among the 105 patients with angiographically confirmed branching-site occlusions included in this study, 20 patients (19.0%) were treated using the DSR technique, while 85 patients (81.0%) underwent thrombectomy with an SSR as the first-line strategy. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Patients in the DSR group were older than those in the SSR group. Cardioembolic stroke etiology and atrial fibrillation were more frequent in the DSR group, whereas intracranial atherosclerotic disease–related occlusion was more common in the SSR group, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. Baseline stroke severity, as assessed by the NIHSS, did not differ significantly between the groups. The distribution of occlusion sites was comparable between groups, with no significant differences in the proportions of ICA terminus and distal M1 occlusions. Time metrics from symptom onset to hospital arrival, arrival (door)-to-puncture, and puncture-to-reperfusion time were similar in both groups. Procedural and clinical outcomes of the SSR and DSR groups are shown in Table 2. The rate of FPE was significantly higher in the DSR group compared with the SSR group (90.0% vs. 27.1%, p <0.001). Similarly, the number of thrombectomy passes required to achieve reperfusion was significantly lower in the DSR group (median, 1 [interquartile range, IQR, 1, 1] vs. 2 [IQR, 1, 3], p <0.001). Despite the marked difference in early angiographic success, overall procedure time did not differ significantly between the groups. Final successful reperfusion, defined as eTICI grade ≥2c, was achieved at a high rate in both groups, with no statistically significant difference. Procedural safety outcomes were comparable between groups, with no significant differences in the incidence of any intracranial hemorrhage or symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage. ENT/DE occurred less frequently in the DSR group than in the SSR group (5.0% vs. 15.3%, p = 0.297), although this difference did not reach statistical significance. Clinical outcomes at 90 days are summarized in Table 2. A favorable functional outcome, defined as an mRS score of 0–2, was achieved in a higher proportion of patients in the DSR group compared with the SSR group (70.0% vs. 48.2%). However, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.089).

Discussion

This study showed that the DSR technique is associated with a significantly higher rate of FPE in patients with branching-site occlusions without a concomitant increase in procedure time or procedural complications. Using Firth’s penalized logistic regression to account for separation due to the small number of outcome events, the DSR technique remained a strong independent predictor of FPE after adjustment for age, baseline NIHSS score, pre-procedural ASPECTS, stroke etiology, and intravenous thrombolysis. These findings indicate that the DSR approach can enhance early angiographic success while maintaining procedural safety. From a mechanistic standpoint, branching-site occlusions pose intrinsic challenges for MT because thrombi often extend into multiple distal branches. This anatomical complexity increases the likelihood of incomplete clot engagement, asymmetric traction forces, clot fragmentation, and ENT/DE, all of which contribute to failure of first-pass reperfusion. When a single device is used, engagement may be limited to 1 branch, resulting in unbalanced mechanical forces during retrieval. By simultaneously deploying stent retrievers into the involved branches, the DSR technique may achieve more comprehensive thrombus–device interaction, improving circumferential clot capture and promoting en bloc retrieval.22) Such balanced engagement may mitigate localized shear stress at the thrombus–vessel interface and reduce the risk of clot fragmentation, a recognized mechanism underlying FPE failure in branching-site occlusions.

The markedly higher FPE rate observed in the DSR group represents the most prominent procedural advantage of this technique. This finding is clinically meaningful, as FPE has consistently been associated with shorter procedure times, reduced endothelial injury, improved microvascular reperfusion, and better downstream tissue perfusion. In addition, the significantly lower number of thrombectomy passes required in the DSR group further supports the hypothesis that more effective initial clot capture was achieved with this approach.23,24) Importantly, the improved angiographic efficacy of DSR was not accompanied by prolongation of procedure time or an increase in procedural complications. Despite the apparent technical complexity of deploying 2 stent retrievers, procedure duration remained comparable between groups. This observation suggests that successful first-pass reperfusion may offset any additional time required for device deployment. When performed within an experienced neurointerventional team, the DSR technique therefore appears to improve procedural efficacy without compromising procedural efficiency or safety. Although the rate of ENT/DE was numerically lower in the DSR group, this difference did not reach statistical significance, likely owing to the low overall event frequency and limited sample size. Nevertheless, this trend is mechanistically consistent with the concept that simultaneous multibranch thrombus engagement stabilizes clot retrieval and reduces DE. Larger studies are needed to determine whether this apparent reduction in ENT/DE represents a reproducible benefit of the dual SR strategy. In addition to angiographic outcomes, the DSR technique was independently associated with favorable functional outcome at 90 days. After adjustment, the use of the DSR technique was associated with a significantly higher likelihood of achieving functional independence. Although functional recovery after acute ischemic stroke is influenced by multiple factors beyond procedural success,25,26) these findings suggest that the substantial improvement in early reperfusion achieved with DSR may potentially translate into meaningful clinical benefit. By contrast, no statistically significant associations were observed between DSR use and embolism to new vascular territories or intracranial hemorrhage after adjustment. While the number of these adverse events was low, the DSR technique can improve reperfusion efficacy without compromising procedural safety when performed by experienced operators. Given the strong association between FPE and clinical prognosis, the DSR technique may represent a valuable first-line or early escalation strategy for selected patients with branching-site occlusions. However, routine use in all cases cannot be advocated, and careful patient selection based on occlusion morphology, vessel caliber, and operator expertise remains essential.

Several limitations merit consideration. This study was retrospective, single center, and included a relatively small sample size, which limits generalizability. Owing to the retrospective design, the determination of the occlusion site relied on pre-procedural imaging and angiographic records, which may be subject to inherent interpretive bias. For instance, even when an occlusion was expected to be at a branching site, a stagnant column of contrast material might have formed proximally, leading to the potential misclassification of these cases as non-branching-site (trunk) occlusions. Additionally, the use of prehospital stroke scales for patient triage may have introduced a selection bias, favoring the inclusion of extensive LVOs with high clinical severity, regardless of the precise anatomical site of the embolus. The choice of thrombectomy strategy and devices was left to the operator’s discretion, introducing potential selection bias. The selection of DSR versus SSR was not randomized. The DSR technique may have been preferentially selected by operators with greater familiarity or confidence in complex branching-site anatomy, which could have influenced procedural outcomes. Therefore, differences in individual technical expertise may have partially contributed to the observed association between DSR use and higher FPE rates. Furthermore, cardioembolic etiology was significantly more prevalent in the DSR group, which may reflect a selection bias where operators preferentially selected the DSR technique for presumed embolic occlusions at complex branching sites. Differences in thrombus composition between etiologies may have confounded the outcomes. Detailed assessments of collateral status and infarct core volume were also not incorporated. Finally, it must be noted that the parallel deployment of the DSR technique is not currently included in the manufacturers’ product information. However, its clinical utility for branching-site occlusions has been previously reported.27) In our series, the incidence of procedure-related complications in the DSR group was 5.0% for intracranial hemorrhage. No instances of device failure or mechanical interference between the dual SRs were encountered, suggesting that this technique can be executed safely by experienced neurointerventionists. Prospective, multicenter studies with standardized treatment algorithms and sufficient statistical power are warranted to validate these findings and to clarify whether the angiographic advantages of the dual SR technique translate into meaningful improvements in long-term functional outcomes.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that the dual SR technique may be feasible and potentially effective for branching-site occlusions, but confirmation in prospective studies is warranted.
