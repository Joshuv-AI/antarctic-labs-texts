Portable structured-light 3D volumetry for breast and flap assessment: Technical validation and workflow development
Authors: Yue Chen, Álvaro García-Cañal, Yingyue Xu, Fangyue Wu, Jose Andre Zapata-Negreiros, Juan Cámara-Pérez, Ignacio Navarro Cuéllar, Carlos Navarro Cuéllar, Clara Rebollo-Fernández, José M Lasso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo establish and technically validate a portable structured light volumetry workflow for breast sized objects and surgical tissues, and to identify practical acquisition strategies for clinically relevant problems such as inframammary fold occlusion and intraoperative tissue assessment.MethodsFour components were performed using a portable structured light scanner (Revopoint MIRACO Plus®): (1) phantom validation using 300–500 mL balloons (n = 25) against calibrated syringe volumes; (2) a mannequin posture experiment (300/500/700 mL) comparing 20° forward lean with supine acquisition using a subtractive modeling method (SMM); (3) biologic tissue simulation using porcine skin-subcutis, porcine muscle, and bovine muscle, with three scans per Near and Far mode; and (4) an observational intraoperative scan series (10 flaps; 10 reduction blocks). Accuracy was assessed using regression, Bland–Altman analysis, mean absolute percentage error, and paired comparisons.ResultsPhantom scans showed near-identity agreement with reference volumes (R² = 0.995), with a mean bias of +2.4 mL and limits of agreement from −7.4 to +12.2 mL. In the mannequin model, forward lean reduced bias by 20.0 mL at 500 mL and 95.6 mL at 700 mL versus supine SMM (both p < 0.001), supporting a feasible strategy when the inframammary fold is obscured. In biologic tissues, Far mode improved completeness and reduced scan time by about 54% versus Near mode. Intraoperatively, Far mode produced volume computable meshes for pedicled flaps, free flaps, and reduction specimens.ConclusionsPortable structured light scanning provides accurate and repeatable volumetry while defining a practical workflow for breast and surgical tissue assessment.

Introduction

Breast reconstruction is a central component of modern breast cancer care, improving quality of life beyond restoration of contour. Breast cancer remains the most commonly diagnosed malignancy worldwide.1 A major goal is the achievement of bilateral symmetry, as asymmetry adversely affects aesthetic outcomes, body image, and patient satisfaction.2 However, achieving durable symmetry remains challenging because it depends on surgical technique, anatomy, tissue characteristics, and postoperative change over time. Accurate breast volume assessment therefore remains an important unmet need across the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative phases of reconstruction.3 MRI and CT are precise but costly, CT involves ionizing radiation, and neither is practical intraoperatively. Anthropometric methods are imprecise, water displacement is limited to excised tissue,4 and volume is still often estimated in practice by specimen weight or visual inspection, with no standardized volumetric assessment in follow-up.5

Three-dimensional surface scanning offers a promising route toward this goal. By projecting structured light or laser and capturing surface deformation, it enables non-contact, rapid, and repeatable acquisition of digital surface models that can be archived and re-analyzed.6 In breast reconstruction, previous studies have focused mainly on evaluating the accuracy of three-dimensional surface scanning for breast volume measurement.7, 8, 9, 10 Reported systems include fixed stereophotogrammetry platforms such as VECTRA XT and 3dMDtorso, handheld structured-light scanners such as Artec Eva, Artec Leo and Revopoint POP2, as well as lower-cost mobile platforms using Structure Sensor or smartphone LiDAR. Despite this promise, their clinical translation remains constrained by posture sensitivity, inframammary fold occlusion, operator dependence, post-processing workload, and device-specific limitations in geometric fidelity. Performance may be sensitive to lighting, motion, and calibration, particularly in the operating room.11

Therefore, this study aimed to establish and technically validate an end-to-end 3D volumetry workflow, including acquisition, quality control, and post-processing, and to characterize its performance under controlled, posture-sensitive, and intraoperative-relevant conditions. Using the portable Revopoint MIRACO Plus®, we evaluated baseline accuracy in balloon phantoms, posture-related effects in a foam-torso mannequin model, scanner-mode effects in surgery-relevant soft tissues, and feasibility in an observational intraoperative scan series of flaps and reduction specimens.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval and institutional oversight

This study did not involve living human participants or live vertebrate animals. The ex vivo porcine and bovine tissues were obtained from commercial food suppliers, and no animals were euthanized specifically for research purposes. Under institutional policy, the project was classified as non-human subjects research and did not require animal ethics review. No identifiable personal data were collected. In the intraoperative component, only isolated surgical tissues were scanned after separation from the patient; therefore, formal ethics board review was not required. All tissues were handled using standard biosafety precautions and disposed of as regulated biological waste.

Scanner, software, and acquisition conditions

All acquisitions were performed using a handheld structured-light 3D scanner (Revopoint MIRACO Plus®; Xi’an Chishine Optoelectronics Technology Co., Ltd., China; dimensions: 200 × 50 × 110 mm), which was already in use in the 3D laboratory of Hospital General Universitario Gregorio Marañón, Madrid, Spain (Supplementary Figure S1). The device is CE-certified and covered by FCC equipment authorization. The scanner was warmed up and calibrated before each session according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Data acquisition and mesh reconstruction were performed in Revo Scan 5 (v5.5.4; Revopoint), and volumetric measurements were completed in Blender (v4.4; Blender Foundation, Amsterdam, the Netherlands).

Far mode was used as the default acquisition setting for all phantom, mannequin, and intraoperative scans. Near versus Far was compared only in the biologic tissue simulation, in which the small irregular specimens lay near the transition range between the two modes. Bench-top and simulation scans were performed under simulated operating-room conditions with indirect diffuse lighting and minimal glare, whereas intraoperative scans were acquired under routine operating-room lighting using the same workflow.

Study design

The study comprised four components: ex vivo phantom validation, a mannequin posture experiment, a biologic tissue simulation, and an observational intraoperative scan series. These components were used to evaluate volumetric accuracy, posture-related effects, scanner-mode performance, and intraoperative feasibility.

Ex vivo phantom validation

Water-filled latex balloons were prepared at five target volumes (300, 350, 400, 450, and 500 mL) using a calibrated syringe. After filling, the balloons were allowed to rest briefly to release residual bubbles, and the injected volume was recorded as the reference value (Supplementary Figure S2). Each balloon was suspended from a thin hook to avoid support contact and minimize underside occlusion and the operator performed a 360° circumferential scan at a constant standoff distance while maintaining stable tracking and full-surface coverage. Five scans were acquired for each target volume. Qualitative repeatability was documented by rendering all 25 meshes in a standardized orthographic view (Supplementary Figure S3).

Mannequin posture experiment

Pendulous breast analogues were simulated by affixing water-filled balloons (300, 500, and 700 mL) to a life-size foam torso. Each phantom was scanned in two positions, first supine and then in a fixed 20° forward lean. These postures were chosen to represent clinically relevant extremes of breast–chest wall interaction: a forward-lean condition with minimal contact and near-complete inframammary fold exposure, and a supine condition simulating a large or ptotic breast resting against the chest wall, in which the lower pole obscures the inframammary fold and the posterior boundary cannot be directly delineated. The forward-lean angle was verified using a digital inclinometer (±1°). In the forward-lean position, the breast analogue was isolated directly by digitally removing the chest wall in Blender and measuring the remaining watertight mesh (Supplementary Figure S4).

In the supine position, volume was calculated using a subtractive modeling method (SMM) (Fig. 1), consistent with prior breast volumetry studies using differential three-dimensional surface models.12 A single watertight reconstruction of the chest and breast together was duplicated to create two aligned models: Model A (chest plus breast) and Model B (chest only). Model B was derived from Model A by removing the breast and patching the resulting chest-wall defect while leaving the remainder of the mesh unchanged. Biologic tissue simulation

Three surgery-relevant soft-tissue types were used to assess the effects of surface characteristics on structured-light capture: porcine skin-subcutis (adipocutaneous tissue), porcine skeletal muscle (low-fat muscle analogue), and bovine skeletal muscle (muscle-dominant analogue). Each specimen was scanned under simulated operative conditions in both Near and Far modes, with three repeated scans per mode. For each scan, acquisition time, tracking stability, and base-edge completeness were recorded. Closed watertight meshes were then generated for volumetric analysis.

Reference volume was determined by triplicate water displacement at 37 °C. A graduated cylinder was prefilled with distilled water, the meniscus was read at eye level, and the uncoated specimen was fully immersed with gentle agitation to remove trapped air. Net displacement was recorded for three independent immersions and averaged for analysis. Mass was measured once before immersion using a calibrated 0.1 g balance, and density (g/mL) was calculated as mass divided by reference volume (Supplementary Figure S5).

Intraoperative tissue scanning

An observational intraoperative scan series was conducted to assess feasibility under operative conditions. Owing to operative time constraints, all acquisitions were performed in Far mode, and one successful model was retained for each tissue. Twenty tissues were scanned, including 10 flaps and 10 reduction blocks. Excised free flaps and reduction blocks were placed on a sterile flat support surface. When skin was present, the skin surface was oriented upward and the cut surface downward to reduce scanning error from irregular darker deep-tissue surfaces; orientation was not standardized for muscle-only specimens. Acquisition was therefore limited to the exposed superior and lateral surfaces, representing an approximately ≤180° exposed-surface scan. Pedicled flaps were scanned in situ when feasible, capturing only surgically accessible surfaces. First-scan success, need for hole filling, and base-edge completeness were recorded using the same post-processing and quality-control workflow as in the simulation studies.

Post-processing and quality control

Raw point-cloud data were reconstructed and pre-processed in Revo Scan 5 using the software’s default workflow, including mesh generation, artifact removal, and hole filling when required. The resulting meshes were exported in PLY format and imported into Blender for region-of-interest isolation, watertight confirmation, and volumetric measurement. No additional manual mesh-smoothing filter or Blender smoothing modifier was applied; any surface regularization was limited to the default automated meshing procedure in Revo Scan 5, without user-defined smoothing parameters or iteration settings. A common post-processing framework was used across study components, but segmentation and closure were adapted to the acquisition geometry. In the mannequin experiment, posture-specific segmentation followed the predefined breast region of interest. For excised intraoperative tissues scanned on a flat support surface, only the exposed superior and lateral surfaces were directly acquired, and the open basal boundary was closed with a planar cap aligned with the support plane, representing a modeled truncation surface rather than a scanned surface.

Scans with persistent tracking loss or mesh defects >3% were repeated; mesh defect was defined as open-hole surface area divided by region-of-interest surface area on the raw mesh before hole filling. Final meshes had to be watertight and manifold, with chest-wall patch continuity additionally verified in SMM cases. For water displacement, reference volumes were accepted only when triplicate readings showed a CV ≤1.0% and a range ≤1.0% of the mean or ≤2 mL, whichever was more permissive; otherwise, immersion was repeated.

Data analysis

Analyses were prespecified by study component. In the phantom-validation experiment, agreement with syringe-fill reference volume was assessed using linear regression, Bland–Altman analysis, and overall Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient (ρc) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Per-volume summaries are reported as mean ± standard deviation, bias, and median percentage error.

In the mannequin posture experiment, forward-lean and supine measurements were analyzed as paired data within each nominal volume using two-sided paired t-tests with 95% CI. Base-edge completeness (%) was defined as the proportion of the expected basal boundary captured on the pre-edit mesh before hole filling, calculated as:Base−edgecompleteness(%)=100%×LcapturedLreference,where Lcaptured is the directly reconstructed boundary length and Lreference is the total boundary length after closure of the same region. For excised tissues, this boundary was the intersection curve between the specimen surface and the truncation plane; for breast volumetry, it corresponded to the inframammary fold contour.

In the biologic tissue simulation, bias, relative error, and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) were calculated for each scan relative to the water-displacement reference volume, and bias against zero was evaluated using one-sample t-tests. Acquisition time was analyzed as paired mode differences with 95% CI and percentage time reduction relative to Near mode.

Results

Phantom validation

Accuracy was evaluated using five balloon volumes (300–500 mL), each scanned five times (25 paired measurements). Linear agreement with the reference was strong (R² = 0.995; Measured volume (mL) = 0.9868 × reference volume (mL) − 7.70) (Fig. 2A). Bland–Altman analysis showed a mean bias of +2.40 mL with 95% limits of agreement from −7.40 to +12.20 mL and no evident proportional bias (Fig. 2B). Biologic tissue simulation

Near and Far modes were compared in porcine skin-subcutis, porcine skeletal muscle, and bovine skeletal muscle (Fig. 3). All specimens met water-displacement quality-control criteria (triplicate CV 0.17–0.34%), and tissue densities were within expected ranges (Supplementary Table S1). Near mode markedly underestimated porcine skin-subcutis and bovine skeletal muscle (−63.5 and −57.3 mL; both p = 0.002), whereas Far mode showed smaller bias (−7.5 mL, p = 0.212; −5.8 mL, p = 0.027) and was essentially unbiased for porcine skeletal muscle (−0.5 mL, p = 0.340) (Table 3). Intraoperative tissue scanning

Far-mode intraoperative scanning produced volume-computable meshes across pedicled flaps, free flaps, and reduction blocks (Table 4). Base-edge completeness was high overall, increasing from pedicled flaps (median 86.2%, range 80.1–98.0) to free flaps (89.3%, 82.3–96.5) and reduction blocks (94.2%, 86.4–96.8). First‑scan pass was lowest in pedicled flaps (1/3), higher in free flaps (5/7), and highest in reduction blocks (10/10). Hole filling was required in 2/3 pedicled flaps, 2/7 free flaps, and 1/10 reduction blocks. Measured volumes spanned a wide clinical range, with larger free flaps (median 958.1 mL) and intermediate reduction blocks (561.5 mL). Density was available only for reduction blocks (median 1.040 g/mL, range 0.981–1.164). Discussion

This study shows that portable structured light scanning can provide accurate and reproducible volumetry while supporting a practical point of care workflow for breast reconstruction. The main contribution is not only technical validation against reference measures, but also the definition of a reproducible strategy for volumetric assessment in anatomically and operatively challenging settings. Together, these findings support the clinical translation of portable three dimensional volumetry as an objective adjunct to perioperative decision making.

On balloon phantoms, scan-derived volumes showed strong agreement with reference values, with a small mean bias and no evident size-dependent drift across the 300–500 mL range. This error level compares favorably with published breast volumetry studies using established 3D surface imaging platforms such as VECTRA XT, where phantom-based relative differences of about 2% have been reported,13 and is consistent with clinical studies showing close agreement between 3D surface imaging and MRI.6 The small positive bias observed in some phantoms is technically plausible, given the sensitivity of structured-light systems to reflective or highly curved surfaces.14 Overall, the bias remained low and stable, supporting use of this workflow as a technical baseline for the subsequent posture and tissue experiments.

The mannequin study addressed a clinically relevant problem: occlusion at the inframammary fold. The supine condition was designed to approximate a challenging scenario, such as a large or ptotic breast resting against the chest wall, in which the inferior boundary is obscured. In this setting, forward lean positioning improved base-edge completeness and reduced the positive bias observed in supine scans, particularly at larger volumes. This is consistent with prior reports that ptotic lower poles contacting the chest wall can compromise capture of the inferior breast boundary and reduce volumetric reliability.15 By contrast, supine scans required reconstruction of the hidden posterior boundary. In our workflow, this was handled by subtractive modeling within a single coordinate frame, which produced internally consistent estimates but introduced a size-dependent positive bias, likely because digital closure of the chest-only model slightly reduced its volume. These findings support the use of posture-adapted acquisition in practice, with upright scanning as the default approach and modest forward lean considered when the inframammary fold is incompletely visible. Overall, the results reinforce that posture is a major determinant of volumetric validity when the inferior boundary is not directly visible.

The biologic tissue simulation supported Far mode as the more practical setting for flap-like tissue volumetry. Although Near mode provides higher local detail, Far mode achieved greater accuracy, better basal-edge completeness, and faster acquisition, likely because the longer working distance improves field of view, depth tolerance, and tracking stability while reducing self-occlusion at steep edges.16,17 These advantages were also reflected intraoperatively, where Far-mode acquisition produced volume-computable meshes across pedicled flaps, free flaps, and reduction blocks. However, the intraoperative findings should be interpreted in relation to the acquisition geometry described in the Methods: excised tissues allowed more reproducible exposed-surface acquisition, whereas pedicled flaps were more affected by in situ access constraints, basal discontinuities, and hole filling. The small residual underestimate in bovine muscle may relate to the reduced reflectance of darker tissue surfaces.18 Overall, these results suggest that, for practical soft-tissue volumetry, coverage, boundary completeness, and workflow speed are more important than maximizing local surface detail, and that portable structured-light scanning may provide a useful alternative to subjective intraoperative volume estimation.19

The practical significance of this study lies not only in confirming the accuracy of portable three dimensional scanning, but also in showing how it may be applied under conditions that are directly relevant to breast reconstruction. In addition to controlled validation, the study provides a feasible approach for situations in which large or ptotic breasts obscure the inframammary fold and direct boundary delineation becomes difficult, and it supports the feasibility of scanning free tissues under operative conditions. Together, these findings move portable three dimensional scanning closer to practical use across the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative stages of breast reconstruction.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The balloon and mannequin models provided controlled references but could not fully reproduce the optical, mechanical, or anatomical complexity of living tissues. All experiments were conducted at a single center using one scanner platform. The intraoperative component was feasibility-based, lacked an independent reference standard, and, for excised tissues, relied on planar basal closure rather than direct basal-surface acquisition. As an early translational study, this work was designed to establish technical validity and workflow feasibility rather than patient-level outcomes. Future studies should validate this workflow in clinical cohorts, assess multi-operator reproducibility, and compare scan-derived volumes with MRI or standardized intraoperative references.

Conclusion

Portable structured light 3D scanning provided accurate and reproducible volumetry in breast sized phantoms and surgery relevant soft tissues. Beyond technical validation, this study defines a practical and reproducible workflow for breast and flap volume assessment under conditions relevant to reconstructive practice. Portable three dimensional volumetry may therefore serve as an objective point of care adjunct to perioperative assessment in breast reconstruction.
