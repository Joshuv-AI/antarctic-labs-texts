Application of 50-keV virtual monochromatic imaging combined with a personalized contrast agent protocol in computed tomography pulmonary angiography: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Zhi-Wei Liu, Xiao-Li Hu, Chang-Wei Li, Shen Gui, Zhu-Yun Tang, Kun Luo, Hong-Ying Wu, Zi-Qiao Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComputed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) is the preferred imaging modality for the diagnosis of pulmonary embolism (PE). Conventional fixed contrast agent protocols are associated with high contrast volume and severe superior vena cava (SVC) artifacts. This study aimed to evaluate the value of 50-keV virtual monochromatic imaging (VMI) combined with a body weight-adjusted personalized contrast agent protocol in reducing contrast load and optimizing image quality in CTPA.MethodsA total of 76 patients undergoing CTPA were randomly allocated to the control group (Group A; n=38) or the experimental group (Group B; n=38). Since this was a small-sample exploratory study on imaging technology, it was not registered on any official platform. Both groups were scanned with a tube voltage of 120-kVp and automatic tube current modulation. Patients in group A received a fixed contrast protocol (flow rate 4.0 mL/s; volume 40 mL) with 120-kVp reconstruction. Meanwhile, patients in Group B were subjected to a body weight-adjusted protocol with weights of 40–60 kg, 60–80 kg, and 80–100 kg, having a flow rate and volume of 2.3 mL/s and 13 mL, 2.6 mL/s and 15 mL, and 2.9 mL/s and 17 mL, respectively. For all weights, 50-keV VMI reconstruction was conducted. Objective and subjective image quality was compared between the groups.ResultsAll images were diagnostically acceptable. Group B had a significantly lower contrast dose (15.1±0.64 mL; 62.25% reduction) and flow rate (2.55±0.18 mL/s; 36.25% reduction) (both P values <0.05). Group B also demonstrated significantly higher computed tomography (CT) values in the right pulmonary artery [406.3±112.9 vs. 345.9±102.6 Hounsfield units (HU)], right upper lobe pulmonary artery (398.3±109.3 vs. 366.7±116.1 HU), and right lower lobe pulmonary artery (441.1±111.7 vs. 357.2±120.6 HU), as well as improved signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in the right pulmonary artery (18.01±5.70 vs. 14.33±5.23), right upper lobe pulmonary artery (17.67±5.55 vs. 15.17±5.78), and right lower lobe pulmonary artery (19.55±5.66 vs. 14.81±6.10). Similar improvements were observed in contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) values for the corresponding regions. No significant difference was observed in pulmonary artery visualization scores between the two groups (P>0.05), but Group B had higher SVC artifact scores and a lower number of artifacts (P<0.05).ConclusionsIn CTPA examination, 50-keV VMI combined with a personalized contrast agent protocol can improve image quality while substantially reducing both the contrast dose and injection flow rate.

Introduction

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a clinical and pathophysiological syndrome characterized by impaired pulmonary circulation due to the obstruction of the pulmonary arteries or their branches by endogenous or exogenous emboli (1). It is the third most common acute condition in cardiovascular disease, following myocardial infarction and stroke. Approximately 10% of patients with acute PE experience sudden death, with nearly two-thirds dying within 2 hours of symptom onset (1,2). Given its atypical clinical signs and symptoms and high mortality rate, the rapid and accurate diagnosis of PE is crucial for timely therapeutic intervention.

In the imaging-based diagnosis of PE, computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) has become the preferred modality for both the exclusion and confirmation of PE due to its high sensitivity and broad clinical applicability (2,3). In CTPA examination, conventional scanning protocols adopting 120 kVp coupled with a fixed contrast agent protocol (350 mgI/mL, volume =60–70 mL, and flow rate =4–5 mL/s) require a relatively high contrast dose (4-6). This increases the risk of adverse events including contrast-associated nephropathy (CAN) among high-risk patient populations, such as those with renal insufficiency, diabetes mellitus, or dehydration (7). Furthermore, this protocol cannot be tailored to individual body habitus, which tends to result in compromised image quality (4,5).

To address these issues, spectral computed tomography (CT) imaging and a body weight-adjusted contrast agent protocol have been proposed. Both techniques have independently demonstrated the ability to reduce contrast dose and improve image quality (8-10). However, their combined application in pulmonary arterial imaging has not been extensively investigated, and the potential synergistic effects on optimizing image quality and lowering contrast dose remain to confirmed.

It is worth mentioning that spectral CT imaging allows postprocessing reconstruction to generate 161 virtual monochromatic energy levels ranging from 40 to 200 keV (11-13). Higher-keV virtual monochromatic images (VMIs) help reduce beam-hardening artifacts but yield lower image contrast, whereas lower-keV VMIs enhance iodine contrast and improve vascular visualization at the cost of increased image noise (14-16). Currently, 50-keV recognized as the optimal reconstruction energy level for computed tomography angiography (CTA) imaging in clinic, as it can balance image noise while maximizing the visualization effect of iodine contrast agents (17,18). Accordingly, we selected 50 keV as the reconstruction parameter for VMI.

To address the lack of relevant research, we examined the value of combining 50-keV VMIs with a personalized contrast agent injection protocol in CTPA examination, with the aim of enhancing pulmonary arterial image quality while reducing contrast dose. The findings of this study may support the clinical adoption of low-dose CTPA protocol. We present this article in accordance with the CONSORT reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1-2787/rc).

Methods

Patient information

Between June and September 2025, 76 consecutive patients clinically suspected of PE and scheduled for CTPA examination were prospectively enrolled from Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. A parallel-group randomized controlled design was adopted, and thus patients were randomly assigned at a 1:1 ratio to either the control group (Group A) or the experimental group (Group B) via a random number table method, with 38 participants in each group. Group A consisted of 23 males and 15 females, aged 31–77 years (mean age 58.6±12 years). Group B included 19 males and 19 females, aged 33–83 years (mean age 63.7±10.6 years). The study flowchart is shown in Figure 1. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) clinically suspected or confirmed PE, with symptoms such as dyspnea, chest tightness, chest pain, hemoptysis, or lower-limb deep venous thrombosis; (II) no history of allergy to iodinated contrast agents; and (III) age >18 years. Meanwhile, the exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) a history of allergy to iodinated contrast agents; (II) severe heart failure; (III) pregnancy or lactation; and (IV) severe cardiac, hepatic, or renal dysfunction.

Flowchart of participant inclusion. Group A: the control group; Group B: the experimental group. PE, pulmonary embolism.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and was approved by Ethics Committee of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (approval No. 1116-01). Informed consent was obtained from all patients. Due to it being a small-sample exploratory study of imaging technology, this study was not registered on any platform.

Scanning parameters

A dual-layer CT (DLCT) system (IQon Spectral CT, Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam, the Netherlands) was used for all examinations. Both groups underwent scanning with identical parameters (a tube voltage of 120 kVp and automatic tube current modulation) and a low-dose bolus test scan. During the formal scan, Group A received a conventional fixed contrast agent injection protocol with a flow rate of 4.0 mL/s and a total dose of 40 mL. Images for this group were directly reconstructed at 120 kVp. Group B underwent a personalized contrast agent injection protocol based on body weight: patients weighing ≥40 but <60 kg received a flow rate of 2.3 mL/s and a total contrast volume of 13 mL, patients weighing ≥60 but <80 kg received a flow rate of 2.6 mL/s and a total contrast volume of 15 mL, and patients weighing ≥80 but <100 kg received a flow rate of 2.9 mL/s and a total contrast volume of 17 mL. After scanning, 50-keV VMIs were reconstructed.

Subjective image analysis

All images were transferred to a postprocessing workstation (Philips Healthcare). Two radiologists (radiologist A and B), each with substantial experience in cardiothoracic imaging, independently and blindly assessed image quality according to volume rendering (VR), multiplanar reformation (MPR), and maximum intensity projection (MIP). The assessment focused on pulmonary artery visualization and streak artifacts originating from the superior vena cava (SVC). Pulmonary artery visualization was graded as follows: 5 points = clear depiction of sixth-order pulmonary arterial branches; 4 points = clear depiction of fifth-order branches; 3 points = clear depiction of fourth-order (subsegmental) branches; 2 points = visualization of third-order branches; and 1 point = visualization limited to the lobar arteries, the right and left pulmonary arteries, or the main pulmonary artery (MPA) (19-21). SVC streak artifacts were scored as: 3 points = no streak artifacts, 2 points = artifacts confined to the perivascular region of the SVC without affecting diagnostic interpretation, and 1 point = artifacts extending to the right upper pulmonary artery with diagnostic impact.

Objective image analysis

Quantitative measurements were performed on axial images for both groups. Regions of interest (ROIs) were placed to obtain CT values of the vessels (CTVessel), including the main pulmonary artery (CTMPA), the right main pulmonary artery (CTRMPA), the right upper-lobe pulmonary artery (CTRULPA), and the right lower-lobe pulmonary artery (CTRLLPA). At the level of the MPA, the CT value and standard deviation (SD) of the right erector spinae muscle (CTRESM) were also measured, in which the SD of the ROI in the RESM was defined as background noise (BN). The ROI area was maintained between 0.5 cm2 and 1.0 cm2, and each parameter was measured three times, with the average value recorded. Care was taken to avoid pulmonary emboli, linear streak artifacts from the SCV, bone-related artifacts, and other nonrepresentative regions during ROI placement. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) were calculated with the following formulae: SNR = CTVessel/BN; CNR = (CTVessel − CTRESM)/BN.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome was comprehensive CTPA image quality as assessed by objective quantitative indices (CT value, SNR, and CNR of the pulmonary arterial segments) and subjective scoring of pulmonary artery visualization. Secondary outcomes comprised SVC artifact scores, total contrast volume, injection flow rate, and BN of the images.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were tested for normality. For data following a normal distribution, independent-samples t-tests or Welch t-tests were applied; for nonnormally distributed data, the nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test was used. Subjective image quality scores were defined as ordinal categorical data, which were compared between groups with the Mann-Whitney U test. A two-tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Interobserver agreement between the two radiologists was assessed via the kappa statistic, with κ<0.40 indicating poor agreement, 0.40≤κ<0.75 indicating good agreement, and κ≥0.75 indicating excellent agreement.

Sample size calculation

This was a prospective randomized controlled study. The CT value of the CTMPA was the primary endpoint, and the sample size was calculated by the method for comparison of means between two independent samples. A two-sided significance level of α=0.05 and a statistical power of 1−β=0.80 were set. According to the actual measured data in this study, the CTMPA was 374.9±111.3 Hounsfield units (HU) in Group A and 389.2±105.7 HU in Group B, with an intergroup mean difference of 14.3 HU and a pooled SD of 108.5 HU. The calculation indicated that 33 cases per group were sufficient to meet the statistical requirements. In this study, 38 cases were enrolled in each group, for a total sample size of 76 cases, and the actual statistical power reached 91.7%, suggesting a sufficient sample size to fulfill the testing requirements of the study.

Regarding BN and erector spinae muscle values, BN did not differ significantly between the two groups (P>0.05), while CTRESM did (P<0.05), with Group B having a lower CTRESM compared to Group A.

In the comparison of SNR and CNR, Group B demonstrated a higher SNRRMPA, SNRRULPA, and SNRRLLPA compared with Group A (P<0.05), whereas SNRMPA was not significantly different. Finally, both the contrast dose and flow rate were significantly lower in Group B than in Group A (P<0.05), with reductions of 62.25% and 36.25%, respectively.

Discussion

This study employed a randomized controlled design to compare the clinical performance of a conventional fixed contrast injection protocol (Group A) with a protocol combining 50-keV VMIs with a personalized contrast injection (Group B) for CTPA examination. The results demonstrated that the combined protocol of 50-keV VMIs and a personalized contrast injection can ensure the visualization of the pulmonary artery and its branches and mitigate streak artifacts in the SVC while substantially reducing the contrast dose.

The advantage of personalized contrast injection protocol lies in its adaptability to individual patient characteristics. Conventional fixed protocols, such as those administered in Group A, do not account for patient body weight. Therefore, in lighter patients, excessive contrast may accumulate in the SVC causing radial artifacts, while in heavier patients, insufficient contrast may result in suboptimal vascular enhancement, impairing the visualization of the subsegmental pulmonary arteries (22-24). In contrast, in Group B, the contrast injection parameters were adjusted according to body weight, ensuring adequate vascular enhancement across patients of different weights while reducing residual contrast in the SVC. In this study, SVC artifact scores were significantly higher in Group B than in Group A, indicating that a personalized contrast protocol can improve image quality by minimizing artifacts.

CAN is one of the most serious complications of CTA, and the risk of developing CAN mainly depends on the patient’s individual underlying conditions (25,26). In healthy people with normal renal function who are administered modern low-osmolar contrast agents, the incidence rate of CAN is only 1–2% (26); however, in high-risk patients with comorbid chronic kidney disease or diabetes, the risk of CAN increases significantly (27). This is particularly critical for high-risk patients with PE and underlying conditions such as renal insufficiency or diabetes, for whom minimizing contrast volume is key to reducing complication risks (28,29). In this study, while Group A received an average fixed dose of 40 mL, Group B received an average of only 15.1±0.64 mL—a reduction of 62.25%—along with a decrease in flow rate from 4.0 mL/s to 2.55±0.18 mL/s. This not only lowered iodine load but also reduced vascular wall irritation from high-flow injection, which is especially beneficial for older adult patients or those with poor vascular conditions. Notably, despite the substantial reduction in contrast volume, 50-keV VMI reconstruction compensated for potential signal loss, maintaining or even improving subjective vascular visualization scores in Group B compared with Group A.

The results of this study are consistent with those of previous studies on individualized scanning protocols (30,31). In a similar study, Fang et al. adopted (31) an individualized dual-energy CT scanning protocol based on BMI, combined with 60-keV VMIs and 80% adaptive statistical iterative reconstruction-V. This protocol reduced the radiation dose of CTPA by 33.93% and the contrast dose by 56.95% while effectively alleviating SVC beam-hardening artifacts and improving image quality. In contrast, our study used 50-keV VMIs combined with protocol of body weight-adapted contrast agent administration. Without compromising image quality, it achieved a 62.25% reduction in contrast agent volume, which was slightly greater than that reported by Fang et al. Both studies confirm that a core strategy consisting of low-keV VMIs and individualized contrast agent administration can facilitate a low contrast dose while providing high-quality imaging in CTPA. Low-keV protocols can effectively compensate for the signal attenuation caused by low contrast dose, and the individualized protocol can adapt to differences in patient body habitus, reduce the risk of contrast-induced nephropathy, and mitigate beam-hardening artifacts. This study further provides direct evidence for the clinical application of 50-keV VMIs combined with a body weight-determined contrast agent protocol in CTPA.

Certain limitations to this study should be acknowledged: (I) the sample size was relatively small, and selection bias might have been introduced; (II) due to constraints related to contrast dose and radiation exposure, it was difficult to implement a self-controlled study design; (III) this study focused on image quality optimization and did not investigate the impact of the two scanning protocols on the diagnosis of PE.

Conclusions

The combination of 50-keV VMIs with a body weight-based personalized contrast injection protocol enables both dose reduction and image quality improvement in CTPA examination. This scheme is particularly suitable for patients requiring repeated CTPA examination, those with renal insufficiency, or those with poor tolerance to contrast agents and therefore holds substantial value for clinical application.
