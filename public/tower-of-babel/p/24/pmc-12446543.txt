Assessing characteristics of mother–infant dyadic synchrony among vulnerable mothers using the Infant CARE-Index☆
Authors: Camilla Ejlertsen, Ingeborg Hedegaard Kristensen, Anne Brødsgaard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe postnatal period crucially influences the mother–infant relationship and subsequently the infant’s cognitive, physical, and psychosocial development. Poor maternal mental health may impact early interactions, potentially causing the infant’s developmental needs to go neglected.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, we used the Infant CARE-Index to examine mother–infant interaction patterns among mothers with a history of depression and/or anxiety. Ten mother–infant dyads recruited from a Danish obstetric department participated in video recordings of mother–infant interactions three months postpartum. The primary outcome was the assessment of mother–infant dyadic synchrony using observational measures of the Infant CARE-Index when infants were three months old.ResultsMother–infant dyadic synchrony was found to lack sensitivity, with maternal behaviour predominantly exhibiting low sensitivity and, occasionally, control, leading to overall unresponsiveness. The infants in the dyads exhibited moderately cooperative behaviour but with tendencies towards compulsive and challenging behaviours.ConclusionThis study offers novel observational insights into early relational dynamics in mother–infant dyads affected by maternal mental health history—an area underexplored in Danish postnatal care. By applying the Infant CARE-Index, the findings highlight specific behavioural patterns that may hinder bonding and development. These results underscore the need for relationship-based, targeted, and flexible interventions to support vulnerable dyads and promote healthier developmental trajectories.Keywords: Infant CARE-Index, Maternal depression, Maternal anxiety, Mother–infant dyadic synchrony, Maternal mental health

Introduction

A nurturing family environment is essential for a child’s optimal development during early infancy. The quality of the early mother–infant relationship is critical, as the postnatal period is a crucial time for establishing healthy bonds that significantly influence infants’ cognitive, physical, and psychosocial growth [1], [2], [3], [4]. Sensitive parenting – characterised by acceptance, awareness, responsiveness, and cooperation – exerts a profound impact on children’s psychosocial development [5], and a mother’s psychosocial well-being and her ability to engage positively with her infant are the primary determinants in this regard [6], [7], [8]. Recent research has highlighted the role of maternal engagement and psychological flexibility in strengthening early mother–infant bonding, particularly during the postpartum period [3]. Positive dyadic interactions not only promote immediate developmental benefits but also predict improved behavioural outcomes in later childhood [9], [10]. However, maternal mental health challenges, such as depression and anxiety, may diminish dyadic synchrony and hinder optimal mother–infant interactions [11], [12].

Globally, 7–14 % of new mothers experience postpartum depression, with a history of depression increasing the likelihood of onset [7], [13]. Anxiety, which often co-occurs with depression, affects 15–20 % of mothers during the perinatal period, though prevalence varies according to population and cultural context [14]. Both conditions can compromise the quality of early interactions, indicating that support is needed to foster attuned mother–infant interactions and healthy mother–infant relationships [15]. The World Health Organization and the Danish Health Authority advocate for healthcare professionals to assist parents in nurturing relational dynamics, developing healthy relationships with their infants from an early stage [16], [17]. To design effective interventions, it is essential to understand the dynamics of early mother–infant interactions. Mothers who experience depression and/or anxiety often exhibit reduced emotional engagement and responsiveness, which may disrupt the formation of the healthy relationships that are crucial for infant development [2], [18]. However, more nuanced descriptions of how mother–infant interactions manifest are required. Key insights may be attained by employing validated methodological tools, such as the Infant CARE-Index [19], [20], which has been utilised globally across diverse cultural contexts [21]. Insights from observational studies that have implemented this index may enhance our understanding of mother–infant dynamics and inform targeted preventive interventions and health policies addressing postnatal care [22], [23].

This study aims to examine the quality of mother–infant interaction at three months postpartum among mothers with a history of depression and/or anxiety, using observational data analysed with the Infant CARE-Index [20]. By identifying patterns of dyadic synchrony and potential risk signals, this study advances understanding of how maternal mental health challenges may influence early relational development. To our knowledge, it is the first study in a Danish context to apply the Infant CARE-Index to observe and describe mother–infant interaction patterns systematically among mothers with a history of depression and/or anxiety. While prior research has linked maternal mental distress to reduced dyadic synchrony [24], [25], [26], no studies have offered fine-grained, observational insights into everyday, non-stressful interactions during the early postpartum period. By employing a validated observational tool within a universal healthcare setting, this study contributes novel, context-specific data that illuminate how maternal mental health history may shape early relational quality. These findings extend existing literature by revealing interactional nuances that may be overlooked in self-report or diagnostic assessments, thereby providing a stronger empirical basis for targeted and timely postnatal interventions by health visitors and healthcare professionals.

Materials and methods

Study design

The present study adopts a cross-sectional design to provide a perspective on dyadic synchrony in a sample consisting of infants and mothers who have a history of depression and/or anxiety, assessed when the infant was three months old. To ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting, the study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [27].

Setting

This study was conducted in a postnatal setting with families recruited from a large obstetric department in Denmark. Postnatal care, as defined by the World Health Organization , involves healthcare that is provided to mothers and newborns immediately following birth. In Denmark, this care is publicly funded and includes services from nurses, midwives, health visitors, and general practitioners.

National recommendations are provided by the Danish Health Authority [28].

Recruitment

The participants were recruited from a larger sample of the intervention study entitled ‘Look! – Your baby is talking to you'. The first author was responsible for the recruitment at a large hospital in Denmark when families arrived at the obstetric department. The study sample included ten mother–infant dyads. The participants were recruited continuously from 14 June 2021 through 20 October 2021. During the recruitment process, two of the mothers declined to participate, citing discomfort with being video recorded. Eligibility required two criteria. First, the mother had a documented history of depression and or anxiety. Second, she could read Danish and speak Danish or English. Maternal mental health history was confirmed through review of the obstetric medical record and a brief verbal screening interview conducted by the first author before enrolment. Women who met both criteria received oral and written study information.

Data collection

Observational data were collected from video recordings of mother–infant interaction and self-administered questionnaires. Each dyad was video recorded in a five-minute free play session when the infant was three months old. Recordings captured rich interactional detail for qualitative coding. A trained coder who was blind to maternal history analysed the recordings. The primary outcome measure was mother–infant interaction quality measured as mother–infant dyadic synchrony and assessed based on a 5–10-minute mother–infant free play interaction that was videotaped and coded according to the Infant CARE-Index [20].

The Infant CARE-Index, developed by Crittenden (2006), assesses the quality of caregiving during non-stressful infant interactions for infants from birth up to 15 months old. It evaluates the caregiver’s sensitivity and responsiveness to the infant’s needs and behaviours, yielding useful insights into attachment theory and relationship formation. The Infant CARE Index is a validated observational tool that separates sensitive, adequate, and high-risk dyads [29]. It correlates with established attachment measures. The index is applicable to a range of caregiver–infant dyads, including mothers, fathers, and other primary caregivers, serving as a tool in research and clinical practice to enhance caregiving quality and support healthy relationships. When implementing the Infant CARE-Index, maternal and infant behaviours are coded separately, with dyadic synchrony assessed on a scale from 1 to 14. The scores indicate levels of sensitivity: 14–11 signifies sensitive dyadic synchrony, 10–7 indicates adequate synchrony, 5–6 reflects inept synchrony requiring parental education, and scores of 0–4 suggest a need for re-evaluation or intervention. Scores of 2 or below may indicate that the parent in question is unprepared for intervention [20].

Maternal interactive behaviour is assessed on a scale of 0–14, with seven aspects taken into consideration: facial expressions, voice, position/body contact, affection, turn-taking within activities, control between activities, and appropriate choice of activity. These are categorised into sensitive, controlling, and unresponsive behaviours [20]. Similarly, infants’ behaviour is evaluated based on the same aspects with four qualifications: cooperative, compulsive, difficult, and passive, also on a scale of 0–14. Higher scores indicate positive results with respect to maternal sensitivity and infant cooperativeness, while lower scores suggest better outcomes in maternal control and unresponsiveness and infants’ difficult or passive behaviour [20].

The first author recorded the videos at each family’s home approximately three months after the child’s birth. The mother was instructed to play or interact with her infant as she usually would.

The data were subsequently coded independently by three health visitors who were certified in the Infant CARE-Index coding system [20]. Triangulation meetings were organised to ensure consensus and reliability in the coding process prior to its completion with the aim of securing validity. The coders were blinded to maternal background data to minimise potential biases that might arise from any prior knowledge of the mothers’ backgrounds, thereby enhancing the validity and impartiality of the coding process. We generated narrative profiles for every dyad. We compared patterns across dyads to identify common strengths and risks.

The secondary self-administered outcome measures were maternal depressive symptoms measured using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) [30]. This questionnaire comprises ten items rated on a four-point scale (0–3) that assesses symptoms during the preceding week and includes questions pertaining to mood, anxiety, and suicidal ideation. Sum scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating increased symptomology of postpartum depression. As recommended, a cut-off threshold of 11 was applied to identify depressive symptoms [31]. We reported medians and ranges to give readers a simple numeric anchor, but we did not hypothesis tests.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were applied to describe the participants’ demographics. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA software version 15.0 (StataCorp LLP, College Station, TX, USA). Cronbach’s alpha was used to test the inter-rater reliability of the Infant CARE-Index video coding. Inter-rater reliability between the three raters was tested on dyadic synchrony and revealed a satisfactory Cronbach’s alpha of 0.88 for the research level [20].

Ethics

To ensure compliance with the criteria regarding informed consent, all participants received comprehensive oral and written information before providing their written consent. The study received approval from the Danish National Data Protection Agency (reference no. VD-2019–96). In accordance with Danish law, no approval from the Danish Ethical Committee (H-18021300) was required as the study involved no biomedical experiments, and no additional approvals were required. The study was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov, on 19 November 2019 with the identifier NCT04169334.

Summary of synchronization categories

The results of the dyadic synchrony showed a low quality in the mother–infant dyads, which are among the main parts of interactions. Approximately one-third of the mother–infant dyads were evaluated as ‘at risk,’ roughly half as ‘inept’, and two-tenths as ‘adequate,’ whereas one-tenth was categorised as ‘sensitive’. Very few mothers exhibited a sensitive maternal interaction style with their infants but rather varying degrees of controlling, and unresponsive interaction styles were observed. No infants exhibited a passive infant interaction style; rather, they displayed varying degrees of cooperation, compulsiveness, and difficult behavior.

Discussion

This study explored the dynamics of mother–infant synchrony among mothers who had a history of depression and/or anxiety using the Infant CARE-Index. Using five-minute free-play recordings, we produced detailed narrative profiles of ten mother–infant dyads at the three-month postpartum that revealed a nuanced array of interaction patterns. Almost none of these dyads exhibited the expected sensitivity in their synchrony, revealing maternal behaviours marked by low responsiveness and occasional controlling tendencies. Qualitative observations further suggested that infant behaviour varied according to maternal interaction style. Infants of mothers displaying a more unresponsive style tended to appear withdrawn and less engaged. In contrast those of mothers with a more controlling style more often showed signs of frustration or resistance. These patterns are consistent with existing evidence linking low maternal sensitivity to insecure attachment, less satisfying interactions [32], and associating controlling maternal behaviours with increased infant compliance or frustration [33]. The absence of maternal responsiveness was mirrored in the infants’ behaviour, which included moderate cooperation alongside compulsive and challenging tendencies. The complexity of the observed interaction patterns underscores the need for further reassessment, as well as early interventions and collaborative efforts to support vulnerable mothers and infants in navigating their relationships and developmental journeys.

The predominance of low synchrony in vulnerable dyads aligns with previous observational work linking maternal mood disorders to reduced sensitivity and increased control [34], [35]. Our findings expand that evidence to a Danish context and the very early postpartum period. The common combination of controlling maternal behavior and compulsive infant responses reflects Crittenden’s [20] conceptualization of defensive dyadic strategies. Although we observed no completely passive infants, this may be due to age, as passivity tends to rise after repeated misattunements later in infancy. Disruptions in early mother-infant interactions can persist across generations, significantly shaping attachment styles and leaving a lasting mark on attachment patterns in future family relationships [6].

Maternity care providers are crucial for postnatal care, and integrating observational practices into routine care can improve early detection and intervention for attachment issues. A family-based, relationship-oriented approach will strengthen mother–infant interactions and help establish the basis for secure attachment. Methods should be flexible to meet individual needs, supported by a broad framework to address diverse early intervention requirements [4], [36].

This study has several strengths. The Infant CARE-Index enables a detailed and accurate examination of mother–infant interactions, moment by moment. The study’s credibility is strengthened by its high inter-rater reliability and triangulation, which enhance both validity and reliability. Assessing mother–infant interaction quality is inherently complex and remains a topic of ongoing debate. Observational measures are often considered more accurate than self-report tools, as they are conducted independently by trained professionals and can capture subtle relational dynamics [37].

The Infant CARE-Index offers a unique opportunity to identify early patterns of caregiver–infant interaction that may place the dyad at developmental risk before attachment strategies are consolidated. Unlike e.g., the Strange Situation Procedure, which classifies attachment security under stress at around 12 months [5], the Infant CARE-Index can be applied within the first months of life and focuses on relational qualities such as sensitivity, reciprocity, and regulation. These qualities are foundational to later attachment and socio-emotional development [38]. This early applicability makes the Infant CARE-Index particularly valuable in preventive and health-promoting contexts, where timely identification of at-risk dyads can guide supportive interventions before maladaptive patterns become entrenched.

Moreover, evidence from interventions such as the Newborn Behavioural Observations (NBO) suggests that even brief, structured feedback to parents can measurably improve interaction quality, as reflected in Infant CARE-Index ratings [39]. This underscores the utility of the Infant CARE-Index not only as an assessment tool but also as an outcome measure in early relational health programmes. In sum, the Infant CARE-Index contributes uniquely to the field by offering a fine-grained, early observational lens on relational dynamics that complements and extends insights gained from later-stage or stress-based assessments. Its integration into universal healthcare settings holds promise for enhancing early identification and intervention efforts aimed to support vulnerable mother–infant dyads.

This study has several limitations. First, the ongoing recruitment of participants may have resulted in a non-representative sample. The participants were older than the typical first-time mothers in the country, and research suggests that older women are more likely to experience postpartum depression. However, factors such as education, employment, relationship length, and planned pregnancy do not significantly influence postpartum depression. Although the sample size limits the generalizability of the findings, the socio-demographic homogeneity of the participants further constrains their applicability. Most mothers in the study were highly educated and partnered, characteristics associated with greater access to resources, stronger social support networks, and, in some studies, more optimal mother–infant interaction quality. As Golds [34] notes, maternal mental health, socio-economic resources, and social support are key factors shaping behavioural synchrony in the early months. This profile may have attenuated the impact of maternal mental health challenges on dyadic synchrony in our sample compared to more socio-economically diverse populations. The findings should therefore be interpreted with caution when considering mothers from different educational, socio-economic, or relational backgrounds. Additionally, the lack of depressive symptoms in participating mothers during the video recordings raises concerns about social desirability bias or the possibility that mothers with more severe symptoms chose to opt out. If more depressed mothers had participated, dyadic synchrony might have been more adversely affected.

EPDS scores obtained at three months postpartum may not fully reflect changes in maternal mental health throughout the entire postpartum period. Moreover, observing and rating interactions with the Infant CARE Index can influence maternal behaviour itself [40]. The relationship between the observer and the mother may also impact the authenticity of the interactions, with trust potentially encouraging natural behaviour. Conversely, discomfort or a lack of confidence might distort the data. The fact that the first author recruited and recorded the participants and was their sole contact may have made the mothers feel more comfortable, possibly leading to more positive interactions recorded. Early intervention is recommended to improve early interactions, especially through collaborative approaches between maternity care providers within and across sectors and the families. The findings of this study underscore the importance of addressing disruptions in early mother–infant interactions, as they impact attachment classifications, socio-emotional development, and family well-being. The integration of studies on patterns and characteristics of dyadic synchrony with studies on maternal depression and anxiety may reveal pathways for supportive and preventive strategies.

The use of reliable assessment tools such as the Infant CARE-Index can assist clinicians and researchers in identifying atypical caregiving patterns, allowing for timely interventions to enhance early interactions in high-risk parent–infant dyads. Danish postnatal services do not offer additional standardised relational support to mothers with previous mental disorders. Our data indicate that such targeted services could be justified even when current depressive symptoms are mild. Given the difficulties in dyadic synchrony, early interventions focusing on parent–infant interactions and parenting skills are crucial globally. Bowlby [41] and Ainsworth [42] established attachment theory, which highlights the importance of these early interactions. The findings of the present study may be used to strengthen interventions that emphasise the mother–infant relationship, emotional security, and infant development.

Future research should examine how dyadic synchrony can help develop more targeted interventions for mothers with a history of depression or anxiety, as these dyads may encounter difficulties in fostering responsive interactions. Incorporating the NBO [43], [44] into postnatal care may support early detection of attachment-related issues and strengthen maternal–infant bonding. The NBO assists caregivers in understanding infant cues and encourages bonding. Integrating the NBO allows for proactive identification of attachment challenges, facilitating timely interventions and ongoing monitoring of progress. Integration of the NBO into clinical practice, particularly in the context of maternal mental health, could allow healthcare professionals to support better mother–infant interactions, promoting secure and responsive bonds. Tailored early interventions, initiated by maternity care providers and extending into primary healthcare, are crucial for fostering attuned and developmentally relationships in both infants and their parents [39], [44], [45].
