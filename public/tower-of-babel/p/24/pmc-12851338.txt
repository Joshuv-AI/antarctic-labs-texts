Health effects at the intersection of climate change and structural racism in the United States: A scoping review
Authors: Suellen Breakey, Donna Hovey, Margaret Sipe, Patrice K Nicholas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionClimate change and structural racism are significant threats to public health and health equity. The impacts of each individual problem on human health and well-being have been documented; what is less known is the impact of the intersection of climate change and structural racism on health.PurposeTo conduct a scoping review to examine the emerging knowledge related to the impact of climate change and structural racism on the health and well-being of racialized minority groups in the United States.MethodsA scoping review was conducted using the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and PubMed, CINAHL, and PsychInfo databases. Two searches were conducted between 11/2021 and 7/2023. Data were extracted and analyzed; and themes were identified.ResultsOf 148 full-text reviews, 28 were included in the final synthesis. Texas was the US state most represented in research studies. Black/African American and Hispanic/Latino populations were the most discussed and studied populations. The overarching theme identified was the enduring legacy of historical redlining on inequitable climate and environmental exposures. Four subthemes included environmental justice impacts, climate justice impacts, ecopandemic injustice, and the emergence of syndemics.ConclusionClimate justice, environmental justice and structural racism have individual impacts on health; however, they are inextricably linked and have wide-ranging impacts on health and well-being. Implications for education, clinical practice, policy and advocacy, and research are outlined. Health providers have an opportunity to partner with disinvested communities to develop realistic strategies to create more resilient, vibrant communities that will lead to improved health outcomes.Keywords: Climate change, Climate justice, Environmental justice, Structural racism, Health, Historical redlining

Introduction

Climate change has been identified as a significant threat to both public health and health equity [1]. Climate-related health impacts in the United States (US) and globally are predicted to increase as the planet continues to warm [2]. Similarly, in recent years, there has been an increased focus on the negative influence of structural racism on health and well-being. Racial/ethnic populations as well as economically, geographically, and socially disadvantaged communities experience disproportionate inequities in access to medical care resulting in less preventative services, higher maternal mortality, higher premature mortality, and differences in treatment outcomes [3]. Moreover, racially, and ethnically minoritized populations are disproportionately impacted by the negative health consequences of climate change despite contributing least to greenhouse gas emissions, amplifying health inequities [4]. Numerous sources are available that highlight racial disparities in the occurrence of negative climate change-related health outcomes [[4], [5], [6]]. However, more recently in the US, particularly since 2020, there has been a shift from discussing the racial disparities associated with climate change to a more explicit focus on the relationship between structural racism, climate exposures, and negative health impacts [7,8]. The purpose of this scoping review is to examine how climate change and structural racism taken together impact health and well-being in the US.

Background and significance

Impact of climate change on health and well-being

The World Meteorological Organization confirmed that 2015 to 2023 were the hottest eight years on record [9]. Higher atmospheric temperatures are leading to more frequent extreme weather events, drought, wildfires, food- and water insecurity, and climate migration resulting in increases in heat-related illness, allergies, cardiovascular, pulmonary, renal, and neurological diseases, vector-borne and water-borne illnesses, poor birth outcomes, and negative impacts on mental health [10]. Those populations most at risk for climate change-related health impacts include older adults, children, low-income communities, Indigenous Peoples, those living with chronic physical and mental conditions, pregnant people, and communities of color–particularly those in historically redlined areas—as well as those living in coastal areas, on islands, or in areas prone to wildfires [11,12].

Structural racism and its impact on health and well-being

Prior to 2020, many studies focused on the role of personally mediated racism on health, with less emphasis on structural racism and health inequities [13]. Since the death of George Floyd in 2020, a greater focus has been placed on structural racism and its disproportionate impact on health inequities. As Bailey and colleagues point out, there is no one agreed upon definition of structural, institutional, or systemic racism [11]; but they are closely related concepts that highlight the role of laws, rules, and practices embedded within our economic system and cultural and societal norms which contribute to ongoing racism. Further, the authors identify the historical context of racism in three interrelated domains and their impact on individual and population health: racialized residential segregation (i.e., historical redlining), mass incarceration and police violence, and unequal medical care [11]. In addition to unequal medical care in the broader sense, Lekas and colleagues identify structural racial injustices specifically in research and practice as well [14].

Braveman et al. (p. 131) describe systemic and structural racism as “forms of racism that are pervasive and deeply embedded in and throughout systems, laws, written or unwritten policies, entrenched practices, and established beliefs and attitudes that produce, condone, and perpetuate widespread unfair treatment of people of color [15].” They identified five examples of systemic racism that have implications for health. These include political disempowerment, segregation, financial practices, environmental injustice, and racial patterning within the criminal justice system. The health of individuals and communities are affected by systemic and structural racism by depriving people of essential resources that effect health such as clean water, affordable housing free of environmental hazards, access to quality education, access to medical care, and financial opportunity. Poor health outcomes associated with racial segregation include poor birth outcomes, increased exposure to air pollution, premature mortality, increased risk of chronic disease, and increased risk of homicide [13]. Further, the disproportionate representation of Black men, women, and children that Bailey and colleagues identify is rooted in structural racism [13]. Few studies have examined the direct effects of incarceration and racial disparities [16]. Direct effects of incarceration on health include an increased burden of infectious diseases, chronic medical conditions and substance use disorders [16]. Incarceration can also have indirect effects of the health of children and families as well as communities of those who are incarcerated related to financial hardship, stress, lack of time, and potential lack of social support [16]. In addition to racial bias, interpersonal racism, and discrimination that persists in healthcare settings, access and quality of care delivery have been influenced by racial segregation and the social and economic disinvestment in communities of color [11,13]; this has led to lower quality facilities, difficulty attracting primary care providers and specialists, and lower qualifications of healthcare providers who practice in these communities. Lastly, increased allostatic load and chronic stress that people of color experience as a result of personally-mediated racism leads to poor health outcomes, for example, cardiovascular sequelae [15].

Materials and methods

A scoping review was conducted to answer the question: What is the impact on health related to the intersection between climate change and structural racism in the United States? The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for conducting scoping review was utilized [17]. The JBI approach builds on the work of Arksey and O'Malley and Levac et al. and was chosen to examine the emerging knowledge specifically related to the intersection of climate change, structural racism, and health [18,19]. A scoping review protocol predefines the objectives, methods, and reporting of the review to ensure transparency will guide the process. The elements of the scoping review protocol include title, development of the question, introduction, inclusion criteria, search strategy, sources of evidence selection, data extraction, analysis of the evidence, and presentation of the results [17]. The Preferred Reporting Item for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-SCR) was used as the reporting guideline for this review. A Populations/Concept/Context (PCC) framework (Table 1) was used to determine the search strategy (Table 2). Databases searched included PubMed, CINAHL, and OVID PsychInfo.

Search Strategy.

Two searches were conducted with the assistance of a health services librarian. The initial search was conducted on November 4, 2021, and included peer-reviewed articles from 2015. An updated search was conducted with a health services librarian using the same search strategy for the time period of November 2021 through July 5, 2023. Ancestry and hand searching was also completed. Inclusion criteria consisted of articles written in English and articles that specified climate change, structural, systemic racism, and health in the US. Articles that discussed historical redlining in relation to climate change and health were also included. Exclusion criteria included books/book chapters, publications written in languages other than English, and articles that addressed climate change, racism, and health in countries other than the US. Articles that discussed racial disparities but did not include the terms systemic or structural racism were excluded.

Screening was done in a two-step process. Titles and abstracts were screened by two team members (SB and DH). Articles deemed appropriate after this stage then underwent independent full-text review by two team members (SB and DH). Any discrepancies were discussed and resolved. Results

Of the 144 manuscripts that underwent full-text review, 28 were included in the final synthesis. The majority of papers were discussion papers [n = 13], followed by quantitative research studies [n = 7]. Table 4 provides the articles by type and number. Most articles included in this review were found in public health [n = 11] and medical literature [n = 6]. Table 5 provides a list of all disciplines represented in this review. Texas was the US state most represented in research studies [[20], [21], [22]]. Two studies had representative samples of the US [23,24]. Two studies were conducted in Southern US [25,26]; and one was conducted in the Northeast [27]. Black/African American and Hispanic/Latino populations were most discussed and studied populations in the articles included in this review. Asian/Pacific Islander, Indigenous, and American Indian/Alaska Native populations were also identified; some articles used the broader term Black, Indigenous, People of Color (BIPOC) in their discussion of the impact of structural racism and climate change on health.

The enduring legacy of historical redlining on inequitable climate and environmental exposures was the overarching theme identified across articles in response to the question, “What is the impact on health related to the intersection between climate change and structural racism in the United States?” The direct and indirect effects of historical redlining, racial segregation, and the subsequent disinvestment in certain communities of color were identified by most authors as the major contributors to present day structural, economic, and environmental inequities that are contributing to poor health and creating increased risk to both climate change impacts (e.g., extreme heat) and emerging challenges such as the COVID-19 pandemic. Four subthemes were identified in this scoping review that arise from the larger overarching theme: environmental injustice impacts, climate injustice impacts, eco-pandemic injustice, and the emergence of syndemics. While these subthemes are interconnected, and in many ways inextricably linked, individual characteristics of each subtheme were identified and are outlined below.

Environmental injustice impacts

Environmental injustice impacts stemming from structural racism that amplify the negative impacts of climate change were identified in eight publications [7,8,20,25,[27], [28], [29], [30]]. Heat exposure risk from a lack of tree cover, increased impervious surfaces, and increased land surface temperatures leads to negative birth outcomes, as well as increases in heat-related illnesses (HRI), asthma, acute myocardial infarction, and poorer mental health [20,27,28]. Exposure to toxic chemicals and other forms of land and air pollution contribute to higher rates of childhood asthma in communities of color [29], in addition to poor birth outcomes and increased rates of HRI, cardiovascular disease, and asthma [8,25]. Morello-Frosch and Obasogie contend these health risks are related to the establishment of sacrifice zones in certain communities, resulting from structural racism resulting in disproportionate exposure to poor air quality [8]. Liddell and Kington identify the relationship between environmental and reproductive injustice, which not only increases chronic diseases but also contributes to infertility, gynecological cancer, and poor pregnancy outcomes including miscarriage, eclampsia, and preterm birth (PTB) in Indigenous groups in the Gulf South [25]. Two papers discussed the combined effects of extreme heat and air pollution and their role in increasing HRI and asthma [7,30]. Lastly, access to healthcare related to historical redlining and the subsequent disinvestment in segregated communities was identified as not only a barrier to good health but also a risk multiplier for the negative health impacts of climate change [22,[25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30], [31]].

Climate injustice impacts

Fourteen articles included in this review identified the impact of inequitable climate exposures on health resulting from historical redlining and other forms of structural racism. These consequences fell into one of two categories: the impact of extreme heat on health and the effect of climate change on physical infrastructure and its influence on health. Extreme heat, which is increasing because of climate change negatively impacts reproductive outcomes, particularly in early pregnancy [20,31]. The disproportionate effects of extreme heat in historically redlined communities amplify heat vulnerability [24], resulting in increases in heat-related mortality and exacerbation of comorbidities [32], premature death from stroke [33], and increases in heat-related emergency room and outpatient visits [22,31]. Similar to environmental inequities, climate injustice leads to increases in asthma morbidity and mortality in Black children [31].

Extreme heat can also lead to poor mental health outcomes, particularly for children. Children in Black communities may experience more intense eco-trauma, eco-stress, and other mental health sequela [31]. Housing displacement due to extreme heat can lead to academic disruption for children as well as an increased risk of being the victim of child trafficking [29,31]. Interestingly, Mason and colleagues found that a lower proportion of Black adults living in low-income communities in the Southeastern United States reported mental health issues related to extreme heat and winter weather than White adults [26]. They postulate that a higher level of resilience in Black adults may be the result of coping with the cumulative stress associated with ongoing racism.

Several papers describe the effects that climate hazards have on already weakened infrastructures and how current and future climate change impacts such as disasters and extreme weather events will disproportionately impact the health of those living in marginalized communities, further widening health gaps [23,[34], [35], [36]]. Prins and Story examined the relationship between mass incarceration, health inequities, and climate change [37]. As lower-income residents of color are being displaced, those who are incarcerated experience threats to their health during extreme weather events, for example, hurricanes, because of rising sea levels and damage to infrastructure leading to lack of power, water, food, and proper ventilation. Increased sensitivity to climate hazards related to weakened infrastructure and subsequent climate exposures leads to increases in infectious diseases, waterborne disease, increases in HRI, cardiac and pulmonary disease, trauma, and cancer [35,36,38]. Grineski and colleagues describe the concept of cascading disasters—extreme events that create progressive interactions between environmental hazards, social vulnerabilities, and infrastructure [21]. The authors discuss how our dependence on large physical infrastructures (for example water, power, broadband internet) highlight our vulnerabilities in the event of an extreme climate event. Further, disruptions or damage to infrastructure impact mental health, disproportionately burdening low-income communities of color and those living with disability. Similarly, Huerta-Montanez and Bernstein describe the significant impact of Hurricanes Maria and Fiona in Puerto Rico on the physical infrastructure and how it specifically jeopardized the health of medically disabled and low-income children [39]. Alhasan and colleagues found that food insecurity was associated with poor sleep health. They point out those living in communities of color who have significantly less access to grocery stores may be disproportionately impacted as climate change continues to threaten food production and supply [23].

Eco-pandemic injustice

Five articles discuss what Powers et al. refer to as the concept of eco-pandemic injustice [40]. Specifically, they identify increased air pollution exposure, which is being exacerbated by climate change as well as environmental contamination as contributors to increased rates of COVID-19 infection rates, morbidity, mortality, and poor mental health outcomes in communities of color and low wealth communities. Other factors identified include housing segregation, appropriation of tribal lands and land use [41]. Black Americans and Hispanic or Latino individuals were identified as most at risk of eco-pandemic injustice across all studies [21,[40], [41], [42], [43]]. Indigenous and Pacific Islander populations as well as BIPOC more broadly were also identified [40,42]. Senay et al. point out that Blacks are twice as likely as Whites to die from COVID-19 related to environmental disparities in air pollution exposure, worsened by climate change, housing segregation, and zoning discrimination [41]. Watson et al. highlight how the interconnectedness of climate change, racism, and the pandemic has led to both individual and collective trauma [43]. Lastly, Grineski and colleagues found that the cascading events of Winter Storm Uri—an extreme cold weather event in Texas—and COVID-19 tripled the odds of having post-traumatic stress in Black and Hispanic individuals [21].

The emergence of syndemics

Syndemics co-occur in temporal or geographical contexts due to harmful social conditions and interact at the population or individual level. The result is mutual enhancement of the negative effects of each condition [44]. Three articles identified syndemics, highlighting the negative impact of climate change and structural racism on health. Al-Aly identified that higher levels of air pollution, decreased access to mitigation strategies, and decreased access to healthcare as a result of historical redlining negatively impact kidney health [45]. NASEM in their proceedings from a workshop entitled, Communities, Climate Change, and Health Equity, identify the syndemic of climate change, environmental injustice, and structural racism on poor health and premature death [46]; and Schenk et al. identified the syndemic of climate change, COVID-19, and racism and its disproportionate impact on mortality and death rates in Pacific Islander, Black, Latinx, and Indigenous communities [42].

Discussion

The results of this review underscore the inextricable and intersecting relationship between environmental injustice, climate injustice, and structural racism and its significant, inequitable, and wide-ranging impact on physical and mental health outcomes in adults, pregnant people, and children. Salas identifies environmental racism as, “…the use of racist practices in determining which communities receive health-protective infrastructure, such as green space, and which receive health-harming highways and industrial complexes” [7]. To that end, weakened physical infrastructure in historically redlined communities has resulted in a lack of adaptive capacity–the ability to prepare for and respond to climate hazards. The consequence is a decrease in both individual and community resilience related to limited protection from climate-related health harms. The themes that were identified in this review support the idea that environmental racism negatively impacts health and explains how climate change and exposure to climate hazards contribute further to health risks and poor health outcomes in these communities. Therefore, it is critical that strategies to address the health impacts of climate change must take into consideration the impact of structural racism on social, economic, and environmental determinants of health. These findings have significant implications for health professions education, clinical practice, research, and future policy development.

Implications for education

The results of this review underscore the importance of ensuring that current and future health professionals have knowledge of how both climate change and structural racism impact the environments of individuals, populations, and communities and ultimately their health. These results also highlight how social determinants–particularly access to quality healthcare and the built environment—are inextricably linked to health and are shaped by the historical context of structural racism and the adaptive capacity of both individuals and communities to respond to climate impacts. Therefore, it is imperative that future and currently practicing clinicians and public health professionals understand these linkages and have the necessary skills to identify the potential environmental and climate risks for individual patients and communities to plan and deliver optimal, comprehensive, and realistic care. Lastly, health professions curricula must go further in health policy education to emphasize action and teach advocacy skills so that healthcare providers and public health professionals can lead in developing targeted upstream solutions to protect frontline and fence line communities from the negative consequences of climate change.

Implications for clinical practice

The results of this review identify the effects of structural and environmental racism on individual and population health and highlight the myriad ways that these issues intensify the risk for exposure to climate hazards for those living in disinvested communities. Therefore, it is essential that consistent implementation of risk assessments that focus on social determinants of health and climate hazards are used across all practice settings [47]. To that end, there are resources clinicians can apply in practice. For example, the Heat-related Illness Screening Tool considers environmental, physical, and social factors as well as populations who are at risk for HRI, and the A-CLIMATE tool provides a structure for incorporating climate change into clinical care [48,49]. Additionally, the Environmental Protection Agency has developed the Environmental Justice Mapping Tool, which provides location-specific data related to environmental and climate hazards as well as social determinants of health and health disparities. The use of these types of tools will allow health care providers to have a more complete understanding of the patients and communities with whom they work to develop more comprehensive strategies—both upstream and downstream—to improve health outcomes.

Implications for research

While numerous studies have examined the impact of structural racism on health as well as racial disparities related to the health impacts of climate change, there are fewer studies that have addressed climate change and structural racism specifically. Notably, the research studies that were included in this review were mainly focused on the Southeast region of the United States. Moreover, Black/African American and Hispanic/Latino populations were the populations most addressed. Therefore, further research in other regions of the US with different populations is important since climate risks vary geographically. The National Institutes of Health (NIH) has developed a framework that identifies climate change research needs that takes into consideration the social, behavioral, and environmental determinants of health (Fig. 2). This conceptual model is useful because it acknowledges the inextricable link between structural racism—embedded within the social, economic, and structural determinants of health—and climate change and underscores the importance of considering these intersecting issues when identifying areas for research.

Impacts of Climate Change on Human Health and Associated Research Needs (NIH, 2022).

Given the interrelationship among environmental justice, climate justice, and structural racism, one essential element of future research is to ensure that partners from the communities and populations under study are involved in all aspects of the research. Using methods such as community-based participatory research (CBPR) and citizen scientists will help to ensure the role of community partners. Healthcare providers are well-positioned to collaborate in CBPR and to lead research efforts related to community-based climate and health education as well as testing interventions aimed at individual and community adaptation strategies. Lastly, the complexity of integrating the role of social and structural determinants of health, including racism, into climate change-related public health and clinical research is often best suited by an interprofessional approach and can lead to more robust knowledge development.

Implications for policy

Policy change is necessary to address the negative health outcomes, which are intensified at the intersection of climate change and structural racism. Local, national, and federal policies to address climate change must center the role of environmental justice and health equity [8]. Moreover, scientific evidence that exposes inequities and prioritizes the needs of environmental justice communities must be used to determine legislative action to advance racial justice and climate and health equity [35].

Healthcare professionals, as trusted professionals, can inform policy by acting and advocating for upstream solutions to address the negative health impacts of climate change and climate change mitigation strategies more broadly. For example, clinicians can recommend to the leaders in their workplace that SDOH and climate-related health risk screening be embedded within the practice setting and used consistently as a necessary first step to informing practice as well as policy.

Conclusion

Climate justice, environmental justice and structural racism have individual impacts on health; however, they are inextricably linked and have wide-ranging impacts on the health and well-being of pregnant women, adults, and children. These findings have implications for education, clinical practice, policy, and research. Communities and populations who are most impacted must be included as stakeholders in decision making and their voices must be centered in clinical practice and policy decision making. Health providers and public health professionals have an opportunity to partner with disinvested communities to develop realistic strategies to create more resilient, vibrant communities that will lead to improved health outcomes.
