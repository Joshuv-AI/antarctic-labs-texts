Protocol RCT for active informed consent in spine and urologic surgery in the metropolitan city of Bologna: does an increased patient engagement improve satisfaction of complex surgical procedures?
Authors: Luca Boriani, Irene Quattrini, Bruna Maccaferri, Chiara Lima, Alice Benuzzi, Marco Salvador, Riccardo Schiavina, Eugenio Brunocilla, Federico Deiure, Vania Maselli, Roberta De Stefano, Francesco Vommaro, Alessandro Gasbarrini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInformed consent is an essential component of surgical care; however, patients often struggle to fully understand procedures, associated risks and available alternatives. Factors such as preoperative anxiety, limited health literacy and the complexity of consent documents can further impair comprehension and information retention. The active informed consent (CIA) pathway, based on a Patient Educational Program that combines multimedia resources with a comprehension test, aims to enhance patient understanding, improve satisfaction and reduce medicolegal issues.Methods and analysisThe study will be conducted as a multicentre, non-pharmacological, randomised controlled trial in three hospitals in the Emilia-Romagna region (Italy). A total of 300 patients undergoing elective complex spinal surgery or robotic radical prostatectomy will be enrolled and randomised (1:1) to the experimental arm or to the standard informed consent arm, using block randomisation stratified by centre. Outcomes will include patient satisfaction (Client Satisfaction Questionnaire), comprehension, psychological distress (Depression Anxiety Stress Scales), pain (Numeric Rating Scale), functional recovery (Oswestry Disability Index/International Prostate Symptom Score/International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire Short Form/International Index of Erectile Function) and medicolegal complaints. Assessments will be performed at baseline (T0), discharge (T1), 2 months (T2) and 6 months (T3), with extended monitoring of medicolegal outcomes for up to 5 years.Ethics and disseminationThe study has been approved by the Regional Ethics Committee of Emilia-Romagna (protocol CIA21, V.1.3 dated 14 December 2022). Participation is voluntary and does not affect standard care. Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals, conference presentations and communication with health authorities. If effective, the intervention may be implemented as a scalable model to improve patient empowerment and transparency in surgical consent.Trial registration numberNCT06059599.

Introduction

Modern surgical informed consent will be intended to ensure that patients are completely informed and capable of making their own treatment decisions. However, the actual consent process is often suboptimal. Many patients undergoing complex surgery fail to understand or remember much of the information provided during consent discussions.1 2 According to studies conducted on surgical patients, immediately after a standard consent discussion, patients only remember around 45% of the key information, and by 6 weeks after the procedure, that number has dropped to about 18%.3 4 Furthermore, over 20% of patients and caregivers may not remember any risks that were discussed, and only 20–40% recall the common risks, in some settings. Multiple factors contribute to poor understanding and retention of information: preoperative anxiety, limited health literacy, complex and lengthy consent documents (often requiring years of higher education to understand), language barriers and the stress of needing to make quick decisions under pressure.57

An ‘informed’ consent is truly valid only if the patient understands and remembers the nature of the proposed surgery, its expected benefits, potential risks/complications and available alternatives.8 9 Inadequate informed consent not only undermines patient autonomy and safety, but also has legal ramifications—failure to ensure proper informed consent is a leading cause of medicolegal complaints and can be considered negligence under the law.10 Yet current standard practice provides no objective way to verify that the patient has genuinely understood the information.

According to our internal data referring to the Spine Surgery Unit and collected by the Public Relations Office of the Rizzoli Orthopaedic Institute, 22 complaints were received between 2016 and 2020. Of these, 50% were related to communication problems. Likewise, the S. Orsola-Malpighi University Hospital reports that during the 2013–2019 observation period, urology-related legal claims accounted for slightly less than 30% of the company’s surgical-related claims, ranking third behind obstetrics and gynaecology (35%) and general surgery (53%) in the same timeframe.

There is growing evidence that multimedia tools and enhanced communication strategies can improve the informed consent process. Powell et al11 proposed a structured, patient-centred consent model for spinal surgery, known as the ‘three-legged stool’ (TLS). The TLS includes written information (clear, illustrated booklets), patient-centred dialogue (tailored discussions about risks, benefits and alternatives) and procedure-specific consent forms (signed with time for reflection and questions). This model responds to legal requirements and aims to improve patient understanding, reduce litigation and ensure ethical, informed decision-making. Video-based and interactive digital interventions were shown to increase patient knowledge retention and satisfaction compared with traditional paper consent forms.2 12 13 For instance, a randomised trial in vascular surgery showed that supplementing the verbal consent with a brief video significantly improved patients’ understanding of the procedure and led to higher satisfaction with the consent process.4 Further studies have found that providing patients with digital support or video about their surgery led to greater long-term retention of information and better clinician–patient communication.3 14 A systematic review and meta-analysis showed that electronic informed consent technologies significantly improved patient satisfaction compared with standard informed consent practices.14 In spine surgery, models based on shared decision-making (SDM) have yielded positive outcomes: patients given decision-aid videos and extended discussions before surgery gained more knowledge and made choices more consistent with their values.5 12 15 Involving family members in the consent process and giving patients time to consider information can further improve understanding and adherence to postoperative regimens.3

Building on this evidence, we propose a novel approach called ‘active informed consent (CIA)’ for patients undergoing complex spinal or urological surgeries. The CIA approach, based on the principles of informed consent and SDM, proposed an innovative Patient Education Program (PEP) for the preoperative consent process. The aim of this study is to suggest an interactive educational experience that improves patient comprehension, satisfaction and decision quality instead of the traditional form-signing event. We hypothesise that an active, multimedia-based consent process will lead to better informed patients who feel more satisfied with their care, experience less anxiety and possibly even achieve better postoperative outcomes. Additionally, by ensuring truly informed consent, this approach could reduce postoperative decisional regret and medicolegal issues caused by lack of information. To test these hypotheses, we designed a multicentre randomised controlled trial (RCT) study comparing the new multimedia-based active consent protocol against the standard consent process in two surgical domains (spine and urology surgery). The standard informed consent procedure was selected as the comparator because it represents the current routine clinical practice across participating centres and provides an appropriate benchmark against which to evaluate the added value of the CIA model.

Objectives and trial design

The primary objective of this study is to determine whether a multimedia-based, CIA process can improve the quality of the patient’s preoperative experience, as reflected by higher patient satisfaction. We posit that patients receiving the enhanced, structured multimedia consent will report greater satisfaction and perceived quality of care compared with those receiving standard consent. Second, we decided to evaluate the effect of the CIA intervention on patient decisions. In particular, we wanted to verify whether the increased information leads to more patients making an informed choice consistent with their values, including the option to decline surgery in favour of conservative management. We hypothesise that fully informed patients may sometimes decide against a high-risk surgery in favour of less invasive alternatives if those alternatives align better with their preferences. Moreover, another secondary objective was to evaluate the intervention’s effect on patient anxiety, stress and depression related to the surgical decision. We hypothesise that patients who receive more information (via video and extended discussions) will exhibit lower presurgical anxiety than those in the standard consent group. In addition, we supposed that well-informed patients may have more realistic expectations and better coping strategies, which could be reflected in their postoperative recovery with better pain management and more engagement in rehabilitation. In conclusion, we wanted to determine whether the CIA process could lead to a reduction in medicolegal complaints related to information and communication.

Methods and analysis

Study setting

This study will be designed as an interventional, non-pharmacological, RCT with a superiority framework. It is a multicentre, two-arm parallel-group trial comparing the experimental intervention called ‘active informed consent’ and the standard informed consent. The study will take place across three hospitals in the Emilia-Romagna region of Italy, involving both spine surgery and urology units. Specifically, the participating centres are the Istituto Ortopedico Rizzoli Spine Surgery Unit and the AUSL di Bologna—Ospedale Maggiore Spine Surgery Unit for vertebral patients; instead, for urological surgery, the units involved are the one in Azienda Ospedaliero-Universitaria di Bologna—Policlinico S. Orsola-Malpighi, and the one in AUSL di Bologna—Ospedale Maggiore. All centres are high-volume tertiary care institutions where complex spinal instrumented fusions and robotic radical prostatectomies are performed. The study will be coordinated by the lead site at Rizzoli Orthopaedic Institute, with local investigators at each participating hospital. The study was scheduled to start on 19 June 2024 and to be completed in October 2026, with an additional medical-legal follow-up monitoring period of up to 5 years.

The trial is open-label due to the nature of the intervention: participants and clinicians will be aware of which consent process is used. The study will be an RCT with allocation 1:1, and a software will generate the randomisation. Patients will be divided into two groups. Group 1 will be treated with PEP with multimedial aids for consent, and group 2 will be treated with traditional consent. The study aims to enrol 300 patients stratified by centre. Assuming a common SD of 5 points on the Client Satisfaction Questionnaire (CSQ-8) scale in both arms, the planned sample size of 300 patients provides 80% power to detect a mean difference of 1.6 points between the two randomisation arms, assessed during hospitalisation before surgery, with a two-sided type I error rate of 5%.

The overall study duration is expected to be 36 months of enrolment plus follow-up. Each individual patient will be in the study from the time of consent (which occurs preoperatively, typically between a few weeks and 2 months before surgery) through a first follow-up at 2 months post-surgery to a second follow-up at 6 months post-surgery for outcome assessments (figure 1). The study staff will only receive the allocation after a participant has been fully enrolled. They will have access exclusively to the current participant’s assignment, without the ability to view past or future allocations or the underlying sequence.

Population and participants

Patients scheduled for elective spine surgery (such as spine fusion) or robot-assisted radical prostatectomy will be enrolled. Eligible procedures are elective surgeries in orthopaedic and urologic fields where the consent process is challenging due to the complexity of information.

Intervention

CIA process

The first step of the project concerned the preparation of the innovative materials of CIA. PEP will be specific for each procedure (spine vs urology), explaining both the diagnosis, the beneficial outcome and potential risks of any surgical procedure and other alternative available treatments with written, graphic and digital materials (website, explanatory PDF documents, 3D models, figures and audio-visual animations). The videos are designed to help patients understand their treatment possibilities: approximately 10–15 min in length, the video will explain the patient’s diagnosis and all proposed treatment options (including the recommended surgery and any alternative treatments such as conservative management). They use lay-friendly language and rich visuals (animations, graphics) to illustrate the nature of the surgery, the risks and potential benefits and expected outcomes. 3D-printed anatomical models were created to support surgeons in explaining the diagnosis and the possible types of intervention to the patient.

PEP is available on a website that serves as a repository for the information materials. It offers dedicated content to explore, as thoroughly as possible, the surgical treatment that patient will undergo, including expected benefits, risks of complications and related mortality, post-discharge instructions, possible alternatives to treatment, potential consequences of refusing treatment, pain management, physiotherapy and timelines for returning to work or everyday activities. All these tools can be consulted repeatedly and at different times, even remotely, not only by the patient but also by family members via the dedicated website with access to two separate reserved areas for spinal surgery and radical prostatectomy. All patients are encouraged to watch all the material during the enrolment phase (on a hospital-provided tablet or via a secure web link at home) and to share it with family members at home. Patients receive access to the web-based repository containing supplementary information. This repository can be accessed from home, allowing patients to review key points as needed and formulate questions. Given the behavioural nature of the intervention, adherence to the CIA programme will be supported by clear instructions encouraging patients to review the content and scheduled reminders to encourage completion of materials.

At the developmental stage of the study, we also created the comprehension questionnaire to assess patients’ understanding and recall of the information provided. These questionnaires (different for spinal surgery and for urological surgery) were optimised through a peer review process that led to a questions selection and a questions simplification for easy access from patients of whatever educational background. Questions were submitted to a multidisciplinary team (psychologists, surgeons, anaesthetists, nurses, etc) for final review and approval.

All materials were developed according to the criterion of intelligibility for patients of every educational level. As decision aids, all these easily readable materials contain comprehensible data on the various topics of consent.

The control group will undergo the standard informed consent process. The surgeon explains risks, benefits and alternatives of the planned surgery to the patient. The primary source of information is the conversation with the doctor. After the discussion, the patient signs the standard informed consent form acknowledging the explanation of the procedure and risks. There are no extra sessions beyond the routine clinical encounters. Essentially, the control replicates the status quo consent scenario. We will ensure that control patients are not exposed to the new materials during the study; for instance, the multimedia resources will be restricted to the experimental group.

All participants, from both groups, after reading the information form and signing the informed consent form (see online supplemental material), perform the comprehension test after the interview (standard or implemented) with the surgeon and then sign the official surgical consent form in accordance with usual legal requirements.

All other aspects of perioperative management are identical between groups. Both groups will continue to receive the same surgical treatment and standard postoperative care pathways; the only difference is in the method of preoperative information delivery and consent.

During the enrolment, demographics and clinical data for each group: age, gender, educational level, diagnosis, planned treatment and previous surgical experiences will be recorded.

Both groups will undergo a battery of tests before and after the surgery that assesses how well they have understood and retained the information received, client satisfaction, anxiety, stress and depression levels, pain and clinical impairments (table 2).

The Comprehension Questionnaire, developed ad hoc, covers every aspect of the care pathway: the disease and proposed surgery, the surgical technique, expected benefits, risks of complications and related mortality, post-discharge instructions and possible alternative treatments. CIA comprehension questionnaire will be developed for each surgery type: thoracolumbar or cervical spine surgery and robotic radical prostatectomy. The comprehension questionnaire is designed as a formative tool rather than a gatekeeping test. Participants are explicitly informed that the questionnaire has no pass/fail consequences for access to care, and that all patients proceed through the clinical pathway regardless of their score. To minimise undue pressure and performance anxiety, the check is administered by a trained psychologist in a supportive, non-evaluative manner. Distress is monitored using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS-21) at prespecified time points. Patients may pause or decline to answer any item without penalty and may withdraw consent at any time without affecting their care. In the experimental group only, participants with low comprehension scores (below 8 out of 10) will repeat the preparatory/educational phase and then retake the test. If they do not pass a second time, they may have an additional interview with the surgeon for a tailored re-explanation to clarify any remaining doubts; this remediation is documented but does not delay or restrict treatment. In the control group, results will be recorded for comparison but will not affect the usual course of informed consent procedures. Potential psychological burden is proactively managed through ongoing monitoring and supportive communication.

Satisfaction is measured by CSQ-8: is an 8-item standardised instrument designed to measure global patient satisfaction with health services.

Preoperative anxiety, stress and depression levels will be measured using DASS-21 before and after the consent process: is a validated self-report instrument designed to measure the negative emotional states of depression, anxiety and stress.

DASS-21 will allow assessment of potential changes in emotional states as secondary outcomes.

In patients undergoing spine surgery, we will measure early rehabilitation performance, using Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) to assess pain levels and the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) to evaluate functional changes during recovery, to see if better-informed patients engage more effectively in rehabilitation.

In radical prostatectomy, we will measure patient-reported functional outcomes like potency and continence pre-recovery and post-recovery using condition-specific questionnaires (assessing erectile function and urinary continence; International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS); International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire Short Form (ICIQ-SF); International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF-5)).

We will track the number of formal complaints or legal claims citing ‘inadequate information or consent’ from participants at all centres. This outcome will be observed over an extended period (up to 5 years post-surgery for all participants) by communicating with the hospitals’ legal/risk management offices. The aim is to monitor this outcome long-term to see if enhanced consent has a lasting effect.

Outcome assessment schedule

Data collection methods

Data will be at first collected on paper-based CRFs, with the help of researchers specialised in clinical psychology and subsequently trained data analysts will process the data into electronic form for statistical analysis. Baseline and follow-up questionnaires will be coded and stored at the Rizzoli Orthopaedic Institute to ensure data quality control. Research staff will maintain regular contact with participants throughout the study via phone or email reminders prior to each scheduled assessment (T1, T2, T3). Participants who miss an assessment will be promptly contacted and offered flexible alternatives, including rescheduling or remote questionnaire completion when appropriate. For participants who discontinue the allocated intervention or deviate from protocol procedures, outcome data will still be collected whenever possible in accordance with the intention-to-treat principle.

Data management

Trained research coordinators will collect questionnaire data directly into an electronic data capture system or on paper forms initially and later entered. All study data will be recorded in a secure electronic database.

Patient and public involvement

Each patient will be assigned a unique study ID; personal identifiers will be kept separate to maintain confidentiality. Only authorised study team members will have access to the data, and all will be trained in Good Clinical Practice for data handling. Since this is a behavioural intervention trial with minimal risk, a formal Data Safety Monitoring Board is not planned, but the investigators will oversee safety and any adverse events. Adverse events related to the consent process itself are unlikely, but any occurrences (eg, extreme anxiety provoked by information) will be noted and managed.

Statistical methods

Continuous variables will be expressed as mean and SD of the mean if normally distributed, as medians and range if not. Categorical variables will be expressed as frequencies and percentages. Normality of the distribution will be assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The Levene test will be used to assess the homoscedasticity of the data. For the primary outcome (CSQ-8 satisfaction score) and the secondary outcome (comprehension test, DASS21 scores, NRS pain or functional questionnaire scores), the repeated measures ANOVA, followed by the post hoc Sidak pairwise test, will be performed to compare the scores at different follow-up times. The one-way ANOVA will be performed to assess the difference between the two groups of continuous and normally distributed and homoscedastic data; the Mann-Whitney test will be used otherwise.

Only for the primary outcome will the general linear repeated model (no missing follow-up and normally distributed and homoscedastic data) or the generalised linear mixed model (all the other cases) be used as multivariate analysis to assess the influences of the groups and other related factors such as age, gender, education and previous surgery on the repeated measures of the outcomes. The group will be the fixed effect; the eventual correction for confounding factors will be considered as a random effect.

Pearson’s χ2 square exact test will be performed to investigate relationships between grouping variables. Spearman’s rank correlation will be used to assess correlations between the numerical scores and continuous data. For all tests, p<0.05 will be considered significant.

Ethics and dissemination

This study has been approved by the ethics committee for all participating institutions (‘Comitato Etico Regionale Emilia-Romagna’ protocol CIA21, V.1.3, approval date 24 December 2021, protocol number CE-AVEC 953/2021/Sper/IOR).

It is important to note that this research consent is separate from (and in addition to) the surgical informed consent that is the subject of the intervention. When patients are approached about the study, they are informed about the trial’s purpose; that is, testing a new consent process. Those who agree will sign a consent form to be randomised to either standard or enhanced consent. In the control arm, they essentially undergo the normal procedure; in the intervention arm, they consent to viewing additional materials. We take care to ensure that study participation is voluntary and does not compromise the standard of care; patients are free to withdraw from the research at any time.

Confidentiality of patient data is strictly maintained. Any audio/video used (the educational materials) is generic and does not contain patient identifiers. The data collected for research (questionnaires, etc) will be stored without names, using study IDs.

There are minimal risks associated with this trial. The intervention itself primarily involves providing information. A potential risk is that some patients might experience increased anxiety when confronted with detailed information about surgical risks (knowledge can be unsettling). We mitigate this by ensuring support is available: patients on the intervention protocol have more opportunities to discuss concerns with clinicians, and the information is delivered in a sensitive, reassuring manner, emphasising that knowledge is empowering and that the care team will support them. Indeed, prior research suggests that being well-informed generally does not increase anxiety long-term; it may initially raise awareness, but overall it leads to better coping.

All adverse events and any unintended effects of the intervention will be monitored.

The results of this study will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication and presentations at national and international conferences in the fields of spine surgery, orthopaedic surgery, urology and medical ethics/health services research. The findings and all possible protocol modifications will also be shared with the participating hospitals and the regional health authorities, especially since this study was regionally funded. If the intervention proves effective, we will work on guidelines or implementation protocols to integrate the CIA approach into routine practice. The educational materials (videos, etc) developed for the trial could be made available for use in other hospitals, possibly via an online platform, so that a wider patient population can benefit. All authors have no competing interest to declare.

The trial is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT06059599), and any deviations from the protocol will be updated there and explained in publications.

We hope this study will contribute evidence to the ongoing efforts to improve informed consent. If successful, it will support a shift from viewing consent as a mere formality to treating it as an integral part of patient-centred care.
