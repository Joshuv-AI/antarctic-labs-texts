A predictive enhanced PID-F control strategy for functional electrical stimulation based on the artemisinin optimization algorithm
Authors: Özay Can, Erdinç Şahin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Functional electrical stimulation (FES) has demonstrated efficacy in enhancing comfort and mobility for individuals with paralysis, neurological diseases, and compromised muscle function. It facilitates motions that would otherwise be challenging or unattainable for these persons. Achieving optimal performance from FES systems necessitates the formulation of a strong and efficient control approach. To address this need, this paper proposes a novel controller called predictive proportional-integral-filtered derivative (PPID-F), which incorporates a predictive term into a filtered PID controller. The controller parameters are ideally optimized utilizing a recently devised metaheuristic method known as the artemisinin optimizer (AO). In contrast to traditional approaches in the literature, AO is employed to minimize a specifically designed objective function (OF) that integrates the integral of time-weighted absolute error (ITAE) criteria and the system’s peak response (ypeak). Comparative simulation results show that the proposed AO: PPID-F controller achieves the lowest objective function (OF) value among all evaluated methods. Specifically, it reduces the OF value from 0.4862 (AO: PID-F) to 0.2519, resulting in a 48.19% improvement. Compared to other benchmark controllers, the improvement ranges between approximately 48 and 68%. The effectiveness of the proposed controller is subsequently evaluated against six established approaches reported in the literature. The results demonstrate that the AO: PPID-F controller significantly enhances time-domain performance while preserving satisfactory frequency-domain features in comparison to alternative controller architectures developed for the FES system.

Introduction

For individuals experiencing muscle function problems, restoring movement can be achieved through the application of external electrical impulses to the skeletal muscles or the nerves connected to them. This process is known as functional electrical stimulation (FES), which generates force in the muscles. This technique offers distinct advantages, including the capacity to regulate the movement of paralyzed limbs and facilitate rehabilitation. However, the implementation of this technique is hindered by the nonlinear and noisy nature of the human musculoskeletal system. Consequently, the implementation of an effective and efficient control mechanism is imperative to enable paralyzed individuals to execute movement with ease1.

The aforementioned challenges associated with FES systems have been the subject of extensive research, leading to the development of numerous control mechanisms for the effective implementation of FES systems. Among these control mechanisms that have been realized and proven effective in recent years are the following: adaptive higher-order sliding mode (AHOSM) controller2, fuzzy logic controller (FLC)3, a model-free adaptable control approach4, iterative learning neural network (NN)-based control5, iterative learning control (ILC)6,7, repetitive control (RC)8,9, and model predictive control (MPC)10.

Notwithstanding the proven efficacy of these control mechanisms, the proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controller remains the preferred option, largely due to its distinct advantages, including its simplicity in structure and ease of design11,12. However, it is not appropriate to use the derivative (D) operator of the PID controller in a purely derivative form. This may result in the derivative kick effect (DKE). The occurrence of this phenomenon can be attributed to unanticipated fluctuations in the system response. This, in turn, precipitates a rapid change in the error signal and precipitates a substantial surge in the derivative of the error, thereby inducing a state of instability within the controller. To circumvent this phenomenon, it is imperative to incorporate a low-pass filter (LPF) into the output of the derivative operator of the PID controller. The controller formed by connecting a filter is known as PID-F. The extant research results demonstrate that the PID-F controller outperforms the PID controller13. Another proposed approach in the extant literature involves deactivating the D effect of the PID controller and integrating a structure consisting of a gain, integrator, and delay time (DT), as in the Smith predictor. This approach is advocated as a predictive PI (PPI) controller structure, and it aims to enhance the controller’s capacity for future prediction through a control signal. In many applications, such as systems with long DT14, wireless control systems15,16, first-order plus DT process control17,18, pressure process plants19,20, and pH control21, PPI structure is successfully implemented, and its superior performance over the classical PI controller is proved in these studies due to the predictive term.

The predictive structure inherent in the PPI controller can also be employed in conjunction with a PID controller. This integration enables the advantageous properties of both the D effect and the predictive structure to be leveraged collectively, thereby enhancing the overall system response. To the best of the author’s knowledge, such a structure has not been presented in the literature yet. Therefore, the performance of the PPID controller resulting from the integration of the predictive term and the PID controller can be considered to be a research topic and constitutes one of the motivations of the present study.

In recent years, numerous advanced optimization algorithms have been successfully applied to PID controller tuning and control applications across various engineering domains. For instance, artificial rabbit optimization (ARO) has been used for precise temperature control in electric furnaces22, while a novel modified electric eel foraging optimizer (mEEFO) has been implemented for the same control system in23. Similarly, a bio-dynamic grasshopper optimization algorithm (BDGOA), and mayfly algorithm (MA) have been employed for load frequency control (LFC)24,25, and RIME algorithm, hybridized approach of the artificial electric field algorithm (H-AEFA), and MA have been performed in the AVR-LFC system26–28. These studies demonstrate the increasing interest in biologically inspired and hybrid metaheuristic algorithms for enhancing control system performance. In this context, the proposed use of the AO represents a novel and promising contribution to this growing research area.

The present study proposes a controller, designated PPID-F, which incorporates PID-F and a series predictive term, and is intended for FES systems. The complexity of the PPID-F controller, particularly the extensive number of parameters requiring optimization, renders the process more arduous. Consequently, the employment of optimization techniques is imperative to achieve the optimal tuning of these parameters. This study proposes an innovative technique, AO, which was developed in 2024 and has not been previously employed in the design of a controller29.

Artemisinin optimizer (AO)

The AO is an efficient meta-heuristic algorithm inspired by the therapeutic process of the artemisinin-based treatment used against malaria29. The algorithm consists of several distinct phases. This section provides an overview of the biological inspiration behind the algorithm and presents the mathematical model that underlies its design.

Initialization phase

In malaria treatment, artemisinin is introduced into the patient’s body either orally or through injection. Inspired by this biological process, in this study, the drug microparticles are modeled as search agents within the algorithm. The entire collection of search agents constitutes the solution set of the algorithm.

Frequency response analysis

In the final subsection, the frequency response characteristics of the proposed AO: PPID-F controller are evaluated. The phase margin (PM) represents the additional phase lag that would bring the system to the verge of instability, the gain margin (GM) indicates how much the system gain can be increased before instability occurs, and the delay margin (DM) refers to the maximum time delay the system can tolerate without losing stability. For the AO: PPID-F controller, the obtained values are GM = 5.61 dB, PM = 35.2°, and DM = 0.412 s. These results, illustrated in Fig. 9, were calculated based on properly selected open-loop input and output points, in accordance with standard control theory practice.

Although the margins may seem modest at first glance, they are within acceptable stability limits and indicate that the proposed controller maintains a reasonably robust response. These results reflect a realistic evaluation of the controller’s frequency-domain performance, without relying on overly optimistic margin interpretations.

Conclusion

FES systems have been shown to have a significant impact on the quality of life of individuals living with paralysis. In order to achieve optimal efficiency from these systems, it is necessary to devise a control process. Consequently, the present study proposes a novel controller, designated PPID-F, which has not been previously documented. In order to optimally tune the parameters of this controller, a newly developed optimization technique called AO is employed with a specialized fitness function consisting of ITAE criteria and the peak of the system response. This is the first instance of AO being used in an FES system. The performance of the proposed controller and the optimization technique is investigated through transient response analysis, robustness analysis, and Bode analysis. The results are then compared with those of PID and PID-F controllers as reported in the literature. As demonstrated by the comparison results, the proposed controller demonstrates superior performance. The present study proposes a controller for FES systems that has been proven to be both successful and effective.

In subsequent studies on this subject, the filter in the PID-F part of the controller can be regarded as fractional order. Consequently, the number of parameters to be optimized will increase, as will the probability of obtaining superior results. Furthermore, studies can be conducted using newly developed and/or hybrid algorithms to optimize the parameters of the controller in order to increase overall system performance. Moreover, in future studies, the proposed AO: PPID-F controller can be tested on nonlinear models of the FES system that incorporate more realistic muscle dynamics, including force–length and force–velocity relationships. This will help further validate the controller’s performance under more complex and physiologically accurate conditions.
