Microbiological control for mosquito larvae: Current progress and applications
Authors: Geng Hong, Lu Yu, Hengqing Ji, Yueqing Cao, Zhengbo He, Cheng Liu, Yuxian Xia, Guoxiong Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEntomopathogenic microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, and viruses, present a promising approach to mosquito control due to their high efficacy, species specificity, and environmental safety. Targeting larval stages is a strategic component of integrated mosquito management, leveraging the accessibility and concentration of immature mosquitoes in aquatic habitats. This review comprehensively examines diverse entomopathogenic larvicides, encompassing their larvicidal molecules, biochemical mechanisms of action, current application strategies, and prevailing challenges. Bacterial agents, notably Bacillus thuringiensis var. israelensis (Bti) and Lysinibacillus sphaericus (Lbs), remain the most widely deployed. Entomopathogenic fungi and viral pathogens demonstrate potent larvicidal activity, highlighting their significant potential for the development of novel bio-larvicides. Innovative formulation strategies and synergistic combinations of microorganisms with chemical pesticides offer promising avenues for efficacy enhancement. Concurrently, the ongoing isolation and characterization of novel larvicidal microorganisms are crucial for expanding the repertoire of biological control agents. These approaches enhance long-term control sustainability by mitigating resistance development. While challenges persist for large-scale implementation, entomopathogenic microorganisms present a compelling and sustainable pathway for effective mosquito management.

Introduction

Vector-borne diseases pose a significant global health challenge, primarily due to pathogens transmitted by arthropods such as mosquitoes, sand flies, and ticks [1–3]. These diseases threaten over 80% of the global population, particularly those living in tropical and subtropical regions [1,2,4]. They account for approximately 17% of all infectious disease cases, representing a substantial portion of the global disease burden. Among these, mosquito-borne diseases – such as malaria, dengue, yellow fever, and Japanese encephalitis – transmitted by Anopheles, Aedes, and Culex species, are of particular concern [5–10]. Malaria, in particular, remains a major global health challenge, with an estimated 249 million new cases reported in 2022 alone across 85 endemic countries and regions [11]. In the absence of effective vaccines for most of these pathogens [12], mosquito control remains the primary strategy for preventing mosquito-borne infectious diseases [13].

Mosquitoes are fully metamorphosed insects, consisting of four distinct stages: egg, larva, pupa, and adult (Figure 1). The initial three stages – egg, larva, and pupa – are aquatic, while the adult stage is terrestrial. Female adults require a blood meal from vertebrates to obtain essential nutrients for egg maturation. During this process, they inject saliva into the host, which can transmit pathogens and facilitate the spread of vector-borne diseases [14]. After blood meal, females lay their eggs on water surfaces, initiating the next generation. The proliferation of mosquito breeding sites is significantly facilitated by human activities, such as the generation of environmental debris that collects standing water. This includes disposable bottles, cans, and discarded tires, which provide ideal habitats for Aedes and some Culex species. Larvae feed on aquatic algae, microorganisms (bacteria and protozoa), and organic material [15]. This stage is critical for accumulating energy reserves necessary for metamorphosis into pupae, which do not feed but instead undergo transformation into adults [16,17]. The current control of mosquito vectors primarily targets adult mosquitoes, with chemical insecticides being the most commonly used method (Figure 2(A)) [2,18]. Over the past two decades, dengue fever has emerged as a major arboviral disease in the China-Myanmar border region, with approximately 63,961 cases reported between 2017 and 2019 [19]. In response to the dengue epidemic, pyrethroid insecticides have been consistently used as a preventive measure to reduce Aedes mosquito population density and curb disease transmission. Chen et al. [20] reported that while Ae. aegypti collected from Gengma remained fully susceptible to deltamethrin, exhibiting a 24-hour mortality rate of 100%, increased resistance was observed in Ae. aegypti from both Ruili and Gengma to permethrin, lambda-cyhalothrin, and deltamethrin. The 24-hour mortality rates for these mosquitoes were reduced to between 11.43% and 36.08%. Similarly, other studies have reported the emergence of resistant mosquito populations following the large-scale use of chemical pesticides [21–23]. In addition to the direct spraying of chemical pesticides, the use of insecticide-treated mosquito nets and indoor residual spraying [24] of insecticides (Figure 2A) represent two important methods of mosquito control [11]. Over the past two decades, these methods have demonstrated efficacy in large-scale malaria control, resulting in a reduction of disease burden in highly endemic regions of sub-Saharan Africa [25,26]. Globally, the proportion of at-risk populations protected by indoor residual spraying in malaria-endemic countries decreased from 5.5% in 2010 to 1.8% in 2022. While shifts in vector biting behavior from nighttime to early evening or early morning have been observed [27–29]. Furthermore, concerns about environmental contamination and toxicity to humans and non-target organisms have prompted the development of safer and more environmentally friendly biopesticides [23,30,31]. Methods based on incompatible and sterile insect techniques (IIT-SIT) [32], spatial repellents [33,34], mosquito traps [35], attractive toxic sugar baits (ATSB) (Figure 2(B)) [36,37], and entomopathogenic fungi (EPF) [38,39] have been developed for adult mosquito control. Some of these methods have been implemented with favorable outcomes. From 2014 to 2017, the IIT-SIT nearly eliminated the world’s most invasive mosquito species, Ae. albopictus, from two isolated inland areas in Guangzhou [32]. The Mosquito Magnet Pro (MMPro), a commercially available mosquito trap that uses propane, offers an affordable and easily deployable option for the general public [40]. The larvae source management (LSM) is the management of aquatic habitats (water bodies) that are potential larval habitats for mosquitoes, in order to prevent the completion of development of the immature stages. The LSM (Figure 2(C,D)) comprises the habitat modification (e.g. eliminating standing water in rain gutters, old tires, buckets, plastic covers, toys, or any other container where mosquitoes can breed), larvicides spraying (the regular application of biological or chemical insecticides to water bodies), and biological control (the introduction of natural predator fish into water bodies) [41,42], Meng et al. 2015 [43,44]. It has been regarded as a complementary measure for mosquito control [42,45], given that it does not directly influence the primary factors determining vector transmission capacity [46]. Prior to the introduction of indoor residual spraying with DDT in the 1950s, LSM was the primary method of malaria control [2,42]. For example, environmental management was used to control malaria in the early 20th century by the Tennessee Valley Authority [47] and engineering works were used to control malaria and dengue to protect the workers constructing the Panama Canal [48]. Challenges associated with insecticide-based control of adult mosquitoes have renewed interest in LSM as an alternative strategy [42,49–54]. Mosquito larvae are concentrated in water, which is more accessible than adult mosquitoes themselves. Control measures targeting larvae may be more effective than those targeting adult, as killing larvae reduces the number of disease vectors before they can cause harm. The targeting of larvae has previously yielded highly effective results in the control of malaria [49,51,55]. This study presents an overview of current advances in mosquito biological larvicides, with a particular focus on microbiological agents.

Bacteria larvicides are the most used currently

The most commonly utilized biological larvicides in the world today are all based on bacteria, including Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis (Bti) and Lysinibacillus sphaericus (Lbs) formerly known as Bacillus sphaericus [56–59]. Due to their environmental safety, low toxicity to non-target organisms, and high efficacy in controlling mosquito larvae – including those resistant to conventional chemical insecticides – these products represent a promising alternative in mosquito management strategies. A number of commercially available formulations of these biological larvicides have been endorsed by the World Health Organization (WHO) and U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA) as mosquito larvicides (https://www.epa.gov/mosquitocontrol/controlling-mosquitoes-larval-stage, accessed on 13 September 2024) [42,60]. Bti for the mosquito larvae control

Recent advances in the use of Bti for mosquito larval control

Bti, identified in 1977 by Goldberg and Margalit [87], is the first B. thuringiensis subspecies (H-14 serotype) toxic to dipteran larvae, including mosquitoes [88–90]. It has since them become a globally utilized biocontrol agent for mosquito larvae [57,89,91,92]. Bti formulations contain spores and parasporal crystals, which, when ingested by larvae, cause mortality through the action of protein protoxins (δ-endotoxins): four primary proteins – Cry4Aa, Cry4Ba, Cry11Aa, and Cyt1Aa – along with at least two secondary toxins, Cry10Aa and Cyt2Ba [93–96]. These proteins dissolve in the alkaline conditions of the larval midgut, then activated by midgut proteases in mosquitoes [93,97,98]. Cry toxins bind to specific protein receptors, such as cadherin [99,100] and alkaline phosphatase [101], causing oligomerization and pore formation, leading to disruption of the gut [97,102,103]. The micro-computed tomography images produced by Alba-Tercedor and Vilchez offer a comprehensive representation of the damage caused to larval tissues by Bti [104]. In contrast, Cyt toxins interact directly with membrane lipids, exerting cytolytic effects [105]. Additionally, Cyt toxins act as membrane receptors for Cry toxins, amplifying their toxicity through a synergistic interaction [106]. Valtierra-de-Luis et al. [107] reported a 68.8-fold synergistic enhancement in larvicidal toxicity between Cry10Aa and Cyt2Ba, which represents one of the most potent synergistic effects described to date, comparable to those observed between Cyt1A and Cry4Aa, Cry4Ba, or Cry11Aa.

New Bti strains continue to be discovered worldwide, such as those from Qatari soil [108], Brazil [109], and India [110]. Advances in understanding Bti toxin structures and mechanisms have driven innovations, including genetic modifications to enhance potency. For example, a chimeric cry gene (4 Ba-1Ac), created by fusing the N-terminal of Cry4Ba with the C-terminal of Cry1Ac, significantly increased toxicity against Cx. pipiens larvae [111]. Similarly, engineering of surface loop residues in Cry19Aa’s domain II structure achieved a 42,000-fold increase in toxicity to Ae. aegypti [112]. Other strategies include novel formulations, such as nanoparticle synthesis using Bti supernatants [113] and hydrogel-based delivery systems [114], as well as synergistic combinations with essential oils or other compounds [115,116] have shown enhanced larvicidal effects. Lukose et al. observed that Bti culture filtrates attract Cx. quinquefasciatus, with GC-MS analyses identifying benzaldehyde as a key oviposition attractant [117]. Conversely, mesocosm experiments indicated that direct Bti application did not significantly impact mosquito oviposition behavior [118]. These observed differences may depend on the concentration of the compound in question. Bti exposure has also been shown to alter the microbiota of Cx. pipiens larvae, shifting the dominant taxa from Actinobacteria to Firmicutes and reducing Actinobacteria abundance with increasing Bti concentration. α diversity analysis confirmed that Bti exposure impacts microbial diversity, likely due to ecological shifts caused by infection, which suppress certain bacteria while allowing other opportunistic taxa to proliferate [119].

Practical application of Bti in the control of mosquito larvae

Bti is currently the most widely industrialized and extensively researched biological larvicide worldwide. Its larvicidal effectiveness has been rigorously validated through numerous field studies, demonstrating a proven track record in malaria control in Africa [120]. A large-scale trial in Burkina Faso, involving 127 rural villages and a semi-urban town, showed that applying Bti to all known breeding sites reduced adult Anopheles mosquito populations by 77.4%. Furthermore, selective treatment targeting the most productive breeding sites based on risk maps generated from remotely sensed data resulted in a 63.5% reduction in adult mosquito numbers [45]. Similarly, Bohair et al. observed a 51-fold reduction in the incidence rate (IR) of dengue per 100,000 population in Sibu, Sarawak, Malaysia, following a 3-month control campaign utilizing Bti [121]. A lot of other studies have also demonstrated the efficacy of Bti in controlling mosquito larvae [41,78,89]. But the efficiency of Bti is challenged by certain circumstances. The presence of a high mangrove canopy density ( > 40% cover) was found to result in a significant reduction in product deposition, larval mortality, and ground-level Bti concentrations, with reductions of 74.5%, 27.7%, and 32.03% respectively, in comparison to application in open salt marshes. Johnson et al. improved Bti application methods in conjunction with affordable unmanned aerial system (UAS) technology [122]. Following the implementation of drone mapping and Bti spraying of potential mosquito larval habitats in rice fields on the outskirts of Kigali, Rwanda, a significant reduction in the populations of all species of mosquito larvae and pupae was observed. A notable decline in malaria prevalence was documented between the intervention and control sites [123].

Bti advantages and disadvantages of mosquito larval control

Bti has a broad larvicidal spectrum, demonstrating significant toxicity against mosquitoes in the Aedes, Anopheles, and Culex genera [93]. Furthermore, resistance to Bti develops at a notably slow rate and has not been observed yet in field populations of mosquitoes, despite its extensive use over nearly 5 decades [124]. However, the effectiveness of Bti is constrained by its relatively short duration of activity. Bti crystals are sensitive to abiotic factors, such as photolysis, as well as biotic factors, including high organic matter content, both of which reduce its residual effectiveness in mosquito breeding habitats [125]. To maintain optimal efficacy, Bti typically requires reapplication at intervals of 7 to 10 days [42]. Although some studies have reported that Bti crystals can persist in the environment for weeks to years after treatment, these findings are typically associated with low-temperature aquatic environments that are unsuitable for mosquito proliferation [126,127].

While B. thuringiensis (Bt)-based formulations are generally considered safe for non-target organisms due to the bacterium’s host specificity, increasing evidence points to potential long-term ecological impacts [128]. Land et al. reviewed the adverse effects of Bti on non-target organisms, particularly Chironomidae, which play an important role in linking aquatic and terrestrial food webs [129]. Declines in both mosquito and chironomid populations could have cascading effects at higher trophic levels [125]. A comprehensive review of Bti’s environmental and socioeconomic impacts in Europe also highlighted public concerns about its potential ecological risks [125]. Moreover, depending on its virulence factors and the host’s immune status, Bt may act as an opportunistic pathogen in mammals [130]. Recurrent reports have linked Bt to various human infections, including food-poisoning associated diarrheas [131], corneal ulcers [132], periodontitis [133], burn [134] and wound infections [135]. These findings suggest that the use of Bti should be approached with greater caution, including strict adherence to recommended application guidelines and regulatory limitations, such as pre-harvest intervals and environmental safety protocols. This is especially important given the potential ecological impacts and emerging evidence of opportunistic pathogenicity in mammals.

Lbs for mosquito larvae control

Recent advances in larval control of Lbs

Lbs, another prominent larvicidal bacterium, has been commercialized over the past few decades, with various isolates showing effective mosquito larvicidal properties [136]. Most Lbs-based larvicides primarily contain the binary (Bin) toxin, which specifically targets Culex, Anopheles, and certain Aedes species [137,138]. The Bin toxin is composed of two subunits, BinA (42 kDa) and BinB (51 kDa) [139], which form equimolar heterodimers after proteolytic activation [140,141]. In Cx. quinquefasciatus larvae, BinB binds to the Cqm1 midgut receptor, a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored α-glucosidase [142,143]. While BinA does not directly interact with Cqm1, it is internalized into midgut cells via BinB binding, leading to toxic effects that ultimately cause larval mortality [144,145]. This binding causes significant intracellular damage, such as cytoplasmic vacuolation [146] and apoptosis [147]. Knockdown of scavenger receptor C1 (SCRC1) in an Anopheles-derived cell line significantly reduced Bin toxin effects, implicating SCRC1 mediates Bin toxin endocytosis [148]. Other toxins, such as Mtx and Cry48Aa/Cry49Aa, synergize with Bin, enhancing larvicidal activity [149,150]. While Bin and Cry48/49 toxins are produced as parasporal bodies in sporangia, Mtx is produced during the vegetative stage [151]. The Cry48Aa1 and Cry49Aa1 (now called Tpp49Aa1) proteins exhibit strong toxicity against larvae resistant to Bin [152], although neither is toxic alone; when combined equimolarly, they demonstrate high toxicity to Culex larvae, similar to Bin toxin [149,153]. The C-terminal domain of Cry49Aa binds specifically to larval gut cell membranes, while its N-terminal domain interacts with Cry48Aa [154]. Additional studies highlight the critical role of 4 cysteine residues (positions 70, 91, 183, and 258) in Cry48Aa’s binding to enterocyte membranes and its pathogenic effects [155]. Distinct targets likely explain Cry48Aa/Cry49Aa efficacy against Bin-resistant mosquitoes, with membrane-bound alkaline phosphatase and alpha-glucosidase identified as putative receptors [152,156]. Recent megahertz serial femtosecond crystallography (MHz-SFX) structural studies resolved Cry49Aa1 at 1.62 Å, providing insights for enhanced microbial insecticides design [157].

Practical application of Lbs

The development of Lbs as a microbial agent for mosquito larval control has faced challenges due to the widespread success and efficacy of Bti. However, after the Indonesian isolate Lbs strain 1593 was identified for its strong larvicidal activity [158], several pharmaceutical companies, including Bthek Ltd. in Brazil, began to screen and commercialize it. Currently, products like VectoLex Granules®, VectoMax® [82], and Griselesf® [65] are available on the market. Numerous field trials conducted across various regions have demonstrated the effectiveness of Lbs-based formulations in diverse settings, particularly in polluted water. For instance, a single application of VectoLex® or VectoMax® in fish farming ponds in Nova Cintra, northwestern Brazil, led to over 95% reduction in late-instar An. darlingi larvae within 7 days [82]. Similarly, Galardo et al. reported significant reductions in An. darlingi populations following VectoLex® treatment, achieving 78% reduction in late-instar larvae, 93% reduction in pupae, and a 53% decrease in adult mosquitoes [76].

Advantage and disadvantage of Lbs in mosquito larvae control

Lbs persists longer in the environment compared to Bti, with formulations of Lbs demonstrating extended residual activity. In some habitats, retreatment intervals for Lbs can be as long as 4 weeks [42]. However, the insecticidal spectrum of Lbs is narrower than that of Bti [159]. For example, Ae. aegypti larvae are significantly less susceptible to Lbs than Culex spp., largely due to the absence of Bin receptors (Cqm1) in the midgut cells of Ae. aegypti, which renders them resistant to Bin toxins and unsuitable for control by Lbs [65,160]. Some researchers have found that mixed cultures of multiple Lbs strains can effectively control Ae. aegypti mosquitoes that have developed resistance to temephos, both in laboratory and field settings. This increased toxicity in Ae. aegypti may be attributed to the combined action of Mtx and hemolysins [161].

A further concern is the rapid emergence of resistance to Lbs, as its modes of action are less complex compared to Bti [162]. In 2016, the Salt Lake City Mosquito Abatement District (SLCMAD) identified a 20,000-fold resistance to Lbs in Cx. pipiens larvae in catch basins. In response, SLCMAD implemented a 3-year suspension of Lbs use and initiated a rotational treatment program incorporating spinosyn and s-methoprene products. After 3 years, Lbs was reevaluated, and its efficacy was found to be comparable to that of susceptible strains. However, during the second year of Lbs reapplication, bioassays conducted in 2021 revealed varying levels of resistance in urban areas under the jurisdiction of SLCMAD. The rapid resurgence of resistance suggests that repeated use of Lbs cannot be relied upon in the near future, even after a three-year hiatus and the implementation of within-season product rotations [163]. This resistance is primarily attributed to mutations in the cqm1 gene, which prevent the expression of full-length, midgut GPI-anchored proteins [164–166]. Previous research has also shown that symbiotic gut bacteria in insects play a significant role in the development of insecticide resistance. Notably, differences in the gut commensal bacterial communities were observed between Lbs-sensitive and Lbs-resistant Cx. quinquefasciatus strains. The resistant strain exhibited higher relative abundances of Firmicutes, Proteobacteria and Proteobacteria in its gut microbiota compared to the susceptible strain [167].

Other bacteria have potential for larvae control

Since the discovery of Bti, numerous larvicidal subspecies in the Bacillaceae family and novel crystal proteins have been identified. Cry11Ba1 and Cry11Bb1 proteins, found in Bt subsp. jegathesan and Bt subsp. medellin, respectively, exhibit 10-fold higher larvicidal activity than Bti’s most potent toxins [168,169]. Additionally, Bt subsp. jegathesan produces Cry19Aa and ORF2, which synergistically increase toxicity toward An. stephensi and Cx. pipiens [170]. Bt serovar higo [171], Bt subsp. fukuokaensis [172], Bt serovar mogi [173], Bt subsp. entomocidus INA288 [174], Bt serovar medellin [175], and Bt strain S2160-1 [176] have shown larvicidal activity comparable to that of Bti. Notably, the supernatant of a native Bt strain isolated from Nigeria exhibited toxicity against adult mosquitoes comparable to that of the chemical insecticide Repel®, with 48-hour mortality rates ranging from 53.33% to 93.33%, while showing only 3.33% mortality in larvae [177].

The insecticidal potential of Xenorhabdus and Photorhabdus bacteria has been extensively studied, especially for controlling key mosquito species such as Ae. albopictus. To date, approximately 24 species of Xenorhabdus and 5 species of Photorhabdus have been identified with insecticidal properties [178]. Many of these strains exhibit significant activity against mosquitoes. In a recent study, Touray et al. examined the larvicidal effects of cell-free supernatants, cell growth cultures, and cell mass from 23 Xenorhabdus and 6 Photorhabdus strains. Their results showed that 3 Photorhabdus spp. and 13 Xenorhabdus spp. released larvicidal compounds in their cell-free supernatants. Additionally, cell growth cultures of all tested species displayed larvicidal activity, except for Xenorhabdus sp. TS4, while 21 strains of Xenorhabdus and Photorhabdus showed high oral toxicity (59–91%) against mosquito larvae [179]. Furthermore, bacterial supernatants have been shown to inhibit mosquito egg hatching [180] and deter egg-laying behavior in a concentration-dependent manner [181]. Brevibacillus halotolerans, a bacterium isolated from Amazonian environments, also demonstrated larvicidal effects against Ae. aegypti [182], marking the first reported instance of such activity in B. halotolerans. Additionally, a newly identified Lysinibacillus sp. VCRC B655 strain displayed strong larvicidal activity with minimal impact on non-target organisms. GC-MS analysis revealed the presence of several bioactive compounds, including diketopiperazine, pyro lo [1,2-a] pyrazine1,4dione, and benzeneacetic acid [183]. Oliveira et al. studied cultivable bacteria across all mosquito life stages and identified 9 strains with larvicidal activity comparable to that of the commercial Bti-based product VectoBac® WG. 16S rRNA gene sequencing showed that these larvicidal strains included 2 species of Bacillus, 5 Enterobacter, and 2 Stenotrophomonas [184]. Similarly, Zhang et al. identified a Chromobacterium haemolyticum strain, Rain 0013 (Ch_R13), which exhibits virulence toward mosquito larvae in aquatic habitats; however, a threshold density is required for lethal efficacy [185]. These findings highlight promising candidates for new biocides in mosquito control, and they offer a valuable resource base for future mosquito larvae control and prevention. However, further field trials and comprehensive studies on their larvicidal mechanisms are necessary to validate their potential for practical use. Additionally, the lack of industrialization represents a significant limitation to their practical application.

Future prospect of bacteria larvicide

The widespread global use of bacterial larvicides necessitates a thorough evaluation of their ecological impact, particularly their effects on non-target organisms. A detailed understanding of the larvicidal mechanisms of pathogenic bacteria provides a theoretical basis for optimizing insecticide formulations to combat the rising prevalence of mosquito-borne diseases. Developing new formulations based on existing bacterial strains is critical for leveraging potential synergistic effects among larvicidal agents, thereby enhancing efficacy [153] and prolonging residual activity [53]. Exploiting these synergies could also slow the development of resistance in mosquito larvae. It is encouraging to note that mosquitoes with resistance to Lbs remain susceptible to Bti [63], underscoring the value of combining different larvicides in resistance management strategies. For example, Santos et al. demonstrated that combining Lbs and Bti significantly reduced the frequency of cqm1 resistance alleles in Cx. quinquefasciatus larvae [186], a result consistent with findings by Su and Trdan [187]. To sustain the effectiveness of limited larvicidal products, comprehensive resistance management strategies must prioritize prevention and rational use. Moreover, the continuous discovery of novel bacterial strains with potent larvicidal properties is imperative to expand the arsenal of available mosquito control tools. A thorough understanding of the dynamics of mosquito populations, particularly in relation to climate and other environmental factors, is essential for optimizing the timing of control interventions. In this context, integrating larval monitoring with big data analytics could enhance the prediction of larval outbreaks and support timely, targeted applications of larvicides.

EPF as a promising tool for mosquito larvae control

Recent advancements have significantly enhanced our understanding of EPFs and their effects on mosquito growth, reproduction, and disease transmission [188]. Several fungal strains have been employed as complementary tools for mosquito vector control [189,190], demonstrating efficacy across life stages, including eggs [191], larvae [192–195], pupae [196], and adults [197]. Although over 700 species of insect-pathogenic fungi are well-documented [198], the commercialization of these products still faces challenges, requiring further improvements to make them competitive with chemical pesticides [199]. To date, more than 170 EPF-based commercial products exist, predominantly using Metarhizium spp. and Beauveria spp [200]. EPFs offer notable advantages, such as slow resistance development [201] and effectiveness against insecticide-resistant mosquito populations [202–205]. However, the mechanisms of EPF infection in mosquito larvae remain poorly understood. Larvae are generally more susceptible to EPFs than adults, with median lethal times (LT50) typically under 7 days for larvae compared to over 10 days for adults, regardless of species [206]. These findings underscore the potential of EPFs as promising tools for integrated mosquito control strategies.

Metarhizium spp. and B. bassiana are the most commonly studied fungi

Advances in larvicidal mechanisms of EPF

The most extensively studied larvicidal EPFs are Metarhizium spp. and B. bassiana. However, the precise mode of infestation remains unclear. The route of infection seems to vary depending on the fungal isolate, the type of propagules, and the target insect species (Figure 3). Alkhaibari et al. observed that, Ae. aegypti larvae were more susceptible to M. brunneum blastospores than to conidia, while the opposite was true for Cx. quinquefasciatus, and An. stephensi larvae showed similar sensitivity to both forms [192]. Similarly, Ae. aegypti larvae exhibit greater sensitivity to B. bassiana blastospores than to conidia [208]. However, Bitencourt et al. found no significant difference in the median survival time (S50) of Ae. aegypti larvae exposed to suspensions of B. bassiana propagules prepared with 0.01% Tween 80 [209]. Additionally, Geetha and Balaraman reported resistance in Ae. aegypti to B. bassiana at high conidial concentrations (108 conidia/mL) [210], contrasting with Sandhu et al., who observed high pathogenicity at LC50 values of 8.3 × 103 conidia/mL [211]. These inconsistencies may be partially explained by differences in larval feeding behavior. Culex and Anopheles larvae, as “collector-filterers,” feed near the water surface where hydrophobic conidia accumulate, whereas “collector-gatherer” Aedes larvae primarily feed on sediment-associated organic material, aligning with the distribution of blastospores [15,212]. However, this hypothesis does not fully explain why Ae. aegypti is more sensitive to dry conidia applied to the water surface compared to wet conidia suspended in aqueous solutions [213], which quickly sink to the bottom. Alkhaibari et al. [207] found that blastospores of M. brunneum are more virulent than conidia to Ae. aegypti larvae because it can attack on several fronts. Blastospores secrete mucilage to enhance cuticle adhesion, penetrate the cuticle more effectively than conidia, and are capable of infecting larvae through the gut, rapidly invading the hemocoel [207]. The head was also identified as a critical infection site [214]. In contrast, blastospores were less virulent to Cx. quinquefasciatus [192,215], attributed to weaker adhesion forces between M. brunneum blastospores and Cx. quinquefasciatus cuticle, as demonstrated by atomic force microscopy [215]. Despite this, the blastospore secretes mucilage on the surface of the Culex cuticle. This aligns with findings by Greenfield et al., who reported weaker adhesion of M. anisopliae conidia to Ae. aegypti compared to terrestrial hosts such as Tenebrio [216]. The authors proposed that the weak interaction between Ae. aegypti and conidia of M. brunneum was due to the absence of specific long-chain hydrocarbons on the body surface of Ae. aegypti, which are essential for the development of the EPF [216,217]. While cuticle composition appears to be a key factor influencing EPF efficacy, further research is required to confirm this hypothesis and clarify the mechanisms underlying these differences.

The most likely route of infection by EPF conidia is through the intestine [193,195,216,218]. Butt et al. reported that M. brunneum conidia kill Ae. aegypti larvae without adhering to the cuticle or germinating in the gut, with mortality linked to caspase activity regulated by Hsp70 [193]. A similar phenomenon was observed in Ae. aegypti larvae infected with B. bassiana conidia, where blastospores were detected in the hemolymph despite no intestinal infection structure were visible (de Oliveira Barbosa [219]). In M. anisopliae, proteinases and adhesin gene expression (Pr and Mad genes, respectively) is upregulated in the presence of mosquito larvae, suggesting host recognition [193,216]. However, blastospores exhibit significantly lower Pr1 enzyme activity than conidia [207], protease inhibitors reduce larval mortality when treated with conidia [193], while it did not alter blastospore virulence [207]. Indicating that conidia rely more on protease secretion for infection, while blastospores primarily penetrate mechanically. The exact mechanisms of conidial intestinal invasion remain unclear. Evidence suggests that conidial hydrolytic enzymes may disrupt the intestinal barrier [193]. Recent studies observed intestinal mucosal rupture and hemocoel invasion in 33% of Ae. aegypti 2nd instar larvae within 24 hours of exposure to M. anisopliae conidia [219]. This aligns with prior findings of enterocyte disruption and brush border degradation caused by EPFs, highlighting their role in overcoming the larval intestinal barrier [219,220].

de Oliveira Barbosa Bitencourt et al. [208] observed that mosquito larvae exposed to M. anisopliae exhibited a substantial accumulation of fungal propagules in the mouthparts and respiratory siphons, suggesting that asphyxiation may be the primary cause of death. Lacey et al. also reported the blockage of the trachea and siphon of Cx. quinquefasciatus by M. anisopliae conidia [221]. But the latest research makes the asphyxiation claim less credible [222]. Alvarez-Costa et al. studied the survival of last instar larvae of Ae. aegypti at different temperatures and measured the consumption of air and water-dissolved oxygen by larvae and pupae under different conditions. Larvae survived much longer than expected, reaching 50% mortality only after 58, 10, and 5 days at 15, 25, and 35°C, respectively. Larvae obtained 12.72% of their oxygen from the water [222]. Hagstrum observed the same result, he reported delayed mortality in Ae. aegypti larvae, despite their tracheae being blocked with petroleum oil. His study suggested that for Ae. aegypti aquatic respiration may account for approximate 5–20% of aerial respiration [223].

Genetic modification increases EPF larvicidal virulence

The integration of insecticide expression genes into EPF significantly enhances their efficacy. For instance, B. bassiana expressing the Bt toxin Cyt2Ba (Bb-Cyt2Bas) demonstrated increased virulence against both larval and adult Aedes mosquitoes, reducing the LT50 for Ae. aegypti larvae by 42% and for Ae. albopictus larvae by 33% at 1 × 107 conidia/mL [224]. Similarly, B. bassiana engineered to express the Bt toxin Cyt1Aa (Bb-Cyt1Aa) showed a 33.3% and 22.2% reduction in LT50 for Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus larvae, respectively, compared to the wild-type (WT) strain [225]. Furthermore, B. bassiana expressing the scorpion neurotoxin AaIT (Bb-AaIT) exhibited higher virulence and faster action against both larval and adult mosquitoes [226]. Beyond genetic modifications, advancing the understanding of EPF pathogenic mechanisms offers additional opportunities for enhancing their effectiveness. Gene knockout studies and transcriptomic analyses have identified phase-specific genes involved in EPF infections, including conidial attachment, germination, penetration, growth, and secondary conidia production. These findings, as reviewed by Shin et al. [227], highlight the potential for targeted genetic modifications to optimize EPF-based mosquito control strategies. While the deployment of genetically modified organisms in the wild raises important ethical and regulatory considerations, such approaches may serve as valuable technological reserves for future application under well-regulated and socially accepted conditions.

Other fungi possessing larvicidal activity

In addition to Metarhizium spp. and B. bassiana, Oomycete pathogens such as Leptolegnia chapmanii [228], Pythium guiyangense [229], and Lagenidium giganteum [230] have demonstrated significant efficacy against mosquito larvae. These pathogens are considered effective larvicides due to their ability to perpetuate infection cycles by producing ascospores in aquatic environments [231]. All three species exhibit high virulence against mosquito larvae from genera such as Anopheles, Culex, and Aedes [230,232,233]. For instance, L. giganteum produces zoospores that attach to the larval cuticle, penetrate into the hemolymph, and consume host nutrients, leaving only the cuticle intact [234]. Upon the host’s death, the pathogen forms asexual fruiting structures containing zoospores, facilitating further infections [5,234]. However, L. giganteum is less effective against certain Anopheles species, such as An. gambiae, due to intense melanization during cuticle penetration, which encapsulates the fungus and allows up to 56% of larvae to survive [235]. Its efficacy is also influenced by environmental factors, such as temperature and salinity [236], with reduced spore formation observed at 15°C [237]. Despite its initial registration as a biocontrol agent (Laginex) by the USEPA in 1995, L. giganteum was later discontinued due to its harm to healthy dogs and its phylogenetic proximity to mammalian isolates [238] (https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2011–09-28/pdf/2011–24832.pdf). Pythium guiyangense, first isolated from garden soil in 1994 [229], employs a similar infection mechanism to L. giganteum, with zoospores attaching to larval cuticles, invading hemolymph, and ultimately destroying host tissues [159]. However, its virulence is inconsistent, with infection rates ranging from 13% to 100% in Cx. quinquefasciatus. To address this variability, two formulations combining P. guiyangense with Bti have been developed: calcium alginate microcapsules and mycelial suspensions [159]. Transcriptomic and genomic analyses suggest that P. guiyangense employs multiple virulence strategies, including protease secretion and Kazal-type protease inhibitors [233]. Other Pythium species, such as P. thermoculicivorax, exhibit larvicidal properties and thrive in high-temperature environments (up to 37°C) [239]. Leptolegnia chapmanii was first documented in 1971, infecting Ae. triseriatus larvae in Louisiana, USA [228]. It shows high virulence against early instars, achieving 100% mortality within 24 hours in 1st and 2nd instar Ae. aegypti larvae, but effectiveness declines in later instars (less than 40% against 3rd and 4th instar larvae within 72 hours) [240]. Recent studies have isolated new strains of L. chapmanii from natural water sources in Argentina, Brazil, and the United States [241]. The LC50 of L. chapmanii against 2nd instar Ae. aegypti larvae was determined to be 280 zoospores/mL at 5 hours post-inoculation and 3 zoospores/mL at 24 hours post-inoculation [242]. Further research is needed to fully evaluate the potential of L. chapmanii and P. guiyangense as biocontrol agents for mosquito management.

Fungi-derived metabolites possess larvicidal activity

Fungus-derived products have demonstrated significant effectiveness in controlling mosquito larvae [243–245]. Metabolites from the ethyl acetate mycelial extract of B. bassiana showed strong larvicidal activity against Cx. quinquefasciatus larvae [243]. Ragavendran et al. [246] demonstrated that ethyl acetate extracts of B. bassiana exhibited strong larvicidal activity against An. stephensi, Cx. quinquefasciatus, and Ae. aegypti larvae, with LC50 values ranging from 25.72 to 72.38 μg/ml across various larval stages. Additionally, metabolites from Aspergillus species, such as A. flavus and A. fumigatus, also showed significant larvicidal effects. GC-MS analysis identified compounds such as di-n-octyl phthalate, (1 H-benzoimidazole-2-yl)-[4-(4-methyl-piperazin-1-yl)-phenyl]-amine, and 6,8-dimethyl-5-oxo-2,3,5,8-tetrahydroimidazo [1,2-a] pyrimidine as responsible for the larvicidal effects of these fungi [247]. Similarly, Baskar et al. identified compounds such as Preg-4-en-3-one, 17. α-hydroxy-17. β-cyano-, trans-3-undecene-1,5-diyne, and pentane, 1,1,1,5-tetrachloro- as contributing to larval mortality caused by A. tamarii [248]. Other fungal species, including Trichoderma asperellum [249], Fusarium begonia [250], and Hyalodendriella spp [251], have also shown larvicidal potential. Additionally, Balasubramanian demonstrated that silver nanoparticles loaded with secondary metabolites from Isaria fumosorosea effectively killed Cx. quinquefasciatus and Ae. aegypti larvae [252].

Practical application of EPFs in controlling mosquito larvae

The application of EPF for mosquito larval control in outdoor settings remains limited, primarily confined to artificial water bodies, with little large-scale implementations to date. Table 2 summarizes examples of EPF use in outdoor or semi-outdoor conditions. Veerwal et al. recently developed a powder formulation combining carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) with B. bassiana conidia to target An. stephensi larvae in Udaipur, India. This formulation significantly reduced pupation rates, demonstrating its potential for outdoor mosquito control [253]. Similarly, oil-based EPF formulations have shown enhanced toxicity against An. gambiae larvae compared to alternatives like 0.1% Tween 80 or dry powder. Field trials in Kenya revealed that An. gambiae pupation rates were reduced by 39–50% when conidia formulated with ShellSol T were applied [254], compared to unformulated conidia. Synergistic applications of EPF with other control agents have further enhanced efficacy. For instance, a combination of M. anisopliae (LC40) and nitenpyram (LC40) achieved larval mortality rates of 73.3 ± 4.7% in laboratory and 71.5 ± 7.4% in field trials [255]. Neem oil has also been shown to enhance the persistence and effectiveness of M. anisopliae conidia [256,264]. Similar synergistic effects have been observed with compounds derived from plants such as llicium verum essential oil [265], Schinus molle essential oil [220], and Azadirachta indica (Suneem 1%) [257]; bacteria-derived compounds like avermectins [266] and Spinosad [255]; and chemical insecticides, including imidacloprid [267], phenylthiourea [205], and diflubenzuron [268]. The synergy between EPF and other agents may result from the attenuation of host resistance mechanisms, thereby amplifying larvicidal activity. For example, EPF infections have been shown to increase insecticide susceptibility in resistant mosquito populations by suppressing detoxifying enzymes [202,204]. Metarhizium anisopliae has been reported to reduce acid phosphatase activity in chlorpyrifos-resistant Cx. quinquefasciatus [269], further supporting the utility of EPF in resistance management strategies. Current problems and future prospect for EPFs control mosquito larvae

Although the killing speed of EPFs is slower than that of bacteria, they have the advantage of acting through both ingestion and direct contact, allowing them to target a wider range of insects [193,221]. EPFs are effective across multiple mosquito life stages, whereas bacterial larvicides are limited to early larval stages, as they require ingestion and become ineffective once larvae stop feeding in the late 4th instar [42]. Genetic modification and screening for highly virulent EPFs strains can also accelerate the larvicidal rate of EPF to some extent. Additionally, combining EPFs with chemical larvicides could mitigate this drawback, enhancing efficacy, reducing reliance on chemical agents, and delaying resistance development.

Comprehensive studies on the pathogenic mechanisms of EPF are essential to optimize strains and develop effective biological products for mosquito larval control. Given their higher virulence against mosquito larvae compared to conidia, blastospores of EPF have become a focal point in current research aimed at uncovering their pathogenic mechanisms. However, they overlook the potential of conidia, which are more commercially viable [270] due to their hydrophobicity, long shelf life, and ease of production on solid substrates [271,272]. In contrast, blastospores, while quickly produced [273] and hydrophilic [274], are sensitive to environmental stress [275,276], and require costly preservation methods [276]. Thus, studying conidia’s larvicidal mechanisms is critical, particularly their ability to penetrate larval gut walls and evade immune defenses. Noskov et al. demonstrated that M. robertsii conidia, when combined with avermectins, could germinate and penetrate the intestines of Ae. aegypti larvae [266], host’s immune and detoxification systems inhibited their invasion. Another limitation of EPFs is their inability to reproduce in aquatic environments, necessitating repeated applications, unlike water molds such as L. chapmanii and P. guiyangense [231,277]. Despite the demonstrated efficacy of EPFs in semi-outdoor simulations, no products have been registered for use. To enable broader application, further research is needed to evaluate EPFs’ environmental impact, improve formulations for aquatic environments, and enhance their adaptability for larval control.

Virus for larvae control

Viruses are ultramicroscopic particles, typically measuring 20 to 500 nanometers, comprising a capsid, core, and genetic material (DNA or RNA). The first mosquito-pathogenic virus, a suspected densovirus, was identified in Cx. tarsalis from California in 1963 [278]. However, research in this field stagnated for decades, overshadowed by the success of microbial insecticides like Bti [57,89,91,92] and Lbs [57,89,91,92]. Additionally, challenges related to manipulation, transmission, and large-scale production limited the development of viruses as biopesticides [279]. Notable progress occurred later, with Becnel et al. demonstrating in 2001 that baculovirus transmission depends on divalent cations, where magnesium facilitates and calcium inhibits transmission [280]. Advances in molecular technologies have since improved the analysis and manipulation of mosquito-pathogenic viruses. Among these, baculoviruses, densoviruses, and iridoviruses, all classified as DNA viruses [281], are the most extensively studied.

Baculovirus

Baculoviruses, first identified in mosquitoes over 50 years ago [281], have since been isolated from 13 mosquito species across the genera Aedes, Anopheles, Culex, Ochlerotatus, Psorophora, Uranotaenia, and Wyeomyia [281,282]. These viruses target the midgut epithelium, with infection process initiated by the ingestion of the occluded virus, which subsequently traverses the peritrophic matrix to invade the midgut epithelium. Subsequently, the virus replicates and spreads in the form of budded viruses, resulting in the hypertrophied nuclei of midgut cells that appear white due to the accumulation of occlusion bodies [279]. Some larvae survive the infection transstadially, excreting occlusion bodies in their meconium after pupation, thereby contaminating new aquatic habitats [283]. Infection rates as high as 60% have been reported in Culex populations in Florida [280]. Although baculoviruses primarily infect larvae [280,284], adult midgut infections can occur [283], likely originating from budded virions invading larval regenerative cells, which later form adult midgut epithelium [283,285]. However, their utility as biocontrol agents is limited by relatively low pathogenicity against mosquitoes [286].

Densovirus

Densoviruses, characterized by their compact genomes (4–6 kb), serve as invaluable molecular tools for expressing antiparasitic genes in cell cultures and live mosquitoes [287]. Since their discovery in 1963 [278], mosquito-specific densoviruses (MDVs) have been isolated from various mosquito species, including major vectors like Ae. aegypti [288], Ae. albopictus [289], An. gambiae [290], and Cx. pipiens [291]. MDVs infect all developmental stages, causing larvae to appear sluggish and deformed [292]. Initial infection occurs via larval anal papillae, with the virus replicating in tissues such as the midgut, Malpighian tubules, neurons, fat bodies, and salivary glands [5], leading to systemic symptoms like segment malformations, whitish cuticles and hypertrophied nuclei [293]. MDVs spread horizontally through water and venereally between adults [294], and transovarial transmission has been observed [295]. In China, Kong et al. reported MDVs reducing Ae. albopictus susceptibility to dengue virus and found over 68% prevalence in field populations [296]. Infected male releases could be an effective strategy to introduce MDV-based paratransgenic tools into naïve females. However, MDVs act slowly and exhibit variable pathogenicity across mosquito species [297], with reported larval mortality rates ranging from 51% in Ae. aegypti to 82% in Ae. albopictus using the Thai strain densovirus (AThDNV) [298]. The strain achieved only 15.5% mortality in An. minimus [299]. MDV efficacy depends on viral titer and infection stage [300,301]. Field studies in Thailand also correlated An. minimus larval infections with rainfall two months prior [302]. Recent advances include Batool et al.’s integration of Bti toxin genes (Cry11Aa and Cyt1Aa) into the Ae. aegypti densovirus genome, significantly increasing mortality in 1st Ae. albopictus larvae [293]. These findings highlight the potential of MDVs for mosquito control and as molecular tools for studying mosquito biology.

Iridoviridae

Mosquito iridescent viruses (MIVs), members of the family Iridoviridae, were first identified by Clark et al. in Ae. taeniorhynchus from Florida [303]. These large, icosahedral dsDNA viruses (~180 nm in diameter) primarily infect fat body cells, forming paracrystalline arrays in the cytoplasm that produce characteristic iridescent colors under dark-field illumination [281]. MIV infections are highly lethal to early instar mosquitoes, while later-stage infections (3rd or 4th instars) often result in survival and transstadial transmission into adulthood. Infected females can transmit the virus transovarially, with studies reporting up to 100% infection rates in eggs [304]. Regular MIVs (RMIVs) demonstrate host specificity, infecting only Ae. taeniorhynchus and Ae. sollicitans [305,306]. To understand the limited susceptibility of other mosquito populations, Undeen and Fukuda investigated the genetic basis of resistance and the virus’s invasion mechanisms [307]. Their findings indicated that RMIVs lack active mechanisms for host penetration, relying instead on breaches in the cuticle or peritrophic matrix. Despite their potential, the use of iridoviruses for biological control is limited by low infectivity and production challenges making them, up to now, unsuitable [297].

Limitation of virus for control mosquito

Viruses hold significant potential for mosquito control; however, their broader application is hindered by the absence of cost-effective in vitro production technologies [308]. Most documents addressing the commercial production of entomopathogenic viruses concentrated on baculoviruses due to broad knowledge of these viruses and the commercial products available [309]. Additionally, the pathogenicity of specific virus strains varies widely across mosquito species, posing a substantial challenge to the widespread use of viral agents for mosquito management. The development of novel formulations, such as oils produced by combining of Ae. albopictus densovirus and Bti [310], has the potential to enhance the control of vector mosquitoes. The majority of studies examining the impact of viruses on mosquito vectors has concentrated on field or experimental infections, with a paucity of research exploring the intrinsic mechanisms governing mosquito physiology and immunity. Furthermore, the introduction of fusion-expressed genes into the mosquito virus’s genome is confined to known traditional insect toxins. To achieve the desired outcome, further development of additional sequences encoding potent virulence proteins or interfering with important mosquito genes is necessary.

Concluding remarks

With the rapid rise in chemical insecticide resistance and shifts in adult mosquito biting behavior, larval management has garnered increasing attention. A survey in a health district of Burkina Faso revealed that over 90% of respondents used bed nets, while only a few relied on alternative measures such as mosquito coils or insecticides. Notably, the survey revealed that a significant proportion of respondents were willing to invest in larvicide interventions [311]. Mosquito larvae are concentrated in aquatic environments, making them more accessible targets than adults [45]. Therefore, controlling the larval stage is a crucial strategy in reducing the overall mosquito population, preventing the emergence of adult mosquitoes that could transmit pathogens. Past practices of larval targeting have demonstrated success in malaria control [49,51]. For example, Tia et al. reported a significant reduction in malaria incidence, from 291.8‰ to 111.4‰, following the combined use of insecticide-treated mosquito nets and Bti [312]. These results underscore the need for developing novel bio-larvicides and application strategies. To ensure long-term efficacy, standardized protocols for monitoring insecticide susceptibility should accompany the introduction of new control strategies. Collaboration among academic researchers, industrial developers, field practitioners, and evaluators is crucial to enhance insecticide efficacy, protect the environment, minimize impacts on non-target organisms, and extend the lifespan of larvicides.

Managing aquatic habitats for mosquito larvae presents significant challenges and costs. The primary obstacle lies in detecting, accessing, and treating domestic or hidden breeding sites, which can limit the effectiveness of larval control strategies [43]. Consequently, larval management must be integrated into broader prevention and control programs. Researchers should focus on understanding the mechanisms of larval lethality in biological agents to develop safer and more effective products. By leveraging these mechanisms, combinations of products targeting multiple pathways can be designed to achieve synergistic effects. Interestingly, some biological larvicides (Lbs) also function as environmental remediators [313], with strains such as FLQ-11–1 capable of degrading chemical pesticides like cyfluthrin from sewage sludge [314]. Combining insecticides with pesticide-degrading strains could enhance larvicidal efficacy while mitigating environmental pollution. Improved formulation technologies further enhance the performance of bio-larvicides [315,316] by ensuring efficient delivery and sustained action [317]. For instance, oil-in-water emulsions [208] and mineral oil-based formulations [209] have been shown to increase the virulence of B. bassiana propagules against Ae. aegypti. Such advancements underscore the potential of microbes as effective pest control agents when optimally formulated. Community and government collaboration is equally critical. Discarded plastic containers often become breeding grounds for mosquitoes, necessitating public participation alongside scientific efforts. Training community members to identify the larval habitats of the primary malaria vectors can be an effective method for larval source management in rural Tanzania. The implementation of community-led larval control – where community members are trained to identify, characterize and target larval habitats of vector mosquitoes – was associated with a reduction in the density of adult and late-stage larvae of both An. funestus and Culex spp. within domestic settings [55]. Another community-based control program of Malaria using Bti in Rwanda significantly reduced the abundance of the adult An. gambiae mosquitoes from rice fields [318]. These examples highlight the potential for greater public engagement in larval management programs to enhance vector control outcomes and reduce mosquito nuisance and improve animal and public health [319].
