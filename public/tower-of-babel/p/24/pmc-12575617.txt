Viral epidemic potential is not uniformly distributed across the bat phylogeny
Authors: Caroline A. Cummings, Amanda Vicente-Santos, Colin J. Carlson, Daniel J. Becker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Characterizing host–virus associations is critical due to the rising frequency of emerging infectious diseases originating from wildlife. Past analyses have evaluated zoonotic risk as binary, but virulence, transmissibility, and death burden can vary dramatically. Recent work suggests bats harbor more viruses with high virulence in humans than other taxa. However, it remains unknown whether all bats harbor viruses of equal viral epidemic potential. We used phylogenetic factorization to flexibly identify clades of mammals (at any taxonomic level) associated with low or high viral epidemic potential. We found that virulence, transmissibility, and death burden only cluster within specific bat clades, often composed largely of cosmopolitan families. Mapping the geographic distributions of these bat clades with anthropogenic footprint data suggests high viral epidemic potential in coastal South America, Southeast Asia, and equatorial Africa. Our results deepen understanding of the host–virus network and identify clades to prioritize for viral surveillance, risk mitigation, and future studies characterizing mechanisms of viral tolerance.

Introduction

Due to the rising frequency of emerging infectious disease outbreaks in humans, there is an urgent need to characterize associations between viruses and their hosts1. Over 70% of human emerging infectious diseases are caused by zoonotic pathogens, which originate in animals and adversely impact human health and economies1–3. Statistical models can improve our capacity to preempt and mitigate zoonotic transmission by identifying the kinds of wildlife species most likely to harbor zoonotic pathogens4,5. For example, past work identified rodent and avian species likely to be undetected reservoirs of zoonotic pathogens6,7. Species predicted to carry high-impact zoonotic pathogens can be prioritized for surveillance and prevention measures4,8,9.

Zoonotic risk is often evaluated as a binary variable: whether a pathogen can or cannot infect humans5. However, zoonotic pathogens differ in virulence (i.e., severity of disease) and transmissibility (i.e., capacity to spread in human populations after emergence). For example, within the genus Betacoronavirus, the case-fatality rates (CFRs) of viral species vary, with 5.9% in severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV) and 34.4% in Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV)5. Further, while the former efficiently spreads from person to person, the latter is much less efficient at human-to-human transmission8,10. Virulence and transmissibility influence death burden (i.e., the total human disease-induced mortality), which collectively can describe a virus’s epidemic potential5,8. To reduce zoonotic emergence and optimize time and resource allocation for public health measures, zoonotic pathogens with the highest viral epidemic potential should be prioritized for surveillance and transmission prevention4,8.

Recent work has demonstrated that bats (order: Chiroptera) harbor more viruses with high virulence in humans than other mammalian or avian orders8. Bats are recognized as “special hosts” because they exhibit high viral diversity (hosting more viruses compared to most other taxa) and appear to tolerate many viruses without exhibiting clinical signs of infection11–14. The high virulence of some of these viruses can also be at least partially explained by the evolutionary distance between bats and humans: humans are less likely to be pre-adapted to cope with these novel infections and show resistance, increasing morbidity and risk of mortality5,8,15. However, as the distance between a vertebrate host and humans increases, a virus is also less likely to be pre-adapted to overcome human host defense mechanisms5,8,15.

As the only flying mammals, bats evolved distinct immune adaptations to cope with the metabolic demands of flight, which likely has enabled them to tolerate otherwise virulent viruses11,16. However, bat immune systems are diverse, and bats also do not uniformly interact with viruses. As the second most speciose order of mammals (1500 species)17, bats exhibit diverse ecological traits that may influence their viral communities, including their wide geographic distribution, use of torpor, heterogeneous diets, gregariousness, high mobility, long lifespan to body size ratio, and deep coevolutionary histories with viruses11,16,18. Thus, the presumption that all bat species equally harbor a large number of virulent viruses may be inaccurate, and this has important implications for both public health and bat conservation19. In particular, bats have received negative media attention given their identity as reservoir hosts of several high-profile viruses (e.g., SARS-CoV, Nipah virus), leading to retaliation against bats16,20–22; this, in turn, can actually increase spillover risk23,24. Clarifying the distribution of viral virulence across the order Chiroptera could provide the public with a more complete picture of viral epidemic potential, thereby improving public perceptions of bats and informing outbreak prevention efforts20,22.

As other measures of viral epidemic potential, transmissibility in humans and high death burdens have had weaker connections to bats specifically. Recent work identified primates as the only order connected with unusually high onward transmission in humans, owing to their small phylogenetic distance from human hosts8. Additionally, no taxonomic orders have been associated with high human death burdens8. Corroborating which, if any, host groups are associated with high transmissibility and death burden in humans is further relevant to zoonotic risk assessment8.

In this study, we test whether viruses with high epidemic potential in humans are uniformly distributed across mammals or if particular clades demonstrate high epidemic potential. We focus on bats to determine whether the entire order organically exhibits equal virulence or if only specific clades of bats exhibit high virulence. Given prior results, we also test whether mammalian host groups at finer taxonomic scales than order differ in transmissibility and death burden5,8. We hypothesize that these measures of viral epidemic potential cluster within clades of bats, based on the unique coevolutionary histories between different bats and viruses5. First, to assess the phylogenetic distribution of viral epidemic potential, we quantify phylogenetic signal of CFRs, onward transmission, and mean death burden across mammals and within bats specifically. Next, we use a flexible graph-partitioning algorithm to identify clades of mammals with unusually high or low viral epidemic potential without the need to a priori assume a specific phylogenetic scale25. Finally, we mapped the geographic distributions of any identified bat clades with unusually high viral epidemic potential in tandem with anthropogenic footprint, visualizing hotspots of potential zoonotic spillover risk to guide outbreak surveillance and prevention efforts.

Methods

Viral epidemic potential data

We extracted mammal–virus associations from the Global Virome in One Network (VIRION), the most comprehensive-to-date database of vertebrate–virus associations26. We retained only those mammals and viruses resolved by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) taxonomy and matched mammals to the latest mammal phylogenetic tree using the ape package in R27,28. There were 2637 unique host–virus associations, of which 99% had a reported detection method (viral isolation, PCR, and/or serology; n = 2611). 60% (n = 1582) represented detections through PCR (n = 1223) and/or viral isolation (n = 798). Both methods can indicate active infection: viral isolation denotes infectious virus and thus successful replication in the host (i.e., implying competence and the capacity to infect new hosts), while PCR indicates viral detection but not necessarily competence29. 38% of unique associations were detected solely using serology (n = 1004), which can signify either active infection or recent exposure and can lack specificity, given cross-reactivity of antibodies30. Finally, the remaining one percent of associations had no specified detection method (n = 26). To quantify typical viral epidemic potential in humans attributed to each host, we averaged measures across all viruses per host species using previously collated viral outcome data5,8. We calculated the average and maximum CFRs, expressed as proportions of human cases resulting in mortality. We calculated the typical transmissibility in humans attributed to each host as the fraction of viruses per host that have shown human–human transmission. To quantify typical death burdens in humans per host, we averaged the death burden since 1950 across all viruses per host species. Given the above mixture of viral detection methods, we caution that these data do not necessarily imply each host meaningfully contributes to zoonotic transmission of high-risk viruses30,31. We present our findings for mean CFR in the main text and maximum CFR in the supplement, due to these measurements being highly correlated (ρ = 0.93, p < 0.001; Fig. S1).

Phylogenetic signal in viral epidemic potential

We first estimated phylogenetic signal for each response variable (mean CFR, maximum CFR, percent onward transmission, and mean death burden) using Pagel’s λ in the caper package32. Pagel’s λ quantifies phylogenetic dependence in a trait by comparing observed variation to that expected under a Brownian motion model of evolution (i.e., traits gradually diverging over evolutionary time), with values of zero indicating complete phylogenetic randomness and values of one supporting high phylogenetic signal (i.e., Brownian motion)33,34. λ is a more reliable measurement of phylogenetic signal than other indices, having a small type I error rate and being less sensitive to sample size35. To complement λ, we also estimated Blomberg’s K, which can further capture traits with phylogenetic signal stronger than that expected under Brownian motion (K > 1)36. We calculated both λ and K across all viruses to test if viral epidemic potential is evolutionarily conserved across species in two analyses: among all mammals and within bats specifically. To assess phylogenetic patterns in viruses within well-studied viral groups, we repeated these analyses for viral families with sufficient sample size (≥23 bat host species)35.

Taxonomic patterns in viral epidemic potential

We used a flexible graph-partitioning algorithm to identify clades of mammals with unusually high or low viral epidemic potential. Phylogenetic factorization iteratively partitions a phylogeny to identify nodes with the maximum contrast in a given response variable, thereby identifying particular clades with greater or lesser propensities to harbor virulent, transmissible, or high-death-burden viruses without needing to specify a given phylogenetic scale a priori (e.g., mammalian order or family). We used the phylofactor package in R to partition each measure in a series of iterative generalized linear models (GLMs) for each edge in our phylogeny25. For mean CFR, maximum CFR, and mean death burden, we used a Gaussian distribution in our GLMs, while we used a binomial distribution for percent onward transmission. We determined the number of statistically significant clades using Holm’s sequentially rejective test with a 5% family-wise error rate37. We first performed phylogenetic factorization across all mammals to test if the order Chiroptera was organically identified as a clade with a greater propensity to harbor viruses with high virulence, transmissibility, or death burdens. We next performed phylogenetic factorization within the order to identify particular bat clades with especially low or high viral epidemic potential in humans. As in our analyses of phylogenetic signal, we repeated these analyses for viral families with sufficient sample sizes.

Uneven sampling of species can lead to inaccurate conclusions, including those reflecting the extent of sampling effort rather than a biological phenomenon38,39. To statistically control for sampling effort, we included the number of viruses with viral epidemic potential data per host species as a precision covariate with the GLMs used by phylogenetic factorization40. To further ensure that any clades identified as having high viral epidemic potential were not simply well-studied clades, we conducted phylogenetic factorization of two measures of sampling effort: the number of viruses with viral epidemic potential data per host (above) and the number of citations per host (square-root transformed to normalize the distribution, obtained using the easyPubMed R package), using a Poisson distribution and Gaussian distribution, respectively.

Geographic distribution of viral epidemic potential

We defined “high-risk” clades as those identified by phylogenetic factorization as harboring higher measures of viral epidemic potential. Focusing on bats specifically, we aggregated the geographic ranges of species belonging to any high-risk clade using International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) data41. Anthropogenic impacts on habitats are shown to increase viral shedding and wildlife–human contact, both of which should increase spillover risk42,43. To visualize hotspots of viral epidemic potential from bats, we co-mapped44 anthropogenic footprint45 and the geographic distributions of bats in high-risk clades (see dark red regions in Fig. 3). Finally, to identify viral surveillance targets, countries falling within these “high-risk regions” (i.e., regions containing the top five percent of combined high anthropogenic footprint and high-risk bat host richness) are given in Table S6.

Results

Phylogenetic signal in viral epidemic potential

Our data on viral epidemic potential included 889 mammal species (including 202 species of bats), infected by 112 unique virus species spanning 23 virus families. Mean CFR (x̄ = 0.26 ± 0.01), maximum CFR (x̄ = 0.42 ± 0.01), and fraction of viruses with onward transmission (x̄ = 0.31 ± 0.01) all ranged from 0 to 1. Mean death burden ranged from zero to 2,581,976 (x̄ = 107,866 ± 11,994). Across all viruses and all mammals, mean and maximum CFR and mean death burden exhibited moderate phylogenetic signal (λ = 0.70, 0.69, and 0.70, respectively), while the fraction of viruses with onward transmission exhibited high phylogenetic signal (λ = 0.78). All estimates of λ were statistically distinct from phylogenetic randomness (p < 0.001) and Brownian motion models of evolution (p < 0.001). Our results were largely recapitulated when we estimated K (Tables S1, S2), and measurements of both λ and K aligned with what is expected under intermediate levels of Brownian motion from simulations35.

These results were similar when we restricted our analyses to bats. Mean CFR (x̄ = 0.64 ± 0.03), maximum CFR (x̄ = 0.84 ± 0.02), and the fraction of viruses with onward transmission (x̄ = 0.27 ± 0.03) again all ranged from 0 to 1. Mean death burden ranged from zero to 2,581,976 (x̄ = 310,733 ± 50,299). Across all viruses in bats, mean and maximum CFR and mean death burden all exhibited high phylogenetic signal (λ = 0.90, 0.83, and 0.94, respectively), while the fraction of viruses with onward transmission showed slightly weaker phylogenetic signal (λ = 0.72). All estimates of λ again departed from both phylogenetic randomness (p < 0.001) and Brownian motion (p < 0.001). Our results were similar when we estimated K (Tables S1, S2).

Four viral families had sufficient sample sizes to stratify our analyses: Flaviviridae (n = 344 mammals), Rhabdoviridae (n = 301 mammals), Togaviridae (n = 150 mammals), and Paramyxoviridae (n = 43 mammals). Sample sizes for onward transmission analyses were insufficient for the Rhabdoviridae. Estimates of phylogenetic signal were consistently moderate to high in the analyses across all mammals. Our results were similar in the bat-specific analyses with two exceptions: phylogenetic signal dropped to zero in the maximum CFR analyses for flaviviruses, rhabdoviruses, and togaviruses, and it also dropped to zero in the mean death burden analyses for flaviviruses and togaviruses. For maximum CFR across all viruses, phylogenetic factorization first identified a high-risk clade within the infraclass Eutheria (i.e., placental mammals)46. The second clade was “low risk” (i.e., harboring unusually low maximum CFRs) and included families from the superorder Cetartiodactyla (i.e., cetaceans and even-toed ungulates; Table S3)46. The third clade was high risk and comprised genera from the rodent subfamily Neotominae. The remaining two clades were low risk (Table S3).

For the fraction of viruses with onward transmission, one clade was identified by phylogenetic factorization, and it was high risk. This clade included families from the primate parvorder Catarrhini (i.e., apes and Old World monkeys; Table S3)46.

Three clades differed in mean death burden. Phylogenetic factorization first identified a high-risk clade comprising the bat family Rhinolophidae (i.e., horseshoe bats, Table 1). The second clade, also high risk, was of the infraclass Eutheria (Table S3). The last clade included mammals from the superorder Cetartiodactyla, which instead had lower death burdens (Table S3). Importantly, the order Chiroptera as a whole was not organically identified as a clade carrying high-risk viruses in any measure of viral epidemic potential.

When stratifying our mammal-wide analyses by the five selected virus families with sufficient sample sizes, phylogenetic factorization identified five clades that differed in mean CFR, five clades that differed in maximum CFR, three clades that differed in the fraction of viruses with onward transmission, and six clades that differed in mean death burden. Importantly, analyses within each of our five viral families only found high-risk bat clades for togaviruses and flaviviruses (Table S3, Fig. 2).

Within togaviruses, one high-risk clade for mean CFR was identified, and it comprised the cosmopolitan (i.e., globally distributed) bat families Molossidae, Vespertilionidae, and Emballonuridae (Table 1, Fig. 2). We identified one high-risk clade for maximum CFR, which included mammals from the infraclass Eutheria (Table S3). For onward transmission, one high-risk clade was identified, consisting of the primate families Hominidae and Cercopithecidae (Table S3). We did not identify any high-risk clades for togaviruses in our death burden analyses.

Within flaviviruses, we identified one high-risk bat clade for mean CFR, which included the bat families Rhinolophidae and Hipposideridae (Table 1, Fig. 2)17. For maximum CFR and mean death burden, the one high-risk bat clade consisted of families in both the Yinpterochiroptera and Yangochiroptera (Table 1, Fig. 2). For the fraction of viruses with onward transmission, we identified one high-risk bat clade spanning the Yangochiroptera and Yinpterochiroptera, similar to the clade identified for maximum CFR and mean death burden (Table 1, Fig. 2).

For a full list of all clades identified in the bat-specific analyses, see Table S4. Overall, the high-risk bat clades identified by phylogenetic factorization were mostly similar between the mammal-wide and bat-specific analyses, both across viruses and within specific virus families (Tables S3 and S4). As such, the order Chiroptera did not emerge organically as a high-virulence clade.

In our sampling effort analyses, phylogenetic factorization identified 13 clades of mammals with usually low or high numbers of viruses with viral epidemic potential data (12 across all mammals and one within bats) and citation counts (12 across all mammals and one within bats). Our results are summarized in Table S5. Importantly, the heavily studied clades almost exclusively differed from those identified as having low or high viral epidemic potential. However, we identified two exceptions. First, when considering the number of viruses with viral epidemic data, the fourth clade across all viruses and mammals matched the clade identified in our analysis of onward transmission across all viruses and mammals (i.e., a subclade within the primate parvorder Catarrhini; Table S5). This suggests that while Old World monkeys and apes are more likely to carry viruses transmissible within humans, these findings might be affected by sampling effort. In contrast, the first clade of bats identified as having especially virulent viruses (mean CFR) in our analysis of all mammals and viruses was instead identified as the first clade of especially less sampled species within both our mammal-wide and our bat-specific sampling bias analyses (number of viruses with viral epidemic potential data; Table S5). Therefore, the identification of this high-virulence bat clade is not an artifact of sampling effort and instead arises despite this group of bats being under sampled.

Across all viruses, we identified geographic hotspots of zoonotic risk in Central America, coastal South America, equatorial Africa, and Southeast Asia (Fig. 3, Table S6). In the togavirus analysis, hotspots clustered in Europe, Sub-Saharan Africa, East Asia, coastal South America, and Central America (Fig. 3, Table S6). In the flavivirus analysis, we identified hotspots in Southeast Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa (Fig. 3, Table S6). Within flaviviruses, hotspots of transmissibility and death burden risk instead clustered in Central America and coastal South America (Fig. S2, Table S6). Discussion

Zoonotic pathogens from wildlife have caused many pandemics and represent the greatest number of high-impact emerging infectious diseases47. Prior research states bats host high viral diversity and the greatest number of viruses with high virulence in humans8,14,48. We investigated whether bats, as an entire order, organically exhibited a higher propensity to host virulent viruses than other taxa, or if analyses that are agnostic to taxonomic order would instead identify patterns at a different phylogenetic scale. Using several approaches to assess phylogenetic patterns, our analyses show that bats do not come out as a group with uniform viral epidemic potential: virulence, transmissibility, and death burden are clustered only within distinct clades of bats, often within cosmopolitan families spanning both the western and eastern hemispheres (i.e., the Molossidae, Vespertilionidae, and Emballonuridae). As surveillance across the Chiroptera is not cost-, labor-, or time-effective due to bats being extremely speciose and challenging to sample (e.g., nocturnal lifestyle, remote roosts), surveillance could monitor species within these clades to discover novel hosts and viruses with high epidemic potential4.

When considering bats, our fine-scale analyses identified host clades across all viruses and within two viral families, the Togaviridae and Flaviviridae, that are more likely than other groups of this order to host virulent, transmissible, and high death burden viruses. Across all viruses for mean death burden, the Rhinolophidae (horseshoe bats) exhibited unusually high death burden, aligning with previous analyses linking these bats to high-impact viruses, including SARS-related CoVs49,50, lyssaviruses51, and Hantaan virus52. These results add nuance to the discussion regarding bats as viral hosts: in lieu of a general profile of viral immune adaptations across this order, different bat taxa likely possess unique immunological adaptations to harbor the particular viruses with which they have coevolved, and many of these traits exist due to the hyperdiverse nature of the chiropteran order11,18,53–56.

However, our results also raise the question of whether all bats uniformly tolerate highly virulent viruses, in addition to differentially harboring viruses12,13,57,58. Tolerance describes an individual coexisting with a replicating virus and exhibiting few to no clinical signs of infection11,59. Future experimental viral infection studies could compare the immune responses and infection outcomes between bat species from the high-risk clades identified here and taxonomically relevant outgroups to the same virus to begin investigating if high-risk species exhibit dissimilar abilities to tolerate viruses53,60.

Our results align with previous work showing certain virus families exhibit non-random associations with specific kinds of host species4–6,61,62. Importantly, all high-risk bat clades identified here differed from clades of heavily sampled species, suggesting biological mechanisms, and not sampling bias, explain why these bats are known to carry viruses with high epidemic potential. Across all viruses for mean CFR, the bat superfamilies Emballonuroidea and Vespertilionoidea emerged as the first high-risk clade. The vast majority of these bats are insectivores63 and can roost in anthropogenic structures such as mines, houses, and tunnels64. Opportunities for zoonotic spillover from species in these superfamilies may thus be higher, given the global distribution of most of the included families and this ability to inhabit anthropogenic structures64. Future studies could analyze whether ecological traits (e.g., insectivory, ability to be widely distributed) or immunological features unique to these superfamilies underlie their ability to carry virulent viruses16,56.

A key finding from our pan-virus and Togaviridae analyses is the bat families identified to harbor more virulent viruses than other mammals (and other bats) largely have cosmopolitan distributions. In the pan-virus analysis, this phenomenon may stem from bat–virus coevolution: viruses typically coevolve with their hosts either by specialization (e.g., recombination) or diversification (e.g., host switching)55. These data suggest diversification was selected for, facilitating future host jumps55. As cosmopolitan families possess broad geographic distributions, diversification is likely advantageous for viruses, as cosmopolitan species could introduce the viruses they carry to many potential hosts55. In the Togaviridae analysis, virulent viruses may cluster within these bats due to the transmission mode of these viruses and the foraging ecology of these hosts65. All togaviruses in this analysis were vector-borne, and most bats within the Vespertilionidae, Molossidae, and Emballonuridae are insectivores, so they could be exposed to diverse togaviruses through their diet66. Of note, only four togaviruses have been isolated from bats, all of which are alphaviruses, which are usually vectored by mosquitoes67. As such, the role of bats in the transmission of these viruses warrants further investigation67,68.

By contrast, virulent flaviviruses clustered within the Rhinolophidae and Hipposideridae bat families, which are insectivores restricted to the eastern hemisphere. Most flaviviruses in this analysis were vector-borne, and given the foraging ecology of these bats, they could also be exposed to flaviviruses through their diet66. Of note, whether bats play an important role in the transmission of flaviviruses is unclear, as our understanding of viral replication of flaviviruses within bats is limited69,70. Further, some Japanese encephalitis virus associations within the Vespertilionidae bat–flaviviruses subset of our data may affect this pattern, given historic viral surveillance efforts made to specifically investigate the role of bats in the circulation of this virus71–73. We therefore caution how the results of our analyses are interpreted, as not all species may play a direct role in the circulation of these viruses70. That being said, the role of bats and other hosts in the transmission of most viruses has yet to be fully understood69,70.

Our final analysis focused on geographic distributions of bat clades found to harbor viruses with high epidemic potential. While the hotspots of viral epidemic potential identified here largely align with those identified previously using ecologically based approaches4,74, an added strength of our phylogeny-based method is that it does not require any additional trait data, allowing for rapid risk assessment62. Those members of the high-risk clades identified here but not included in our analysis could be prioritized for viral discovery, and the overlap with anthropogenic footprint can provide targeted areas for detection of viruses due to increased potential of spillover into humans and amplifying hosts, such as domestic animals and threatened wildlife42,75,76. For example, Nyctalus lasiopterus (present across Eastern Europe, France, and Spain), and Pipistrellus javanicus (found throughout Southeast Asia) from the high-virulence clade in the pan-virus analysis, as well as Rhinolophus silvestris (found in Gabon and the Republic of the Congo) in the pan-virus high death burden analysis, are not known to host human-infective flaviviruses, togaviruses, rhabdoviruses, paramyxoviruses, or coronaviruses but would be good targets because their habitats exhibit high anthropogenic pressure42,76,77. These three species have also been identified in previous machine learning analyses as high-priority targets for coronavirus surveillance due to their similar ecologies to known hosts4.

Our phylogenetic analyses do not a priori assume a particular phylogenetic scale, which can help uncover particular clades of interest above or below a typically specified taxon (e.g., mammalian order)25,78. They can also complement trait-based analyses and help guide new research directions to explain particular phylogenetic clustering25. Our results can encourage the next identifying the underlying traits that differentiate bat clades with high virulence viruses from other bat clades62. For example, bats exhibit diverse immune systems and use different (and often non-overlapping) adaptations to cope with viruses (e.g., constitutive expression of interferons, increased heat shock protein expression, dampened inflammatory response)11,56. Since selection for virulence is influenced by the host immune system, comparing potential dissimilarities between the immune systems of high-risk and low-risk clades of bats will be an important area for future work55,79,80.

Finally, bats perform critical ecosystem services, such as seed dispersal, pollination, and nutrient redistribution19. Bats in the cosmopolitan families Molossidae, Vespertilionidae, and Emballonuridae are either obligate or facultative insectivores, consuming insects known to be agricultural pests19. Bats also provide recreational and cultural value to humans and inspire technological innovations19,81,82. However, many species are at risk of extinction due to habitat loss and climate change, and these risks are exacerbated by bat exterminations driven by fears over perceptions of bats-as-virus-hosts19,20,78. For the latter point, culling bat colonies can have unintended consequences, as it can increase viral prevalence in bats and, in turn, amplify spillover risk42,83. We hope our analyses can aid in facilitating conversations highlighting the ways in which human activities, not bats inherently, drive zoonotic viral emergence1,42,43.
