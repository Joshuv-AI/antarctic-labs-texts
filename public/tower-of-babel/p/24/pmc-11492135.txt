Comparing AI/ML approaches and classical regression for predictive modeling using large population health databases: Applications to COVID-19 case prediction
Authors: Lise M. Bjerre, Cayden Peixoto, Rawan Alkurd, Robert Talarico, Rami Abielmona
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResearch comparing artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML) methods with classical statistical methods applied to large population health databases is limited.ObjectivesThis retrospective cohort study aimed to compare the predictive performance of AI/ML algorithms against conventional multivariate logistic regression models using linked health administrative data.MethodsUsing Ontario's population health databases, we created a cohort of residents of the city of Ottawa, Ontario, who underwent a PCR test for COVID-19 between March 10, 2020, and May 13, 2021. Using demographic, socio-economic and health data (including COVID-19 PCR test results and available, symptom data), we developed predictive models for the purpose of COVID-19 case identification using the following approaches: classical multivariate logistic regression (LR); deep neural network (DNN); random forest (RF); and gradient boosting trees (GBT). Model performance comparisons were made using the area under the curve (AUC) swarm plot for 10-fold cross-validation.ResultsThe cohort consisted of n = 351,248 Ottawa residents tested for COVID-19 during the study period. Among whom, a total of n = 883,879 unique COVID-19 tests were performed (2.6 % positive test results). Inclusion of COVID-19 symptoms data in the analysis improved model performance and variable predictive value across all tested models (p < 0.0001), with the 10-fold cross-validation AUC increasing to near or over 0.7 in all models when symptoms data were included. In various pairwise comparisons, the GBT method had the highest predictive ability (AUC = 0.796 ± 0.017), significantly outperforming multivariate logistic regression and the other AI/ML approaches.ConclusionsConventional multivariate regression-based models are better than some and worse than other machine learning algorithms to provide good predictive accuracy in a moderate dataset with a reasonable number of features. However, whenever possible, the AI/ML GBT approach should be considered.

Introduction

Background

For decades, frequentist multivariate regression modeling has been the cornerstone of large population database analyses in observational population health research [1]. Historically, computational capacity was a limiting factor, restricting both the number of observations that could be included in an analysis (i.e. database ‘size’) and the number of variables that can be included in a model (i.e. model complexity). In recent decades, computational capacity has grown dramatically, yet the methods employed have largely remained unchanged. With the rapid development of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) algorithms and techniques in the field of computer science, and the increase in computational capacity worldwide, new opportunities have arisen for the analysis of large datasets and databases, including health-related data [2,3].

The most commonly used approach to data analysis in healthcare research is the application of multivariate regression analysis to determine predictors of an outcome [4,5]. However, AI/ML approaches (e.g. Gradient Boosting, Random Forest, and Complex Neural Network algorithms) can also be used to analyze routinely collected population health data, based on multiple interaction factors, while providing an efficient and integrative database and research platform for tasks related to disease spread and prevention [6]. Integrating machine learning into the routine collection of health data allows for data mining and analysis in real time, i.e., sorting and cleaning health administrative data as it is collected (or transferred), and scanning the data to detect novel patterns or correlations [5]. This could allow for more efficient and accurate data management and analysis processes compared to conventional methods.

Previous research comparing regression modeling with AI/ML approaches in health research settings has found that AI/ML methods have similar accuracy and predictive performance compared to logistic regression models [[7], [8], [9]]. Similarly, Chowdhury et al. compared machine learning algorithms with Cox proportional hazard modeling to predict hypertension incidence using Canadian population-health survival data and found little difference in predictive performance between both approaches [10]. However, research comparing AI/ML methods with classical multivariate regression methods using large population health databases, where the number of observations is of the order of tens to hundreds of thousands of patients, remains limited [[11], [12], [13]]. The present study addresses this knowledge gap from the perspective of both health data analysis methodology and applications to real-life problems.

Objectives

The objectives of this study were to: (1) apply AI/ML models (Random Forests (RF), Deep Neural Networks (DNN), and Extreme Gradient Boosted Trees (GBT)) and multivariate logistic regression (LR) as predictive modeling tools, using COVID-19 case identification as determined by a positive PCR test as an example; and (2) identify and quantify the best AI/ML approaches in terms of precision, accuracy, and reproducibility in comparison with logistic regression.

Methods

Study design, setting and participants

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted using population health administrative data for the city of Ottawa, Ontario, Canada (population: approximately 1,000,000 inhabitants). The study period extends from March 10th, 2020 (first PCR test conducted in Ottawa), through May 13th, 2021 (end of available data at the time of analysis). The study population included all community-dwelling residents of the City of Ottawa who underwent PCR testing for COVID-19 during the study period. During this time, individuals who experienced an exposure to COVID-19 (e.g., contact with a COVID-19 positive individual) or were experiencing any COVID-19 related symptoms were recommended by provincial and municipal health authorities to undergo PCR testing at a community testing site in Ottawa. PCR testing was therefore available to all residents of the City of Ottawa potentially exposed to COVID-19 or experiencing COVID-like symptoms during the study period. For subgroup analyses, we created a study cohort composed of all patients who had a PCR COVID test during the study period, and a cohort composed of patients who underwent a PCR COVID test and for whom symptom data was available. This study complied with the Reporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data (RECORD) guidelines (Appendix 1) [14].

The study cohort comprised all residents of the City of Ottawa who underwent PCR testing for COVID-19 between March 10th, 2020 to May 13th, 2021 (Fig. 1). We excluded individuals missing income quintile or COVID-19 vaccination data, residents in federal prisons and Long-Term Care (LTC) residents. This is because the nature of the disease spreading in LTCs, does not match the behavior of disease spread in community-based households, particularly in the context of COVID-19 [15]. Data sources

This study uses data from ICES (formerly the Institute for Clinical Evaluation Sciences), the province of Ontario's population health data steward. ICES is an independent, nonprofit research institute whose legal status under Ontario's health information privacy law allows it to collect and analyze health care and demographic data, without consent, for health system evaluation and improvement. The ICES data employed in this study include COVID-19 testing data, COVID-19 symptoms data, COVID-19 vaccine data, and cohort data for residents of the City of Ottawa, Ontario. See Appendix 2 for more information.

Ethics

The use of ICES data in this project was authorized under section 45 of Ontario's Personal Health Information Protection Act, which does not require review by a Research Ethics Board.

Outcome

In this study, three (3) machine learning (ML) models were used to model the main outcome, which was the probability of contracting COVID and testing positive for COVID-19 via a PCR test, for both symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals. This outcome was coded as a binary variable (positive/negative PCR COVID test).

The employed ML models in this study were Random Forests, Deep Neural Networks, and Extreme Gradient Boosted Trees. These were compared with classical, frequentist multivariate Logistic Regression. Descriptions of the ML models are provided in appended materials (Appendix 3). The models were constructed using python 3.9. Open-source libraries such as NumPy, Pandas, TensorFlow 2.8.0, scikit-learn 1.0.2, xgboost 1.6.0, and Shapely 1.8.1, were utilized in the analysis.

Predictors

The analyses in this study were conducted on data with and without symptom information. Therefore, the COVID-19 testing data is filtered to include data records with symptom information available. COVID-19 symptoms data includes information about the presence of symptoms, and the type, such as runny nose and/or headache. As the symptom information was recorded as free-form text, in a non-systematic manner, one symptom can be represented in various forms in the data. For example, the symptom of headaches could be found in the symptom data as HEADACHES, HEAD ACHE, and HEADA. To make the data usable by the ML models, the symptoms were cleaned and processed to have a unique label for each symptom that appeared in the data. The variables included in the models for analyses performed with and without symptom data are listed in the data supplement (Tables S1 and S2).

Statistical analysis methods

Model training

To estimate the likelihood of testing positive for COVID-19 after being tested, we evaluated six different machine learning algorithms: Logistic Regression, balanced Logistic Regression (LR balanced), Random Forest, balanced Random Forest (balanced RF), Deep Neural Networks, and Extreme Gradient Boosting Trees. To account for imbalanced data, where one class (positive COVID-19 cases) is underrepresented compared to the other [16], we utilized a weights adjustment method [17]. This technique adjusts the weights of instances in the minority class- balancing the dataset without generating synthetic samples. Further details on how the data balancing can be found in the supplementary materials (Appendix 4). DNN and GBT inherently handle imbalanced data well and do not require additional balancing techniques [[18], [19]]. This is because DNNs are capable of automatically learning complex relationships in the data, while GBTs iteratively correct for errors by focusing on misclassified instances, making them less sensitive to imbalanced datasets. To fine-tune the models' performance, we employed grid search, a method used to systematically search for the optimal hyperparameters of the models [20]. This approach allowed us to effectively optimize the models' performance.

Model validation

To ensure the reliability of our models and evaluate their performance, we employed a technique called 10-fold cross-validation (CV) [21]. This method involves dividing the dataset into ten equal parts, or folds, and iteratively training the model on nine of these folds while testing its performance on the remaining fold. By repeating this process ten times with different combinations of training and testing sets, we gained a robust understanding of how well the model generalizes to new, unseen data. This cross-validation splitting was performed before any pre-processing steps, including balancing, to prevent data leakage. Moreover, balancing was only applied to the training set, and not to the test set, to ensure that the test set remained a true reflection of unseen data.

The effectiveness of each model was assessed using the Area Under the Curve (AUC) metric, which measures how well the model distinguishes between positive and negative COVID-19 cases. A higher AUC value indicates better performance and more accurate predictions. This rigorous validation process ensured that our models were reliable and capable of making accurate predictions on unseen data. Model performance (AUC values) between the six tested models in this study were compared using two-sided, unpaired t-tests.

Feature importance analysis

To analyze the contribution of the different features to the models' AUC scores, we estimated SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values [22,23], a state-of-the-art algorithm in Machine Learning explainability. SHAP values are based on Shapley values, a concept borrowed from game theory, which quantifies the contribution that each feature brings to the predictability of the model. Briefly, higher SHAP values indicate greater positive predictive power. Therefore, for this study, the higher the SHAP value, the better a variable in the model is at predicting a positive COVID-19 PCR test. While SHAP values typically fall between −1 and 1, they are not constrained within that range. SHAP values represent a calculation of the average contribution of each feature in a dataset to the prediction, therefore can take any real value depending on the magnitude of a feature's impact.

Model performance

To assess the contribution of symptom data to the performance of the prediction models, we compared the 10-fold cross-validation (CV) for the six predictive algorithms (LR, LR balanced, RF, RF balanced, DNN and GBT) modelled with and without symptom data. As illustrated in Fig. 1, Fig. 2, the performance of RF balanced, with and without symptoms data, is significantly better compared to the performance of RF (p < 0.0001). Likewise, the performance of LR balanced is higher than the performance of LR, but this result was only statistically significant when calculated with symptoms data (p = 0.0248). Feature important analysis

For models trained without symptom data, variables that scored high in predictive value, i.e. higher SHAP values, included age, gender (M or F), and Number of Outpatient Physician Visits (count_outpat_phys) in the two years prior to the COVID test date (Fig. 3a-c). Alternatively, for models trained with symptom data, variables with high predictive value included the presence or absence of any symptoms, age, cough, fever, and having received the first dose of vaccine at least 14 days prior to COVID-19 test (protective effect). Discussion

Interpretation

This study evaluated the predictive ability of AI/ML algorithms and compared their performance with classical multivariate regression. The AI/ML approaches and multivariate logistic regression models performed well, with the 10-fold cross-validation AUC being near or over 0.7 in all six tested models when COVID-19 symptoms were included in the model algorithms (Fig. 2). Cross-validation analysis found that the gradient boosting tree method produced the strongest model performance. Across all six tested models, the inclusion of COVID-19 symptoms data increased model performance and variable predictive value, as demonstrated by increased AUC (Table 3) and SHAP (Table 4) values.

Among the tested AI/ML models in this study, only gradient boosting trees and balanced random forest performed better than the classical logistic regression model. In fact, GBT was found to perform significantly better than all 5 other tested methods in this study (LR, LR Balanced, RF, RF Balanced, and DNN). On the other hand, Logistic regression (balanced) performed better than random forest (RF) in both settings (with and without symptom data), and better than deep neural network (DNN) when symptom data were available. While limited, previous research using large clinical or health-administrative datasets to compare AI/ML methods with logistic regression helps support these findings. In a systematic review of the use of machine learning methods to predict acute kidney injury, Song et al. found that compared to other machine learning methods, GBT commonly exhibited the highest accuracy and predictive performance [12]. GBT has also been found to have greater predictive accuracy, compared to both random forest and logistic regression, in predicting incident diabetes [24], and renal function decline [25]. These findings, along with present results, support the use of AI/ML methods, particularly GBT, for predictive modeling across a variety of healthcare contexts and settings, and suggest that GBT may outperform both logistic regression and other AI/ML models when using clinical or population health data. On the other hand, our results also provide reassurance about the continued validity of the large corpus of classical epidemiologic predictive modeling work using logistic regression that predates the advent of AI/ML approaches.

The inclusion of COVID-19 symptom data significantly improved predictive performance across all six tested methods in this study (Table 3). Therefore, overall model accuracy and predictive ability may be improved with increased model complexity (additional variables relevant to the outcome). However, there is a lack of comparators in the literature for our findings, as previous research comparing regression and AI/ML model performance using (training) datasets of different complexity is limited, uses much smaller datasets, and is often inconclusive [[11], [12], [13]]. Although some studies have similarly reported improved model performance with the inclusion of additional variables of interest [26,27], further research investigating the effect of symptoms data on predicting diagnoses of other infections and diseases is warranted to confirm this association. Regardless, these results provide evidence for the importance of capturing relevant symptoms data at the clinical and health-administrative level, as this data may help improve statistical modeling in epidemiological research.

Notably, the predictive contributions (SHAP values) of the non-symptoms data variables in this study were either increased or attenuated by the inclusion of symptoms data in the models (Fig. 3a-c). Given the improved model performances, the SHAP values when symptoms data were included are likely to be more representative of a variable's true predictive contribution [28]. These findings are also consistent with what would be expected in clinical practice based on the diagnostic reasoning of an experienced clinician.

Limitations

While this study took measures to improve model training data quality through model training (Appendix 3), bias may still have been introduced due to under- or overfitting of the training dataset(s). Training datasets may not reflect the true distribution of a population, which can negatively impact the generalizability of a model's predictions [29]. To address this potential bias and to prevent over- and underfitting during model training, we used multiple, random samples of study data to train the AI/ML models. However, the generalizability of our findings may also be limited due to study data being Ottawa-based; it is currently unknown if our results would be replicated using similar databases from other larger Canadian jurisdictions, such as Toronto, Montreal or Vancouver, or from jurisdictions in other countries. Nonetheless, our findings provide evidence that AI/ML methods can be applied to large population administrative health data with comparable predictive accuracy to multivariate logistic regression. Future research involving large population health data should consider applying and comparing AI/ML methods along with classical regression, to determine potential circumstances in which ML may outperform regression analysis, and vice versa.

Another limitation of this study was the restricted availability of variables to train models. Model predictive ability could be improved by including other data sets, such as publicly available data on wastewater viral levels, real-world mobility data, and data about government confinement measure (lockdowns, travel bans, etc.), and linking them with the population health data used in this study. Our study initially planned to include a secondary analysis that would have linked publicly available, additional data to our core population health dataset. However, long administrative delays in obtaining approval for and executing such linkages prevented that part of the study from being completed.

This situation illustrates a major challenge in health research using big data: Data, although it exists, may not be accessible in the form or within the timeframe required by a time-limited, fixed-budget research project. As AI/ML approaches will require larger and more comprehensive training data sets than what has traditionally been needed for classical epidemiological population health data studies, this problem deserves the attention of policy decision-makers, privacy and legal officers, and researchers alike. Otherwise, the benefits of big data analytics may never be reaped to their full potential.

Conclusion

In this population health data cohort study, we compared the predictive performance of machine learning algorithms (Random Forests, Deep Neural Networks, and Extreme Gradient Boosted Trees) against multivariate logistic regression for determining individuals most likely to be diagnosed with COVID-19 in the community (as determined by a positive PCR test). Across the models, differences in performance were small, but significant, with GBT having a better predictive ability than both logistic regression or the other AI/ML algorithms. Based on this study's findings, conventional regression-based models are comparable to machine learning algorithms to provide good predictive accuracy in a moderate dataset with a reasonable number of features, however, whenever possible, AI/ML approaches, and GBT in particular, should be considered. Additional studies using a diversity of datasets and clinical settings would help establish a firm foundation for the comparative study of different modeling approaches, both conventional and AI/ML-based. Data access and privacy-related concerns remain a major hurdle when attempting to link external, publicly available data with proprietary population health data sets for such purposes, and deserve the attention of multiple stakeholders if the potential of big data for health knowledge and improvement is to be realized.
