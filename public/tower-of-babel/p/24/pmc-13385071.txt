Hugo™ RAS for robot-assisted radical prostatectomy: outcomes from a consecutive single-center study at a Hugo-exclusive institution
Authors: Hiroki Kamikawa, Shun Takai, Kosuke Tsuda, Soichiro Yamada, Keiko Okumura, Nobuyuki Fukuhara, Tomoyasu Sano, Shohei Ishida, Yoshihisa Matsukawa, Shusuke Akamatsu, Kenji Zennami, MEGUMI (MEidai GenitoUrinaryMega Investigation) project
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Most early reports on the Hugo™ Robotic- assisted surgery (RAS) system originated from institutions with concurrent access to established robotic platforms, potentially introducing device selection bias. We conducted a retrospective assessment of 103 patients with localized prostate cancer treated using Hugo™ RAS-assisted robot-assisted radical prostatectomy at a Japanese municipal hospital where no alternative robotic platform was available. The analysis focused on perioperative performance, safety outcomes, and consistency of procedural implementation. A standardized surgical setup, including a W-shaped port configuration and predefined floor-marking-based docking strategy, was applied. Surgery was carried out by six surgeons with different levels of robotic experience, including one surgeon without prior robotic operative experience. Operative, console, and docking durations showed median values of 152 min (IQR 129–218.5), 108 min (IQR 85–174.5), and 5 min (IQR 4–7), respectively. Estimated intraoperative blood loss was 100 mL (IQR 50–214). Neither intraoperative blood transfusion nor conversion to open surgery was necessary in any patient. Postoperative complications occurred in four patients (3.9%), including one major complication (Clavien–Dindo > II; 1.0%). Positive surgical margins were observed in 35 patients (34.0%) and 79.6% achieved urinary continence at 3 months. In this Hugo-exclusive, single-platform environment, RARP using the Hugo™ RAS system was performed without major safety concerns across surgeons with varying experience. Although four independent-arm carts needed to be rolled in, the median docking time was 5 min, possibly facilitated by a standardized floor-marking strategy adopted at our institution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11701-026-03704-y.

Introduction

The Hugo™ Robotic- assisted surgery (RAS) system became the first robotic surgical platform at our institution, a 500-bed municipal hospital in Japan, enabling evaluation in a uniform, real-world setting where alternative robotic systems were unavailable.

Robot-assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP) has been widely adopted for the surgical management of clinically localized prostate cancer. Previous comparative studies have demonstrated lower perioperative morbidity with RARP than with open radical prostatectomy, particularly regarding intraoperative bleeding and postoperative recovery, with preserved oncological and functional outcomes [1, 2]. Pooled analyses and meta-analytic data corroborate these findings, showing no meaningful inferiority relative to open or laparoscopic approaches in oncological or functional endpoints [3, 4]. Nationwide cohort data from England have likewise demonstrated comparable patient-reported recovery across surgical modalities at a medium-term follow-up [5].

Following national health insurance coverage in November 2022, the Hugo™ platform entered Japanese urological practice, and early clinical experiences have begun to appear [6, 7]. To date, however, nearly all published Hugo™ RARP series have originated from institutions where an established robotic platform such as da Vinci was concurrently available [6, 8, 9]. In such dual/ multi-platform settings, surgeons can transfer existing robotic skills and institutional workflows from one system to the other, and the allocation of cases between platforms may introduce device-selection bias. Outcomes from a Hugo-exclusive institution — where all robotic workflows, team training, and surgical routines must be established de novo — remain unreported.

We therefore report 103 consecutive Hugo™ RARP procedures performed at a community hospital where the Hugo™ system was the sole robotic platform, with the aim of providing the first outcome data from a Hugo-exclusive institutional environment. Specifically, we evaluated perioperative safety across multiple surgeons, including a novice, and examined whether standardized surgical workflows could produce consistent results without the support of pre-existing robotic infrastructure.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective study included patients treated using Hugo™ RAS-assisted RARP for clinically localized prostate cancer at a municipal hospital in Japan. Patients undergoing salvage surgery or requiring simultaneous bladder resection for cT4 disease were excluded. Tumor stage was determined using the TNM classification system and risk stratification was performed based on the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) criteria.

Surgical technique

Port configuration is illustrated in Fig. 1a. To minimize external interference between robotic arms, a W-shaped arrangement was used. Pneumoperitoneum was established using optical trocar entry through a 5-mm assistant port, followed by the sequential placement of the remaining trocars under direct visualization. Arm angulation and tilt parameters are presented in Fig. 1b.

A 30° endoscope was routinely used, whereas a 0° endoscope was selected depending on the surgeon’s preference and the patient’s anatomy. The right robotic arm was equipped with monopolar curved shears, while bipolar Maryland forceps were used in the left arm. Cadiere forceps served as the fourth arm when required. Large needle drivers were mounted on both robotic arms during suturing.

Patients underwent surgery in the supine position under a 25° Trendelenburg inclination before docking the Hugo™ RAS platform. The operation was subsequently completed from the console, with the bedside assistant standing on the patient’s left side. A transperitoneal route was used in all cases.

Dorsal venous complex control, nerve-sparing techniques, and pelvic lymph node dissection were selectively applied according to tumor characteristics, oncologic risk, and both the surgeon’s and patient’s preferences. Posterior reconstruction was routinely performed using either a modified Rocco stitch [10] or “advanced reconstruction of vesicourethral support” [11], depending on the surgeon’s preference. A continuous 3 –0 V-Loc™ suture was used for both posterior reconstruction and vesicourethral anastomosis.

Surgeon’s experience

Six surgeons performed RARP during the study period. One surgeon was a novice in RARP and performed 21 procedures, whereas the remaining five had sufficient prior experience, defined as having performed more than 200 procedures using other robotic platforms, and performed 70, 3, 3, 3, and 3 procedures, respectively. Perioperative outcomes were compared between experienced surgeons (n = 82) and the novice surgeon (n = 21). To visualize temporal trends in the novice surgeon’s performance, operative and console times were plotted sequentially and fitted with LOWESS curves (smoothing fraction = 2/3). The novice surgeon’s cases were also divided into two chronological halves for exploratory comparison.

Data collection and outcome measures

Clinical background information and perioperative variables were extracted retrospectively from institutional electronic records. Evaluated perioperative parameters included operative, console, docking, and vesicourethral anastomosis times; intraoperative blood loss; transfusion requirement; performance of extended pelvic lymph node dissection; conversion to open procedures; postoperative hospitalization; and urethral catheter duration. Pathological outcomes and postoperative prostate-specific antigen (PSA) persistence were additionally assessed.

For patients with very-high-risk disease, ePLND was recommended and performed after obtaining informed consent. For patients with high-risk disease or lower-risk disease, ePLND was performed only at the patient’s request.

When intraoperative blood loss was described as “minimal,” a value of 1 mL was assigned for statistical analysis.

Postoperative adverse events arising during the first 30 days after surgery were categorized according to the Clavien–Dindo grading system. Events exceeding grade II were categorized as major complications.

Urinary continence status was assessed 3 months postoperatively using outpatient follow-up data. Patients requiring either zero pads or one precautionary pad daily were regarded as continent, in line with definitions frequently adopted in previous RARP studies [6, 8, 12].

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented using median values and interquartile ranges (IQRs), whereas categorical data are described as numbers and percentages. The Mann–Whitney U test was applied for comparisons involving continuous variables, whereas Fisher’s exact test was selected for categorical data analysis. A P value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data processing was conducted using EZR ver. 1.70 (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan) [13], a graphical interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). LOWESS curve fitting was performed using R directly.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Kasugai Municipal Hospital (No. 647). Because of the retrospective study design, written informed consent was waived and an opt-out approach was used.

Results

Patient characteristics

This study cohort consisted of 103 patients undergoing Hugo™ RAS-assisted RARP (Table 1). The median age was 74 years (IQR 70–77) and body mass index was 23.2 kg/m² (IQR 21.7–25.7). Most patients were categorized as American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status I or II (95.1%). The median follow-up period was 10 months (IQR 7–16 months).

The median preoperative PSA value was 7.89 ng/mL (IQR 5.66–12.1). Based on NCCN criteria, the cohort included 3 patients (2.9%) with very low-risk disease, 7 (6.8%) with low-risk disease, 15 (14.6%) with favorable intermediate-risk disease, 25 (24.3%) with unfavorable intermediate-risk disease, 44 (42.7%) with high-risk disease, and 9 (8.7%) with very high-risk disease. Clinical T stage and biopsy grade group distributions are detailed in Table 1.

Perioperative outcomes

Details of perioperative outcomes are summarized in Table 2. Median operative, console, docking, and vesicourethral anastomosis durations were 152 min (IQR 129–218.5), 108 min (IQR 85–174.5), 5 min (IQR 4–7), and 13 min (IQR 10–17), respectively.

Estimated blood loss during surgery was 100 mL (IQR 50–214) and intraoperative transfusion was not required in any patient. Extended pelvic lymph node dissection was performed in 10 patients (9.7%), although no operation required conversion to an open approach. Median postoperative hospitalization and urethral catheter placement durations were 7 days (IQR 7–7) and 5 days (IQR 5–5), respectively.

Postoperative complications

Complications within 30 days developed in four patients (3.9%) (Table 2), including urinary leakage in three patients (2.9%) and bowel obstruction caused by an incisional hernia in one patient (1.0%). One patient (1.0%) experienced a major complication classified as Clavien–Dindo grade > II.

No readmissions occurred within 30 days after surgery. Reoperation within 30 days was required in one patient (1.0%) and no perioperative deaths were recorded.

Pathological findings

Pathological outcomes are summarized in Table 3. Acinar adenocarcinoma was identified in all but one patient and variant histology was present in two patients (2.1%). Pathological stage pT3 disease was identified in 24 patients (23.3%) and positive surgical margins (PSM) were observed in 35 patients (34.0%). Pathological T stage showed a significant association with surgical margin status (P < 0.001; Online Resource 1). Outcomes stratified by organ-confined (pT2) and non-organ-confined (≥ pT3) disease are presented in Online Resource 2.

Urinary continence

Three months postoperatively, 82 patients (79.6%) were considered continent.

Outcomes stratified by surgeon experience

Perioperative outcomes stratified by surgeon experience are presented in online resource 3. Patient characteristics, including age, BMI, preoperative PSA, and NCCN risk distribution, did not differ significantly between groups. The novice surgeon had significantly longer operative time (258 vs. 141 min, P < 0.001), console time (201 vs. 98 min, P < 0.001), anastomosis time (24 vs. 12 min, P < 0.001), and greater estimated blood loss (200 vs. 100 mL, P = 0.001) compared with experienced surgeons. Docking time did not differ between groups (5 vs. 6 min, P = 0.640). The novice surgeon performed DVC suturing at a significantly higher rate than experienced surgeons (71.4% vs. 14.6%, P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in overall complication rate (4.8% vs. 3.7%, P = 1.000), major complication rate (4.8% vs. 0%, P = 0.204), or positive surgical margin rate (19.0% vs. 37.8%, P = 0.127). Urinary incontinence at 3 months was significantly higher in the novice group (42.9% vs. 14.6%, P = 0.012). The novice surgeon’s sequential operative and console times are shown in Fig. 2. A trend toward improvement was observed, with median operative time decreasing from 270 min in the first 10 cases to 234 min in the last 11 cases, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.098).

PSA persistence

Three months postoperatively, PSA persistence (> 0.2 ng/mL) was detected in three patients (8.6%) with PSM, whereas no cases were identified in patients with negative surgical margins (NSM) (Online Resource 1, P = 0.037).

Discussion

In this series of 103 patients undergoing Hugo™ RAS-assisted RARP for localized prostate cancer, perioperative outcomes during the initial implementation phase are presented in the context of previously published Hugo™ RARP data [6, 8, 12]. Because the Hugo™ system was the sole robotic platform at our institution, the present cohort reflects outcomes attributable entirely to this single platform. The inclusion of six surgeons with varying levels of experience, including one surgeon without previous robotic experience, increases the generalizability of the present findings. Even with substantial variability in surgeon experience, operative times and complication rates did not raise major safety concerns [9], suggesting that the introduction of this modular platform is feasible in a clinical setting. Importantly, the present study differs from prior Hugo™ RARP reports in that no alternative robotic platform was available at our institution. In dual/ multi-platform centers, Hugo™ is typically introduced alongside an existing da Vinci program, allowing surgeons and operating room teams to leverage established robotic routines. At our institution, all aspects of robotic surgery — including port placement strategy, docking protocols, instrument handling, and team coordination — were developed specifically for the Hugo™ system from the outset. The stratified analysis presented herein, showing that a novice surgeon achieved comparable safety outcomes to experienced surgeons (Online resource 3), and the multivariable analysis identifying pathological stage rather than surgeon experience as the dominant predictor of PSM (Table 4), are insights that are uniquely interpretable in this single-platform institutional setting.

Console and docking times in the present cohort were numerically lower than those in previously published Hugo™ RARP series [6, 8, 12], although direct comparison across different institutional settings is limited. Although console time may be affected by the surgeon’s experience and patient-related factors, docking time can potentially be standardized through surgical setup optimization.

Before initiation of the first clinical case, roll-in simulations using the actual robotic system were conducted to determine the optimal operating table position and fixation points for each robotic arm; these positions were marked on the operating room floor. During surgery, the robotic arms were aligned according to these predefined markings. Minor discrepancies between arm-cart positioning and port placement caused by patient body habitus could be compensated by the three joints incorporated into each arm cart. In this context, docking in the first case was completed within 7 min. Docking duration did not significantly differ according to surgeon or assistant team combinations, suggesting that the standardized setup, including the floor-marking protocol, may have contributed to consistent docking performance, although the effect of general team learning cannot be separated from this observation.

Regarding robotic arm configuration, tilt and angulation settings were initially determined according to the report by Takahara et al. [6], and all procedures were performed in a closed-leg supine position. Adoption of a W-shaped port configuration helped overcome a recognized limitation of the Hugo™ RAS platform, namely extracorporeal arm interference. In RARP, maintaining at least 3 cm of craniocaudal separation between the camera trocar and bilateral working ports effectively minimizes arm collision.

The median vesicourethral anastomosis time was 13 min. The Hugo™ RAS platform incorporates a rotational clutch mechanism that facilitates precise needle manipulation. Unlike conventional match-grip systems, this mechanism allows movements beyond the natural constraints of the human wrist. In addition, rotational scaling can be adjusted to 1.5× or 2×, enabling wider intracorporeal movement with relatively small hand motion. These characteristics may partially explain the short anastomosis times observed in the present cohort. For institutions planning to introduce the Hugo™ RAS system, the technical adaptations described above — including the W-shaped port configuration, floor-marking-based docking protocol, and utilization of the rotary clutch mechanism — may serve as a practical reference. A consolidated summary of these recommendations is provided in Online Resource 4.

Major complications (Clavien–Dindo grade > II) occurred in one patient (1.0%) and reoperation within 30 days was required in one patient (1.0%) because of an incarcerated incisional hernia at the prostatectomy site. Neither open conversion, intraoperative transfusion, nor perioperative mortality occurred. Published Hugo™ RARP data report similar complication rates [10], though direct comparison is limited by differences in study design and patient populations [9]. Vesicourethral anastomotic leakage occurred in three patients (2.9%), of whom two were in the novice surgeon group, likely reflecting the early learning curve. All cases were classified as Clavien–Dindo grade < III and resolved with conservative management.

PSM were observed in 35 patients (34.0%). Our cohort included a relatively high proportion of advanced disease, with 51.5% classified as NCCN high or very high risk and 23.3% having ≥pT3 disease. On multivariable analysis, ≥pT3 was the dominant predictor of PSM (OR 20.16, P < 0.001), while surgeon experience was not a statistically significant factor (P = 0.069) (Table 4). In prior Hugo™ RARP series, Takahara et al. reported a PSM rate of 7.7% [6], Scarcia et al. 44% [8], and Totaro et al. 40.9% [12], and a meta-analysis of RARP outcomes reported pooled PSM rates of 13.5–34.3% depending on pathological stage [9]. The anatomical distribution of PSM (Online resource 5) showed that the apex was the most common location (16/35, 45.7%), followed by the base (7/35, 20.0%) and multifocal margins (6/35, 17.1%). The apex is the most frequently reported PSM site after RARP across platforms [14], reflecting the inherent trade-off between sphincter preservation and oncological clearance at the prostatic apex. Notably, in the novice surgeon, 3 of 4 positive margins (75.0%) occurred at the apex, with no multifocal or anterior margins, suggesting that margin positivity was attributable to the anatomical constraints of apical dissection rather than inadequate overall surgical technique. PSA persistence exceeding 0.2 ng/mL at 1 month was identified in 14.3% and 1.5% of patients in the PSM and NSM groups, respectively. At 3 months, these values decreased to 8.6% and 0%, respectively. The persistence of PSA has been linked to higher risks of biochemical recurrence, clinical recurrence, and cancer-specific mortality [15]. However, the available follow-up duration remains insufficient for the reliable evaluation of durable oncological control.

At 3 months after surgery, continence was achieved in 82 patients (79.6%), suggesting a favorable early urinary functional recovery. The present study included all cases performed during the institutional introduction phase, including procedures conducted by a surgeon without prior robotic experience. Despite this, perioperative and functional outcomes remained acceptable. Therefore, the present cohort likely reflects a practical real-world setting encountered during the early implementation phase of robotic surgery programs.

Stratified analysis revealed that the novice surgeon required significantly longer operative and console times and had higher blood loss than experienced surgeons, which is expected during the early learning phase. Notably, major complications and positive surgical margin rates did not differ significantly between groups, suggesting that safety was maintained despite the learning curve. The novice surgeon adopted DVC suturing in 71.4% of cases, compared with 14.6% among experienced surgeons (P < 0.001), which may reflect a more cautious surgical strategy aimed at minimizing intraoperative bleeding. The higher rate of urinary incontinence at 3 months in the novice group (42.9% vs. 14.6%, P = 0.012) warrants further follow-up to determine whether continence recovery improves with longer observation. The LOWESS curve in Fig. 2 suggests a trend toward shorter operative and console times with increasing case experience, although the 21-case volume is insufficient to identify a definitive plateau.

Nonetheless, this study has some important limitations. First, because the analysis was conducted retrospectively at a single municipal institution, caution is warranted when extrapolating these results to other practice settings. Second, no historical institutional data from an alternative robotic platform is available for intra-institutional benchmarking. Third, postoperative functional evaluation focused primarily on urinary continence at 3 months, whereas detailed data regarding sexual outcomes and patient-reported quality-of-life assessments were not available. Fourth, the relatively short follow-up duration precludes definitive conclusions regarding long-term oncological outcomes, including biochemical recurrence. Finally, the clinical impact of the absence of haptic feedback, a commonly discussed characteristic of the Hugo™ RAS platform [16], was not specifically evaluated. Further investigation using prospective multicenter designs and longer follow-up periods may provide more robust evidence regarding reproducibility, functional recovery, and oncological durability using Hugo™ RAS-assisted RARP.

Conclusions

Hugo™ RAS-assisted RARP was performed without major safety concerns during the early institutional adoption period, including cases performed by a novice robotic surgeon.
