Engaging stakeholders to strengthen coproduction of knowledge in the Long‐Term Agroecosystem Research (LTAR) indicator framework
Authors: Alia N DeLong, Matthew M McIntosh, Megan Donovan, Sheri A Spiegal, Elizabeth H Boughton, Hilary M Swain, Maria Lucia Silveira, Brent Sellers, Zachary M Hurst, Brandon T Bestelmeyer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRapidly changing agricultural conditions need solutions to address complex decision‐making in an evolving socio‐ecological landscape. One way to accelerate the pace of solution development is by working directly with end users to design potential tools. The Long‐Term Agroecosystem Research (LTAR) network has developed a performance‐based sustainability indicator system as one possible tool for producers, land managers, and other stakeholders to aid decision‐making. Two LTAR sites (in New Mexico and Florida) engaged with stakeholders to further develop the network‐level indicator framework to account for site‐level differences. We compared the sites’ engagement processes using a coproduction of knowledge framework to assess similarities and differences in outcomes. The New Mexico site used a less structured, dialogue‐based approach while the Florida site used a formalized social science methodology. We found that a structured engagement strategy that is exploratory allows for more creative insight from stakeholders. The strength of a dialogue‐based approach was identifying specific metrics that stakeholders find are feasible for data collection. Both approaches resulted in coproduced knowledge that increased relevancy of indicator frameworks at the local level. Maintaining an indicator framework that encourages common ground and is adaptable to different agroecosystem contexts is essential for continued usability. A variety of engagement strategies can strengthen the network‐level indicator framework. Without coproduction of knowledge, we could miss key indicators not considered by scientists and be less aware of the usability of the framework on the ground. Coproduction of knowledge between scientists and stakeholders can help identify areas to improve the indicator framework.

INTRODUCTION

Rapid changes in environmental and economic conditions have accelerated the need for data‐driven decision‐making in agriculture. One way to address the need for solutions that meet the pace of change in conditions is by directly working with end users of science and technology. Multiple groups across the agricultural sector, such as research scientists, technical advisors, and producers, can work collaboratively to develop tools that are relevant and support the need for complex and time‐sensitive decisions. Performance‐oriented sustainability indicators that collectively measure the multiple outcomes of agricultural management are a promising tool/solution for providing information to support decision‐making in agricultural systems. Effective sustainability indicator frameworks allow users to assess their production system and make informed decisions based on their own data or publicly available data in relation to contextual metrics (Toffolini et al., 2016). Engagement of a variety of end users in the tool development process is a key opportunity to ensure direct input and influence from stakeholders.

Collaborative science is a multidisciplinary, cross‐sector approach that involves multiple ways of knowing in the research process. One form of collaborative science, called coproduction of knowledge, involves engaging with stakeholder groups in decision‐making from research question development through dissemination of research and adoption of technology (Frank et al., 2022). Each stakeholder group representative offers their unique perspective, and the confluence of multiple sources of expertise can result in more robust decision‐making about research and its products. Together, these perspectives can increase the chance that research products are more useful and usable, especially when compared to the outcomes from scientists working alone (DeLong et al., 2022; Dumont et al., 2019; Reed et al., 2014). In the United States, stakeholders of agriculture range from small groups of producers who grow and harvest food commodities, to the entire US population and individuals abroad who consume them. It is vital to identify the needs of agricultural stakeholders and develop agricultural innovations to satisfy their needs. Oftentimes, however, engagement is limited because of logistical constraints and the relatively small number of researchers with expertise in this field. One area that could benefit from coproduction is networked science, which balances the needs of production with the demands for a safe and sustainable environment. One network to do that in is the United States Department of Agriculture's (USDA) Long‐Term Agroecosystem Research (LTAR) network, which is a network of a collaboration of 19 long‐term USDA Agricultural Research Service (ARS) and nonprofit/university sites focused on improving both agricultural efficiency and the well‐being of America's farming and ranching communities (Tsegaye et al., 2024).

Stakeholder engagement encompasses a vast range of strategies—from one‐time field days to series of in‐depth interviews (DeLong et al., 2024; Kujala et al., 2022; Thizy et al., 2019; Winowiecki et al., 2021). How researchers engage stakeholders is often complex and requires time and planning. However, many successful cases exist that scientists can learn from and adapt for their own purposes (Dinesh et al., 2018; Martinez et al., 2019). Effective coproduction of knowledge often requires resources like a trained social scientist and commitment over a period of several years (Djenontin & Meadow, 2018; Lemos & Morehouse, 2005), but other ways of engaging with stakeholders are possible that are less formal and less structured. The less rigorous approaches may not yield generalizable knowledge, but they still hold value for informing research and building relationships with stakeholders (Ricart et al., 2019; Sumane et al., 2018).

Coproduction of knowledge among agricultural scientists, producers, and service providers is an innate but typically informal part of the agricultural research cycle, ongoing for decades. In an effort to formalize the process in agriculture and other fields, a theoretical framework of the coproduction of knowledge emerged. According to Miller and Wyborn (2020), coproduction of knowledge independently but simultaneously emerged in at least three academic areas of study (public administration, science and technology studies, and sustainability science). While the definition of coproduction varies based on discipline, its use in the agricultural sciences generally describes it as ranging from problem identification to delivering solutions involving multiple ways of knowing (Akpo et al., 2015; Campbell et al., 2016; Djenontin & Meadow, 2018; Reed et al., 2014; Utter et al., 2021).

Djenontin and Meadow (2018) proposed a framework for coproduction of knowledge based on examples from various disciplines from around the globe. Detailed in Figure 1, their coproduction framework considers context, inputs, activities, and outputs as components for outcomes and impacts. Notably, activities and outputs are grouped together to denote a process occurring. This representation is a result of research that aimed to determine how coproduction of knowledge happens practically, including challenges faced and how they were overcome.

Framework of coproduction of knowledge, adapted from Djenontin and Meadow (2018).

Success in the coproduction framework is defined by whether or not a project produces “information usable by decision‐makers” (Djenontin & Meadow, 2018, p. 887). Two main ways identified by the authors to meet this criterion for coproduction are as follows: (1) beneficial changes in practice by either stakeholders, researchers, or both and (2) increasing the relevance of the knowledge base to decision‐makers (e.g., producers). Because coproduction generally occurs from problem identification through solution delivery, we are not able to report on the full coproduction framework. At the time of this publication, coproduction is ongoing at the research sites. The research reported in this study is a snapshot of two steps in the larger coproduction framework.

Our comparison was made in the context of a performance‐based sustainability indicator framework developed by the LTAR network. LTAR seeks ways to facilitate coproduction that feeds into and supports common approaches and frameworks to design, analyze, and synthesize across a whole suite of sites with different agriculture and physical conditions.

Sustainability indicator systems have emerged to understand and monitor the contexts and outcomes of agricultural management worldwide (Cabell & Oelofse, 2012; Latruffe et al., 2016). Many have been organized around sustainability concepts introduced in the United Nations Brundtland Report, which defined sustainability as meeting “the needs of the present without compromising the ability of the future generations to meet their own needs” (Brundtland, 1987). In line with the report, indicator frameworks often address the three sustainability pillars: environmental, economic, and social.

Over the past decade, scientists from diverse subject matter backgrounds in LTAR have worked toward a performance‐based sustainability indicator framework for use at the ranch level (Spiegal et al., 2022). Throughout the indicator framework's development, nine existing frameworks were assessed for applicability to the LTAR network's purpose and goals (detailed in Table 1 in Donovan et al., 2025). LTAR scientists considered existing indicator frameworks’ scale (e.g., farm vs. food system), whether it was holistic, how inclusive it was for diverse agroecosystems, and whether it was user‐driven. Those existing frameworks helped to inform a 5‐year process of iterative network‐wide discussions and synthesis toward an architecture for a performance indicator system, including a set of indicators that reflects the values of LTAR scientists and their perceptions of values of LTAR's many agricultural stakeholders nationwide (Tables A1 and A2).

The version of the indicator framework codified by the network in 2022, “v.2022” included three domains (Figure 2), two attributes of a sustainable farm or ranch per domain, and two–four indicators of the status of each attribute. In the LTAR system, multiple ranches (or farms) can measure the same indicator, but each can choose its own metrics to match local context (Spiegal et al., 2024; Webb et al., 2020). A more recent version was selected in 2024, with details in Donovan et al. (2025).

Collaborative research and the coproduction of knowledge has the potential to increase the usability of tools developed by LTAR scientists to help the network achieve its research and innovation goals in a reproducible manner. Sustainability indicator frameworks often involve stakeholder groups during the testing phase (Gunnarsdottir et al., 2021; Mascarenhas et al., 2015). However, more robust tools can be created if they are designed with stakeholders and if they play an active role in the research process (Reimer et al., 2021; Stuart et al., 2018).

We explore two examples of stakeholder engagement that helped to inform the LTAR indicator framework v.2022 and subsequent versions. One example (the USDA‐LTAR Jornada Experimental Range [JER] site in south‐central New Mexico) illustrates a dialogue‐focused approach with a flexible structure, whereas the second example (USDA‐LTAR Archbold Biological Station–University of Florida [ABS‐UF] in central Florida) demonstrates a structured approach involving deliberative decision‐making techniques.

The sustainability indicator framework has continued to evolve, and the latest network‐wide version is published in Donovan et al. (2025). Donovan's paper presents a framework that used a modified Delphi technique with multiple subject matter experts. The result was a framework with an additional domain (production) and modified indicators that can be used at the network level. Further, the social domain comprises attributes producer well‐being and community well‐being, part of which is also in Friedrichsen et al. (2025) present community well‐being consisting of the indicators conditions, capabilities, and connections. These changes occurred in 2024 and 2025 based on expert and stakeholder involvement, but were not examined as a part of this study. The coproduction processes presented in this study occurred in 2022 and 2023, and stakeholders examined the 2022 framework. This paper's primary focus is on comparing coproduction processes and the generation of new knowledge.

Coproduction of knowledge is a tool research teams can use to expand the perspectives that contribute to science. Coproduction requires engaging with diverse groups of stakeholders. In this study, we used the context of the LTAR sustainability indicator framework to assess two different coproduction processes. We examine the similarities and differences between the two approaches, describe their results, explore their strengths, weaknesses, benefits, and tradeoffs, and describe how they helped to improve the network‐wide framework. The purpose of this paper is to compare two coproduction processes (i.e., activity and output at two different research locations) and analyze if and how they resulted in improved relevance of knowledge.

METHODS

LTAR network

LTAR's 19 sites are geographically representative of US agroecosystems that use strategic research, coordinated experimentation, and common measurements to address agricultural challenges within and across major US agricultural ecosystems (croplands, grazinglands, and integrated systems). The network was formed in 2012, and initial sites were chosen based on regional representativeness and availability of long‐term datasets to set the stage for and inform the most pressing research needs across agricultural production sectors (Tsegaye et al., 2024). LTAR is funded by US Congress, with funds flowing through the USDA‐ARS and functions in close collaboration with university and nongovernmental partners to develop innovative research that is driven implicitly and explicitly by agricultural producers/stakeholders. The USDA‐ARS has a broad goal to “deliver cutting‐edge, scientific tools and innovative solutions for American farmers, producers, industry, and communities” (our key stakeholders) and LTAR seeks to “develop national strategies for the sustainable intensification of agriculture production” (Kleinman et al., 2018; LTAR, 2022, 2024; Tsegaye et al., 2024).

Part of LTAR's strategic plan aims for sites to develop tools that are “useful, usable, and used” (Tsegaye et al., 2024). “Stakeholder engagement” is an important factor in each of the seven research and organizational initiatives in LTAR's strategic plan, although stakeholder engagement is still loosely defined within the network. Protocols to engage stakeholders are still being developed. A one‐size‐fits‐all approach is insufficient (Tsegaye et al., 2024). At this time, all LTAR sites engage stakeholders in some manner across a broad spectrum from consultation to coproduction (see Journal of Environmental Quality special issue on the LTAR Common Experiment), and the human dimensions working group and engagement working group (EWG) have been established to assist biophysical scientists in engaging stakeholders (Tsegaye et al., 2024).

Study design

This work was designed to support LTAR's efforts to define and establish protocols for stakeholder engagement by demonstrating and comparing two cases of strategic capacity‐building stakeholder engagement that were aimed at informing an emerging scientific sustainability performance indicator framework. We employed a cross‐sectional comparison of two cases embedded in the LTAR network (Yin, 2017). Both cases are long‐term research units studying grazing production in different climatic regions of the United States. They both have a history of over 50 years of long‐term research (Boughton et al., 2024; Spiegal et al., 2024). Each site engages stakeholders in different manners within the context of the process of coproduction of knowledge (i.e., activity and output in Figure 1). Coproduction is an ongoing process at each site and this study serves as an analysis of a snapshot of coproduction as opposed to the analysis of the full cycle outlined in Figure 1. Each site has its own context and inputs, which we briefly present but do not describe exhaustively.

Jornada experimental range

The USDA‐ARS JER is a long‐term research site located in the northern Chihuahuan Desert of southern New Mexico. Scientists focus on identifying and testing sustainable rangeland management practices and improving scientific understanding of dryland ecology via several projects (Spiegal et al., 2024). Jornada's Sustainable Southwest Beef Coordinated Agricultural Project (SSB‐CAP), was a 5‐year project funded by the USDA‐National Institute of Food and Agriculture that tested strategies for ranch and rangeland resilience with over 100 research, teaching, and extension collaborators (Spiegal et al., 2020). As part of the SSB‐CAP, scientists enlisted five cooperating ranches spanning five states including New Mexico (JER; Chihuahuan Desert Rangeland Research Center), California (Rancho Corta Madera), Utah (The Nature Conservancy Canyonlands Research Center and Dugout Ranch), and South Dakota and Arizona (Evergreen Livestock & Ranching). Each ranch adopted at least one novel technology developed by the SSB‐CAP (heritage cattle genetics, precision ranching technologies, and marketing and supply chain options) (Spiegal et al., 2023). Although the impacts of these novel technologies had been hypothesized in comparative scientific studies (e.g., Cibils et al., 2023), a reproducible approach was needed for the empirical measurement and documentation of impacts and associated tradeoffs. The project team decided that ranch‐level sustainability indicators were needed to fill the gap. Synergistically, the researchers also believed that development of local indicators could help inform and improve the LTAR network‐level indicator system.

In May 2022, JER researchers sought to identify the ranchers’ priorities and standardize a system of indicators and their respective metrics that each participating ranch could collect. Inquiry about indicators was approved by the New Mexico State University Institutional Review Board (IRB; protocol #2407137289). Inquiry was conducted mainly at the annual SSB‐CAP meeting held at the 776‐km2 JER in Las Cruces, NM, a convening of cooperating ranchers, researchers, and education and extension team members.

The day began with a field tour during which participants were given a 21‐page packet (Supporting Information 1) with detailed information and examples of ways researchers and producers can measure indicators, produce benchmarks of desired condition, and apply them to various parts of a ranch system. Researchers presented indicators in terms of three sustainability domains: environmental, economic, and social. Those domains were subdivided into eight attributes: greenhouse gas emissions and biotic integrity (environmental domain); commodity quality, total factor productivity, water use efficiency, and stability of profits (economic domain); and social cohesion and human health (social domain). The stakeholders were generally familiar with the list of attributes and indicators, as it had been informed by previous informal discussions with them as part of ongoing efforts to develop LTAR's network‐wide indicator framework. Over the course of the day, overviews of long‐term projects were paired with general descriptions of indicators and descriptions of the benefits of “benchmarking,” a form of measuring system performance by comparing itself to desired outcomes set by the user (gleaned via, e.g., regional averages or past data collected on the ranch).

Generation of indicators

Directly following the tour, researchers facilitated a semi‐structured discussion to garner feedback from the group at an indoor meeting (Morgan, 1996). This process enabled a feasibility discussion to inform and strengthen the existing indicator framework by determining the overlap of indicators being collected and those of interest to stakeholder producers. During the meeting, producers were provided those eight attributes and their corresponding definitions and were asked which indicators from the provided list that they already measure—or were of interest to measure in the future. Meeting facilitators documented which indicator metrics were already being collected at each cooperating ranch, and which could be feasibly collected and were of interest. Researchers presented attributes to the group and solicited feedback from all stakeholders but focused on the ranch operators present in the room. Participating ranchers then identified existing, aspirational, and needed indicators and metrics at the ranch level. Stakeholder comments were recorded by researchers in an engagement log (Martinez et al., 2019).

Three of the five cooperating ranches were represented at the meeting by producers and ranch workers. The other ranches were represented to the degree that attending researchers and producers knew some previous information about their data collection protocols, which was recorded in the meeting and is represented in the data shown (i.e., secondary knowledge).

Archbold–University of Florida

ABS‐UF conducts long‐term research on grazing practices such as the use of prescribed fire, nutrient management, and plant species manipulation in native, seminative, and cultivated grazing lands in the Headwaters of the Everglades and the Peace River watershed (Boughton et al., 2024). Research at this LTAR site focuses on understanding the role of agriculture in relation to water management, biodiversity, and a changing climate. The site represents subtropical humid grassland and shrub landscapes in North America and globally. ABS‐UF formed an advisory council in the fall of 2022 (DeLong et al., 2024). The advisory council consists of a cross‐section of stakeholders who represent different areas of the cow‐calf production industry. We used a purposive sampling method to recruit participants according to subject matter expertise (Malterud et al., 2021). At the time of this research, the advisory council consisted of six members, including representatives from the University of Florida State Extension Service, the Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services, the Natural Resource Conservation Service, The Nature Conservancy, the Florida Cattlemen's Association, and producers. This research was approved by the University of Florida IRB (study number IRB202200184).

Generation of indicators

The ABS‐UF collaborative approach combined the expertise of multiple groups of stakeholders to strengthen research products and LTAR tools. This research project began involving stakeholders formally in 2022 with prior informal involvement dating back to 2018 when the development of the indicator framework began.

We presented the advisory council with information about the LTAR indicator framework including the purpose, the domains, and the attributes, but we intentionally withheld information about the existing indicators. Part one of the activity involved the advisory council members independently creating a list of indicators they believed fit each of the six attributes. We asked them to propose up to four indicators per attribute based on their expertise, only knowing attribute definitions (Table A1). We instructed the participants to consider the long‐term sustainability of an operation, tradeoffs in agricultural systems, logistical feasibility, measurability, and benchmarking when developing suggestions.

Next, advisory council members shared their ideas one at a time. We used the online virtual whiteboard software Miro to organize ideas, grouping them where applicable and noting multiple independent ideas. Once everyone shared their ideas, we discussed which indicators were the most applicable and why. The purpose was to allow expert perspectives without biasing responses based on the existing framework. Next, we shared the existing indicators with the advisory council and noted where the indicators overlapped (i.e., which indicators were identified by advisory council members that already existed in the framework) and where they differed.

Prioritization of sustainability indicators

There was a pool of indicators to consider following part one of the activity. To refine the list by priority, we used the dot voting method. Dot voting is a participatory decision‐making tool where participants select top priorities from a list of ideas with the goal of finding consensus among a group (Boaz et al., 2018; Bowles et al., 2016; Hidalgo, 2018; Mallakin et al., 2023). Each participant had four votes per attribute where they could specify which indicators were high priority based on their expert judgment. One weakness of dot voting is the bandwagon effect. If participants see one idea with many votes, it may sway their vote in that direction. To avoid this, each participant voted privately on an individual worksheet which the facilitator collated and shared once all votes were cast. Finally, the group discussed the qualities of the indicators with the most votes and why it was important for them to be included in the framework.

Reliability check

The methods used in this study at JER and ABS‐UF are outlined in Figure 3. The key differences are that at JER, there were only producers as participants rather than a cross‐section of experts; the JER held a one‐time event, whereas the ABS‐UF advisory council meets twice per year; and JER's approach relied on dialogue, whereas ABS‐UF included a voting and ranking component to come to consensus.

Side‐by‐side comparison of methods used at the Jornada Experimental Range (JER) and Archbold Biological Station–University of Florida (ABS‐UF).

Researchers from ABS‐UF and JER met after the respective engagement events to analyze the data collected and develop modified frameworks. We used a data analysis technique called a reliability check. The purpose of a reliability check is to ensure the data are being interpreted the same by multiple research team members—that the data are both valid and reliable (Drost, 2011; Leung, 2015; O'Connor & Joffe, 2020; Sadik, 2019). Two scientists with cross‐site representation met to come to a consensus on the ABS‐UF modified framework and three scientists met for the JER modified framework. At each reliability check, scientists reviewed indicators proposed by stakeholders to determine their suitability for the sustainability indicator framework. To do this, we compared proposed indicators to the attribute definitions located in Table A1. If an indicator was relevant to the corresponding attribute, we included it in the modified framework. As our assessment continued, we considered where proposed indicators could be merged, if applicable. In the event of a disagreement, the scientists provided their argument for or against indicator inclusion and reassessed their original decision. This continued until all scientists agreed and achieved consensus. We achieved consensus before continuing with an analysis comparing the modified sustainability indicator frameworks.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

JER engagement

Indicator generation by the SSB‐CAP resulted in a combined total of 27 responses at the rancher‐researcher meeting in May 2022 (Table 1). Despite a 4‐h workshop about the use of sustainability indicators, producers still only identified metrics of interest. We believe this is due in part to the nature of sustainability indicators; indicators consist of several metrics, which are the on‐the‐ground data and are inherently easier to discuss and identify at the farm or ranch scale. A primary reason is that producers are directly involved in collecting data in support of individual metrics such as cow weights, supply costs, or vegetation productivity.

Producers identified eight metric concepts associated with “production abundance and quality,” seven for “human health,” four for “financial stability,” five for “social cohesion,” and two for “land and water health” (Table 1). The disproportionate attention given to the “production abundance and quality” attribute is a testament to the focus producers place on their animals, which serve as their most important money‐generating asset. “Production” is largely missing or of minor importance in other well‐established sustainability indicator frameworks (e.g., Ahlering et al., 2021 and sources therein; Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, 2013). This outcome recently came to light and was represented in a new iteration (v.2024) of the LTAR Performance Indicator Framework, where “production” emerged as a fourth domain among existing environment, society and economic domains (Donovan et al., 2025); this serves as an excellent example of how stakeholder input from our semi‐structured facilitated discussion was reflected and implemented into the newest version of the LTAR framework.

Modified framework after reliability check

Five out of 27 responses (Table 1) could not be classified as outcome metrics nor indicators, because they more closely aligned with management strategies or ranch context: marketing strategies, automated sensor impacts, human resource requirements, cooperative vegetation management, and fulfillment to local food production needs. For instance, producers were interested in cattle or beef marketing strategies when discussing financial stability, but marketing strategies are not a measurable outcome of a management approach, though operations with different marketing strategies could result in different measures due to operational differences. During the reliability check, researchers categorized them as “operational characteristics.”

After cleaning the data of “operational characteristics” a total of 22 metrics were found to be already collected or of future interest to the participating producers (Table 1). We grouped metrics with a corresponding indicator to best represent the indicator level outcomes. This procedure was imperative to determine feasibility and pertinence of the indicators proposed in LTAR's indicators v.2022 with producer‐generated measurement goals. In other words, to provide cooperating producers with a usable sustainability indicator system, we needed to aggregate metric‐level data into a higher level framework for easier interpretability and utility. The result was 11 indicators in this modified, interpreted framework (Table 2).

In the JER case, producers did not identify any indicators initially outlined in the v.2022 framework by name. However, after researchers interpreted the results and matched metrics to corresponding indicators, 11 of 17 (65%) of v.2022 indicators were represented. In this way, the assessment confirmed the usability of the researcher generated indicator framework, wherein roughly two‐thirds of the indicators initially proposed were being collected or of interest to cooperating stakeholder producers (albeit most were being represented at the metric scale).

ABS‐UF engagement

The ABS‐UF advisory council facilitated discussion reflected a vested and continued interest in the integrity of ranching systems in Florida and beyond. Major discussion themes included the importance of focusing on all kinds of biodiversity in ranching systems, the persistence of ranches over time and ways to anticipate changes to relevant markets, and the desire to offer a workplace culture that supports employees on multiple fronts that permeates the community.

Indicator generation resulted in a combined total of 46 indicators, 34 brainstormed by the advisory council. There were an additional 12 indicators in the existing framework not initially identified by the advisory council (Table 3, indicator column marked by asterisk). Research scientists met to conduct a reliability check, interpret the results from the voting activity, and develop a cohesive framework that represented both stakeholder priorities and the integrity of the original framework architecture (Figure 2). This explains why, for example, biotic integrity remains an indicator in the interpreted framework. The combination of invasive species, wildlife biodiversity, and plant biodiversity reflects the overarching indicator of biotic integrity. The prioritization activity revealed three primary results. First, there were indicators in the existing framework that were prioritized over others. For example, the indicator “satisfaction” in the original framework (under the human health attribute) received zero votes. This result did not necessarily indicate “satisfaction” is of no importance to stakeholders, but that there were other, higher priority indicators they determined relevant in their specific context. Second, there were indicators in the existing framework recognized as a priority but were not initially identified by the advisory council. For example, the advisory council did not independently identify “worker safety” as a human health indicator but still voted for it as a high priority indicator. This demonstrates the advantage of multiple co‐disciplinary groups of experts contributing to a research project as research scientists can identify blind spots for stakeholders and vice versa.

Finally, there were indicators generated by the advisory council that were not in the original framework, such as “the public perception of ranching.” In Florida, this is a major concern among producers, service providers, and scientists alike, but public perception may not be as much (or as high) of a priority in other regions and contexts. One salient example is public support versus cattlemen's support of Florida panther (P.c. couguar) protection and recovery (Jacobs et al., 2015; Kreye et al., 2017). There is tension between ranchers whose lands are affected by Florida panther federal regulations and the public who value protecting an endangered species (Langin & Jacobson, 2012). Ranchers reported in a study by Kreye et al. (2017) that ranchers feel frustrated with the regulations they have to follow for a species they do not often see on their land. This impacts their business, which the public who overwhelmingly view Florida panther protection positively may not be aware of or sensitive toward (Jacobson et al., 2012).

Ultimately, this activity serves as evidence of the need for multiple expert opinions (i.e., coproduction of knowledge) to contribute to the science of indicator framework development. These outcomes also point to the need for local or region‐specific indicator inputs and demonstrate how a single large‐scale indicator framework will not be successfully applied nationally without iterative and coproduced modifications.

Comparing strategies and sites

We found several key similarities and differences at JER and ABS‐UF. When comparing the methodological approaches during analysis, the coproduction processes were different. We named these approaches confirmatory and exploratory to distinguish them. The primary difference appeared to be the level of structure and organization. JER's confirmatory approach focused on gaining feedback about an existing indicator framework developed by research scientists over several years of confirming the scientists’ indicator framework. ABS‐UF, however, presented the indicator framework at a more general level and invited participants to propose indicators to add to the framework, which is the primary feature of the exploratory approach.

Comparing site‐level frameworks, there were five overlapping indicators, eight indicators unique to ABS‐UF, and five indicators unique to the JER (Table 5). Several ABS‐UF indicators are context‐specific and not present in the original framework. A structured engagement strategy that is exploratory allows for more creative insight from stakeholders when they think beyond the bounds of a given framework. However, the strength of the confirmatory approach at the JER is that the indicators have specific metrics identified by stakeholders feasible for data collection. Alternatively, research can integrate exploring indicators with establishing feasible metrics in one engagement event, especially for those with limited time and resources.

Despite different approaches, some results were consistent. Different engagement strategies might reach similar outcomes—a stakeholder‐informed indicator framework. However, the structure of engagement should remain a key consideration. Access to resources, labor, time, and funds can affect the strategies available. The shared results in this study suggest there is flexibility in strategies a research team pursues, especially when stakeholder types are similar. For instance, both producer groups in this study involved cattle ranchers; if corn farmers and cattle ranchers had both been engaged, it is unknown if the versatility of the two engagement strategies would have persisted. Stakeholder group members should include diverse perspectives that lend relevant expertise to the subject matter. One 2019 study found that government‐led and bottom‐up engagement processes in establishing water policy should combine formal and informal approaches to find a middle ground that is flexible for regions and contexts (Hassenforder et al., 2019). Future work may consider opportunities and tradeoffs of engagement strategies between differing stakeholder types.

Based on results from stakeholders at both sites, we learned that the indicators and metrics named by scientists for the network‐level indicator framework have significant overlap with the final modified local frameworks (Table 5). All indicators from the JER were present in the network‐level framework, with six of 17 unnamed. At ABS‐UF, there were eight indicators that overlapped with the network‐level framework, six network‐level indicators that were not prioritized at the site‐level, and five new indicators prioritized for the site‐level framework.

At JER, we learned we need tight rules for what is and what is not an indicator to avoid conflation with metrics. The local stakeholder interest in production concepts reinforced adding a fourth domain for production in the network framework (see Donovan et al., 2025). There will always be contextual priorities to consider, but the network‐level framework is designed so that, ideally, the local priorities can be treated as “metrics” within one of the 24 indicator categories.

We encourage scientists to engage stakeholders in the way that best works for their research needs and resources. Engagement strategies should be adaptable to different conditions research teams face and available resources. Planned engagement and the incorporation of social science can add rigor to an interdisciplinary research project. Most agricultural research teams have undoubtedly worked with their stakeholders in some capacity during their research. Informal interactions with stakeholders are important for building relationships and trust between researchers and those they work with (Sumane et al., 2018). With the use of social sciences and planned stakeholder engagement strategies, researchers can incorporate stakeholder input in an intentional way that benefits the research design and end users of research. In this study, both approaches resulted in information that was only obtainable by working with stakeholders, highlighting the need for multiple groups of experts contributing to a usable indicator framework (Akpo et al., 2015; Djenontin & Meadow, 2018; Reed et al., 2014). The social science approach conducted at ABS‐UF has methodological advantages in terms of validity, reliability, and reproducibility of the research (Dumont et al., 2019; Perrone et al., 2023). However, this requires social science expertise. The technical advantages to the dialogue‐based approach at the JER include the focus on the application of the indicator framework in real‐world scenarios. But this approach may sacrifice methodological integrity in favor of practicality.

Long‐term indicator framework scalability and usability

Stakeholder engagement and the coproduction of knowledge can ensure that the sustainability indicator framework has longevity, scalability, and usability. Scientist and researcher expertise has contributed to several iterations of the framework to date (Donovan et al., 2025; Spiegal et al., 2022). However, the data in this study demonstrated that there are metrics that could be included in the indicator framework that end‐users prioritize. The emphasis on these metrics by stakeholders can help LTAR researchers as they support users in developing useful and usable benchmarks per indicator. Further, maintaining a framework that encourages common ground that is adaptable to different agroecosystem contexts is essential for its continued usability. Direct stakeholder engagement is one way to enable framework adaptations alongside scientific research and innovation progress. One example showcasing the need for framework adaptability is financial stability, identified as important by stakeholders in New Mexico and Florida, but with different regional meanings and drivers. One group emphasized marketing, whereas the other did not mention it at all. With widespread adoption of the LTAR indicator system and its benchmarks, LTAR can parse these regional trends.

Stakeholder participants at both JER and ABS‐UF tended toward identifying metrics rather than indicators, despite having definitions of what an indicator is and how it differs from a metric. We suspect that stakeholders whose daily responsibilities focus on data collection (for example, calf weights) will focus more on what is relevant to their direct work. On the other hand, those working with large groups or theoretical knowledge may contribute indicators with the understanding that they can be represented by many metrics. Both are important for developing context‐specific benchmarks that reflect high priority areas for monitoring sustainable agricultural performance. Metrics inform on‐the‐ground usable data that framework users may already be collecting. Participants who identify indicators may be thinking of groups of metrics and conceptualize them as indicators. Regardless, interpreting the metrics into indicators and the indicators into metrics is essential for developing a usable framework. This supports the use of a reliability check in the methodological approach wherein interpretation by a subset of the research team occurs.

Contributions to coproduction literature

The definition of the coproduction of knowledge has been debated for decades (Miller & Wyborn, 2020). One area of thought is whether or not coproduction is something that occurs to be observed or something that is prescribed as a methodology (Bandola‐Gill et al., 2023). While coproduction certainly occurs naturally, we believe that a social scientific approach where coproduction of knowledge is an outcome will help advance the field of rapidly changing agricultural science. This research supports the coproduction framework put forth by Djenontin and Meadow (2018) as a way to produce knowledge that has increased relevance to end users. The concept of coproduction is used in this paper as a way to prescribe the coproduction of knowledge rather than observe it occurring.

Our study also supports a format for coproduction of knowledge that is structured yet remains flexible. Arguments in the literature base currently debate two primary modes for using coproduction in a way that impacts policy (Adelle et al., 2019; Cooke et al., 2021). For example, a coproduction process that is institutionalized is at risk of becoming too rigid and failing to meet the collaborative nature of the coproduction of knowledge. Conversely, flexible coproduction that has no underlying structure can undermine the scientific rigor of the knowledge being produced. Both coproduction processes in this study had an underlying structure. At JER, the confirmatory approach was the more rigid example, where participants were not specifically invited to brainstorm new ideas. At ABS‐UF, the exploratory approach was iterative and intentionally incorporated new ideas from participants. We found that context was important in determining the main ideas discussed in each instance, which emphasizes the collaborative nature of coproduction of knowledge over an institutionalized prescription.

There is much scholarly debate regarding the value of knowledge. Traditionally, knowledge production has occurred within scientific laboratories using the scientific method. The emphasis is on reliable, valid, and replicable data. The introduction of nonscientific individuals into this process is contested as a detriment to the integrity of the data and research outcomes (Hernandez et al., 2022; Hodgson et al., 2019; Maderson, 2023). Conversely, inviting subject matter experts from diverse—yet relevant—areas has the opportunity to expand the expert knowledge base beyond the laboratory. Our study demonstrated on several occasions that inviting multiple groups of experts resulted in data that were more relevant to a wider group of people. That is, scientists working alone would have missed certain ideas if they had excluded stakeholders as members of the research team.

We found that different coproduction processes can still have similar outcomes and impacts (Table 6). Key differences highlight the need for a diversity of coproduction processes (such as methods and activities) to understand how different processes work under the same goal of coproducing a sustainability indicator framework. Methodologically speaking, the similarities and differences in outcomes do not appear to be the result of a confirmatory nor exploratory approach. Participants at JER still identified metrics and indicators outside the network‐level framework despite not being explicitly asked to do so like they were at ABS‐UF. However, there were more new ideas for metrics and indicators at ABS‐UF that were thoughtfully proposed based on attribute definitions. Despite this, we still argue that following established social scientific approaches supports a replicable, valid, and reliable methodology that research teams can apply across contexts. This argument does not preclude informal or less structured approaches from being valuable. However, qualitative data analysis and interpretation are necessary to interpret human subjects research. It is possible that when social science expertise is not accessible in the coproduction process as was the case at JER, that post hoc analysis can reveal useful and relevant results. Because of the uncertainties in such a situation, we still recommend designing the coproduction process with social scientists to ensure rigorous methodologies.

Assessing different coproduction processes at JER and ABS‐UF reveals the importance of considering the entire coproduction framework, in the case where certain indicators appear to differ based on context, such as climatic conditions, rather than inherent issues with the indicator framework itself. By identifying similarities and differences, we can identify how local priorities can be represented with a site‐level metric and indicator system while also considering similarities as themes occurring at a regional or network level.

Limitations

One major limitation of coproduction of knowledge that has gotten much scholarly attention is the representation of multiple world views and including diverse participant perspectives. In this study, neither JER nor ABS‐UF used purposive sampling to recruit participants who may typically not be included in agricultural science decision‐making. Indigenous knowledge systems are an example of another way of understanding human–environment interactions that is underdeveloped in the coproduction of knowledge literature base (Adelle et al., 2019; Utter et al., 2021). Even for studies including multiple ways of knowing, it is necessary to share decision‐making with stakeholders in the creation of scientific knowledge (Knapp et al., 2019). Future research activities should purposefully recruit underrepresented voices and design studies addressing ways to minimize inequalities among participants and scientific staff.

A primary methodological limitation for the study was the cross‐sectional design. The data analyzed in this study were collected at one point in time and represent a snapshot of the coproduction process. The coproduction of knowledge is typically a years‐long commitment to scientific collaboration that starts at research idea generation and is maintained throughout the entire scientific process through dissemination of findings (Cooke et al., 2021; Ligtermoet, 2025). In this study, we are only able to make limited conclusions regarding the activities and outputs of two cases of LTAR sites using coproduction to develop a modified indicator framework. At the time of this publication, LTAR sites are continuing to engage stakeholders to develop context‐specific indicator frameworks.

CONCLUSION

Looking at the whole perspective of understanding, contextualizing, reciprocating, and evaluating, engagement strategies should be part of a sustained engagement. In this research at the JER and ABS‐UF, we offered example strategies that strive to incorporate as many of these elements as possible. We hypothesize that engagement will strengthen science and ultimately make research results more applicable to end users and in the long‐term innovations more adoptable by end users such as scientists, producers, and agencies. This will be tested over time as the LTAR network incorporates coproduction into agricultural science to develop “useful, usable, and used” tools (Tsegaye et al., 2024). At the time of this publication, the EWG is developing a toolkit for engagement, providing resources and examples to accommodate a variety of skill levels and access to resources. The EWG also serves as a space where site‐level scientists can consult with engagement specialists and social scientists to design the coproduction process and analyze the data in a rigorous way.

The LTAR network has an opportunity to not only incorporate stakeholder engagement but also move beyond to inform the science of stakeholder engagement and contribute to a transformative collaborative agricultural science that was traditionally siloed (Boughton et al., 2021). In this paper, we presented two coproduction processes and how they contributed to the development of an indicator framework. The different engagement approaches utilized in this study served specific purposes, and had pros and cons, but using a variety of engagement strategies can help strengthen the indicator framework overall. The network‐wide and site‐level indicator frameworks will continue to evolve as scientists improve it with updated knowledge systems (as in Donovan et al. [2025] and Friedrichsen et al. [2025]). We demonstrated in this paper how to invite stakeholders to be part of those knowledge systems to ensure the framework meets the needs for a variety of users while also maintaining scientific integrity. Without stakeholder engagement, we would likely miss key indicators not considered by scientists and be less aware of the usability of the framework on‐the‐ground. We found that collaboration between scientists and stakeholders can help identify areas to improve the framework and make it more usable at a national and local level. Therefore, the LTAR sustainability performance indicator framework should continue to incorporate stakeholders at each step of the development process and across scales (e.g., sites and regions) to meet its overall goal of widespread use.
