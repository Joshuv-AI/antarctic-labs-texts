Phillygenin Improves the Focal Adhesion Kinase/Glycogen Synthase Kinase 3β/β‐Catenin Axis to Promote Mesenchymal Stem Cell Osteogenic Differentiation
Authors: Yuchao Yang, Haidong Zhou, Yining Wang, Ming Yin, Fengying Gong, Shutong Wu, Zhiyin Luo, Zhenyu Bi, Jiahou Xu, Yongfu Chen, Konghe Hu, Jun Ouyang, Jiajun Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Forsythiae Fructus, a traditional herb known for its anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant properties, has demonstrated inconclusive effects on bone metabolism in prior studies. The aim of this study is to investigate whether phillygenin (PHI), an active component of Forsythiae Fructus, promotes osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) by modulating the FAK/GSK‐3β/β‐catenin signaling axis. Experimental procedure: In vivo, an ovariectomized (OVX) mouse model was employed to mimic postmenopausal bone loss. In vitro, osteoclastogenesis was evaluated using tartarate‐resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) enzymatic assays, while osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs was assessed through alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and alizarin red S (ARS) staining. Cell proliferation and viability were examined using the CCK‐8 assay. Osteogenic markers, including ALP, RUNX2, osteopontin (OPN), as well as signaling proteins, such as phosphorylated FAK at Y397 (pFAKY397), phosphorylated glycogen synthase kinase‐3 beta (pGSK‐3β), and β‐catenin, were analyzed by western blot and immunofluorescence analyses. Specific inhibitors of the FAK and β‐catenin (Y15 and XAV‐939) were employed for mechanistic validation. PHI treatment significantly decreased the number and size of osteoclasts in a dose‐dependent way. In OVX mice, PHI preserved trabecular microarchitecture, increased bone mineral density, and inhibited osteoclast formation dose‐dependently. Low doses of PHI significantly promoted osteogenic differentiation and mineralization of BMSCs without affecting cell proliferation and viability. Mechanistically, PHI upregulated the expression of pFAKY397, pGSK‐3β, and β‐catenin. Inhibition of the pathway, achieved through the use of Y15 and XAV‐939, attenuated PHI‐induced osteogenesis. These findings indicate that PHI enhances BMSCs osteogenesis via the FAK/GSK‐3β/β‐catenin axis, thereby restoring bone metabolic balance. The results highlight PHI's dual regulatory potential in the management of bone disorders, particularly, estrogen deficiency‐related osteoporosis, and suggest its potential applications in the therapeutic intervention of bone diseases.Keywords: β‐Catenin, bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), focal adhesion kinase (FAK), osteogenesis, osteoporosis, phillygenin (PHI)

Introduction

Osteoporosis, a condition marked by fragile and fracture‐prone bones, results from an imbalance in bone remodeling where osteoclastic resorption exceeds osteoblastic formation (Fonseca et al. 2014). Bone resorption, which is mediated by osteoclasts, is critically dependent on macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (M‐CSF), which is essential for both the survival and proliferation of osteoclasts (Frech et al. 2021). Additionally, receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa‐B ligand (RANKL) plays a pivotal role in the differentiation and activation of osteoclasts (McDonald et al. 2021). Although current therapeutic strategies predominantly focus on antiresorptive agents (e.g., bisphosphonates) (Li et al. 2024) and lifestyle modifications (Guadalupe‐Grau et al. 2009), these methods exhibit limitations in long‐term efficacy. Recent research has underscored the potential of natural phytonutrients as promising alternatives, particularly, lignans derived from Forsythiae Fructus, which may serve as substitutes for reducing the risk of postmenopausal and age‐related bone loss (Hwang et al. 2019). Notably, its bioactive constituent phillygenin has demonstrated potential in preventing osteoporosis (Zhang et al. 2024), although its role in bone regeneration remains underexplored.

Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) possess the capacity to differentiate into osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and myocytes. These cell types are valuable in osteoporosis treatment due to their immunomodulatory and self‐renewal properties (Papadopoulou et al. 2012; Uccelli et al. 2008). Stimulating the recruitment of stem cells to the microenvironment surrounding fracture defects or osteoporotic areas is crucial for promoting osteogenesis and supporting new bone reconstruction (Kangari et al. 2020). Crucially, the osteogenic production of new bone is facilitated by focal adhesion kinase (FAK) (He et al. 2025). FAK phosphorylation at Tyr397 facilitates osteoblast adhesion and RUNX2 activation (Xu et al. 2012), while Wnt/β‐catenin signaling enhances BMSCs osteogenesis and suppresses adipogenesis (Liu et al. 2022). Chinese herbal medicines contain bioactive compounds that can accelerate the differentiation of proliferating stem cells. Targeting these pathways offers therapeutic potential, but natural modulators such as PHI require further mechanistic investigation.

Using a validated virtual screening model (Liu et al. 2021), we identified PHI from Forsythia suspensa and evaluated its dual role in inhibiting osteoclastogenesis and promoting BMSCs osteogenesis. Furthermore, we elucidated the underlying mechanism mediated by the FAK/GSK‐3β/β‐catenin axis to evaluate the influence of PHI on bone metabolic balance. These findings provide new insights into phytochemical‐based bone regeneration strategies.

Materials and Methods

Virtuals Screening

A deep learning based virtual screening protocol published previously (Liu et al. 2021) was used for virtual screening against SPECS natural products. A pretrained self‐attentive message passing neural network (P‐SAMPNN) was established and fine‐tuned using the anti‐osteoclastogenesis dataset. The SPECS natural product library (www.specs.net) contains 792 purchasable natural products and was converted into smiles formats. The model predicted the anti‐osteoclastogenesis probability using smiles of the natural products. Furthermore, requirements considered to be relevant in recent guidelines for best practice in natural products pharmacological research have been taken into account (Heinrich et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2024).

Reagents

Phillygenin, with a purity of ≥ 98% as identified by high performance liquid chromatography, was purchased from Weikeqi Biological Technology Co. Ltd. (Chengdu, Sichuan, China). The chemical structure and molecular formula of PHI are shown in Figure 1A. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma, St Louis, Missouri, USA) was used to dissolve PHI, and the resulting solution was kept at −20°C to avoid light exposure. Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA) was the supplier of phalloidin. FAK scaffolding inhibitor Y15 (2 μM) and XAV‐939 (10 μM) were obtained from Selleck Chemicals (Houston, TX, USA) as inhibitors of the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway. Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA) provided Runt‐Related Transcription Factor 2 (RUNX2), Phospho‐FAK (Tyr397) (pFAKY397), Glycogen Synthase Kinase 3β (GSK‐3β), Phospho‐Glycogen Synthase Kinase 3β (pGSK‐3β), and β‐catenin antibody. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and osteopontin (OPN) were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Santa Cruz (Dallas, TX, USA) supplied FAK antibody.

Osteoclastogenesis Assay In Vitro

We used a previously described protocol to perform an osteoclastogenesis experiment (Lin et al. 2022). Six‐week‐old C57BL/6J mice (male, 12–15 g) were purchased from Southern Medical University and used for obtaining bone marrow‐derived macrophages (BMMs); cells were seeded overnight with M‐CSF (10 ng/mL) in 96‐well plates at 6 × 103 cell/well. Then, cells were treated with complete medium containing M‐CSF (10 ng/mL, P5313, Beyotime Biotech Inc., Shanghai, China) and glutathione‐S transferase (GST)‐RANKL (100 ng/mL) with PHI at different concentrations (0–33 μM). The cell culture medium was refreshed every third day until osteoclast formation. Using a leucocyte acid phosphatase staining kit, cells were fixed for 20 min with 3.7% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Beijing Solarbio science and technology co., ltd, Beijing, China) and stained for tartarate‐resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) activity (Beyotime Biotech Inc., Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. TRAP‐positive multinucleated cells (> 3 nuclei) were counted as osteoclast‐like (OCL) cells.

Ovariectomized Mouse Model

A previously described procedure was used to extract the ovaries from C57/BL6J mice (female, 8–12 weeks, 20–25 g) (Helfrich and Ralston 2012) and were treated daily with purified water or drugs through gavage for 3 months, during which no vomiting was observed. Food and water were freely available to the animals. All protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Southern Medical University. For the ovarian surgery, anesthesia (2% pentobarbital sodium, 45 mg/kg) was administered before a midline dorsal incision was made using small scissors. A blunt puncture was then used to gently release the connective tissue from the underlying muscle on both sides. One ovary was gently severed from the oviduct under the thin muscle layer of the abdominal cavity. The fat pad and remaining part of tissue were then replaced and the same process was done on the other side. After that, the skin incision and the layer of muscle were sutured. Alendronate (Ma et al. 2018) and phillygenin (Hu et al. 2020) were selected as a positive control and experimental study based on the well‐established efficacy in suppressing osteoclast activity and preventing bone loss in OVX mouse models. For OVX mice, they were randomly assigned to either the sham group, OVX with purified water as a negative control, OVX with alendronate sodium (ALE, 1 mg/kg/day) as a positive control, OVX with high (40 mg/kg/day, drug concentration of 4 mg/mL, PHI‐H) or low (10 mg/kg/day, drug concentration of 1 mg/mL, PHI‐L) concentration of PHI as an experimental study group using a randomization list to minimize selection bias (n = 6). Organs, tissues, and femurs were obtained for subsequent analyses after euthanizing the mice. Investigators performing outcome assessments (e.g., micro‐computed tomography [micro‐CT] analysis, histological scoring) were blinded to group allocation during data collection and analysis to ensure objectivity.

Radiographic Analysis

Animals were euthanized after 3 months of gavage with a PHI, ALE, or purified water solution. The posterior limbs of the mice were obtained and preserved in 3.7% PFA for 48 h at room temperature. The distal femur was scanned for bone formation using a micro‐CT imaging system (ZKKS‐MCT‐Sharp, Zhongke Kaisheng Medical Technology, Guangzhou, China) with the following scan parameters: the scanning voltage was set to 70 kV, power to 7 W, stacking of four frames, angle gain 0.72° with an exposure time of 100 ms, and rotating one circle to complete the scanning. ZKKS‐Scan software was utilized to reconstruct the scanned images and examine the trabecular bone characteristics (ZKKS‐MicroCT 4.1). Bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume‐to‐tissue volume ratio (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), connection density (Conn.Dn), and structural model index (SMI) were determined.

Biomechanical Analysis of Mouse Femur

The mice femurs were obtained after 3 months, and an ElectroForce 3510 Bose instrument (TA instruments, DE, USA) was used to conduct a biomechanical test on the mice femurs, and a stress–strain curve was obtained. The loading speed of the biomechanical instrument was set at 3 mm/min, and the maximum load on the regeneration tissue was defined according to the stress–strain curve of the vertices.

Histological Evaluation

All samples were decalcified using formic acid decalcification solution (Beijing Solarbio Science and Technology Co. Ltd., Beijing, China) after micro‐CT, changing the solution every 3 days for 2 weeks. When each bone sample became soft enough to be penetrated by a needle and was homogeneous and transparent, the samples were dehydrated, cleared, waxed, embedded, and sliced. Using a microscope, pictures were taken following dewaxing, rehydrating, and hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining (Olympus BX51, Tokyo, Japan). All assessments were executed in a triple‐blind way.

Cell Isolation and Culture

Human BMSCs (hBMSCs) were purchased from ScienCell (San Diego, CA, USA) and murine BMSCs (mBMSCs) were isolated from femoral bones of 4‐week‐old C57BL/6J mice (male, 8–10 g). Adherent hBMSCs and mBMSCs were then cultured with high‐sugar Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (H‐DMEM; Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) consisting of 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco), and 0.2% plasmocin prophylactic (InvivoGen, San Diego, CA, USA) in an incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. The following investigations were conducted using cells from Passages 3–7.

Simultaneously, the femur and tibia of C57BL/6J mice were rinsed with a syringe to isolate primary BMMs, which were then cultured in α‐minimum essential medium (α‐MEM) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin for 24 h, and 5 ng/mL M‐CSF was added. Cells in the supernatant were then collected as primary BMMs and seeded to induce osteoclast differentiation.

Cell Proliferation and Viability Assay

The influence of PHI on cell proliferation and viability was tested in 96‐well plates with 5000 cells per well (12 h). To measure the proliferation of BMMs and hBMSCs, the cells were then exposed to different doses of PHI for 2 days. To measure the viability of BMMs and hBMSCs, the cells were treated with PHI and dexamethasone (DXM, 1 mM, Beijing Solarbio Science and Technology Co. Ltd., Beijing, China) for 2 days.

The medium was changed to 100 μL DMEM without FBS (Gibco) containing 10% CCK‐8 (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) for 4 h at 37°C. The absorbance of the solution at 450 nm (A450) was assessed using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, Multiskan Go, Danvers, MA, USA) to evaluate the number of live cells.

ALP and ARS Staining

To examine early mineralization, hBMSCs were stimulated in PHI solution at various concentrations without any osteogenic differentiation reagents in six‐well plates. An ALP color development kit including 3‐bromo‐4‐chloro‐3‐indolyl phosphate (BCIP)/nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) was used for ALP staining (Beyotime Biotech Inc.). Under an optical microscope, the positive area of ALP was evaluated in three randomly selected fields using Image J software (Olympus BX51, Tokyo, Japan).

The late mineralization was evaluated by ARS staining (Cyagen Biosciences, Suzhou, China). hBMSCs were plated into six‐well plates, and PHI was added in different concentrations for culture. After 3 weeks, the hBMSCs were fixed in 3.7% PFA at room temperature for 20 min and washed three times with double distilled water (ddH2O). Finally, after 30 min of staining with ARS solution, the cells were washed with ddH2O. An optical microscope (Olympus BX51) was used to capture the staining on three randomly selected fields of the ARS‐positive area, and the staining area was evaluated using Image J software.

Western Blotting Analysis

The BMSCs (hBMSCs and mBMSCs) exposed to different treatments were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) solution that contained protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime Biotech Inc., Shanghai, China). Following protein separation using 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE), the resultant protein samples were placed onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Then they were first incubated in 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation at 4°C overnight with antibodies specific to glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (1:10,000, Bioworld, MN, USA), RUNX2 (1:1000, Rabbit IgG, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), OPN (1:1000, Rabbit IgG, Abcam), FAK (1:200, Mouse IgG, Santa Cruz), pFAKY397 (1:1000, Rabbit IgG, CST), GSK‐3β (1:1000, Rabbit IgG, CST), pGSK‐3β (1:1000, Rabbit IgG, CST), and β‐catenin (1:1000, Rabbit IgG, CST). After three washes (10 min per wash) in Tris‐buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST), the PVDF membranes were treated with horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. After three further TBST washes, enhanced chemiluminescence blotting reagents (FUDE Biological Technology, Hangzhou, China) were used to detect proteins. A Tanon‐5500 chemiluminescence detection system (Tanon Science & Technology Ltd., Shanghai, China) was used to measure the signal intensity.

Immunofluorescence Assay

Cells were cultured on microscope cover glass in 24‐well plates with PHI solution. mBMSCs and hBMSCs were washed three times in DMEM after 14 and 21 days of PHI treatment, fixed in 3.7% PFA for 10 min at room temperature, and then treated with 0.1% Triton X‐100 for 10 min to increase membrane permeability, followed by a 30 min incubation with 2% bovine serum albumin to block non‐specific effects. The cells were then washed and incubated overnight with anti‐RUNX2 (1:500), anti‐β‐catenin (1:500), anti‐FAK (1:200), or anti‐pFAKY397 (1:500) antibodies and phalloidin (excitation/emission: 495/518 nm). The cells were further stained with a fluorescently tagged secondary antibody (1:200, Beyotime Biotech Inc) for 1 h after being washed twice in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), and 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI, 1:500) at room temperature for 30 min. After three PBS washes, cells were examined under a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 880, Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

Tissue Immunohistochemical and Immunofluorescence Staining

Regarding immunohistochemical staining, the sections were incubated with the sodium citrate buffer (Boster) for antigen repair. The sections were then washed with pure water and incubated with endogenous peroxidase blockers for 20 min. Afterwards, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with the Cathepsin K antibodies (CTSK, Rabbit, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). After washing thrice with PBS for 5 min each, they were incubated with the secondary antibody (Goat Anti‐Mouse and Rabbit HRP, ORIGENE, Rockville, MD, USA) at 37°C for 1 h. The sections were washed with PBS, counterstained with hematoxylin, and sealed with neutral gum. The positive staining areas of the two groups were observed using an optical microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

For tissue immunofluorescence staining, the formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded tissue sections were dewaxed and rehydrated before heat‐induced antigen retrieval was performed at a pH of 9.5 (i.e., 0.1 M Tris/HCL + 5% Urea, pH 9.5). The tissue sections were prerinsed in PBS for 30 min, and then the tissue sections were incubated with the CTSK antibody at 4°C overnight. The tissue sections were washed twice for 30 min in TBST, then incubated with the blocking buffer for 30 min. Afterwards, the tissue sections were incubated with a fluorophore‐conjugated secondary antibody for 90 min in the blocking buffer and then washed once for 30 min. The tissue section was incubated with Hoechst stain for 30 min and then washed in the buffer. The tissue section was also washed once for 30 min. Coverslips were placed over the tissue sections with the appropriate water‐based mounting media.

Statistical Analysis

Three separate trials were carried out at least. Results are shown as means ± SEM (standard error of the mean) and the means were compared between groups using Student's t test. p < 0.05 was designated as the statistical significance threshold (GraphPad Prism 8, San Diego, California).

Results

Virtual Screening

As previously published that a deep learning‐based virtual screening protocol was used for virtual screening against SPECS natural products (Liu et al. 2021). Phillygenin was predicted as an active compound for antiosteoclastogenesis. Then PHI was selected as a screening hit and purchased to validate the anti‐osteoclastogenesis activity (Figure 1A).

PHI Significantly Inhibits RANKL‐Induced Osteoclastogenesis

BMMs were cultured in the presence or absence of different concentrations of PHI containing 10 ng/mL M‐CSF and 100 ng/mL RANKL to investigate the potential function of PHI in RANKL‐induced osteoclast formation. After 5 days of induction, TRAP staining revealed that PHI effectively attenuated RANKL‐induced osteoclastogenesis efficiently with no cytotoxic effects. PHI treatment markedly reduced the multinucleated osteoclast formation as well as the quantity and size of osteoclasts (Figure 1B). OCL cells were defined as multinucleated TRAP‐positive cells with more than three nuclei (Figure 1C,D). To determine how PHI affected the proliferation and viability of BMMs, a CCK‐8 assay was performed for 2 days. PHI did not significantly affect the growth of the BMMs (Figure 3E). There was no appreciable difference between the PHI‐treated group and the 0 μM PHI group. However, there was a discernible decrease in the number of cells in the DXM‐treated group (Figure 3F). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that PHI had no impact on the proliferation and viability of BMMs.

PHI has Beneficial Effects on Bone Loss in Vivo

To assess the impact of PHI on preventing osteoporosis in vivo using the OVX‐induced bone loss mouse model. Using H&E staining, we first evaluated the effect of PHI gavage treatment of osteoporosis on liver and kidney toxicity (Figure S1). No structural morphological alterations were observed, indicating the biological safety of PHI. The trabecular bone in the femur was then observed for deterioration or weakening using 3D‐micro‐CT imaging. The OVX group showed a significantly deteriorated trabecular bone structure compared to the sham group. However, after PHI‐H and PHI‐L gavage in the OVX‐induced model of bone loss, there was a reduction in the degradation of femoral trabecular bone compared with the OVX group. The trabecular bone was better preserved by lower PHI concentrations (1 mg/mL) than by higher PHI concentrations (4 mg/mL). Under the same experimental conditions, the ALE group (drug concentration of 0.1 mg/mL) demonstrated a protective effect on trabecular bone microarchitecture, similar to PHI (Figure 2A).

We used 3D‐micro‐CT to analyze the microstructural index parameters of the OVX‐induced bone loss model, as indicated in Figure 2B. BMD in the OVX group was lower in comparison to the sham group. PHI‐L dramatically improved the BMD, BV/TV, Tb.N, Tb.Th, and Conn.Dn of the femoral trabecular bone in mice compared with the OVX group (p < 0.05). Regarding the Tb.Sp and SMI, the values in the PHI‐L group were lower than those in the OVX group, whereas the decreases observed in the PHI‐H group were minimal (Figure 2B). Cortical bone thickness, observed via micro‐CT cross‐section, in the PHI and ALE groups considerably improved when compared to the OVX group but decreased in comparison to the sham group (Figure S2A,B). Biomechanical tests were conducted on the femurs of mice in each group using the ElectroForce 3510 Bose system. While the maximum load did not fully recover to the level of the sham group, PHI substantially mitigated the loss of femur mechanical properties caused by osteoporosis compared with the OVX group (Figure 2C). These findings demonstrated that in the OVX‐induced bone loss mouse model, which simulates estrogen shortage, PHI‐L therapy enhanced bone characteristics and prevented degradation of the trabecular microarchitecture.

Histological analysis of H&E staining in the osteoporosis model revealed that the changes in bone trabeculae of OVX mice models in each group were consistent with micro‐CT imaging results; the PHI‐L group had beneficial effects in preventing bone trabecular degeneration (Figure 2D). Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining show that the PHI‐L group inhibits CTSK expression compared with the OVX group (Figure 2E,F). This finding may be related to the promotion of osteogenesis by PHI.

Effect of PHI on Proliferation and Viability of hBMSCs

To determine how PHI affected proliferation and viability of hBMSCs, a CCK‐8 assay was performed. Cells were cultured in hBMSCs growth media containing different concentrations of PHI (0, 1, 10, or 20 μM) for 2 days. PHI did not significantly affect growth of the hBMSCs (Figure 3A). To assess the viability of hBMSCs treated with PHI, the cells were cultured for 2 days in hBMSCs basal medium containing 1% FBS and various concentrations of PHI (0, 1, 10, or 20 μM) and DXM (1 mM). There was no appreciable difference between the PHI‐treated group and the 0 μM PHI group. However, there was a discernible decrease in the number of cells in the DXM‐treated group (Figure 3B). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that PHI had no impact on the proliferation and viability of hBMSCs.

PHI Enhances Calcium Deposition and Increases Expression of Osteogenic‐Related Proteins

ALP and ARS staining were used to determine whether PHI affected osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs. In addition, immunofluorescence staining and western blotting were employed to measure the levels of osteogenic‐specific proteins.

To investigate the impact of PHI on the early stages of mineralization during osteogenesis, ALP staining was utilized. ALP expression peaked at 1 μM PHI concentration as opposed to higher PHI concentrations (10 and 20 μM). This was observed under a microscope and with the unaided eye (Figure 3C). We determined calcium deposition and mineralized node formation at the late stage of the bone mineralization process using ARS staining. As with ALP staining, ARS staining showed that PHI enhanced calcium deposition and mineralization after 3 weeks (Figure 3D).

The expression of ALP, RUNX2), and OPN exhibited striking increases in expression following 3 weeks of PHI treatment. The expression of ALP, RUNX2, and OPN did not change as PHI concentration increased, which was congruent with the pattern observed in the staining data (Figure 3E). Indeed, 1 μM was identified as the optimum concentration of PHI for improving the expression of osteogenesis‐related proteins. The difference in protein expression levels was found to be statistically significant upon semi‐quantitative analysis (p < 0.05). Therefore, based on the aforementioned results, 1 μM of PHI was used in subsequent experiments studying the underlying mechanism of PHI in promoting osteogenic differentiation. With this concentration, western blotting (Figures 3F and S3A) and immunofluorescence analyses (Figure 3G and S3B) revealed that ALP, RUNX2, and OPN expression were considerably greater than the control group.

PHI Stimulates BMSCs Osteogenesis Partially Through the FAK/GSK‐3β/β‐Catenin Axis

FAK is a significant upstream signal that controls osteogenic differentiation pathways, regulates the function of early osteoblast lineages, and increases GSK‐3β activity to prevent β‐catenin degradation. In this study, western blotting revealed that PHI (1 μM) increased the expression of total β‐catenin and phosphorylated GSK‐3β in BMSCs and activated phosphorylated FAK (pFAKY397) signaling (Figure 4A). In accordance with these findings, immunofluorescence analyses revealed that there was a significant up‐regulation of β‐catenin intracellular expression (Figures 4B and S3C) and phosphorylated FAK and phosphorylated GSK‐3β (pGSK‐3β) expression significantly higher in comparison to the control group (Figure 4D,F). However, there was no discernible change in FAK and GSK‐3β following PHI therapy (Figure 4C,E). These observations suggest that the capacity of PHI to stimulate the BMSCs osteogenesis is influenced by the FAK/GSK‐3β/β‐catenin signaling axis.

PHI Promotion of Osteogenesis Is Partially Inhibited by Y15 and XAV‐939

Next, we examined the role of FAK phosphorylation and β‐catenin in the FAK/GSK‐3β/β‐catenin signaling axis on PHI promotion of osteogenic differentiation using the inhibitors Y15 and XAV‐939. Based on hBMSCs (Figures 5 and 6) and mBMSCs (Figures S4 and S5) treated with varying concentrations of Y15 and XAV‐939, we selected the optimum inhibitory concentrations of the two compounds (Figures 5A and 6A and S4A and S5A). We found that when the small molecule inhibitor Y15 inhibited the phosphorylation of FAK (Figures 5C,D and S4C,D), the activation state of the downstream molecule GSK‐3β was inhibited (Figures 5E,F and S4E,F), and the expression of the osteogenic marker molecules ALP and RUNX2 was reduced. However, PHI could partially reverse the weakened osteogenic differentiation ability caused by the pathway inhibition of Y15 (Figures 5B and S4B). On the other hand, western blotting showed that the expression of RUNX2, ALP, and β‐catenin in PHI (1 μM)‐induced hBMSCs and mBMSCs was significantly reduced after XAV‐939 treatment (Figures 6B and S5B). According to immunofluorescence analysis, XAV‐939 suppressed the nuclear translocation of RUNX2 and the level of β‐catenin in hBMSCs and mBMSCs. Meanwhile, PHI partially counteracted the suppressive effect of XAV‐939 on the expression of osteogenic proteins (Figurse 6C,D and S5C,D).

Discussion

Overactivation of osteoclasts is the main cause of clinical osteolytic diseases such as osteoporosis (Tolar et al. 2004). Over 200 million people globally experience osteoporosis, and over one‐third of postmenopausal women suffer from osteoporotic fractures after the age of 50 (Cosman et al. 2014; Willers et al. 2022). Consequently, inhibiting osteoclast development and function is a crucial strategy for treating osteolytic diseases (Bi et al. 2017; Drake et al. 2008). Current medication management practices predominantly comprise pharmaceuticals that inhibit osteoclast activity and anti‐resorptive medications, which do not stimulate new bone regeneration. Therefore, there is an urgent need for new, well‐tolerated oral medications that inhibit osteoclast formation and promote bone formation.

In this study, the natural active ingredient, PHI, exhibited good anti‐osteoclastogenic activity in vitro and in vivo and an excellent effect on promoting osteogenesis in vitro. The inhibitory activity curve demonstrated that PHI treatment significantly repressed RANKL‐induced osteoclastic activity in a dose‐dependent way (Figure 1).

Senile osteoporosis is an age‐related osteoporosis resulting from an imbalance in bone turnover, especially in postmenopausal elderly women, when bone resorption preponderates over bone formation (Wu et al. 2020). The OVX model was utilized to determine the influence of PHI on osteoporosis in vivo (Figures 2A and S2). This study, for the first time, elucidates that PHI exhibits a unique “bidirectional regulatory” function by modulating the FAK/GSK3β/β‐catenin signaling pathway. This modulation inhibits osteoclast formation and promotes the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. In contrast, alendronate sodium, a representative bisphosphonate, primarily interferes with osteoclast function by inhibiting farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase (FPPS), thereby reducing bone resorption. However, its direct promoting effect on osteoblasts remains unclear (Russell 2011). A distinctive advantage of PHI is its ability to simultaneously enhance bone formation; low concentrations of PHI (1 μM) have been shown to increase ALP activity, an effect not observed with alendronate in similar experiments. This difference suggests that PHI may be more suitable for pathologic conditions requiring simultaneous reversal of both excessive bone resorption and insufficient bone formation, such as late postmenopausal osteoporosis. Compared to PHI‐H, PHI‐L had a superior preventative effect on osteoporosis in mice. The same phenomenon was seen in subsequent cell experiments. Congruent with these observations, studies have shown that higher pharmacological dosages have been related to an increased risk of adverse effects and a greater susceptibility to allergic reactions, so higher doses are not necessarily more effective than lower doses (Dimmitt and Stampfer 2009). The possibility that low‐dose drugs may improve the prognosis of chronic diseases has been reported in different fields, and there is increasing evidence that chronic diseases can be effectively treated with low‐dose drugs, reducing the risk of adverse effects. Women taking higher doses of atorvastatin had statistically lower BMD compared with women taking lower doses of the drug (Antonenko et al. 2022). In postmenopausal women with sufficient vitamin D and reduced bone mass, treatment with 6500 IU of vitamin D per day for a year was not superior to 800 IU of vitamin D and was less effective in reducing bone turnover (Grimnes et al. 2012). Therefore, we believe that PHI also has a low‐dose therapeutic advantage in the treatment of osteoporosis.

Based on the obtained results, we speculated whether PHI also promoted new bone formation while inhibiting osteoporosis. Therefore, we explored the effect of PHI on hBMSCs proliferation and osteogenesis. In the concentration range of 1–20 μM, PHI did not promote or inhibit hBMSCs proliferation in vitro. Moreover, these concentrations of PHI did not affect the viability of the hBMSCs, confirming the safety of PHI in this concentration range (Figure 3A,B). By western blotting and immunofluorescence, we observed that protein levels of RUNX2 and ALP were significantly increased after PHI treatment, respectively (Figures 3F,G and S3A,B). Other osteo‐related proteins, such as OPN, were also meaningfully increased after PHI treatment (Figure 3F). We further examined the influence of PHI on early and late mineralization through ALP and ARS staining and found that PHI improved hBMSCs mineralization in a dose‐independent manner (Figure 3C–E). Therefore, PHI significantly stimulated hBMSCs osteogenesis and improved mineralization. Our in vivo experiments showed the biosafety profile of PHI against hepatorenal toxicity (Figure S1) and the functional repression of osteoclast development and bone resorption (Figures 2 and S2). Osteoblasts and osteoclasts determine the balance of bone homeostasis (Al‐Bari and Al 2020; Kim et al. 2020). Our research demonstrates that PHI has dual advantageous roles in balancing bone homeostasis. Meanwhile, we provide Supporting Information about the pharmacological effect of PHI in the orthopedics field. PHI may have the potential to be both antiosteoclastic and encourage osteogenic differentiation.

The main signaling pathways involved in osteogenic differentiation include the FAK, GSK‐3, Wnt/β‐catenin, phosphoinositide 3‐kinase‐protein kinase B (PI3K‐Akt), MAPK/ERK, and NF‐κB signaling pathways. Our findings showed significant changes in phosphorylated FAK, phosphorylated GSK‐3β, and total β‐catenin protein following PHI treatment, indicating that a signaling axis of FAK/GSK‐3β/β‐catenin may be vital in the promotion of osteogenesis by PHI. Increased expression levels of phosphorylated FAK, phosphorylated GSK‐3β, and total β‐catenin were detected during treatment after PHI osteogenesis in vitro (Figure 4A). FAK is an intracellular non‐receptor tyrosine kinase that plays a key role in mediating integrin‐mediated signal transduction. FAK has been shown in previous studies to stimulate ADSCs osteogenesis through a Wnt/β‐catenin signal (Yuan et al. 2018). Phosphorylated FAK promotes the expression of downstream signaling mediators, such as increasing the expression of phosphorylated GSK‐3 and β‐catenin level. GSK‐3 is a serine/threonine kinase that has a central role in regulating the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway (Huang et al. 2017). Our results showed that Y15 reduced osteogenesis by inhibiting the phosphorylation of FAK, which may be related to the decreased β‐catenin expression caused by the decrease in phosphorylation of GSK‐3β (Figure 5B). While Y15 partially attenuated PHI‐induced FAK(Y397) phosphorylation, the incomplete suppression suggests that PHI may engage additional upstream regulators (e.g., Src kinase) to sustain FAK activation. This compensatory mechanism underscores the complexity of PHI's pro‐osteogenic signaling and warrants further investigation into its multitarget nature. Wnt proteins are crucial in modulating therapeutic progression and promoting osteoblast properties (Hill et al. 2005; Houschyar et al. 2019). Historically, the β‐catenin signaling pathway was divided into the canonical‐dependent and noncanonical‐independent pathway according to the specified Wnt–FZD receptor and co‐receptor which trigger various diverse intracellular signaling cascades (Logan and Nusse 2004). The canonical Wnt pathway is required for the maintenance and translocation of β‐catenin into the nucleus, which is determined through this pathway. β‐catenin moves into the nucleus while Wnt binds to FZD receptors and their co‐receptors. Consequently, β‐catenin triggers the transcription of osteo‐related genes, such as RUNX2 and Osx (Liu et al. 2008). In addition, osteoclastogenesis inhibitory factor (OPG) contributes to bone restoration induced by canonical Wnt signaling to inhibit osteoclastogenesis (Koide et al. 2017; Kramer et al. 2010). However, as seen in our immunofluorescence results (Figures 4B and S3C), β‐catenin protein in PHI‐treated cells largely accumulated in the cytoplasm and around the nucleus, with no evidence of the nuclear translocation expected in the canonical β‐catenin‐dependent pathway. Wnt likely binds to the FZD receptor complex and may result in the phosphorylation and recruitment of the Lrp co‐receptor, as well as GSK‐3β and axin binding to the ligand–receptor complex. Subsequently, GSK‐3β is endocytosed and inhibited through isolation into polyvesicular endocytosis, leading to β‐catenin stabilization and accumulation in the cytoplasm (Blitzer and Nusse 2006; Brunt and Scholpp 2018; Taelman et al. 2010).

Our results also demonstrated that ALP, RUNX2, and β‐catenin in hBMSCs and mBMSCs were decreased after XAV‐939 treatment compared with PHI treatment (Figures 6B–D and S5). Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) is a member of the COX2 enzyme system and is involved in β‐catenin transcription. The COX2 inhibitor diminishes β‐catenin expression levels in the cytoplasm through ubiquitination and proteasomal disruption (Pai et al. 2017). Through activation of the COX2 enzyme system, PHI may be associated with triggering the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway. However, PHI may simply directly trigger the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway (Figure 6E). Our future work will further explore this possibility. We will also use a calvarial defects model—established using a previously reported method (Yang et al. 2020)—to examine the influence of an osteogenic scaffold combined with PHI on bone defect repair and the mechanism by which PHI induces osteogenesis in vivo. Collectively, our data indicate that PHI promotes osteogenesis of BMSCs (hBMSCs and mBMSCs) partially through regulating the FAK/GSK‐3β/β‐catenin signaling axis.

Conclusion

Our studies demonstrated that PHI efficiently suppresses RANKL‐induced osteoclastogenesis and bone absorption in vivo and in vitro. Simultaneously, the findings indicated that PHI can promote osteogenesis in vitro. The FAK/GSK‐3β/β‐catenin signaling axis may be a key regulatory component for promoting bone regeneration and preventing bone loss in osteoporosis, thus providing a new therapeutic target for osteoporosis, suggesting that PHI might be a prospective therapeutic candidate to promote osteogenesis and osteoporosis‐related osteolytic diseases.
