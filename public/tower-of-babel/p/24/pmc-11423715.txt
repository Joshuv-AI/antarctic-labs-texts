Investigations and referral for suspected gastric cancer by primary care physicians: a cross-sectional study in Southeastern China
Authors: Jiayi Chen, Xiaonan Xu, Yiyang Liu, Carla Zi Cai, Li Ping Wong, Yulan Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly recognition and accurate diagnosis are particularly important in the context of gastric cancer. This study mainly aimed to investigate primary care physicians’ (PCPs’) clinical behaviour and their readiness to consider investigation or referral for symptoms possibly indicative of gastric cancer.DesignCross-sectional study.SettingA self-administered online survey was carried out in five selected cities in the Fujian province of China between February 2022 and May 2022.ParticipantPCPs working in the departments, such as Internal Gastroenterology and Hepatology, General Internal Medicine, Internal Medical Oncology Gastrointestinal Surgery or other clinical departments, have the chance to diagnose or treat patients with suspected gastric cancer.Main outcome measuresPercentage of PCPs identifying gastric cancer patients either by undertaking an endoscopy at the primary hospital or by referring patients to an upper-level hospital.ResultsA total of 1210 complete responses were received. Nearly half of responding PCPs (46.4%) only had less than 5 years of clinical experience, and the majority worked in suburban or rural regions (64.4%). Direct access to blood tests for cancer diagnosis (77.9%), X-ray (77.2%), CT (55.7%), ultrasound (85.3%), upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (54.4%) and colonoscopy (51.9%) was common. Of the respondents, 85.5% reported that they could get specialist advice for a suspected cancer patient within 48 hours in terms of investigations and 84.0% in terms of referral. In patients indicative of gastric cancer, a total of 1148 (94.8%) physicians were ready to investigate cancer either by sending patients to an endoscopy test (49.7%) or referring them to an upper-level hospital (45.1%).ConclusionsFindings indicate that PCPs in five selected cities of Southeastern China have wide and rapid access to diagnostic tests and specialist advice. Furthermore, PCPs in this region seem to have a high level of readiness to consider investigation or referral for symptoms possibly indicative of gastric cancer.

Introduction

Gastric cancer remains a significant global health concern, ranking fifth in terms of incidence and fourth in terms of mortality globally.1 The poor outcomes associated with this malignancy are widely believed to be linked to late presentation, diagnostic delays, treatment differences or combinations of these factors.2 In China, more than 60% of patients with gastric cancer are diagnosed at an advanced stage, usually resulting in a low 5-year survival rate (35%–40%).3 In contrast, early-stage gastric cancer diagnosis boasts a 5-year survival rate of over 90%.4 5 Therefore, improvement of early diagnosis and treatment of gastric cancer plays an important role in reducing mortality.

Early recognition and accurate diagnosis are particularly important in the context of gastric cancer. In countries without mass screening programmes, including China and many Western countries, most gastric cancer patients presenting with symptoms seek medical assistance in primary care. The presenting symptoms reported by these patients are usually non-specific, which could lead to misinterpretation by physicians, who may mistake them for acid reflux disease. Therefore, the timely diagnosis of patients by primary care physician (PCPs) hold critical significance for a better prognosis. Research suggests that a delay in diagnosis from the patient’s initial presentation to a healthcare institution until a confirmed diagnosis6 is linked to poorer outcomes.79 In other words, the readiness of PCP to investigate cancer—either through direct examination or referral to a higher level of care—is critical for ensuring an early diagnosis of gastric cancer. Many factors can influence a PCP’s clinical decisions, including initial misdiagnosis, adherence to guidelines, access to investigations and the efficiency of the referral system.10 11 While the role of PCP in early cancer diagnosis has been extensively studied in several Western countries,12 as of now, no such study has been conducted in China.

China has not yet implemented a strict healthcare referral and gatekeeping system.13 Hospitals in China are usually classified as primary, secondary and tertiary levels based on characteristics such as the number of beds, professional healthcare force, diagnosis and treatment equipment and operational area sizes.13 Primary hospitals, similar to those in many other countries, are designed to serve as the initial point of contact for individuals, families and communities with the healthcare system, ensuring healthcare delivery is as close as possible to where people live and work. In China, village clinics, community health centres, township health centres and county hospitals are usually categorised as primary hospitals, serving as the first element of the care continuum, especially in rural areas.14 The current hospital-centric delivery system in China is both costly and overburdened by high patient demand. To address these challenges, China policymakers have implemented measures aimed at redirecting patients from tertiary hospitals to primary hospitals. Since most Chinese patients with gastric cancer are often diagnosed at an advanced stage, it is imperative to examine the role of PCP and their readiness to perform further investigations or make referrals for suspicious cancer symptoms to facilitate early detection of cancer. Such research can enlighten stakeholders about scaling up healthcare reform. Therefore, the study’s main objectives are to assess the PCP’s clinical behaviour and their inclination to pursue further investigation or referral for patients presenting with symptoms suggestive of gastric cancer.

Method

Study design and participants

We initiated a cross-sectional, web-based anonymous survey using an online questionnaire between February 2022 and May 2022. The research team used WeChat (the most popular social media platform in China) to advertise and circulate the survey link to their clinical network members. Network members were requested to distribute the survey invitation to all their contacts working in primary medical institutions in the study region. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) PCPs working in primary medical institutions in the following five cities of Fujian province—Fuzhou, Putian, Xiamen, Zhangzhou and Quanzhou city; (2) PCPs working in departments such as Internal Gastroenterology and Hepatology, General Internal Medicine, Internal Medical Oncology Gastrointestinal Surgery or other clinical departments that have the chance to diagnose or treat patients with suspected gastric cancer. In China, the primary medical institutions include village clinics, community health centres, township health centres and municipal/county hospitals. Participants who completed the survey also received a note encouraging them to share the survey link with colleagues meeting the inclusion criteria. The participants were informed that their involvement was voluntary, and consent was implied through their completion of the questionnaire.

Measures

We adapted and modified a survey originally developed and validated by the International Cancer Benchmarking Partnership (ICBP).15 The survey instrument underwent review and modification by our local research team, consisting of one epidemiologist, one gastroenterologist and two local PCPs, to align the survey with the primary care system in China. Additionally, it was pretested by a small group of senior local PCPs in Fujian province. One question regarding career satisfaction was deleted after pretest as it was considered not relevant to the study.

The survey (online supplemental appendix 1) consisted of three parts: the first part gathered demographic details of the PCPs; the second part explored diagnostic and therapeutic behaviour, including structured questions on access to investigations and average waiting times for diagnostic tests and secondary care referrals; and the third part presented two vignettes on management choices for patients presenting with gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms. Respondents were requested to complete the survey based on their actual practice rather than perceived best practice.

Demographics

We collected demographic background data, including age, gender, ethnicity, marital status, highest education level, current working institution, number of years of clinical experience, current working city, current working location (urban/suburban/rural), average monthly income and current working department.

Clinical practices and knowledge

PCPs were asked to report their routine clinical practices including (1) average time spent for each patient’s consultation; (2) appointment method; (3) follow-up method. Additionally, their attitudes toward early gastric cancer were also questioned.

Access to diagnostic tests, specialist advice and referral

Respondents were also questioned about direct access to diagnostic tests, specialist advice and referrals. For diagnostic tests, PCPs were asked whether their working institution has direct access to blood tests for cancer diagnosis, X-rays, CT, MRI, ultrasound, upper GI endoscopy and colonoscopy. Additionally, the survey also included inquiries about the average patients’ waiting time for tests to be conducted and reported. Regarding specialist advice, PCPs were asked if they could obtain specialist advice within 48 hours, specifically in terms of investigations or referrals. In terms of referrals, PCPs were questioned about the existence of a fast-track referral system for suspicious patients and the waiting time for patients to see a doctor in the referral hospital.

Clinical vignettes

As respondents were aware that the survey was part of a study linked to gastric cancer, we designed two vignettes to avoid a hypothetical gastric cancer diagnosis (online supplemental figure 1). The first case scenario (patient 1) was designed as a patient with a mild gastric ulcer, while the second case scenario (patient 2) was designed as a patient presenting with symptoms suggestive of gastric cancer. All respondents were required to answer both vignettes. As the original survey developed by ICBP did not contain vignettes for gastric cancer, our local research team developed vignettes relating to the healthcare system in China.

The primary interest of the response was to identify possible cancer, either by referral to an upper level of care or by undertaking a definitive diagnostic investigation in primary care. The definitive test for gastric cancer was upper GI endoscopy.

Sample size calculation

We aimed to recruit at least 200 respondents for each involved city to give 95% confidence limits of ±7% for equally distributed responses (eg, 50% responding ‘yes’), and ±6% for less equally distributed responses (eg, 20% responding ‘yes’).

Statistical analysis

Direct questions relating to demographics, primary care structure factors and choice for patient management were analysed using descriptive statistics. The questions related to average waiting times for tests and results were analysed by estimating the mean time from ordering a test to receive the results, using the midpoint of each waiting time category. All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences V.24.0.

Patient and public involvement

None of the participants was involved in designing or developing the research questions or outcome measures. The participants were encouraged to disseminate the survey link to all their contacts.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 1210 physicians returned complete responses. Table 1 (located at the end of the document text file) presents the demographics of our study participants. The mean age was 35.2±8.42 (SD) years, and the majority (50.2%) were in the age group of 30–40 years. Most of the respondents were married (76.2%) and had the highest education level of undergraduate and above (89.3%). The primary working healthcare institution was the township health centre (37.9%), followed by the community health centre (27.1%), the municipal/county hospital (26.2%) and village clinics (8.8%). Of note, nearly half of the participants had less than 5 years of clinical working experience (46.4%) and worked in the department of general internal medicine (40.7%).

Clinical practices, knowledge and cancer education

Table 2 (located at the end of the document text file) displays the PCP’s routine practices. Most physicians reported that they spent an average of 6–9 min (face to face 41.2%; online 30.2%) or 10–12 min (face to face 31.0%; online 30.7%) for either face-to-face or online consultation at the patient’s first visit. The most commonly used appointment method for follow-up treatment was ‘help patients make a follow-up appointment at the first visit’ (29.7%), ‘advise patients to book their own follow-up appointments at the first visit’ (31.2%) and ‘advise patients to make their own appointments in advance’ (31.7%). Regarding the question ‘how to ensure your patient’s test results are followed up?’ most physicians responded that ‘once the test result report is out, the patient will be informed’ (54.9%) or ‘advise patients that the hospital will only contact them if the test results are positive’ (23.5%). Only one-third (37.9%) of physicians correctly identified the definition of early gastric cancer, although most of them agreed that early diagnosis of gastric cancer can improve prognosis (98.4%). In the year preceding the survey, most physicians had received education on cancer diagnosis and treatment (84.6%).

Direct access to blood tests for cancer diagnosis (77.9%), X-ray (77.2%), CT (55.7%), ultrasound (85.3%), upper GI endoscopy (54.4%) and colonoscopy (51.9%) were common. Nearly 90% (89.3%) of PCPs stated that they could expedite testing for clinically suspicious cases. Discussion

To our best knowledge, the present study is the first to report that PCPs in China appear to have wide and rapid access to diagnostic tests and specialist advice. Furthermore, PCPs demonstrate a high level of readiness to consider investigation or referral for symptoms possibly indicative of gastric cancer.

Our findings found that the majority of PCPs have direct access to various diagnostic tests, especially upper GI endoscopy and colonoscopy. In 2015, the ICBP project investigated 11 Western countries regarding the accessibility of different diagnostic tests by their PCPs.12 The ICBP study found that on average, the reported access to upper GI endoscopy was 41.5%, compared with 54.4% in the current study. Access to colonoscopy in the ICBP study was 29.2%, compared with 51.9% in our current study. More importantly, patients’ diagnosis waiting time were much shorter in primary care in China. Particularly for upper GI endoscopy, the average diagnosis waiting time was only 1.36 weeks in China, while, in contrast, ICBP countries reported an average diagnosis waiting time of 8 weeks.12 An even longer diagnosis waiting time was reported in New Zealand (10.9 weeks).16 These findings suggest that primary care hospitals in China are equipped with excellent infrastructure, enabling patients to undergo endoscopy tests and get results quickly during their visit to a primary care hospital. This could significantly reduce the risk of delayed diagnosis. Furthermore, analyses of the vignettes revealed that 94.8% of PCPs were prepared to further investigate patients who were suggestive of gastric cancer, either by sending patients for an endoscopy or specialist referral. These findings indicate that as long as patients visit the hospital, there is a high probability that they will be diagnosed. However, in contrast, we also noted that more than 60% of patients with gastric cancer are diagnosed at an advanced stage in China.3 We can infer that the late diagnosis of gastric cancer in China is primarily due to patients delaying their hospital visits. Hence, it is crucial to implement public-directed interventions aimed at raising awareness about the importance of early cancer screening and recognising alarm symptoms of gastric cancer in China. The government should also improve the healthcare system to reduce the obstacles for patients seeking hospital care.

However, it is also worth noting that nearly half (49.0%) of the PCPs also chose to send patients for endoscopy in case scenario 1, which involved patients who were at low risk of gastric cancer. This raises concerns about inappropriate referrals and the overuse of endoscopy—a costly and invasive procedure, despite its universal acceptance and recommendation for routine screening.17 Many studies have demonstrated that the overuse or inappropriate use of endoscopy not only strains endoscopy service and imposes a huge financial burden on the country but also expose patients to potential health risks due to the invasive nature of the test. Common complications related to the endoscopy procedure include cardiopulmonary complications, bleeding, perforation, pancreatitis, cholangitis and infection.18 In a recent systematic review, predominantly comprising data from North America and Western Europe, a composite infection rate of 0.2% was reported following GI endoscopic procedures.18 Hence, the development of a strategic early cancer screening tool that takes into account both medical and economic considerations is crucial for the optimising population health and efficiently allocating resources. Using risk prediction tools could prove beneficial in identifying high-risk groups in primary care for subsequent endoscopy. Several risk prediction models for gastric cancer have demonstrated sufficient discriminative abilities.19 20 In particular, the models developed by Lee et al showed a good prediction performance, as evaluated by receiver operating characteristic curve (0.904) and internal validation c-statistics (0.90).21 The tool is practical for use in primary care, as the predictors mainly consist of lifestyle and demographic factors that can be easily assessed by questionnaires. However, before its implementation in our local setting, it is essential to conduct appropriate validation using our local population. On the other hand, serological tumour biomarkers have been widely employed in clinics for early detection of cancer. Atrophic gastritis caused by Helicobacter pylori could affect the secretion of pepsinogens and gastrin, which serve as promising biomarkers for predicting the status of the gastric mucosa.22 Using a combination of the presence of H. pylori antibody and serum pepsinogen could be potentially considered as a strategy for early gastric cancer screening, especially in Asian countries with a high incidence of gastric cancer, such as Japan and China.23 24 Although most of these serological markers may be relatively challenging in primary and community care settings, they offer higher specificity and result in improved generalisation performances.25

The findings of this study highlight the need to improve the identification of gastric cancer patients in primary care through several key interventions. These include providing enhanced training and education for PCPs on current diagnostic criteria, developing standardised referral protocols and improving communication channels between primary care providers and specialists to ensure timely and efficient patient management. Patient-level interventions are equally important, as noted earlier. Interventions such as education campaigns are crucial for encouraging individuals to seek early medical advice. Additionally, using technology, including telemedicine, will further enhance the early identification and management of gastric cancer by improving patient engagement

Limitations

This study contains several limitations that are worth mentioning. The primary limitation of this study is the use of a self-administered online survey answered by self-reporting PCPs, which introduces potential biases. PCPs may provide socially desirable responses, potentially impacting the accuracy and reliability of the findings. Second, the representativeness of the PCP population was constrained in a specific region of China, as we only included 200 PCPs in each of the five cities selected from Southeastern China, and the majority were under 40 years of age. However, the near equal distributions of participants from urban/rural regions, as well as different types of primary care hospitals, enhance the representativeness of our samples. The second shortcoming of the study is that the study exclusively focused on public primary care hospitals and did not include private hospitals/clinics. It is worth stating that in China, private hospitals necessitate out-of-pocket medical expenses, leading most patients to choose public hospitals. Future studies should incorporate data collected from private hospitals. Third, this study intended to also include an investigation of factors influencing PCP’s readiness to investigate cancer. However, with 94% of the participating physicians expressing their readiness to further investigate cancer, we lacked a sufficient sample size to demonstrate the desired differences.

Of important note, in an effective cancer referral pathway, factors that hinder collaboration between clinics and hospitals are undoubtedly important.26 However, this is beyond the scope of our study. Our research primarily focuses on understanding PCPs’ practices and readiness in diagnosing and referring patients with suspected gastric cancer. We recognise the significance of this issue and recommend that future studies explore the barriers to effective collaboration within the healthcare system. Such research could offer valuable insights and lead to strategies that enhance communication and streamline referral processes.

Conclusions

PCPs in China have wide and rapid access to diagnostic tests and specialist advice. Furthermore, participating physicians demonstrated a high level of readiness to investigate gastric cancer. These findings suggest that patients with gastric cancer in China have a low risk of delayed diagnosis in primary care hospitals. It is crucial to implement public-directed interventions aimed at raising awareness about the importance of early cancer screening and recognising alarm symptoms of gastric cancer in China. Furthermore, our study underscores the possible overuse of endoscopy and its potential harms. It is also noteworthy that future studies should focus on developing reliable risk prediction tools suitable for use in primary care to enable timely diagnosis and referral.
