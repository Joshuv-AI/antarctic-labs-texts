Genetic diagnostic yield by MRI pattern in children with cerebral palsy: a population-based study
Authors: Jesia G Berry, Ajay Taranath, Robert Goetti, Michelle A Farrar, Simona Fiori, Huy-dat Pham, Murthy N Mittinty, Mark A Corbett, Lyle J Palmer, Dani L Fornarino, Kelly Harper, Catherine S Gibson, Shaneen J Leishman, Shona C Goldsmith, Sarah J McIntyre, Alicia Montgomerie, Rhiannon M Pilkington, John W Lynch, Remo N Russo, Michael C Fahey, Jurgen Fripp, Roslyn N Boyd, Margaret J Wright, James E Rice, Mary-Clare Waugh, Alastair H MacLennan, Jozef Gecz, Clare L van Eyk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundNeuroimaging abnormalities are detected in 80–86% of individuals with cerebral palsy (CP). Lesional white or grey matter injuries (WMI, GMI) are most common and typically attributed to environmental factors, while genetic causes are thought to underlie non-lesional injuries and normal brain imaging. This hypothesis has not been formally tested, and we aimed to evaluate it using the Australian CP biobank.MethodsThis population-based study included 331 children with CP (195 males, 136 females), born between 1986 and 2018. Genomic DNA extracted from blood or saliva samples underwent sequencing, variant filtering, classification using ACMG-AMP criteria, and variant curation. Probands were classified as ‘resolved’, ‘candidate variant(s) identified’, or ‘no candidate variant identified’. Paediatric radiologists/neurologists coded brain MRI, CT, and ultrasound using the Magnetic Resonance Imaging Classification System (MRICS). Data analyses included descriptive statistics and multinomial logistic regression.FindingsA genetic aetiology was identified in 80 children (24%), while 165 (50%) had candidate variants and 86 (26%) had no candidate variant identified. Among children with predominant WMI or GMI (50% and 21%, respectively), 19% and 10% were genetically resolved. Children with maldevelopments, miscellaneous findings, or normal neuroimaging (10%, 10%, and 8%, respectively) were more often genetically resolved (41%, 48% and 39%) compared to those with lesional injuries (WMI or GMI), with relative risk ratios (RRR) of 3.54 (95% CI: 1.65–7.59), 4.75 (95% CI: 2.21–10.2), and 3.27 (95% CI: 1.42–7.52), respectively.InterpretationThese findings support the hypothesis that genetic aetiologies are more common in non-lesional CP. However, genetic diagnoses were observed across all MRICS categories, including 17% of lesional brain injuries. Notably, almost half of all genetically diagnosed children (39 of 80) were in the WMI and GMI groups. Therefore, we emphasise that neuroimaging should be used as a guide, not an exclusion criterion for genomic testing in CP.FundingChannel 7 Children's Research Foundation, Cerebral Palsy Alliance Research Foundation, The Hospital Research Foundation, the NHMRC, and in-kind support from Illumina.Keywords: Cerebral palsy, Neuroimaging, MRI, Brain injury, Genetic testing, Genetic diagnoses, Molecular diagnostic yield

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed for manuscripts published before September 10, 2025, using combinations of the terms: “cerebral palsy” AND (“neuroimaging” OR MRI OR “brain imaging”) AND (gene∗ OR genom∗), without language restrictions. We also reviewed reference lists of relevant review articles and primary studies. Studies were included if they examined the relationship between structural brain imaging findings and genetic aetiology in individuals with cerebral palsy (CP), regardless of study design. Many identified studies were small case series or cohorts, often focused on monogenic CP in relation to one or a few brain injury patterns, such as normal imaging, maldevelopments, or MRI not compatible with the clinical picture. Larger studies typically examined genetic yield in unselected CP cohorts or in samples enriched for specific phenotypes. These studies often stratified by clinical risk factors such as neurodevelopmental comorbidities, presumed birth asphyxia, or CP without identifiable risk factors (cryptogenic CP). However, definitions of cryptogenic CP varied: most excluded perinatal complications, prematurity, and specific brain lesion types (e.g. periventricular leukomalacia, infarcts, hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy, malformations), though the application of these exclusions differed. Several overlapping systematic reviews were identified, each differing in their inclusion criteria, cohort selection strategies, and interpretation of cryptogenic CP—factors that can introduce bias in estimating diagnostic yields. None of the large-scale studies provided detailed phenotyping with both standardised neuroimaging classification and contemporary genomic curation to assess associations between brain injury patterns and genetic diagnoses.

Added value of this study

This study used a population-based cohort of 331 children with CP who underwent systematic genomic sequencing alongside standardised neuroimaging classification using the qualitative Magnetic Resonance Imaging Classification System (MRICS). Genetic diagnoses were present across all five MRICS categories, including injury types commonly attributed to environmental causes. Diagnostic yield was significantly higher in children with maldevelopments, miscellaneous abnormalities, or normal MRI compared to those with predominant white or grey matter injury. Multinomial logistic regression was used to explore associations between MRICS-defined injury patterns and genetic aetiology, with adjustment for birthweight centile as a potential confounder. These findings support the role of brain imaging as one component within a broader risk stratification approach to guide genetic testing for children with or at high risk of CP.

Implications of all the available evidence

Aetiological classification of CP is complex, particularly where both genetic and environmental factors may co-exist. Our findings demonstrate the value of genetic testing across all neuroimaging patterns, even in children with injury types previously thought to be non-genetic. No single imaging pattern reliably predicts genetic aetiology, highlighting that clinical judgement and comprehensive assessment, including neuroimaging and neurodevelopmental profile, can help prioritise genomic testing where resources are limited. Further research should investigate genetic findings within MRICS groups, and the functional significance of candidate variants of uncertain significance. Evaluation of the cost-effectiveness and clinical utility of genomic testing in CP populations is also warranted. Broader implementation of genomic diagnostics has the potential to refine CP classification, inform clinical management, and support personalised care.

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) encompasses a diverse group of permanent non-progressive disorders of movement and posture, resulting from disturbances in the developing foetal or infant brain.1 Although neuroimaging is not required for diagnosis, clinicians consider it important in the CP diagnostic workup to aid early detection, explore aetiology, and inform prognosis.2 Similarly, genomic testing is not routinely considered or offered for all children with or at high risk of CP,2 yet it can potentially clarify aetiology, enhance neuroimaging interpretation, and for some, inform treatment pathways.3

Systematic reviews indicate that abnormalities on Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) or Computed Tomography (CT) scans are detectable in 80–86% of individuals with CP, while 14–17% show no abnormalities on conventional neuroimaging.4, 5, 6 Since CP diagnosis is based on clinical signs rather than causation,3 normal imaging does not rule it out.5 Globally, 23–31% of people with CP are estimated to have an underlying genetic aetiology,7,8 a figure expected to grow as genetic testing becomes more prevalent, the list of known CP genes expands, and variants of uncertain significance continue to be studied and resolved.9

Guidance on whether to test, and if so, whom to prioritise for testing remains unclear. In a US cohort of 1345 mostly paediatric patients referred for diagnostic exome sequencing (ES),10 diagnostic yield was higher in those with multiple neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs)—such as intellectual disability (ID), epilepsy or autism spectrum disorders (ASD)—compared to those without (33.4% vs 19.7%). A similar trend was observed in an Australian whole genome sequencing (GS) study of 150 clinically unselected children with CP, where yield was higher among those with co-occurring NDDs (27.3% vs 16.3%).9 A GS study of 327 Canadian children with CP reported an 11.3% molecular diagnostic yield, increasing in those with cognitive impairment or normal MRI, albeit with incomplete ascertainment.11 Yields in this cohort were also higher for epilepsy and maldevelopments pattern, though not statistically significant.11 Likewise, in an ES study of 1578 Chinese children with CP, diagnostic yield was higher in those with global developmental delay or ID than without (32.4% vs 24.5%), especially if maldevelopments (40.3%) or clinically assigned perinatal asphyxia at birth (47.2%) were also present.12

A systematic review of mostly case reports and cohort studies compared 272 individuals carrying pathogenic variants in CP genes (reported in ≥ two probands; 55 genes) with the broader CP population.13 Genetic CP was more often associated with dyskinesia (39% vs 7%) and less often with spasticity (55% vs 86–90%). Moderate genetic indicators included higher ID prevalence (80% vs 50%), lower preterm birth (6% vs 34%), and a reduced unilateral CP distribution (8% vs 18–36%). MRI abnormalities were not significantly different (74% vs 80%, p = 0.32), although detailed descriptions were often lacking.13

The Magnetic Resonance Imaging Classification System (MRICS), developed by Himmelmann et al., in 2016,14 with the Surveillance of Cerebral Palsy in Europe (SCPE) network, categorises brain pathology by presumed timing of insult in utero or around birth.14,15 Among 3818 participants from European CP registries (1999–2009), the most common patterns were predominant white matter injury (WMI: 48.7%; n = 1861), predominant grey matter injury (GMI: 21.0%, n = 803), maldevelopments (11.1%, n = 423), normal findings (10.6%, n = 405) and miscellaneous abnormalities (8.5%, n = 326).16

A recent study by Horber et al.15 suggests specific MRICS patterns may correlate with genetic aetiology in CP. Brain maldevelopments are thought to have a strong genetic contribution, and congenital anomalies are more common in children with CP than in the general population.17 Similarly, a normal MRI may indicate a genetic origin, with two caveats: (1) mild lesions and subtle maldevelopments may be overlooked in children under two years, particularly on lower field MRI (1.5T), and (2) degenerative disorders or spinal-origin spasticity misclassified as CP may underlie these findings. The miscellaneous category includes acquired, non-specific, or genetically suggestive patterns, as detailed in Table 1. WMI and GMI are often attributed to environmental insults, though genetic predispositions or other unknown mechanisms may contribute. Horber et al.15 recommends considering genetic testing in WMI or GMI when there is a family history of CP or no obvious environmental cause.

In a cohort of clinically unselected children with CP who underwent neuroimaging and genomic testing, we investigated whether specific MRICS patterns correlate with genetic aetiologies. We hypothesised that pathogenic genetic variants would be more common in maldevelopments and normal imaging, while miscellaneous patterns might show a weaker association. We assessed these associations while adjusting for neonatal and maternal confounders.

Methods

Participants

This population-based study included children recruited to the Australian CP Biobank (ACPB) and the IMAGINE CP cohort (Imaging and Genomics of children with CP). Blood samples from probands and unaffected parents (when available) and clinical data were obtained with informed consent. Participants from South Australia (SA), New South Wales (NSW), and Queensland (Qld) were included, as neuroimaging was accessible for these jurisdictions.

In SA and NSW, children were recruited through Botulinum Toxin injecting clinics, orthopaedic theatre lists, and referrals from paediatricians, rehabilitation specialists, neurologists, and clinical geneticists, as well as through outreach to the CP community. In SA, 193 participants were recruited from the Women's and Children's Hospital and Flinders Medical Centre between April 2007 and August 2023. In NSW, 181 participants were recruited from the Children's Hospital at Westmead and Sydney Children's Hospital between October 2013 and April 2015. The Qld cohort included 56 children with a confirmed diagnosis of CP, identified through state-wide CP registers or clinical services and recruited during the PREDICT-CP study (aged 1.5–5 years at enrolment, and reassessed at 8–12 years), with blood sample collection from 2019 to 2020.18 All cohorts aimed to broadly represent the CP population by recruiting without restrictions based on clinical features.

All participants had a confirmed clinical diagnosis of CP, defined by permanence and non-progression beyond age four (though not unchanging).1,19 Children with post-neonatal CP (brain injury after 28 days of life) were excluded. Clinical data were collected through parent/guardian questionnaire, clinician assessment, and medical record review. To address missing data, verify accuracy, and resolve implausible values, we linked the ACPB with the SA CP Register and the Perinatal Statistics Collection via the state-wide Better Evidence Better Outcomes Linked Data (BEBOLD) platform. Similarly, the ACPB was linked with the NSW/ACT and Queensland CP registries to improve data quality.

The Women's and Children's Health Network Human Research Ethics Committee (WCHN HREC) in SA oversees the national ACPB (2020HRE01105) and genomic investigations (2020HRE01273). SA CP register linkage was approved by the SA Department of Health and Wellbeing (DHW) (HREC 2021/HRE00227), with governance authorisation from the DHW and WCHN. NSW/ACT CP register linkage was approved by the Sydney Children's Hospital Network (SCHN, HREC/12/SCHN/222) and authorised by the Cerebral Palsy Alliance Research Committee (2022_09_02). HREC approval for the IMAGINE cohort was provided by Children's Health Queensland Hospital and Health Service (HREC/16/QRCH/229) and the University of Queensland (2014001487).

Genetic analysis

Genomic sequencing (ES, GS, or gene panel) has been previously reported for some participants.9,20,21 GS was performed for 109 additional participants (53 ACPB and 56 IMAGINE CP) on a NovaSeq 6000 with PCR-free library preparation and paired-end 150 bp reads. GS data were mapped/re-mapped to GRCh38 and all variant types [single nucleotide variants (SNVs), small insertions and deletions (indels), copy number variants (CNVs), some structural variants (SVs), short tandem repeats (STRs) and mitochondrial variants] were called using DRAGEN 4.0 implemented through Illumina Emedgene (v33). ES data were mapped to GRCh37 (hs37d5) using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner and variants called using GATK best practices workflow for germline short variant discovery (GATK v 4.1.6.0). Detection of CNVs from ES data was performed using CoNIFER algorithm as described previously.22 Tertiary analysis of variants was performed using the Illumina Emedgene (v33) and Genoox Franklin platforms for GS and ES respectively between 2022 and 2024. Variants were classified using American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics-Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG-AMP) guidelines.23 Probands were classified as.

Neuroimaging

Participants with at least one structural cranial MRI, CT or ultrasound were included. Imaging was classified using the MRICS by paediatric radiologists and MRI-trained paediatric neurologists, each responsible for a specific cohort: SA (AT), NSW hospital one (RG), NSW hospital two (MAF) and Qld (SF), into maldevelopments, predominant WMI, predominant GMI, miscellaneous and normal (Table 1). Imaging classification was based on direct review of scans wherever possible. When scans were unavailable (e.g. archived off-site), classification relied on diagnostic radiology reports. When more than one pattern was identified, the predominant pattern or the one that is believed most likely to cause the CP phenotype was selected. Specialists could consult radiologist reports in the electronic medical record when viewing images, as this context could support accurate identification of injury mechanisms and timing.

All available scans were considered, with greater interpretative weighting given to those acquired after two years of age, consistent with recommendations for accurate myelination assessment.14,24 Early MRI classification is valid if a clear brain injury pattern (maldevelopments, WMI, GMI) is identified before age two; however, if no abnormalities are seen, a repeat MRI at this age should confirm normal findings.14,24 For neonates (<1 month of age), in whom brain development and injury patterns are distinct from those in older children, the neonatal neuroimaging classification system (NNICS) was used.24 The NNICS employs the same categories as the MRICS, ensuring consistency in classification across age groups. For older infants ≥1 month, MRICS classification was applied.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using Stata version 18.5 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Birth year distributions were compared between participants with and without neuroimaging to assess historical differences in neuroimaging availability. MRICS interrater reliability was estimated using kappa statistics in the one jurisdiction (Qld) where a second rater (AT) was permitted access to patient imaging data. Genetic, neuroimaging, and CP phenotype data were assessed against MRICS classification using descriptive statistics. Fisher's exact test was used for comparisons due to small cell sizes. Frequencies and proportions were compared with national Australian Cerebral Palsy Register (ACPR) data.25

This study adheres to STROBE guidelines for reporting cross-sectional studies (see Supplementary Material). MRICS subclassification is presented by resolved gene/loci and variant type (Table S1) and by summary genetic findings (Table S2).

Multivariable regression and sensitivity analysis

Exploratory multinomial logistic regression analyses were adjusted for customised birth weight centile, calculated using the Gestation Related Optimal Weight (GROW) software26 identified a priori as a key continuous covariate to control for confounding. This composite measure incorporates gestational age, sex, birthweight, maternal ethnicity, parity, height and pre-pregnancy weight, thereby avoiding collinearity from including these factors separately. The use of customised centiles for predicting morbidity and mortality outcomes and for covariate adjustment is standard practice in perinatal epidemiology (See Alternative model specifications in Supplementary Material).

In the primary analysis, missing data were assumed to be missing-at-random, reflecting incomplete register reporting rather than systematic differences by brain injury pattern, and adjusted analyses were restricted to complete cases. Probands were dichotomised as genetically resolved versus all others (i.e. unresolved with candidate variant(s) or no candidate variant identified). The level of statistical significance was set at 0.05.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess robustness. Multiple imputation by chained equations was used (20 datasets; see Methods: multiple imputation in Supplementary Material), with comparison of fully imputed and complete-case models (Table S3). We also conducted an analysis restricted to MRI only (Table S4). Alternative model specifications varied the adjustment set and regression approach: (i) using categorical centile groupings with additional adjustment for multiple birth (Table S5); (ii) replacing centile with risk factors identified as significant in cross-tabulations (Table S6); and (iii) two models clustering by jurisdiction: one treating genetic findings as a binary variable (Table S7) and the other as an ordinal variable (three groups) (Table S8).

Role of funders

The funding bodies had no role in the study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, writing of the manuscript, or the decision to submit it for publication.

Results

Participant inclusion is summarised in the flow diagram (Fig. 1). Three were excluded: one withdrew, and two were siblings of included probands, also diagnosed with CP. To maintain independence of observations in statistical analyses, only one sibling per pair was retained. Among the remaining 427 participants, 87 (20%) had no imaging, 20 (5%) had ultrasound only, 299 (70%) had MRI, and 21 (5%) had CT. Seven scans (2 MRI, 2 CT, 3 ultrasounds) were excluded due to inaccessibility or inability to code, leaving 333 participants with eligible neuroimaging.

A PRISMA flow diagram of recruitment of participants from SA, NSW, and Qld.

To assess whether neuroimaging availability varied by birth year, we compared distributions among participants with (n = 333) and without imaging (n = 87). A higher proportion without imaging were born before 2000 compared to those with imaging (25% vs 16%), but this was not statistically significant (Fisher's exact p = 0.10). Most participants in both groups were born in the 2000s (62% vs 68%), with fewer born in the 2010s (13% vs 17%), indicating broadly similar age distributions. For 61 participants whose scans were archived off-site, radiologist reports were used for classification.

In Qld, interrater reliability for the main MRICS groups (A–E) was 0.85 (95% CI: 0.70–0.97; 47/50 scans), indicating almost perfect agreement. Subclassification reliability was lower (κ = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.47–0.79; substantial agreement) but improved to 0.80 (95% CI: 0.69–0.91) when weighted for concordance within main groups (user-defined weights: 1.0 for identical subclasses, 0.8 for different subclasses).

GS was performed in 191 participants (including 82 from van Eyk et al.9), 140 underwent ES,20,21 and two had prior genetic diagnoses by targeted gene panel.27 Two GS participants were excluded due to sequencing failure or a post-neonatal CP diagnosis.

The final cohort for analysis comprised 331 participants, with a median age at recruitment of 8.4 years (range: 1.2–23.5 years) and median age at last neuroimaging (MRI or CT scan) of 2.6 years (range: day 4–29 years). As this was a retrospective study, we could not fully adhere to recommendations that myelination assessment be undertaken at ≥ 2 years of age. MRI was available for 295 participants: 184 (62%) underwent MRI at ≥2 years, 48 (16%) between 1 and 2 years, and 63 (21%) <1 year. Of those imaged in infancy, 15 were neonates (<1 month), all but one of whom underwent imaging within the first two weeks of life. CT was performed in 19 participants: 9 (47%) at ≥2 years, 4 (21%) at 1–2 years, and 6 (32%) < 1 year, of whom two were imaged within the first two weeks of life. Cranial ultrasound was performed in 17 participants: 14 were serial studies commencing in the first week of life, and the remainder (3) were performed <1 year.

Overall, the genetic aetiology was diverse and heterogeneous, with 63 different genes or genomic regions impacted among the 80 participants (Table 3 and Table S1). Most genes were unique to a single participant. Among the 165 participants with candidate variants, 151 had VUSs in a known disease gene and 14 had a potentially deleterious variant in a gene not yet known to be associated with human disease (GUS) (data not shown).

Brain injury patterns were categorised as maldevelopments (10%), predominant WMI (50%), predominant GMI (21%), miscellaneous (10%) or normal (8%). Genetically resolved children were unevenly distributed across these categories (p < 0.001), with lower genetic diagnoses in lesional injuries (WMI: 19%, GMI: 10%), and higher proportions in maldevelopments (41%), miscellaneous (48%), and normal neuroimaging (39%) as hypothesised.

Brain injury profiles differed between term- and preterm-born children (p < 0.001). WMI was more common in preterm children (52 term vs 114 preterm children). Apart from the normal pattern, which was more evenly distributed (14 term vs 14 preterm children), all other injury patterns were more prevalent in term-born children: maldevelopments (29 vs five children), GMI (60 vs ten children), and miscellaneous injury (24 vs nine children).

Genetic diagnoses were more common in term-born participants (50/179, 28%) than in those born pre-term (30/152; 20%; p = 0.036). Term-born participants accounted for nearly all genetically resolved individuals with maldevelopments (13), and at least two-thirds of those with miscellaneous abnormalities (11) or normal findings (eight). In WMI, resolved participants were more evenly distributed between term (11/52, 21%) and preterm (21/114, 18%) births, whereas in GMI all resolved participants (7) were born at term.

As shown in Table S2, 17 participants were classified as A.2 (other maldevelopments, which include cerebellar hypoplasia) or D.3 (miscellaneous, encompassing patterns suggestive of a genetic background such as cerebellar atrophy or myelination disorders). Six of these were classified based on a single MRI study (one aged <1 year and two aged <2 years). The remaining ten participants had more than one MRI, with only one <1 year, the others were aged ≥4 years.

The cohort's demographic profile largely aligned with national estimates from the ACPR, except for a higher frequency of dystonic CP, likely due to our inclusive reporting of motor pattern that did not distinguish between predominant and secondary classifications. About one-quarter (27%) of children were small for gestational age, regardless of lesion type. While birthweight centile and mode of delivery are not reported by the ACPR, a national study of singletons born in Australia between 1995 and 2014 reported a slightly lower prevalence of small for gestational age (<10th centile) at 19%.29 ID appeared less prevalent in our cohort; however, direct comparison was limited, as our dataset combined ID and developmental delay. We identified ID by capturing mentions throughout the study database, linking with CP registers, and reviewing medical records where available. Epilepsy prevalence was comparable to national estimates and was higher among children with GMI and maldevelopments. Congenital anomalies were slightly more prevalent than national estimates and were particularly common among participants with maldevelopments, as expected, and among those with miscellaneous abnormalities. Speech impairment data were unavailable in our cohort, as was ASD in the ACPR. Mother's ethnicity was also not reported in the ACPR; instead, 906 of 10,855 children (8%) were born overseas. In our cohort, 29 of 331 children (9%) had mothers of an ethnicity other than Australian European, not counting five mothers who identified as Aboriginal.

Discussion

Previous studies9, 10, 11, 12 demonstrate the value of genetic testing in individuals with CP who also have comorbid NDDs (such as intellectual disability, epilepsy, and autism8,10). These conditions share genetic aetiologies that contribute to a higher diagnostic yield. Building on this, we investigated whether specific neuroimaging patterns also show associations with underlying genetic aetiology and, therefore, could assist in making decisions about genetic testing in CP.

In this genomic study of 331 children with confirmed clinical diagnosis of CP, 80 (24%) were genetically resolved, with candidate variants or genes identified in an additional 165 children (50%), and no candidate variants or genes in 86 children (26%). Genetic diagnoses were observed across all five MRICS categories, including both lesional and non-lesional patterns.

Lesional injuries—white matter injury (WMI, 50%) and grey matter injury (GMI, 21%)—were the most prevalent patterns. Notably, 19% of children with WMI and 10% with GMI were genetically resolved. Fewer children had maldevelopments, miscellaneous findings, or normal neuroimaging (10%, 10% and 8%, respectively), yet these groups had markedly higher proportions of genetically resolved children (41%, 48% and 39%).

Two-thirds of children with WMI were born pre-term, whereas all other brain injury patterns were more common among term-born children. Consequently, the genetic contribution was significantly higher in term-born than pre-term children (28% vs 20%).

Horber et al.15 hypothesised that individuals with maldevelopments and normal neuroimaging are more likely to have a genetic aetiology, with miscellaneous abnormalities potentially yielding similar results—though the mix of acquired and genetic patterns could dilute this signal. Our multinomial logistic regression models support this hypothesis, showing higher genetic diagnostic yields in children with CP whose neuroimaging exhibited these three MRICS patterns compared with lesional injuries (WMI, GMI) or WMI alone. Interestingly, the miscellaneous category had the highest genetic diagnostic yield relative to its sample size (48%), slightly exceeding that of maldevelopments (41%; Table 2). These findings suggest that genetic factors may play a substantial role in individuals with CP and within mixed pathology categories.

However, these three categories account for only 29% of the cohort, while WMI and GMI comprise 71%. Although typically attributed to environmental insults,15 17% of children with WMI or GMI were genetically resolved. Because these groups are large, their absolute contribution was substantial, representing nearly half of all genetically diagnosed children (39 of 80). This highlights the importance of genetic factors even in lesional injuries, commonly regarded as non-genetic. While genetic aetiology clearly plays a role in CP, the underlying causes are frequently multifactorial. For example, a GS study of 327 Canadian children found that 73% of those with a genetic aetiology also had multiple antenatal or perinatal risk factors, supporting the involvement of both genetic and environmental influences in CP causation.11 Children with normal neuroimaging or malformations of cortical development may be referred for genetic testing based on clinical judgement of a genetic cause, a practice supported by findings from a recent survey of clinicians.2 Nonetheless, while no single brain injury pattern reliably determines the need for genetic testing, our findings support using neuroimaging as an important component in a broader risk stratification approach.

As a retrospective cohort study, we were unable to systematically capture how genetic findings informed clinical management. Nonetheless, emerging evidence highlights both the opportunities and challenges faced in translating genetic diagnoses of CP into clinical practice. In a recent systematic review, pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were reported in 243 genes (together accounting for 502 genetic diagnoses from 1841 individuals tested from both clinical and research cohorts).30 Of these, 58 genes had actionable treatments, affecting 140 individuals (8% of all those referred for genetic testing).30 Despite such potential, a population-based study of more than 2.4 million children in Israel found that CP, together with epilepsy, had the lowest rate of genetic counselling referrals (24.6% each). This finding reflects the absence of established guidelines for genetic counselling in CP, as well as the persistence and impact of historical assumptions that its causation is largely non-genetic.31

Our clinically unselected cohort broadly represents the national Australian CP population, enhancing the reliability of the inferences drawn from this study. CP diagnoses and comorbidities were verified by data linkage to CP registers, and MRICS coding was performed by specialised radiologists/neurologists to an internationally recognised standard, using multiple images per participant in 82% of children, or otherwise radiologist reports. The sensitivity analyses demonstrate that the findings of this study are robust to missing data and analytical modelling approach.

MRICS classification can be challenging when multiple patterns are present and it is unclear which one is predominant or causing CP.14 In our reliability analysis, agreement for the main MRICS groups (A–E) was high (κ = 0.85), exceeding the original study of CP registers in 10 European countries (κ = 0.69), where the majority of 41 raters had general paediatric or rehabilitation backgrounds.14 Subclassifications showed substantial agreement (κ = 0.64) improving to 0.80 when weighted for concordance within main groups. This indicates the MRICS framework is reproducible, particularly at the level of main groups. A limitation is that reliability could only be tested in one jurisdiction due to patient confidentiality restrictions. Notably, the two raters (AT and SF) coded 229 of 331 participants (69%).

Although MRI was the primary imaging modality, a small number of CT scans (5%) and cranial ultrasounds (5%) were included to maximise sample size. Their lower sensitivity compared with MRI may have underestimated certain abnormalities. CT is useful for detecting calcifications, haemorrhage, cysts and gross atrophy, but less sensitive for many lesion types. Cranial ultrasound is routinely used in preterm neonates to track evolving injury. It is excellent for identifying intraventricular haemorrhage, ventriculomegaly, and cystic periventricular leukomalacia, but offers limited whole-brain visualisation and may miss cortical malformations, deep grey matter injury, subtle or diffuse white-matter lesions, or delayed myelination.32 Despite these limitations, ultrasound and MRI have shown good agreement for MRICS classification.32

Nearly 40% of MRI scans were performed before age two, when incomplete myelination and cortical maturation can obscure subtle malformations or migrational abnormalities, potentially reducing sensitivity. Interpretation in very young infants also carries inherent variability, even with structured application of the NNICS.24 Nonetheless, integrating these early scans with genomic testing demonstrates utility for precision diagnosis despite age-related constraints.

Although MRI is recommended as the first diagnostic step following neurological examination and history taking, we found that 20% of eligible children had no neuroimaging performed at tertiary public children's hospitals, and 5% underwent ultrasound only. Some imaging may have been performed elsewhere but was not available to the study team. Imaging from private providers was occasionally obtained through extensive inquiry and specific patient consent. The age at MRI varied, introducing inconsistency in classification. We included neonatal scans (<1 month) classified using the NNICS to maximise sample size, although validation studies of the MRICS have primarily focused on postneonatal imaging (≥1 month).14,16 The reliability of imaging interpretation in children under two years, particularly neonates, requires further validation, which we acknowledge as an important limitation.

In addition, a small subset (n = 17) was classified as MRICS A.2 or D.3. These categories include cerebellar hypoplasia and cerebellar atrophy, which can be difficult to distinguish, especially in infants and when only a single MRI examination is available. The specialist coders applied the published criteria of Poretti et al.,33 who describe hypoplasia as reduced volume of a structurally normal cerebellum and atrophy as loss of tissue volume with enlargement of interfolial spaces. Differentiation is more reliable in older children, once normal cerebellar volume and sulcation are established.33 As only four of the 17 cases were younger than two years, potential misclassification is likely limited, but should nonetheless be considered when interpreting these results.

Single patient studies provide only tentative conclusions, and the small sample size reduced precision. As shown in Tables 2 and 5, the wide compatibility intervals reflect the low numbers of resolved cases for non-lesional injury patterns and constrain the generalisability of MRICS subgroup-specific findings, warranting cautious interpretation. Additionally, half of the cohort had candidate variants, some of which may be reclassified as pathogenic as the body of knowledge grows, potentially increasing diagnostic yield and refining genotype–phenotype correlations. Further investigation of novel candidate genes through functional studies and case series reports is required to resolve these variants and clarify their role in cerebral palsy. Focussing on neuroimaging patterns associated with specific genes or variants may also provide deeper insights into the genetic aetiology.

Further studies leveraging large population datasets that include genomic and neuroimaging data—such as the UK Biobank—may help validate these findings and represent an important future direction. However, direct replication is challenging due to limited diagnostic specificity (CP identified only via ICD-10 codes), the need for MRICS classification of imaging data, and the evolution of lesion patterns from childhood to adulthood, which hinders comparison.

This study reinforces the importance of genetic testing in the diagnostic work-up of children for a CP diagnosis regardless of neuroimaging pattern. While maldevelopments, miscellaneous findings, and normal neuroimaging are strong genetic indicators, a substantial number of children with lesional injuries of the white and grey matter (WMI, GMI) also have genetic diagnoses and should not be overlooked—indeed, almost half of all children with a genetic diagnosis fell into these groups. Early genetic testing can provide aetiological insights, inform diagnosis, guide treatment, and support family counselling.

A standardised neuroimaging work-up is essential for children with CP or at high risk of CP. Improving IT infrastructure, digitising historic medical imaging, clinician education, and electronic health record prompts can facilitate this. While semi-quantitative scoring34 adds depth beyond MRICS, generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) may offer superior three-dimensional brain injury analysis.35 The heterogeneity of CP, including congenital anomalies, has driven the development of bespoke segmentation algorithms and deep learning technologies.36 Machine learning may also improve prognostication by quantifying lesion severity, linking it to CP features, and detecting subtle abnormalities in scans previously deemed normal, using methods like voxel-based morphometry. National high-performance computing repositories could be enabled through cross-jurisdictional data sharing agreements with hospitals and private providers, using frameworks such as the Brain Data Imaging Structure (BDIS) or open-source platforms.37 Our future focus is on expanding neuroimaging and genetic biobanks for larger studies.
