Protection from Chronic Musculoskeletal Pain: A Scoping Review
Authors: Barry E Moore, Leon Schleidgen, Chan P Hang, Hung C Y Chelsea, Martin Rabey, Leo Ng, Kevin E K Chai, Phillip Melton, Gill Cowen, Darren Beales, Niamh Moloney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and ObjectiveResearch in the management of chronic musculoskeletal pain often focuses on negative factors associated with poor outcomes. In contrast, strengths‐based approaches attempt to identify factors associated with positive outcomes, referred to as protective factors. Significant potential value lies in understanding protective factors in chronic musculoskeletal pain to deliver strengths‐based care. This scoping review aimed to identify protective factors associated with lower pain‐related disability or higher physical function in adults with chronic musculoskeletal pain.Databases and Data TreatmentSearches were conducted across five databases: Medline, CINAHL Ultimate, Embase PsycINFO and SPORTDiscus. Research Screener was used for abstract screening.ResultsSeventy‐nine articles met inclusion criteria for data extraction. Overall methodological quality was low and most studies were cross‐sectional. A range of psychological, lifestyle, social and physical factors were associated with lower pain‐related disability/higher physical function. Self‐efficacy was the most frequently reported factor. Other psychological factors included psychological resources, coping strategies, psychological health, acceptance, cognitive factors, perceptions of control and motivational factors. Lifestyle factors included health promotion practices, Mediterranean diet, sleep and physical activity. Identified social factors were socioeconomic and interpersonal factors. Physical factors included exercise capacity, walking performance, strength and range of motion.ConclusionsThese factors align with the biopsychosocial understanding of pain and offer a balancing point to the prevailing deficits‐based focus. The findings can be used as a unique resource for clinicians, researchers and educators working in the field of chronic musculoskeletal pain and may assist in developing strength‐based approaches to musculoskeletal pain care.Significance StatementThis scoping review maps available evidence on psychological, lifestyle, social and physical factors that have shown an association with lower pain‐related disability or higher physical function. This offers a contrasting yet complementary perspective to the prevailing focus on deficits in the literature and shines light on factors that could underpin a strengths‐based approach for chronic musculoskeletal pain.Keywords: chronic musculoskeletal pain, pain‐related disability, physical function, protective factors, scoping review, self‐efficacy, semi‐automated screening, strengths‐based approach

Introduction

Chronic musculoskeletal pain (CMP) is common, affecting an estimated 1.71 billion people globally (Cieza et al. 2021). Furthermore, CMP is recognised as a leading cause of disability, with low back pain, neck pain, osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis contributing a combined 107.4 million years of life lived with disability in 2019 (Cieza et al. 2021).

Evidence‐based pain management is dominated by the biopsychosocial model (Driscoll et al. 2021), recognising the multidimensional nature of pain (Engel 1977). Research often focuses on deficits‐based biopsychosocial factors associated with pain and disability, for example, decreased muscle strength, depressive symptoms and poor social support (Dunn et al. 2024; Kiesel et al. 2024). Deficits may include risk factors associated with a poor outcome (e.g., pain catastrophising) or impairments (e.g., reduced range of motion) or pathology (Dunn et al. 2024; Kiesel et al. 2024). Many interventions targeting these factors are deficits‐based approaches (Pulla and Francis 2014) as they focus on deficits, such as maladaptive beliefs and behaviours (Fenn and Byrne 2013), and emphasise correcting or addressing the deficit. Although such interventions show positive results (Driscoll et al. 2021), effect sizes are mostly small (Sanabria‐Mazo et al. 2023). The significant impact of CMP emphasises a need for better management strategies.

A strengths‐based approach (SBA) is a person‐centred approach explicitly focusing on a person's protective biopsychosocial factors to overcome adversity (Pulla and Francis 2014; Simmons 2012). These approaches align with the Salutogenic Model of Health, which was one of the first models to offer a resilience perspective on health, shifting focus from disease to factors that promote well‐being (Antonovsky 1979). Since then, a number of models, such as the Broaden and Build theory (Fredrickson 2004) and Seligman's proposed field of Positive Health (Seligman 2008), have advocated for an SBA for health by recognising the potential for protective factors in optimising well‐being. For the purpose of this review, we have defined protective factors as ‘biopsychosocial characteristics, internal or external to the individual, that facilitate recovery or reduce the likelihood of negative outcomes in the presence of adversity or promote positive outcomes independent of adversity’ (Hirschauer and Scheerer 2021; Oliveros et al. 2022).

Delivery of SBAs requires assessment of an individual's protective factors, so that they can be leveraged in management (Simmons 2012). Despite calls for recognition of protective factors (Goubert and Trompetter 2017), there has been limited research on their application in CMP. Conversely, there has been extensive research into the association of deficits with the development and persistence of CMP (Dunn et al. 2024). Importantly, SBAs do not ignore deficits, but seek to balance them with strengths to create a holistic picture of the person (Simmons 2012). Given the prevailing focus on deficits in the literature, the aim of this review is to identify a range of psychological, lifestyle, social and physical factors associated with lower pain‐related disability and higher physical function that could provide balance to the established deficits‐based literature and underpin a strengths‐based approach for chronic musculoskeletal pain.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This scoping review was conducted following guidance from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) (Peters et al. 2020) and the methodological framework outlined by Arksey and O'Malley (2005). A scoping review was deemed to be an appropriate methodology as it sought to identify a range of available evidence and key factors related to pain‐related disability and physical function in CMP. We utilised scoping review methodology as scoping reviews attempt to determine the scope of the body of literature on a topic, give an indication of the volume of studies and provide an overview of the content (Munn et al. 2018). Furthermore, scoping reviews are indicated when the aim is to attempt to identify key characteristics or factors related to a concept and to understand how research is conducted on a given topic (Munn et al. 2018). We also sought to map a broad array of factors where the existing evidence base is still limited or emerging, which would be facilitated by the use of scoping review methodology rather than systematic review methodology (Munn et al. 2018).

Protocol Registration and Deviations to the Protocol

The protocol was registered on The Open Science Framework Registries (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/VT65S). Three notable changes were adopted from the initial protocol. First, it was decided to add key terms to the search strategy relating to five common musculoskeletal conditions (low back pain, neck pain, rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis and gout) as per the Global Burden of Disease Study (Gill et al. 2023), as well as protective factors that had been identified from background literature searches to narrow the results. Due to our initial searches returning a large amount of potentially relevant information sources, for feasibility reasons it was decided to focus on outcomes relating to pain‐related disability and physical function only and to exclude qualitative studies and studies measuring changes in disability or physical function following interventions that targeted protective factors. Studies focusing on other pain‐related outcomes will be reported elsewhere. To improve transparency and identify the gaps in research quality, we decided to conduct a critical appraisal of the included studies, although this is an optional reporting element according to JBI (Peters et al. 2020).

Eligibility Criteria

This scoping review utilises the Population, Context and Concept format for inclusion (Peters et al. 2020).

Population

Human adults in any setting.

Concept

Protective factors with a specific focus on associations with a favourable outcome in CMP. The concept of this scoping review is protective factors demonstrating a statistically significant association (p < 0.05) with lower pain‐related disability or higher physical function.

Context

To be included in this scoping review, studies could be from any country or culture published in English. Prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case–control studies, cross‐sectional studies, case series, individual case reports, systematic reviews and both experimental and quasi‐experimental designs (randomised controlled trials, non‐randomised controlled trials, before‐and‐after studies and interrupted time‐series studies), narrative and opinion papers and clinical practice guidelines published since 1979 were eligible. The cut‐off year of 1979 was chosen as this was when the Salutogenic Model of Health was first proposed, where the emphasis is on factors that promote well‐being (Antonovsky 1979).

Exclusion Criteria

Studies were excluded if they: focused on protective/positive factors unrelated to pain‐related disability or physical function; were not investigating musculoskeletal conditions, were experimental pain studies or qualitative studies, only considered risk factors or negative factors; used outcome measures that have not been validated for measuring pain‐related disability or physical function; focused on psychometric properties of questionnaires/measurements or were theses or conference abstracts. Intervention studies were excluded when their analysis was focused on evaluating the effect of an intervention, e.g., measuring change in disability after a positive psychology intervention. However, randomised and non‐randomised controlled trials were eligible for inclusion if they reported associations between protective factors and baseline or post‐intervention pain‐related disability or physical function.

Search Strategy

A preliminary search of MEDLINE, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews and JBI Evidence Synthesis was conducted and no current or underway systematic reviews or scoping reviews on the topic were identified. To find relevant sources of information, the following databases were searched: MEDLINE (OVID), CINAHL Ultimate (EBSCO), Embase (OVID), PsycINFO (OVID) and SPORTDiscus (EBSCO). Search strategies were drafted in consultation with the Curtin University Health Sciences Librarian (Methods S1), with key words reflecting the population, concept and context. Given the broad nature of this scoping review, early iterations of the search strategy produced unmanageably large numbers of returned results. As such, the search strategy development process underwent multiple trials until a consensus was reached between the research team in consultation with the librarian to optimise the results while maintaining feasibility. The process undertaken is outlined in Methods S2. The search strategy was modified as relevant for each included database. The final search took place on 24/06/2024.

Sources of Evidence Selection

The titles and abstracts from the final search results were exported into EndNote (Version 21, Clarivate Analytics) where duplicates were removed. Titles and abstracts were subsequently exported to Research Screener, a tool that utilises machine learning to semi‐automate the abstract screening process of review articles (Chai et al. 2021). Pre‐identified ‘seed abstracts’ (Methods S3) that the research team deemed to be appropriate to answer the research question were imported into Research Screener. These seed abstracts are used to initially train Research Screener to recognise potentially relevant studies. Subsequently, Research Screener ranked all abstracts in order of predicted relevance, and two reviewers (BEM and CPH) independently screened the top 50 abstracts (cycle 1). Following cycle 1, Research Screener re‐ranks the unread abstracts to determine the next 50 most relevant abstracts (cycle 2), and the process continues in cycles of 50 abstracts. Screening continued until both reviewers were confident that all potentially relevant studies had been identified, with Research Screener returning five consecutive rounds of screening 50 abstracts (i.e., 250 abstracts) without additional relevant abstracts. Conflicts were resolved within Research Screener through a consultative process involving the two reviewers and an independent third reviewer (LS). All abstracts deemed relevant from this process then underwent full‐text review by two reviewers (BEM and LS) independently, with a third blinded reviewer (MR) available to resolve disagreements. Additionally, reference lists of sources for full‐text review were screened for relevant sources to be added for data charting.

Data Charting

Two authors (BEM and LS) charted the data from eligible information sources independently, using a standardised data charting form developed for this scoping review, with any disagreements resolved through discussion. The data charting process was undertaken following guidance from Arksey and O'Malley (2005). The form was piloted by two reviewers on two included studies prior to the data‐charting process and modified as necessary.

The following data were extracted, determined through consultation with the research team: author(s), title, year published, context (country of origin, study setting, study design, population, sample size, age, gender, duration of symptoms), concepts (protective factors), measures for protective factors, outcome measures for primary outcomes (pain‐related disability or physical function), mean (SD) of measures protective factors & disability or physical function, measures of association for univariate, bivariate & multivariate analyses, results of univariate, bivariate & multivariate association, i.e., correlation coefficient, beta coefficient, 95% CI and p‐value, other analyses (e.g., latent class analysis) and relevant results. Where authors reported F‐Values in regression models, they were converted to ηp2 using the formula ηp2=F×dfeffectF×dfeffect+dferror (Lakens 2013).

For this scoping review, we have used the term ‘association’ to present results of potentially protective factors; however, we acknowledge that without meta‐analysis, a definite strength of association cannot be determined. As this scoping review set out to identify factors that showed associations with higher physical function or lower disability, studies demonstrating no association were not included, and as such meta‐analysis would be beyond the scope of this review.

Double extraction was carried out on up to 20% of information sources and checked for agreement. Good agreement was defined as reaching 90% consensus on study characteristics, outcome measures, participant demographics and findings and interpretations. Good agreement was achieved, so the two reviewers (BEM and LS) then extracted the remaining 80% of data independently.

Methodological Quality

Methodological quality was evaluated using the Newcastle Ottawa Scale (Wells et al. 2000) for longitudinal studies and a modified version for cross‐sectional studies, which has been used in previous systematic reviews (Patra et al. 2015). For cross‐sectional studies, a score of 5/7 or higher was deemed high quality, 3–4/7 moderate quality and 0–2 low quality. For longitudinal cohort studies, scores of 6/9 or higher were deemed high quality, 4–5/9 moderate quality and 0–3/9 low quality. These cutoffs were determined through discussion and group consensus, as no reported cut‐off scores exist for the Newcastle Ottawa Scale. The Risk of Bias in Systematic Reviews (ROBIS) tool (Whiting et al. 2016) was used to evaluate risk of bias in included systematic reviews, followed by an assessment for certainty of evidence using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach (Prasad 2024). Aligned to the study aim, the explorative nature of the research and principles of scoping reviews, we did not exclude studies based on their methodological quality.

Two independent reviewers (BEM and MR) carried out quality assessment on 20% of included sources in two separate rounds, then checked for agreement. Good agreement was defined as consensus of 80%. Agreement was 66% after the first round, so four reviewers (BEM, MR, DB and NM) discussed and clarified interpretation of the criteria to ensure consistency of interpretation and rating. Following this, a second round was undertaken, and agreement was 85%, so the remaining 80% was carried out by BEM. As some studies included multiple factors, critical appraisal was carried out for each individual factor.

Data Analysis

After extracting data from the included studies, data were synthesised and presented narratively, with additional use of tables and figures. Studies were grouped based on the context to describe the range of populations being investigated, the geographical distribution and range of settings and the research methods used. Studies were also grouped thematically based on the concept (i.e., the positive or protective factor being investigated), with descriptive analysis summarising the findings related to the association of identified positive of protective factors with pain‐related disability and physical function.

Consumer Engagement

The development of this scoping review involved consumer engagement according to the GRIPP2 checklist for reporting public and patient involvement in research (Staniszewska et al. 2017). As part of a wider research project, a consumer advisory panel was formed, consisting of a Specialist Musculoskeletal Physiotherapist (as awarded by the Australian College of Physiotherapists), an individual with lived experience of musculoskeletal pain and an individual who has recovered from musculoskeletal pain. Panel members were asked for input on the overview of the topic and its relevance to the community, specific research questions as they were developed and the methods used to address research questions. Panel members were also consulted on the interpretation of this scoping review's results.

Results

Selection of Sources of Evidence/Study Selection

A total of 48,521 sources were identified through searching the five databases, seed abstracts and reference list screening (Figure 1). Following this, 13,364 records were removed as duplicates, 1484 by manual title screening and 17 were marked ineligible by Research Screener (short abstracts less than 100 characters). Manual title and abstract screening was conducted by BEM and CPH using Research Screener, until five consecutive cycles returned no potentially relevant articles. In total, 3360 titles and abstracts were screened at this stage, of which 3044 were excluded. The remaining 30,296 were not screened. During the title and abstract screening process, BEM and CPH disagreed on the inclusion/exclusion of 14% (n = 481) of titles and abstracts for full text review, which were resolved by engaging a third reviewer (LS). A total of 135 full texts were assessed for eligibility, with reviewers disagreeing on 5.2% (n = 7) of studies, which were resolved by engaging a third reviewer (MR). A total of 56 full texts were excluded as not meeting the inclusion criteria. Finally, 79 studies were deemed eligible and included in the review.

Participants

A total of 19,897 participants were included across all studies: minimum 26 (Hankin and Killian 2004); maximum 5724 (Alamam et al. 2021). Two studies did not report the proportion of males to females (Alamam et al. 2021; Hankin and Killian 2004). Of the remaining 77 studies reporting gender distribution (n = 14,147 participants, 71.1%), there were 4575 males (32.3%) and 9572 females (67.7%). Mean age was reported in 71 studies (n = 12,887 participants, 64.3%). The combined mean age for these studies was 54.7 years. Symptom duration ranged from 2 months to 76.7 years.

The most common conditions represented were chronic low back pain (n = 26 studies, n = 9849 participants, 49.5%) and hip or knee osteoarthritis (n = 19 studies, 3141 participants, 15.8%).

Outcome Measurement Instruments Employed

Thirty‐one different data collection instruments for pain‐related disability or physical function were employed across the studies. The most used measures were the Arthritis Impact Measurement Scale (Davidson 2014) (n = 13 studies, 16.5%) and the Roland‐Morris Disability Questionnaire (Roland and Fairbank 2000) (n = 13 studies, 16.3%). There was significant heterogeneity in the measures used for assessing protective factors (see Tables 2 and 3). Eighty‐four different measures were reported, and self‐efficacy alone was measured with 14 different instruments.

Results for Protective Factors Demonstrating Associations With Lower Pain‐Related Disability or Higher Physical Function

A range of protective factors demonstrating significant associations with lower pain‐related disability and/or higher physical function were identified. Protective factors were grouped into categories informed by the biopsychosocial model (Engel 1977). Factors were organised primarily on whether they reflected psychological, social, lifestyle or physical factors as they reflect commonly used biopsychosocial domains. Sub‐categorisation was undertaken to improve clarity and to enable meaningful synthesis across studies reporting conceptually similar factors. This was developed iteratively through discussion within the research team and with reference to biopsychosocial literature. For this review, factors in the psychological domain have been further sub‐categorised into psychological resources (nine factors), psychological health (four factors), cognitive factors (seven factors), coping strategies (five factors), perceptions of control (seven factors), acceptance (four factors) and motivational factors (one factor). Factors in the lifestyle domain have been further sub‐categorised into lifestyle (three factors) and physical activity (three factors). Factors in the social domain have been sub‐categorised into socioeconomic (five factors) and interpersonal factors (four factors). Factors in the physical domain (seven factors) have not been sub‐categorised.

Factors identified within each sub‐category are presented in Figures 2 and 3. Detailed definitions for each factor are provided in ResultsS6. Where possible, definitions were taken directly from the included study. Where multiple studies report a single factor, the definition that was deemed most representative was chosen. Where no definition was provided in the included studies, a definition was sought from a peer‐reviewed publication that conceptualises that protective factor, with the corresponding reference included in ResultsS6. Table 2 provides the results for the identification of protective factors associated with lower disability. Table 3 provides the results for the identification of protective factors associated with higher physical function. Full details on measures of association, strength of association and statistical significance, are provided in the appendices for factors investigated in univariate and bivariate analyses (ResultsS7), in multivariate analysis (ResultsS8) and in other analyses (e.g., cluster analysis) (ResultsS9).

Psychological Factors

Psychological Resources

Cross‐Sectional

Resilience was the most frequently reported psychological resource, identified in six studies, demonstrating associations with lower pain‐related disability (low quality) (Ahmed et al. 2019; Bartley, Hossain, et al. 2019; Booker et al. 2020; Chen and Jackson 2018; Ramirez‐Maestre et al. 2012; Ramirez‐Maestre et al. 2014) and higher physical function (low quality) (Ramirez‐Maestre et al. 2012). Optimism was reported in four studies, demonstrating associations with lower pain‐related disability (low quality) (Bartley, Hossain, et al. 2019; Wright et al. 2011) and an association with higher physical function (low quality) (Verhiel et al. 2019). One study (moderate quality) conducted a cluster analysis to identify different resilience profiles and between‐group differences in pain‐related disability, with optimism being included as a measure of multi‐system resilience (Bartley, Palit, et al. 2019). Those in the high resilience cluster demonstrated lower levels of pain‐related disability than those in the low resilience cluster.

Psychological resources such as hope (Bartley, Palit, et al. 2019), psychological flexibility (Trainor et al. 2019), hardiness (Kee 2003), self‐compassion (Wren et al. 2012) as well as gratitude (Verhiel et al. 2019) and vitality (Wright et al. 2008) were reported in one study each. Mindfulness was reported in two studies (Rezaei and Hassanzadeh 2019; Verhiel et al. 2019). Gratitude, vitality (both low quality) and hardiness (moderate quality) were associated with higher physical function, while psychological flexibility and self‐compassion (both moderate quality) were associated with lower pain‐related disability. Mindfulness demonstrated associations with both lower pain‐related disability and higher physical function. One study (moderate quality) found that those scoring higher in hope had lower levels of disability than those with low levels of hope (Bartley, Palit, et al. 2019).

Psychological Health

Cross‐Sectional

Positive affect was the most frequently reported measure of psychological health and demonstrated cross‐sectional associations in three studies (Bartley, Palit, et al. 2019; Furlong et al. 2010; Hassett et al. 2016). Overall, the association between positive affect and lower pain‐related disability was of moderate quality. One study (Furlong et al. 2010) demonstrated an association with lower pain‐related disability, while three studies evaluating between‐group differences (Bartley, Palit, et al. 2019; Hassett et al. 2016; Sambuco et al. 2022) found that those scoring higher on positive affect reported lower levels of pain‐related disability than those who scored low on positive affect or high on negative affect. Positive psychological well‐being and satisfaction with life were reported in five studies, demonstrating associations with lower pain‐related disability (low quality) (Bartley, Hossain, et al. 2019; Schleicher et al. 2005) and higher physical function (low quality) (Helminen et al. 2016; Verhiel et al. 2019). In their cluster analysis, Bartley, Palit, et al. (2019) (moderate quality) found that those with higher scores on measures of psychological well‐being reported lower levels of pain‐related disability than those with low scores. Good mental health was associated with lower pain‐related disability (low quality) (Abbott et al. 2010).

Longitudinal

Positive affect (moderate quality) (Fisher et al. 2004; Sambuco et al. 2022) was associated with lower pain‐related disability at 2 year follow‐up. Satisfaction with life (low quality) (Helminen et al. 2016) and better mental health at baseline (low quality) (Sharma et al. 2003) were associated with higher physical function at 1‐year follow‐up and lower pain‐related disability at 3‐year follow‐up, respectively.

Cognitive Factors

Cross‐Sectional

Self‐efficacy, the most frequently reported factor across all domains, demonstrated cross‐sectional associations with lower pain‐related disability or higher physical function in 31 studies and was the only factor that included high‐quality studies (ResultsS3 and ResultsS4). Twenty studies (Abbott et al. 2010; Ahmed et al. 2019; Alamam et al. 2021; Ayre and Tyson 2001; Brister et al. 2006; Chen and Jackson 2018; Duray et al. 2018; Ferrari et al. 2016; Harrison 2004; Hott et al. 2022; La Touche et al. 2019; Levin et al. 1996; Odole et al. 2019; Rodríguez‐García et al. 2020; Taylor et al. 2006; Thompson et al. 2010; Trainor et al. 2019; Turner et al. 2005; Varela and Van Asselt 2022; Woby et al. 2007) reported associations with lower pain‐related disability (moderate quality), while 12 studies (Brister et al. 2006; Buckelew et al. 1995; Dwyer 1997; Ferreira‐Valente et al. 2014; Gaines et al. 2002; Helminen et al. 2016; Pells et al. 2008; Porter et al. 2008; Schiaffino and Revenson 1992; Somers et al. 2010; Somers et al. 2014; Strahl et al. 2000; Wright et al. 2008) reported associations with higher physical function (moderate quality). One study (low quality) conducted a cluster analysis, finding that those scoring higher on self‐efficacy measures reported lower levels of pain‐related disability compared to those scoring lower (Nishigami et al. 2021). There were several different operationalisations for self‐efficacy (e.g., self‐efficacy for pain management, self‐efficacy for functional tasks, self‐efficacy for overeating), making comparisons across studies difficult.

Other cognitive factors such as competence perceptions (Podlog et al. 2019), perceived age (Booker et al. 2020), personal beliefs in a just world (McParland and Knussen 2010), pain outcome expectancy (Abbott et al. 2010), cognitive appraisals of challenge (Ramirez‐Maestre et al. 2008) and cognitive function (Sambuco et al. 2022) were reported in one study each. Perceived age, personal beliefs in a just world and pain outcome expectancy (all low quality) demonstrated associations with lower pain‐related disability, and cognitive appraisals of challenge demonstrated associations with both lower pain‐related disability and higher physical function (both low quality).

Longitudinal

Self‐efficacy was examined longitudinally in ten studies (moderate quality) (Altmaier et al. 1993; Buckelew et al. 1996; Demmelmaier et al. 2010; Dobkin et al. 2010; Gere et al. 2014; Helminen et al. 2016; Keedy et al. 2014; Rabey et al. 2017; Schiaffino et al. 1991; Sharma et al. 2003). Baseline self‐efficacy was associated with lower pain‐related disability at a range of follow‐up time points including 3 weeks, 1 month, 6 months, 1 year and 3 years. Self‐efficacy was associated with higher physical function at 6 weeks, 1 year and 14 months. Baseline cognitive function (moderate quality) was associated with lower pain‐related disability at 2‐year follow‐up, while baseline competence perceptions (low quality) were associated with lower pain‐related disability at 6‐week follow‐up.

Coping Strategies

Cross‐Sectional

Coping strategies were reported in five studies. These included coping through humour (Verhiel et al. 2019), active coping (Ramirez‐Maestre et al. 2008, 2012), coping through ignoring sensations or distraction (Rapp et al. 2000) and task persistence (Ferreira‐Valente et al. 2014). Task persistence (low quality), coping through humour and ignoring and distracting coping (both moderate quality) demonstrated associations with higher physical function, while active coping demonstrated an association with both higher physical function (moderate quality) and lower pain‐related disability (low quality).

Longitudinal

Problem‐solving coping (low quality) (Schiaffino et al. 1991) was associated with lower pain‐related disability at 14 months.

Perceptions of Control

Cross‐Sectional

Pain control beliefs were the most frequently reported factor relating to perceptions of control, reporting cross‐sectional associations in eight studies (Abbott et al. 2010; Rapp et al. 2000; Scharloo et al. 1998; Schiaffino and Revenson 1992; Spinhoven et al. 1989; Summers et al. 2019; Tan et al. 2005; Woby et al. 2007), demonstrating associations with both lower pain‐related disability and higher physical function (both moderate quality). Health locus of control was investigated in two studies, with associations with lower pain‐related disability being observed for chance health locus of control (Fapojuwo et al. 2023) and internal locus of control (Furlong et al. 2010) (both low quality). Life control (low quality) (Oraison and Kennedy 2021) and having an incremental implicit theory of pain (moderate quality) (Summers et al. 2019) demonstrated associations with lower pain‐related disability. Perceived ability to reduce pain (low quality) (Abbott et al. 2010; Rapp et al. 2000; Woby et al. 2007) and pain‐control and rational thinking (moderate quality) (Beckham et al. 1991; Keefe et al. 1987) were associated with both higher physical function and lower pain‐related disability.

Acceptance

Cross‐Sectional

Pain acceptance was reported in four studies. Three studies (Ramirez‐Maestre et al. 2012; Ramirez‐Maestre et al. 2014; Wright et al. 2011) reported associations between pain acceptance and lower pain‐related disability (moderate quality), and one study (Ramirez‐Maestre et al. 2012) reported an association with higher physical function (moderate quality). One study reported on two constructs underlying pain acceptance (activity engagement and pain willingness), both demonstrating associations with lower pain‐related disability (moderate quality) (Thompson and Woby 2017). Psychological acceptance (moderate quality) was associated with lower pain‐related disability in a single study (Trainor et al. 2019).

Motivational Factors

Longitudinal

Two stages of readiness to change (action and maintenance) were assessed in one study (Hankin and Killian 2004), both of which were associated with lower pain‐related disability (low quality) at baseline only.

Lifestyle Factors

Lifestyle

Cross‐Sectional

Adherence to a Mediterranean diet (low quality) (Ingegnoli et al. 2020) was associated with lower pain‐related disability, while adopting health promotion practices (low quality) (Kee 2003) was associated with higher physical function.

Longitudinal

Self‐reported sleep quality (moderate quality) (Wippert et al. 2017) was associated with lower pain‐related disability at baseline only.

Physical Activity

Cross‐Sectional

Higher step count (low quality) (Wright et al. 2008) was associated with higher physical function.

Longitudinal

Higher volume of aerobic exercise at baseline (low quality) (Sharma et al. 2003) and intensity of leisure‐time physical activity (light (low quality) and moderate to vigorous activity (moderate quality)) (Pinto et al. 2014) were associated with lower pain‐related disability at 3‐year and 12‐month follow‐up, respectively. Frequency of exercise (> 2 times per week) (moderate quality) (Helminen et al. 2016) was associated with higher physical function at 1‐year follow‐up.

Social Factors

Socioeconomic Factors

Cross‐Sectional

Education level was the most frequently reported socioeconomic factor, being investigated in seven studies. Higher years of education were associated with lower levels of pain‐related disability (overall low quality) (Booker et al. 2020; Chen and Jackson 2018; Juhakoski et al. 2008; Kim et al. 2014; Rabey et al. 2017) and higher physical function (moderate quality) (Juhakoski et al. 2008). Having a university education specifically was associated with lower pain‐related disability (moderate quality) (Trainor et al. 2019). Being currently employed was associated with lower pain‐related disability in three studies (low quality) (Booker et al. 2020; Rabey et al. 2017; Trainor et al. 2019). Higher income (low quality) was associated with lower pain‐related disability (Booker et al. 2020), while higher socioeconomic status (low quality) (Kee 2003), was associated with higher physical function.

Longitudinal

Greater years of education was associated with lower pain‐related disability at 6.6 months (moderate quality) (Fliesser et al. 2017) and 1‐year follow‐up (low quality) (Rabey et al. 2017). Higher job position (moderate quality) (Fliesser et al. 2017) and being employed (low quality) (Rabey et al. 2017) were associated with lower pain‐related disability at 6.6‐month and 1‐year follow‐up, respectively.

Interpersonal Factors

Cross‐Sectional

Social support showed cross‐sectional associations with lower pain‐related disability in two studies (overall moderate quality) (Bartley, Hossain, et al. 2019; Oraison and Kennedy 2021) and higher physical function in three studies (overall moderate quality) (Dwyer 1997; Ferreira‐Valente et al. 2014; Kee 2003). Bartley, Palit, et al. (2019) (moderate quality) included social support as a component of multisystem resilience, with those with high social support being included in the high resilience group; this group demonstrated lower levels of pain‐related disability than the low resilience group in the cluster analysis. Marital status (Booker et al. 2020) and relationship cohesion (Campbell et al. 2012) (both low quality) were associated with lower pain‐related disability.

Longitudinal

Social support (moderate quality) (Sharma et al. 2003) and marital status (being married) (low quality) (Fisher et al. 2004) were associated with lower pain‐related disability at 3 year and 2 year follow‐up, respectively. Spouse confidence in self‐efficacy (moderate quality) (Gere et al. 2014) was associated with both lower pain‐related disability and higher physical function at 6 month and 1 year follow‐up.

Physical Factors

Cross‐Sectional

Physical measures, such as greater leg extensor (quadriceps femoris, gastrocnemius and soleus) power (Juhakoski et al. 2008) and greater range of motion (hip, lower back, trunk) (Duray et al. 2018; Juhakoski et al. 2008; La Touche et al. 2019) were associated with lower pain‐related disability and higher physical function (all low quality). Greater low back strength (La Touche et al. 2019) and better performance in the dynamic sit‐up test and lateral side bend test (both low quality) (Duray et al. 2018) were associated with lower pain‐related disability. Higher exercise capacity measured by the 6 min walk test was associated with higher physical function (moderate quality) and lower pain‐related disability (low quality) (Juhakoski et al. 2008; Somers et al. 2014). Quicker walking speed on the 10‐m walk test (low quality) was associated with higher physical function (low quality). Better walking performance on the timed‐up‐and‐go test was associated with lower pain‐related disability (moderate quality) and higher physical function (low quality) (Juhakoski et al. 2008).

Longitudinal

Greater low back strength (low quality) (Hutten et al. 2001) was associated with greater improvements in disability at 7‐week follow‐up.

Consumer Feedback on the Results

The consumer advisory panel was consulted to provide feedback on the study findings. A face‐to‐face meeting was initially held with one consumer with lived experience of musculoskeletal pain and two authors (BEM and MR), followed by a Microsoft Teams meeting with the remaining two consumers (Specialist Musculoskeletal Physiotherapist and individual who has recovered from musculoskeletal pain) and three authors (BEM, MR and NM). Feedback was positive, with panel members being in broad agreement that the findings were relevant and meaningful, and that SBAs could be an acceptable approach in managing CMP from a lived experience and clinician perspective.

Discussion

We identified 79 articles reporting associations of protective factors with pain‐related disability and/or physical function in CMP. These incorporate psychological resources, psychological health, cognitive factors, coping strategies, perceptions of control, acceptance, motivation, lifestyle, physical activity, exercise capacity and fitness, socioeconomic and interpersonal factors. The output provides a unique resource documenting protective factors for clinicians, researchers and educators.

The Salutogenic Model of Health offers a contrasting, yet complimentary, perspective to the deficits‐based model commonly used in healthcare (Antonovsky 1979). The model emphasises focusing on factors promoting health, rather than those causing disease (Antonovsky 1979). Since the Salutogenic Model was proposed, there has been increasing awareness of protective factors and calls for their consideration in research and treatment in pain (Goubert and Trompetter 2017). This aligns with the emergence of the framework of positive health (Seligman 2008) which is underpinned by the assumption that protective factors and risk factors need to be considered equally to obtain positive outcomes (Goubert and Trompetter 2017), and this is consistent with SBAs (Simmons 2012).

Other models also recognise the role of protective factors in achieving positive outcomes (Goubert and Trompetter 2017). For example, the psychological flexibility model outlines how cognitive processes, such as acceptance, assist individuals in broadening their range of responses to pain. Furthermore, the Broaden‐and‐Build theory suggests that positive emotions can widen people's attention and thinking, in addition to undoing the deleterious effects of negative emotions, building resilience and personal resources and triggering upward spirals towards achieving wellbeing in the future (Fredrickson 2004).

Despite the emergence of these models and the recognition that CMP is a biopsychosocial experience (Driscoll et al. 2021) identification of factors related to CMP often focuses on deficits (Dunn et al. 2024). Indeed, a meta‐analysis identified 35 factors associated with the development of CMP, which were mostly psychological or psychosocial risk factors (Dunn et al. 2024). Furthermore, evaluation of expert knowledge on important factors in low back pain (Cholewicki et al. 2019) catalogued numerous contributing elements, highlighting the multidimensional nature of these conditions. However, while research has identified some positive factors, demonstrating an awareness of their role in pain, the focus is largely on deficits. Thus, while important, it points to a propensity for a deficits‐based view of CMP. Importantly. central to delivering SBAs is finding balance between strengths and deficits (Simmons 2012). Our review, while scoping in nature, suggests an abundance of potential protective factors that could be integrated into future research on the biopsychosocial nature of CMP. This would align with the recognition of both strengths and deficits, consistent with strengths‐based principles (Simmons 2012).

This review identified a range of psychological factors that could be important components of SBAs for CMP. Self‐efficacy was the most frequently reported factor, identified in 41 studies, showing associations across a range of conditions and time points in longitudinal analyses. Self‐efficacy is proposed to be key in influencing behaviour in CMP, assisting in confronting pain‐related fear (Bunzli et al. 2017), and as such could be a potent component of SBAs. An important consideration in the frequent and strong associations between high self‐efficacy and low pain‐related disability is that these associations may be due, at least partly, to the content overlap in self‐efficacy and disability questionnaires, for example, items about performing household tasks on both the Pain Self‐Efficacy Questionnaire (Nicholas 2007) and Roland‐Morris Disability Questionnaire (Roland and Fairbank 2000).

Other psychological factors such as resilience and optimism are thought to have important influences on continued engagement in valued activities (Boselie and Peters 2023). Leveraging positive cognitions such as perceived controllability of pain may influence an individual's representation of pain, decreasing threat and pain‐related fear, assisting in re‐engagement and reducing pain‐related disability (Bunzli et al. 2017). The range of psychological factors identified and their importance in confronting pain and continued engagement highlights their potential as important components of SBAs for CMP.

Socioeconomic factors might contribute as much as 40% to health outcomes (Magnan 2017). This review identified a range of socioeconomic and interpersonal factors that could be protective in CMP. This aligns with previous reviews showing that social support positively influences outcomes, has a stress‐buffering effect and facilitates coping in pain (Che et al. 2018). Although the biopsychosocial model is well accepted, social components are often neglected (Potthoff et al. 2025). Social prescribing, involving connecting individuals with social needs with non‐medical social supports in the community such as peer support groups, has been implemented in various settings with the aim to improve health and well‐being (Bickerdike et al. 2017). Such interventions may have merit in enabling individuals to utilise existing social resources as part of an SBA to pain care.

In addition to social determinants, lifestyle factors play a significant role in promoting health outcomes. The benefits of healthy lifestyle behaviours such as exercise are well documented (Fiuza‐Luces et al. 2013). Exercise interventions show small to moderate effects in improving physical function in chronic pain (Geneen et al. 2017) and are considered low‐cost and lower‐risk alternatives to pharmacological therapies (Fiuza‐Luces et al. 2013). Exercise interventions are consistently recommended in high‐quality clinical practice guidelines for musculoskeletal conditions (Lin et al. 2020), can be adapted to the needs of an individual and can be completed independently (Geneen et al. 2017). Many physical factors identified in this review such as strength and exercise capacity are modifiable through lifestyle interventions such as exercise (Dibben et al. 2024). The upward spiral of positive behaviour change (Van Cappellen et al. 2018) builds on the Broaden‐and‐Build theory. It proposes that positive emotions experienced while undertaking positive lifestyle behaviours can trigger upward spiral dynamics of continued engagement and positive emotions (Van Cappellen et al. 2018). Therefore, given health and lifestyle factors contribute an estimated 30% to health outcomes (Magnan 2017) and that physical factors modifiable through lifestyle interventions may be protective, strategies to optimise healthy lifestyle behaviours that are valued by and meaningful to people with CMP would be valuable in a SBA.

Strengths, Limitations and Methodological Considerations

This review aligned with established scoping review procedures and reporting guidelines to ensure methodological rigour. Research Screener enabled screening large numbers of titles and abstracts while also maintaining rigour (Chai et al. 2021). A broad range of pain conditions was included, increasing the generalisability of the findings. However, as most studies were cross‐sectional it is not possible to determine causative relationships. Among longitudinal analyses, identified factors varied, which makes it difficult to draw conclusions on which factors consistently show associations with improved outcomes over time. Further longitudinal research is required to investigate these associations in more detail. Most identified protective factors were psychological, which may indicate weighting towards psychological factors in the broader literature and in our search strategy, as well as comparatively fewer studies investigating the protective nature of social, lifestyle, or physical factors. We acknowledge that some papers reflecting the broad literature available may have been excluded if they measured protective factors as outcome measures for randomised controlled trials, as we only included baseline or post intervention associations and not changes due to an intervention. Methodological heterogeneity adds complexity in drawing comparisons between studies and factors. We did not select factors based on the strength of the associations but have provided details ResultsS7‐S9 for interested readers. Furthermore, only factors that showed a positive association with lower pain‐related disability or higher physical function were included. To further evaluate the strength of association for each factor, a systematic review and meta‐analysis would be warranted. Lastly, protective factors were included based on statistical significance using p values. We acknowledge this limitation; however, the methodological quality and reporting within the included studies precluded the use of more robust statistical approaches. Furthermore, we acknowledge that filtering findings using statistical significance may result in factors with mixed or context‐dependent associations being overlooked.

Conclusions

Our findings provide a unique review of the breadth of biopsychosocial protective factors associated with lower pain‐related disability and higher physical function. This review summarises a large suite of research on protective factors in CMP for the first time, for clients, clinicians and researchers specifically through a strengths‐based lens. While this review provides valuable insights into what factors may underpin an SBA, further longitudinal research is required to explore the relationships of these factors with pain outcomes over time. Future research should focus on which protective factors are most important and how they can be integrated into management strategies for individuals with CMP.
