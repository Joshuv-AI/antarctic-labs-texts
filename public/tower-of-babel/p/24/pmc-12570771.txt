Post-pandemic resurgence of respiratory virus infections: age and department-specific patterns in a Chinese tertiary hospital (2020–2024)
Authors: Shan Hong, Maoshu Zhu, Yanqian Huang, Linlin Chen, Dandan Han, Wenliu Qiu, Shujing Li, Linghui Ding, Heping Zheng, Feng Guo, Jun Mi, Xiuli Li, Jie Chen, Dongjie Li, Liangneng Zou, Yanli Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic dramatically altered the epidemiology of respiratory viral infections worldwide, yet comprehensive post-pandemic recovery dynamics remain poorly understood, particularly in China. This study analyzed patterns of respiratory virus infections in a Chinese tertiary hospital setting across the pandemic and post-pandemic period.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 9,237 patients who are tested for 13 respiratory viruses across multiple age groups and hospital departments at Xiamen Fifth Hospital in Southeatern China between January 2020 and early 2024. Advanced statistical approaches including seasonal Mann-Kendall trend analyses, and multivariate logistic regression were employed to identify temporal patterns and demographic risk factors.ResultsChildren comprised 57.2% of patients, adults 30.2%, and elderly 12.6%. Age-specific viral patterns showed human rhinovirus (HRV) and Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) are two highest predominating in children, while influenza A virus (IAV) displayed an inverse age relationship with higher positivity in elderly patients. Seasonal Mann-Kendall analysis revealed significant increasing trends for IAV across all age groups and MP showing extraordinary temporal surges during 2023–2024, indicating substantial rebound effects following initial pandemic suppression. Co-infection patterns increased with advancing age, reflecting heightened vulnerability in older populations. Importantly, department-specific analysis revealed that viral distribution patterns extended beyond simple age demographics, with children-dominant departments showing strong associations with RSV and Mycoplasma infections, while adult-focused departments demonstrated elevated risks for influenza viruses.ConclusionsThis analysis demonstrates heterogeneous post-pandemic respiratory virus recovery patterns that vary significantly across demographic and clinical settings. Department-specific analysis revealed that viral distribution extended beyond simple age demographics.These findings support implementing specific clinical protocols including: (1) enhanced multiplex testing prioritization for pediatric populations given their highest co-infection rates, (2) prioritized MP and RSV testing in pediatric departments, (3) enhanced IAV surveillance in elderly populations and (4) department-based patient cohorting strategies. Healthcare systems should establish routine seasonal surveillance calibrated to local subtropical patterns and prepare resource allocation for anticipated pathogen surges.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11919-x.Keywords: Respiratory viruses, Age groups, Hospital departments, COVID-19 pandemic, Viral co-infection, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Influenza, Seasonality, Post-pandemic surveillance, Infection control, Southeastern China

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic fundamentally altered respiratory virus circulation patterns globally, followed by complex post-pandemic resurgence dynamics that vary by pathogen, age group, and healthcare setting [1–4]. Understanding these evolving patterns is critical for hospital infection control and resource allocation.

Hospital departments represent distinct epidemiological microenvironments with varying patient populations, clinical practices, and transmission risks. Department-specific viral profiles, when combined with age and seasonal data, can inform targeted infection control strategies, optimize diagnostic testing protocols, and guide prophylactic interventions. However, most post-pandemic surveillance studies focus solely on community-level or age-stratified patterns, overlooking these crucial healthcare setting dynamics [5–7]. Despite the recognized importance of healthcare-associated transmission, comprehensive analyses examining the intersection of department type, patient demographics, and seasonal factors in post-pandemic respiratory virus epidemiology remain scarce, particularly in Asian healthcare settings. In China, respiratory infections account for a significant proportion of healthcare visits and hospitalizations, with children and the elderly bearing the highest burden [8]. Xiamen, a coastal city in southeastern China with a subtropical monsoon climate, experiences seasonal variations in respiratory infections that may differ from those observed in temperate regions [9].

This study presented a comprehensive analysis of 13 respiratory viruses detected at Xiamen Fifth Hospital from 2020 to 2024, spanning the COVID-19 pandemic period. The data included samples from outpatient visits, inpatient admissions, and emergency departments, allowing for detailed comparisons across different patient populations and clinical departments. We examined patterns by department type, age group, and season, and investigate correlations between different viruses and co-infection rates. Our findings provide actionable evidence for implementing age-stratified testing protocols, department-specific infection control measures, and targeted surveillance strategies to optimize respiratory virus management in hospital settings during the post-pandemic era.

Methods

Study setting and data collection

This retrospective study analyzed laboratory data from Xiamen Fifth Hospital, a tertiary hospital in Xiamen, China. We included all respiratory virus tests conducted between January 2020 and early 2024. Patients tested were those presenting with respiratory symptoms or with clinical suspicion of infection. All respiratory virus tests conducted during the study period were included in the analysis. When patients had multiple tests for the same pathogen, each test was analyzed independently to capture the full testing pattern and diagnostic yield across different clinical encounters. Patients with missing key demographic or test result data (< 2% of records) were excluded.As a tertiary referral center, Xiamen Fifth Hospital may overrepresent more severe cases, introducing potential referral bias. The following 13 viruses were included in the analysis: Adenovirus (ADV), Chlamydia pneumoniae (CP), Coronaviruses (CoV, excluding Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2)), Human Bocavirus (HBOV), Human Rhinovirus (HRV), Influenza A (IAV), IAV-H1N1, IAV-H3N2, Influenza B (IBV), Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP), Parainfluenza Virus (PIV), Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV), and Human Metapneumovirus (hMPV). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiamen Fifth Hospital (Approval No. XMWY-KY-2022-111-01).

Laboratory testing

Respiratory specimens (nasopharyngeal swabs or throat swabs) were analyzed using the ResP® 13 Respiratory Pathogen Multiplex Kit (Ningbo Health Gene Technologies Co., Ltd., Zhejiang Province, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. This kit employs a Multiplex Real-Time Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR) approach to simultaneously detect 13 common respiratory pathogens. Reagents provided in the kit— including the RT-PCR enzyme mix, controls, and standard mix—ensure high sensitivity and specificity during amplification. All procedures, from nucleic acid extraction to amplification and detection, were conducted strictly in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions to guarantee reliable diagnostic performance.Although multiplex PCR panels simultaneously test for all pathogens, some individual pathogen results were excluded due to invalid or indeterminate results caused by technical failures, insufficient sample volume, or assay-specific interference. This resulted in slightly different denominators for each pathogen (Additional file 1).

Data categorization

In this study, patients were categorized based on four key dimensions: age groups, department types, seasons, and years. For age classification, patients were divided into three groups: Children (< 18 years), Adults (18–64 years), and Elderly (≥ 65 years). Department types were classified into four categories: Children-dominant, Elderly-dominant, Adults-dominant, and Mixed, based on the predominant patient population within each department. Specifically, a department was classified as Children-dominant if more than 50% of its patients were children (< 18 years), as Elderly-dominant if more than 50% of its patients were elderly (≥ 65 years), and as Adults-dominant if more than 50% of its patients were adults (18–64 years). Departments where no single age group accounted for more than 50% of the patient population were classified as Mixed. Seasonal categorization was based on four clearly defined periods: Spring (March to May), Summer (June to August), Fall (September to November), and Winter (December to February). Finally, the data spanned five years, including the calendar years 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, and the early months of 2024. This comprehensive categorization allowed for detailed analyses of respiratory virus patterns across different patient demographics, healthcare settings, and temporal variations.

Statistical analysis

In our study, we implemented a comprehensive statistical analysis framework using R software (version 4.3.3) to examine respiratory virus infection patterns. The proportion of missing values in our dataset was extremely low (less than 2% for each covariate). Therefore, we opted not to perform imputation and proceeded with a complete-case analysis in all statistical models. For logistic regression analyses, we utilized the glm() function from the stats package with binomial distribution to identify significant predictors of viral positivity across department types, seasons, years, and gender. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the confint() function, and results were visualized with the forestplot package.Temporal trend analysis was conducted using Mann-Kendall tests from the Kendall package, with automatic selection between standard and seasonal variants based on time series characteristics. Seasonal strength was first assessed using Seasonal and Trend decomposition using Loess (STL) decomposition, calculated as the ratio of seasonal component variance to total variance. For time series with seasonal strength > 0.1, indicating significant seasonal patterns, Seasonal Mann-Kendall tests were applied using the SeasonalMannKendall() function to account for inherent seasonal autocorrelation in respiratory pathogen circulation. For series with weaker seasonality (≤ 0.1), standard Mann-Kendall tests were conducted using the MannKendall() function. This adaptive approach ensures accurate detection of monotonic trends over the study period (2020–2024) while controlling for seasonal confounding effects that could bias trend estimation in respiratory surveillance data. All analyses were performed separately for overall populations and age-stratified groups (children < 18 years, adults 18–64 years, elderly ≥ 65 years) to identify age-specific temporal patterns.

To evaluate associations between viruses and age groups, we employed Chi-square tests (chisq.test() from stats) with Yates’ continuity correction for larger samples, and Fisher’s exact test for cells with expected counts below 5. The strength of these associations was quantified using Cramer’s V coefficients calculated with the assocstats() function from the vcd package.

Correlation analyses examining relationships between different respiratory viruses were performed using the cor() and cor.test() functions from stats. Monthly and annual correlation matrices were generated using Spearman’s rank correlation for non-normally distributed data and visualized as heatmaps using the corrplot package with hierarchical clustering to identify virus clusters.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of our department classification approach, we conducted comprehensive sensitivity analyses using multiple cut-off points for defining four types of departments. We systematically varied the threshold from 50% to 70% in 5% increments (50%, 55%, 60%, 65%, and 70%) of pediatric patients to classify departments as children-dominant versus adult-dominant. For each cut-off point, we performed identical logistic regression analyses as described in the primary analysis, with Department Type recategorized at each threshold.

We calculated two primary stability metrics for each virus-department association. The coefficient of variation (CV) measured the relative variability of odds ratios across the five cut-off points, calculated as the standard deviation of odds ratios divided by the mean, then multiplied by 100%. Lower CV values indicate more stable associations across different thresholds. Consistency was defined as the percentage of cut-off points where the logistic regression coefficient for department type remained statistically significant (p < 0.05), providing a measure of how reliably each association maintained significance across varying classification criteria. Additionally, we calculated Consistency_BH by applying the Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing across virus-department combinations before determining significance percentages.

Model performance was evaluated for each cut-off point using area under the curve (AUC) from receiver operating characteristic analysis, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) for model fit comparison, Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, pseudo R-squared (Nagelkerke’s R²), and variance inflation factor (VIF) to assess multicollinearity. Virus-department associations were classified as highly stable (CV < 10% and Consistency ≥ 80%), moderately stable (CV 10–25% and Consistency ≥ 60%), or unstable (CV > 25% or Consistency < 60%). The 50% threshold was selected as the optimal cut-off based on producing the highest number of stable associations, clinical interpretability, and consistency with established pediatric hospital practices, representing an intuitive majority-based classification that aligns with clinical decision-making processes. All sensitivity analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3, with logistic regression models fitted using the glm() function and multiple testing corrections applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.

Data visualization throughout the analysis relied on the ggplot2 package for generating publication-quality figures, including forest plots for ORs, seasonal trend analysis, and department-specific positivity patterns.

The gender distribution showed a slight male predominance (57.3% male vs. 42.7% female) that was consistent across all age groups (Table 1).

Association between viruses and age groups

Chi-square analysis revealed significant age-pathogen associations for most respiratory viruses (Table 1, Additional file 2). MP and HRV showed the strongest age associations (Cramer’s V = 0.156 and 0.137, respectively), while three pathogens (CoV, IAV-H3N2, and CP) showed non-significant associations (Table 1, Additional file 2 and Fig. 1).

Temporal trends in pathogen positivity by age group (2020–2024)

Longitudinal analysis revealed significant temporal heterogeneity across pathogens and age groups (Table 2; Fig. 2). The 2020 pandemic phase showed suppressed positivity for most pathogens, followed by variable resurgence patterns. Notable trends included IAV surge in 2022 (particularly in elderly), RSV peak in 2021 across all ages, and MP extraordinary increase in 2023 (especially pediatric populations). Virus co-infection patterns across age groups

Analysis of viral co-infection data revealed distinct age-related patterns, with co-infection rates progressively increasing from children (22.14%, 769/3,473 cases), followed by adults (16.37%, 213/1,301 cases), and lowest in elderly (13.31%, 47/353 cases) (Table 3, Additional file 4, Fig. 4). To ensure accurate representation of genuine co-infections, we excluded virus pairs representing assay hierarchy artifacts (IAV with its subtypes IAV-H1N1 and IAV-H3N2), as these reflect the inherent testing methodology rather than true biological co-infections. Statistical analysis using Fisher’s exact test with BH correction for multiple comparisons within each age group identified significant associations with marked age-related differences in co-infection diversity (Fig. 4).

Among co-infection patterns, the most prominent association was consistently observed between IBV and hMPV across age groups, showing strong positive relationships in both children (OR = 2.27, p < 0.001) and adults (OR = 2.47, p < 0.01). Conversely, HRV-related pairings frequently demonstrated negative associations (OR < 1) across multiple virus combinations, particularly with MP, RSV, and hMPV, suggesting potential viral interference mechanisms that were most pronounced in pediatric populations.

The co-infection landscape showed striking age-specific characteristics. Children exhibited the most diverse co-infection profile with 15 virus pairs analyzed and 12 achieving statistical significance, reflecting high viral exposure and developing immune responses. Adults demonstrated intermediate diversity with 9 statistically significant pairs among 15 analyzed combinations(Fig. 4). In contrast, the elderly population exhibited a markedly restricted co-infection profile with no virus pairs meeting the minimum case requirements for statistical analysis indicating limited co-infection diversity in this age group.

This age-related pattern of decreasing co-infection rates and diversity may reflect several factors: (1) acquired immunity reducing susceptibility to multiple simultaneous infections in older populations, (2) differences in social exposure patterns, with children having higher contact rates in educational settings, (3) age-related changes in respiratory tract microenvironment affecting viral replication and persistence, and (4) potential survival bias in elderly populations with severe co-infections.

Of note, co-infection rates reported here may be overrepresented due to the use of highly sensitive multiplex PCR testing, which can detect viral nucleic acids from recent infections that may not represent concurrent active infections. These findings highlight the complexity of respiratory pathogen interactions and underscore the clinical importance of comprehensive pathogen testing, particularly in populations with high co-infection rates.

Multivariate analysis for respiratory virus infection

Define department type

In the above analysis, we analyzed virus infection patterns according to age group and found compelling epidemiological trends, including the significant resurgence of IAV across all age strata and pronounced seasonal variations in RSV positivity. For this section, we decided to analyze viral infections from a hospital administration and healthcare systems perspective, as department-based stratification offers enhanced operational relevance for administrators and infection control teams, given that protocols and interventions are typically implemented at the departmental level.

We classified departments into four categories based on their predominant patient population using a 50% cut-off threshold. We also performed sensitivity analysis using five different cut-off points and ultimately selected 50% to define department type (Methods, Additional file 5–8). Using the 50% cut-off, adult-dominant departments (n = 37) constituted the majority of hospital units and primarily included specialty internal medicine departments (Rheumatology and Immunology, Hematology, Oncology), various respiratory medicine departments, neurological units, specialty surgery departments, and emergency services (Fig. 5a and f). Elderly-dominant departments (n = 7) included ICU wards, general surgery, cardiology, radiotherapy, orthopedics, and geriatrics (Fig. 5b and f). Children-dominant departments (n = 7) consisted of pediatric wards, pediatric outpatient departments, pediatric isolation units, neonatal wards, and nucleic acid testing departments (Fig. 5d and f). Mixed departments (n = 4) with no predominant age group included general surgery wards, ophthalmology, and dermatology departments (Fig. 5c and f).

Given the distinct epidemiological characteristics and detection patterns among the 13 respiratory pathogens, we categorized them into three groups for clearer visualization: (1) Classic respiratory viruses (RSV, MP, PIV, hMPV), (2) Influenza and coronaviruses (IAV subtypes, CoV), and (3) Enteric-associated and other viruses (ADV, HBOV, IBV). This classification considers viral structure (enveloped/non-enveloped), clinical syndrome associations, and seasonal patterns. Although ADV is traditionally classified as an enteric virus, its active transmission in respiratory specimens and temporal distribution patterns similar to HBOV justified its inclusion in the third category.

For respiratory viruses, children-dominant departments showed markedly elevated risks for classic pediatric pathogens including RSV (OR = 11.76), MP, PIV, and hMPV (Fig. 6a). Notably, MP exhibited dramatic temporal surges in 2023 (OR = 18.95) and 2024 (OR = 12.20), reflecting post-pandemic resurgence patterns, while RSV risk decreased progressively after 2021.

Influenza and coronavirus patterns revealed adult-dominant departments had higher IAV risk compared to pediatric settings, with pronounced seasonal variations including summer IAV peaks and spring IAV-H1N1 surges (Fig. 6b). The year 2022 marked substantial increases in both IAV-H3N2 and overall IAV infections, consistent with post-pandemic influenza resurgence.

For enteric and other viruses, children-dominant departments strongly predicted ADV and HBOV infections, with gender effects limited primarily to male predominance for IBV (Fig. 6c). IBV demonstrated strong winter seasonality and progressive risk increases from 2021 to 2024. Complete odds ratios, confidence intervals, and statistical significance are detailed in Additional file 9.

Discussion

Our comprehensive analysis of respiratory viral infections in a tertiary hospital in Xiamen, China revealed distinct epidemiological patterns across different demographic and clinical dimensions. The significant age associations observed for most respiratory viruses, particularly MP and HRV, align with established understanding of these pathogens’ predilection for specific age groups. The markedly higher odds of RSV, MP, and HRV in children aligned with the known pediatric predilection of these pathogens [10, 11]. TThese age-related patterns reflect fundamental differences in immune system maturation, respiratory tract anatomy, and exposure histories. Children’s immature adaptive immunity and smaller airway diameters create optimal conditions for certain pathogens like RSV and MP, while their limited influenza exposure history makes them more susceptible to novel strains. Conversely, elderly populations demonstrate increased influenza susceptibility due to immunosenescence and accumulated comorbidities that compromise respiratory defense mechanisms, while cross-protective immunity from childhood exposures may explain their lower rates of typically pediatric viruses.

The temporal trends observed during 2020–2024 provide valuable insights into the complex dynamics of respiratory virus circulation during and after the COVID-19 pandemic [12–14]. The observed patterns likely reflect complex interactions between population immunity debt, viral evolution, and environmental factors. The MP resurgence in 2023 can be mechanistically explained by the accumulation of immunologically naive individuals following reduced circulation, creating a large susceptible population. Additionally, the cyclical nature of MP epidemics (typically 3–7 year intervals) combined with pandemic-related circulation interruption created conditions for explosive resurgence once social mixing resumed.

Our results are generally consistent with recent studies in China and internationally regarding the post-pandemic resurgence and changing patterns of respiratory virus infections. For example, Quan et al. reported significant increases in respiratory virus detection rates after COVID-19 in Weifang, China, particularly among older adults, which aligns with our observation of increasing influenza A positivity in the elderly population [15]. Studies from Hangzhou [16] and Hong Kong [17] also found shifts in pediatric respiratory virus distributions and age-specific trends, comparable to our findings for RSV and rhinovirus in children. Mauro et al. observed age-related differences and increased co-infection rates among older adults in Italy, supporting our co-infection results [18]. Notably, our study uniquely emphasizes department-level differences, offering new insights for hospital infection control not commonly addressed in previous work. These comparisons underscore the importance of integrating age and department factors for targeted infection management in the post-pandemic era.

The department-specific transmission patterns reflect complex interactions between host factors, environmental conditions, and pathogen characteristics. Pediatric departments create ideal transmission environments through several mechanisms: high patient density facilitating aerosol transmission, immature immune responses leading to prolonged viral shedding, and behavioral factors (crying, inadequate hand hygiene) that enhance droplet dispersal. The microenvironmental conditions in these departments—including shared play areas, frequent staff-patient contact, and family clustering—amplify transmission through both direct and indirect contact routes. In contrast, adult departments show different risk profiles due to altered immune responses, different care protocols, and distinct visitor patterns that modify exposure dynamics.

The co-infection patterns observed, particularly the positive association between IBV and hMPV and the negative associations involving HRV, suggest distinct viral interaction mechanisms operating at cellular and immunological levels. The IBV-hMPV synergy may reflect shared cellular entry pathways or complementary immune evasion strategies that facilitate dual colonization. Conversely, HRV’s interference patterns likely result from innate immune activation—specifically interferon production—that creates an antiviral state limiting secondary viral replication. The age-related decline in co-infection diversity may reflect both immunological maturation leading to more effective viral clearance and reduced exposure intensity in older populations.

In our subtropical region, the seasonal patterns reflect the complex interplay between environmental factors and viral biology. Temperature and humidity variations affect viral stability and transmission efficiency, while seasonal changes in human behavior (indoor crowding, school attendance) modify contact patterns. The altered influenza seasonality observed may result from disrupted global circulation patterns during the pandemic, leading to genetic drift and immune escape that altered traditional seasonal dynamics [19, 20].

This study has several important limitations that should be acknowledged. As a single-center, hospital-based retrospective analysis from one tertiary hospital in Xiamen, the generalizability to other regions or healthcare settings may be limited.The findings may not be applicable to different healthcare systems, socioeconomic settings, or geographic regions with distinct climatic conditions, population demographics, or viral circulation patterns. Moreover, our tertiary hospital setting likely attracts more severe cases and specific patient populations, potentially overestimating disease burden compared to primary care or community settings. The study design is inherently prone to selection and information bias, as our population consists only of patients who sought care and received viral testing, which may not represent community-wide viral circulation patterns. Selection bias may be particularly pronounced as testing decisions were influenced by clinical presentation, physician judgment, and resource availability, potentially favoring detection of certain viral syndromes over others. Detection bias may have occurred due to evolving testing protocols and varying clinical awareness of different pathogens during the study period. The decision to test or admit patients can be influenced by clinical severity, patient comorbidities, and evolving hospital policies—factors that could confound the observed associations and temporal trends, particularly given the substantial changes in admission criteria, testing policies, and care-seeking behaviors during and after the pandemic. Additionally, we did not collect detailed data on patient comorbidities, disease severity, or clinical outcomes, which limits our ability to assess confounding by underlying health status or to link epidemiological trends with clinical impact.Misclassification bias may have occurred due to several factors: the sensitivity and specificity limitations of multiplex PCR platforms for different viruses, potential cross-reactivity between related pathogens, and the inability to distinguish between active infection and prolonged viral shedding from previous infections. The retrospective data collection approach may have introduced information bias through incomplete or inconsistent documentation practices across different departments and time periods. Furthermore, the use of sensitive multiplex PCR platforms may have led to overestimation of viral co-infections by detecting prolonged viral shedding or residual nucleic acids from recent infections rather than true concurrent active infections. Given these limitations, caution should be exercised when extrapolating these findings to broader populations, and future prospective, multi-center studies with comprehensive clinical and epidemiologic data are needed to validate and extend our results.

Despite these limitations, the main epidemiological trends—including the post-pandemic resurgence of MP and IAV, the distinct age and department-specific transmission risks, and the variation in co-infection profiles—provide actionable knowledge for clinical and public health settings. Hospitals can use this information to guide resource planning, optimize testing strategies, tailor infection control interventions, and inform outbreak preparedness in the ongoing post-pandemic era.

Conclusions

This comprehensive analysis reveals distinct epidemiological patterns requiring targeted clinical and public health responses. Based on our findings, we recommend implementing specific practical measures: Age-stratified testing protocols should prioritize MP and RSV screening in pediatric populations, while emphasizing IAV surveillance in elderly patients. Multiplex testing should be prioritized in children given their elevated co-infection rates.

Department-specific infection control measures should include enhanced environmental controls and cohorting strategies in children-dominant departments for RSV and MP management, while adult-dominant units require targeted IAV prevention including vaccination campaigns and isolation protocols. Surveillance systems in subtropical regions must establish locally-calibrated monitoring frameworks with season-adjusted thresholds to anticipate pathogen surges, particularly MP resurgence patterns demonstrated in our analysis. These findings highlight the need for flexible surveillance integrating demographic profiling, departmental risk assessment, and season-adjusted monitoring to optimize respiratory infection management in the post-pandemic era.
