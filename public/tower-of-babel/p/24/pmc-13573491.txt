Virus-like particles in cancer immunotherapy: bridging human and veterinary medicine through one health
Authors: Yingqi Zhu, Zhuangli Bi, Zichen Zhang, Miao Zhang, Qingqing Du, Mingxing Hu, Ting Zhou, Yiming Fan, Shu Zhang, Guijun Wang, Guangqing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Virus-like particles (VLPs) are engineered nanoplatforms that mimic viral structures, offering high immunogenicity, biocompatibility, and functional versatility for cancer immunotherapy. While widely explored in human oncology as nanovaccines and targeted delivery systems for chemo-/immuno-therapeutics and genetic payloads (e.g., mRNA, siRNA, and CRISPR/Cas systems), their potential in veterinary oncology remains underexploited. This review synthesizes recent advances in VLP design, including scaffold engineering, antigen display, cargo encapsulation, and surface functionalization, and discusses the mechanistic basis of VLP-induced antitumor immunity, encompassing dendritic cell activation, adaptive immune amplification, and tumor microenvironment remodeling. Importantly, we highlight the emerging role of companion animals with spontaneous tumors—such as lymphoma, melanoma, and mammary carcinoma—as immunocompetent translational models within the One Health framework. Comparative oncology reveals striking parallels in oncogenic pathways, immune landscapes, and therapeutic responses, supporting the use of canine and feline cancers as biologically relevant intermediates between murine studies and human clinical trials. We provide an evidence-based assessment of representative VLP platforms, evaluate their translational readiness, and examine cross-species opportunities for shared target development, biomarker discovery, and regulatory convergence, while also addressing species-specific biological and technical limitations. Finally, we propose a forward-looking roadmap that prioritizes manufacturing standardization, biomarker development, comparative validation, precision engineering, and emerging technologies such as AI-guided design and tumor-on-chip systems. Collectively, we position One Health as an operational strategy to accelerate the bidirectional translation of VLP-based immunotherapies for both human and veterinary cancer patients.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy has revolutionized oncology, offering durable responses in subsets of patients [1]. However, major challenges—such as immune evasion, tumor heterogeneity, and off-target toxicity—continue to undermine its broad efficacy [2–4]. Current frontline immunotherapies, including immune checkpoint inhibitors and CAR-T cell therapies, have achieved notable success but remain restricted by low response rates in solid tumors and significant safety concerns [5, 6]. These limitations underscore the urgent need for novel platforms that can elicit robust, tumor-specific immune responses with minimal systemic toxicity—and that can be evaluated in clinically relevant preclinical models.

Virus-like particles (VLPs), self-assembling nanostructures derived from viral structural proteins, represent such a promising platform [7]. They mimic native viruses in morphology but lack genetic material, rendering them non-infectious and inherently safe. Their well-defined size (20–200 nm), symmetrical geometry, and high surface repetitiveness confer superior immunogenicity [8], efficient drainage to lymphatic vessels [9, 10], and preferential uptake by antigen-presenting cells (APCs) [11]. Furthermore, bearing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), VLPs potently activate dendritic cells (DCs) and other innate immune sensors via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), triggering robust innate immune response [12, 13]. Critically, when engineered to display or carry tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) or neoantigens, this innate activation bridges seamlessly to adaptive immunity [14]. It drives the cross-presentation of tumor antigens, leading to the priming and expansion of tumor-specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) — a key cell-mediated effector response [15]. Concurrently, VLPs can elicit potent humoral immunity, characterized by the production of opsonizing antibodies that can mediate tumor cell killing via antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC), and phagocytosis. This coordinated innate-to-adaptive immune cascade engages multiple effector mechanisms to directly counter immune escape and foster durable, multifaceted antitumor immunity.

VLPs are also highly engineerable platforms. Through genetic fusion or chemical conjugation, they can be functionalized with tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) [16], immune adjuvants [17], cell-penetrating peptides [18], or targeting ligands [19], enabling multivalent antigen display and tailored immune activation. Moreover, VLPs can encapsulate diverse payloads, including mRNA, siRNA, chemotherapeutics, and CRISPR components, providing a multimodal strategy to address tumor heterogeneity and immune resistance [20–23]. This flexibility, combined with their biodegradability and favorable safety profile, positions them as versatile nanoplatforms for both precise immune targeting and efficient therapeutic delivery.

Despite these advantages, the clinical translation of VLP-based immunotherapies faces bottlenecks, including manufacturing scalability [24] and, crucially, the over-reliance on rodent models. These models often fail to recapitulate the complexity of human tumor immunobiology, including immune checkpoint regulation, stromal interactions, and inter-patient heterogeneity [25–27].

This limitation underscores the unique value of companion animals, such as dogs and cats, which naturally develop spontaneous cancers (e.g., melanoma, lymphoma, osteosarcoma) that closely mirror human malignancies in histology, genetics, immune response, and therapeutic outcomes [28, 29]. Clinical trials in these immunocompetent hosts, which exhibit intact and heterogeneous tumor microenvironments and diverse genetic backgrounds [30, 31], offer an unparalleled opportunity to evaluate VLPs under clinically relevant conditions, providing translational insights rarely achievable in rodents [32, 33]. Integrating VLP research into a One Health framework facilitates this bidirectional knowledge exchange between human and veterinary medicine, creating a powerful translational bridge from the outset.

This review provides a comprehensive overview of VLP-based strategies in cancer immunotherapy, uniquely framed through the lens of comparative oncology and its One Health translational potential. We first summarize VLP design principles, antigen/adjuvant display methods, and their immunological mechanisms of action. We then discuss recent advances in VLP-mediated drug/gene delivery, cancer nanovaccines, and immunomodulatory applications in human oncology. Finally, we explore how spontaneous tumors in companion animals can serve as a critical translational platform for VLP evaluation, highlighting how this approach offers mutual benefits and accelerates progress in both human and veterinary medicine.

Design and engineering strategies for antitumor VLPs

VLPs are highly immunogenic nanostructures with flexible design features, making them a promising platform for cancer immunotherapy. Their repetitive, multivalent display and ability to mimic viral morphology enable VLPs to effectively engage the immune system. In addition to their inherent immunostimulatory properties, VLPs possess a modular structure that supports rational engineering. This allows for the presentation of tumor antigens, encapsulation of immunomodulatory agents, and surface modifications for targeted delivery (Fig. 1). These capabilities, combined with recent advances in synthetic biology and nanotechnology, empower VLPs to address major challenges in tumor immunology, including antigen heterogeneity, inefficient antigen presentation, and immune evasion [34].

To guide the rational design of antitumor VLPs, this section discusses critical engineering elements—scaffold selection, antigen display, payload delivery, surface targeting strategies, and nanotechnological integration for enhanced stability and function.

Selection of VLP scaffolds

The selection of an appropriate virus-like particle (VLP) scaffold is a critical first step in the development of cancer nanovaccines and immunotherapeutics. VLPs used in cancer immunotherapy can be derived from both non-enveloped and enveloped viruses. Typical non-enveloped VLP platforms include HPV, HBV (HBsAg particles), AP205, CPV, and MS2, offering high structural stability and ease of antigen display [35]. Enveloped VLPs, such as those based on retrovirus Gag, influenza, alphaviruses, and coronavirus spike proteins, better mimic native viral entry mechanisms and allow incorporation of membrane-bound antigens but require more complex manufacturing processes [36]. Each class exhibits distinct structural and functional features: non-enveloped VLPs generally offer superior physical stability and ease of purification, whereas enveloped VLPs provide a lipid bilayer suitable for incorporating membrane-bound tumor antigens or immune modulators.

Biosafety and innate immunogenicity are also important considerations. Bacteriophage-derived VLPs such as MS2, Qβ, and AP205 are non-infectious in humans, exhibit high thermostability, and are thus widely used in both human and veterinary applications [37, 38]. Similarly, plant virus-based VLPs (e.g., CPMV, TMV) offer strong immunostimulatory capacity, high biocompatibility, and scalable production, making them attractive for solid tumor immunotherapy [39, 40]. In contrast, VLPs derived from mammalian viruses such as HIV or MLV possess relatively larger internal space and adaptable membrane surfaces, allowing for the incorporation of therapeutic components, including engineered transmembrane proteins (e.g., tumor-associated antigens or immune modulators) and targeting molecules (e.g., peptides or antibodies that direct VLPs to specific cells)—making them suitable for gene delivery and tumor microenvironment (TME) modulation [41, 42].

Recent advances in synthetic biology have enabled the development of VLP backbones that are genetically or chemically customizable to meet diverse immunotherapeutic needs [43, 44]. One strategy involves the construction of chimeric scaffolds, in which exogenous tumor antigens or immune modulators are genetically fused to viral capsid proteins—often through reverse genetic engineering—without disrupting particle self-assembly [45]. This approach allows precise antigen placement on the VLP surface, as seen in platforms like AP205 or HBcAg, and facilitates robust immune activation. Alternatively, de novo-designed protein nanoparticles represent a fully synthetic approach in which self-assembling protein subunits are computationally engineered to form highly ordered nanostructures with defined symmetry, valency, and surface geometry. Systems such as I53–50 enable modular and multivalent antigen presentation in a spatially controlled manner, optimizing B cell receptor engagement and downstream immune responses [46, 47]. Together, these technologies significantly expand the versatility and precision of VLP-based vaccine and immunotherapy platforms.

Ultimately, scaffold selection should be tailored to the immunological and therapeutic requirements of specific cancer types. For instance, solid tumors with poor lymphatic drainage may benefit from highly immunogenic, self-adjuvanting scaffolds like plant VLPs [48, 49], while tumors requiring membrane protein targeting or gene delivery may necessitate enveloped scaffolds with high cargo capacity [50]. Thus, scaffold choice must balance structural attributes, functional versatility, and compatibility with the intended therapeutic context to achieve optimal antitumor efficacy.

Antigen display and epitope engineering

Effective tumor immunotherapy relies on the precise presentation of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) or neoantigens to elicit robust cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses. VLPs excel at antigen display through both genetic fusion and chemical conjugation strategies. Genetic fusion allows direct insertion of epitopes into surface-exposed loops or terminal regions of coat proteins, enabling dense and repetitive antigen presentation, which is critical for B cell receptor crosslinking and T cell priming. This strategy is widely used in HPV and HBV VLP-based vaccines [13, 51, 52]. Alternatively, chemical conjugation techniques, such as click chemistry, sortase-mediated ligation, or maleimide-thiol coupling, allow for the site-specific attachment of synthetic peptides, glycopeptides, or even full-length proteins to VLP surfaces without compromising particle assembly [53–56]. These strategies provide versatile modularity for vaccine and therapeutic applications. Site-specific conjugation further ensures controlled orientation and optimal epitope display, thereby enhancing the specificity and magnitude of the elicited immune responses.

Rational epitope engineering plays a central role in the development of next-generation VLP-based cancer vaccines, enabling precise control over antigen composition, spatial arrangement, and immunological outcome [57]. By leveraging structural modeling and immunoinformatics, multiple tumor-associated epitopes—derived from either a single heterogeneous tumor or different cancer types—can be strategically selected and integrated into a single VLP platform, forming multi-epitope constructs capable of eliciting broad and cross-reactive T cell responses [58, 59]. These epitopes can be displayed in tandem as peptide fusions to capsid subunits or grafted into surface-exposed loops, preserving particle integrity while maximizing immune visibility. In addition to tumor-specific antigens, the incorporation of immunomodulatory epitopes has emerged as a powerful strategy to enhance vaccine efficacy. For example, universal CD4 + T helper epitopes such as PADRE (Pan-DR epitope) can be co-presented to enhance helper T cell activation and thereby support cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) priming [60, 61]. Likewise, fusing toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists—such as flagellin (TLR5 ligand) or CpG motifs (TLR9 ligands)—directly to the VLP backbone or encapsulating them within the particle can activate innate immunity and facilitate more efficient antigen processing and presentation [62, 63]. These rationally designed constructs not only enhance the magnitude of the immune response but also improve its quality—favoring Th1-polarized responses and durable memory formation. Importantly, epitope engineering offers a modular and customizable approach adaptable to patient-specific neoantigens or tumor mutational landscapes, paving the way for personalized VLP-based cancer immunotherapies [64].

Designing VLPs for efficient cargo packaging and delivery

Beyond antigen display, VLPs can function as nanocarriers for diverse payloads, including adjuvants, immunostimulatory RNA (e.g., CpG, poly(I: C)), cytokines (e.g., IL-12), checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., anti-PD-L1 nanobodies), and even small-molecule drugs or mRNA [7, 65, 66]. Encapsulation can be achieved either during VLP self-assembly or through post-assembly loading methods, such as osmotic swelling or electrostatic adsorption.

RNA and mRNA delivery using VLPs is particularly promising, as their viral origin naturally supports nucleic acid packaging. For example, MS2 and Qβ capsids can encapsulate in vitro-transcribed mRNA encoding tumor antigens or immunoregulatory proteins [67, 68]. The combination of VLP protection and targeted delivery enhances mRNA stability, cellular uptake, and translation efficiency. In addition, the nanoscale size and repetitive protein architecture of VLPs facilitate efficient endocytosis and endosomal escape, further improving cytosolic delivery of mRNA cargos [69].

Encapsulation also supports immunomodulation within the tumor microenvironment. VLPs loaded with STING agonists or siRNA targeting immunosuppressive pathways (e.g., IDO1, TGF-β) can reshape the TME toward a pro-inflammatory, antitumor state [55, 70]. Importantly, VLP-based co-delivery systems allow coordinated release of antigen and adjuvant in time and space, which is critical for effective immune priming. This co-delivery strategy not only improves therapeutic efficacy but also offers a modular platform adaptable to various cancer types and treatment regimens [71, 72].

Surface modification and targeting ligands

To enable tumor-specific delivery, virus-like particle (VLP) surfaces can be functionalized with targeting ligands via genetic fusion or site-specific bioconjugation. Representative ligands include peptides (e.g., RGD for integrin αvβ3⁺ tumors, iRGD for deep tissue penetration) [73, 74], antibodies or affibodies (e.g., anti-HER2 for breast carcinoma, anti-EGFR for glioblastoma) [75, 76], aptamers (e.g., AS1411 targeting nucleolin in lung cancer) [77], and tumor-homing cytokines (e.g., IL-2-fused VLPs for regulatory T cell depletion) [78, 79]. This active targeting strategy not only enhances cellular uptake but also minimizes off-target toxicity, which is particularly crucial for potent immunostimulatory payloads with narrow therapeutic windows (e.g., STING agonists). For instance, cRGD-functionalized nanoparticles facilitated tumor-specific delivery by preferentially homing to and retaining within αvβ₃ integrin-overexpressing breast tumors, highlighting the utility of cRGD for targeted cancer nanotherapy [80]. A DEC-205-targeting nanovaccine functionalized with the hydrolysis-resistant peptide hr-8 enabled selective delivery to dendritic cells, thereby amplifying cross-priming efficiency and eliciting robust CD8⁺ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity [81].

In addition to active targeting, surface modification enables precise control over VLP pharmacokinetics and immunogenicity. Shielding strategies such as PEGylation or glycosylation can prolong circulation half-life and attenuate innate immune recognition, although excessive PEG density (> 15%) may impede receptor-mediated uptake [82, 83]. Tumor-specific unmasking strategies take advantage of TME conditions—such as MMP-2–cleavable PEG coatings in breast cancer—to expose targeting ligands only at the tumor site [84]. Furthermore, the spatial distribution and surface density of targeting ligands require precise optimization. Empirical studies indicate that a moderate ligand density—typically around 10 to 30 ligands per VLP—achieves an optimal compromise between effective tumor targeting and avoidance of immune detection [85]. Collectively, such precision surface engineering transforms VLPs from passive delivery vehicles into programmable nanoplatforms for integrated diagnosis and therapy, enabling highly localized action while improving safety and therapeutic efficacy.

Nanotechnology integration for stability and functionality

Nanotechnological integration offers a multifaceted approach to enhance the physicochemical stability and functional tunability of virus-like particle (VLP)-based systems, thereby facilitating their clinical translation. Various engineering strategies—such as nanoparticle encapsulation, layer-by-layer coating, inorganic hybridization (e.g., gold or silica shelling), and covalent crosslinking—can significantly improve the resistance of VLPs to enzymatic degradation, temperature stress, and colloidal instability [86–89]. These modifications help preserve structural integrity and prolong shelf life under physiological and storage conditions.

Moreover, incorporating magnetic or fluorescent moieties enables VLPs to function as multifunctional theranostic platforms, allowing real-time imaging, biodistribution tracking, and image-guided delivery [90, 91]. The use of stimuli-responsive materials—such as pH-sensitive or redox-cleavable coatings—further enhances therapeutic precision by enabling controlled release in response to the acidic tumor microenvironment or intracellular redox conditions [92].

From a manufacturing perspective, advanced techniques like microfluidic synthesis and spray-drying offer scalable, reproducible production methods with improved batch-to-batch consistency [93, 94]. Collectively, these nanotechnological innovations contribute to the clinical translation of VLPs by improving their pharmacological stability, delivery specificity, and compliance with regulatory standards.

Immune mechanisms of VLP-based anticancer therapy

Virus-like particles (VLPs) initiate potent coordinated antitumor immune responses by closely mimicking conserved viral recognition pathways [35, 95]. Their virus-like architecture—characterized by high-density pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), nanoscale (20–100 nm) repetitive geometry, and spatially organized antigen presentation—simultaneously engages multiple layers of the immune system: (i) innate immune activation via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on dendritic cells (DCs); (ii) efficient priming of adaptive lymphocytes through enhanced cross-presentation; and (iii) when appropriately engineered, immunological reprogramming of the tumor microenvironment (TME) through targeted delivery of immunomodulatory agents. Notably, these mechanisms are evolutionarily conserved across mammalian species, supporting translational relevance from companion animal cancers (e.g., canine melanoma, feline fibrosarcoma) to human oncology [96, 97]. Together, these interconnected processes constitute a coordinated mechanistic cascade linking innate immune activation, adaptive immune amplification, and tumor microenvironment remodeling to achieve durable antitumor immunity (Fig. 2). Importantly, immune responses observed in companion-animal VLP studies—including dendritic cell activation, CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, antibody induction, and tumor microenvironment remodeling—closely parallel the conserved antitumor mechanisms characterized in human VLP immunotherapy studies, thereby supporting the translational validity of companion animal tumor models within the One Health framework.

Structural basis for immunogenic superiority

Virus-like particles (VLPs) are supramolecular assemblies that mimic the morphology of native viruses but lack the viral genome, rendering them replication-incompetent. Typically measuring 20–100 nm in diameter, their nanoscale dimensions favor passive lymphatic drainage and efficient access to secondary lymphoid organs, where antigen-presenting cells (APCs) are abundant [98]. The structural hallmark of VLPs lies in their dense and repetitive display of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), which facilitate multivalent engagement of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs). Such geometrical regularity and epitope density promote potent B-cell receptor (BCR) cross-linking, leading to rapid activation of B cells without the need for extensive T-cell help [99].

Beyond surface geometry, the immunostimulatory capacity of VLPs is amplified by their molecular composition. Many VLPs either encapsulate naturally derived nucleic acids during assembly or can be engineered to carry synthetic adjuvants, enabling simultaneous activation of endosomal Toll-like receptors (e.g., TLR7/9) and cytosolic sensors such as cGAS, which converges on the STING pathway [100]. These pathways synergistically drive NF-κB and IRF activation, culminating in pro-inflammatory cytokine release and type I interferon production [101]. Beyond these molecular signaling cascades, the physical presentation of antigens also critically influences immune potency. For example, synthetic platforms like viromimetic polymer nanoparticles (VPNVax) can recapitulate the high epitope density characteristic of natural VLPs, far exceeding the antigen display capacity of conventional liposomal carriers. This biomimetic feature is a primary driver of their superior immunogenicity in both prophylactic and therapeutic contexts [102].

Finally, advances in protein engineering have allowed chimeric VLPs to display multiple tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) or neoantigens simultaneously, enabling polyvalent responses against heterogeneous tumor cell populations [103]. Structural rigidity and ordered antigen presentation also enhance antigen stability in circulation, reducing the need for high-dose administrations [104]. This architectural superiority forms the mechanistic foundation for the potent and durable immune activation observed in VLP-based anticancer therapies.

Innate immune ignition: PRR engagement, APC maturation, macrophage repolarization

Upon administration, VLPs are rapidly recognized by cells of the innate immune system, particularly dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages. Their uptake primarily occurs through receptor-dependent pathways, including scavenger receptors and C-type lectins (e.g., DEC-205), which facilitate efficient endosomal trafficking [105]. Within endosomes, VLP-associated nucleic acids activate Toll-like receptors (TLR7/8 by ssRNA or TLR9 by CpG DNA), triggering MyD88-dependent signaling cascades. Concurrently, cytosolic leakage of these nucleic acids engages cytosolic sensors such as RIG-I or cGAS, leading to STING activation and robust production of type I interferons (IFN-I) [106, 107].

The resulting cytokine milieu, characterized by high levels of IFN-α/β, IL-12, and TNF-α, initiates a direct and potent antitumor assault by innate effector cells. First, IL-12 and IFN-I potently activate natural killer (NK) cells, enhancing their proliferation and cytotoxic potential. Activated NK cells recognize and lyse tumor cells through the release of perforin and granzymes, serving as a critical first line of defense prior to the engagement of adaptive immunity [108]. Second, the same inflammatory mediators drive the repolarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) from an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype to a pro-inflammatory M1 state. M1 macrophages not only improve antigen presentation but also acquire direct tumoricidal capability by producing nitric oxide (NO) and reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby contributing to the direct debulking of tumor mass [109].

In parallel to mounting this direct innate attack, the PRR-driven cytokine response is indispensable for orchestrating adaptive immunity. High levels of IL-12 and IFN-I are critical for driving the maturation of dendritic cells (DCs) [110]. Mature DCs markedly upregulate surface expression of co-stimulatory molecules (CD80/CD86) and peptide-MHC complexes, and enhance their antigen cross-presentation capacity, thereby efficiently bridging innate sensing to the priming of T-cell-mediated adaptive immunity [111].

In summary, VLPs mount a concerted attack on the tumor microenvironment by unleashing immediate innate cytotoxicity (via NK cell-mediated killing and M1 macrophage repolarization) while simultaneously conditioning the immune landscape for adaptive responses (through robust DC maturation). This dual mechanism not only directly debulks tumor mass but also establishes the critical immunogenic context necessary for initiating potent and sustained antitumor immunity.

Adaptive immunity amplification: CTL/B-cell responses and epitope spreading

The innate immune activation triggered by VLPs serves as a potent adjuvant for adaptive immunity. Antigens carried by or displayed on VLPs are processed and presented via both MHC-I and MHC-II pathways, facilitating activation of CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and CD4⁺ helper T cells [112]. The particulate nature of VLPs promotes efficient cross-presentation, a key process in eliciting robust CTL responses against tumor-associated antigens [113]. Activated CTLs produce effector molecules such as IFN-γ, TNF-α, and granzyme B, directly mediating tumor cell killing [114].

B-cell responses are profoundly enhanced by the characteristic structural features of VLPs. The high-density, repetitive epitope arrays enable exceptionally strong B cell receptor (BCR) cross-linking, which acts as a potent intrinsic adjuvant [35]. This triggers robust B cell activation and rapid, T cell-independent IgM secretion. Crucially, the proteinaceous nature of VLPs provides cognate T helper epitopes. This facilitates potent T cell-dependent help in germinal centers, which is essential for driving affinity maturation, class switching to high-affinity IgG isotypes, and the generation of long-lived plasma cells and memory B cells [115]. The resulting high-titer antibodies can opsonize tumor cells, facilitating their clearance via complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) or cytotoxicity (ADCC) [116–118].

A critical secondary benefit of VLP-induced adaptive responses is epitope spreading. As CTLs and antibodies eliminate tumor cells, previously concealed or subdominant neoantigens are released and taken up by APCs. This broadens the immune repertoire, allowing recognition of tumor variants beyond the original vaccine formulation [107]. Clinically, personalized neoantigen vaccines in melanoma patients have demonstrated durable T cell responses and documented epitope spreading, underscoring the potential to control heterogeneous tumors and mitigate immune escape [119]. Modulation of the tumor microenvironment (TME)

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a complex network of tumor cells, stromal cells, immune infiltrates, and extracellular matrix components that collectively influence therapeutic outcomes [120]. VLP-based vaccines can remodel the TME toward an immunostimulatory state through both direct and indirect mechanisms. In immuno-oncology, “cold” and “hot” tumors are commonly defined by the density, spatial distribution, and functional state of antitumor immune infiltrates. Immunologically “cold” tumors are characterized by low or excluded CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, weak IFN-γ-associated inflammatory gene signatures, impaired antigen presentation, limited CXCL9/CXCL10-mediated effector-cell recruitment, and enrichment of immunosuppressive components such as regulatory T cells (Tregs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and M2-like macrophages. In contrast, “hot” tumors exhibit abundant intratumoral CD8⁺ T cells, active IFN-γ/CXCL9/CXCL10 signaling, enhanced antigen presentation, robust cytotoxic activity, and greater responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade [121–123]. Directly, PRR activation by VLPs leads to elevated secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-12) within the tumor site, which enhances recruitment of effector T cells and NK cells [12, 124]. Indirectly, VLP-driven activation of innate immune cells induces chemokine production, particularly CXCL9 and CXCL10, which facilitates infiltration of CXCR3⁺ effector lymphocytes into previously “cold” tumors and promotes their conversion into inflamed, immune-responsive lesions [125, 126] (Fig. 4). Beyond facilitating effector lymphocyte infiltration, VLP-mediated immunomodulation critically reprograms immunosuppressive cellular subsets. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and regulatory T cells (Tregs) are often enriched in the TME and contribute to immune evasion [127, 128]. VLP-induced pro-inflammatory signaling can downregulate M2-associated genes in TAMs, reduce the expansion of MDSCs, and impair Treg suppressive capacity [129]. Additionally, the destruction of tumor cells through CTL and NK cell activity releases danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as HMGB1 and ATP, further amplifying inflammatory signaling and antigen availability in situ [130].

The VLP-remodeled immunostimulatory TME further sensitizes tumors to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB). Preclinical models have demonstrated that combining VLP vaccination with immune checkpoint blockade (e.g., anti-PD-1 or anti-CTLA-4) synergistically enhances TME reprogramming [131, 132]. This strategy not only elevates the ratio of intratumoral effector T cells (CD8⁺/Th1) to regulatory T cells (Tregs) but also maintains functional activity of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes over time, thereby overcoming adaptive resistance — a key mechanism by which tumors evade immune attack after initial ICB therapy.

Synergistic mechanisms with combination therapies

VLP-based vaccines exhibit potent synergism when integrated with established cancer treatments, leveraging their unique immunomodulatory properties to overcome therapeutic resistance. The combination with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) demonstrates particularly compelling mechanistic synergy: VLP vaccination expands the pool of tumor-specific T cells, while anti-PD-1/CTLA-4 antibodies prevent the functional exhaustion of these activated lymphocytes. This dual approach sustains T-cell effector capacity within the tumor microenvironment, enhancing intratumoral IFN-γ production and reversing adaptive resistance mechanisms that typically limit ICB monotherapy efficacy [133, 134] (Fig. 5).

Beyond ICB, VLPs potentiate conventional cancer modalities through coordinated immunological actions [135]. When administered with radiotherapy, VLPs amplify the immunogenic cell death triggered by radiation—capturing released tumor antigens and danger signals (e.g., HMGB1, ATP) to prime broader adaptive responses [136, 137]. Similarly, chemotherapeutics that selectively deplete immunosuppressive cells (such as low-dose cyclophosphamide targeting Tregs) create a permissive microenvironment for VLP-induced antitumor immunity [138, 139]. This multimodal cooperativity transforms conventionally immunosuppressive treatments into components of systemic antitumor immunity.

Recent engineering advances further expand VLPs’ combinatorial potential. By packaging small-molecule drugs, RNA interference agents, or CRISPR-Cas components within their particulate structure, chimeric VLPs enable simultaneous immune activation and direct genetic/pharmacological targeting of oncogenic pathways [67, 140, 141]. For instance, VLPs co-delivering tumor antigens and immunomodulators (e.g., STING agonists) can achieve synergistic effects within a single particle [142]. This multifunctional delivery capacity positions VLPs as a versatile platform for next-generation precision immuno-oncology regimens.

Cross-species validation of mechanistic conservation

The remarkable conservation of antitumor immunity mechanisms across mammals provides a powerful rationale for utilizing spontaneous companion animal cancers as translational models [143, 144]. Key pathways—including T-cell activation, cytotoxic killing (perforin/granzyme), immune checkpoint interactions (e.g., PD-1/PD-L1), and cytokine signaling (e.g., IFN-γ, IL-12)—are highly conserved between humans, dogs, and cats [97, 145–148]. This shared immunological framework means that spontaneous tumors in pets, such as canine oral melanomas and feline oral squamous cell carcinomas (OSCC), are not merely analogous but are true homologues of human diseases [149–151]. They recapitulate the hallmark features of human oncology, including high mutational burdens, an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), and adaptive immune evasion mechanisms. Consequently, immunotherapeutic interventions that succeed in these models are inherently more likely to translate to human patients, as they are operating upon a conserved biological system.

This cross-species conservation is empirically supported by the congruent immunological outcomes observed. For example, studies in canine melanoma have shown that immunotherapy can induce robust and polyfunctional CD8⁺ T-cell responses, a significant increase in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), and a favorable shift in the TME (e.g., increased CD8⁺/Treg ratio)—mirroring the exact immune correlates of success sought in human clinical trials [150, 152, 153]. The survival benefits observed in these veterinary settings provide a highly predictive glimpse into potential human efficacy, bridging the gap between rodent models and human clinic.

Ultimately, veterinary oncology offers an unparalleled platform for translational immuno-oncology. The ability to evaluate long-term safety, optimal dosing, and immunopathological risks in an immunologically intact, large-animal model with spontaneous disease is a unique advantage over rodent studies. With several cancer immunotherapies already approved for veterinary use, such as the adoptive cell therapy ELIAS Cancer Immunotherapy (ECI) for canine osteosarcoma, the oral selective inhibitor of nuclear export (SINE) Laverdia-CA1 (Verdinexor) for canine lymphoma, and other modalities like Palladia (toceranib) for canine mast cell tumors, the real-world efficacy and safety data generated serve as a vital de-risking bridge, significantly accelerating and informing the development of human cancer vaccines.

Applications of VLPs in human cancer immunotherapy

The transition of virus-like particles (VLPs) from infectious disease vaccines to powerful tools in oncology represents a paradigm shift in cancer immunotherapy. Their unique biophysical properties—including highly repetitive surface structures, tunable size and morphology, and exceptional biocompatibility—make them ideal platforms for eliciting robust and specific immune responses. This chapter explores the diverse and expanding applications of VLPs in human cancer therapy, spanning three overarching strategic domains: their foundational role as nanovaccines, their adaptation as targeted drug delivery systems, and their cutting-edge use as vectors for genetic payloads. The progression of topics mirrors the evolution of the field itself, from prophylactic interception of virus-induced cancers to sophisticated therapeutic interventions against established tumors.

Nanovaccines for prophylaxis and therapy

Virus-like particles (VLPs) have become prominent platforms in cancer immunotherapy owing to their self-assembling nanostructures that mimic native virions, thereby enhancing immune recognition and antigen presentation. Their versatility enables incorporation of tumor-associated antigens, neoantigens, or viral oncoproteins, making them suitable for both prophylactic and therapeutic applications. This section outlines the spectrum of VLP-based cancer vaccines, beginning with clinically validated prophylactic vaccines, moving to investigational therapeutic candidates in clinical development, and finally highlighting promising preclinical strategies.

Licensed prophylactic VLP-based cancer vaccines

The most successful and clinically validated applications of VLPs in oncology are prophylactic vaccines targeting oncogenic viruses, notably hepatitis B virus (HBV) and human papillomavirus (HPV). Both vaccines utilize VLPs composed of viral structural proteins that self-assemble into highly immunogenic particles, inducing potent neutralizing antibody responses.

The HBV vaccine, based on recombinant hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) VLPs produced in yeast or CHO cells, has been in clinical use for over four decades [154]. By preventing chronic HBV infection—a major risk factor for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)—this vaccine has significantly reduced liver cancer incidence worldwide, especially in endemic regions such as East Asia and sub-Saharan Africa [155, 156].

Similarly, HPV vaccines (e.g., Gardasil®, Cervarix®) are composed of L1 capsid protein VLPs and provide protection against high-risk HPV types 16 and 18, which are responsible for approximately 70% of cervical cancer cases globally [157]. These vaccines have demonstrated near-complete protection against cervical intraepithelial neoplasia and HPV-related cancers in both women and men, and are now being adopted in national immunization programs across over 100 countries [158, 159].

These landmark successes underscore the feasibility, safety, and long-term efficacy of VLP-based vaccines in cancer prevention, providing a powerful precedent for the development of next-generation therapeutic cancer VLP vaccines.

Clinical-stage VLP-based vaccines

VBI-1901 represents a clinically advanced enveloped virus-like particle (eVLP) vaccine, targeting the cytomegalovirus (CMV) antigens gB and pp65 prevalent in glioblastoma tissues [160]. Administered with GM-CSF adjuvant to enhance immunogenicity, it demonstrated promising efficacy in a Phase 1/2a trial (NCT03382977) for recurrent glioblastoma (rGBM), achieving a 44% disease control rate and a median overall survival of 12.9 months—notably, one patient exhibited a 93% tumor reduction and survived over 40 months [161]. These encouraging results have propelled an ongoing randomized Phase 2b study, further investigating its potential against standard chemotherapy.

Beyond eukaryotic VLPs, bacteriophage-derived platforms like the Qβ VLP offer a robust and well-characterized alternative for cancer vaccine development. For instance, a vaccine displaying the melanoma-associated antigen Melan-A/MART-1 epitope along with the intrinsic TLR9 agonist CpG (G10) demonstrated the ability to elicit potent innate and adaptive immunity by combining repetitive antigen presentation for B cell activation with CpG-mediated dendritic cell maturation [162]. This approach has demonstrated translational promise: in a Phase I/II clinical trial, Qβ VLPs displaying the Melan-A/MART-1 epitope together with the CpG oligodeoxynucleotide G10 induced strong antigen-specific CD8⁺ T cell responses in patients with metastatic melanoma, highlighting the capacity of VLP-based vaccines to elicit durable cellular immunity [163]. Concurrently, highly engineered platforms like the adenovirus-inspired ADDomer VLP are being explored preclinically. The ADDomer allows for precise, high-density antigen display, as shown in a B16-OVA melanoma model where a multivalent formulation (ADDomer-Duo) adjuvanted with poly (I: C) elicited potent T-cell responses, enabling complete tumor rejection in preventive settings and significantly curtailing tumor growth in therapeutic models [164, 165]. Together, these strategies highlight the versatility of VLPs in overcoming immunosuppression and driving cytotoxic immunity.

The versatility of nucleic acid-based vaccine platforms is further exemplified by mRNA vaccines encapsulated in virus-like particle (VLP)-inspired delivery systems. BNT113, an mRNA vaccine encoding HPV16 E6/E7 oncoproteins delivered in lipid nanoparticle (LNP) carriers, represents one such approach currently in Phase 2/3 evaluation (AHEAD-MERIT trial) for HPV16-positive head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [166]. When administered with pembrolizumab, early immunomonitoring data indicate an increased effector-to-regulatory T cell ratio and sustained cytotoxic activity [167]. This strategy exemplifies the successful convergence of nucleic acid vaccine technology with biomimetic delivery platforms, extending the principles of VLP engineering to create potent new modalities for cancer immunotherapy.

Preclinical-stage VLP vaccines

At the preclinical level, novel multivalent HPV VLPs have shown promise in expanding coverage and cross-protection. The HPV chVLP integrates L1 pentamers from nine HPV types into a single particle, achieving broad neutralizing antibody titers comparable to Gardasil 9® [168]. Recent advances in VLP engineering facilitate the co-display of multiple tumor-associated antigens on a single particle, creating multivalent chimeric VLPs that combat tumor heterogeneity and broaden immune recognition by simultaneously targeting both humoral and cellular immune pathways [103, 169]. Furthermore, the intrinsic immunogenicity of VLPs can be enhanced by incorporating immune-stimulatory ligands (e.g., CpG oligonucleotides or specific TLR agonists), which promote dendritic cell maturation and potent cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses [170]. This synergistic integration of multiplexed antigen presentation with innate immune activation represents a powerful strategic platform for developing next-generation therapeutic cancer vaccines.

Other innovative platforms include the P22 VLP system, which displays model antigens such as ovalbumin (OVA) on its surface. This configuration enhances CD8⁺ T cell activation and reduces myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) infiltration in tumor-bearing mice, suggesting potential for therapeutic cancer vaccination [58, 171]. Additionally, a microneedle-assisted hepatitis B core (HBc) VLP vaccine delivering OVA257–264 peptides has demonstrated sustained antigen release, potent dendritic cell maturation, and the establishment of long-term immunological memory [172]. These diverse preclinical advances underscore the continued innovation in VLP design, highlighting their potential to address current limitations in cancer vaccine development through enhanced antigen presentation, tailored immune activation, and improved delivery strategies.

Targeted delivery of chemo-, immune-, and oncolytic agents

Beyond their role in vaccination, virus-like particles (VLPs) have emerged as adaptable nanoplatforms for the targeted delivery of chemotherapeutics, immunomodulators, and oncolytic agents. Their highly ordered capsid structures allow for precise surface engineering with tumor-homing ligands, such as RGD peptides or anti-HER2 affibodies, enabling selective binding to overexpressed receptors on malignant cells [173]. In parallel, the incorporation of tumor microenvironment (TME)-responsive linkers—such as pH-sensitive or matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-cleavable motifs—enables precise timing and localization of payload release, ensuring that therapeutic agents are predominantly discharged within the tumor microenvironment [69]. The therapeutic potential of these engineering strategies has been convincingly demonstrated across a diverse range of anticancer modalities, as highlighted in the following examples.

VLPs have been successfully engineered for the targeted delivery of conventional chemotherapeutics, significantly enhancing their therapeutic index. A prominent example involves the use of RGD-modified Hepatitis B core (HBc) VLPs for encapsulating doxorubicin (DOX) [141, 174]. This system demonstrated markedly enhanced tumor growth inhibition compared to control groups in preclinical models, while simultaneously reducing systemic toxicity. The RGD peptide mediates active targeting to integrin αvβ3-overexpressing tumor cells, ensuring precise drug delivery. In another innovative approach, Physalis mottle virus (PhMV) VLPs loaded with the acid-sensitive prodrug DOX-EMCH and coated with PEG exhibited pH-responsive release and superior antitumor efficacy. In a breast cancer mouse model, DOX-PhMV-PEG achieved complete tumor regression in 92% of treated animals, compared with only 27% in the free doxorubicin group, underscoring the potential of VLPs to markedly improve chemotherapeutic outcomes [175].

Beyond chemotherapy, VLPs are highly effective for delivering immunomodulatory agents to reprogram the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). Cowpea chlorotic mottle virus (CCMV) VLPs encapsulating the TLR9 agonist CpG-ODN 1826 have been designed to specifically target tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). This approach successfully activated TLR9 signaling, enhanced phagocytic activity of TAMs, and subsequently suppressed tumor growth in models of colon carcinoma and melanoma [176]. Similarly, the concept of using engineered viral vectors for localized immunomodulation is demonstrated by a modified vaccinia Ankara (MVA) virus expressing IL-12. Intratumoral administration of MVA-IL-12 effectively reprogrammed the TME by reducing immunosuppressive M2 macrophages while increasing pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages and recruiting dendritic cells. This switch from an immunosuppressive to an immunostimulatory microenvironment promoted robust CD8⁺ T cell priming and expansion, resulting in complete remission of established tumors in multiple murine models, including those resistant to checkpoint inhibitors [177].

Furthermore, VLPs have been explored as versatile platforms for oncolytic virotherapy, either by engineering them to deliver oncolytic peptides or by designing self-assembling virus-like oncolytic particles (VOPs). For example, D-chiral peptide-based VOPs encapsulating the lytic peptide LTX-315 have been shown to induce potent immunogenic cell death (ICD), characterized by the rapid release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as ATP and HMGB1. This creates a pro-inflammatory tumor microenvironment and, when combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors like anti-CTLA-4, can lead to near-complete tumor remission in murine melanoma models [178]. In addition, plant virus-based VLPs themselves can function as potent in situ oncolytic immunotherapies. Intratumoral administration of cowpea mosaic virus (CPMV) VLPs has been shown to directly activate innate immune pathways through TLR2, TLR4, and TLR7 signaling. In murine models of melanoma and ovarian cancer, CPMV treatment triggered robust infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells, suppressed both local and distant tumor growth, and synergized with checkpoint blockade therapy to induce durable tumor regression and long-term survival [179].

Together, these findings underscore the versatility of VLPs as targeted carriers for small molecules and biologics, demonstrating that the combination of molecular targeting and stimulus-responsive design can substantially enhance the efficacy and safety of cancer therapeutics.

Genetic payloads: mRNA, siRNA, and CRISPR/Cas systems

Building on these advances in small-molecule and protein delivery, VLP technology has also been adapted for the transport of genetic payloads, including messenger RNA (mRNA), small interfering RNA (siRNA), and gene editing platforms such as CRISPR/Cas systems. In these applications, design priorities include maximizing nucleic acid encapsulation, directing payloads to specific tissues or cell types, and reducing off-target effects to ensure therapeutic precision.

For mRNA-based therapeutics, VLPs enable innovative cell engineering strategies. Preclinically, HIV-mimetic T-FVLPmCAR particles have delivered CD19-specific CAR mRNA directly to circulating T cells, facilitating in vivo CAR-T cell generation in humanized mouse models. This approach achieved up to 83.5% tumor inhibition in B-cell lymphoma-bearing mice without inducing cytokine release syndrome, effectively bypassing the complexities of ex vivo T-cell manufacturing and highlighting its translational potential [180]. For siRNA-based therapeutics, bacteriophage-inspired VLPs have been bioengineered to package RNA interference scaffolds with high precision. In a recent study, Qβ VLPs were loaded with a broccoli light-up three-way junction (b-3WJ) RNA construct carrying dual siRNA/miRNA sequences and a fluorescent aptamer, enabling real-time monitoring of Dicer processing. When designed to co-deliver siRNAs targeting EGFR and IKKα, these VLPs effectively suppressed NF-κB signaling, impaired DNA repair, and sensitized glioblastoma cells to radiotherapy. In vivo, convection-enhanced delivery of these VLPs prior to a low-dose (2 Gy) irradiation doubled median survival from 31 to over 60 days without systemic toxicity, demonstrating a powerful synergistic antitumor effect [136].

For CRISPR/Cas-based applications, VLPs have been harnessed as transient and non-integrating delivery vehicles to maximize genome editing precision while minimizing systemic toxicity. In a recent preclinical study, lentivirus-derived VLPs engineered by fusing an RNA aptamer-binding protein to the Gag nucleocapsid efficiently packaged Cas9/gRNA ribonucleoproteins targeting the brachyury oncogene in chordoma. Intratumoral administration of these Cas9-VLPs achieved nearly 85% knockdown of brachyury, suppressed tumor growth, and exhibited transient Cas9 expression that peaked within 24 h and diminished by 96 h, thereby reducing the likelihood of off-target editing [181]. Complementing these experimental findings, a recent review emphasized that transient VLP-mediated CRISPR delivery represents a promising cancer therapeutic strategy, offering enhanced safety compared with persistent viral vectors, while also underscoring key translational challenges such as editing efficiency, tumor-specific targeting, immune responses, and scalable production [182].

Despite these promising advances, key translational challenges remain. The cargo capacity of many virus-derived carriers (e.g. AAV-based systems) is limited (< ~ 5.2 kb), which constrains the size of Cas9/sgRNA or other gene editing payloads [183]. Moreover, host immune responses are known to impair repeated dosing in various nanoparticle or viral vector systems, reducing efficacy in some settings, although exact reductions vary and have not been uniformly quantified. Delivery across the blood–brain barrier into the central nervous system remains inefficient as well, with many studies reporting substantial loss of payload or delivery efficiency, particularly when systemic administration is used [184]. Overcoming these limitations will require innovative strategies for enhanced cargo loading, immune evasion, and improved CNS penetration to fully realize the potential of VLP-mediated genetic medicine. Comparative and translational oncology: cross-species insights for VLP platforms

Virus-like particles (VLPs) have emerged as highly versatile immunotherapeutic platforms capable of integrating antigen presentation, innate immune activation, and targeted delivery within a single nanoscale architecture. However, despite substantial progress in platform engineering and encouraging preclinical results, translation into clinically effective cancer therapies remains limited. A major challenge is the lack of preclinical models that faithfully recapitulate the biological and immunological complexity of naturally occurring human cancers. In this context, comparative oncology provides a powerful framework for evaluating VLP-based immunotherapies by leveraging spontaneous tumors in companion animals to bridge the gap between reductionist murine models and human clinical trials [28, 208].

Companion animals, particularly dogs and cats, develop cancers that arise spontaneously within intact immune systems and evolve under the influence of lifelong environmental exposures, host genetics, and immune editing [28, 208]. These tumors exhibit substantial histopathologic, molecular, and immunological similarities to their human counterparts, including intratumoral heterogeneity, stromal remodeling, immune suppression, and treatment-induced selection pressures. As summarized in Table 2, cross-species comparisons reveal extensive conservation of key biological processes relevant to VLP-based immunotherapy, while also highlighting species-specific differences that must be considered during translational development.

Mechanistic conservation of VLP-induced antitumor immunity across species

The fundamental immunological mechanisms that underpin VLP-based cancer immunotherapy are highly conserved across mammals [235, 236]. Regardless of species, VLPs are recognized by pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs), including Toll-like receptors (TLRs), which trigger dendritic cell maturation, cytokine secretion, and antigen cross-presentation [237]. These events initiate adaptive immune responses characterized by expansion of antigen-specific CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes, activation of CD4⁺ helper T cells, and generation of humoral immunity [238].

In humans, these pathways have been extensively characterized and constitute the mechanistic basis for both licensed prophylactic VLP vaccines and emerging therapeutic cancer vaccines. Similar immunological responses have also been documented in companion animals. In canine tumors, intratumoral administration of CPMV/eCPMV virus-like nanoparticles has been associated with increased immune-cell infiltration, including T lymphocytes and CD8⁺ T-cell activation, together with broader remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment [239–241]. These findings are consistent with conserved VLP-mediated immunostimulatory mechanisms described in human and murine cancer studies, although direct evidence in feline tumors remains limited.

Beyond adaptive immune priming, VLPs can remodel the tumor microenvironment by activating and repolarizing tumor-associated myeloid cells, including macrophages, modulating suppressive myeloid populations such as MDSCs, and promoting local production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [203, 242, 243]. These immunosuppressive cell populations and pathways, including regulatory T-cell infiltration, tumor-associated macrophages, and PD-1/PD-L1 signaling, have been increasingly documented in canine and feline cancers [214, 216, 218, 219], suggesting that many TME-directed effects observed in murine models are likely to be biologically relevant in naturally occurring veterinary tumors.

Collectively, these observations support the concept that companion animal studies can validate VLP-induced immune responses under clinically realistic conditions rather than serving merely as larger-scale efficacy models.

Companion animals as translational intermediates between murine models and human trials

Traditional murine tumor models have been indispensable for elucidating the principles of VLP engineering and immunobiology. However, most syngeneic or xenograft models rely on rapidly growing tumors derived from relatively homogeneous cell lines and implanted into genetically uniform hosts with limited prior therapeutic exposure [244]. As a result, these models often fail to capture the full complexity of tumor evolution, immune suppression, stromal remodeling, and pharmacologic constraints encountered in clinical oncology [245].

Companion animals serve as a uniquely informative bridge between murine models and human clinical studies [246]. Dogs and cats develop spontaneous cancers within immunocompetent hosts, frequently after years of chronic inflammation, environmental exposure, and immune surveillance [209, 247]. These tumors exhibit substantial interindividual heterogeneity, prior treatment exposure, and clinically relevant stromal architecture [218, 248]. Moreover, the body size and physiology of companion animals more closely approximate human conditions, enabling more realistic assessment of biodistribution, dose scaling, toxicity, and repeated dosing [234, 249].

Veterinary clinical trials also permit serial blood sampling, advanced imaging, and repeated tumor biopsies, facilitating longitudinal evaluation of pharmacokinetics, immune biomarkers, and treatment response. This capacity is particularly valuable for VLP-based therapeutics, where correlating capsid-specific immunity, tumor penetration, and intratumoral immune remodeling is essential for understanding clinical performance.

Examples from comparative oncology have already demonstrated the translational value of spontaneous canine tumors in evaluating immunotherapies, including therapeutic vaccines, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and oncolytic viruses [246, 250, 251]. Applying similar study designs to VLP-based therapeutics can help de-risk candidate platforms before human trials.

Species-specific limitations and considerations

Although companion animal models provide important translational advantages, they are not direct surrogates for human patients and should be interpreted within the context of species-specific biological differences. First, the immune systems of dogs and cats, while broadly conserved, differ from humans in the composition and functional properties of certain lymphocyte subsets, cytokine networks, and antigen-presenting cell populations [252, 253]. These differences may influence the magnitude, kinetics, and durability of VLP-induced responses. Second, Fc receptor repertoires and expression patterns vary among species, potentially affecting antibody-mediated effector functions such as antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, phagocytosis, and immune complex clearance [254, 255]. Such differences are particularly relevant when evaluating VLPs that induce strong humoral responses or incorporate antibody-based targeting strategies. Third, the availability of species-specific reagents, validated antibodies, and genomic annotations remains more limited in veterinary medicine than in human oncology, especially in cats [256]. This can constrain mechanistic studies and biomarker development. Finally, veterinary clinical cohorts are often smaller and may include greater heterogeneity in diagnostic workup, prior treatment history, and follow-up schedules [234, 257]. While this heterogeneity enhances real-world relevance, it can complicate statistical interpretation. These limitations do not diminish the value of companion animal models; rather, they define the biological context within which veterinary findings should be interpreted and integrated with data from murine and human studies.

Operationalizing one health to accelerate VLP translation

The One Health framework extends beyond conceptual alignment between human and veterinary medicine by providing a practical strategy to address major barriers in clinical translation. In VLP development, this framework can link platform-specific challenges—such as tumor targeting, repeat dosing, pharmacodynamic monitoring, safety assessment, and patient selection—to opportunities uniquely enabled by cross-species research. This bidirectional translational framework is illustrated in Fig. 6.

For example, naturally occurring tumors in companion animals provide a clinically relevant setting for evaluating tumor targeting, repeat dosing, pharmacodynamic biomarkers, and tolerability before first-in-human studies [234]. Comparative molecular and immunological datasets can identify conserved response signatures, improve patient stratification, and guide the rational design of advanced VLP platforms [258, 259]. Parallel development in veterinary and human oncology may also inform translational study design and regulatory-facing discussions by generating complementary safety, pharmacodynamic, and efficacy data across species [233, 234].

Emerging technologies, including AI-guided antigen prioritization, computational protein or capsid engineering, and tumor-on-chip systems, may further strengthen this translational pipeline [260, 261]. In the context of VLP research, tumor-on-chip models offer a controllable platform to evaluate nanoparticle transport and penetration, immune-cell recruitment, antigen-presenting cell functions, and interactions with stromal or vascular barriers before in vivo testing [262, 263]. When integrated with comparative oncology studies, these tools can help prioritize the most promising strategies for clinical advancement.

Taken together, One Health provides a bidirectional translational framework in which discoveries in human nanomedicine inform veterinary applications, while spontaneous cancers in companion animals generate mechanistic and clinical insights that can accelerate human therapeutic development.

Comparative oncology as a strategic advantage for VLP-based cancer immunotherapy

The modularity of VLPs makes them particularly well suited to a comparative oncology approach. Their composition can be engineered to display tumor antigens or targeting ligands, incorporate immune modulators, and deliver therapeutic or nucleic-acid payloads, allowing platform optimization across different tumor types and host species [264, 265]. At the same time, conserved immunological principles, including PRR activation, antigen uptake and presentation by antigen-presenting cells, and downstream T-cell priming, provide a mechanistic basis for cross-species translation [223, 266].

Rather than viewing veterinary oncology solely as a testing ground for human therapeutics, a One Health perspective recognizes companion animals as active contributors to therapeutic innovation [259, 267]. Studies conducted in dogs and cats with naturally occurring cancers may reveal determinants of response, identify resistance mechanisms, and refine delivery strategies relevant to human therapeutic development. Conversely, advances in human VLP engineering can be adapted to address unmet needs in veterinary oncology.

This bidirectional relationship positions comparative oncology as a strategic advantage for VLP-based cancer immunotherapy, transforming naturally occurring cancers in companion animals into an integrated component of translational development rather than a peripheral validation step.

Because dedicated VLP-based cancer immunotherapy studies in client-owned companion animals remain less extensive than human and murine studies, current veterinary evidence is most appropriately interpreted through a translational-functional framework rather than by tumor type alone. Within this framework, companion animals can serve as: (i) spontaneous tumor models for evaluating immune activation and tumor microenvironment remodeling; (ii) clinically relevant large-animal systems for assessing delivery, biodistribution, repeat dosing, pharmacodynamic biomarkers, and tolerability; and (iii) bidirectional translational platforms for refining VLP engineering, patient stratification, and regulatory-facing development strategies. Collectively, these roles reinforce the value of comparative oncology as an integrated component of VLP translational development within the One Health paradigm.

Future perspectives: advancing VLP-based cancer immunotherapy through precision engineering and one health translation

Despite substantial advances in capsid engineering, antigen display, and combinatorial immunotherapy, the clinical translation of virus-like particles (VLPs) for cancer treatment remains at an inflection point. VLPs possess several attributes that are highly desirable for oncology applications, including multivalent antigen presentation, efficient lymphatic trafficking, potent innate immune activation, and modular incorporation of targeting ligands, adjuvants, and therapeutic cargoes, highlighting their unique potential as multifunctional nanoplatforms for precision cancer immunotherapy [268]. However, realizing their full therapeutic potential will require coordinated progress in manufacturing, translational modeling, biomarker development, and regulatory science. Near-term priorities: standardization, biomarkers, and comparative validation

The most immediate priorities for the field are those that directly influence clinical feasibility. First, manufacturing standardization remains essential. Variability in self-assembly efficiency, cargo encapsulation, surface modification, and post-translational processing can substantially affect immunogenicity, biodistribution, and stability. Establishing well-defined critical quality attributes, scalable Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) processes, and robust release assays will be crucial for improving reproducibility and facilitating regulatory review [271, 272]. Second, biomarker development should become an integral component of VLP studies. Although many preclinical reports demonstrate tumor growth inhibition, comparatively few identify mechanistic correlates of response. Future studies should incorporate standardized immune monitoring, including antigen-specific T-cell responses, cytokine signatures, intratumoral CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, and longitudinal assessment of anti-capsid immunity [266, 273]. Cross-species validation of these biomarkers in companion animal trials could improve patient stratification and support clinical development [233]. Third, comparative oncology studies should be more systematically integrated into development pipelines. Naturally occurring tumors in companion animals, particularly dogs, provide a clinically relevant setting for evaluating biodistribution, repeat dosing, pharmacodynamic biomarkers, and therapeutic combinations under conditions that better approximate human oncology than conventional murine models. Incorporating veterinary clinical trials earlier in development may help reduce translational attrition by linking standardized manufacturing, mechanism-based biomarkers, and clinically relevant validation within a unified translational framework.

Mid-term opportunities: personalized design and rational combination strategies

As VLP technologies mature, increasing opportunities are emerging to tailor platform design to the biological characteristics of individual tumors. One promising direction is the development of personalized VLP vaccines displaying patient-specific neoantigens [274, 275]. The structural versatility of VLPs enables multivalent antigen display and the incorporation of selected epitopes, immune stimulators, targeting ligands, or delivery-enhancing elements, making them attractive platforms for individualized immunotherapy [264, 276]. When combined with next-generation sequencing, immunopeptidomics, and neoantigen prediction pipelines, VLPs may support more efficient and scalable production of precision cancer vaccines [277, 278].

Equally important is the rational integration of VLPs with complementary therapies. VLPs are particularly well suited for combination strategies because they can prime de novo antitumor immunity while remodeling the tumor microenvironment [203, 242]. Synergistic combinations with immune checkpoint inhibitors, cytokines, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or oncolytic viruses may help overcome primary or acquired resistance by enhancing antigen release, promoting inflammatory reprogramming, relieving T-cell suppression, or amplifying local immune activation [279].

The success of these approaches will depend on mechanistically informed trial design. Rather than testing combinations empirically, future studies should leverage immune profiling, spatial analysis, and systems biology to identify tumor contexts most likely to benefit from specific VLP-based regimens [273, 280]. In this way, personalized VLP design and rational combination therapy can evolve from broad platform concepts into biomarker-guided translational strategies.

Long-term innovations: artificial intelligence, tumor-on-chip systems, and digital twins

Several emerging technologies, including AI-guided design, computational protein engineering, and tumor-on-chip systems, may transform the development and evaluation of next-generation VLP therapeutics by enabling more precise control over immune activation, tumor targeting, and therapeutic function [281, 282]. In the context of VLP-based cancer immunotherapy, tumor-on-chip platforms may enable physiologically relevant evaluation of VLP penetration, antigen presentation, immune-cell recruitment, and spatiotemporal tumor microenvironment remodeling under controlled multicellular conditions. These systems may also facilitate comparative assessment of delivery efficiency, immune activation dynamics, and therapeutic responsiveness across distinct tumor architectures and species-specific cellular microenvironments. Artificial intelligence and machine learning can accelerate antigen prioritization, predict structural constraints relevant to capsid assembly and stability, and optimize physicochemical features that influence immunogenicity and biodistribution. These approaches may reduce the experimental burden associated with iterative platform design and support increasingly rational engineering of immunotherapeutic nanomaterials [283].

Tumor-on-chip platforms represent another promising approach. By recreating multicellular tumor microenvironments under controlled conditions, these systems can be used to evaluate VLP penetration, stromal interactions, immune-cell recruitment, antigen-presenting cell functions, and localized cytokine responses before in vivo testing [263]. Such models may help bridge the gap between conventional in vitro assays and animal studies by providing a controllable intermediate platform for mechanism-based screening.

In the longer term, digital twin approaches integrating genomic, immunologic, imaging, and clinical data could enable in silico prediction of patient-specific responses to distinct VLP formulations [284, 285]. Although digital twins remain largely conceptual for VLP-based cancer immunotherapy, they illustrate how engineering, computational modeling, and comparative oncology datasets may eventually converge to support more precise and adaptive immunotherapy development.

One health as a translational operating strategy

A central conclusion of this review is that One Health should be viewed not merely as a conceptual perspective, but as an operational strategy for therapeutic development. By integrating discoveries from human oncology, veterinary medicine, nanotechnology, and immunology, this framework provides a practical means to address the major barriers limiting VLP translation. In this bidirectional model, innovations in human VLP engineering can be rapidly adapted to companion animal cancers, while studies in spontaneous canine and feline tumors generate mechanistic and clinical insights that inform human development. Comparative oncology thus serves as both a bridge to clinical application and a source of biological discovery. The convergence of VLP technology and One Health is particularly powerful because both emphasize modularity, adaptability, and cross-disciplinary integration. Together, they create a development paradigm in which engineering optimization, mechanistic validation, and clinical translation proceed in a mutually reinforcing manner.

Conclusions

Virus-like particles represent one of the most versatile and mechanistically powerful nanoplatforms currently being developed for cancer immunotherapy. Their ability to integrate antigen delivery, innate immune activation, and multifunctional engineering within a single nanoscale scaffold positions them uniquely at the intersection of vaccinology, nanomedicine, and oncology.

The evidence reviewed here indicates that VLPs can induce robust antitumor immunity, remodel immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments, and synergize with established immunotherapeutic modalities. At the same time, advances in manufacturing, biomarker development, and computational design are steadily addressing longstanding translational challenges.

Companion animals with spontaneous cancers provide a uniquely informative bridge between preclinical studies and human trials, enabling evaluation of VLP-based therapeutics under biologically and clinically relevant conditions. Through a One Health lens, these models become integral components of an iterative development ecosystem rather than ancillary validation tools.

Taken together, VLPs and comparative oncology constitute a highly complementary strategy for advancing precision cancer immunotherapy across species. By uniting engineering innovation with cross-species translational science, the One Health framework has the potential to accelerate the development of more effective and broadly applicable cancer immunotherapies for both human and veterinary patients.
