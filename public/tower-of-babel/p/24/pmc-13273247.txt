The 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans are associated with higher land, water and nitrogen use, and greenhouse gas emissions
Authors: Alon Shepon, Tamar Makov, David L. Katz, Gidon Eshel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dietary guidelines form the foundation of federal food policy, informing programs such as the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP), government food procurement, school meals, and military and hospital food services. The most recent guidelines, released in early 2026, advise avoiding “hyperprocessed” (generally known as ultraprocessed foods, UPFs), while also recommending higher overall protein intake chiefly from animal sources. Here, we quantified the greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, freshwater use, land use, and nitrogen fertilizer use of adherence to these new guidelines in comparison to the current Mean American Diet (MAD). We constructed isocaloric diets with varying degrees of added protein intake and protein sources that are similar to current dietary patterns, but with no UPFs as recommended. We find that although eliminating ultraprocessed foods from the MAD could yield substantial environmental benefits—given their large share of total intake and animal-based makeup—the recommended increase in animal-based proteins, will more than offset this environmental dividend in GHG, land use, and nitrogen fertilizer use by as much as 32%, but not in water use (i.e., water use is lower by 7 to 19% than the MAD). Across all environmental metrics measured, favoring plants- over animal-based foods leads to better environmental outcomes. Overall, adherence to the 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans would confer net environmental harm across most key metrics relative to the prevailing U.S. diet.

Every five years, a scientific Dietary Guidelines Advisory Committee (DGAC) is convened to synthesize the best available evidence and advise the federal government on what constitutes a healthy diet. This report is intended to shape the Dietary Guidelines for Americans (DGAs), which are foundational to U.S. food policy, with direct implications for federal food policy including SNAP, public school, military and hospital food, with far-reaching public health, economic, and environmental ramifications. In early 2026 the US Departments of Health and Human Services and the US Department of Agriculture (USDA) released their 2025–2030 dietary guidelines. Prominent criticism followed quickly, highlighting instances where the guidelines disregarded the DGAC and pertinent evidence, along with salient conflicts of interest (1, 2).

The guidelines received support (3) for emphasizing the avoidance of “hyperprocessed” foods, more typically referred to as “ultraprocessed foods,” or UPFs, a term which benefits from an operational definition (4). In contrast, increasing daily protein consumption from 0.8 g per kg body weight to 1.2 to 1.6 g/kg body weight (+50 to 100%) was not supported by the DGAC report, and has been met with widespread opposition (2, 3, 5). First, many Americans already overconsume protein, needlessly raising their environmental footprint while risking adverse health outcomes (6). Second, the DGAs lump together plant- and animal-based protein sources, emphasizing the advantages of the latter, despite substantial evidence for widely disparate health effects, with plant-based protein foods strongly preferable (7).

Given that the majority of protein currently consumed in the United States is animal-based (Dataset S1) and that the new inverted food pyramid places red meat and other meats at the top (indicating that they should be consumed regularly) (8), the public is likely to interpret calls to increase protein intake as encouragement to consume more meat, which is disproportionately environmentally costly (9, 10). Moreover, the new DGAs are branded as a return to eating “real food”, eliminating anything processed or industrial, which implies eating “natural” red meat. For most consumers, this means grass-fed beef. But producing grass-fed beef for the entire population at current or higher intake levels is simply impossible. A nationwide transition to grass-fed beef could only support half of the current beef consumption levels in the United States (11). Increasing protein intake by ≥50% thus necessarily means expanding industrial beef production.

Following the above rationale, we explore the environmental implications of adhering to the 2025–2030 DGAs. Building on the current Mean American Diet (MAD), we removed all UPFs and constructed diets with varying degrees of protein intake and protein sources, while keeping total caloric consumption constant (at 2,500 kcal/day). We then quantified greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, freshwater use, land use, and nitrogen fertilizer use for each diet and compared them against the MAD.

Results

We evaluated the environmental impacts of various isocaloric diets (2,500 kcal.cap−1.d−1) which adhere to the new 2025–2030 DGAs. To capture the effects of different protein intake levels and reduction in UPFs, alongside the MAD, the starting point of this analysis (the leftmost bar in all panels of Fig. 1), we examined different diets with a range of protein intake recommendations while eliminating all UPFs. This range starts from 0.8 g per kg weight (67 g.cap−1.d−1) corresponding with the former dietary recommendation to 1.6 g per kg weight (134 g.cap−1.d−1), representing the upper bound of the increased protein intake recommended by the 2025–2030 DGAs (Fig. 1; see Dataset S1 for further information). We also considered two additional diets, both with 1.6 g protein per kg body weight per day, from either plant (“1.6 g/kgPLANT”) or animal (“1.6 g/kgANIMAL”) sources, shown as the two rightmost bars of each panel.

The “1.6 g/kgPLANT” and 1.6 g/kgANIMAL’ diets both provide 1.6 g of protein per kilogram of body weight (≈134 g.cap−1.d−1), with protein derived primarily from plant-based and animal-based foods, respectively.

We find that the MAD protein intake level is 1.2 g/kg, 50% above the 0.8 g/kg protein level of the previous DGAs recommendation. The share of environmental impacts attributed to UPFs in the current MAD are substantial (40 to 58%), reflecting their large contribution to total energy intake and the high prevalence of animal-based foods in UPFs (see hatched yellow segments in MAD bars, Fig. 1). Our results indicate that GHG, land use, and nitrogen fertilizer dividends of eliminating UPFs from the MAD are outweighed by the impacts associated with increased protein intake (1.2 to 1.6 g/kg) by as much as 32%. In contrast, eliminating UPF and increasing protein levels to 2025–2030 DGAs reduced the water footprint compared to MAD by 7 to 19%. The “1.6 g/kgANIMAL” scenario had higher environmental impacts compared with the “1.6 g/kgPLANT” scenario, due to its greater reliance on animal-based foods, yet closely resembled “1.6 g/kg”, given the ubiquity of animal-based foods in the MAD.

Discussion

Our results demonstrate the changes in environmental impacts should the American diet follow the new 2025–2030 DGAs. Our findings highlight the high environmental costs of UPFs and animal sourced foods, and the need to reduce both to achieve sustainable diets. While eliminating UPFs carries a large environmental benefit, assuming all UPFs, including animal-dominated ones are eliminated, the addition of protein offsets most of these environmental gains. For land use, GHG emissions, and nitrogen fertilizer use the net effects are adverse. For water use, the net effect is beneficial, but would be even more so were the avoidance of UPFs combined with a lower protein threshold, and the sourcing of protein preferentially from plants. These findings relate to UPFs’ high content of animal-based ingredients and caloric dominance (more than half of MAD calories) and by the high water footprints of some UPFs.

In our analysis, high protein plant-based diets (“1.6 g/kgPLANT”) had considerably lower environmental impacts than equally protein rich animal-based diets and in some cases even lower than mixed, lower protein diets. There is considerable potential benefits for both public and planetary health if prevailing diets remove UPFs, and replace them with plant dominated whole foods. Notably, there is near unanimity that replacing animal with plant protein would simultaneously improve environmental performance and health outcomes (12). If UPFs are removed, and animal foods, notably beef, replace them- the net effect is, instead, net harm to planetary health, with uncertain net effects on human health. If plant-based UPFs are removed, animal-food UPFs are retained, and more animal foods are appended- net harm to human and planetary health alike are all but ensured.

Hard on the heels of hostility toward climate science, disregard for environmental and public health is the hallmark characteristic of the 2025–2030 DGAs. The dietary guidelines must be revised so as to realign them with overwhelming evidence and established science. Most urgently this requires prioritizing plant-based foods over animal-based ones. This would markedly enhance environmental integrity, public health, and food affordability in the United States (13).

Materials and Methods

The MAD and its breakup into food groups, NOVA categories (4), and total caloric intake is taken from Steele et al. (14) (Dataset S1). This average diet is derived from 24-h dietary recalls from the 2001–2018 cycles of What We Eat in America (WWEIA), as part of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). To better reflect reality and correct for known reporting biases, we scaled the MAD diet from 2,100 kcal to 2,500 kcal, while preserving protein content to approximately 100 g (15) consistent with loss adjusted food availability data (16). Protein and caloric values of food types were derived from FAO food composition tables (17). We used GHG, water, nitrogen fertilizer, and land impact coefficients from the Katz et al. (18) DIEM (Dietary Impacts on Environmental Measures) dataset. The advantage of using this dataset is twofold. First, it combines a set of reliable, high-quality LCAs specifically focused on the United States into robust statistics. Second, DIEM also combines individual item information into combined categories such as “refined grains,” which is better suited for calculating impacts of ready-to-eat foods containing multiple ingredients.

We constructed three 2,500 kcal diets which adhere with the former (0.8 g protein per kg body weight) and new 2025–2030 DGAs protein intake recommendations (1.2 to 1.6 g protein per kg body weight) and excluded UPFs (and their associated environmental impacts) by removing all NOVA group 4 foods (e.g., pizza, cakes, soft drinks; see Dataset S1) as recommended by the DGAs. These diets were constructed using quadric optimization that minimizes distance from the current MAD while delivering exactly 2,500 daily kcal and each diet’s specific protein intake. This approach allowed us to construct diets that vary in terms of protein intake (“0.8 g/kg”, “1.2 g/kg”, and “1.6 g/kg”), but closely resemble the MAD’s dietary patterns, including the proportion between plant- and animal-based foods. To calculate total protein content for each diet in grams, we used the average weight of an American adult, 84 kg (19). To evaluate the differences between animal- and plant-based foods, we devised two additional diets of 2,500 kcal and 1.6 g protein per kg weight (“1.6 g/kgPLANT” and “1.6 g/kgANIMAL”) which favor plant and animal protein sources, respectively. Finally, the contribution of animal-based foods to UPFs’ overall environmental impacts (hatched segments within the UPFs bars in Fig. 1) represents animal-based foods (e.g., milk drinks) as well as mixed dishes (e.g., pizza). For the latter, we extracted the share of animal-based foods and used the above environmental coefficients to derive their overall impacts (Dataset S1).
