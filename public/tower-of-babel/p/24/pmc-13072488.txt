Background

Post-coronavirus disease (COVID) cardiovascular complications remain a topic of considerable research interest. Coronavirus disease-19 (COVID-19) infection can exacerbate preexisting chronic heart failure, worsening cardiac symptoms and increasing the risk of adverse outcomes, even in cases of mild infection [1, 2]. This report describes a patient with preexisting presumptive familial dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) who developed worsening biventricular failure following multiple episodes of mild COVID-19 infection, despite receiving guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT). The patient demonstrated clinical improvement after the administration of intermittent intravenous calcium-sensitizing therapy.

Case presentation

A 37-year-old Indonesian man with advanced heart failure secondary to familial DCM presented to the emergency department with worsening dyspnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, and bilateral ankle edema over the preceding week. This was his fifth hospitalization for decompensated heart failure. On admission, his vital signs showed a blood pressure of 89/57 mmHg (mean arterial pressure (MAP) 68 mmHg), a heart rate of 89 beats per minute with frequent extrasystoles, a respiratory rate of 24–28 breaths per minute, and an oxygen saturation of 96% on room air. Physical examination revealed jugular venous distension, a loud pulmonic component of the second heart sound (S2), and pansystolic murmurs at the apex (grade 3/6) and the left lower sternal border (grade 3/6). Hepatomegaly and pretibial edema were present, while pulmonary rales were absent. The surface electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus rhythm, poor R-wave progression, and multifocal premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) (Fig. 2A). Serial echocardiographic evaluations performed every 6 months demonstrated progressive deterioration in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) from 31 to 20%, along with a reduction in tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) from 1.8 to 1.1 cm. Echocardiography also showed interventricular septal flattening and an increase in mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) from 37 to 49 mmHg, with a tricuspid valve gradient (TVG) of 35 mmHg. On admission, his NT-proBNP level was 4457 pg/mL.

The patient had a family history of DCM, with both his brother and father diagnosed with heart failure before the age of 40. Genetic screening was not available in our country. He had experienced a mild COVID-19 infection 1 year earlier, after which his heart failure symptoms progressively worsened. His regular medication regimen included furosemide 80 mg twice daily, ivabradine 5 mg twice daily, sacubitril/valsartan 100 mg twice daily, hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg once daily, and spironolactone 25 mg once daily. Despite receiving the maximum tolerated GDMT, he experienced two episodes of decompensated heart failure precipitated by mild COVID-19 infections. Carvedilol 1.625 mg twice daily was prescribed but later discontinued owing to several episodes of compensated shock.

Several investigations were planned throughout 2023 to determine the cardiomyopathy profile in this patient (detailed clinical timeline is presented in Fig. 1). Right heart catheterization (RHC) revealed a right atrial pressure (RAP) of 12 mmHg, pulmonary artery pressure of 67/42 mmHg (mPAP 48 mmHg), pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) of 33/39 (34) mmHg, pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) of 5.4 Wood units (WU), right ventricular stroke work index (RVSWi) of 513 g·m/beat/m2, and a pulmonary artery pulsatility index (PAPI) of 2.08. The thermodilution cardiac output was 2.6 L/min, with a cardiac index of 1.34 L/min/m2, indicating low cardiac output and cardiac index, elevated left atrial and pulmonary filling pressures, preserved right ventricular function, and pulmonary hypertension (PH) secondary to left heart disease (group 2, detailed parameters are provided in Table 1). These findings were consistent with advanced heart failure. Owing to the unavailability of mechanical circulatory support—such as a left ventricular assist device (LVAD), which would have been ideal—the patient was planned for intermittent inotropic therapy in addition to optimal GDMT.

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) and pulmonary embolism were excluded by computed tomography pulmonary angiography. (CTPA; Fig. 2G). Subsequently, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed left atrial (LA), left ventricular (LV), and right ventricular (RV) dilatation, with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 20% and a right ventricular ejection fraction (RVEF) of 22%. Prolonged T1 and T2 relaxation times, in the absence of myocardial edema, suggested microinflammatory changes. Nonischemic scarring was identified in the apicoanterior, basal anterior, and mid-basal anterolateral segments (Fig. 2D–F). The patient declined endomyocardial biopsy.

The patient underwent intermittent inotropic therapy, initially with dobutamine, followed by levosimendan infusion. Levosimendan was administered at an initial dose of 0.05–0.1 µg/kg/minute without a loading dose, to be repeated in 2–4 weeks. During observation, occasional multifocal PVCs were noted; however, no significant hemodynamic deterioration occurred. The levosimendan dose was not further titrated due to low blood pressure and symptomatic headache. In addition to intermittent inotropic therapy, GDMT was optimized. Sacubitril/valsartan was increased to 200 mg twice daily, spironolactone was maintained at 25 mg once daily, dapagliflozin 10 mg once daily was added, ivabradine was continued at 5 mg twice daily, and furosemide was maintained at 80 mg twice daily. Iron panel evaluation was not performed due to unavailability.

At 6 months after the initiation of levosimendan therapy, the patient demonstrated improved functional capacity, reductions in mPAP and TVG, resolution of the LV D-shaped configuration, and improvement in TAPSE (detailed parameters are provided in Supplementary Table S1). He did not experience any further hospitalizations. In addition, NT-proBNP levels showed a significant reduction of more than 30%. A Holter monitor performed 1 month after levosimendan initiation revealed frequent multifocal PVCs with a PVC burden of 15% and episodes of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia VT. Consequently, the patient was started on oral amiodarone 200 mg three times daily, with gradual dose tapering thereafter. Although an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) would have been the ideal intervention, its availability was extremely limited.

Discussion

We hypothesize that the clinical deterioration in this case was driven by worsening PH and right heart failure secondary to COVID-19 infection. These complications have been reported in several studies and are associated with prolonged hospitalizations [3–6]. Alterations in PAP and right ventricular (RV) function may persist for up to two months following recovery from the acute infection [7]. Although some studies have demonstrated reductions in PAP, RV dilation, RV global longitudinal strain (GLS), and TAPSE after COVID-19 infection, the mechanisms underlying COVID-19–associated PH are thought to involve a combination of diffuse pulmonary injury owing to alveolar inflammation (resembling group 3 PH) and thrombotic or thromboembolic processes with endothelial damage (resembling group 4 PH). These effects may persist even after mild COVID-19 infection [1, 8, 9].

The patient was diagnosed with presumptive familial DCM because two first-degree relatives (his father and brother) had heart failure with similar clinical characteristics, fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for familial DCM [10, 11]. Cardiac MRI also revealed an incidental finding of noncompaction segments in both the left and right ventricles (Fig. 2B–C). However, current consensus indicates that myocardial development does not support the concept of compact myocardium forming from initially noncompacted tissue, nor the idea that an arrest in this process results in so-called “noncompaction.” Recent publications have advised against the use of the term noncompaction cardiomyopathy, as it may not represent a distinct pathological entity but rather a morphological trait seen in epiphenomenon observed in pressure/volume overload (e.g., in athletic hearts, hypertensive heart disease, or valvulopathies) or a genetic predisposition (e.g., in sarcomeric gene mutations also associated with hypertrophic or dilated cardiomyopathies) [12].

Levosimendan is an inotropic agent with three principal mechanisms of action: positive inotropy, vasodilation, and cardioprotection [9, 13–15]. Current evidence suggests that, compared with conventional inotropes such as dobutamine, levosimendan may provide distinct therapeutic advantages in patients with advanced heart failure accompanied by elevated PAP and impaired RV function—making it a suitable option in this case. Levosimendan activates adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-dependent potassium channels in vascular smooth muscle cells, leading to vasodilation of both arterial and venous beds, thereby reducing RV preload and afterload. In contrast, while dobutamine primarily increases cardiac output, its effects on pulmonary pressures are less favorable and often inconsistent. Experimental models have demonstrated that levosimendan decreases RV afterload through pulmonary vasorelaxation, enhances RV contractility and function, and—most importantly—restores right ventricular–pulmonary arterial coupling via its long-acting metabolite, OR-1896, contributing to sustained hemodynamic improvement. These mechanisms are particularly beneficial in group 2 pulmonary hypertension secondary to left heart disease. However, levosimendan may be associated with tachycardia, systemic hypotension, and an increased need for vasopressor support [16, 17].

In a study by Jiang et al., patients with PH in World Healthy Organization (WHO) functional class IV demonstrated an improvement by one functional class (P = 0.008), along with significant increases in TAPSE (P < 0.005), Borg scores, 6-minute walk distance (6MWD), and reductions in NT-proBNP levels (P < 0.001) [14]. In patients with PAH functional class III and IV and severe RV failure, a 24-hour levosimendan infusion resulted in reductions in pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP), mPAP, mean RAP, PVR, and NT-proBNP levels, while increasing exercise tolerance. Notably, hemodynamic parameters and functional class remained improved even at 12 weeks after discontinuation of levosimendan [18].

In our case, the patient’s clinical condition deteriorated several months after the second COVID-19 infection. Serial echocardiograms demonstrated an increase in PV AccT and mPAP, TAPSE, worsening tricuspid regurgitation (TR) severity, elevated TVG, and a decline in LVEF. Hemodynamic evaluation confirmed the presence of advanced heart failure; however, implantation of a LVAD was not yet a feasible option. Therefore, an intermittent levosimendan protocol was initiated [19]. The results demonstrated notable improvements across clinical status, biomarker levels, echocardiographic parameters, and hemodynamic profiles (Fig. 3).

The patient received GDMT, including up-titration of sacubitril-valsartan, spironolactone, dapagliflozin, and ivabradine to the maximum tolerated doses, along with maximal sequential nephron blockade using furosemide, thiazide, and tolvaptan [20]. Beta-blocker therapy was not initiated due to recurrent episodes of compensated shock during previous trials. The burden of PVCs decreased following amiodarone therapy.

Limitation

This case report has several important limitations. First, genetic testing was not performed; therefore, the diagnosis of familial DCM was established on clinical and familial grounds without molecular confirmation. Second, this represents a single-center, single-patient experience, which inherently limits the generalizability of the findings. Third, long-term clinical outcomes could not be assessed owing to the relatively short duration of follow-up. Finally, post-therapy right heart RHC was not conducted, preventing objective evaluation of the hemodynamic response to levosimendan therapy.

Conclusion

Managing advanced heart failure with mixed-type pulmonary hypertension following COVID-19 infection presents a significant therapeutic challenge. A tailored, patient-centered approach is essential, and further investigation into novel therapeutic options is warranted. This case highlights the potential role of levosimendan as an adjunctive therapy for managing mixed-type PH post-COVID-19, particularly in patients with persistent congestion and limited access to advanced mechanical support.
