Spatial and functional polarization of cancer-associated fibroblasts with CXCR4-mediated immune modulation in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Daichi Nakamura, Takahiro Nishio, Yo Oguma, Kojiro Taura, Yukinori Koyama, Mengyang Zhan, Yoshihiko Kurata, Keisuke Okura, Tomoaki Yoh, Hiroto Nishino, Masayuki Okuno, Kaori Teranaka, Hanako Ogawa, Masakazu Fujimoto, Hironori Haga, Keiko Iwaisako, Akira Watanabe, Etsuro Hatano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) represent a predominant cell population in the tumor microenvironment (TME) of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). How the spatial distribution of CAF subtypes relates to immune modulation within the TME remains incompletely understood. This study aimed to characterize the spatial organization of CAF subtypes in HCC and to explore their potential associations with immune contexture.Methods:Spatial transcriptomic analysis was performed on 6 HCC cases to identify CAF-enriched clusters and their spatial localization. Immunohistochemical analyses were conducted on 132 HCC sections to examine the relationship between CAF-related features, immune cell distribution, and clinical outcomes.Results:Spatial transcriptomic analysis identified distinct CAF sub-clusters, including myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs), which were predominantly localized within tumor regions and enriched for extracellular matrix–related gene expression; inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), which were preferentially localized within tumor septa and associated with inflammatory signaling; and antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs), which were enriched in the peritumoral zone and exhibited gene expression patterns suggestive of immune-related functions. Interactome analysis suggested that apCAFs in the peritumoral region showed transcriptional patterns consistent with CXCL12–CXCR4–related interactions with CD8+ T cells accumulating at the tumor periphery. The enrichment of apCAFs on the outer side of tumor septa was associated with reduced intra-tumoral CD8+ T-cell infiltration and poorer prognosis, suggesting a link between apCAF distribution and an immune-excluded TME.Conclusions:Spatial transcriptomic profiling suggests that CAFs exhibit functional and spatial polarization in the TME of HCC. ApCAFs are associated with CXCR4-related immune exclusion at the tumor–stroma interface and may represent a stromal component relevant to immune modulation. These findings provide a framework for CAF–immune interactions and serve as a basis for future functional studies targeting the CXCL12–CXCR4 axis.

INTRODUCTION

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most common cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide.1 It predominantly develops in the setting of chronic liver diseases, including viral hepatitis, alcohol-associated liver disease, and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH),2 and most cases arise in fibrotic or cirrhotic livers. Increasing evidence indicates that the tumor microenvironment (TME), shaped by chronic liver fibrosis, plays a pivotal role in hepatocarcinogenesis.3 During chronic liver injury, fibroblasts differentiate into myofibroblasts that produce extracellular matrix (ECM) components to facilitate tissue repair; however, persistent injury results in excessive ECM deposition and fibrosis.4 In the liver, myofibroblasts mainly derive from activated hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and portal fibroblasts, contributing to the characteristic lobular architecture and fibrotic remodeling.2

The TME is now recognized as a critical determinant of tumor progression.56 Stromal components, including fibroblasts, immune cells, and endothelial cells, dynamically interact with malignant cells to regulate tumor growth and invasiveness.7 In HCC, where fibroblasts accumulate within a premalignant fibrotic background, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) constitute a major stromal population that shapes both tumor behavior and antitumor immunity.89 Nevertheless, the mechanisms underlying CAF activation and their context-dependent roles in HCC remain incompletely defined.

Recent studies have classified CAFs into myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs), inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), and antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs) based on transcriptional profiles, functional properties, and spatial localization.8 myCAFs promote tumor invasion through ECM production,1011 whereas iCAFs secrete inflammatory mediators that support tumor progression.12 apCAFs express major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II molecules and are implicated in immune regulation.13 Notably, CAFs may also exert tumor-restraining effects by forming physical barriers, underscoring their functional heterogeneity.1415 However, how these CAF subtypes are spatially organized in HCC and how they influence immune contexture remain largely unknown.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have emerged as a cornerstone therapy for advanced HCC.161718 The combination of atezolizumab and bevacizumab demonstrated superior survival outcomes compared with sorafenib in the IMbrave150 trial and is now established as first-line therapy worldwide.161920 Despite these advances, clinical responses to ICIs are heterogeneous, and primary or acquired resistance remains a major unmet need.21 Accumulating evidence suggests that CAFs contribute to immune exclusion and immunotherapy resistance through spatially and functionally distinct mechanisms.22

In this study, we sought to define the spatial distribution and functional interactions between CAF subtypes and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in HCC. By integrating spatial localization with immune crosstalk, we aimed to clarify how CAF heterogeneity shapes the immunological TME and influences disease progression, thereby providing a framework for patient stratification and the development of novel therapeutic strategies to enhance immunotherapy efficacy in HCC.

METHODS

Collection of samples for spatial transcriptomics analysis

Tumor specimens were prospectively collected from 7 patients who underwent hepatectomy at Kyoto University Hospital, including 6 cases of HCC and 1 case of adenoma. Case selection was based on tissue availability and suitability for spatial transcriptomic analysis, requiring well-preserved specimens containing both tumor and adjacent non-tumor regions within the same section to enable spatial comparison. Clinical characteristics are summarized in Supplemental Table S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C287. The adenoma sample was included as a non-malignant reference. All specimens were processed using standardized institutional protocols to ensure RNA quality compatible with Visium spatial transcriptomic analysis. Specifically, tissue specimens, ~10×10 mm in size, were excised from the tumor–non-tumor boundary immediately after hepatic resection. The samples were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 6–48 hours. Fixed samples were embedded in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) blocks and stored under refrigeration until sectioning. All research was conducted in accordance with both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul, written informed consent was obtained from all patients, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kyoto University Hospital (Approval Number: R3475-2).

Spatial transcriptomics analysis for human HCC specimens

Spatial transcriptomic analysis of human HCC specimens was performed using the 10x Genomics Visium CytAssist platform for FFPE tissues. Detailed experimental procedures and bioinformatic analyses are provided in the Supplemental Methods, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C287.

Immunohistochemical staining and image analysis

FFPE blocks from patients with HCC who underwent liver resection at Kyoto University Hospital were sectioned at 4 μm. After deparaffinization and endogenous peroxidase quenching, antigen retrieval was performed by steaming in DAKO Target Retrieval Solution (pH 6) for 40 minutes. Sections were blocked with DAKO Serum-Free Protein Blocking and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against αSMA (D4K9N; Cell Signaling Technology), CD8 (C8/144B; Cell Signaling Technology), CD4 (EP204; Cell Signaling Technology), and CXCR4 (ab124824; Abcam).

For visualization, αSMA was detected using Histofine Simple Stain AP (R) with ImmPACT Vector Red, CD8 using Histofine Simple Stain AP (M) with Vector Blue, CD4 using Dako EnVision+ HRP with HIGHDEF Yellow chromogen, and CXCR4 using Dako EnVision+ HRP with DAB. Positive areas were quantified using ImageJ2 (version 2.3.0/1.53q; NIH).

HCC patient cohort

Slides and a database of 136 HCC cases were used to validate the spatial transcriptomic findings. Among 132 patients who underwent liver resection at Kyoto University between August 2017 and May 2022, 4 cases were excluded: 2 postoperative deaths within 30 days, 1 case reclassified as intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, and 1 diagnosed with small duct cholangiocarcinoma. The database was maintained by the Department of Surgery, Kyoto University. Use of clinical records was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kyoto University Hospital (Approval No. R1721-3).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as medians (ranges). Comparisons among 3e or more groups were performed using ANOVA, with data expressed as mean±SD. For 2-group comparisons, the Student t test or the Welch t test was applied as appropriate, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for non-normally distributed data. Statistical significance is indicated as *p<0.05, **p<0.01, and ***p<0.001.

Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from liver resection to death or last follow-up, and recurrence-free survival (RFS) as the time to recurrence or death. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Cutoff values for group stratification were determined using the minimum p-value approach.

Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. Variables were dichotomized using median values, and multivariate models included covariates with p<0.05 in univariate analyses. The Spearman rank correlation coefficient was used for correlation analyses. All tests were 2-sided, with p<0.05 considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R and JMP Pro version 17.0.0 (SAS Institute).

RESULTS

Spatial transcriptomics reveals heterogeneity in HCC microenvironments

Spatial transcriptomic analysis was performed on 7 cases, including 6 HCC and a case of adenoma for comparison (Figure 1A). The underlying diseases of HCC cases included MASH (n=2), hepatitis C (n=2), hepatitis B (n=1), and alcohol-associated liver disease (n=1) (Supplemental Table S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C287). Each HCC case was histologically classified into tumor, tumor septa, non-tumor, and non-tumor stromal regions (Figure 1B). Next, we visualized the spatial distance from the tumor and tumor septa borders on the tissue image, where negative values represent the tumor side and positive values represent the non-tumor side (Figure 1C), to support the spatial annotation based on the histological localization around the tumor and non-tumor boundary. Cluster color-coding on spatial maps and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) plots for each case was illustrated, showing transcriptional heterogeneity between cases (Supplemental Figure S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C288). In addition, heat maps of the top 10 marker genes for each cluster displayed specific transcriptional patterns across cases, highlighting the diversity of the TME in each case (Supplemental Figure S2, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C289).

By adjusting for the potential heterogeneity of each specimen, integrated analysis of all cases revealed that the spots in HCC tissues were transcriptionally divided into 21 distinct clusters (Figure 1D). Because each Visium spot contains transcripts from multiple cells rather than a single cell, the identified clusters represent transcriptionally enriched regions rather than pure cell populations. The UMAP visualization demonstrated that these clusters were spatially segregated according to their histological classification (Figure 1E). Clusters 2, 4–10, 13, and 18 were predominantly localized to tumor regions; clusters 1, 3, 12, 14–17, and 21 were associated with non-tumor regions; and clusters 0, 11, 19, and 20 were enriched in tumor septa and non-tumor stroma regions (Figure 1F).

Consistent localization of CAF clusters within the tumor and tumor septa in human HCC

In the integrated human HCC analysis of 7 cases, spots co-expressing COL1A1 and ACTA2 were defined as fibroblast-enriched spots. Spots with expression levels of both genes exceeding a threshold of 1 were classified as fibroblast-enriched spots (Figure 2A). Mapping of fibroblast-enriched spots onto HCC tissue sections demonstrated their correspondence to histological structures, supporting the validity of this fibroblast classification (Supplemental Figure S3, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C290).

Focusing only on fibroblast-enriched spots, 10 fibroblast clusters (Fibro-0 to Fibro-9) were identified. Histological annotations of the sections were classified as tumor, tumor septa, non-tumor, and non-tumor stroma (Figure 2B). The composition of the histological classification within each fibroblast-enriched cluster was evaluated (Figure 2C). Fibro-3 and 7 were enriched in tumor regions; Fibro-0, 2, and 4 in tumor septa; Fibro-1, 8, and 9 in non-tumor regions; and Fibro-5 in non-tumor stroma. Fibro-6 was distributed across tumor septa, non-tumor, and non-tumor stroma at comparable levels.

Spatial mapping of fibroblast-enriched spots across sections confirmed consistent patterns of the spatial distribution of fibroblast clusters according to histological classification across cases (Supplemental Figures S3, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C290 and S4, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C291). Notably, case 3 showed a distinctive CAF pattern, with Fibro-7 localized in the tumor region and Fibro-1, Fibro-8, and Fibro-9 enriched in non-tumor areas. In contrast, the remaining 6 cases (cases 1, 2, 4–6, and 7) exhibited a consistent pattern in which Fibro-3 was enriched in tumor regions and Fibro-1 predominated in the non-tumor parenchyma. To illustrate both patterns, case 3 and case 4 were selected for the main figures (Figure 2D). Of note, Fibro-0, 2, and 4 collectively constituted the tumor-septa–associated CAF clusters. Fibro-0 and 2 were predominantly localized on the tumor side, and Fibro-4 cluster mainly accumulated on the non-tumor side, displaying a potential polarization of CAF clusters according to the distance from the tumor border (Figures 2F, G, and Supplemental Figure S4, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C291).

With reference to previously reported CAF classifications,8 fibroblast clusters were designated based on functional properties as defined by gene enrichment as well as spatial features as follows: Fibro-0 as iCAF, Fibro-1 as HSC-1, Fibro-2 as myCAF-1, Fibro-3 as myCAF-2, Fibro-4 as apCAF, Fibro-5 as Perivascular-Fibroblast-1, Fibro-6 as Perivascular-Fibroblast-2, cluster 7 as myCAF-3, cluster 8 as HSC-2, and cluster 9 as HSC-3 (Figures 2E, G, H). While HSC clusters were predominantly localized to areas corresponding to fibrotic scars in the sinusoidal area of non-tumor regions, perivascular fibroblasts (Fibro-5 and Fibro-6) are positioned adjacent to portal tracts and vascular structures (Supplementary Figure S5), based on the histological morphology of these structures. The other fibroblast clusters comprising the tumor and tumor septa were defined as cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and were classified into myCAF, iCAF, and apCAF categories. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis identified cluster-specific genes in each CAF cluster, including myCAF (Fibro-2, Fibro-3, and Fibro-7), iCAF (Fibro-0), and apCAF (Fibro-4) (Supplemental Figure S6, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C292). Furthermore, enrichment analysis revealed biological processes and functions associated with each CAF cluster (Figure 2E and Supplemental Table S2, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C287). For example, fibroblast cluster 3 (myCAF-2) exhibits high expression of genes such as PEGE4, ALDH3A1, ANG, AQP9, and CYP3A4, which are associated with liver function. Fibroblast cluster 0 (iCAF) showed high expression of CCN1, CCN2, DCN, and other ECM-related genes, suggesting its role in ECM formation and angiogenesis inhibition. Fibroblast cluster 2 (myCAF-1) exhibited high expression of SPP1, ANXA4, and MMP7, indicating its function in ECM remodeling. Fibroblast cluster 4 (apCAF) is characterized by genes such as CD74, CXCR4, and IL7R, which are linked to immune responses and T-cell activation. These findings highlight spatially polarized gene expression, with fibrotic genes, such as those in fibroblast clusters 0 (iCAF) and 2 (myCAF-1), enriched on the tumor side of the septa, while immune-related genes, such as those in fibroblast cluster 4 (apCAF), are enriched on the non-tumor side. Each CAF cluster contributed to assembling the TME, exhibiting a characteristic spatial distribution with a specific function represented by CAF classification (Figure 2H). Based on gene enrichment scores related to inflammatory and antigen presentation signatures (Supplemental Table S3, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C287), iCAFs and apCAFs were functionally supported as distinct subtypes from the myCAF cluster, which is primarily associated with extracellular matrix (ECM) formation. Among the immune-enriched and inflammation-enriched fibroblast clusters, Fibro-0 and Fibro-4, Fibro-4 (defined as apCAF) exhibited high expression of genes involved in antigen presentation, whereas Fibro-0 (iCAF) showed relatively low contribution to antigen presentation and displayed features consistent with an inflammation-associated cluster (Supplemental Figure S7, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C293).

Spatial distributions of TIL clusters in human HCC

The spatial and functional diversity of the lymphocyte population was analyzed across 7 cases using spatial transcriptomics to assess the behavior of TILs in HCC pathology. A gradient representation of TIL proportions was visualized in tissue sections (Figure 3A and Supplemental Figure S8, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C294), revealing that T-cell–enriched spots were predominantly located in non-tumor regions, particularly around the tumor septa. Scatter plots showing the proportion of TIL subsets (y-axis) against the distance from the tumor boundary (x-axis) further confirmed that T cells were abundant, traversing the tumor septa and extending into the non-tumor region (Figure 3B).

TIL-enriched spots with proportions above the median were extracted, and sub-cluster analysis of TILs was performed, which identified 8 distinct TIL sub-clusters (Figure 3C). T cells were concentrated in TIL-0, whereas B cells were enriched in TIL 1, 3, and 5. Plasma cells were broadly distributed across all TIL clusters, except for lower proportions in clusters 0, 2, and 6 (Figures 3D, E). Spatial mapping of TIL clusters revealed an approximate consistency in the localization patterns across the tumor and non-tumor regions. B-cell–enriched, or plasma cell-enriched, clusters were commonly found outside the tumor septa (Supplemental Figure S8, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C294). Notably, T-cell–enriched TIL-0 was consistently observed along the non-tumor side of the tumor septa and extended throughout the non-tumor region (Figure 3F), suggesting that these TIL clusters played a causal role in immune modulation, accumulating around the non-tumor side of the tumor septa.

Cellular interactions between apCAFs and TILs at the peritumoral interface of HCC

The specific spatial and functional distributions of CAFs and TILs might imply a potential modulation of the TME via cellular interactions, including CAFs and TILs. The interactions between CAFs, TILs, and tumor clusters were analyzed using CellChat. This analysis included CAF clusters 0, 2, 3, 4, and 7 (corresponding to iCAF, myCAF-1, myCAF-2, apCAF, and myCAF-3), T-cell–enriched spots, B-cell–enriched spots, plasma cell-enriched spots, and tumor clusters (2, 4–10, 13, and 18) (Figure 4A). To confirm that the analyzed interactions occurred between distinct cell types, we first performed CellChat analysis, focusing on cell membrane adhesion molecules (Figure 4B). Among the top 10 interactions detected between fibroblast cluster 4 (apCAF) and T cells, most were not detected in the reverse direction (from T cells to apCAF), supporting that the analyzed spots represent different cell types. These findings indicate directional signaling from apCAFs to T cells rather than an autocrine loop. Furthermore, a strong interaction via the VCAM1–(ITGA4+ITGB1) signaling axis was identified. VCAM1 expressed on apCAFs and VLA-4 (ITGA4+ITGB1) expression on T cells suggest a potential mechanism for limited T-cell infiltration into the intra-tumoral region.

Analysis of interactions originating from Fibro-4 (apCAF) with T-cell–enriched spots revealed specific signaling mediated by secreted factors, and pathway-specific projections identified CXCL as the dominant signal (Figure 4C). Further analysis of ligand–receptor pairs highlighted CXCL12–CXCR4 as the strongest signal (Figure 4F). ApCAF–TIL interactions occur at the non–tumor-facing side of the tumor septa, which represents the peritumoral immune-entry interface within the TME. These findings suggest that apCAFs and T cells, located close to the non-tumor side of the tumor septa, play a critical role in tumor immune modulation, with apCAFs functioning as a checkpoint where T cells pass through the septa.

In addition, secreted factor-mediated interactions between Fibro-3 (myCAF-2) and Fibro-7 (myCAF-3) and tumor clusters were analyzed. Both CAF clusters were found to exhibit strong signals related to angiogenesis (ANGPTL and VEGF) and factors associated with immunity and tumor progression (MIF and Midkine) (Figures 4D, E, and F). These results suggest that CAFs located within the tumor (myCAFs) may play a role in tumor progression by interacting with immune and tumor cells within the TME.

Differentiation trajectory of CAF subtypes from HSCs

Trajectory analysis was performed using only fibroblast-rich clusters from case 3, excluding fibroblast clusters 5 and 6, which were associated with the vascular system. This analysis aimed to elucidate the temporal differentiation process of HSCs into various CAF sub-clusters. Although the pseudotime trajectory was constructed using case 3, the relative positions of CAF subtypes in the integrated UMAP across all 6 HCC cases showed a consistent arrangement—myCAFs (Fibro-3, 7) in the tumor, HSC-like clusters (Fibro-1, 8, 9) in non-tumor liver, iCAF (Fibro-0) and myCAF-1 (Fibro-2) on the tumor side of the tumor septa, and apCAF (Fibro-4) on the non-tumor side of the tumor septa—supporting the generalizability of the proposed differentiation trajectory. A 3D scatter plot revealed that the stem region was predominantly composed of HSC clusters (Fibro-1, 8, 9). Branch 1 contained myCAF-1 (Fibro-2), localized within the tumor septa; branch 2 included myCAF-3 (Fibro-7), enriched in the tumor; and branch 3 consisted of apCAF (Fibro-4). Moreover, pseudotime analysis clearly illustrated that HSCs serve as the origin and temporally differentiate into distinct CAF clusters (Figure 5A). Next, to assess whether each pseudotime branch was influenced by cell type composition, we visualized the predicted proportions of major cell types along the trajectory (Supplemental Figure S9, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C295) and plotted the relationship between the predicted cell type proportions and pseudotime in each branch, dividing the pseudotime into 3 phases: early (0%–33%), mid (33%–66%), and late (66%–100%), based on the pseudotime (Supplemental Figure S10, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C296). As a result, in branch 3, the proportion of B cells increased toward the terminal end, whereas in branch 2, tumor cells became predominant shortly after branching. In the following step, we performed differential gene expression analysis between the Mid and Late stages within each branch to identify genes that change dynamically with pseudotime (Figure 5B). Among these genes, those that increased in expression with pseudotime progression were used as inputs for transcription factor analysis using iRegulon. In branch 1 (myCAF located in the tumor septa), HNF1B, CTCFL, and ARID5B were identified as activated transcription factors (Figure 5C), suggesting that activation by liver-specific repair-related factors may lead to excessive ECM production, ultimately contributing to the formation of a physical barrier enclosing the tumor. In branch 2 (myCAF located within the tumor), activation of lipid metabolism–related transcription factors such as SREBP1/2 and NR1H3 (LXRβ) was observed (Figure 5C), implying that these myCAFs may support the metabolic environment of the tumor and play a tumor-promoting role. In branch 3 (apCAF), ELF3, MTA3, and STAT5A were identified as activated transcription factors (Figure 5C), all of which are involved in antigen presentation, inflammation regulation, and cytokine signaling. These results suggest that these transcription factors may influence T-cell behavior and immune responses, providing molecular support for the role of apCAFs as immune barriers.

Functional characterization of intra-tumoral myCAFs

Next, a comparison of fibroblasts in HCC was conducted, focusing on fibroblasts in non-tumor regions (Fibro-1: HSC-1) and tumor regions (Fibro-3: myCAF-2) (Figure 5D). The volcano plot revealed that Fibro-3 (myCAF-2) exhibited higher expression of genes such as AMACR and ALDH3A1, which are involved in fatty acid metabolism and detoxification, DHRS2, which is related to redox reactions, and AQP9, which is associated with water and small-molecule transport across membranes (Figure 5E). These findings indicated that myCAF-2 was enriched with genes associated with hepatic function. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses further demonstrated that Fibro-3 (myCAF-2) was associated with detoxification, metabolism, bile production, and tumor development (Supplemental Table S4, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C287). Notably, GO analysis highlighted strong associations with retinol and retinoic acid metabolic processes, while KEGG pathway analysis emphasized the link to retinol metabolism (Figure 5F). These results consistently support that HSCs, which are responsible for vitamin A storage and metabolism, are likely the origin of myCAF-2 within the tumor.

Taken together, these trajectory-based and function-based analyses suggest that apCAFs (Fibro-4), which represent a distinct lineage and are localized to the non–tumor-facing side of the tumor septa, may be involved in T-cell positioning. Based on these observations, their potential role at the peritumoral immune interface was further examined in the subsequent clinical analyses.

CXCR4-mediated immune modulation by apCAFs in the outer side of HCC

To validate the spatial distribution and functional roles of CAFs and TILs, pathological and prognostic analyses were conducted in patients with HCC who underwent liver resection (Figure 6A and Supplemental Table S5, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C287). HCC sections that included tumor and non-tumor border zones were stained for αSMA (red), CD8 (blue), and CD4 (yellow) (Figure 6A). TME was analyzed according to the border zone divided into 6 areas based on distance from the tumor septa: [i] tumor (>100 μm inside the septa), [ii] septa-side tumor (≤100 μm inside the septa), [ii] tumor-side septa (≤100 μm inside the septa), [iv] non-tumor-side septa (≤100 μm outside the septa), [v] septa-side non-tumor (≤100 μm outside the septa), and [vi] non-tumor (>100 μm outside the septa) (Figure 6B). The positive areas for αSMA, CD8, and CD4 were quantified, and αSMA, CD8, and CD4 were most abundant in the outside tumor septa (iii and iv) (Figure 6C) and attenuated toward tumor and non-tumor areas.

The effect of immune modulation in the TME of HCC sections on patient prognosis was assessed. High CD8 expression in the tumor area [i] (p=0.032) and low CD8 expression in the non-tumor area [vi] (p=0.003) were associated with better overall survival (OS). In addition, high CD8 expression in the tumor area [i] (p=0.022) and low CD8 expression in the non-tumor area [vi] (p=0.007) were associated with longer recurrence-free survival (RFS) (Figure 6D). Multivariate analysis using a COX proportional hazards model revealed that high CD8 expression in non-tumor area [vi] (HR 3.72; 95% CI, 1.48–9.35; p=0.005) and low intra-tumoral/extra-tumoral CD8 ratio (HR 0.28; 95% CI, 0.09–0.90; p=0.03) significantly contributed to OS (Supplemental Table S6, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C287), whereas low intra-tumoral/extra-tumoral CD8 ratio (HR 0.53; 95% CI, 0.32–0.89.; p=0.02) was significantly associated with RFS (Supplemental Table S7, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C287).

The association between CAF activation and TIL distribution was indicated by the correlation between high αSMA expression in area [iv] and lower CD8 expression across areas [ii]–[iv]. In contrast, there was no significant correlation between αSMA and CD8 expression within the tumor (Figure 6E). The intra-tumoral/extra-tumoral CD8 ratio revealed that CD8 T-cell exclusion from the intra-tumoral region was associated with poor OS (p=0.002) and RFS (p=0.007) (Figure 6F). These findings suggest that high αSMA expression in area [iv], where apCAFs predominantly accumulated, restricted CD8 T-cell migration into tumors, and the resulting immune-exclusive modulation negatively affected patient prognosis.

To examine the spatial distribution and functional role of apCAFs in clinical HCC, we focused on CXCR4, which was identified among the top 10 apCAF marker genes in spatial transcriptomics (Supplemental Figure S6, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C292). In addition, the interactome analysis suggested that apCAFs interacted with TILs via the CXCL12–CXCR4 axis (Figure 4F). Gradient visualization of CXCR4 expression confirmed its high expression on the non-tumor side of the septa (Figure 7A). High expression of CXCR4 in the tumor septa and adjacent outer areas (areas [iii]–[v]) was associated with increased tumor malignancy, including larger tumor size and the presence of portal vascular invasion (Vp) (Figure 7B). Focusing on the immune status assessed by an intra-tumoral/extra-tumoral CD8 ratio, higher expression levels of CXCR4 and αSMA just outside the tumor septa were associated with inhibition of CD8 T-cell infiltration (Figure 7C). Furthermore, immunohistochemistry revealed the colocalization of CXCR4-positive regions and CD8 T cells accumulated outside the tumor septa (Figure 7D). These findings suggest that CAFs located just outside the tumor septa are enriched in CXCR4-positive cells, which may contribute to immune modulation in the TME by regulating CD8 T-cell infiltration into tumors, potentially serving as immune barriers associated with the immune exclusion status of HCC (Figure 7E).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we demonstrate that cancer-associated fibroblasts in HCC exhibit spatial and functional polarization within the tumor microenvironment. Spatial transcriptomic analysis identified distinct CAF subtypes with compartment-specific localization, with myCAFs enriched in tumor regions, iCAFs in tumor septa, and apCAFs at the peritumoral interface. Notably, apCAF enrichment on the non–tumor-facing side of tumor septa was associated with peripheral accumulation of CD8+ T cells, reduced intra-tumoral infiltration, and poorer clinical outcomes, indicating that septa-associated apCAFs contribute to an immune-excluded TME in HCC.

CAFs exert diverse, context-dependent roles within the TME, promoting tumor growth and metastasis while also displaying tumor-suppressive properties under certain conditions.23 This paradox likely reflects the marked heterogeneity and plasticity of CAFs, as previous studies have shown reversible phenotypic changes in response to microenvironmental cues or therapeutic interventions.1224 Consistent with this, our study defines a distinct spatial organization of major CAF subtypes within tumor septa. In particular, the localization of apCAFs at the tumor–non-tumor boundary suggests a role in immune modulation through regulation of CD8+ T-cell infiltration.

apCAFs express MHC class II molecules and modulate T-cell responses.25 In breast and pancreatic cancers, apCAFs promote regulatory T-cell differentiation and immune suppression,26 whereas in lung cancer, they may enhance T-cell activation,27 highlighting their context-dependent functions. Our data suggest that, in HCC, apCAFs regulate CD8+ T-cell infiltration through the immunosuppressive CXCL12–CXCR4 axis. Although the apCAF designation is based on MHC class II gene expression, our findings suggest that, in HCC, the functional impact of apCAFs may be more closely associated with CXCL12-mediated interactions with T cells and immune exclusion than with classical antigen presentation.

Tumors are classified into immune-inflamed, immune-excluded, and immune-desert phenotypes based on T-cell infiltration patterns.28 Immune-inflamed tumors show abundant intra-tumoral T cells and respond well to ICIs, whereas immune-excluded tumors restrict T cells to the peritumoral stroma, resulting in limited ICI efficacy.2129 In this study, apCAFs were shown to contribute to an immune-excluded TME in HCC, suggesting a mechanistic basis for ICI resistance in a subset of patients.

We also identified CXCR4 as a key marker of apCAFs in HCC. CXCR4, a chemokine receptor highly expressed in HSCs,2 implicated in tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis.30 In pancreatic cancer models, CXCR4 inhibition enhances intra-tumoral T-cell infiltration,31 supporting CXCR4-targeted strategies as a potential means to improve ICI efficacy in HCC.

Although immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are now standard therapy for HCC, response rates remain suboptimal, and resistance is common.32 Immunotherapy aims to restore cytotoxic CD8+ T-cell function by blocking immunosuppressive signaling,22 and combination strategies incorporating VEGF inhibition have shown efficacy by reversing VEGF-mediated immunosuppression and enhancing T-cell infiltration.3334 In this context, targeting CXCR4-expressing apCAFs may represent a promising strategy to overcome immune exclusion and improve ICI responses in HCC. In pancreatic cancer models, CXCR4 inhibition combined with PD-1 blockade enhances antitumor activity by increasing intra-tumoral T-cell infiltration,30 and a phase I trial in metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma demonstrated that the CXCR4 antagonist AMD3100 increased CD8+ T-cell tumor infiltration.35 The safety and efficacy of AMD3100 plus anti–PD-1 therapy are currently under evaluation in a phase II trial (NCT04177810).36 These findings suggest that CXCR4-targeted approaches may also be applicable to HCC.

Recent advances in combination immunotherapies, including atezolizumab plus bevacizumab, tremelimumab plus durvalumab, and nivolumab plus ipilimumab, have significantly improved outcomes in advanced HCC. However, therapeutic resistance remains closely linked to the immunological context of the TME. Accordingly, strategies that predict treatment response and overcome resistance through TME modulation are essential. In this regard, CAF-dependent immune exclusion mediated by the CXCL12–CXCR4 axis may serve as a relevant diagnostic and therapeutic biomarker. Nevertheless, translation of CXCR4-targeted strategies to HCC may be challenged by tumor heterogeneity, tumor septa architecture, and background liver fibrosis, which may limit intra-tumoral drug penetration, underscoring the need for further optimization of dosing, combination regimens, and drug delivery approaches.

This study has several limitations. First, although Visium spatial transcriptomics enabled spatial gene expression analysis in HCC tissues, its resolution is limited, as each 55-μm spot may contain multiple cells. Consequently, the cellular source of CXCR4 cannot be determined with certainty using spatial transcriptomics alone. While our analyses are consistent with CXCR4 expression in apCAFs, additional studies incorporating single-cell RNA sequencing and in vitro experimental approaches will be required to confirm its precise cellular specificity within the peritumoral niche. Nonetheless, Visium analysis reliably captured consistent spatial localization of fibroblast clusters, underscoring its value for spatial profiling. Second, the exploratory cohort included only 7 HCC cases, which may not fully reflect disease heterogeneity. Given the diverse etiologies of HCC, CAF composition and immune responses may vary by underlying liver disease. Although major etiologies were represented, the sample size precluded etiology-specific analyses of tumor septa morphology or extent. Larger cohorts stratified by etiology will be necessary to address these differences. Importantly, the consistent CAF spatial patterns observed across etiologies suggest a conserved organizational framework of the HCC-TME. Moreover, validation in an independent clinical cohort (n=136) confirmed the association between CXCR4-expressing apCAFs, immune-excluded TME features, and poor postoperative prognosis. Third, our conclusions are based on spatial and pathological correlations and do not establish causality. Although we identified robust spatial associations among apCAFs, CXCR4 expression, and CD8+ T-cell exclusion, mechanistic validation will require functional perturbation studies, such as selective apCAF depletion or pharmacologic CXCR4 inhibition.

In conclusion, we demonstrated a distinct spatial organization of CAF clusters within the tumor septa of HCC and identified CXCR4-expressing apCAFs that regulate CD8+ T-cell infiltration. These findings provide new insights into the immunosuppressive mechanisms within the HCC-TME and suggest that targeting CXCR4 could enhance the efficacy of ICI therapy. Future studies should explore the therapeutic potential of CXCR4 inhibitors in combination with ICIs to overcome immune exclusion and improve HCC treatment outcomes.
