Protocol for in vitro immunofluorescence staining in a Transwell co-culture system
Authors: Jenny YY Ooi, Yung Shing Tsang, Lawrence P McMahon, Limy Wong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryImmunofluorescence (IF) is a key technique for localizing target molecules. Here, we present a protocol for in vitro IF staining optimized for skeletal muscle myotube formation cultured on Transwell inserts. We describe steps for primary cell culture, fixing, permeabilization, and antibody incubation. We then detail procedures for quantifying myotube differentiation by IF microscopy to calculate fusion index. This protocol will enable direct IF staining on cell monolayer seeded on permeable support in co-culture or other Transwell studies.Subject areas: cell Biology, microscopy, Molecular Biology

Before you begin

Body tissues are composed of diverse cell types that work in concert to perform specific functions. To better reflect this complexity in vitro, heterogeneous co-culture systems that mimic the native tissue environment are highly valuable. One such approach is an indirect two-dimensional (2D) co-culture system, where two distinct cell types are cultured on opposite sides of a permeable membrane, allowing the exchange of soluble factors such as cytokines.1,2 Porous membranes serve as versatile platforms, effectively separating cellular microenvironments while enabling both physical and biochemical communication. Culturing cells on opposing sides of a membrane offers several key advantages: (1) it facilitates the development of barrier models that closely resemble physiological structures like the gut epithelium, vasculature, and lung; (2) it allows for easier recovery of cells from each side compared to three-dimensional (3D) co-culture systems; and (3) it maintains cells in a single focal plane, simplifying imaging and analysis.3,4,5,6,7

Immunofluorescent (IF) staining is a powerful technique for visualizing specific proteins or cellular structures in cultured cells, making it well-suited for co-localization and morphological studies. Traditionally, cells are grown on glass or plastic solid support, then fixed, permeabilized, and stained using a primary antibody targeting the protein of interest. This is followed by adding a fluorescently-labeled secondary antibody for detection. However, this is not well-suited for co-culture systems where one cell type is seeded on a permeable support and the other cell type is cultured on the basolateral compartment of the well.

Innovation

To the best of our knowledge, existing IF staining protocols for cells cultured on glass and plastic solid supports are not optimized for staining cells grown on permeable supports. Here, we present a modified IF protocol that enables direct staining and visualization of cells cultured on Transwell membrane. We optimized this method using primary human skeletal muscle myoblasts seeded on a Transwell insert, co-cultured with primary osteoblasts grown on the well below. While a previous study reported a modified IF staining protocol for HeLa cells grown on Transwell membranes,8 their method involved fixation and permeabilization using methanol at −20°C. We tested this approach in our cell model but did not achieve comparable results (see troubleshooting 1). In our optimized protocol, human skeletal muscle myotubes are cultured on Transwell supports, fixed with 10% neutral buffered formalin, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X in PBS, and stained with fluorescently labeled antibodies. The membrane insert is then carefully excised using a scalpel blade and mounted onto a glass slide for imaging. We quantify myotube formation by calculating fusion index using Image J. This modified IF staining protocol allows researchers to study the cellular changes in monolayer cells cultured on the upper permeable support in the context of co-culture experiments. This protocol can also be adapted for other Transwell studies such as drug efflux and cell migration assays.

Analysis

Myoblast fusion is the essential step for the formation of myofiber, the basic contractile unit of skeletal muscle. Thousands of multinucleated myoblasts recognize each other, fuse and differentiate to form myotubes. A myotube is defined as a syncytium with an elongated tubular shape that expresses structural proteins like myosin and contain two or more nuclei.9,10 Myotubes then mature into functional myofibers.

To measure the rate of differentiation, fusion index can be calculated. The fusion index is the ratio of nuclei within myotubes relative to total nuclei. Nuclei are visualized using DNA binding compounds (DAPI) and myotubes are typically identified by staining for myosin.

Statistical analyses

We recommend taking multiple images for one well/condition. Repeat the analysis for each image and calculate the average fusion index obtained from analysis of 4–5 images. If you have several experimental conditions, perform statistical analyses (t-test or ANOVA) to compare fusion indices across conditions.

Expected outcomes

This protocol describes the co-culture and the differentiation of primary skeletal muscle myoblasts, followed by the visualization of myotube formation in a Transwell insert. The myotubes are preserved by fixation and subsequently permeabilized to allow antibody binding. We used commercially available antibodies to validate the differentiation of skeletal muscle myoblast by staining the myotubes. We confirm myoblast fusion in our model as the myotubes are elongated and multinucleated (Figure 3A). This contrasts with myoblasts that appear spindle shaped and have only one nucleus (Figure 3B). Our immunofluorescent images suggest that the differentiation of myotubes is achieved with staining for myosin, phalloidin and DAPI (Figure 4). To measure the rate of differentiation, fusion index can be calculated.

Limitations

As with standard immunofluorescence staining, common limitations apply. Fluorescence signals are known to fade over time with light exposure and increasing temperature, therefore we recommend storing the glass slide in dark in the fridge and taking images on the microscope as soon as possible. The IF protocol is limited to fixed or “dead” cells. The fixation step can often lead to loss of information due to epitope masking, cell/protein conformational changes and loss of proteins.11 Background staining can also be a limiting factor; arising from non- specific antibody binding, auto-fluorescence of a sample and bleed-through between fluorescent channels in multiplex assays.12 Some proteins may not be well labeled by commercial antibodies, and will interfere with antibody penetration and protein detection.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Co-staining of myotubes with other proteins might not be well visualized by using the 10% NBF fixation method.

Potential solution

Optimization of different fixative agents (formaldehyde, alcohol-based fixatives) is necessary to achieve the best results for a particular antibody and protein. A previous study reported an IF staining protocol for HeLa cells grown on Transwell membranes using methanol at −20°C for fixation and permeabilization.8 Formaldehyde is the most common fixative used for cell culture. One major drawback of formaldehyde is that it needs to be freshly prepared and used within a short period as it polymerizes with time. We chose 10% Neutral Buffered Formalin (buffered solution of formaldehyde that contains methanol as a stabilizer) because it is more stable, less toxic and has a longer shelf life compared to formaldehyde. We firstly compared fixing and permeabilizing human skeletal muscle myotubes with 1) methanol and 2) 10% NBF and 0.1% Triton X in PBS. A side-by-side comparison showed that the intensity of myosin staining was much stronger and clearer when cells are fixed with 10% NBF compared to a much weaker signal when fixed with methanol (Figure 5). In Table 1, we list the main advantages and disadvantages of various fixatives. While 10% NBF works as a fixative for human skeletal muscle myotubes, we recommend researchers to test different fixatives to determine which is best for their cell type and antibody.
