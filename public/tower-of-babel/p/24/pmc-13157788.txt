The economics of quitting: estimating the uplift potential of Indian households through tobacco cessation
Authors: Montu Bose, Prashant Kumar Singh, Lucky Singh, Shalini Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTobacco use imposes significant economic burden, particularly in low and middle-income countries, diverting household income from essential needs. This study estimates the potential economic uplift of Indian households through tobacco cessation, informing tobacco control and poverty alleviation policies aligned with SDG 1 (No Poverty) and SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being).MethodsWe analysed data from the National Sample Survey 2022–23 Household Consumption Expenditure Survey covering 261 746 households. Households were classified into quintiles based on monthly per capita consumption expenditure (MPCE). Economic uplift was estimated by adding household tobacco expenditure back to total consumption, assuming increased financial capacity post-cessation, with separate analyses for rural and urban households.ResultsTobacco cessation could uplift 20.49 million households (10.6%) to higher economic classes, with greater impact in rural areas (11.64%; 17 million) against urban regions (7.26%; 3.5 million). Among the poorest households, 12.4% (5.62 million) could improve their economic status, enabling spending on essential needs. Middle-income households demonstrate substantial upward mobility potential (16.8%; 7.1 million).ConclusionThese findings demonstrate the direct economic benefits of tobacco cessation, emphasising the need to integrate tobacco control with poverty reduction strategies through enhanced taxation, cessation support programmes and awareness campaigns targeting both health and financial benefits.

Introduction

Tobacco use imposes substantial economic burden globally, with pronounced effects in low and middle-income countries (LMICs).1 2 Approximately 80% of tobacco users reside in LMICs, where significant household income is allocated to tobacco products.3 The financial implications are multifaceted, including direct medical expenses for treating tobacco-related diseases and indirect costs from lost productivity due to illness and premature death, contributing to global economic losses exceeding $1 trillion annually.4

In LMICs, tobacco expenditure detracts from essential spending on food, education and healthcare.5 Research indicates daily tobacco use by primary income providers is associated with 8% reduction in educational spending and 5.5% decrease in healthcare expenditures.6 This resource diversion perpetuates poverty cycles and hinders human capital development.7 From disease prevention perspectives, tobacco use is a leading risk factor for cancers and non-communicable diseases.8 Conversely, tobacco cessation frees household resources, enabling increased spending on nutritious food, education and necessities. University of Minnesota research found households where members quit smoking experienced lower food insecurity risk in subsequent years.9

Despite these insights, research gaps remain regarding long-term economic benefits of tobacco cessation at household levels in LMICs. This study examines how many households could escape poverty through tobacco cessation. Understanding this relationship is crucial for shaping tobacco control policies and poverty alleviation programmes. Given Sustainable Development Goals, particularly SDG 1 (No Poverty) and SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being), this evidence is vital for countries like India, where large populations face both tobacco use and economic deprivation.10 These findings could inform other LMICs, providing frameworks for integrating tobacco control with poverty reduction strategies.

The theoretical framework (figure 1) connecting tobacco cessation to economic mobility integrates multiple economic theories explaining how tobacco consumption affects household economic status. This framework posits tobacco consumption creates a ‘double burden’ through direct expenditure and indirect health costs, diverting limited resources from essential investments in education, nutrition and healthcare.11 This resource diversion contributes to poverty traps12 by preventing capital accumulation necessary for economic advancement. The framework suggests tobacco cessation acts as economic intervention by enabling resource reallocation toward productive investments13 14 with potentially stronger relative effects for lower-income and rural households due to Engel’s Law and diminishing marginal utility theory.15 Drawing from human capital theory,16 the framework proposes that resources freed from tobacco expenditure can be invested in education, health and nutrition—critical components for improving economic outcomes and facilitating upward mobility between consumption quintiles.17

This theoretical foundation establishes clear pathways through which tobacco cessation can function as an economic intervention, particularly for vulnerable populations. The framework predicts differential impacts across income groups and geographic areas, providing a theoretical basis for our empirical analysis of economic mobility potential.

Methods

Data source and sampling design

The National Sample Survey (NSS) 2022–23 Household Consumption Expenditure Survey (HCES) was conducted by India’s Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation using a rigorous scientific sampling methodology designed to ensure national representativeness.18 19 The survey employed multi-stage random sampling without replacement, treating rural villages and urban blocks as first-stage units (FSUs) and households as ultimate stage units (USUs). This stratified sampling approach covered all Indian states and Union Territories except inaccessible villages in Andaman and Nicobar Islands.

The survey achieved comprehensive coverage with 15,016 FSUs and approximately 18 households per FSU, totalling 261 746 households with 59% rural representation. The sampling design incorporated appropriate weights to account for differential selection probabilities across strata, ensuring estimates accurately reflect national population characteristics. Data collection was concluded from July 2022 to June 2023, providing seasonal variation capture across the survey period.

Data collection methodology

Data collection was structured into five comprehensive sections: list of households (LHQ), household characteristics (HCQ), food items (FDQ), consumables and services (CSQ), and durable items (DGQ). To minimise recall bias and capture consumption variations, different reference periods were employed: 30 days for cereals, pulses, sugar and salt; 7 days for perishable items including milk products, fruits, vegetables, meat and spices. For consumables and services, 7 day reference periods covered pan, tobacco and intoxicants; 30-day periods for utilities and services; and 1 year periods for major expenses like education and hospitalisation.

The survey meticulously recorded household consumption of various tobacco products over 7 day periods, including bidis, cigarettes, gutka, zarda, kiman, suri, leaf tobacco, cheroot, snuff and other forms. Quantities were measured in standardised units for combustible products and grams for non-combustible products, with expenditures documented in Indian rupees. This comprehensive approach captured both traditional and modern tobacco product consumption patterns across diverse Indian contexts.

Monthly per capita consumption expenditure classification and equivalence scale methodology

Monthly consumption expenditure estimation followed rigorous methodology detailed in the HCES 2022–23 Fact Sheet.18 The study employed Deaton’s equivalence scale,20 selected over alternative approaches21 for its superior theoretical properties and empirical appropriateness in Indian contexts. Unlike earlier methods that relied solely on food expenditure shares or ignored economies of scale, Deaton’s approach uses semi-logarithmic demand models incorporating household composition effects, spatial price variations and within-household economies of scale.

The equivalence scale assigns differential weights recognising varying consumption needs: weight of 1.0 for household heads, 0.7 for additional adult members and 0.5 for children. These weights reflect empirical evidence on relative consumption requirements while accounting for economies of scale in larger households.

Therefore, based on the age composition of the household, we have used the following equations to derive the adjusted household size (equation-1) and the adjusted monthly per capita consumption expenditure (MPCE) (equation-2) –

(1) Adjusted household size: γ=[1+(0.7δ_a+0.5δ_c)]

(2) Adjusted MPCE: χ=σ/τ

Where, τ=adjusted household size, χ = (adjusted) MPCE, σ=monthly consumption expenditure of a household, δ_a=number of adult members in the household excluding the head of the family, δ_c=number of children in the household.

Adjusted MPCE was divided into quintiles representing economic classes: poorest (first quintile), poorer (second), middle (third), richer (fourth), and richest (fifth). Classifications were made separately for rural and urban sectors of each state to accommodate cost-of-living differences, with Union Territories and north-eastern states combined due to smaller sample sizes ensuring statistical reliability and representativeness.

Analytical strategy and assumptions

Our analytical approach transforms weekly tobacco consumption values to 30-day periods by multiplying weekly expenditure by (30/7), then adding this amount to monthly consumption expenditure. This methodology rests on the fundamental assumption that households ceasing tobacco consumption in period t would experience proportional consumption capacity increases in the period (t+1). While this assumption provides valuable insights into economic uplift potential, it represents a simplified model that may not capture complex behavioural adaptations following cessation.

The analysis employs a static comparative approach, examining economic class transitions that would occur if current tobacco expenditures were redirected toward other consumption categories. This methodology aligns with established economic frameworks for examining consumption reallocation effects while acknowledging limitations inherent in cross-sectional analysis.

Statistical analysis and weighting procedures

All analyses incorporated appropriate sampling weights provided in the NSS dataset to ensure national representativeness and account for scientific survey design effects. Economic mobility was assessed by calculating proportions and absolute numbers of households potentially moving to higher MPCE classes following tobacco cessation. Descriptive statistics present sample characteristics stratified by rural and urban areas, with Chi-squared tests examining associations between background characteristics and residence.22

Discussion

This study provides groundbreaking empirical evidence quantifying economic mobility potential through tobacco cessation in India, revealing that tobacco consumption functions as a significant barrier to economic advancement for millions of households. The finding that 20.49 million households could transition to higher economic classes represents unprecedented documentation of tobacco’s direct economic constraints and cessation’s liberation potential. Our results demonstrate that tobacco cessation is not only a health imperative but also a powerful economic tool for poverty reduction.

The findings reveal that substantial proportions of households, particularly in the poorest and lower-middle economic groups, remain trapped in economic deprivation due to tobacco expenditures. In the bottom 20% MPCE class, 12.4% (5.62 million) households could improve their economic standing through tobacco cessation, while in the 20th-40th percentiles, 16.8% (7.12 million) households could move to higher economic classes. This represents the first direct quantification of households potentially escaping poverty by reallocating tobacco expenditures toward essential needs, reinforcing arguments that tobacco cessation serves as a catalyst for economic mobility.

Our results align with established economic theory regarding consumption patterns and poverty dynamics. The inverse relationship between income levels and tobacco expenditure shares confirms Engel’s Law predictions while demonstrating tobacco’s regressive burden distribution. The poorest households allocating 6.4% of consumption to tobacco face opportunity costs that compound over time, creating poverty persistence mechanisms consistent with poverty trap theories.12 The systematic variation in economic mobility potential across income quintiles demonstrates tobacco consumption’s role in perpetuating economic stratification, while resources currently allocated to tobacco could be redirected toward human capital investments—education, healthcare, nutrition—that generate long-term economic returns.16 The concentration of mobility potential among lower-income households supports human capital theory predictions regarding investment returns, suggesting tobacco cessation functions as an economic intervention with differential returns across the income spectrum.

The pronounced rural-urban differential, with rural households demonstrating 60% higher mobility potential (11.64% vs 7.26%), reflects multiple reinforcing factors including lower income levels, higher tobacco expenditure shares and limited economic opportunities in rural areas. Rural areas’ limited economic opportunities, infrastructure constraints and different social norms create environments where tobacco expenditure represents larger relative burdens, with the persistent rural advantage in mobility potential across all income levels indicating structural rather than purely income-based effects. This finding supports differential intervention strategies recognising geographic heterogeneity in tobacco’s economic burden and aligns with previous findings that rural tobacco users in India face greater economic instability due to high tobacco expenditures.23

These results complement international evidence from China,24 Vietnam17 and Bangladesh25 demonstrating tobacco expenditure’s role in perpetuating household poverty, while providing the first large-scale quantification of specific household numbers potentially achieving economic mobility through cessation. The scale of the potential impact—20.49 million households representing 10.6% of Indian households—suggests tobacco cessation could function as a significant poverty reduction intervention at national scale, placing tobacco control among major economic development opportunities, particularly given its concentration among populations facing the greatest economic constraints.

From a policy perspective, these findings suggest tobacco control measures should be integrated with poverty alleviation strategies to maximise economic development impact. Progressive tobacco taxation could generate dual benefits—reducing consumption while generating revenue for development programmes, with our findings suggesting tobacco taxes could be a particularly effective poverty reduction tool in rural contexts where mobility potential is highest. Traditional cessation programmes emphasising health benefits should incorporate economic messaging highlighting financial liberation potential, with rural populations and lower-income households as priority targets given their higher mobility potential and greater economic constraints. Public awareness campaigns should emphasise economic alongside health benefits, highlighting specific financial gains possible through cessation.

The reallocation of tobacco expenditure towards essential needs has profound implications for child health and nutrition outcomes. Resources freed from tobacco spending can be redirected towards nutritious food, healthcare and education investments critical for child development, with international evidence demonstrating tobacco cessation’s positive impacts on household nutrition and food security.9 In the Indian context where childhood malnutrition remains a significant challenge, the economic benefits of tobacco cessation could contribute meaningfully to achieving better health outcomes for children, particularly in rural households where the economic impact is most pronounced. The substantial resources freed through tobacco cessation—averaging 6.6% of MPCE for the poorest rural households—represent meaningful opportunities for improved child nutrition, healthcare access and educational investments. Evidence from Vietnam and other LMICs17 suggests similar patterns could be expected in India, where the 5.62 million poorest households potentially achieving economic mobility through cessation represent substantial child welfare improvement opportunities.

The economic impact of tobacco cessation extends beyond direct expenditure reallocation to include substantial healthcare cost reductions from preventing tobacco-related diseases. While our current analysis focuses on immediate consumption reallocation effects, comprehensive economic benefits include avoided medical expenses, reduced productivity losses and decreased premature mortality costs. Tobacco-related diseases impose significant healthcare costs on Indian households,26 27 suggesting that beyond immediate economic mobility through expenditure reallocation, households can mitigate long-term financial risks associated with tobacco-related illnesses.

International development organisations should consider tobacco cessation support as a poverty reduction strategy, not merely as a health intervention, given its potential to achieve dual objectives of improved health outcomes and economic development with particular effectiveness in rural areas of LMICs. Our findings suggest such approaches could maximise both health and economic impacts while addressing some of the most vulnerable populations in developing countries.

Limitations and future research

The cross-sectional design limits causal inference capabilities, preventing definitive demonstration of tobacco cessation leading to economic mobility. Our analysis assumes direct reallocation of tobacco expenditure to other consumption categories without behavioral adaptation, which may not fully capture complex adaptations following actual cessation. Tobacco expenditure measurement relies on 7-day recall periods, potentially introducing recall bias, while converting weekly to monthly expenditure assumes uniform consumption patterns that may not account for variations such as higher consumption during payment periods. State-wise analysis would provide crucial insights for targeted interventions but presents methodological complexities including varying cost-of-living adjustments and smaller sample sizes that limit the scope of this study. Health-related data limitations in the NSS prevent quantification of additional benefits from avoided healthcare costs, representing an important gap in comprehensive benefit assessment.

Future research priorities include longitudinal studies tracking households through tobacco cessation processes to provide causal evidence while capturing behavioral adaptation dynamics. State-wise analysis considering regional variations would enable more targeted intervention design. Research incorporating health expenditure data would quantify comprehensive economic benefits including avoided medical costs. Studies examining optimal intervention design combining economic incentives with health messaging could maximize cessation effectiveness while enhancing economic development impacts.

Conclusion

This study offers a unique and pioneering contribution to literature by quantifying, for the first time, numbers of Indian households potentially escaping economic deprivation through tobacco cessation. The findings demonstrate that tobacco cessation has the potential to elevate millions of households to higher economic classes, particularly among the poorest and rural populations. These insights provide a compelling case for integrating tobacco control policies with national poverty alleviation strategies. By prioritising tobacco cessation, policymakers can unlock significant economic benefits, enhance household financial resilience and improve overall societal well-being, making it an essential component of sustainable development efforts.

The evidence provides a robust foundation for evidence-based policymaking that recognises tobacco cessation as a pathway to economic empowerment and social advancement.
