What Is Inclusive Occupational Therapy for People Living With Dementia? Lessons From Collaborative Workshops With Diverse Communities
Authors: Sally Day, Kate Laver, Kylie Radford, Yun‐Hee Jeon, Rhonda Smith, Patricia Laranjeira, Grahame Smith, Gail Daylight, Gwenda Darling, Lee‐Fay Low
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveOccupational therapy interventions for people living with dementia have been shown to have positive outcomes for the person and family carers. However, people from culturally diverse communities and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples are largely missing from research studies. People with dementia from these communities have unique needs, and they can experience health inequity. The aim of this study was to use inclusive methods to explore key factors contributing to and identify potential adaptations required for culturally inclusive and acceptable occupational therapy interventions for people living with dementia.MethodsWe adopted best practice research methodology for working with people living with dementia from diverse communities. We conducted three collaborative workshops using co‐design techniques. Attendees discussed a hypothetical vignette using group problem‐solving. Findings were mapped to the key cultural adaptation framework elements using content analysis.ResultsTwenty‐one people from diverse communities with living experience of dementia attended one of three collaborative workshops. Participants were accepting of occupational therapy intervention components. Priority areas for delivering inclusive interventions were knowing the person, establishing a therapeutic relationship and expressing cultural humility.ConclusionThe therapist themselves is the key factor that supports the delivery of inclusive interventions. They should be aware of and be responsive to the cultural needs of diverse communities, adjust their therapeutic approach and be prepared to offer flexibility. Working in partnership fostered successful research collaboration, showcasing how people living with dementia from diverse communities can be supported to contribute to research and potentially influence ‘higher order’ healthcare issues.Keywords: community‐based participatory research, dementia, health equity, occupational therapy

Introduction

In Australia, over 400,000 people are living with dementia, including 1 in 12 people aged over 65 years and 2 in 5 people aged over 90 years; over 70% of people living with dementia live at home in the community [1]. People living with dementia from diverse communities have unique needs; for example, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples develop dementia at a younger age than non‐Indigenous Australians [2], and people living with dementia from culturally diverse communities report lower quality of life, while their carers experience higher stress [3]. Additionally, people's openness to seeking help for dementia is influenced by cultural perceptions of health and illness, which also influence interactions with healthcare providers [4]. People's ability to seek help for dementia for themselves or a family member can be compromised by limited understanding of the disease and available support services. This can be compounded by systemic barriers to accessible and culturally sensitive care [5]. Health and aged care providers need to consider dementia services that cater to the needs of diverse communities [6], the demands for which are projected to significantly increase in coming decades [7, 8].

People living with dementia are often referred to see occupational therapists, whose role is to promote health and well‐being by supporting people to participate in meaningful activities [9]. Occupational therapists refer to these meaningful activities as occupations; ‘the things people need to, want to or are expected to do’ [9]. For example, occupational therapists might keep someone involved in cooking at home by creating a clear, well‐lit space in the kitchen, developing a ‘check list’ of the steps to be completed and simplifying the chosen meal (e.g., using a pre‐made pasta sauce rather than making from scratch). The evidence shows that occupational therapy interventions for people with living dementia are beneficial to both the individual and their family carer [10]. These home‐based, dyadic interventions (working with the person living with dementia and a primary care partner) tend to be multi‐component, encompassing carer skill training, problem‐solving and task simplification. These interventions are also recommended in clinical practice guidelines for dementia [11].

However, people recruited to participate in dementia research, including research relating to occupational therapy, are not representative of the diverse Australian population [10, 12]. For occupational therapy to be effective for people living with dementia and their families, it is imperative that interventions align with people's cultural experiences, values and beliefs. This includes appreciation of their understanding of dementia, the meaning they attribute to the home environment and how caring is situated as a cultural activity [13]. Established psychosocial interventions for people living with dementia have been successfully tailored for culturally diverse communities. Tailoring can occur through translation of materials, addition of culturally relevant examples and resources, inclusion of additional intervention components such as role‐play or religious coping strategies and change from community to home‐based program for privacy [14]. It is suggested that the active components of an intervention are retained during cultural adaptation in order to maintain effectiveness [14]. Yet, there is limited research on the cultural tailoring required of occupational therapy interventions for people living with dementia to be inclusive of diverse communities.

We use the term ‘Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples’ on the advice of our Reference Group and in line with Aboriginal Health and Medical Research Council recommendations [15], acknowledging the diversity of community, cultural and personal identities encapsulated by this term. We use the term ‘living experience of dementia’ to describe people with dementia, their care partners and families. This is the preferred term specified in the Dementia Australia Consumer Engagement Framework [16].

This research is part of a body of doctoral work on improving occupational therapy for people with dementia from diverse communities conducted by the first author [17]. In this study, we aimed to explore key factors contributing to and identify potential adaptations required for culturally inclusive and acceptable occupational therapy interventions for people with dementia from diverse communities. This paper also discusses the application of inclusive methodologies for people with ‘living experience of dementia’ from diverse communities in practice.

Who Are We?

We are a group of health researchers and community advisors working in partnership. We have expertise in dementia care, diversity and interventions across disciplines of occupational therapy, psychology and nursing. We are committed to culturally inclusive research and practice with people with living experience of dementia; we each offer diverse perspectives to this research. Authors G Daylight, G Darling and RS are Aboriginal peoples who bring their lived experience and insights. Authors G Darling and GS are living with dementia and offer their living experiences and insights. Authors from ethnic minority backgrounds (YHJ, PL and LFL) offer lived experience and their extensive experience working with culturally diverse communities. Authors of European settler ancestry (SD, KL and KR) acknowledge their privilege living and working within the Western knowledge systems inherent in Australia and the potential bias of their colonial lens. The authors acknowledge culture's place within broader society and are committed to respectful research partnerships with people with living experience of their culture or condition. All authors, with the exception of GS, are female.

Methods

Ethics Approval and Consent

This study was approved by the University of Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) (2021/403). The inclusion of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples in this study was approved by the Aboriginal Health and Medical Research Council (AH&MRC) Ethics Committee (1817/21). For all research involving Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, we adhered to the CONSIDER statement for reporting [18]. All attendees provided written or verbal consent to participate, in English or in another preferred language, prior to commencing. We considered active engagement and participation in workshops an indication of ongoing consent [19].

Study Design

We utilised qualitative, collaborative workshops based on co‐design principles to achieve the research aim. Our study design was informed by transformative research methodologies, an approach to empower marginalised communities with living experience of the research question [20]. Acknowledging the power imbalance inherent in research with diverse communities, we drew on co‐design techniques to allow flexibility and enhance true partnership between the researchers and the community members [21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Co‐design in research highlights community insights, enables equal contributions between stakeholders, values the process of generating ideas and supports inclusion [21]. Using inclusive co‐design with people living with dementia, people from culturally diverse communities and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples offered the opportunity to value different knowledge and amplify the voices of people often missing from research, including people living with dementia from diverse communities [2, 24, 26]. The study is reported in accordance with the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research guidelines [27].

Gibson et al. reflected that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples are ‘active and self‐determining partners in the development and delivery of [occupational therapy] practice, policies and projects that affect our health, wellbeing and occupations’ [25] (p. 218). In this vein, we adopted research methods and provided supports that would be inclusive of and empowering to the diverse communities involved as well as being accessible to people living with dementia. Some examples of the supports included using tangible materials for people with dementia, avoiding questions that appeared to be testing people and providing people with the opportunity to participate in their preferred language or using their preferred mode of communication [23, 24, 26].

Reference Group

As we were working with diverse communities often under‐represented in dementia research, we formed a Reference Group reflective of our target populations (people with living experience of dementia, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples and culturally and linguistically diverse people). This approach ensured the research was meaningful and relevant to the communities [18] and offered an additional layer of cultural awareness and safety to the research. The Reference Group contributed to the design and conduct of the research, to data analysis and were authors on this paper.

Projective Techniques

Transformative research adopts research tools based on the community need [20]. We used projective techniques, a method that has been used effectively in dementia research, to enhance clarity of the research purpose and support the active involvement of people with living experience of dementia [28]. Projective techniques use creative activities to allow researchers to understand people's true (conscious or sub‐conscious) attitudes, beliefs or motivations [28]. We incorporated the use of a hypothetical case study, a ‘vignette’. This technique enabled researchers to present the story and watch and listen as people discuss the topic of interest, an approach respectful of the ‘Ways of Knowing and Ways of Being’ inherent within Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander research conduct [22]. Additionally, vignettes can be particularly useful when discussing sensitive topics people find hard or upsetting to articulate, for example due to stigma about dementia [5]. To complement the vignettes, we used photo elicitation techniques, shown to create a comfortable ‘space’ for participants to share their experiences [29]. The combination of these techniques has been shown to enhance the meaningfulness of the research to people with dementia [28].

We developed a vignette about a hypothetical couple, an older person living with dementia residing at home with their spouse. The older person living with dementia is finding it harder to manage everyday activities and is referred by their GP to a home‐based occupational therapy program for dementia (See Appendix 1, for complete scenario). The scenario incorporated the core components of community based occupational therapy interventions for people living with dementia [10] and progressed through stages (i.e., initial contact with the therapist, assessment and intervention). We chose photo stimuli relevant to each stage of the vignette to prompt discussions [29]. The Reference Group supported cultural tailoring of the scenario for each community workshop.

Sampling and Recruitment

We worked with an Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander community‐controlled organisation, and organisations specialising in culturally diverse care and dementia, to purposively recruit people with living experience of dementia from a range of cultural groups to participate in the workshops. Organisations approached eligible and potentially interested people in person at existing groups, or over the phone. We provided translated and culturally adapted flyers to support recruitment. All attendees were provided with remuneration to thank them for their time and contributions.

Data Collection

We undertook three 90‐min collaborative workshops, each workshop built on the discussions of the previous, aiming to compile viewpoints rather than obtain consensus. Then, attendees were invited to a final feedback session ‘to re‐present the research to the participants and to maintain their relatedness to the work’ [22] (p. 213) and to ensure each group's ideas and priorities were still reflected in the collated findings. Based on advice from our Reference Group and to facilitate engagement, initial workshops were held with specific rather than mixed cultural groups and with no more than 10 attendees. Attendees were invited as contributors to the research, rather than research participants. Workshops were held locally and were facilitated by a research team member (SD, an occupational therapist, PhD candidate and dementia researcher), in conjunction with culturally aligned, local facilitators (PL, RS, PH, all community workers); venues and local facilitators were familiar to attendees. Workshops were held on Country for our Aboriginal attendees. One workshop was held in Chinese, and an interpreter (ZZ) was present to support the researcher. One additional person (staff or family member) was present at each workshop to support participants' engagement.

We presented the vignette and used photo‐elicitation to stimulate engagement. We used ‘group problem solving’ through projective technique research methods to draw on attendees' experiences as well as their underlying values and beliefs [30]. Any ideas generated were written on wall‐mounted sheets of paper visible to the whole group. These ideas were refined and prioritised by the group during the workshop. Where the groups had differing viewpoints, both were retained. Workshops were not audio‐recorded in acknowledgment of historical injustices experienced by diverse communities in research and to emphasise the shared power dynamic in these workshops [20, 22]. Workshop facilitators retained field note reflections on how each workshop ran, including use of the projective techniques, attendees' level of engagement and their connection to the workshop activities [28].

Data Analysis

All data recorded on the wall‐mounted sheets of paper were typed up and imported into NVivo [31] for content analysis [32]. Categories were developed deductively based on the identified key common elements of cultural adaptation frameworks: context, content, engagement, fidelity and cultural competence [16]. One researcher, (SD), conducted preliminary coding, with coding to each category discussed iteratively by the researchers and then reviewed with the Reference Group. Field notes were discussed between facilitators after workshops and with the team between workshops to ensure the research retained a collaborative focus and that we reflected on and embedded any lessons learnt.

In keeping with co‐design principles, the analysed findings, including key categories and examples of data coded to each category, were presented to the workshop attendees at the final feedback sessions. Final session contributors refined and prioritised the findings and confirmed they were reflective of their group's contribution. Asking the attendees for their reflections on the findings supported a culturally appropriate, strength‐based approach to data analysis and interpretation [18].

Factors Contributing to Inclusive Occupational Therapy Interventions

All groups were generally accepting of a dyadic, home‐based occupational therapy intervention that included assessment and multiple sessions of intervention. The workshop attendees provided insights into how occupational therapy interventions for people with dementia could be more acceptable to and inclusive of diverse communities; these insights were themed as cultural adaptations to context, content and engagement and through therapist cultural competence (see Table 2 for summary of considerations). Attendees did not suggest considerations related to program fidelity.

Context

Context refers to possible adaptions to intervention delivery including format, setting and personnel involved. Attendees at all workshops were generally accepting of a dyadic approach (working with the person living with dementia and one family carer). However, they all agreed that to be culturally inclusive the format should change to include more people. There was no agreement across nor within workshops about who the additional people should be. Instead, attendees suggested the occupational therapists be open to working with a larger group and familiarise themselves with unique roles and stressors within families.

Workshop attendees were accepting of the setting of an intervention being in the home. However, they suggested occupational therapists need to be sensitive to areas of the home that may be considered private spaces. Many attendees agreed that they would be quite shocked if the occupational therapist looked around their home before they felt comfortable with the therapist. They would want to understand why they needed to enter or assess a specific area of the home. Aboriginal workshop attendees had specific suggestions regarding the setting of the intervention. These included that initial consultations may need to take place outdoors or away from the home while rapport and trust are being established. They felt this would ensure the person living with dementia and their family feel comfortable with the occupational therapist and the intervention.

Attendees suggested considerations about the personnel involved in the intervention delivery. People reflected on the characteristics of the occupational therapist and had different views about which were important. Chinese workshop attendees more strongly preferred someone who spoke their language or at least appeared of the same cultural background. Others prioritised personal and professional characteristics (see Engagement below). Attendees also suggested the therapists should collaborate with other services, such as support services, to deliver the intervention.

Content

All groups agreed that intervention delivery and communication should be tailored to each family. The occupational therapist could commence interactions by getting to know the person living with dementia and their family and asking, ‘how can I help?’ Attendees felt this approach would support the delivery of assessments and interventions that are understood by and aligned to each family's need. Attendees raised the need for flexibility in the timing of intervention delivery. Some attendees felt flexibility was required to enable families to manage competing demands. Others thought that the family might be slow to adopt the therapist's suggestions, however, as trust was developed this would change. Attendees also raised that families would need the therapist to revise any suggestions and strategies to build their confidence. They suggested that diverse communities may benefit from repetition and practice over multiple consultations to embed the occupational therapist's suggestions. Attendees reflected that interpreting for verbal information is helpful however written materials are often only available in English. They suggested occupational therapists should find out people's preferred language, arrange translation and provide written materials in the relevant language to facilitate engagement in the intervention. People recommended materials in English should also be provided to support English‐speaking family members.

Engagement

All attendees agreed that engagement in the intervention centred around building a trusting relationship by making a connection with the occupational therapist. Elements of trust building included having a consistent therapist, with skills and experience in dementia, who articulated the purpose of the intervention. Facilitators to engagement included having a therapist with positive therapeutic and communication skills and having the person living with dementia and their family invested in being an active part of the intervention. Practical strategies were suggested such as using interpreters or having a peer recommend and explain the intervention to support a new family to get on board. Barriers to engagement included therapists using technical or unfamiliar language, an intervention with unclear purpose or one misaligned with the person's roles, families feeling judged or powerless or the family's perception that an occupational therapist might have entrenched negative stereotypes about them or their culture. Across workshop groups and within groups, there was disagreement on the impact of the therapist's perceived professionalism on engagement. For example, wearing a uniform would alienate some communities, whereas for others, a uniform conveys a sense of authority that builds confidence. The difference in opinion highlighted the importance of a therapist understanding a community and tailoring their dress, demeanour and interactions to each individual family.

Cultural Competence

Workshop attendees expected therapists who had some understanding of their culture and were willing to ask questions to find out more. They expected therapists who were culturally appropriate in their interactions and had a cultural understanding of family. Further, they valued therapists who applied cultural humility to their interactions, which is recognising the diversity within and across cultures, being flexible in interactions and recognising power imbalances to build safe therapeutic relationships. In particular, attendees emphasised the importance of two‐way meaningful communication, ensuring that both parties had the same understanding of each interaction.

Discussion

This study used collaborative workshops with the aim to explore key factors contributing to and identify potential adaptation required for culturally inclusive and acceptable occupational therapy interventions for people living with dementia from diverse communities. Three key factors were prioritised by all. First, the therapist needs to ensure they understand the person living with dementia and the family before they commence the intervention. They can achieve this by preparing and spending time getting to know the family on the first visit. Second, a therapeutic connection should be established between the family and the therapist before and during intervention delivery. Thirdly, therapists must be culturally aware and demonstrate their cultural humility.

We found that attendees were generally accepting of the core components of occupational therapy interventions for people living with dementia. This finding suggests adaptations can be made to enhance cultural inclusivity and acceptability while retaining the effectiveness [14]. Several practical adaptations were agreed by all workshops to enhance cultural inclusivity and acceptability. The first was to include more people in the intervention. This includes occupational therapists both working with additional family members, beyond the dyadic approach and partnering with cultural advisors, support services or interpreters to deliver the intervention. This suggestion aligns with existing recommendations that partnering with families and broader support networks can help people from diverse communities feel valued and engaged [36]. The second suggested adaptation is to provide materials in both English and in the preferred language. This suggestion aligns with the cultural tailoring used in other psychosocial interventions for dementia [14]. Finally, the workshops highlighted the need for flexible intervention delivery, a factor noted in other occupational therapy research with diverse groups [37].

Due to low health literacy, communication barriers and cognitive impairment, compounded by systemic barriers, people from marginalised communities are often perceived as not being interested in contributing to research [38]. However, our workshop attendees were able to draw on their experiences to contribute to higher order issues, that is, the complex and systemic issues that extend beyond individual patient care. Attendees made suggestions about what people from diverse communities might want, expect and need from occupational therapy interventions for people living with dementia. The attendees prioritised factors linked to the theme of ‘engagement’, with the relationship between the therapist, the person living with dementia and the family being the most important factor. This relationship is key to successful uptake of interventions for people with dementia [39].

The workshops showed that people with living experience of dementia from diverse communities have a range of different experiences and expectations of occupational therapy. The workshops found that an occupational therapist's knowledge and active demonstration of cultural awareness and sensitivity contribute to a strong therapeutic relationship. A therapist's ability to demonstrate cultural knowledge and adapt their application of cultural skills combined with a reflexive approach to cultural awareness underpin culturally responsive caring in occupational therapy [40]. The groups' reflections of the need for sensitive consideration about where and how the intervention should be delivered echo earlier research with culturally diverse groups that suggested getting to know a family before starting an intervention and adapting home‐based assessments may facilitate success [13].

Reflection on the Application of Inclusive Research Methodologies in Practice

People from diverse communities have historically been excluded from dementia research [38]. Conventional research approaches are often not appropriate to facilitate recruitment and meaningful engagement of people living with dementia, culturally diverse groups nor Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples in research [41]. In this study, we adopted recommended recruitment, methodology and practical considerations required for inclusive research with people with dementia and with diverse communities [23, 24, 28, 36].

Our research demonstrates that it is possible to obtain input from people from diverse communities, including people with living experience of dementia, on their health care. Our partnership with culturally aligned organisations offered the opportunity for positive endorsement of the project to community members and facilitated recruitment [42]. Our recruitment experience reinforces the benefit of a community‐based, relational approach to recruitment highlighted in previous research [38].

We facilitated active participation from attendees, people with living experience of dementia from diverse communities, in the workshops by using co‐design techniques informed by transformative research methodologies. We prioritised building relationships and developing genuine partnerships between researchers and attendees as the foundation of equitable research [20]. We spent time at the outset of each workshop building trust by creating a safe space, showing we were open to honest feedback, and ensuring people felt empowered to contribute and share their insights and stories [42]. To facilitate collaboration and establish credibility, we felt it was important that we shared our interests and experience yet also spent time conveying our limitations (e.g., we didn't necessarily have experience of being from their cultural group nor receiving services for dementia at home) and validated the expertise that the community members would provide [43]. This approach seemed to positively influence the interactions in the workshops [44]; attendees wanted to teach us by sharing their experiences. The strength of the collaborative workshops was observed through a shift in the attendees' confidence as we described them as ‘experts’, demonstrated by their open reflections and confidence challenging the facilitator. Collaborative co‐design techniques have been successfully applied to research with people with dementia and to research with diverse communities [22, 23, 24, 28]. Our research showcases how the techniques can be successfully applied to diverse communities with living experience of dementia.

There were some limitations to this research. Our workshops were not representative of all the diverse cultural groups in Australia, in part due to the challenges in recruitment with some cultural groups due to stigma around dementia [5]. The findings may be representative of people who are already accepting or participating in dementia services rather than broader culturally diverse views of dementia and occupational therapy in Australia. Of the 21 attendees, only four were living with dementia, which may have restricted their contributions to the workshops. Further, the vignettes developed and photographs used may not have been meaningful for some community members, restricting their ability to engage in discussions, a recognised limitation of projective technique [28]. Also, we have considered Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples and culturally and linguistically diverse together as ‘diverse communities’; therefore, we may have missed culturally specific elements that are key to inclusive occupational therapy interventions. However, the purpose of our research was to understand the perspective of diverse communities on the factors, which contribute to acceptable and inclusive occupational therapy interventions for people living with dementia rather than create culturally specific recommendations.

Conclusions

Collaborative workshops using co‐design techniques provide people from diverse communities, including people with living experience of dementia, the opportunity to influence research and their own health care. Our workshops found that to meet the varied expectations of people from diverse communities, occupational therapists must develop deep cultural awareness, adapt and be flexible in their approach, ensuring inclusive and acceptable care for all. Further, workshops offer the opportunity to move beyond exploring individual experiences and empower people from diverse communities to shape broader conversations about how their health care is delivered, driving meaningful change.
