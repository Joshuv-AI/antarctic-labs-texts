TIGD6 in gastric cancer: exploring its prognostic value and therapeutic potential through molecular and clinical investigations
Authors: Quanli Han, Muhong Deng, Zhi Cui, Qi Wang, Dongbing Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastric cancer (GC) predominantly contributes to cancer mortality, with adenocarcinomas accounting for more than 95% of incidences. Early detection improves survival, but most cases are diagnosed at advanced stages due to subtle symptoms and rapid progression. The role of TIGD6 in GC is unclear.MethodsWe analyzed TIGD6 expression using TCGA data and correlated it with clinical features and outcomes in GC patients. Bioinformatics tools, including GSEA and single-cell sequencing, were used to elucidate TIGD6’s role in GC. In the GC cell lines AGS and HGC-27, TIGD6 was knocked down using RNA interference, and subsequent in vitro experiments were conducted to evaluate cell proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities.ResultsThe expression of TIGD6 was markedly elevated in GC tissues relative to normal tissues (p < 0.001). Higher TIGD6 levels were linked to residual tumors (p = 0.027), history of reflux (p = 0.019), and antireflux treatment (p = 0.0012). Increased TIGD6 expression was associated with decreased overall survival (OS, p = 0.009) and disease-specific survival (DSS, p = 0.008), and it served as an independent predictor of worse OS (p = 0.043). Knocking down TIGD6 in GC cells suppressed proliferation, migration, and invasion, while enhancing apoptosis through modulation of the Hedgehog signaling pathway.ConclusionTIGD6 is overexpressed in GC and linked to unfavorable outcomes. It could potentially function as a biomarker and therapeutic target for this malignancy. Future studies should validate its clinical relevance and explore its detailed molecular mechanisms. Collectively, this study provides the first functional, mechanistic, and immune-phenotypic characterization of TIGD6 in GC, positioning it as a dual biomarker and therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03219-6.Keywords: Gastric cancer, TIGD6, Prognosis, Hedgehog signaling pathway, Therapeutic target, Drug sensitivity

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC), a common gastrointestinal malignancy, is frequently the leading cause of cancer-related deaths [1]. In 2020, GC accounted for 7.7% of all cancer-related deaths and 5.6% of all cancer-related diagnoses [2]. More than 95% of GC pathological types are adenocarcinomas [3]. It is noteworthy that early detection of GC can yield a 5-year survival rate nearing 90% [4]. However, the majority of GC cases are identified at a more advanced stage due to the disease’s subtle initial symptoms and swift progression [3]. Despite significant improvements in surgical interventions and supplementary treatments such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy, the prognosis for patients with stage II or later stages of GC has shown a swift decline in recent years [5]. This underscores the pressing requirement for the discovery of novel biomarkers that could facilitate the early identification and targeted treatment of GC.

The emergence of large-scale genomic initiatives such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) has transformed our understanding of cancer biology, enabling the identification of molecular subtypes, driver mutations, and immune signatures across diverse tumor types. These datasets have been instrumental in uncovering prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic vulnerabilities. For instance, recent studies have identified exhausted CD8⁺ T-cell markers in breast cancer [6], highlighted CDK2 and IGFBP family members as actionable targets in glioma [7, 8], and revealed the prognostic significance of the voltage-gated sodium channel β3 subunit SCN3B in glioma [9]. In kidney renal clear cell carcinoma, cuproptosis-related genes have been linked to immune infiltration and metabolic reprogramming [10], while in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, auxiliary proteins such as CNIH4 and AIMP1 have been proposed as prognostic indicators [11, 12]. In gastric cancer, recent work has also shown that Helicobacter pylori infection may promote tumor cell migration and influence clinical outcomes [13]. The Tigger Transposable Element Derived 6 (TIGD6) gene encodes a protein that is part of the tigger subgroup within the broader pogo superfamily, which encompasses DNA transposons in humans. This protein shares a close relationship with DNA transposons observed in fungi and nematodes and exhibits a more distant affiliation with the Tc1 and mariner transposase. Additionally, it bears a striking resemblance to a key component of mammalian centromeres, specifically the major centromere protein B. Research into the function of TIGD6 in oncology is relatively sparse. A prognostic risk score model for colorectal cancer (CRC) has been formulated, incorporating seven pivotal genes linked to regulatory T cells (TREG), namely GDE1, GSR, HSPB1, AOC2, TBX19, TAM41, and TIGD6 [14]. However, the clinical significance and the comprehensive regulatory pathways of TIGD6 in GC patients still need to be fully explored [15]. Across malignancies, large-scale integrative analyses have recently converged on a core set of genes that exert cross-tumor biological and clinical relevance. For example, single-cell and bulk transcriptomic meta-analyses have nominated CENPA as a universal proliferation marker and actionable target [16]. Similarly, cuproptosis-related gene signatures have been profiled pan-cancer to reveal copper-dependent death vulnerabilities [17], whereas TRPM7 has emerged as a multi-tumor regulator of ion homeostasis and immune evasion [18]. Mitotic DNA-integrity checkpoint kinases [19], the homologous-recombination factor RAD51 [20] and disulfidptosis-related genes [21] have likewise been subjected to systematic pan-cancer characterization, underscoring the value of cross-tumor comparisons in prioritizing biomarkers that transcend tissue-of-origin. Guided by this conceptual framework, we interrogated TCGA and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) datasets to determine whether TIGD6—hitherto uncharacterized outside colorectal cancer—displays pan-cancer deregulation and represents a candidate therapeutic target in GC.

We evaluated TIGD6 transcript levels in diverse malignancies, with a focus on gastric adenocarcinoma, leveraging TCGA datasets to investigate its potential as a diagnostic biomarker [22]. We also investigated how TIGD6 expression correlates with the clinical characteristics and patient outcomes in GC. The research encompassed an analysis of the regulatory mechanisms involving TIGD6, its association with immune cell presence, and immune scoring in GC. Furthermore, we analyzed its genetic alterations in the disease setting [22]. The GSE125611 dataset served as a reference for TIGD6 expression validation. We employed RNA interference to knock down TIGD6 in gastric cancer cell lines AGS and HGC-27, verifying the gene silencing efficiency via quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and Western blot (WB) analysis [23]. Subsequently, we explored TIGD6’s influence on GC cell proliferation through in vitro assays, including the CCK8 assay, Transwell, and flow cytometry. Our findings suggest that TIGD6 holds promise as both a prognostic indicator and a predictor of immunotherapy efficacy in GC [24]. This breakthrough may enable tailored therapeutic approaches and refined diagnostic tools, ultimately improving clinical outcomes for GC patients through precision medicine strategies.

Material and methods

Expression of TIGD6 in pan-cancer and TCGA-GC

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) provided us with 375 samples of GC patients and 32 samples of normal tissue [25–31]. The molecule of interest was TIGD6 [ENSG00000164296.7]. We processed RNA-seq data using the Toil pipeline, which standardizes TPM (Transcripts Per Million) values from UCSC XENA for cross-platform compatibility with TCGA and GTEx repositories [32, 33]. Pan-cancer expression profiles were extracted from TCGA, while reference normal tissue data were sourced from GTEx [25, 34]. To normalize and prepare the data for analysis, we applied the Log2 transformation with an offset of 1. For data visualization, we selected ggplot2, tailored to the dataset’s format. Furthermore, we engaged relevant statistical techniques from the stats and car R packages to perform our analyses. TCGA-GC RNA-seq datasets were processed using the STAR alignment pipeline optimized for TCGA-GC, with expression levels quantified as TPM values [35]. Subsequently, the TPM values were normalized by applying a logarithmic transformation with an additive constant of 1, represented as log2 (value + 1).

Correlation of TIGD6 expression with clinical parameters and its diagnostic utility

We used software version R (4.2.1) for the statistics. Our approach to data curation involved the exclusion of any data points that corresponded to non-diseased states and non-clinical data. For the analytical phase, we leveraged the pROC R package to process the filtered data, while ggplot2 was the tool of choice for graphically representing the outcomes [36, 37].

The relationship between TIGD6 and prognosis

Utilizing the survival package, we conducted hypothesis testing based on the proportional hazards assumption and developed survival regression models. The graphical representation of these statistical analyses was achieved using the survminer and ggplot2 libraries [38, 39]. Key survival outcomes analyzed included overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival (DSS) [40]. Forest plots were constructed using R version 4.2.1 with the ggplot2 package [41, 42]. For the development of the nomogram, we integrated the rms and survival packages [43]. The nomogram was designed to predict OS, incorporating variables such as pathological stage and T stage.

Gene set enrichment analysis

The statistical analyses were performed in R 4.2.1, utilizing the clusterProfiler package (v4.4.4) [44]. Initially, the molecular identifiers in our dataset underwent a conversion process. Subsequently, GSEA was executed utilizing the same clusterProfiler package [45, 46]. Analyses were restricted to human genes, utilizing the canonical pathway gene set collection (c2.cp.all.v2022.1.Hs.symbols.gmt) containing 3050 gene sets [34].

The relationship between TIGD6 and immune infiltration

The degree of immune cell presence within the dataset was determined by applying the ssGSEA method, which is accessible through the GSVA R package, version 1.46.0 [47]. Immune cell infiltration levels were quantified using ssGSEA via the GSVA package (v1.46.0). This approach utilized a set of 24 immune cell-specific markers as delineated in the referenced literature [48, 49]. Statistical analyses were performed in R 4.2.1 using ggplot2 (v3.3.6), stats (v4.2.1), and car (v3.1–0) [47, 50]. The variables under investigation were categorized, and the choice of statistical techniques, which included functions from both the stats and car packages, informed the choice by the nature of the data. We proceeded with statistical analysis only after confirming that the data met the necessary criteria. Data visualization was performed using ggplot2, with statistical significance assessed via the Wilcoxon rank-sum test [34, 51–53]. Matrix and immune scores were calculated using the estimate package (v1.0.13) as described previously [54, 55].

Examination of TIGD6 genetic variations in GC patients

We sourced RNA sequencing data at the third level, mutation data in MAF, and relevant clinical details for GC from the TCGA database [56]. Somatic mutations in GC patients were analyzed and visualized using maftools, with horizontal bar plots revealing a high mutation frequency [22, 56].

The relationship between TIGD6 and response to drug treatment

To assess the responsiveness of TIGD6 to various drugs across a spectrum of cancers, we referred to the RNAactDrug platform, which can be accessed online through the provided URL: http://bio-bigdata.hrbmu.edu.cn/RNAactDrug/index.jsp. [22, 57].

Validation of TIGD6 expression from GSE125611 and cell lines

The dataset GSE125611, comprising nine GC and nine control samples, served as a resource for confirming TIGD6 expression levels in GC [58]. GES-1 normal gastric epithelial cells and GC cell lines (AGS, HGC-27, MKN28) were obtained from Beijing ChosenMed Clinical Laboratory Co., Ltd. [59]. For this study, GES-1 and MKN28 cell lines were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Kinlogix, Cat. No. M1002) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) [60]. AGS cells were maintained in F-12 K medium (Kinlogix, Cat. No. M1005) supplemented with 10% FBS. The HGC-27 cells were nurtured in MEM medium (Kinlogix, catalog number: M1003), supplemented similarly with 10% FBS. All cell cultures were maintained under a 5% CO2 atmosphere at a temperature of 37 °C. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed to assess TIGD6 expression in HGC-27, AGS, and GES-1 cells, following established protocols [15, 42, 61–63]. Primer sequences are provided below:

GAPDH-F: 5′-CAAGAAGGTGGTGAAGCAGG-3′,

GAPDH-R: 5′-AGGTGGAGGAGTGGGTGTCG-3′;

TIGD6-F: 5′-CCTCCTCAAGCTCAACAGCA-3′,

TIGD6-R: 5′-GATGGCTTGACTGACCACCA-3′.

Verification of interference with the TIGD6 effect

Two GC cell lines—AGS and HGC-27—were selected for transfection assays [64]. The efficacy of TIGD6 gene silencing was subsequently confirmed through qRT-PCR and WB analysis. We designed and synthesized the TIGD6 siRNA. The primer information was as follows:

si-TIGD6-1-SS: 5′-CUAGGAAUAGAUAAGAUUATT-3′,

si-TIGD6-1-AS: 5′- UAAUCUUAUCUAUUCCUAGTT-3′;

si-TIGD6-2-SS: 5′-GGAUCCUCUUGCUCAUAGATT-3′,

si-TIGD6-2-AS: 5′-UCUAUGAGCAAGAGGAUCCTT-3′;

si-NC-SS: 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′,

si-NC-AS: 5′-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3′.

Western blotting

Total cellular proteins were isolated using RIPA lysis buffer, and protein concentration was determined via BCA assay (Beyotime, Cat# P0012S). Equal protein amounts (30 µg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE, followed by electrotransfer to PVDF membranes [65]. To minimize nonspecific binding, membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk (Beyotime, Cat# P0216) for 1 h at room temperature before antibody incubation [66, 67]. Detection was performed using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit [68]. The panel of primary antibodies utilized encompassed TIGD6 antibody (HPA044599 from Sigma), PTCH1 (A0826 from ABCLONAL), PTCH2 (55,091–1-AP from Protein Tech), GAPDH (60,004–1-Ig from Protein Tech), GLI1 (66,905–1-Ig from Protein Tech), and GLI2 (18,989–1-AP from Protein Tech). For detection, horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies specific to rabbit (BL003A from Biosharp) and mouse (BL001A from Biosharp) were applied. The quantification of protein bands observed in the Western blotting analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Experimental groping

Three experimental groups were established for GC cell analysis: Control Group (si-NC): AGS and HGC-27 cells transfected with non-targeting siRNA (si-NC) to serve as baseline reference [69]. TIGD6 Knockdown Group 1 (si-TIGD6-1): GC cells treated with the first TIGD6-targeting siRNA sequence (si-TIGD6-1) to assess initial gene silencing effects [69]. TIGD6 Knockdown Group 2 (si-TIGD6-2): parallel cultures of AGS and HGC-27 cells transfected with a distinct TIGD6-specific siRNA (si-TIGD6-2) for comparative validation.

Cell proliferation assay

For CCK-8 viability assays, 3000 cells/well were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated with Servicebio’s CCK-8 reagent (Wuhan, China) for 2 h at 37 °C [70, 71]. The cellular metabolic activity, which serves as an indicator of cell viability, was assessed at 24, 48, and 72 h post-treatment. The assessment was conducted using a spectrophotometric technique that measured the absorbance at a wavelength of 450 nm [72, 73].

Cell migration and invasion assays

The cell migration capability was assessed using a Transwell chamber (produced by Corning, USA) with 8-micron pore membranes. These membranes were either coated with a diluted Matrigel solution (mixed at a 1:40 ratio with DMEM medium, also from Corning, USA) or left untreated. A 30,000-cell suspension, prepared in DMEM medium devoid of FBS, was introduced into the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with DMEM supplemented with 20% FBS to establish a chemotactic gradient [73]. After a 24-h incubation, the cells that migrated through the pores were fixed with paraformaldehyde solution for 20 min and subsequently stained with crystal violet solution for 15 min to enable visualization.

Flow cytometry

In accordance with the protocol of the Annexin V-FITC/7-AAD apoptosis detection kit (catalog number MA0428, sourced from Dalian, China), cells were cultured in the presence of Annexin-V conjugated to FITC at a concentration of 20 μg/mL, along with propidium iodide (PI) at a final concentration of 50 μg/mL [74, 75]. Subsequently, the cells were subjected to flow cytometric analysis using a FACScalibur instrument.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate the differences between the two study groups, we utilized an independent samples t-test, presenting the results as the mean ± standard deviation [76]. For correlation analysis between the groups, we applied Spearman’s rank-order correlation coefficient [77]. Additionally, the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was conducted to investigate how TIGD6 expression correlates with patient outcomes, considering a p-value threshold of less than 0.05 to denote statistical significance [61, 78–81]. All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the results. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) [82]. For each statistical test, a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance [83].

Correlation of TIGD6 with clinical traits in TCGA-GC patients

To investigate the clinical relevance of TIGD6, our study performed a thorough analysis to examine the correlation between TIGD6 expression levels in GC and various clinical parameters. The detailed results are presented in supplementary Tables S1-S2 and Fig. 1E–G, which demonstrate a statistically significant association between elevated TIGD6 levels and the presence of a residual tumor (p = 0.027), reflux history (p = 0.019), and antireflux treatment (p = 0.0012) in GC.

TIGD6's correlation with patient prognosis in TCGA-GC

Our study further delved into the impact of TIGD6 on the OS and DSS in GC patients, with results graphically represented in Fig. 2A, B. Individuals with increased TIGD6 levels demonstrated a notably reduced OS, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.56 and a 95% confidence interval (CI) from 1.12 to 2.17 (p = 0.009), and a reduced DSS, with an HR of 1.79 and a 95% CI from 1.17 to 2.75 (p = 0.008). These results imply that TIGD6 could be a prognostic indicator for GC, potentially guiding the formulation of therapeutic approaches. A univariate Cox regression analysis identified significant associations between overall survival (OS) and several factors, including T stage (p = 0.011), N stage (p = 0.002), M stage (p = 0.004), pathological stage (p < 0.001), primary therapy outcome (p < 0.001), age (p = 0.005), residual tumor (p < 0.001), and TIGD6 expression (p = 0.009). These results are detailed in Table S3. The subsequent multivariate analysis underscored the influence of treatment outcomes (p < 0.001) and TIGD6 (p = 0.043) on GC patients’ OS, as depicted in Table S3 and Fig. 2C; this established TIGD6 as an independent prognostic marker. To forecast the survival rates of GC patients at the 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year marks, survival analysis was conducted by integrating TIGD6 expression data with clinical parameters, as depicted in Fig. 2D.

Pathways involved in TIGD6 in TCGA-GC

GSEA was performed to explore the underlying mechanisms through which TIGD6 may exert its effects in GC. Nine pathways significantly associated with TIGD6 included glycerolipid metabolism, N glycan biosynthesis, type II diabetes mellitus, ABC transporters, glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis heparan sulfate, hedgehog signaling pathway, and others (Figure S1).

A strong correlation was observed between TIGD6 expression levels and immune cell infiltration in GC

Figure S2A demonstrates a significant positive correlation between TIGD6 expression and the infiltration of multiple immune cell types, including eosinophils, macrophages, T helper cells, central memory T cells (Tcm), effector memory T cells (Tem), and Th2 cells, supported by statistically significant p-values. In contrast, TIGD6 levels showed a negative association with B cells, cytotoxic cells, and plasmacytoid dendritic cells, as evidenced by their respective p-values. Additionally, Figure S2B shows that GC patients with higher TIGD6 expression levels had a significantly elevated stromal score, suggesting a pronounced desmoplastic reaction in these tumors.

Genomic alterations of TIGD6 in GC

Figure S3A delineates the spectrum of somatic alterations within the TIGD6 gene, comprising three instances of missense mutations alongside a single frameshift deletion. These findings indicate a mutation frequency for TIGD6 in GC of 1.08%. The analysis captured in Figure S3B reveals that the top tier of mutated genes, such as TTN, TP53, and others, were detected in both groups stratified by TIGD6 expression levels, with the percentages of these genes reflecting their prevalence. Moreover, Figure S3C highlights that missense mutations constituted the majority of variant types, with single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), especially transitions from cytosine to thymine (C > T), being the most commonly identified.

Association of TIGD6 expression with drug sensitivity

The results revealed a significant positive correlation, suggesting that higher TIGD6 expression is associated with increased drug efficacy for drugs such as AICA ribonucleotide, methotrexate, afatinib, SNX-2112, and AT-7519, as detailed in Table S4. Conversely, an inverse relationship was observed with drugs like bleomycin and tanespimycin, where higher TIGD6 expression was linked to reduced drug sensitivity, as documented in Table S4. These findings hint at a possible role of TIGD6 in mediating resistance to certain pharmaceutical agents.

Overexpression of TIGD6 in GC

Figure 3A shows that TIGD6 levels are significantly higher in GC-affected tissues compared to healthy stomach tissues (p < 0.001). Figure 3B demonstrates that TIGD6 expression is much higher in the AGS cell line than in the normal GES-1 cell line (p = 0.0057). Similarly, the HGC-27 and MKN28 cell lines exhibited significantly elevated TIGD6 expression when contrasted with GES-1 cells, both with p-values far below 0.0001. Collectively, these observations underscore the substantial overexpression of TIGD6 in GC cell lines relative to normal gastric cells.

Discussion

Gene expression varies significantly as GC evolves, a phenomenon that has garnered considerable interest in clinical studies. With advancements in molecular biology, there has been a concerted effort to comprehend the genetic underpinnings of GC’s onset, advancement, and patient outcomes, as well as to uncover molecular treatment strategies. The quest for discovering new biomarkers that could predict GC prognosis is crucial for its precise diagnosis and therapy. Within the scope of this research, the gene TIGD6 displayed abnormal expression patterns in certain malignancies, prompting speculation on its potential as a biomarker across various cancers—a proposition that could be the subject of collaborative research efforts. This investigation revealed a significant difference in TIGD6 levels between cancerous and healthy gastric tissues, with a highly significant p-value. Additionally, TIGD6 expression was found to be linked to the presence of residual tumors, reflux history, and antireflux treatment, with a particularly high expression noted in patients with residual tumors classified as R1 and R2. This correlation suggests that TIGD6 might play a role in GC advancement. Significantly, TIGD6 expression in gastric cancer tissues showed a strong association with residual tumor status, particularly demonstrating elevated levels in R1 and R2 classified cases. This observation implies TIGD6’s potential involvement in tumor persistence and recurrence mechanisms. Furthermore, increased TIGD6 expression correlated with reflux history and related treatments, indicating its possible interaction with gastric cancer pathophysiological factors to modulate disease development. Future studies could further explore the specific mechanisms of TIGD6’s role in these clinical features and its potential application in gastric cancer treatment. Importantly, elevated TIGD6 expression was significantly correlated with poorer OS and DSS in gastric cancer patients (p < 0.05). The study also highlighted a significant and independent link between TIGD6 levels and OS, further reinforcing its potential as a prognostic indicator.

The mechanism of action of TIGD6 in tumors has not been reported yet. The exact function of TIGD6 in GC is still unclear. Our research revealed a marked upregulation of TIGD6, both transcriptionally and translationally, within GC tissues and associated cell lines. The suppression of TIGD6 was observed to impede the growth, invasive behavior, and mobility of GC cells, concurrently promoting cell death through apoptosis. Mechanistically, silencing of TIGD6 resulted in marked elevation of PTCH1 and PTCH2 expression concomitant with suppression of GLI1 and GLI2 in GC cells. Notably, this investigation represents the inaugural demonstration of TIGD6 upregulation in GC, delineates its functional relevance, and assesses its immunomodulatory implications for patient outcomes. Nevertheless, it should be emphasized that our mechanistic insights establish an associative rather than direct relationship between TIGD6 and Hedgehog pathway activity, as evidenced solely through the observed alterations in downstream effectors PTCH1/2 and GLI1/2. Although our data demonstrate that TIGD6 knock-down consistently alters the expression of key Hedgehog components (PTCH1/2, GLI1/2), we acknowledge that direct pathway modulation (e.g., SMO inhibitors or GLI rescue experiments) remains to be performed; such studies will be the focus of our subsequent project.

Studying immune infiltration in GC has become a prominent research field [33]. Immunotherapy has achieved good anti-tumor effects in many cancers [34]. Although ICIs have good therapeutic effects, additional immunotherapies for GC patients are still under development [35]. The connection between TIGD6 and the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) remains to be fully understood. Our research delves into how TIGD6 expression correlates with TIME, particularly in the context of GC therapy. The results indicate an inverse relationship between TIGD6 levels and the presence of B cells, cytotoxic cells, and pDC. Conversely, TIGD6 expression exhibited significant positive correlations with the abundance of eosinophils, macrophages, T helper lymphocytes, Tcm, Tem, and Th2 cells within the tumor microenvironment. Within the scope of this research, GC patients exhibiting elevated TIGD6 levels demonstrated a notably higher stromal score. In theory, this could provide a theoretical foundation for exploring TIGD6 as a possible immunotherapy target for GC.

TIGD6 knock-down directly dampens GC proliferation/invasion and inhibits the intrinsic Hedgehog pathway, fitting the “direct malignant-cell targeting” strategy [84]. High TIGD6 enriches pro-tumoral macrophages, Th2, and eosinophils while excluding cytotoxic T and B cells. Therefore, anti-TIGD6 therapy could synergize with immune-checkpoint blockade or stromal-modulating agents to normalize the microenvironment.

Bleomycin is a widely studied anti-tumor antibiotic that works by activating oxygen molecules and cleaving DNA. Recent investigations have advanced our understanding of bleomycin resistance mechanisms. Notably, pulmonary delivery of scFv@iLNP-HP08LOOP demonstrated superior therapeutic efficacy compared to systemic administration of IL-11 scFv in murine models of bleomycin-associated pulmonary fibrosis [85]. In bleomycin-exposed murine lungs, we observed a marked diminution in ciliated cell proportion, implicating primary cilia as a pivotal contributor to fibrotic disease progression [86]. New strategies and treatments for Bleomycin resistance are constantly being developed and investigated with the aim of improving treatment efficacy and overcoming the problem of drug resistance. Tanespimycin is an inhibitor against heat shock protein 90 (HSP90), which reduces the stability of HER2 by blocking HSP90, resulting in an anti-tumor effect. Whether in the field of cancer therapy or antimicrobial drugs, scientists are actively searching for new approaches and drugs to overcome the problem of drug resistance in order to improve therapeutic efficacy and reduce the burden on patients. The molecular mechanisms by which TIGD6 mediates resistance to bleomycin and tanespimycin need to be further investigated.

Collectively, our data position TIGD6 as a candidate driver of GC progression that operates, at least in part, through amplification of Hedgehog signaling. To move from association toward causation under clinically relevant conditions, we now propose a two-tier validation strategy. First, we will exploit patient-derived xenograft (PDX) mice that faithfully recapitulate the histological and molecular heterogeneity of human GC. Following the blueprint established in breast cancer [87], freshly resected GC fragments (≥ 3 mm3) from 10 high-TIGD6 and 10 low-TIGD6 tumors (confirmed by qRT-PCR) will be implanted orthotopically into the gastric wall of NOD-SCID-IL2Rγnull (NSG) mice. After engraftment (> 100 mm3), mice will be randomized to receive either (i) control siRNA-nanoparticles, (ii) TIGD6-siRNA-nanoparticles (2 mg kg−1, i.p., twice weekly), or (iii) the FDA-approved Hedgehog pathway inhibitor vismodegib (100 mg kg−1, oral gavage, 5 days on/2 days off). Primary tumor volume, metastatic burden (bioluminescence and μCT), and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) will be monitored for 8 weeks. We hypothesize that TIGD6 silencing will phenocopy vismodegib by reducing tumor growth and metastasis, and that the combination will produce additive effects, thereby providing orthogonal evidence that TIGD6’s pro-tumor activity is Hedgehog-dependent in vivo. Second, to test clinical translatability, we are applying for RNAscope analysis of TIGD6 expression in a retrospective cohort of 120 GC patients receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy ± vismodegib. Correlating TIGD6 H-scores with pathological response (Becker grade), progression-free survival, and residual Hedgehog-pathway activity (PTCH1/GLI1 IHC) will reveal whether high TIGD6 identifies tumors that are addicted to Hedgehog signaling and therefore most likely to benefit from pathway inhibition. This sequential PDX-to-clinic design mirrors the successful validation pipeline recently deployed for voltage-gated sodium channels (VGSCs) by Jackson and colleagues. Their initial pan-cancer bioinformatics screen nominated SCN3B as a prognostic marker [88]; targeted knock-down in C6 glioma cells modulated motility [89], and ongoing PDX work is now testing whether VGSC blockers can be repurposed as anti-metastatic agents. By adopting an analogous framework for TIGD6, we will close the current gap between our in vitro mechanistic insights and actionable clinical hypotheses.

Despite the comprehensive nature of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the reliance on the TCGA database and a small validation cohort (GSE125611) may limit the generalizability of our findings, as the majority of TCGA samples are from Western populations and may not fully represent the global diversity of GC patients. Secondly, while TCGA provides a large dataset, subgroup analyses based on specific clinical features may still be underpowered due to limited sample sizes. Thirdly, although we identified the Hedgehog signaling pathway as a potential mechanism underlying TIGD6’s oncogenic role, the exact molecular interactions between TIGD6 and this pathway remain to be fully elucidated through targeted mechanistic studies, ideally incorporating gene editing tools and both in vitro and in vivo models. Fourthly, our immune infiltration analysis focused primarily on immune cell types, without fully integrating other components of the tumor immune microenvironment such as immune checkpoint molecules or cytokine profiles. Fifthly, while we explored associations between TIGD6 expression and drug sensitivity using computational tools, these findings lack validation through experimental drug response assays. Sixthly, given the known heterogeneity of GC, future studies should investigate TIGD6 expression and function across different pathological and molecular subtypes. Seventhly, our functional assays were limited to in vitro experiments; in vivo animal models are needed to confirm the biological relevance of TIGD6 in GC progression and its potential as a therapeutic target. Eighthly, although we demonstrated a correlation between TIGD6 expression and short-term survival outcomes, long-term follow-up data are needed to assess its impact on extended survival. Ninthly, this study did not evaluate the predictive value of TIGD6 for treatment responses such as chemotherapy or immunotherapy, which is critical for its clinical application as a biomarker. Lastly, a comprehensive regulatory network of TIGD6—including transcriptional and epigenetic regulators—has not been established; future multi-omics studies are warranted to uncover the upstream and downstream mechanisms governing TIGD6 expression and function. Although the prognostic associations identified here were consistently observed in both TCGA and the small public validation set (GSE125611), they remain retrospective and await confirmation in a large, independent GC cohort; such validation is planned as part of our future collaborative project. Addressing these limitations will be essential for translating our findings into clinical practice and for fully understanding the role of TIGD6 in GC.

Conclusions

In this study, we provide the initial functional evidence that si-RNA-mediated knock-down of TIGD6 in GC cells suppresses proliferation, migration, and invasion while promoting apoptosis, thereby establishing TIGD6 as an active oncogenic driver rather than a mere biomarker. TIGD6 showed a significant upregulation in GC. High levels of TIGD6 were significantly associated with residual tumor, reflux history, and antireflux treatment. Elevated levels of TIGD6 were associated with poor OS and DSS in patients with GC. The study identified TIGD6 as a significant, independent predictor of OS. Additionally, TIGD6 expression was linked to the degree of immune cell infiltration and the overall immune response. Notably, an inverse relationship was observed between TIGD6 levels and the sensitivity to drugs, including bleomycin and tanespimycin, in GC. The study also highlighted TIGD6's role in promoting GC cell growth, invasiveness, and mobility through modulation of the hedgehog signaling pathway. These insights suggest that TIGD6 could serve as a valuable biomarker for predicting GC prognosis as well as assessing therapeutic responses.
