Neutrophilic granule protein promotes lipopolysaccharide-induced iNOS/NO expression via JAK2/STAT1 signaling pathway and augments bacteria clearance of macrophages
Authors: Lixing Tian, Shuangqin Yin, Jing Wang, Hongyan Xiao, Tianyin Kuang, Li Luo, Shengxiang Ao, Jing Zhou, Xue Yang, Lin Qiao, Yidong Yang, He Jin, Mu Yuan, Yongming Li, Xiangwei Hou, Xiang Xu, Huaping Liang, Junyu Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeutrophilic granule protein (NGP) was identified as a granular protein in neutrophils, but its function in macrophages has not been fully understood. In our prior investigation, NGP was observed to be significantly upregulated in macrophages following stimulation with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) or Escherichia coli (E.coli). Furthermore, NGP demonstrated an anti-inflammatory effect by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway through its extracellular distribution. Nitric oxide (NO), as a crucial factor in the immune regulation of macrophages mainly controlled by inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), plays an indispensable role in the eradication of bacteria. However, the regulatory effects of NGP on NO remains unknown. In this study, we observed an upregulation and central role of Nos2, the gene encoding iNOS, in macrophages with high NGP expression induced by LPS. Overexpression of NGP significantly enhanced NO production in LPS-induced macrophages, while NGP knockout attenuated NO production. Furthermore, exogenous complementation experiments confirmed that NGP primarily exerted its up-regulatory effect on iNOS/NO through intracellular distribution. Additionally, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) analyses revealed that NGP predominantly regulated NO levels via the JAK2/STAT1 pathway. Finally, our in vitro and in vivo experiments provided evidence supporting the involvement of NGP in bacterial clearance through modulation of NO. The evidence suggests that NGP plays a crucial role in regulating NO production and promoting bacterial clearance in macrophages, making it a promising target for the prevention and treatment of infectious diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05865-9.Keywords: Neutrophilic granule protein, Lipopolysaccharide, Macrophages, Inducible nitric oxide synthase, Nitric oxidate

Introduction

Macrophages orchestrate innate immune defense in response to pathogenic invasion, especially bacteria and viruses, by production of multiple cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and NO [1–3]. NO is a highly reactive and unstable small molecule gas that plays a crucial role in maintaining normal cellular function [4]. In the physiological state, NO functions as an essential neurotransmitter and exerts regulatory effects on vasomotor activity, thrombosis prevention, and the scavenging of free radicals [5–7]. Conversely, under pathological conditions, NO becomes a significant inflammatory mediator, orchestrating immune processes and inflammatory responses [8–11]. Nevertheless, it is critical to recognize that elevated NO levels can have deleterious effects and are associated with a range of pathological conditions, including septic shock, cardiac insufficiency, pain, diabetes, and cancer [12–15]. Given the dual role of NO, precise regulation of NO expression levels is crucial for optimizing cytoprotective effects while mitigating cytotoxicity.

Upon stimulation by pro-inflammatory mediators, macrophages are capable of producing significant amounts of NO, which is essential for the defense against invading pathogens [16, 17]. The binding of NO to iron in respiratory chain complexes 1 and 2 in microbial mitochondria results in respiratory chain electron transport disorders and impaired internal respiratory function, leading to growth arrest and eventual death [18]. The reaction between NO and superoxide radicals yields superoxide nitroso, which subsequently decomposes into NO2− and hydroxyl radicals, exhibiting potent antimicrobial activity [19]. Therefore, precise regulation of NO expression levels in infectious diseases such as sepsis is crucial for optimizing the bactericidal efficacy of macrophages.

Nitric oxide synthase (NOS) serves as the critical rate-limiting enzyme in the biosynthesis of NO. There are three distinct isoforms of NOS: neuronal (nNOS), endothelial (eNOS), and inducible (iNOS) [20, 21]. Of these isoforms, iNOS is primarily localized within macrophages, leukocytes, and epithelial cells in areas of inflammation [22, 23]. Existing studies have demonstrated that iNOS in macrophages is primarily regulated by the NF-κB and JAK/STAT pathways during LPS stimulation [24–26]. In our previous study, we discovered that NGP can suppress the activation of the NF-κB pathway [27], which contradicts our finding that NGP can enhance NO production. Therefore, this study will focus on elucidating the mechanism by which NGP regulates iNOS through alternative pathways such as the JAK/STAT pathway.

NGP belongs to the superfamily of cysteine protease inhibitors and functions as a ubiquitous natural inhibitor of cysteine proteases, which are present in a wide range of organisms, including protozoa, plants, fungi, and viruses. While the precise mechanisms underlying NGP in immune regulation remain largely elusive, several studies have demonstrated that NGP can effectively suppress excessive inflammatory responses in macrophages. Emerging evidence has revealed that exogenous NGP can attenuate the excessive activation of macrophages induced by LPS through blocking LPS-LBP (LPS binging protein) complex binding [28]. Our previous study also showed that exogenous NGP ameliorates macrophages overactivation via inhibiting NF-κB pathway [27].

In this study, we provide the inaugural evidence demonstrating that NGP can modulate iNOS and NO expression via its intracellular localization rather than its exogenous function, concurrently enhancing bacterial clearance. Therefore, modulating NGP expression in macrophages may constitute a promising strategy to inhibit excessive inflammatory responses and enhance pathogen clearance in the context of infectious diseases, such as sepsis.

Materials and methods

Reagents

LPS (Escherichia coli 0111: B4) and thioglycolate were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich Corporation (St. Louis, MO, USA). Trypsin, fetal bovine serum (FBS), Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium, and penicillin–streptomycin solution were sourced from Gibco (Carlsbad, CA, USA). E. coli was obtained from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). Mice NGP recombinant protein (rNGP) were purchased from MCE (Shanghai, China). The JAK2 inhibitor (Ruxolitinib) and the STAT1 inhibitor (Fludarabine) were purchased from TargetMol (Boston, USA). L-NAME was purchased from Selleck. Nitric Oxide Assay Kit was purchased from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). Flow cytometric antibody CD11b-BV605, F4/80-FITC, iNOS-PE were purchased from R&D Systems (Minnesota, MN, USA). Polyclonal NGP antibody was purchased from Rockland (Pennsylvania, USA). GAPDH antibody (#5174), STAT1 antibody (#9172), p-STAT1 antibody (#9167), JAK2 antibody (#3230), p-JAK2 antibody (#3776), and IgG antibody (#3900) were purchased from CST (Danvers, MA, USA). STAT1 antibody (bsm-33270 M) for LSCM was obtained from Bioss (Beijing, China).

Mice

C57BL/6 wild-type male mice (NgpWT/mice) 6–8 weeks of age weighing 20–22 g were purchased from Si Pei Fu Biotechnology (Beijing, China). NGP knockout mice (NgpKO/mice) on the C57BL/6 background were purchased from Gempharmatech (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). The mice were maintained under the following conditions: approximately 40%–70% humidity, 19–23℃ temperature, and a 12/12 h light/dark cycle. The mice had free access to water and food. The experimental animals were fed and raised in the SPF animal room of the Experimental Animal Center of Daping Hospital, Army Medical University (Chongqing, China), and the experimental unit license number was SYXK (Yu) 20170002. The animal experiments included in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of Army Medical University (AMUWEC20211996). In vivo experiments, mice were injected with LPS (15 mg/kg) or E.coli (3 × 107 CFU/20 g) to induce acute peritonitis. The validation data and information of NgpWT/mice and NgpKO/mice was shown in Supplemental Fig. 1.

Cell culture

Raw264.7 cell line (RAW) were produced by ATCC. Negative control RAW (NgpNC/RAW), NGP overexpression RAW (NgpHI/RAW) cell strain were constructed and cultured by Genechem (Shanghai, China). The cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium, supplemented with 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 mg/mL streptomycin, and 10% FBS. Peritoneal macrophages (PM) were obtained from mice by flushing the peritoneal cavity with 4 mL PBS after intraperitoneal injection of 4% thioglycollate for 3 days. The peritoneal lavage fluids (PLF) were centrifuged (300 × g) to collect cells. Then adhered to a Petri dish for 2 h at 37 ℃ in RPMI 1640 medium. Non-adherent cells were removed by gently washing three times with warm PBS, and PM were then harvested from the adherent cells. All the cells were grown at 37 ℃ and 5% CO2 in humidified air. The validation data and information of NGP overexpression RAW was shown in Supplemental Fig. 1.

RNA-seq and GSEA analysis

Total mRNA was extracted by TRIZOL reagent from treated cells and subjected to computer sequencing (Majorbio) for acquisition of the raw data (The raw data are available in the NCBI BioProject database under accession number PRJNA1276271). Subsequently, Clean Reads from each sample were aligned with the designated reference genome (GRCm39). Read count, RPKM, and FPKM values were calculated using internal software. Based on quantitative expression levels, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis was performed to identify significantly differentially expressed genes between groups. DEGs-seq was employed as the software for differential expression analysis with a screening threshold set at |log2FC|> = 1.000 & padjust < 0.001. Pathway and GSEA analysis were performed in Majorbio Cloud platform by using DEGs and ranked gene list based on log2 fold change values.

Phospho-specific protein microarray analysis

The Phospho Explorer Antibody Array, developed and produced by Full Moon Biosystems, was utilized to analyze cell lysates. Data analysis was conducted by Wayen Biotechnologies. To determine the level of protein phosphorylation, a ratio calculation method was employed: phosphorylation ratio = value of phosphorylated proteins/value of unphosphorylated proteins.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the RNApure High Purity Total RNA Rapid Extraction Kit (Centrifugal column) from Bioteke (Beijing, China). Removal of residual genomic DNA and synthesis of first-strand cDNA were performed using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Japan). The qPCR system was as follows: 1 μl of sample and 5 μl TB Green mixture (Takara, Japan), 4 μl of primer and water mixture per well. For each sample, three replicates were run to calculate the average Ct values. The qRT-PCR analysis was carried out on a CFX96 Touch™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA). The data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Relative gene expression levels were normalized to the expression of the housekeeping gene β-actin. The qRT-PCR primers were listed as follows: Actin-F:5’-AGCCATGTACGTAG.

CCATCC-3’, Actin-R:5’-CTCTCAGCTGTGGTGGTGAA-3’, Nos2-F:5’-GTTCTCA.

GCCCAACAATACAAGA-3’, Nos2-R:5’-GTGGACGGGTCGATGTCAC-3’, TNF-α-F:5’-GAGTCCGGGCAGGTCTACTTT-3’, TNF-α-R:5’-CAGGTCACTGTCCCA.

GCATCT-3’, IL-6-F:5’-ACCACGGCCTTCCCTACTTC-3’, IL-6-R:5’-CTCATTTCCACGATTTCCCAG-3’, IL-1β-F:5’-GCAACTGTTCCTGAAACTAACT-3’, IL-1β-R:5’-ATCCTTTTGGGGTCCGTCAACT-3’.

Western blotting

The cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) containing 1 mM PMSF for 30 min on ice. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 ℃. After centrifugation, the supernatant was transferred to a pre-cooled EP tube and mixed with 5 × loading buffer. The sample was then heated at 100℃ for 10 min and subsequently stored at −80 ℃. Equal amounts of proteins were separated by electrophoresis on polyacrylamide gels containing 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (0.22 μm). The membranes were incubated with QuickBlock™ western blocking buffer for one hour at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4℃. On the following day, the membranes were washed and probed with respective HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (CST) for one hour. No stripping or reprobing of the membranes occurred; instead, they were cut horizontally into sections. Immunoreactive bands were detected using a Bio-Rad enhanced chemiluminescence detection system, and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software through densitometric analysis.

Laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM)

The confocal dishes (Nest) were seeded with 2 × 105 cells, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime) for 30 min, and permeabilized in PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100 (Beyotime) for 10 min. Subsequently, the cells were blocked with QuickBlock™ immunostaining blocking solution (Beyotime) at room temperature for 15 min. Primary antibody (1:200) was incubated overnight at 4 ℃, followed by incubation with diluted secondary antibody (1:400, Invitrogen) in the dark for one hour. Finally, DAPI staining (Beyotime) was performed for 10 min. All incubations were followed by three washes with PBS before imaging on a laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus).

Flow cytometry

A total of 1 × 106 cells were incubated and stained in dark at room temperature for 20 min using 1 μl of F4/80-FITC and CD11b-BV605. Subsequently, the cells underwent overnight incubation in the dark at 4 °C with a fixation/permeabilization diluent. Following this, the samples were centrifuged at 600 g for 5 min to remove the supernatant. An iNOS intracellular antibody staining solution was then prepared by adding 200 μl of permeabilization buffer and 1 μl of iNOS-PE. Following vortexing and mixing, the cells were incubated and stained in dark at room temperature for durations of up to 30 min. Then, the cells were subjected to two washes with PBS prior to analysis using an ACEA NovoCyte™ flow cytometer.

IP-MS

The cells were lysed in Western and IP buffer (Beyotime, China) and quantified using the BCA kit (Beyotime, China). Protein A/G magnetic IP beads were pre-incubated with NGP antibody or IgG antibody and subsequently co-incubated with cell lysates at 4 ℃ overnight. The treated beads underwent mass spectrometry analysis using a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer coupled to an EASY-n LC 1200 liquid system. The target protein was assessed by western blotting.

Molecular docking

The predicted structures of NGP and STAT1 were predicted using AlphaFold. Protein–protein docking simulations were performed using the GRAMM web server (v2.0), followed by interaction analysis and structural visualization with PyMOL (v2.4). The final interaction diagram was constructed by integrating hydrogen bond networks and hydrophobic contact surfaces.

RNA interference

STAT1-targeting siRNAs (#1 ~ #4), scrambled control siRNA, and Lipo2000 were obtained from GenePharma (Jiangsu, China). Cells (2 × 10⁶/well) were plated in 6-well plates and transfected with 120 nM siRNA using Lipo2000 for 24 h. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh RPMI 1640 for further experiments. Following rigorous validation of transfection efficiency through quantitative RT-PCR, siRNA #3 demonstrated superior silencing efficacy and was consequently selected from the four candidate siRNAs for subsequent functional investigations. The validation data was shown in Supplemental Fig. 5.

Bacterial burden detection

In vivo experiments, PLF was collected from NGP wild-type or knockout mice after 12-h LPS (15 mg/kg) and E.coli (3 × 107 CFU/20 g) challenge and diluted in different proportions and evenly smearing on a 60 × 15 mm solid Luria Broth (LB) culture plate. In vitro experiments, supernatants of LPS and E.coli-treated cells (MOI = 20:1) were harvested and diluted in different proportions and evenly smearing on a 60 × 15 mm solid LB culture plate. For conditioned-medium (CM) experiments, NgpNC/CM or NgpHI/CM were exposed to LPS and E.coli (MOI = 20:1) for 60 min and diluted in different proportions and evenly smearing on a 60 × 15 mm solid LB culture plate. Each dilution was plated on LB agar plate at a volume of 200 μl and incubated at 37 °C under sterile air atmosphere for 18 h. The bacterial colonies were then enumerated as CFU.

Statistical analysis

The majority of data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) derived from at least three independent experiments. Comparisons of multiple group means were conducted using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by least significant difference (LSD) multiple-comparison tests. For the comparison of continuous variables between two groups, a two-tailed Student’s t-test was employed. Survival rates were analyzed using the Mantel-Cox test. All statistical analyses were executed utilizing SPSS version 16.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) or Prism version 6.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

RNA sequencing revealed that Nos2 (iNOS gene) and NGP exhibited positively correlated expression changes in LPS-stimulated macrophages with Nos2 playing a critical role in NGP-mediated genes regulation

Our previous study demonstrated that NGP is upregulated in activated macrophages and mitigates the pro-inflammatory response during LPS challenge, suggesting a potential role of NGP in regulating innate immune [27]. Given the critical role of iNOS/NO in macrophages pathogen defense, however, the regulatory impact of NGP on iNOS/NO remains unknown. To further explore the underlying role of NGP involved in LPS-induced iNOS/NO expression in macrophages, NGP gene-edited RAW cell lines were constructed in collaboration with Genechem Co., Ltd and subjected to RNA-seq analysis. The results showed that 220 DEGs were upregulated in response to LPS challenge, among which the expression level of iNOS gene (Nos2) was significantly elevated (Fig. 1A). The enrichment analysis of upregulated DEGs using the reactome pathway revealed that NGP overexpression could enhance the activation of innate immune in macrophages and potentially regulate NO synthesis (Fig. 1B). The protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis of 220 upregulated DEGs disclosed the significant involvement of Nos2 following NGP overexpression, positioning it among the top 10 hub-genes (Fig. 1C). Further, a venn intersection analysis and GO enrichment were employed to identify 126 core DEGs, which shed light on the relationship between NGP-mediated Nos2 regulation and LPS-induced response (Fig. 1D, E). The volcano map and heat map also showed that Nos2 was significantly upregulated in 126 core DEGs (Fig. 1F, G). Taken together, these findings suggest that iNOS plays a pivotal role in NGP overexpression macrophages upon exposure to LPS.

NGP knockout reduces iNOS/NO expression in LPS-induced septic mice

To explore the regulatory effect of NGP on iNOS/NO expression in vivo, NGP knockout mice were injected with LPS to establish acute peritonitis model. Notably, genetic ablation of NGP exhibited no discernible impact on total peritoneal cell counts compared to wild-type controls (Supplemental Fig. 2). NO levels in serum and PLF were reduced in LPS-treated NGP knockout mice compared with wild-type mice (Fig. 2A, B). The expression levels of Nos2 in PM were significantly reduced upon knockout of NGP (Fig. 2C). Consistently, the proportion of iNOS-positive cells in PM was significantly reduced by NGP knockout (Fig. 2D). These results confirmed the presence of a positive regulatory effect of NO by NGP in animal models as well.

NGP enhances iNOS/NO expression via transcriptional regulatory effect

As the effect of NGP on iNOS/NO was confirmed in vivo, the NGP gene-edited RAW cell lines were employed to systematically investigate the NGP-mediated molecular mechanisms underlying LPS responsiveness. Examination of the supernatants and mRNA derived from LPS-treated PM cells revealed that NGP knockout weakened NO and Nos2 expression, which is consistent with in vivo results (Fig. 3A, B). Conversely, NGP overexpression upregulated NO and Nos2 levels (Fig. 3C, D). Flow cytometry analysis also showed that NGP overexpression increased the proportion of iNOS-positive cells (Fig. 3E). Further, we treated NGP gene-edited RAW with actinomycin D to explore whether the regulation of NO by NGP is achieved through the transcriptional level. The result indicated that actinomycin D treatment remarkably abolished Nos2 high expression caused by NGP (Fig. 3F). Collectively, we confirmed that NGP positively regulates iNOS/NO expression at the transcriptional level.

NGP modulates iNOS/NO expression via intracellular distribution rather than its extracellular autocrine

Previous study has demonstrated that NGP acts as a LPS antagonist, effectively disrupting the formation of the LPS-LBP complex [28]. Consistently, our findings also indicate that NGP conditioned medium alleviates LPS-induced sepsis in mice [27]. These studies have elucidated the key role of extracellular NGP in the regulation of macrophage-related inflammation. However, the functional significance of NGP as an intracellular protein remains elusive. To investigate the up-regulatory effect of NGP on iNOS/NO, either through its intracellular distribution or extracellular autocrine effect, we quantified the concentration of NGP in supernatant obtained from RAW cells overexpressing NGP and subsequently treated wild-type or NGP knockout RAW cells with exogenous rNGP. The supernatants were diluted 250 or 2500 times, respectively, and co-loaded with rNGP at a concentration of 100 ng/ml for the detection of NGP expression. The results demonstrated that the expression level of NGP in the supernatant diluted by 250 times was comparable to that achieved with a concentration of 100 ng/ml rNGP (Fig. 4A). Therefore, the subsequent NGP supplement experiment utilized a concentration of rNGP at 25 μg/mL. Contrary to the results obtained from NGP overexpression experiments, exogenous supplementation of rNGP did not lead to an increase in Nos2/NO expression but rather resulted in a decrease in Nos2/NO expression, even in NGP knockout PM (Fig. 4B). This suggests that NGP may upregulate Nos2/NO levels through intracellular distribution. Notably, the exogenous addition of rNGP did not result in an increase in NGP content within PM cells (Fig. 4C), thereby further indicating that NGP exerts its regulatory effect on NO through intracellular distribution. As previously reported, extracellular NGP attenuates LPS-induced Toll-like receptor signaling pathway through disrupting LPS-LBP complex formation [28]. To validate the bioactivity and anti-inflammatory capacity of our recombinant NGP (rNGP), we performed RNA sequencing on LPS-challenged PM with or without rNGP pre-treatment. Clustering analysis of transcriptomic profiles revealed marked suppression of inflammation-related genes in rNGP-treated groups (Fig. 4D). Complementing this finding, GSEA analysis demonstrated significant inhibition of both Toll-like receptor signaling pathway and TNF signaling pathway (Fig. 4E-G). Furthermore, exogenous rNGP administration elicited dose-dependent suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β (Supplemental Fig. 3), conclusively establishing that our rNGP construct retains anti-inflammatory bioactivity consistent with native NGP mechanisms.

NGP controls the expression of iNOS/NO by regulating the JAK2/STAT1 signaling pathway

The current evidence supports the notion that NF-κB and JAK/STAT serve as the principal signaling pathways governing iNOS expression in macrophages [24–26]. Our previous finding has demonstrated a significant reduction in NF-κB pathway activity induced by LPS upon overexpression of NGP [27], leading us to hypothesize that NGP may up-regulate iNOS expression via the JAK/STAT pathway rather than through NF-κB. Indeed, KEGG analysis revealed that overexpression of NGP not only exerted an impact on the NF-κB pathway but also exhibited a regulatory influence on the JAK/STAT pathway (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, we observed that the overexpression of NGP significantly enhanced the activation of the JAK2/STAT1 pathway through CSP100 PLUS phosphorylation microarray analysis, which was further validated by western blotting (Fig. 5B, C). Additionally, the interaction between NGP and STAT1 was identified through IP-MS screening, which was further validated by Co-IP experiment (Fig. 5D, E) and immunofluorescence co-localization (Supplemental Fig. 4). To further investigate the interaction mechanism between NGP and STAT1, we performed molecular docking simulations using the GRAMM online service. The full-length protein models of NGP and STAT1, predicted by AlphaFold, were subjected to docking analysis, which revealed 12 potential binding sites (including 10 hydrogen bonds and 2 salt bridges). These findings lay the groundwork for subsequent site-directed mutagenesis experiments to explore the mechanistic details of this interaction (Fig. 5F-H). The effect of NGP on LPS-induced JAK2/STAT1 activation in macrophages was also confirmed by confocal laser scanning microscopy (Fig. 5I, J). The iNOS and NO overexpression induced by NGP were significantly attenuated when JAK2 or STAT1 inhibitors were applied to pretreat the NGP overexpression RAW cells and negative control RAW cells prior to LPS stimulation (Fig. 5K-M). To further validate the regulatory role of STAT1 in NGP-mediated NO expression enhancement, we employed siRNA-mediated STAT1 knockdown. Notably, this genetic intervention phenocopied the effects of pharmacological STAT1 inhibition, with both approaches significantly attenuating NGP overexpression-induced upregulation of NO and Nos2 (Fig. 5N, O). These findings provide additional evidence suggesting that NGP may modulate the expression of iNOS/NO in macrophages via the JAK2/STAT1 pathway.

NGP-mediated NO production is beneficial to pathogen clearance

As mentioned above, NO plays an important role in pathogen clearance. Therefore, we further explored the role of NGP-mediated NO in the clearance of E.coli, a conditional pathogen. The co-culture of E.coli and RAW cells demonstrated that the overexpression of NGP significantly attenuated the bacterial burden in the medium, which was subsequently reversed upon treatment with the NO antagonist L-NAME (Fig. 6A). Similarly, incubation of E.coli with NGP overexpression RAW culture supernatant resulted in a decrease in bacterial load within the supernatant, and this effect were also reversed by L-NAME (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, wild-type or NGP knockout mice were subjected to LPS and E.coli challenge in order to investigate the role of NGP in the regulation of NO and its subsequent biological effects in vivo (Fig. 6C). Overall, knockout of NGP significantly enhanced the mortality rate in septic mice induced by LPS and E.coli (Fig. 6D). Meanwhile, bacterial load in the PLF of NGP knockout mice was remarkably elevated (Fig. 6E). Taken together, the role of NGP in the clearance of E.coli through NO was investigated both in vitro and in vivo, revealing its significant contribution to the process of pathogen elimination.

Discussion

Macrophages function as essential effector cells within the innate immune system. The secretion of a diverse array of inflammatory mediators by macrophages is integral to the host defense mechanisms against pathogenic invasion. Nevertheless, the overproduction of pro-inflammatory factors can result in deleterious effects on the host organism [29, 30]. Hence, it is of paramount importance to accurately and scientifically regulate the secretion of inflammatory factors by macrophages in pathological conditions, as this has significant implications for inflammation-related diseases such as sepsis [31–33]. Based on the aforementioned reports, the objective of this study is to investigate the regulatory effect and mechanism of NGP, a novel immunomodulatory protein, on NO production during macrophages overactivation in order to provide a therapeutic strategy for excessive inflammatory response and infectious diseases.

NGP is a member of the cystatin family, and proteins in this family have been demonstrated to participate in immune response regulation through multiple pathways. In line with our previous finding, Type 1 cystatin stefin B (cystatin B) has demonstrated an anti-inflammatory effect by attenuating NF-κB activation during neuro-inflammation [34]. Cystatin C, an inhibitor of cysteine proteinase, has been found to inhibit the expression of TNF-α and L-1β by suppressing the phosphorylation of extracellular signal-regulated kinase-1/2 in monocytes induced by LPS. This suggests that cystatin C acts as a negative regulator of the mitogen-activated protein kinase pathway [35]. Slightly deviating from our study, it has been confirmed that a recombinant type 2 cystatin (SJC statin) effectively inhibits the production of TNF-α, IL6, and other pro-inflammatory factors in LPS-induced RAW cells. Additionally, it also suppresses the generation of NO [36]. These findings suggest that different proteins within the cystatin family exhibit variations in their immune regulatory capabilities.

Our previous study revealed that NGP exhibited high expression levels in mouse peritoneal macrophages induced by Staphylococcus aureus, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, E.coli, or LPS. Following induction by LPS, NGP was found to enhance the expression of IL-10 while inhibiting the expression of IL-1β and TNF-α in macrophages [27]. These findings suggest that NGP may play a role in regulating the synthesis of various inflammatory factors and potentially participate in inflammatory responses. However, the impact of NGP on NO remains unclear. By RNA-seq and cytoscape hub-gene analysis, we found that Nos2 exhibits increased expression and plays a vital role in LPS-induced NGP overexpression macrophages, the regulation of NO by NGP has thus come under our scrutiny (Fig. 1). Further, the positive regulatory role of NGP in the production of NO by macrophages under LPS stimulation was confirmed through experiments involving NGP knockout mice and NGP overexpression RAW cells (Figs. 2 and 3). More importantly, we confirmed for the first time that NGP primarily regulates NO by modulating its intracellular distribution through exogenous supplementation experiments (Fig. 4). These results suggested that the regulation of NGP on the expression of NO is primarily mediated by its intracellular location, whereas the expression of TNF-α, IL-6, and other proinflammatory factors is governed by its extracellular distribution. Since iNOS is mainly regulated by NF-κB and JAK/STAT, we have confirmed that NGP can inhibit NF-κB activation, therefore it is necessary to explore the potential mechanism of NGP regulating NO expression from other perspectives. By KEGG pathway and phospho-specific protein microarray analysis, we found that NGP may modulate the expression of NO via the JAK2/STAT1 signaling pathway, and the enhancement of NO induced by overexpression of NGP can be attenuated by employing an inhibitor targeting this pathway. Further, immunoprecipitation, mass spectrometry, molecular docking and RNA interference revealed an interaction between NGP and STAT1, which provided additional evidence supporting the involvement of NGP in the regulation of the JAK2/STAT1 pathway (Fig. 5). Collectively, the diverse intracellular and extracellular localization of NGP may confer distinct regulatory roles in inflammation-related pathways such as NF-κB and JAK/STAT. Furthermore, the findings from this study suggested that intracellular NGP exerts a predominant influence on NO regulation compared to its intracellular counterpart.

It has been proved that macrophages not only regulate the host immune response by secreting inflammatory factors, but also play a bactericidal role by producing NO [37–40]. Herein, we evaluated the effects of NO produced in response to NGP on bacterial clearance in vitro and in vivo. By co-cultivating E.coli with RAW cells overexpressing NGP or treating E.coli with conditioned medium from NGP-overexpressing cells, we confirmed that the overexpression of NGP enhanced bacterial clearance. However, the augmented effect of NGP overexpression on bacterial clearance was nullified when NO inhibitors were employed. Furthermore, we demonstrated that knockout of NGP led to an elevated mortality rate in mice with LPS + E.coli induced acute peritonitis, and impaired bacterial clearance in PLF (Fig. 6). Thus, we have demonstrated for the first time that NGP plays an important regulatory role in macrophage-mediated bacterial clearance.

The study is subject to several limitations. Although we observed that NGP binds to STAT1 with 12 predicted binding sites (Fig. 5D-H) (Supplemental Fig. 4), the regulatory role of this interaction in STAT1 activation requires further exploration through mutagenesis experiments targeting these sites. Notably, NGP constitutively interacted with STAT1 under basal conditions (without LPS stimulation), while LPS treatment increased STAT1 recruitment in Co-IP assays (Fig. 5E), potentially due to elevated basal NGP expression. These findings suggest that enhanced NGP-STAT1 binding may sensitize JAK-STAT pathway activation upon LPS stimulation. Future studies will focus on delineating the precise mechanisms by which NGP regulates STAT1 activation. In this study, we have demonstrated that the upregulation of NO expression induced by NGP overexpression is implicated in E.coli clearance, but its role in bacterial clearance across various infection models, such as CLP-induced sepsis, remains to be investigated.

In conclusion, NGP effectively suppresses the expression of pro-inflammatory factors, such as TNF-α and IL-6, while enhancing NO production in an LPS-induced macrophage inflammation model. This effect is primarily attributed to its differential intracellular and extracellular localization. By employing RNA-seq and immunoprecipitation mass spectrometry analysis, we have identified that NGP potentially modulates NO levels by facilitating the activation of the JAK2/STAT1 signaling pathway. This finding has been further substantiated through an inhibition assay targeting the JAK2/STAT1 pathway. The protective effect of NGP on the survival of mice in an LPS + E. coli induced acute peritonitis model was also confirmed, thereby validating the ability of NGP-mediated NO production to enhance bacterial clearance. The above findings further elucidate the role of cystatin protein family in immune regulation, unveil the regulatory impact and mechanism of NGP on NO expression in inflammatory macrophages for the first time, and establish a theoretical foundation for utilizing NGP as a novel target in the prevention and treatment of sepsis and other infectious diseases.
