Longitudinal associations between maternal love withdrawal and behavior problems among Chinese preschoolers: the joint moderating effects of child gender and effortful control
Authors: Lingjun Chen, Meishuo Yu, Chenran Shao, Shufen Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLove withdrawal, an emotionally manipulative practice where caregivers withdraw affection to elicit compliance, is theorized to harm child development. However, longitudinal evidence in early childhood, particularly within the Chinese context, remains scarce. This study therefore investigated the longitudinal relations between maternal love withdrawal and preschoolers’ behavior problems, and explored the moderating effects of child gender and effortful control in China.MethodsIn this two-wave, one-year longitudinal study, participants were 148 Chinese preschoolers (86 boys, Mage = 4.02, SD = 0.79) and their mothers. Mothers reported their use of love withdrawal at Wave 1 and rated their children’s effortful control and internalizing and externalizing problems at Wave 2. Data were analyzed using multi-group path analysis to examine gender-specific effects and the moderating role of effortful control separately for boys and girls.ResultsWave 1 maternal love withdrawal positively predicted Wave 2 externalizing problems, but not Wave 2 internalizing problems. These effects varied by child gender and their levels of effortful control. For boys, maternal love withdrawal interacted with effortful control in predicting externalizing problems one year later, and only boys with lower effortful control exhibited more externalizing problems in response to love withdrawal. For girls, maternal love withdrawal positively predicted internalizing problems one year later, regardless of the child’s effortful control level.ConclusionsThe findings underscore that maternal love withdrawal is a risk factor for behavior problems among Chinese preschoolers, though its effects vary by gender and effortful control. These results provided guidelines for future interventions: reducing caregivers’ use of love withdrawal, strengthening boys’ effortful control skills, and providing girls with greater support for managing internalizing problems.Keywords: Maternal love withdrawal, Internalizing problems, Externalizing problems, Effortful control, Gender

Introduction

Love withdrawal is defined as parents threatening or temporarily withdrawing love and attention when a child cannot meet their expectations [1]. A typical example is a parent telling the child “If you don’t behave, Mommy won’t love you anymore.” Children exposed to parental love withdrawal often display elevated internalizing and externalizing problems [2–4]. Internalizing problems manifest as inwardly directed emotional or psychological distress, including symptoms of anxiety, depression, and social withdrawal. Conversely, externalizing problems refer to outwardly directed behaviors, such as aggression, impulsivity, and conduct problems [5].

However, research examining the longitudinal associations between love withdrawal and behavior problems during early childhood, when attachment relationships are especially formative, remains limited. Moreover, the Chinese cultural context provides a unique setting for this investigation, as Chinese parents tend to use love withdrawal more frequently than Western parents [6], partly due to cultural emphasis on compliance and parental anxiety about children’s success [7]. In addition, individual characteristics such as child gender and effortful control may moderate the effects of love withdrawal, influencing whether children exhibit internalizing or externalizing responses. The present longitudinal study addresses these issues by focusing on Chinese preschoolers and employing two-wave data to clarify the longitudinal consequences of maternal love withdrawal on behavior problems, as well as the role of child gender and effortful control as moderators.

Theoretical background and conceptualization of love withdrawal

Love withdrawal is considered an emotionally manipulative tactic. When parents suggest that love and attention will not be given to them until the child changes their behavior, many children adjust their actions accordingly [8]. However, does this approach’s immediate convenience outweigh its potential harm to child development? Many scholars express concern about its negative impacts. This practice maintains children’s dependence and compliance by exploiting the parent-child love relationship [9] and frustrates children’s basic psychological needs, especially their needs for relatedness [10]. The negative impact of love withdrawal is explained through key theoretical perspectives. Attachment theory establishes its harm to the foundational caregiver-child bond, critical in early childhood [11, 12]. Children internalize experiences of parental attitudes toward them into working models that shape their views of themselves, others, and relationships, which further affect their developmental adjustment [13]. The perception of a lacking of acceptance in parent-child relationships can lead to relational insecurity and foster expectations of peer rejection or conditional acceptance [4]. These negative expectations in relationships may render the child more vulnerable to developing both internalizing and externalizing problems [14–16].

From a complementary perspective, self-determination theory explains how this relational threat frustrates the child’s basic need for relatedness and unmet needs adversely affect development in various domains [17, 18]. Consistently, frustration-aggression theory suggests that when love withdrawal frustrates children’s fundamental emotional needs, it can evoke anger and frustration that manifest either inwardly as internalizing problems or outwardly as externalizing problems [19, 20]. Taken together, these frameworks imply that maternal love withdrawal threatens children’s attachment security and basic psychological needs, thereby increasing the risk for behavioral maladjustment.

Empirically, love withdrawal has also been studied within the broader framework of parental psychological control (PPC)—a form of indirect control in which parents manipulate children’s thoughts and feelings to influence behavior [1, 21]. PPC encompasses various behaviors, such as restricting verbal expression, invalidating feelings, guilt induction [1]. Although PPC has been widely linked to children’s maladjustment in Western samples, findings in collectivistic cultures have been mixed [22, 23]. One possible explanation is that prior studies typically relied on global measures of psychological control, which combine different tactics into a single scale [24]. Theoretically, love withdrawal uniquely threatens children’s attachment security [11] and frustrates their need for relatedness [17], distinguishing it from tactics such as guilt induction, which primarily constrain autonomy [4]. Some cultural psychologists have categorized guilt induction as a benign form of PPC, particularly in collectivistic contexts, where it involves inductive reasoning that highlights the impact of a child’s behavior on family members. Such practices may foster empathy for others’ perspectives and be interpreted as expressions of parental concern [22, 25]. Love withdrawal, in contrast, is considered a harsher form of PPC that involves little reasoning, threatens the parent–child bond, and is not thought to carry the same culturally adaptive meaning [25, 4]. Aggregating items from different dimensions into an overall PPC score may obscure the distinct negative effects of love withdrawal as a specific parenting practice [24].

Empirical evidence and research gaps

Empirical evidence suggests that a multidimensional model of psychological control—one that treats love withdrawal as a separate dimension alongside guilt induction or shame—fits the data better than a unidimensional model [24, 2]. Across diverse cultural and developmental contexts, findings further underscore the particularly detrimental role of love withdrawal in child development. Supporting the multidimensional conceptualization of psychological control, Nelson and his colleagues examined Russian preschoolers and found that different dimensions of parental psychological control were differentially associated with child aggression, highlighting the importance of distinguishing among specific control practices [16]. Building on this distinction, among Indian adolescents, parental love withdrawal was associated with lower self‑esteem, whereas guilt induction and shaming were linked to higher self‑esteem [25]. Similarly, in a sample of first‑generation Chinese American mothers and their preschool‑aged children, cross‑lagged structural equation modeling showed that maternal love withdrawal predicted higher levels of teacher‑reported bullying and aggression six months later, whereas guilt induction predicted lower levels of these behaviors [4]. Consistent with these patterns, in a U.S. sample of children aged 2.5–5 years, maternal love withdrawal was positively associated with relational aggression in both girls and boys, whereas guilt induction showed no such association; moreover, guilt induction was linked to lower levels of physical aggression among girls, while love withdrawal was not [15]. Finally, in a diverse sample of early adolescents (Grades 5–6), parental love withdrawal was positively related to internalizing and externalizing symptoms and negatively associated with attachment security [2]. Collectively, these studies highlight the distinct and harmful impact of parental love withdrawal on child development and underscore the importance of distinguishing it from other dimensions of parental psychological control.

It should be noted that there are several limitations in previous studies. First, most prior research focused primarily on participants older than middle childhood, and it remains unclear whether similar patterns are present in early childhood. Studying preschoolers may offer unique and valuable insights, as attachment to primary caregivers remains especially salient during this developmental period and young children are particularly vulnerable to negative parenting practices [11, 12]. Consistent with attachment and self‑determination perspectives, parental love withdrawal during early childhood may be especially likely to undermine attachment security and frustrate children’s need for relatedness [26]. Given that the need for secure attachment is pronounced in the preschool years, the impact of parental love withdrawal may be more substantial at this age [27], pointing to the value of studying this developmental period.

Second, previous research on parental love withdrawal has mainly focused on Western cultural countries, lacking attention to Chinese parents. However, Chinese parents may use love withdrawal strategies more frequently than parents in other countries. Wu and colleagues examined various parenting practices prevalent in China and the US by comparing mothers of preschool-aged children from both countries. They found that Chinese mothers scored much higher than their US counterparts in love withdrawal [6]. Additionally, parenting anxiety is prevalent in contemporary China, and parents may adopt love withdrawal practices driven by this anxiety [28, 7]. Many Chinese parents feel anxious about their children’s future and about the best ways to raise them. A recent study shows that 25.75% of parents of elementary school children experience high levels of anxiety [29]. Highly anxious parents may use love withdrawal even with young children [30], yet this issue remains underexplored in early childhood. Given its potential negative impact, the influence on children deserves more attention. The unique salience of love withdrawal among Chinese parents [6] highlights the need for further research on its impact on preschoolers’ adjustment. Addressing this underrepresented population could inform parenting practices that support children’s emotional well-being.

Moderating roles of child gender and effortful control

While examining how parental love withdrawal affects Chinese preschoolers’ behavior problems, potential moderators should also be considered. The differential susceptibility model suggests that certain susceptible factors may modify the links between parenting practices and children’s development [31]. Gender may be a potential moderator, as children’s maladjustment can manifest differently across genders. Gender roles may influence which types of behavior problems are more likely to develop. Boys, often associated with traits like competitiveness, boldness, and strength, may be more prone to externalizing problems, while girls, associated with traits like caution and submissiveness, may be more inclined toward internalizing problems [32]. Consistent with this, research has shown that boys tend to display more externalizing problems, while girls exhibit more internalizing problems [33]. When boys experience maternal love withdrawal, they may develop more externalizing problems rather than internalizing problems. However, this trend may be the opposite among girls. Unfortunately, most previous studies only consider gender as a covariate, but fail to examine gender differences in more depth [18, 4], which further increases the necessity of testing gender’s moderating role in the current study.

Another moderator worth considering is effortful control. Defined as “the ability to suppress a dominant response to execute a subdominant response, detect errors, and perform as planned” [34], effortful control enables preschoolers to better regulate their emotions to meet environmental demands [35]. In contrast, children who lack effortful control struggle with attention and impulsivity, making them more vulnerable to contextual risks [36]. Although no research examined how effortful control moderates the relationships between parental love withdrawal and children’s developmental outcomes, studies on its interactions with PPC offer relevant insights. Only among adolescents low in effortful control, but not those high in effortful control, PPC was positively associated with depressive symptoms [37]. A meta-analysis also found that the relationship between negative parenting and externalizing problems was moderated by the levels of effortful control. Specifically, for children low in effortful control, their externalizing problems were more strongly affected by negative parenting [38]. Overall, the evidence shows that children and adolescents not good at effortful control are more vulnerable to negative parenting.

Furthermore, the literature regarding effortful control also suggested examining its moderating role jointly with child gender. First of all, the moderating role of children’s effortful control in some studies is gender-specific. For instance, Choe and colleagues examined the relationship between maternal depressive symptoms and children’s externalizing problems using a cross-lagged model from early to middle childhood, as well as the joint moderating role of gender and effortful control. The results indicated that if experiencing high levels of maternal depressive symptoms, boys with lower effortful control were at risk for later externalizing problems, but this mechanism was not significant among girls [39]. In another study, how parental autonomy support interacted with effortful control in predicting adolescent rule-breaking behaviors differed by gender [40]. Additionally, multiple studies found that low levels of effortful control were associated with more externalizing problems only for boys but not for girls, indicating that effortful control may have unique developmental implications only for boys [41]. For these reasons, in the current study, the joint moderating effects of child gender and effortful control were examined regarding the effects of maternal love withdrawal on preschoolers’ behavior problems.

The present study

Taken together, we aim to test the longitudinal effects of maternal love withdrawal on preschoolers’ behavior problems and to examine the roles of child gender and effortful control as joint moderators. Following attachment theory [11, 12], differential susceptibility model [31], and evidence from previous research [36, 38], we build the conceptual model (Fig. 1) and hypothesized that: (a) maternal love withdrawal positively predicted later behavior problems; (b) among boys, maternal love withdrawal positively predicted later externalizing problems, but not internalizing problems; (c) among girls, maternal love withdrawal positively predicted later internalizing problems, but not externalizing problems; (d) effortful control would moderate the longitudinal effect of love withdrawal on preschoolers’ externalizing problems among boys, but not girls. Specifically, maternal love withdrawal would predict more externalizing problems among boys with low effortful control, but not among highly effortful control ones. Due to the inconsistent moderation effect of effortful control in the associations between negative parenting and internalizing problems, the directional hypothesis for the moderating role of effortful control when predicting internalizing problems was not formed.

The research framework for the current study

Method

Participants

In this two-wave, one-year longitudinal study, participants were 148 Chinese preschoolers (Mage = 4.02 years, SD = 0.79; 86 boys) and their mothers, who were recruited from two kindergartens in two Chinese Provinces (Hebei and Shandong). Preschoolers’ demographic information (at Wave 1), effortful control, and behavior problems (at Wave 2) were reported by their mothers. Of the mothers, 70.3% had less than a high school education, 23.6% held a high school diploma, and 6.1% possessed an undergraduate or graduate degree. In terms of household income, 39.2% earned Chinese yuan (CNY) 4,500 or less monthly, 37.2% fell into the CNY 4,500–6,000 range, 18.3% earned between CNY 6,000 and 10,000, and 5.3% reported monthly incomes exceeding CNY 10,000.

Procedures

Before conducting the study, the research ethics committee of the corresponding author’s academic institution reviewed and approved this study. With permission from the kindergarten, children were asked to take home after school an envelope of study materials, including a consent form, a cover letter describing the project, and a questionnaire for mothers. Parents learned from the study materials that their participation was voluntary and that they were free to withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences. If parents agreed to participate, they provided written consent. Participating mothers provided demographic information and their own love withdrawal towards children at Wave 1 (June 2020), and reported children’s effortful control and behavior problems at Wave 2 (June 2021).

Measures

Maternal love withdrawal

Mothers rated their own behaviors by using the love withdrawal subscale of Olsen’s psychological control questionnaire [42]. This subscale consists of 5 items. Example items are “Will be less friendly to my child when my child does not see things the way I do” and “Will ignore my child when my child has disappointed me”. Mothers reported how often they used the love withdrawal behaviors on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always) at Wave 1. Averaged score of all items was used as the indicator of love withdrawal. The Cronbach’s alpha for the love withdrawal questionnaire in this study was 0.62.

Effortful control

Mothers also rated preschoolers’ temperamental effortful control using the Child Behavior Questionnaire [43] at Wave 2. On a 7-point Likert scale, mothers rated 12 items that reflect preschoolers’ temperamental effortful control abilities. Sample items include “When the child is doing coloring games or drawing, he/she will appear attentive”. Averaged score of all items was calculated, with higher scores implying higher levels of effortful control. This questionnaire showed good reliability and validity in Chinese culture [44]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.75.

Behavior problems

The 25-item Chinese version of the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) was used to evaluate preschoolers’ behavior problems. SDQ, initially developed by Goodman [45], was further revised by Du to create the Chinese version [46]. The instrument includes five subscales focusing on children’s behavior and emotional problems in five aspects: emotional symptoms, peer relationships, hyperactivity, conduct problems, and prosocial behavior. Mothers assessed their children’s behavior problems on a 3-point scale ranging from 1 (never) to 3 (always). We took the mean of the emotional symptoms subscale and peer relationship subscale as an indicator of internalizing problems, and the mean of the hyperactivity subscale and conduct problems subscale as an indicator of externalizing problems. Cronbach’s alphas were 0.60 and 0.69 for internalizing problems and externalizing problems scales, respectively, in the current sample.

Data analyses

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 26.0 and Mplus 7.4 [47]. As suggested by our hypotheses, conducting analyses separately by gender is more suitable. To evaluate this plan, preliminary analyses were first conducted. Preliminary regression analyses were conducted before path analyses to test the longitudinal associations between maternal love withdrawal and child outcomes. Maternal love withdrawal, effortful control, and their interaction term were regressed on child outcomes after controlling for covariates. In the full sample, maternal love withdrawal positively predicted externalizing problems (β = 0.23, t = 3.31, p < .01), but not internalizing problems (β = 0.14, t = 1.69, p > .05). According to the differential susceptibility model, this non-significant result may indicate that gender, as a susceptibility factor, modifies the link between love withdrawal and internalizing problems. Additionally, multi-group analyses were used in Mplus to examine whether each gender should be analyzed separately. Specifically, after building the model of maternal love withdrawal and effortful control in predicting behavior problems, both the unconditional model (all paths being estimated freely) and the constrained model (all paths across genders being constrained to be equal) were estimated. The unconstrained model fitted the data well (χ2(12) = 7.62, CFI = 1.00, TLI = 1.00, RMSEA = 0.00), whereas the constrained model fitted the data poorly (χ2(24) = 38.377, CFI = 0.789, TLI = 0.807, RMSEA = 0.09; ΔCFI = 0.211, ΔTLI = 0.311, ΔRMSEA = 0.090). Additionally, the unconstrained model fit significantly better than the constrained model, Δχ2 = 30.757, p = .002; ΔCFI ˃ 0.01 [48], which indicates the existence of gender differences. As a result, analyses were conducted separately in each gender.

For the formal analyses, first, descriptive statistics were conducted, and Multivariate Analysis of Variance (MANOVA) was used to examine gender differences. Second, correlational analyses were conducted in each gender using SPSS 26.0. Third, a multi-group path analysis was conducted to test how behavior problems in each gender were affected by maternal love withdrawal and effortful control. All predictor variables were standardized, and the interaction term (i.e., love withdrawal × effortful control) was included in the model to test the moderating effect of preschoolers’ effortful control. Lastly, significant interaction would be further probed via simple slopes analysis and Johnson-Neyman analysis. The Expectation Maximization algorithm was used to account for the missing data. The following indices were used to evaluate the model fitness: the Chi-square statistic (χ2), the comparative fit index (CFI; acceptable > 0.90, good > 0.95), the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI; acceptable > 0.90, good > 0.95), and the root-mean-square error of approximation (RMSEA; acceptable < 0.08, good < 0.05) [49].

Follow-up univariate analyses indicated that boys showed more externalizing problems and less effortful control than girls at Wave 2. No differences were found between boys and girls in age, family income, Wave 1 (W1) maternal love withdrawal, or Wave 2 (W2) internalizing problems.

Path analyses

As expected, there were different result patterns in boys and girls (see Fig. 2). Among boys, neither W1 maternal love withdrawal nor the interaction term (i.e., maternal love withdrawal × effortful control) were significant predictors of W2 internalizing problems. Additionally, maternal love withdrawal positively predicted externalizing problems 1 year later, and the interaction term significantly predicted W2 externalizing problems. Among girls, although W1 maternal love withdrawal significantly predicted W2 internalizing problems, the interaction term did not significantly predict internalizing problems. Neither maternal love withdrawal nor the interaction term were statistically significant predictor of externalizing problems one year later.

Simple slope tests (Fig. 3) showed that W1 maternal love withdrawal positively predicted W2 externalizing problems (β = 0.53, p < .001) for boys low in effortful control (1 SD below the mean), but not for boys high in effortful control (1 SD above the mean; β = –0.08, p = .65). Furthermore, the Johnson-Neyman plot (Fig. 4) revealed that when effortful control scores were below 0.06 SD, maternal love withdrawal significantly affected externalizing problems. These results indicated a buffering effect, namely, if boys are high in effortful control, Wave 1 maternal love withdrawal would not predict W2 externalizing problems.

Discussion

Although prior studies have examined love withdrawal-related constructs using different terminology, research on the longitudinal effects of maternal love withdrawal in non‑Western contexts remains limited. The present study extends this literature by examining long‑term associations between maternal love withdrawal and preschoolers’ behavior problems in China. The findings show that maternal love withdrawal positively predicted externalizing problems but not internalizing problems. Further analyses revealed significant gender differences: for boys, low effortful control heightened their vulnerability to maternal love withdrawal, leading to more externalizing problems, while for girls, maternal love withdrawal predicted internalizing problems regardless of effortful control. These results are particularly meaningful in the current context.

As anticipated, maternal love withdrawal at Wave 1 positively predicted boys’ externalizing problems and girls’ internalizing problems a year later, consistent with prior research on preschoolers [26, 50]. By conveying conditional regard, maternal love withdrawal ties a child’s worth and the mother-child bond to the child’s behavior or achievements. Even within cultures that emphasize interdependence, children may perceive love withdrawal as a threat to their relationship with parents, experiencing it as rejection or hostility [4]. This association may be due to neural mechanisms, as physiological evidence from Event-Related Potential (ERP) studies shows that children experiencing higher levels of love withdrawal are more sensitive to inconsistencies between others’ words and actions [51], potentially developing more behavior problems.

We also found that the effects of maternal love withdrawal varied by child gender, providing empirical evidence for the differential susceptibility model. Specifically, as expected, maternal love withdrawal positively predicted externalizing problems in boys and internalizing problems in girls. This finding aligns with previous studies, where love withdrawal is associated with relational aggression in preschoolers only among boys, but not girls [25], and PPC’s association with depressive symptoms was found only in girls, but not in boys [52]. One explanation for these gender differences is that boys and girls may appraise and cope with maternal love withdrawal differently due to the different socialization of gender roles [53]. Girls are generally better at interpreting the emotional content of verbal and nonverbal cues and are more likely to prioritize maintaining relationships [54, 55]. In the context of maternal love withdrawal, girls may sacrifice their sense of identity to avoid making their mother feel bad and to preserve the parent-child bond. This tendency may increase girls’ risk of developing internalizing problems [52]. Conversely, facing maternal rejection, boys may increase externalizing behaviors to express noncompliance and resistance. This trend is more evident in early childhood, when compliance is a central issue [56–58].

Additionally, effortful control moderated the relationship between maternal love withdrawal and externalizing problems only in boys, with no moderation effect found for girls. One explanation is that girls tend to have more advanced effortful control abilities during early childhood [59, 60]. Effortful control, the ability to suppress dominant reactions and exhibit subdominant ones [61], helps children to regulate their behavior despite negative parenting. Under parental love withdrawal, children with higher effortful control can better manage their behavioral responses [62]. This suggests that girls, with their higher effortful control, may exhibit fewer externalizing problems than boys, making both effects non-significant (the main effect of maternal love withdrawal on externalizing problems and the interaction effect of effortful control). However, boys with lower effortful control showed greater vulnerability to maternal love withdrawal, aligning with previous findings that in early childhood, the association between negative parenting and externalizing behaviors was moderated by effortful control [63, 64]. High effortful control appears to protect against negative parenting, buffering the negative impact of maternal love withdrawal on externalizing problems. Because effortful control was measured at Wave 2, it is possible that Wave 1 love withdrawal had already influenced it. Consequently, the interaction effects should be interpreted with caution, and future studies should consider designs that include Wave 1 effortful control to clarify these relationships.

It is necessary to acknowledge several limitations of this study and connect them with directions for future research. First, the study’s generalizability is limited by its relatively small sample that was primarily composed of preschool children from lower-SES families. Future research should address this in two key ways: by recruiting larger and socioeconomically balanced cohorts to examine the broader applicability of the findings; and by including children at various developmental stages to test age as a key moderator and better illuminate developmental trends. Another limitation of this study is that baseline behavior problems were not controlled for, which may mean that the observed effects partly reflect stable individual differences or potential reverse causality. Future research should include baseline measures to clarify the direction of these associations. Moreover, both maternal love withdrawal and children’s behavior problems were reported by mothers, which may introduce mono-informant and common method bias. Future research should consider incorporating observed parent-child interactions, or combining objective and subjective measures with multi-informant designs (e.g., including teacher ratings), to strengthen the validity of the findings. Finally, this study only examined two moderators. To further understand the associations between parental love withdrawal and behavior problems, it will be interesting to assess other sources of variability, such as child cognitive appraisals, maternal sensitivity, and sibling relationships among children.

Conclusions

In summary, a significant longitudinal link was found between maternal love withdrawal and preschoolers’ behavior problems in China. Maternal love withdrawal emerges as a risk factor for children’s social-emotional development during the preschool years. Specifically, the findings highlight that maternal love withdrawal was associated with externalizing problems in boys and internalizing problems in girls, with effortful control moderating the effect on externalizing behaviors in boys.

These findings have several practical implications. Parenting programs should help caregivers recognize and reduce love withdrawal, emphasizing its potential harm to preschoolers’ well-being. Educating parents to respect their children’s thoughts and feelings may prevent behavior problems. Interventions for children exposed to high levels of maternal love withdrawal should adopt gender-sensitive approaches: focusing on enhancing effortful control and self-regulation in boys, and promoting open communication and emotional expression in girls to manage the potential internalizing problems. Educators and childcare professionals should also be informed about the negative impact of love withdrawal to better support affected children. Overall, this study highlights key parenting risk factors for early behavior problems and provides actionable insights for parents, educators, and practitioners.
