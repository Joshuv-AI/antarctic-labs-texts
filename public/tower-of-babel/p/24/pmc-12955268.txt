Objective assessment of long-term impact of COVID-19 on multiple sensory functions
Authors: Ahmad Odeh, Veronica L Formanek, Christina Smith, Nidhi Jha, Junichi Tajino, Jessica H Lewis, Lauren Gastineau, Shivani Patel, Songzhu Zhao, Lai Wei, Aaron C Moberly, Daniel M Merfeld, Christopher T Simons, Megan J Kobel, Kai Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis ongoing study investigates the impact of post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection (PASC) on broad sensory functions.MethodsSixty subjects aged 27–78 years were recruited who had contracted COVID-19 between 1/17/2020 and 12/21/2023 and had persistent symptoms (4.3–52.9, median = 27.48 months). Quantitative sensory assessments included (1) smell: 9-Item NIH toolbox odor identification, detection threshold to phenyl–ethyl alcohol, and retronasal candy test; (2) taste: modified NIH toolbox; (3) chemesthesis: nasal menthol lateralization thresholds and oral capsaicin identification; (4) hearing: pure-tone audiometry, otoacoustic emissions, words-in-noise recognition, and Dichotic Digits Test; (5) vestibular/balance: video head impulse testing, Subjective Visual Vertical, vestibular perceptual thresholds, and modified Romberg balance test; and (6) cognitive assessment: The Self-Administered Gero-Cognitive Exam, Digit Symbol Substitution Test, and Trail-Making Test.ResultsOverall, subjects self-reported high and overlapping dysfunctions: 67.3% smell, 63.6% taste, 56.5% balance and dizziness, 31.8% auditory, and 51.3% brain fog or cognitive dysfunctions, with varying discrepancy to the measured and confirmed deficits (smell 65.5%, taste 16%, vestibular 31.6%, hearing 53.4%, and cognition 19.1%). Significant associations were found between confirmed vestibular and auditory impairments (p = 0.04), and cognitive impairments with central components of vestibular (p = 0.03) and auditory (p = 0.01) impairments, but not with their peripheral components. Similarly, strong associations were found between identification components of smell and taste tests (p = 0.003), which may involve more central processing, but not with threshold tests (a more peripheral process). Hospitalization significantly associated with smell (p = 0.05), cognitive (p = 0.009), vestibular (p = 0. 001), and peripheral auditory (p = 0.01) dysfunctions. Age significantly associated with auditory (p = 0.02), vestibular (p = 0.01) and central olfactory (p = 0.03) dysfunctions.ConclusionsCOVID-19 impacts sensory systems broadly and differently, both peripherally and centrally, driven in part by aging, initial disease severity, and underlying post-COVID cognitive dysfunctions. Background

Since the outbreak in December 2019, the COVID-19 pandemic has ravaged the world, with over 775 million cases and more than 7 million deaths (as of March 2024, WHO). While most current patients with new COVID-19 infections report symptoms resembling those of the flu or common cold, neurological symptoms are still common among people with COVID-19, including sensory dysfunction. Smell dysfunction has become a hallmark symptom of COVID-19 and can present in isolation (the only symptom) or precede the occurrence of other symptoms [1–4]. The mechanism of smell dysfunction is unclear, potentially involving peripheral or central pathways. Expression of ACE2, the receptor targeted by SARS-CoV-2, is found not in olfactory sensory neurons (OSNs) but instead in supporting cells (sustentacular cells) and horizontal basal cells in the olfactory epithelium [5]. Sustentacular cells are functionally important for mucus composition (glucose, ion exchange, and gradients) and for structurally supporting OSN dendrites [6, 7]. Experimental manipulation of sustentacular cells has been shown to cause anosmia [6, 7]. Central mechanisms may also be involved, given reports of inflammation in the olfactory bulb [8].

Related to smell dysfunctions, early reported COVID-19-related “taste dysfunction” has been speculated to be due to impaired flavor perception consequent to retronasal smell impairment. However, recent quantitative measurements suggest that loss of taste function (beyond flavor perception) and chemesthesis (trigeminal function) may indeed exist in some COVID-19 patients [9–12]. There is no previous evidence that viral upper respiratory infections, such as flu or cold, would cause loss of taste or chemesthesis functions, and it remains unclear how these dysfunctions manifest in different taste sub-modalities.

In other sensory systems, up to 15–20% of patients report subjective changes in hearing, tinnitus, and/or dizziness [13, 14]. Evidence of potential auditory and vestibular impairments is additionally supported by several case studies of COVID-19-related sudden hearing loss or vertigo and cross-sectional studies identifying sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) and/or changes in cochlear function [14–18]. While there is a strong precedent of viral infection affecting inner ear function (e.g., Zika, West Nile, herpes simplex) [19], the exact impact of COVID-19 on inner ear function is unknown. Several mechanisms have been suggested, including direct neural effects, neural inflammation, and hypoxic insults [13, 18, 20].

Recent clinical observations also reveal that a significant portion (73–76%) of COVID-19 patients may still have long-term and persistent symptoms 60–180 days after diagnosis [1]; the most common symptom to persist is fatigue or muscle weakness, as well as neurological disorders; one study reported that over 91% of patients, whether or not they had a neurological diagnosis when first hospitalized for COVID-19, had such problems 6 months after discharge [21]. It is unclear how multisensory dysfunctions manifest among these long COVID patients or how the prolonged vs acute phases differ. We aimed to use a comprehensive set of sensory tests to determine the long-term impact of COVID-19 on broad sensory functions (smell, taste, chemesthesis, hearing, and vestibular/balance function).

Methods

Recruitment

Patients with an ICD10 code U09.9 (“Post COVID-19 condition, unspecified”) were recruited through electronic medical records direct messaging. All interested subjects were then asked to complete a questionnaire probing the presence and severity of sensory dysfunctions (see Additional file 1). To be eligible, they must have longstanding complaints (minimum 90 days) in at least one sensory aspect: smell, taste, hearing, balance, or dizziness. Exclusion criteria (to rule out non-COVID-related pre-existing conditions) included:

Olfactory, taste, or hearing dysfunctions due to any reason other than COVID-19.

Olfactory, taste, or hearing dysfunctions due to any reason other than COVID-19.

Any prior balance issue unrelated to COVID-19.

Any prior balance issue unrelated to COVID-19.

History of severe head trauma or traumatic brain injury.

History of severe head trauma or traumatic brain injury.

Any previously existing neurologic conditions and severe health problems unrelated to COVID-19.

Any previously existing neurologic conditions and severe health problems unrelated to COVID-19.

Current pregnancy

Current pregnancy

Study design

This was a longitudinal prospective cohort study approved by the Ohio State Biomedical Sciences Institutional Review Board (approval number: 2022H0414). All subjects signed consent and authorization forms for participation and to enable their data to be included in this study. Olfactory, taste, chemesthesis, auditory, and vestibular functions were tested extensively: (1) smell: 9-Item NIH toolbox odor identification (ID) [22], Ortho nasal odor detection threshold (ODT) to phenyl–ethyl alcohol (PEA) [23], and retronasal candy test [24, 25]; (2) taste: modified NIH toolbox [26]; (3) chemesthesis: nasal menthol lateralization thresholds [27, 28], oral perception of capsaicin solution, which activates TRPV1 trigeminal nociceptors; (4) hearing: pure-tone audiometry (PTA) [29], otoacoustic emissions (OAE) [30], words-in-noise (WIN) recognition [31, 32], and Dichotic Digits Test (DDT) [33, 34]; (5) vestibular/balance: video head impulse testing (vHIT) [35], Subjective Visual Vertical (SVV) [36, 37], vestibular perceptual thresholds [38, 39], and modified Romberg balance test [40]; (6) cognitive assessment: Self-Administered Gero-Cognitive Exam (SAGE) [41], Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSS) [42], and Trail-Making Test (TMT-A and TMT-B) [43]. The testing was split in two sequential sessions: (1) chemosensory testing, (2) hearing, vestibular and balance testing, with each session taking about 2–3 h. Please see Additional file 2: Supplementary Methods [44–49] for details of these tests.

Statistical analyses

Fisher’s exact test (univariate analysis) was used to examine the association between each pair of complaints including smell, taste, auditory, vestibular, and cognitive. Sensitivity and specificity of each measure, as well as the combination of measures, relative to patients self-reported complaints of sensory dysfunction were determined. Differences between subgroups (i.e., those reporting different symptoms) were examined using t-tests, ANOVAs, or non-parametric equivalents when normality of data was not identified. To quantify relationships between test measures, regression analyses or Pearson correlations were performed. Given that these analyses are preliminary and exploratory in nature, post-hoc corrections for multiple comparisons were not applied; results should be interpreted with caution. These analyses were conducted in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Sixty subjects (aged 27–78 years, median 51 years) with long COVID symptoms lasting more than 3 months were recruited. They contracted COVID-19 between 1/17/2020 and 12/21/2023, with symptoms persisting until enrollment for 4.3 to 52.9 months (median = 27.5 months). A majority of subjects reported only one confirmed COVID-19 infection; however, nineteen subjects had multiple infections, with three reporting three infections, one having four infections, and one having more than four infections. Since Delta, a variant of concern [50], dominated US circulation from June 26 to Dec 18, 2021, we estimated n = 22 were pre-Delta variants (original, Alpha, and other); 27 were post-Delta variants (mostly Omicron family); and only five were likely the Delta variant. The majority of patient cases were considered as mild per CDC standard, as only eight were hospitalized due to COVID-19 infection, among which two were intubated. The majority of subjects were fully vaccinated (n = 47) at the time of study enrollment, but only 21 subjects were fully vaccinated prior to their COVID-19 infection. As the initial step following recruitment, patients were asked to complete a REDCap questionnaire assessing a range of sensory complaints and cognitive function; however, only n = 55 completed.

Table 1 presents the number and percentage of individuals self-reporting sensory issues alongside confirmed deficits based on specific sensory tests. Overall, there was a high prevalence of self-reported sensory complaints. Subjects’ self-reporting of smell and taste complaints largely overlapped. Of those who reported ongoing chemosensory impairments (38/55 = 69.1%), 92.1% (35/38) reported taste impairment, while 97.3% (37/38) reported smell impairment. Among those reporting smell impairment, 17/37 (46%) reported a predominantly diminished sense of smell (anosmia or hyposmia), while 20/37 (54%) reported smell distortion (parosmia/phantosmia) in addition to a diminished sense of smell. Among those reporting taste impairment, 19/33 (57.6%) reported a predominant diminished sense of taste (hypogeusia), while 14/33 (42.4%) reported taste distortion (dysgeusia) in addition to diminished taste. Dizziness and balance issues were reported by 56.5%, hearing problems by 31.8%, and cognitive difficulties, particularly brain fog, by 51.3%. Finally, 7 subjects (16%) reported subjective unilateral or bilateral tinnitus.

NIH toolbox odor ID testing

Fifty-five participants completed the in-person chemosensory testing. Using the published age and gender adjusted normative data provided by the NIH Toolbox [22], 30.9% (17/55) participants had some olfactory impairment. 14/17 of these participants self-reported smell complaints. Another 23 participants self-reported smell complaints but tested within the normative range. Significant differences were found between subjects with self-reported diminished smell function (anosmia/hyposmia n = 17, ID score 5.76 ± 2.5) and with smell distortion (parosmia/phantosmia, n = 20, ID 7.45 ± 1.6, p < 0.05). The mean ID score for all 55 subjects was 7.0/9 (median 8/9), and among those without smell complains (7.8 ± 1.1, n = 18) (see Fig. 1). Significant group differences were also found for 5 of the 9 items: lemon odor, playdoh odor, bubble gum odor, coffee, and flower odor, all p < 0.05. We asked all subjects to rate odor intensity and odor pleasantness (0–100 linear scale) for two odors: coffee and flower, and the rating significantly differed across subgroups (see Fig. 1). In general, subjects with diminished sense of smell had poorer odor identification, lower perceived odor intensity, but similar reported odor pleasantness as subjects without smell complaints. Subjects with smell distortion complaints had close to normal odor identification, moderate declined perceived odor intensity, but significantly lower perceived odor pleasantness than other cohorts.

Plot of NIH toolbox odor identification scores (left), identification correctness of each odor (right), and intensity and pleasantness ratings (bottom), grouped by subject’s complaint. * p < 0.05

The subjects as a whole performed similarly across different odors (correctness from popcorn 43/55 = 78.25% to natural gas: 49/55 = 89.1%), except the chocolate odor with significantly poorer performance (25/55 = 45.5%) and with no significant difference between sub-groups (no smell complaint 50.0%, hypo 45.0%, para 41.18%).

Retronasal candy flavor testing

One subject was not able to complete this test due to concerns of food allergy, making the total 54. The mean candy retronasal flavor ID score was 4.4/7 and the median 4/7. We defined the cutoff to be below the median or ≤ 4/7, and 51.9% (28/54) subjects got 4 or less correct (below the cutoff). Twenty-three of these 28 subjects had self-reported smell complaints. Subjects with self-reported diminished sense of smell (anosmia/hyposmia n = 17) had a significantly lower (worse) retronasal candy flavor ID score (no smell complaints 5.2 ± 1.1, hypo 3.6 ± 2.5, paro 4.4 ± 1.6, p < 0.05), but not for subjects with distorted smell complaints (parosmia/phantosmia, n = 20) (see Fig. 2). Hyposmia subjects also perceived lower flavor intensity of all 7 candies compared to subjects without smell complaints (see Fig. 2), but paro/phantosmia subjects only had significantly lower perceived flavor intensity for two of the seven candies (grape and raspberry).

Plot of retronasal candy flavor identification score (top left), intensity rating (top right), and candy sweet and sour intensity (bottom left) and their correlations (bottom right), grouped by patient complaints. * p < 0.05

Across different candies, lime, peach, pineapple, and orange flavors are more easily identified by the subjects as one cohort (correctness between 43/54 = 79.6% to 38/54 = 70.4%), whereas apple, raspberry, and grape flavors are more difficult to identify (correctness between 29/54 = 53.7% to 21/54 = 38.9%).

PEA ODT

Fifty-four of the 55 subjects took part in unilateral PEA detection threshold measurements. With a cutoff below 8 reflecting a clinically significant diminution in olfactory sensitivity [23], 12/54 on the left and 12/54 on the right side were below the cutoff, which includes 8 bilateral deficits, 8 unilateral deficits, and a total of 16 subjects with at least one side deficit, and a majority of them (14/16) with self-reported dysfunctions. Another 23 subjects self-reported olfactory dysfunction, but scored normal on both sides. Significant differences were found between subjects with and without self-complaints (no smell complaints 26.1 ± 8.1, hypo 18.7 ± 12.8, paro 17.9 ± 11.2, p < 0.05, see Fig. 3).

Combining quantitative psychophysical olfactory testing results

In general, different olfactory tests had high specificity (from 88.9 to 72.2%) as the majority of subjects who tested abnormal, self-reported some olfactory dysfunctions, but poor sensitivity (from 37.8% to 63.9%), with the best being Candy retronasal flavor ID with 63.9% sensitivity and 72.2% specificity. This may reflect that each test only captures one aspect of olfactory function. Thus, we further define confirmed olfactory impairment of a subject by their failure on any of the single olfactory tests. In doing so, 36/55 = 65.5% had some measured olfactory impairments, which was very close to the self-reported prevalence (37/55 = 67.3%). Majority of those self-reporting smell dysfunction (29/37 = 78.4%) had failed one or more tests (14/17 self-reported hyposmia, 15/20 self-reported parosmia/phantosmia). Only 8/37 who had self-reported smell dysfunction tested normal on all olfactory tests, and predominantly self-reporting parosmia/phantosmia (5/8). Another 7/18 did not self-report any smell dysfunction, yet tested with some measured impairments. Overall, combining all olfactory tests captures subjects’ self-reported olfactory symptoms with 78.4% sensitivity and 61.1% specificity, an improvement over each individual test.

Menthol lateralization

All 55 participants completed the menthol lateralization detection thresholds. A significant difference in thresholds was found between subjects with self-complaints of olfactory dysfunction (especially hyposmia) and subjects without (no complaint 8.7 ± 5.2, hypo 5.7 ± 5.6, paro 7.6 ± 5.0, p < 0.05, see Fig. 3). Based on the previous published normative cutoff of ≥ 6 [27, 28], 20/55 on the left and 19/55 on the right side were below the cutoff, which includes 9 bilateral deficits, 21 unilateral deficits, and a total of 30 subjects with deficits on at least one side. Interestingly, the majority of those had self-reported smell or taste dysfunction (23/30 = 76.7%). Only 15/38 = 39.5% who self-reported chemosensory dysfunction tested normal on menthol lateralization, and the majority of them (11/15 = 73.3%) had self-reported parosmia/phantosmia/dysgeusia. A higher percentage of subjects who self-reported hyposmia or hypogeusia (11/15 = 73.3%) were found to have confirmed nasal trigeminal impairments, but only 12/23 = 52.2% subjects who self-reported parosmia/phantosmia/dysgeusia were found to have confirmed nasal trigeminal impairments.

Oral chemesthesis and taste testing

For oral chemesthesis, all subjects, regardless of self-reported taste or smell status, correctly identified the 5 ppm capsaicin solution (a trigeminal TrpV1 ligand), with 100% correctness. For the modified NIH taste test, overall correctness for sweet, salty, bitter were quite high from 78.2% (bitter) to 89.1% (salty), with the exception of sour with only 50.9% correctness and with no significant difference between subjects with self-reported taste dysfunction or without (42.1% vs 63.6%). Taking into account the potential sour/bitter confusion, a common phenomenon [51], we defined confirmed taste impairment as subject misidentification of one more taste solution other than either a sour/bitter or bitter/sour mistake. 16.4% (9/55) participants had confirmed taste impairments. Six of nine of these participants self-reported taste dysfunction, but there are a large number of subjects who self-reported taste dysfunction but without measured and confirmed taste impairment (29/35). No significant difference in taste identification score was found between subjects with self-complaints and subjects without (Fig. 4).

Plot of NIH toolbox taste identification scores (left) and taste intensity ratings (right), grouped by subjects’ complaints. * p < 0.05. ID: identification; LMS: Labeled Magnitude Scale

During the candy test, we also asked subjects to rate the sweetness and sourness intensity using a linear 0–10 scale. The data shows that the rated sour intensity seemed to be in a different trend than that of sweet taste (Fig. 2), and subjects with self-reported dysgeusia rated sourness higher than subjects with no taste complaints (not statistically significant), and than subjects with hypogeusia (significant, see Fig. 2). In the NIH taste test, however, the rated intensity of the sour solution for subjects with self-reported hypogeusia was significantly lower than that of subjects without taste complaints and subjects with self-reported dysgeusia (see Fig. 4). Sourness was the only rating that significantly differed across subgroups in all taste intensity ratings using both the NIH toolbox and candy taste tests.

Auditory testing

Overall, forty-eight participants completed the auditory test batteries (52.57 years ± 12.8), among which four self-reported hearing issues prior to COVID-19 infection, thus were excluded from the analysis, yielding n = 44 included in analyses. Fourteen of these 44 (31.8%) participants self-reported ongoing hearing issues post-COVID-19 infection.

Audiogram

Based upon WHO hearing impairment guidelines (SNHL: PTA > 25 dB HL), 10/44 were identified as having a left sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL), and 10/44 had a right SNHL. This represents 8 bilateral hearing losses, 4 unilateral losses, yielding a total of 12 subjects with SNHL on at least one side. Among the 14 participants who self-reported hearing issues associated with COVID-19 onset, 6 had a confirmed bilateral SNHL, while the remaining 8 had hearing within normal limits (i.e., ≤ 25 dB HL), bilaterally. Among the 30 participants who denied any hearing difficulty, SNHL was actually identified in 6 subjects (see Table 2). Significant difference in PTA was found between subjects with self-complaints of hearing issues and subjects without (PTA: self-report HL 22.54 ± 12.82, no HL 16.13 ± 10.83, p < 0.05).

Words-in-noise (WIN) testing

Words-in-noise (WIN) testing revealed speech-in-noise deficits in 27/44 participants: mild loss (WIN threshold 6.8–10) was seen in 21 participants, moderate loss (WIN threshold 10–14.8) in 3 participants, severe (15.6–19.6) in 2 participants, and profound (> 20.4) in 1 participant, including 12 bilateral WIN deficits and 15 unilateral WIN deficits. Among the 14 participants with self-reported HL, 10 were found to have abnormal performance on the WIN (7 mild, 2 moderate, 1 severe). Normal bilateral WIN thresholds were found in 17 subjects, including 4 subjects who self-reported a COVID-19-associated hearing loss (see Table 3). There was not a statistically significant difference between the self-complaint and no complaint groups for WIN thresholds (see Table 3). A significant correlation was found between audiogram (PTA) and WIN thresholds (r = 0.78, p < 0.05), suggesting the WIN performance was correlated to peripheral pure tone sensitivity. All 13 subjects with documented SNHL displayed WIN losses, as expected. However, of the 28 participants with abnormal WIN thresholds, 15 had normal hearing sensitivity bilaterally.

Dichotic Digits Test (DDT)

Three subjects did not complete the Dichotic Digits Test (DDT), one with pre-existing hearing loss and 2 without self-reported hearing loss, reducing the total n = 42. Using age corrected data [33], 14/42 subjects exhibited abnormal DDT free recall scores (9 bilateral and 5 unilateral losses), suggesting potential central auditory dysfunction. Of these, 7 denied (self-report) any hearing issues associated with COVID-19. Among the 13 participants who self-reported hearing issues associated with COVID-19, 7 exhibited abnormal DDT performances—6 also exhibited abnormal WIN and 4 had a bilateral SNHL (see Table 4). Moderate differences in DDT scores were found between those with and without self-complaints (DDT: self-report HL 84.05 ± 19.28, no HL 92.02 ± 8.66, p = 0.05). Moderate correlations were also found between DDT and audiogram PTA (r = 0.35, p < 0.05) and WIN thresholds (r = 0.35, p < 0.05), in line with the fact that DDT is impacted by peripheral auditory sensitivity and captures central auditory function, as does the WIN.

Otoacoustic emissions (OAEs)

Three subjects did not complete distortion product otoacoustic emissions (DPOAEs), one with self-reported HL and 2 without, reducing the n = 42. Of these subjects, 14/42 subjects had absent DPOAEs in at least one ear, defined as an absent response at more than two adjacent frequencies. Among the 13 subjects with self-reported COVID-19 related hearing complaints, 7 had absent DPOAEs, 6 of whom also had a confirmed bilateral SNHL as well as abnormal WIN thresholds, consistent with degree of hearing loss. Another 7 subjects with absent DPOAEs denied COVID-19 related hearing complaints, 4 of whom had abnormal WIN and 3 had a documented SNHL.

Combining quantitative hearing testing results

In general, different hearing tests, which assessed peripheral and/or central aspects of hearing functions, had high specificity (from 75.9% to 87.1%) that the majority of subjects who tested abnormal, have some self-reported hearing problems, but had lower sensitivity (from 50.0% to 53.8%). The only the exception being WIN test, had high sensitivity (71.4%) but low specificity (43.3%). A total of 32 participants were identified with some auditory abnormality on one of the tests (i.e., mild or greater speech-in-noise deficit on WIN, absent DPOAEs, PTA > 25 in one or both ears, or worse age-adjusted performance on DDT). These 32 participants include 12 subjects with self-reported COVID related hearing complaints, yielding high sensitivity (n = 12/14, sensitivity: 85.7%) but extremely poor specificity, as 20 out of 30 participants with no self-reported hearing issue were identified with some auditory abnormality (specificity: 33.3%). However, if we combined all hearing test results, excluding WIN, it resulted in both higher sensitivity (11/14 = 78.6%) and high specificity (60%) in capturing self-reported hearing problems, an improvement over individual test. Therefore, while other hearing tests potentially underestimate auditory dysfunctions, WIN likely overestimates, potentially due to either subtle changes in peripheral and/or central auditory functions or due to the documented impact of age on speech in noise recognition and wide age-range of participants included.

Vestibular/balance data

Overall, 46 subjects completed one or more vestibular or balance tests. In the self-report questionnaires, two subjects reported clinically diagnosed vestibular disorders prior to COVID-19, which were excluded from the analysis, making n = 44. Among the 44 subjects, 26/44 (56.5%) self-reported ongoing dizziness, vertigo, or imbalance associated with COVID-19.

vHIT

Thirty-three subjects completed horizontal semicircular canals (SCC) vHIT. Four subjects were identified as displaying abnormally low gain (i.e., gain < 0.8)—three were unilateral, and one was bilateral. Of these 4 subjects, one reported being diagnosed with a vestibular disorder after COVID-19-associated symptoms. The remaining three subjects all self-reported ongoing COVID-19-associated dizziness and imbalance.

Subjective visual vertical

Overall, 43 subjects completed SVV testing (mean = 0.045; SD = 1.009). Three subjects’ data were deemed invalid due to excessive variability in performance (i.e., SD of performance was 1.5 × larger than mean performance) and were excluded. Of the remaining n = 40, only 4 were identified with an abnormality, (i.e., SVV deviation > 2.0), including one reporting a diagnosis of a vestibular disorder after COVID-19, one self-reporting COVID-19-associated dizziness symptoms and two denying any significant dizziness.

Vestibular perceptual thresholds

For vestibular thresholds, only 24 subjects completed due to additional equipment exclusionary criteria (i.e., weight over 250 lbs, height over 6′ 2″, and/or inability to safely ambulate) or due to the length of the test protocol. Performance indicating an abnormality was defined as greater than the mean + 1.5 SD for their age matched cohort 100 healthy subjects [52]. Overall, one participant exhibited an abnormal 0.5 Hz roll tilt threshold, suggesting impaired central integration of canal-otolith integration; 3 participants exhibited abnormally high y-translation thresholds, suggesting impaired perception with predominant utricular contributions; 2 participants exhibited abnormally high yaw rotation thresholds; but half of the participants (12/24) exhibited an abnormal z-translation threshold, suggesting impaired perception with predominant saccular contributions or the central integration required to separate transient earth-vertical translations from the steady presence of gravity. Overall, 14 participants had one or more abnormal thresholds, among whom 9 self-reported dizziness or balance issues.

Balance testing

Thirty-eight subjects completed balance testing due to additional equipment exclusionary criteria (i.e., weight exceeding 300 lbs, inability to stand unassisted for 60 s, and/or prosthetic limb). Analyses focused on Condition 4 (eyes-closed, standing on foam) due to the predominant vestibular contributions [53] and medial lateral (ML) root mean square (RMS) due to the links to fall history [40] and associations to vestibular threshold testing [54, 55]. An abnormality was defined as an inability to complete testing (e.g.: having to open eyes, uncross arms, or take a recovery step during the trial). Additionally, an abnormality was defined as ML RMS greater than the mean + 1.5 SD obtained using identical methodology in 100 healthy control adults [52]. Overall, 12 subjects exhibited impaired balance performance. Of those, 8 self-reported dizziness or imbalance, 3 did not exhibit abnormalities in any vestibular threshold while 6 exhibited an elevated z-translation threshold.

Combining quantitative vestibular testing results

Based on testing, 25 participants were identified as having one or more abnormalities, however, they poorly matched with those self-reported dizziness or balance issues (sensitivity 50%, specificity 40%). Balance performance during eyes-closed foam surfaces maximizes vestibular contributions to postural control [53] and ability to complete this test has been used to identify potential vestibular dysfunction in multiple large cohort studies [56]. However, tests of vestibular reflexes (e.g., vHIT) show weak relationships to balance [57–59] while measures of vestibular perception exhibit stronger relationships [55, 60]. Nevertheless, if we use subjects’ balance test as the outcome variable, then vestibular abnormality (i.e., abnormality on SVV, vHIT, and/or vestibular thresholds) can predict subjects’ failure of balance test with 64.3% sensitivity and 61.5% specificity. This suggests that subjective perception of dizziness or balance issue does not necessarily predict true functional outcome (i.e., balance test), potentially reflecting the multi-sensory and motor components of postural control [53]. Nevertheless, quantitative vestibular assessments may explain some measured functional impairments (balance test), but do not capture all subjects reporting subjective perception of dizziness/imbalance.

Cognition

A total of thirty-nine participants completed cognitive assessments, and 20 of whom (51.3%) self-reported COVID-19-associated brain fog. The SAGE is a cognitive screening test, and only four subjects showed performance below the normal cutoff of 17, two of whom reported COVID-19-associated brain fog (see Table 5). Three participants exhibited performance below age-matched normative data for the DSS and all 3 self-reported COVID-19-associated brain fog. For the TMT-A, 3 participants displayed abnormally low performance suggestive of cognitive deficits, and two of them self-reported COVID-19-associated brain fog. The 3 with abnormal performance on TMT-A also exhibited poor performance on the TMT-B, for which 3 additional subjects demonstrated abnormality. Overall, a majority of subjects who performed poorly in cognitive testing self-reported COVID-19-associated brain fog or cognitive difficulties (high specificity). But of those with self-reported COVID-19 brain fog or cognitive issues, a high percentage (15/20, 75%) performed normally in all cognitive assessments (low sensitivity). There were no significant differences between those with and without self-reported COVID-19-associated brain fog for the DSS (t (39) = − 0.41, p = 0.677), TMTA (t (39) = − 0.0293, p = 0.977), or TMTB (t (39) = 1.00, p = 0.322).

Multisensory association

We examined the pattern of multi-sensory dysfunctions (see Fig. 5). No associations were found between measured confirmed smell and taste impairments, nor between chemosensory and other sensory impairments. However, confirmed vestibular impairment was significantly associated with confirmed auditory impairment; participants with vestibular impairment had a higher proportion of auditory impairment compared to those without vestibular impairment (59.4% vs. 25%, Fisher exact p = 0.025). Additionally, cognitive impairment was significantly associated with vestibular impairment, with 77.8% of participants exhibiting cognitive impairment in the vestibular impaired group compared to 28.95% in the group without vestibular impairment (p = 0.012). Most COVID-19 related parameters (likely infected variant, number of infections, vaccination prior to COVID-19 infection, symptom duration) did not significantly associate with any of the individual sensory dysfunctions, except for hospitalization, which significantly associated with smell (p = 0.05), cognitive (p = 0.0086), and vestibular dysfunctions (p = 0.0011).

Scatter plot of measured confirmed impairments by subject ID. Each sensory impairment is denoted by a number on the y-axis (1—smell, 2—taste, 3—auditory, 4—vestibular, 5—cognition)

Previous studies suggest that smell dysfunction is more frequent in milder cases [61], a contradiction to our finding that may be due to different study scopes and methodology. Most importantly, our study used quantitative/objective assessments to define sensory dysfunctions, while most other studies used self-reporting, which can contribute to some of the discrepancies. Secondly, our study assessed the impact of hospitalization from a multi-sensory perspective. Among the 8 subjects who were hospitalized during their COVID-19 infection, all still had confirmed olfactory dysfunction after 7.8 to 52.4 (median 29.75) months; 7 still had confirmed vestibular dysfunction, 6 auditory dysfunction, and 4 cognitive dysfunction. This certainly sheds light on the impact of initial disease severity on broad sensory dysfunctions. Among the 8 hospitalized subjects, 5 were pre-delta, 2 post-delta, and only 1 likely delta. The two intubation subjects were both pre-delta and non-vaccinated. Although vaccination did not associate with most sensory and cognitive functions (except olfaction, p = 0.03), it does protect against hospitalization, as among the 8 hospitalizations, 7 were not vaccinated and only 1 was fully-vaccinated prior to COVID-19 infection (p = 0.037). Age was significantly associated with auditory (0.027) and vestibular (p = 0.0075) dysfunctions as well.

Finally, to further explore potential relationships underlying multi-sensory dysfunctions, we attempted to categorize sensory impairments into potential peripheral vs. central dysfunctions. For auditory measures, pure tone thresholds and DPOAEs were classified as predominantly peripheral due to their known relationships to cochlear function [62, 63], while DDT was classified as predominantly reflecting central auditory function [33, 34, 64, 65]. Speech-in-noise performance reflects both peripheral [66] and central auditory functions [31, 67, 68]; thus, it was not used in this classification. Similarly, for vestibular function, as the vHIT captures disruption in the vestibulo-ocular reflex and is sensitive to peripheral horizontal canal pathology [35, 69], vHIT abnormality was classified as a peripheral impairment. Vestibular perceptual thresholds are reliant on thalamo-cortical projections [70, 71]; thus, it was classified as reflecting central pathology. As balance performance is reliant on both sensory information and central processing in order to generate an appropriate motor response [53], it was not used in these classifications. The classification interestingly showed that cognitive impairment is only significantly associated with central auditory (p = 0.013) and central vestibular impairments (p = 0.027), but not with peripheral auditory nor peripheral vestibular impairments. There are no significant associations between central vs peripheral components of auditory (nor vestibular) impairments.

For chemosensory tests, we classified them into identification tests (e.g., NIH toolbox smell and taste ID, candy flavor ID), which may involve more central processes, vs threshold tests (e.g., PEA ODT, menthol LDT), which reflect more peripheral functions [72]. Even though we have shown above that no associations were found between overall smell and taste dysfunctions, this new classification showed that smell identification impairments are significantly associated with taste identification impairments (p = 0.0028). We did not perform any threshold tests for taste function; nevertheless, olfactory/trigeminal threshold impairments are not significantly associated with taste identification impairments.

Discussion

Subjects suffering from long COVID experience a wide variety of symptoms that affect various sensory systems. This study performed extensive quantitative testing on long COVID subjects to assess deficits in smell, taste, trigeminal, hearing, vestibular function, and balance. There was a large discrepancy between self-reported symptoms compared to measured symptoms for a majority of sensory functions. Generally, far more patients self-reported sensory impairments than the number who quantitatively tested abnormally. For example, for smell, 67.3% of subjects self-reported some dysfunctions. Yet only 29.6% showed confirmed impairment on PEA thresholds, 30.9% showed confirmed impairment on the NIH toolbox, and 51.9% showed confirmed impairment on retronasal odor ID. 64% of subjects reported taste dysfunction, but only 16% actually displayed deficits in taste ID. This large discrepancy in taste is likely due to common confusion between flavor (which is mediated in part by olfaction) and taste. By combining results from several olfactory testing methodologies, we can achieve higher sensitivity and specificity, approaching self-reported prevalence, suggesting the added value in multi-modal assessments. Even though no associations were found between overall smell and taste impairments, strong associations were found between identification components of smell and taste tests, which may involve more central processing, but not between threshold components of the tests (more peripheral process). This corroborated previous studies showing that proficiency in executive and cognitive functioning contributed significantly to odor discrimination and identification performance, whereas it is unrelated to performance in the odor threshold test [72]. For chemosensory functions, another key finding is the very different sensory dysfunction patterns between patients with self-reported diminished sense vs. distorted sense. In general, patients with diminished sense of smell and/or taste had poorer identification and lower perceived intensity, but their perceived pleasantness was equivalent to patients without complaints. In contrast, patients self-reporting a distorted sense had close to normal identification and moderate perceived intensity, but their perceived pleasantness was significantly less than other cohorts. These symptom patterns may also depend on the specific chemo-stimuli used. For example, for taste, sourness seems to follow a different trend than that of sweet that patients with self-reported dysgeusia even rate sourness higher than patients with no taste complaints, even though we did not see any significant difference in taste identification among these subgroups. The mechanism for such discrepancies may be multi-factorial. During the test, many patients with distorted senses would report that, e.g., “this is not the coffee that I used to smell, it smells very differently, but now I know it is coffee”—such that even though they can correctly detect and identify the odor, it does not fully capture their dysfunction. To our knowledge, few olfactory tests currently exist that effectively assess parosmia or phantosmia beyond subjective self-reporting, but their hedonic perception (e.g., pleasantness) of an odor is often more affected than identification or intensity ratings.

Similarly, more patients (56.5%) have self-reported dizziness and balance deficits than actually found in quantitative vestibular testing, in part due to that dizziness is a vague and broad complaint, and not always related to vestibular deficits. Nevertheless, combining several vestibular tests (vHIT, thresholds, SVV) resulted in a similar prevalence close to self-reported deficits. For vestibular perceptual thresholds, many more participants exhibited an abnormal z-translation threshold (n = 12) than an abnormal 0.5 Hz roll tilt threshold (n = 1) and y-translation thresholds (n = 3), suggesting impaired perception with predominant saccular contributions or impaired central integration. Interestingly, half of these subjects with an abnormal z-translation threshold (6/12) had impaired balance test performance which agrees with past data suggesting associations between postural sway and perceptual sensitivity with otolith contributions [55, 73].

Additionally, overall, vestibular pathways exhibit distinct neural pathways, and perceptual measures exhibit higher level thalamo-cortical projections in comparison to reflexive pathways [70]. While a small subset (n = 4) exhibited vHIT deficits suggesting less potential peripheral vestibular hypofunction, a larger proportion exhibited deficits involving higher-order central processes. Particularly, our past data suggests that z-translation, when performed seated and earth-vertical (i.e., parallel to gravity), reflects aspects of central processing required to disambiguate co-planar linear acceleration and gravity which are both encoded by the saccule [38, 44]. Thus, the relatively high proportion of participants exhibiting z-translation deficits suggests more profound changes in central processing of vestibular information. Further classification based on peripheral or central involvement of each test interestingly showed that confirmed cognitive impairment is only significantly associated with measured central vestibular impairment (vestibular thresholds, p = 0.027), but not with peripheral vestibular dysfunction (vHIT, p = 0.64). As postural control is reliant on sensory integration, another central vestibular task, co-occurring balance deficits may reflect larger changes in sensory integration. While SVV reflects visual and otolith integration [74], due to the simplicity of the task used and long duration after onset of symptoms, this paradigm may be unlikely to identify any subtle central deficits.

COVID-19, even in mild cases, is known to cause cognitive symptoms in a large fraction of patients, such as deficits in attention, executive functioning, language, processing speed, and memory—collectively referred to as “brain fog” [75, 76]. In this study, over 50% of subjects self-reported brain fog or cognitive difficulty; however, few (~ 20%) were actually confirmed with impairments based on the cognitive screening tests. Only 3 subjects exhibited abnormal performance on SAGE, 3 on DSS, 3 on TMT-A, and 6 on TMT-B; even combined (n = 9), these measured results were far less than self-reported, partly due to the sensitivity of these tests. Subjects may need to exhibit significant brain fog or focusing difficulties to be tested cognitively abnormal [77]. While the exact causes of these cognitive impairments are still under investigation [77–79], we showed in this study that they significantly associated with central sensory impairments (auditory, vestibular, and potentially smell and flavor identifications).

One notable exception is hearing in that far fewer patients (n = 14) self-reported COVID-19-associated changes in hearing with similar prevalence as quantitative testing, however, the testing results do not fully align with self-reported symptoms. This is in agreement with past data suggesting under-reporting of hearing loss dependent on audiogram configuration [80] and lack of sensitivity of auditory testing to capture perceived auditory deficits (i.e., hidden hearing loss) [81]. For example, only 6 of the patients who self-reported hearing loss, had confirmed hearing loss on pure tone audiometry while the rest had thresholds within normal limits, bilaterally. Similar to chemosensory tests, different hearing tests may probe different aspects of hearing function, capturing varying prevalence, e.g., 14 subjects exhibited abnormal DDT scores, 14 had absent DPOAEs, and 27 abnormal in WIN testing. DPOAEs mainly reflect outer hair cell functions, and DDT and WIN may capture more cognitive central language and speech perception. However, both the DDT and WIN are influenced by pure tone sensitivity, as captured by significant correlations in our dataset and partially reflected in the number of participants with co-occurring deficits on multiple tests. Combining these auditory tests (audiogram, DPOAEs, DDT, WIN) results in far more (n = 32) patients with abnormality on at least one test than self-noted hearing impairment. This may reflect the fact that the clinical tests captured changes in auditory function unrelated to COVID-19 (e.g., noise, age) and/or that auditory dysfunction was overestimated due to lacking properly age-adjusted normative data of some of the tests, particularly the WIN. Additionally, this may reflect that differential changes in peripheral cochlear or central auditory function are unaccounted for by a single test. A classification based on peripheral or central involvement of each test interestingly showed that cognitive impairment is significantly associated with central auditory (DDT, p = 0.013) impairment, but not with peripheral auditory impairment (audiogram + DPOAEs). However, there were no significant associations between central vs peripheral components of auditory impairments.

In addition to the discrepancy seen in testing, the incidence of sensory systems affected differed for every subject. While some subjects experienced deficits in a multitude of sensory symptoms, others were only experiencing impairments in one or two (see Fig. 5). Even though taste and smell dysfunctions have been a major focal point of COVID-19 research, we found that subjects experienced broad and overlapping sensory symptoms including balance issues, hearing loss, and brain fog. Some participants stated they struggled with other symptoms not tested in this study, including neuropathy, fatigue, and breathing difficulties to name a few. One of our hypotheses is that there may be common pathways, e.g., a cognitive perceptual process that broadly involves and affects all sensory function. If so, we would expect to find strong associations between different sensory modalities and that a sensory deficit in one system could predict sensory deficits in another system. Indeed, strong associations were found between confirmed vestibular impairments and confirmed auditory impairments (p = 0.04), potentially reflecting their shared structures in the inner ear. Additionally, cognitive dysfunction was significantly associated with central components of auditory and vestibular dysfunction, implicating likely involvement of central mechanisms. These results are also in agreement with a large body of literature suggesting a link between cognition and auditory and vestibular functions in multiple populations [82, 83]. Surprisingly, we did not find any association between measured smell and taste dysfunctions, and no association between chemosensory dysfunctions with other sensory/cognitive dysfunctions. However, we did find a strong association between identification components of smell and taste tests, which may involve more central processing than detection thresholds. Furthermore, the nasal trigeminal deficits, which have received less attention, were strongly associated with self-reported smell/taste dysfunction (23/30 = 76.7%), especially among the cohort with diminished, as opposed to distorted sense. This association could drive some patients’ smell/taste complaints, as trigeminal sensations are often misidentified by subjects as part of smell or taste perception, similar to how retronasal olfactory flavor perception is misidentified by people as taste perception.

Another hypothesis is that the severity of patients’ long COVID sensory symptoms could be linked with disease severity during COVID-19 infection: whether they were hospitalized or intubated, whether they were fully vaccinated prior to infection, the number of COVID-19 infections, the length of their long COVID symptoms, as well as the variant. It is often thought that the Delta is one of the more severe variants on sensory functions [50], as compared with the original and especially the later Omicron family [84]. However, surprisingly only 5 subjects among our cohort were likely infected by the Delta variant, while 27 long COVID subjects were likely infected by variants of the Omicron family. Most of these COVID-19 related parameters did not significantly associate with any of the individual sensory deficits, except for hospitalization, which significantly associated with smell (p = 0.05), cognitive (p = 0.0086), and vestibular (p = 0.004) dysfunctions; this suggests that initial disease severity may shape the landscape of some long-term sensory dysfunctions. Unfortunately, with only 8 out of 60 subjects hospitalized during their COVID-19 infection, the sample size is too small for a full meaningful subgroup analysis. Future studies with larger hospitalized cohorts are necessary. Nevertheless, this observation also highlights a key public health implication—that more long-term sensory sequelae may arise even following mild, non-hospitalized COVID-19 infections.

Limitations

One of the limitations of this study was patient retention. While many subjects were interested in participating in the study, the time commitment, transportation difficulties, work conflicts, health limitations and etc., yielded many participants with incomplete data sets, particularly for vestibular and balance assessments. Additionally, many participants did not qualify for aspects of vestibular testing (e.g., exceeding height or weight limits, inability to stand unassisted, known oculomotor disorders).

As aging has well-known consequences on multiple sensory modalities, the lack of an age-matched control group is a major limitation. When possible, age-adjusted normative data was used to determine if a clinically significant sensory deficit was noted. However, we are unaware of relevant normative data for some of the tests administered (e.g., WIN). Further, many of the auditory tests are impacted by peripheral hearing loss (e.g., WIN, DPOAEs). While all participants who identified hearing loss prior to COVID-19 were excluded from analyses, there were likely participants included who were exhibiting hidden age-related hearing loss due to the range of participants included. While analyses qualitatively attempted to segregate results on the basis of documented hearing loss (Tables 3, 4 and 5), the impact of peripheral hearing loss and age, as well as other confounding factors, e.g., recovery over time, cannot be fully accounted for in this cross-sectional single-cohort study. However, these preliminary results examining baseline multi-sensory dysfunction can inform future efforts in this patient cohort.

Other limitations of this study were the measures used to assess sensory deficits. While these measures allowed us to extensively test for dysfunctions, there are other measures that could be utilized but did not fit into a comprehensive and extensive protocol. Future directions for this study include completing follow-up testing on subjects who had confirmed sensory deficits in any of the systems tested. Our goal is to complete follow-up testing on subjects at 3-, 6-, 9-, and 12-month intervals in order to assess whether confirmed impairments continue to persist and develop as time passes. Future studies should focus on understanding how long COVID could impact other systems not tested in this study. These studies should use sensitive and quantitative measures to gain a thorough understanding of what deficits present in each subject and how they develop over time.

Finally, as with any clinical study, we acknowledge that our cohort includes significant variability in age, educational background, and COVID-19 symptom profiles. We have attempted to control for key confounding variables where possible, but we acknowledge that such heterogeneity may limit the identification of uniform deficit patterns.

Conclusions

Current research suggests that long COVID affects everyone and every sensory system differently, shaped likely by initial disease severity, aging and cognitive impairments post-COVID-19. For chemosensory dysfunctions, the type of dysfunction (diminished vs distorted), as well as common central processing (i.e., identification), may significantly impact patients’ symptom profile and sensory testing outcome. Strong associations were found between confirmed vestibular and auditory dysfunction, and to cognitive impairment, implicating likely involvement of central mechanisms. Combinations of different sensory tests may better reflect self-reported dysfunctions, suggesting added value in multi-modal assessments. A future detailed study on the systematic impacted by long COVID—both objective and subjective—could further help to answer why the symptoms subjects experience vary drastically and to improve patient care.
