Thinking Globally, Acting Locally: Water Use in a Hospital Hemodialysis Unit
Authors: Samuel Haddad, Noah Kittner, Jennifer E. Flythe
Published: 2024

Hemodialysis is a lifesaving treatment for over 3.5 million people with kidney failure worldwide, but, in its present form, comes at considerable environmental costs because of its high water and energy consumption and large amounts of generated waste. Hemodialysis treatments, including prime and rinse phases, can require as much as 490 L of water, equivalent to 158% of the average daily water use of an American household.1,2 Globally, dialysis-related water consumption is approximately 265 billion L/yr.3 With approximately 7500 outpatient clinics serving over 500,000 patients, hemodialysis in the United States accounts for a substantial portion of this water usage.4 Hospitals also consume significant water resources, accounting for 7% of the water used in commercial buildings across the United States.5 Estimates suggest that implementation of hospital water-saving practices may reduce water use and operational costs by as much as 15% and 11%, respectively.5

Water stress, resulting from diminished water resources in the setting of climate change and population growth, is an emerging concern. Models predict that approximately 40% of United States water basins will experience water shortages in the next 20–50 years if only relying on renewable water supplies.6 Southwestern and central United States are at particularly high risk. Improved water use efficiency is vital to ensuring adequate water supply for sustenance, agriculture, power, industry, and hygiene.

Water treatment systems that include reverse osmosis (RO) machines remove ionic, larger inorganic, organic, and microbial contaminants and generate high-quality water for the formulation of dialysate for hemodialysis machines.1 A portion of the water entering the RO machine is used for hemodialysis, whereas the remainder is flushed down the drain as reject water. Although modern RO machines are more efficient than older models, the RO process remains a major source of water waste, and the efficiency of RO systems can range from 40% to 80%.1,3,7 Although RO reject water is unfit for hemodialysis, it is suitable for other uses.1,3 Conservation programs in France, Australia, and the United Kingdom demonstrate the feasibility and cost-effectiveness of reducing hemodialysis-associated water use, including reusing RO reject water for sanitation and irrigation.1,7,8 However, to date, there are limited published data about dialysis-related water consumption and exemplar water reuse projects in the United States. Reasons for limited water reuse in the United States are not well described, but may relate to concerns about cost, sanitation, and/or lack of awareness.

While dialysis provider organizations are well positioned to lead water conservation efforts in the outpatient setting, hospital dialysis programs present opportunities for local action. The water footprint is a life-cycle–based assessment that supports (1) quantifying direct and indirect water consumption associated with a product, process, consumer, or area; (2) assessing the social, environmental, and economic effects of this consumption; and (3) creating a response strategy.9 In this perspective, we estimate the direct water footprint of a hospital hemodialysis unit, aiming to provide a blueprint for how local hospital systems might approach quantifying dialysis-related water consumption and developing responsive conservation strategies.

Water Consumption by a Hospital Hemodialysis Unit

We estimated the annual direct water consumption by the hemodialysis unit at University of North Carolina (UNC) Hospitals (Chapel Hill, NC) on the basis of water used in the water treatment process, hemodialysis treatments, and sanitation of unit-based hemodialysis machines (Figure 1). The 11-machine unit provided approximately 4200 hemodialysis treatments using Fresenius 2008T Bluestar machines to patients hospitalized at UNC Hospitals in 2023. The unit uses a MarCor CWP 102H RO System that operates 15 hours/d, 6 days/wk. We excluded water used by portable hemodialysis and continuous renal replacement therapy machines and water used in peritoneal dialysis. Because of potential variation in the proportion of water rejected by the RO machine on the basis of factors such as water temperature, flow rate, and patient census, we performed convenience sampling of RO reject flow rates (ml/min) from different days and times (15 observations over 6 weeks). Measurements were consistent (2.5–3.6 L/min) across time points and patient census sizes, except for one outlier value (9.1 L/min), which reflected a system flush. Overall, mean±SD (median [interquartile range]) RO reject flow rate was 3.3±1.6 L/min (2.8 [2.8–3.0] L/min). In calculating the average dialysate flow rate (700 ml/min) and treatment time (222 minutes), we used the unit's hemodialysis standard dialysate flow rate of 750 ml/min and treatment time of 240 minutes for 80% of treatments and lower flow rates (400 and 600 ml/min) and shorter treatment times (120 and 180 minutes) for the remainder (20%) of treatments to reflect the ramping of flow rates and treatment times during hemodialysis initiations. Using these values, we calculated the average hemodialysis treatment–related water consumption to be 155 L. We used the amount of water required for heat disinfection (360 L 6 days/wk) and bleach disinfection (375 L water 1 day/wk) to estimate the water consumption associated with hemodialysis machine sanitation.

We estimated the total direct water consumption by the UNC Hospitals hemodialysis unit in 2023 to be 1,717,000 L. Of this, RO reject water comprised approximately 927,000 L (54%). This calculation underestimates total water consumption because it does not account for indirect water use, for example, water associated with power consumption and the manufacturing of hemodialysis consumables, including the dialysate solution, as well as some direct operational water use. Differences in hemodialysis prescriptions (e.g., dialysate flow rates and treatment times), equipment (e.g., hemodialysis and RO machines), and RO reject flow rates affect water consumption, emphasizing the importance of conducting facility-specific analyses.

Potential Response Strategies

Water conservation strategies include use of lower dialysate flow rates, replacement of older RO machines with more efficient models, and consideration of incremental hemodialysis and/or less intensive dialysis in patients with sufficient residual kidney function.3,7 Because our estimates showed that RO reject water accounts for half of all water directly consumed in the hemodialysis process, implementation of RO reject water conservation strategies could substantially reduce water consumption in our hospital unit.7 On the basis of prior reports demonstrating cost-effectiveness of select reuse strategies and considering the specific needs of our hospital, we identified three potential opportunities for reuse of RO reject water. First, RO reject water could be harvested and rerouted to irrigate nearby recreation fields or landscaped areas adjacent to the hospital. Agar et al. demonstrated RO reject water reuse in irrigating cricket fields, bowling greens, and golf courses in Geelong, Australia.1 Second, RO reject water could be reused in hospital laundry facilities. For example, reject water makes up 10% of the water used in an Ashford, Kent, hospital laundry facility. Use of RO reject water in this capacity carries the added benefit of lowering detergent requirements because of the softened, preprocessed water.8 Finally, RO reject water reuse for hygiene, especially in toilets, is a well-documented conservation strategy.1,3,8 Reuse of gray water in toilets is an existing practice in UNC campus buildings that have double plumbing. Double-plumbed systems have one set of pipes for potable water and a second set of pipes for processed gray water that supplies toilets. Although our hospital currently lacks infrastructure to route RO reject water for use elsewhere, we intend for this water footprint assessment to catalyze interest in investing in water reuse infrastructure at our institution. Important future considerations for our hospital and others undertaking similar initiatives include cost-effectiveness analyses; clarification of local regulations that might restrict RO water reuse practices; and, if reuse of the water for irrigation is being considered, measurement of salt and total dissolved solids levels.10

Given the rising threat of water stress, prioritization of responsible water management is paramount. RO reject water is a well-recognized source of waste in hemodialysis, but action in the United States remains scant. In this direct water footprint analysis, we estimate that approximately 927,000 L/yr of RO reject water from a single hospital could be rerouted and repurposed. Using the water footprint assessment to quantify dialysis-associated water consumption is an important first step toward identifying local opportunities for conservation. Although targeting hemodialysis water use in hospitals would affect just a fraction of the total hemodialysis-related water consumption in the United States, it presents an opportunity for local health systems to contribute to global efforts to increase water conservation and environmental sustainability.
