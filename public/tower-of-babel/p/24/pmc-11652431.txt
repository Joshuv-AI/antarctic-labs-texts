Long COVID-19 and Coexistence of Fatigue and Depression: A Cross-sectional Study from Saudi Arabia
Authors: Abdulrahman Alharbi, Faisal Almogbel, Unaib Rabbani, Ziad A Memish
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesCoronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) is associated with various manifestations even after infection resolution. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of post-COVID-19 fatigue and its predictors.MethodsWe conducted a nationwide cross-sectional study among Polymerase Chain Reaction test confirmed COVID-19 cases in Saudi Arabia from July 2021 to February 2022. We collected data through telephonic interviews covering socio-demographics, comorbidities, body mass index, smoking, illness severity, and COVID-19 vaccination status. We assessed fatigue using Fatigue Severity Scale while depression was assessed using Patient Health Questionnaire-2. Logistic regression was employed to analyze the relationship between post-COVID-19 fatigue and depression.ResultsThe analysis included 361 participants with a mean age of 37 ± 10.5 years, among whom 43% were female. Approximately 10% had comorbidities, and 21% were current smokers. Nearly two-thirds (68%) of the participants reported mild illness. The prevalence of perceived fatigue was 22.7%, while fatigue measured by the Fatigue Severity Scale was 14.4%. The multivariable logistic regression model revealed that COVID-19 severity and depression were significant predictors of post-COVID-19 fatigue; adjusted odds ratio 1.87 (95% CI: 1.10 to 3.18) and 14.3 (95% CI: 4.55 to 45.0), respectively.ConclusionOur findings suggest a higher prevalence of perceived fatigue compared to that measured by the Fatigue Severity Scale, underscoring the importance of using a valid assessment tool for fatigue among COVID-19 patients to ensure proper management. The significant association between post-COVID-19 fatigue and depression highlights the need for psychological assessment of COVID-19 patients to enhance their post-infection quality of life.Keywords: COVID-19, Fatigue, Long COVID-19, Mental health, Saudi arabia

Introduction

Coronavirus 2019 (COVID-19) is a disease caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) that primarily affects the respiratory system. By the end of March 2024, it had impacted 775,251,779 millions of people worldwide [1]. COVID-19 patients may experience respiratory symptoms such as coughing or shortness of breath, as well as general symptoms like a high fever, fatigue, or headache, which are common during acute illness [2, 3]. While most COVID-19 patients fully recover, a significant proportion may experience persistent symptoms. This condition, known as long COVID-19 or post-acute COVID-19 syndrome, is characterized by the presence of new or ongoing symptoms 3 months after the initial onset of COVID-19 illness [4].

Fatigue is one of the most common symptoms reported by individuals experiencing post-COVID-19 or long-COVID. Along with respiratory symptoms like cough and shortness of breath, fatigue often persists for an extended period in many COVID-19 survivors [5–7]. In one study done in Ireland in 2020, 52% of the participants had fatigue, fatigue was not correlated with severity during acute infection [8]. Another study done in Iran in 2021, showed that fatigue was present in 17.5% of the population [9]. Local studies done in Saudi Arabia reported a prevalence of fatigue of 11.5% and 55.7% [10–12]. Persistent fatigue has been a common complaint reported for months after many infectious diseases such as community-acquired pneumonia, Giardiasis, Epstein Bar Virus, and Ross River virus [13, 14]. Follow up of other coronavirus survivors such as Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome (SARS), 64% reported fatigue at 3 months and 54% after 6 months [15, 16]. Following Middle East Respiratory Syndrome (MERS) infection, 48% had fatigue at 12 months [17].

Other common symptoms of long COVID can include cognitive difficulties (often referred to as “brain fog”), muscle or joint pain, chest pain, and difficulty sleeping, among others. However, the specific symptoms and their severity can vary widely from person to person. Studies have shown that patients with long COVID-19 experience anxiety, stress, and depression [18]. A study from Germany reported a significantly higher prevalence of stress, anxiety, and depression among patients with long-term COVID-19 as compared to controls and fully recovered patients [19]. They further reported that the post-COVID-19 mental illnesses were not related to pre-infection mental illness. On the other hand, a study from Sweden reported a weak correlation between general fatigue and insomnia while no relationship was found between depression and anxiety [20].

Given the co-existence of fatigue and mental illness in long-term COVID-19 patients, it is crucial to investigate their association within the local context to inform treatment and support plans for affected individuals. There have been studies conducted in Saudi Arabia to investigate the burden and predictors of symptoms including fatigue among long-COVID-19 patients [10, 12, 21–24]. Another recent study compared mental illnesses and social functionality including fatigue in the Ha’il region of Saudi Arabia [25]. However, none of these have explored the relationship between post-COVID-19 fatigue and mental well-being. Therefore, the present study seeks to determine the prevalence of post-COVID-19 fatigue and evaluate its predictors in Saudi Arabia.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study was a national cross-sectional investigation encompassing all Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) confirmed COVID-19 cases throughout Saudi Arabia from July 2021 to February 2022. Participant data were collected from the national database of confirmed PCR-positive COVID-19 cases.

Sample Size and Sampling

The estimated required sample was 382, based on the formula: n = (Z2 x P x (1 - P))/e2. Where: Z = value from standard normal distribution corresponding to desired confidence level (Z = 1.96 for 95% CI), P is the expected true proportion, which was assumed to be 50%, and e is the desired absolute precision (5%). A comprehensive list of all cases during the specified period was compiled for each month. We selected 1000 cases by simple random sampling from the total cases during the specified period. Given the pandemic’s evolving nature, which includes changes in testing rates, positivity rates, and the emergence of new strains, it was critical to consider the temporal effects on the burden of cases. To address this issue, the sample size was evenly distributed across all study months, with 50 cases selected from each month, regardless of the number of reported cases. Participant selection was conducted through simple random sampling using the MS Excel random number generation command. Subsequently, selected participants were contacted via phone calls, and their consent was obtained.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The study included all confirmed COVID-19 adults above 18 years old up to 65 years of age. We excluded individuals who declined to participate or discontinue the interview, as well as participants with any missing data or communication issues, including language barriers.

Data Collection Procedure and Tool

Data collection took place between March and April 2022, conducted via telephonic interviews by eight trained medical students.

The questionnaire comprised four parts. The first part gathered sociodemographic data including; age, gender, educational level, occupation, and marital status. The second section measured severity scores based on COVID-19 clinical severity parameters as per the NIH classification guidelines [26]. It included questions related to acute illness such as the presence of symptoms, symptoms during illness, use of oxygen therapy, and hospital & ICU admission. The third part collected information about medical history including BMI, smoking status, chronic diseases, regular medications, COVID-19 vaccination history, and number of doses received.

The fourth part consisted of an assessment of fatigue and depression. Fatigue assessment initially included questions about the presence of fatigue. Followed by an Arabic Translated version of the Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS). This scale comprised nine statements where participants rated the impact of fatigue on motivation, exercise, performance, daily activity, and social life. Participants scored each item from 1 to 7 (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree), with a mean score of 4 or more was considered a cut-off indicating fatigue [27, 28]. Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2) screening was applied using a validated Arabic-translated version, and a score of 3 or more was considered positive [29, 30].

Statistical Analysis

We transferred the data from Google Sheets and analyzed it using STATA (Statistical Software for Data Science) software. We conducted a descriptive analysis to calculate frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. The mean and standard deviation for continuous variables. We used Pearson Chi-Squared and Fisher’s exact tests for the qualitative analysis. We used a t-test for the quantitative variables. The relationship between post-COVID-19 fatigue and other variables was assessed using logistic regression. A P-value less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Ethical Considerations

We took verbal consent from each participant after providing a clear explanation of their role in the study. Confidentiality and privacy of data were ensured to comply with the ethical standards. The study protocol was approved by the regional research committee in Qassim province. Data collectors were instructed to avoid the collection of any personal identifiers. Participants who tested positive on the PHQ-2 screening were scheduled for follow-up with their primary care physician for psychiatric evaluation.

Results

We contacted a total of 707 participants, out of which 381 agreed to participate, resulting in a response rate of 53.9%. However, the study only included 361 participants, as 20 participants had missing data. The mean age of the participants was 37 ± 10.5 years, with females accounting for 43% of the participants. Among the study population, 9.7% had other comorbidities, while 5.5% were on chronic medications associated with fatigue. Smokers constituted 21% of the study participants, with 1.6% being past smokers. The majority of the participants (68%) were classified as having a mild infection, while 16% had moderate severity, and asymptomatic participants comprised 13.3%. Severe/critical cases accounted for approximately 2% of the remaining cases. Vaccinated participants made up 97% of the study population, while unvaccinated participants made up only 3%. Discussion

Our study revealed that about a quarter of our COVID-19 survey respondents experienced fatigue. In contrast, a national-level cross-sectional study conducted in Saudi Arabia by AlRadini et al., reported a fatigue prevalence of 11.5% based on self-reported answers [11]. However, AlOtaibi et al., in a cross-sectional single-center study conducted in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, using the Chalder Fatigue Scale, found fatigue to be prevalent in approximately 56% of their study participants. Shendy et al., studied the prevalence of fatigue in a single city in Egypt using the Modified Fatigue Impact Scale (MFIS), reporting fatigue in approximately 64% of the study sample [10, 31]. Additionally, Townsend et al., explored the prevalence of fatigue among recovered COVID-19 patients in a single center using the Chalder Fatigue Scale (CFQ-11). Fatigue was present in 52% of their study population. Similarly, Stavem et al. in a cross-sectional study conducted in Norway using the Chalder Fatigue Scale (CFQ-11) found that 46% of their participants experienced fatigue [8, 32]. The variance in prevalence between our study and the previous research may be attributed to differences in the study settings, the tools used to assess fatigue, the stage of the pandemic, and variations in SARS-CoV-2 strains. Additionally, differences in the season in which the data was collected can also affect the perceived fatigue. An important observation from our study is the significant difference in prevalence measured by self-reports compared to FSS. This suggests a potential overestimation of fatigue. Hence, it is imperative for clinicians to utilize validated tools to accurately evaluate the patient’s conditions and plan for management. This also opens the door for further research in this area to determine the validity of various fatigue assessment methods.

Positive PHQ-2 was identified as a strong predictor associated with the occurrence of post-COVID fatigue in our study. It’s worth noting that we utilized only positive PHQ-2 as a variable, which is not diagnostic for depression. This finding aligns with similar studies [33–35]. While fatigue and depression coexist in long COVID-19 syndrome, their interplay may stem from common mechanisms or independent causes. Fatigue could result from physical exhaustion due to the infection, potentially leading to mental stress and subsequent depression. Conversely, neurological and immunological factors, along with fears related to the infection and disruptions in daily life, may contribute to mental health issues that manifest as physical fatigue. Regardless of the underlying mechanisms, it’s crucial to address both the physical and mental well-being of patients with long COVID-19 infection to facilitate a prompt and smooth recovery.

In our study, COVID-19 severity emerged as a predictor of fatigue, consistent with findings from several other studies [36–38]. However, another set of studies reported no association between severe acute illness with post-COVID fatigue [8, 39]. It might be explained by different clinical severity classification systems used and the study settings. To our knowledge, no studies have studied the direct association between them. A study from the UK reported that the COVID-19 vaccine reduced the severity and duration of COVID-19 symptoms [40]. However, this study did not include a comparison group which makes it difficult to infer whether vaccine reduces the risk of post-COVID-19 fatigue. Thus, further studies are recommended to gain more knowledge about the relationship.

We used validated tools for outcome measures and our sample fairly represents the population of Saudi Arabia. For instance, the mean age in our sample (adults < 18 years) was 37 years while that for the Saudi general population is 29 years which includes a population less than 18 years as well. Similarly, the distribution by age categories is also close to that of the general population [41]. Males represented 57% of our sample, which is close to the general population (61%) in Saudi Arabia [41]. However, our study has some limitations that need to be considered while interpreting the results. First, it was a cross-sectional study conducted at a single time point precluding the establishment of causality. Second, we used PHQ-2 for screening, instead of the PHQ-9 questionnaire to confirm the diagnosis of depression or screen for other anxiety disorders among our participants, despite literature suggesting an association of post-COVID fatigue with psychological disorders [8, 42]. Third, data was not available about the time between the diagnosis of COVID-19, recovery, and the time of fatigue onset, which may introduce bias in the reported prevalence. Lastly, while a priori sample size was calculated for prevalence only, power calculations were not conducted for the regression analyses. Nonetheless, post-hoc power calculations indicated that with the observed frequencies and exposure distribution, our sample had achieved 83.9% power.

Conclusion

Fatigue remains prevalent among COVID-19 survivors in Saudi Arabia, with severe acute COVID-19 infection and depression identified as predictors of post-COVID fatigue. Further investigations are welcomed to explore the psychological factors associated with fatigue in COVID-19 patients. Given the coexistence of fatigue and depression, it is imperative to schedule follow-up visits and conduct clinical evaluations to assess the presence and chronicity of these conditions, ensuring appropriate management strategies are implemented.
