Combination of chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors in non-small cell lung cancer with actionable gene alterations other than EGFR, ALK, and ROS1 mutations: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Ji Eun Shin, Sehhoon Park, Hyun Ae Jung, Jong-Mu Sun, Se-Hoon Lee, Jin Seok Ahn, Myung-Ju Ahn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiscoveries of actionable gene alterations (AGAs) have driven significant advances in targeted therapies, enhancing survival for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients. In cases of AGAs other than common EGFR, ALK, and ROS1 mutations, target therapies have various strengths of the recommendation depending on their previous studies. Meanwhile, the immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) combined with chemotherapy is still widely used as a first-line treatment for most of NSCLC with AGAs due to limited accessibility of target therapy.MethodsThis study included patients with NSCLC harboring AGAs who received chemotherapy plus ICIs (CT + IO) or chemotherapy alone (CT) as a first-line treatment between January 2019 and May 2023 at Samsung Medical Center. We assessed the objective response rate (ORR), progression-free survival (PFS), time to next treatment (TTNT), and overall survival (OS) between treatment groups. Subgroup analyses were conducted in the CT + IO group according to the type of AGAs and PD-L1 expression.ResultsIn total, 163 NSCLC patients with AGAs other than common EGFR, ALK, and ROS1 were included, with a median age of 62.4 years. 57 (35.0%) patients received CT + IO and 106 (65.0%) received CT. The proportions of patients with EGFR exon 20 insertion (E20I), HER2 mutation (mHER2), RET fusion (RET), and MET exon 14 skipping (METex14) were 28.8% (n = 47), 39.9% (n = 65), 16.6% (n = 27), and 14.7% (n = 24), respectively. With 32.0 months of median follow-up duration (range: 28.5–37.0), median PFS was 8.0 months (95% CI, 6.0–12.8) in CT + IO and 6.4 months (95% CI, 5.5–8.0) in CT (HR: 0.71, 95% CI, 0.49–1.03). In CT + IO group, median PFS by AGAs were 5.0 months (95% CI, 3.2–NA), 8.3 months (95% CI, 6.0–NA), 5.8 months (95% CI, 4.5–NA), and 17.1 months (95% CI, 10.7–NA) for E20I, mHER2, RET and METex14 respectively. Among these, 44.4% of METex14 patients had a PD-L1 TPS of 50% or higher. 24-month OS rate according to PD-L1 expression were 45.4%, 56.3%, and 81.5% in PD-L1 TPS < 1%, 1 to 49%, and ≥ 50%.ConclusionsThe combination of chemotherapy with ICIs in NSCLC patients with AGAs other than common EGFR, ALK, and ROS1 mutations demonstrated similar clinical outcomes compared to conventional chemotherapy. Patients with METex14 were associated with higher response rate and longer PFS among patients with AGAs when treated with chemotherapy plus ICIs. Also, the efficacy of the chemotherapy plus ICIs is positively correlate to the PD-L1 expression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14834-1.

Background

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related death, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) constituting nearly 85% of newly diagnosed cases [1, 2]. There have been significant improvements in the treatment of metastatic NSCLC with development of targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), leading to 5-year survival rates range from 15 to 62.5%, depending on biomarkers [3, 4].

Therefore, identification of actionable gene alterations (AGAs) is crucial for treatment decision in advanced NSCLC. These include anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) rearrangements, BRAF p.V600E point (BRAF) mutations, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) (also known as ERBB2) mutations, Kirsten Rat Sarcoma virus (KRAS) mutations, mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor exon 14 (METex14) skipping mutations, neurotrophic tyrosine receptor kinase 1/2/3 (NTRK1/2/3) gene fusions, rearranged during transfection (RET) rearrangements, and ROS proto-oncogene 1 (ROS1) gene rearrangements. Currently, NCCN guideline recommend abovementioned biomarkers to be assessed for patients with metastatic nonsquamous NSCLC [5, 6].

Although EGFR, ALK, or ROS1 tyrosine kinase inhibitors are widely used as a first-line treatment for NSCLC patients with common EGFR (exon 19 deletions or exon 21 L858R mutations), ALK, or ROS1 mutation, the use of targeted agents for other AGAs are still limited due to reimbursement issue or approval only for second or further line of therapy.

In metastatic adenocarcinoma of lung without AGAs, pemetrexed/platinum doublet combined with ICI, pembrolizumab or pembrolizumab monotherapy based on PD-L1 expression are considered as a standard of care as first-line treatment. In Keynote 189, the combination of pembrolizumab plus pemetrexed/platinum significantly improved not only progression-free survival (PFS) but overall survival (OS) compared to pemetrexed/platinum (PFS, 8.8 months vs. 4.0 months, HR: 0.52, 95% CI, 0.43–0.64; OS, 22.0 months vs. 10.6 months, HR: 0.56, 95% CI, 0.46–0.69) [3, 7]. In Keynote 189, patients with common EGFR or ALK mutations were excluded, therefore, the results of patients with other AGAs was not assessed. In this context, platinum doublets with or without ICI are still most commonly used for patients with AGAs excluding common EGFR or ALK mutation.

It has been known that patients with AGAs are associated with poor response to ICI monotherapy. In the Immunotarget registry, clinical activity was lower in most group except the KRAS mutation group, which derived the most benefit with a median PFS of 3.2 months on ICI monotherapy (95% CI [2.7; 4.5]) [8, 9]. But given the lack of randomized trial comparing chemotherapy alone to chemotherapy plus ICIs, the role of ICIs combining with platinum doublets in patients with AGAs is controversial.

This study is a retrospective analysis to assess the clinical outcomes of platinum doublets with ICIs compared to platinum doublets alone as a first-line treatment in NSCLC patients with AGAs other than common EGFR, ALK, and ROS1 mutations.

Methods

Study participants and data collection

We conducted a retrospective analysis on patients diagnosed as NSCLC with AGAs at the Samsung Medical Center from January 2019 to May 2023. All patients had histologically confirmed NSCLC, were identified as harboring AGAs including EGFR exon 20 insertion (E20I), HER2 mutation (mHER2), RET fusion (RET), or MET exon 14 skipping (METex14), and received either platinum doublets or platinum doublets with ICIs as a first-line therapy. Patients with KRAS and BRAF mutations were excluded due to their small numbers. Patients who received single ICIs, TKI, or anti- vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibitors (e.g., bevacizumab) were excluded.

We reviewed the electronic clinical records and extracted data on age, sex, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, smoking status, type of AGAs, previous history of treatment, histopathology, PD-L1 expression, and distant metastasis at initiation of treatment.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Samsung Medical Center with a waiver for informed consent (Institutional Review Board no. 2024-02-123).

Identification of AGAs and PD-L1

AGAs included in this study were as follows: EGFR exon 20 insertion (E20I); HER2 mutation (mHER); RET fusion (RET); and MET exon 14 skipping (METex14). AGAs were detected using specialized diagnostic methods for each genetic alteration, such as next-generation sequencing (NGS) or polymerase chain reaction (PCR). NGS used in this study comprises CancerSCAN panel (GENINUS, Seoul, Republic of Korea), TruSight Oncology 500 assay (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), Oncomine Focus Assay (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA), Guardant 360 (Guardant Health, Palo Alto, CA, USA), and FoundationOne (FOUNDATION MEDICINE, Cambridge, MA, USA).

E20I were also identified using a PNAclamp kit (Panagene, Daejeon, Republic of Korea) or GenesWell ddEGFR Mutation Test (Gencurix, Seoul, Republic of Korea). We excluded patients detected as positive E20I through the Cobas EGFR Mutation Test version 2 (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, Indiana, USA) because of its high incidence of false positives for E20I.

PD-L1 expression levels were assessed using anti-PD-L1 antibodies (22C3 and SP263), and categorized into TPS < 1%, 1 to 49%, and ≥ 50%.

Outcomes

Clinical outcomes were evaluated for objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), progression-free survival (PFS), time to next treatment (TTNT), and overall survival (OS). Tumor response was evaluated as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD), according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumor, version 1.1. ORR was defined as the percentage (%) of patients with confirmed CR or PR. PFS was measured from the start of the treatment to the date of disease progression or death from any cause using RECIST 1.1. TTNT was measured from the date of initiation of each treatment until the date of initiation of the next line of therapy or death. OS was calculated from the start of the treatment to the date of death from any cause.

Statistical analysis

The Categorical and continuous variables were summarized using descriptive statistics. The survival analysis was performed using Kaplan-Meier curves and compared using a log-rank test. All P values were two-sided and confidence intervals (CI) were at the 95% level, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27 and R version 4.3.0. The data cutoff date was September 11, 2023.

Results

Study population and clinical characteristics

A total of 242 NSCLC patients with AGAs other than common EGFR, ALK, or ROS1 were included in this study (Fig. 1). 8 patients with co-mutations of EGFR, ALK, or ROS1, 1 patient with double primary cancer, 1 patients who were diagnosed as neuroendocrine carcinomas were excluded due to the possibility of false positives. Also, 19 patients with E20I detected by the Cobas EGFR mutation test version 2 were excluded. Among 213 patients, 1 patient who did not receive anticancer treatment, 20 who received single ICIs, 14 who received TKI, and 15 who received other treatments were also excluded. 163 patients were divided into two treatment groups; platinum doublets plus ICIs group (CT + IO), and platinum doublets alone group (CT).

Flow diagram of study population

Table 1 presents baseline characteristics of the study population. Of 163 patients, with a median age of 62.4 years, 54.6% were female and 62.0% were never-smokers. Most patients had an ECOG performance status of 0–1 (98.2%) and adenocarcinoma histology (93.9%). At the time of treatment, the proportions of patients with initial brain, liver, and bone metastases were 40.5%, 17.8%, and 51.5%, respectively. The composition of AGAs was as follows: E20I (n = 47, 28.8%), mHER2 (n = 65, 39.9%), RET (n = 27, 16.6%), METex14 (n = 24, 14.7%). Overall, 57 patients received CT + IO and 106 received CT. Compared to CT + IO, CT comprised a higher number of patients with brain metastases (26.3% vs. 48.1%). Other clinical characteristics were balanced between two groups.

Anticancer therapy regimens in treatment groups are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. In CT + IO, all patients received a combination of ICIs with pemetrexed or paclitaxel plus platinum-based drug. And among 57 patients, 44 (77.2%) received pemetrexed and carboplatin with pembrolizumab. In CT, 72 (67.9%) out of 106 patients received a combination of pemetrexed and cisplatin.

Efficacy of CT + IO in NSCLC with AGAs

The ORR was 47.4% in CT + IO, and 52.8% in CT. DCR were 93.0% and 84.0% in CT + IO and CT, respectively (Table 2). With 32.0 months of median follow-up duration (range: 28.5–37.0), median PFS was 8.0 months (95% CI, 6.0–12.8) in CT + IO and 6.4 months (95% CI, 5.5–8.0) in CT (HR: 0.71, 95% CI, 0.49–1.03) (Fig. 2A). The median TTNT was 8.8 months (95% CI, 6.9–15.4) and 7.1 months (95% CI, 6.0–8.4), respectively (HR: 0.70, 95% CI, 0.48–1.03) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Median OS was 34.1 months (95% CI, 23.7–NE) in CT + IO and 30.0 months (95% CI, 22.7–39.3) in CT (HR: 0.65, 95% CI, 0.37–1.16) (Fig. 2B). There were no statistically significant differences between two groups in PFS (p = 0.068), TTNT (p = 0.065) and OS (p = 0.14), even though there was a tendency toward superiority of TTNT or PFS in CT + IO group.

Efficacy of CT + IO by AGAs in NSCLC with AGAs

For CT + IO group, we compared clinical outcomes based on the type of AGAs. Baseline characteristics by AGAs are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. Notably, high proportion of patients in METex14 group had high PD-L1 expression compared with other groups, which 4 (44.4%) patients had PD-L1 TPS of 50% or more, and 3 of them exhibiting PD-L1 expression levels 90% or higher.

The ORR was 35.7% for E20I, 35.0% for mHER2, and 50.0% for RET, respectively, whereas 88.9% for METex14 (Table 3). Median PFS were 5.0 months (95% CI, 3.2–NA), 8.3 months (95% CI, 6.0–NA), and 5.8 months (95% CI, 4.5–NA) for E20I, mHER2, and RET, respectively. In contrast, median PFS of METex14 was 17.1 months (95% CI, 10.7–NA) (Fig. 3A). Median TTNT were 6.1 months (95% CI, 5.0–NA), 8.9 months (95% CI, 7.5–NA), and 5.8 months (95% CI, 4.7–NA) for E20I, mHER2, and RET, whereas 18.0 months (95% CI, 18.0–NA) for METex14 group (Supplementary Fig. 2 A). Median OS were 34.1 months (95% CI, 9.3–NA) and 21.6 months (95% CI, 18.9–NA) for E20I and mHER2 group, and not reached for RET and METex14. 24-month OS rate were 68.1%, 37.1%, 66.9% and 100.0% respectively (Fig. 3B).

Efficacy of CT + IO according to PD-L1 TPS in NSCLC with AGAs

We compared clinical outcomes according to PD-L1 expression in CT + IO group. PD-L1 tests were conducted on 54 out of 57 patients. 48 patients (90.6%) used 22C3 antibody and 5 (9.4%) used SP263 antibody. There were 28 patients (51.9%) with PD-L1 TPS < 1%, 14 patients (25.9%) with 1 to 49%, and 12 patients (22.2%) with ≥ 50%.

Median PFS were 6.0 months (95% CI, 5.4–NA) for PD-L1 TPS < 1%, 10.7 months (95% CI, 6.5–NA) for 1 to 49%, 8.2 months (95% CI, 65.5–NA) for ≥ 50% (Fig. 4A). Median TTNT were 6.0 months (95% CI, 5.4–NA), 10.7 months (95% CI, 6.5–NA), and 8.2 months (95% CI, 5.5–NA) respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2B). The median OS was 23.7 months (95% CI, 18.9–NA) for PD-L1 TPS < 1%, and not reached for PD-L1 1 to 49% and ≥ 50%. The 24-month OS rate were 45.4%, 56.3%, and 81.5% in PD-L1 TPS < 1%, 1 to 49%, and ≥ 50%, respectively (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

In this study, platinum doublets with ICIs in NSCLC with AGAs other than EGFR, ALK, and ROS1 mutations shows similar clinical outcomes compared with platinum doublets alone. The ORR was 47.4% in CT + IO vs. 52.8% in CT and median PFS was 8.0 months for CT + IO vs. 6.4 months for CT (HR: 0.71, 95% CI, 0.49–1.03). It is of note that based on the type of AGAs, the median PFS of CT + IO was 5.0–8.3 months for E20I, mHER2, and RET, whereas 17.1 months for METex14. Assessing the outcome of CT + IO based on PD-L1 expression, 24-month OS rate according to PD-L1 expression were 45.4%, 56.3%, and 81.5% in PD-L1 TPS < 1%, 1 to 49%, and ≥ 50%.

Numerous retrospective studies have reported that NSCLC with AGAs is relatively resistant to immunotherapy. In Immunotarget registry, median PFS was reported 2.8 months in entire cohort, with OS 13.3 months, and the best response rate 19% for patients who received ICI monotherapy [8]. Tumors with oncogene addiction are known to be associated with low TMB, limited CD8 + T cell and NK cell infiltration, a restricted TCR repertoire, and an abundance of regulatory T cells. These factors create an immunosuppressive TME, which interfere anti-tumor effect of ICIs. Additionally, oncogenic signaling pathways can lead to T cell exclusion, induction of regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, dysfunctional IFNγ signaling, and increased PD-L1 expression [10]​.

In particular, for EGFR-mutant NSCLC, both CheckMate 722 and Keynote 789 investigated the efficacy of chemo-immunotherapy after TKI failure, but neither study demonstrated a survival benefit. (CheckMate 722, PFS 5.6 months vs. 5.4 months, HR 0.75, 95% CI, 0.56–1.00; Keynote 789, PFS 5.6 months vs. 5.5 months, HR 0.80, 95% CI, 0.65–0.97) [11, 12]. The results of our study on NSCLC patients with AGAs were also not significantly different from the previously known survival results of Keynote 189 (median PFS 8.8 months, 95% CI, 7.6–9.2) [3].

Additionally, previous studies have shown that the sequential use of PD-(L)1 blockade and osimertinib is associated with severe immune-related adverse events (irAEs), particularly pneumonitis and colitis [13, 14]. In previous study, 15% of patients treated with sequential PD-(L)1 blockade followed later by osimertinib developed a severe irAE [13, 14]. As a result, the use of chemo-immunotherapy followed by sequential treatment with TKIs in NSCLC patients with AGAs requires careful consideration due to the risk of adverse events.

The use of a platinum doublets with ICIs as a first-line treatment for NSCLC with AGAs remains controversial. In the Keynote 189, which established platinum doublets with pembrolizumab as a standard treatment, patients with EGFR and ALK mutations were excluded. Prospective studies evaluating the efficacy of chemo-immunotherapy in NSCLC with AGAs are rare, and retrospective study results show only modest clinical benefits, with a median PFS of 5.53 months (95% CI, 4.73–6.97) [15]. Also, there are only few retrospective studies that examine the outcomes of chemo-immunotherapy according to each AGA. For patients with E20I, which is known to be ICI-resistant [16], the addition of platinum-based chemotherapy to ICIs reported an ORR of 18.8% with median PFS and OS of 4.5 and 11.3 months, respectively [17]. In the case of RET, phase 3 studies have shown that TKIs are more beneficial than chemo-immunotherapy [18]. For mHER2, a retrospective study for chemo-immunotherapy reported an ORR of 28.9% and a median PFS of 5.2 month which was not beneficial than chemotherapy alone [19, 20]. Therefore, there appears to be no rationale for using a combination of chemo-IO in patients with AGAs.

In this study, patients with METex14 demonstrated a notably prolonged median PFS of 17.1 months with chemo-immunotherapy, prompting questions regarding the efficacy of ICIs in this molecular subset. This finding is consistent with prior retrospective studies in which NSCLC patients harboring METex14 alterations exhibited favorable responses to ICIs, with reported PFS ranging from 18 to 49 months [21]. The extended PFS observed in our METex14 cohort may be partially attributed to the high prevalence of PD-L1 expression, as 54.2% of patients exhibited a TPS ≥ 50%, which is consistent with previous findings indicating that a substantial proportion of MET exon 14-altered NSCLCs exhibit high PD-L1 expression [22].

Nevertheless, other retrospective analyses have shown only modest clinical benefit from ICIs in this subgroup, suggesting that these findings should be interpreted with caution. For example, Sabari et al. reported an objective response rate (ORR) of 17% and a median PFS of just 1.9 months, even among tumors with PD-L1 TPS ≥ 50%, which comprised 41% of their cohort [22]. Similarly, Uehara et al. reported a median PFS of 2.8 months in patients receiving first-line ICI-based therapy [23]. These results underscore the distinct tumor biology of METex14-driven cancers, which are often characterized by low tumor mutational burden (TMB), non-inflamed tumor microenvironments, and reduced infiltration of CD8 + T cells—features that may limit responsiveness to ICIs despite elevated PD-L1 expression [24]. Therefore, observed prolongation in PFS among METex14-altered patients receiving chemo-immunotherapy is more attributable to the synergetic effect of cytotoxic chemotherapy or favorable clinical selection factors, rather than intrinsic sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade conferred by the METex14 alteration itself. This warrants further investigation through preclinical studies or prospective clinical trials.

Overall survival appeared more favorable with higher PD-L1 expression, but without statistical significance. However, this trend was not supported by the observed PFS and TTNT outcomes. This is consistent with the results of the Keynote 189, where the efficacy of combining chemotherapy and ICIs was stratified based on PD-L1 expression [7]. Previous study also demonstrated PD-L1 expression acting as a positive biomarker for ICIs in NSCLC with AGAs [25]. Also, in both Checkmate 722 and Keynote 589, there is a tendency to observe a correlation between PD-L1 expression and outcomes [11, 12]. Further research is needed on the role of ICI combination for NSCLC with AGAs and high PD-L1 expression.

This study has several limitations. As an observational, retrospective study, our analysis is inherently susceptible to selection bias, residual confounding, and heterogeneity in clinical decision-making. Additionally, missing data on variables such as comorbidities, post-progression treatment regimens, and longitudinal follow-up may have influenced outcome interpretation and limit the external validity of our findings. In particular, there are differences in baseline characteristics between the CT + IO and CT groups. A significantly higher incidence of brain metastases was observed in the CT group compared to the CT + IO group (48.1% vs. 26.3%, p = 0.011). This baseline imbalance may have confounded treatment outcomes, particularly given the known poor prognosis of brain involvement in NSCLC. Given the relatively short duration of follow-up, OS data was immature in subgroup analysis. Additionally, CT + IO group was relatively small, making the sample size limited for a meaningful comparison by AGAs. Data on adverse events in both arms were not collected. Also, data on targeted therapy after progression of CT or CT + IO were very limited.

Conclusions

In conclusion, Platinum doublets with ICIs in NSCLC with AGAs other than common EGFR, ALK, and ROS1 mutations demonstrated similar clinical outcomes compared to platinum doublets alone. Our study showed patients with METex14 treated with CT + IO were associated with high response and longer PFS suggesting the most favorable type of AGAs. Also, the clinical outcomes of platinum doublets with ICIs positively correlates with PD-L1 expression in patients with NSCLC with AGAs. Our results provide important clinical insights into the choice of a first-line treatment of NSCLC patients with AGAs, particularly when using platinum-based chemotherapy combined with ICIs in phase III clinical trial as a control arm.
