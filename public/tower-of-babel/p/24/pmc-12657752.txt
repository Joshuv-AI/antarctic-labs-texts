In silico investigations of albumin-GLP-1 receptor agonist complexes for diabetes drug delivery applications
Authors: Gillian Murphy, David Cheung, Michael Fitzgerald, Abhay Pandit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper describes the specific conformational folding patterns and residues involved in stabilizing the Glucagon-like Peptide-1 Receptor Agonist (GLP-1 RA) peptide-albumin protein complex using molecular dynamic (MD) simulations and surface plasmon resonance (SPR). We quantified the binding energies, thermodynamics, conformational shifts and specific residue-residue interactions of the peptide-albumin complex. The study presents molecular-level findings of the relationship between these interactions and the formation of amide bonds in a PEGylated hydrogel drug delivery system. This research outlines the potential of harnessing MD simulations as a biomaterial characterisation tool, giving deeper insights beyond the established in vitro methods. Further research in this field could lead to advancements in the rational design and development of next generation controlled release formulations and long-acting GLP-1 RA therapeutics.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists (hereafter referred to as GLP-1 RAs) are a class of anti-diabetic drugs that act by replacing the deficient GLP-1 in patients [1]. First-generation GLP-1 RAs were characterized by short lifetimes due to enzymatic degradation and renal filtration and therefore were administered as twice-daily subcutaneous injections [2]. Once daily and weekly injections were developed using fatty acid moiety substitutions and linker combinations that reversibly bind to albumin to extend peptide lifetimes further [3]. However, due to the lifespan of the disease, the issues associated with frequent injections persist. Frequent injections give rise to the development of lipodystrophy, resulting in injection site pain, reduced transdermal absorption, and poor disease management, all of which negatively impact the patient’s quality of life [4], [5]. A daily tablet co-formulated with an absorption enhancer agent was brought to market and had similar safety and tolerability to existing injectable formulations [6]. However, low bioavailability and high within-subject variability mean next-generation formulations must continue to address these challenges to improve disease management [7].

This paper explores an albumin-based hydrogel that addresses the limitations of current GLP-1 RAs by reducing the injection frequency from once daily to once monthly while maintaining the existing clinical benefits of the drug product. The proprietary system is administered subcutaneously as an aqueous solution where the in-situ crosslinking reaction is activated at body temperature and the hydrogel is formed. The GLP-1 RA cargo is bound to the albumin protein and embedded in the hydrogel matrix during the in situ curing process. The hydrogel slowly degrades over time to release the embedded cargo and is fully resorbable at the injection site. GLP-1 RA binds to albumin's fatty acid (FA) binding sites (FA1, FA2, FA3, FA4, FA5, FA6, FA7) by nonpolar interactions [8], [9]. Albumin-binding extends the peptide half-life by sterically shielding the peptide from enzymatic degradation and protecting the peptide from renal filtration attributed to the large size of the protein [10]. Hydrophilic spacers between the lipid and the peptide also increase FA binding affinity to albumin and peptide oligomerisation[10]. Oligomerization also protects against catabolism in the subcutaneous (SC) tissue by increasing the tertiary structure and concealing cleavable linkers, extending the peptide half-life and therapeutic window [11].

The binding and release of GLP-1 RA and albumin has been subject to several in vitro studies; at a clinically relevant GLP-1 RA concentration (104 pM), a GLP-1 RA was shown to have an in vitro plasma protein binding affinity greater than 98.9 % [12]. In vitro GLP-1 RA-albumin dissociation constant (Kd) values were reported in the literature for both glycated and non-glycated albumin [13]. Glycated albumin following glucose incubation in suprapathological conditions showed a 7-fold higher Kd of 240 ± 10 μm compared to the non-glycated control 35 ± 8 μm [13]. Albumin from non-diabetic subjects displayed a GLP-1 RA Kd of 48 ± 13 μm, like the above in vitro value 48 ± 2 μm obtained from moderately uncontrolled diabetic patient samples [13]. Diabetic patients above 11 % glycated haemoglobin display a significantly higher Kd value of 224 ± 60 μm. However, most critically, the exact binding sites involved could not be identified by these techniques.

In silico molecular modelling approaches are an established tool in drug discovery, with literature offering many success stories of active compounds brought to market [14]. This molecular dynamics (MD) study reports the specific albumin-GLP-1 RA complexes and their potential application in a biomaterial drug delivery system. The findings in this study provide a molecular-level description of the specific moieties and conformational changes involved in peptide binding to albumin and how they may relate to peptide loading and polyethylene (PEG) crosslinking in an albumin hydrogel (Fig 1). This knowledge could be transferable to other lipidated peptides and may enable a more rational design of lipidated albumin-binding biomaterial systems in the future. In comparison, we believe the in-depth capabilities of in silico modelling may offer a useful tool in the rational design of biomaterial devices. The systems considered were a proprietary glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA), Human Serum Albumin (HSA) and HSA-GLP-1 RA complex. The initial HSA structure was taken from the protein database (entries 4K2C) respectively. The first three residues (Asp-Ala-His) were absent from the initial structure for HSA; these were added using the PSFGen tool in Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD) software package, version 1.9.3. These structures were then solvated, and K+ and Cl- counter-ions added to neutralize the system to give a physiological ionic strength of ∼150 mM. Structures for the HSA-GLP-1 RA complex were created starting from the experimental structure of the HSA-Palmitoyl complex (PDB:1E7H). Missing residues at the N- and C- termini were also added using the PSFGen tool. To add GLP-1 RA into each fatty acid (FA) binding site (FA1-FA7) of the protein structure, the palmitoyl molecules were removed from all binding sites; where necessary, the palmitoyl groups were reconstructed in the remaining FA binding sites unbound by the GLP-1 RA peptide. The remainder of the GLP-1 RA molecule was placed outside the binding site by increasing the length of the C-N bond joining the Palmitoyl chain and Glutamine (Glu) residue. This complex is then solvated, and counter-ions added as previously done. To generate the initial structure of the complex, a series of energy minimizations was performed by reducing the length of the C-N bond until the equilibrium bond length is reached. Each system was energy minimized and short simulations (10 ps) were performed in both the constant-volume, constant-temperature (NVT) ensemble, and the constant-pressure, constant-temperature (NPT) ensemble prior to the full equilibration and production simulations. Simulations were run with and without position restraints on the protein.

Simulations of each system were performed in the NPT ensemble, with a temperature of 310 K and pressure of 1 standard atmosphere (atm). The system was modelled using the CHARMM36m force field with the modified TIP3P water model. Van der Waals interactions were truncated at 12 A. Electrostatic interactions were determined using a PME sum, with a real space cut-off of X, and X wavevectors in the X, Y, Z directions. The simulations were performed in the NPT ensemble at temperature 310 K and 1 atm of pressure. Temperature was controlled using a velocity-rescale thermostat and the Paranello-Rahman barostat was used to control the pressure, with relaxation times of 0.1 ps and 1 ps respectively. Bonds involving hydrogen atoms where constrained using the LINCS algorithm and the geometry of water molecules was constrained using the SETTLE algorithm. A time step of 2 fs was used for integrating the equations of motion. All simulations were performed using the Gromacs MD package, version 2018.3. Analysis was performed using in built Gromacs tools and in-house Python scripts written using MDAnalysis Python library. Solvent accessible surface area (SASA) calculations were performed using the gromacs sasa tool. Secondary structure was determined using the STRIDE algorithm. The binding free energy was found using MM-PBSA calculations.

SPR data also confirmed the highest association constant of GLP-1 RA (Ka1 = 2.75 ×105) compared to both the GLP-1 negative control (Ka1 = 3.64 ×103) and palmitoyl control (Ka1 = 2.74 ×104), as seen in Fig. 2d. As GLP-1 RA has a higher binding association compared to palmitoyl alone and displays a similar profile to palmitoyl docking (0 sec) and GLP-1 control (90–200 sec), this suggests a possible two-state binding mechanism of the GLP-1 RA-albumin complex structure (Fig. 2c) [19]. The two-state model describes an initial rapid association of the ligand (GLP-1 RA) with albumin, followed by a slower conformational stabilization step that increases overall affinity. This mechanism has been previously reported for other albumin-binding molecules and fatty acid conjugates [20], [21]. Palmitoyl also has the strongest dissociation constant kd1 of 7.92 × 10−4 and induces a dissociation response curve below baseline (Fig. 2c). Due to the small size of palmitic acid (256.42 g/mol) and being tightly bound within the protein, dissociation may be less likely to occur resulting in a response curve below baseline [16].

To get more insight into the interactions between GLP-1 RA and HSA, we can consider the specific residue-residue (R-R) contacts formed between the molecules, the mode of interaction, the number and the relative probability of each interaction occurring (Fig. 3). As the earlier ∆Gbind analysis expected, GLP-1 RA is bound to the highest binding affinity sites (sites 1, 3 and 5) with the highest number of R-R contacts. In contrast, fewer contacts are found for the lower affinity sites (sites 2, 4, 6 and 7). In our analysis of the probability of R-R interactions, we examined residues pairs that were in contact over 70 % of the simulation length. At the highest binding affinity sites, a range of modes of interaction are found between GLP-1 RA peptide and HSA. Sites 1 and 5 have many contacts between hydrophobic and polar residues; this may arise due to hydrophobic interactions with the non-polar parts of the side chains or through hydrogen bonding with the backbone of the hydrophobic residues. Hereon after, we refer to these interactions between hydrophobic and polar residues as non-specific interactions. The contacts for site 3 are primarily between hydrophobic residues, suggesting a hydrophobic effect is the primary driving force for this binding site. The main interactions observed in the lower affinity binding sites 2 and 4 are mediated by non-specific interactions and to a lesser extent, polar interactions. Few hydrophobic interactions are observed overall; site 4 and site 6 only have 1 hydrophobic interaction each, and sites 2 and 7 have no hydrophobic interactions. Significantly for site 6, as shown above, the palmitoyl group leaves the binding site whereas it remains bound for site 7. Much of the large difference in the binding affinity for these two sites (∼34 kcal mol-1) may be attributed to the absence of the fatty acid chain from the binding site. Contact maps for sites 2, 4, 6 and 7 display more conserved regions of binding interaction, and a lower probability as indicated by the colour gradient. The residue-residue contact maps can also show information on the specific interactions and probability (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 2).

In addition, we extrapolated the hydrophobic interaction energies of the residues to transfer free energy (ΔGtr) using empirical data of nonpolar solvent cyclohexane to water, corrected for size [22]. The use of ΔGtr in this study provides an empirical means of estimating the hydrophobic contribution to peptide–protein binding. ΔGtr represents the free energy change associated with transferring nonpolar surface area from an aqueous environment into a hydrophobic binding site. While not a direct measure of the total binding free energy, it serves as a valuable semi-quantitative descriptor of hydrophobic stabilization—particularly relevant for lipidated peptides such as GLP-1 RA, where the palmitoyl chain partitions into albumin’s fatty acid–binding cavity.

This empirical approach has been successfully applied in previous studies examining the energetics of protein–ligand and peptide–albumin interactions [23], [24]. In our context, ΔGtr helps rationalize how hydrophobic anchoring complements electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding interactions to stabilize the GLP-1 RA–albumin complex. The structure of the complex also varies depending on the binding site. Changes to the structure of HSA and GLP-1 RA during complex formation can be gleaned by comparing their Rg and those for HSA and GLP-1 RA in solution. Shown in Fig. 4b is radius of gyration (Rg) for the complex for each binding site, along with Rg for HSA and GLP-1 RA units. Most binding sites Rg for the complex are similar (∼27.5 Å). Only sites 2 and 6 differ significantly from this, having larger values of Rg (28.2 A and 28.7 A respectively). This is consistent with the GLP-1 RA having fewer contacts with the protein, resulting in a more extended structure.

We also examined changes in protein secondary structure (Supplementary Table 4). For HSA the alpha-helical content is slightly larger for the complex than for HSA on its own, with a similar decrease in the amount of coil (disordered) regions. More variation is seen in the secondary structure composition for GLP-1 RA. Typically the number of alpha-helix decreases compared to GLP-1 RA in solution, but with a wide variation (ranging from 0.22 to 0.52). Only for site 3 is the proportion of alpha-helix higher than for GLP-1 RA in solution. Typically, the proportion of beta turn also increases for the complex, while the amount of coil decreases. This suggests that complex formation increases the amount of ordered secondary structure. In common with the Rg the secondary structure for GLP-1 RA in site 6 is the most similar to that in solution.

MM-PBSA simulations yielded a comparable ranking order of total interaction energies to ∆Gbind for the HSA-GLP-1 RA complex. As shown in Fig. 4d, sites 4 and 6 had the lowest interaction energies which correlated with our ∆Gbind study. Sites 1 and 6 also had the lowest number of hydrogen bonds formed in the complex structure. Site 5 had the second strongest interaction energies and number of hydrogen bonds formed, correlated with ∆Gbind data.

To evaluate the effect of the peptide on hydrogel crosslinking, Solvent Accessible Surface Area (SASA) of the lysine residues was calculated; SASA is a key feature for determining protein folding patterns and stability [25], [26], [27]. In this study, we investigated any change in SASA of peptide-bound albumin protein compared to the unbound state. Any changes of SASA to albumin lysine residues may indicate a modulation of hydrogel crosslinking efficiency, as accessibility to the lysine residues are pertinent to the NHS-ester crosslinking reaction between albumin amine residues and esterated-PEG. Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 5 details the specific changes observed whereby a negative value indicates a reduced capacity for PEG crosslinking, and a positive value indicates an improved capacity for PEG crosslinking. The value of change (nm2) is quantified as any value larger than twice the standard deviation. Overall, peptide binding to albumin reduces SASA to albumin lysine residues, with only 9/41 lysine residues resulting in an increased PEG crosslinking capacity. The expected outcome of the SASA results is a reduced crosslinking efficiency of our albumin hydrogel delivery system. A confirmatory TNBS assay was performed to evaluate hydrogel crosslinking due to relative PEG concentration and peptide loading by quantifying the % free amino groups. As observed in Fig. 5d, the peptide loaded samples have a higher % of free amino acid protein residues when compared to the unloaded hydrogels. This indicates peptide loading reduces the NHS-ester crosslinking reaction between esterated-PEG and albumin amine residues. In this work, we have used MM-PBSA simulations to study an albumin-GLP-1 RA complex as a proxy for hydrogel drug loading and binding mechanism and to understand the potential effect of crosslinking using SASA. We corroborated our in silico findings with literature and in vitro studies. All FA sites exhibited hydrogen bond formation, and hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions, excluding the FA6 which interacts with albumin only by electrostatic interactions. We determined the Gbind and thermodynamics of the complex, as well as conformational shifts and specific residue-residue contact points involved. Furthermore, SASA demonstrates the blocked albumin lysine residues due to peptide binding and subsequent PEG blockage. This mechanistically may demonstrate why lower crosslinking efficiency was observed in our in vitro TNBS studies. Future specific modeling of PEG-albumin reactivity may bring further valuable insights regarding the exact mechanistic bridge from MD results to experimental hydrogel behavior. While albumin-peptide binding has been the topic of other studies, there is yet no consensus on peptide binding mechanisms within complex biomaterial drug delivery systems. Deeper understanding of peptide-protein affinities by addressing the impact of known interfering molecules could lead to optimized pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics (PK/PD), enhancing the efficacy and safety of peptide-based drugs. One such area is the known glycation of albumin in diabetic conditions which warrants further investigation in this context [28]. In this paper, we have demonstrated a deeper understanding of the specific residues and mode of interactions that can be modelled using MM-PBSA and SASA tools, applied in the context of a GLP-1 RA-albumin hydrogel drug delivery system. Such tools may also be used to guide the optimization of linker chemistries or hydrophilic spacer design in future GLP-1 RA analogs and carrier systemss. Future research will expand on these potential applications of in silico methodologies, including their use in biomaterial characterization and rational drug design.
