Microbial dysbiosis in oral cavity determines obesity status in adolescents
Authors: Md. Zubbair Malik, Rasheeba Nizam, Sindhu Jacob, Hend Al Alqaderi, Fahd Al-Mulla, Hend Alqaderi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The prevalence of obesity is rapidly increasing among adolescents in Kuwait. The ecological and dynamic changes within the oral microbiota during this developmental stage remain elusive. This study aimed to investigate the impact of body mass index (BMI) on salivary microbiome diversity and composition in Kuwaiti adolescents by utilizing next-generation sequencing technologies. DNA was extracted from saliva samples of 62 Kuwaiti adolescents enrolled in the nationwide Kuwait Healthy Lifestyle Study, categorized as underweight, normal weight, overweight, and obese based on their BMI percentiles. The 16 S metagenomic profiling was performed to identify the key oral lineages and genera associated with obesity through comprehensive analysis involving taxonomic composition, co-occurrence networks, and key metabolic profiles. Our study reveals an inverse relationship between oral bacterial diversity and obesity status in Kuwaiti adolescents. The obese and overweight groups showed comparatively low microbial taxa compared to those of normal weight. We identified three potential microbial biomarkers linked to obesity and overweight: Prevotella melaninogenica, Veillonella dispar, and Veillonella parvula. The abundance of Neisseria subflava and Rothia mucilaginosa in normal weight adolescents indicates their role in weight homeostasis. In- silico analysis of differentially expressed microbiota revealed increased activity of major metabolic enzymes such as glucose- 6- phosphate dehydrogenase, pyruvate oxidase, and glycogen phosphorylase, along with oxidative stress- related enzymes including superoxide reductase and glutathione peroxidase in obese and over-weight adolescents. Conversely, normal weight adolescents exhibited heightened activity of pyruvate synthase and tRNA- methyltransferase, which are linked to antioxidative pathways and balanced energy metabolism. Our study highlights taxonomic and functional shifts in the oral microbiota of Kuwaiti adolescents across varying BMI categories, signifying key microbial markers that could pave the way for future research focused on microbiome- targeted interventions in obesity management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05889-1.

Introduction

The epidemic of obesity has been challenging in both developed and developing countries, leading to diverse long-term health complications, including dyslipidemia, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and several types of cancer [1, 2]. The outbreak of obesity has been primarily linked to sedentary lifestyle, reduced physical activities, and improved socioeconomic status; though, other predominant factors such as genetics, immunologic, and environmental triggers also significantly contribute to its pathogenesis [3]. Recent years have witnessed a growing interest in the impact of the salivary microbiome on obesity, and dysbiosis of the oral cavity has been considered as one of the major characteristics associated with the disease [4].

The salivary microbiome is the most diverse microbial community in the human body after the gut [5]. The changes in host microbiome composition and function have been widely linked to varying lifestyle and geographic factors, including host genetics, age, diet, pregnancy, recurrent use of antibiotics, vaccines, smoking status, and other environmental hazards [6, 7]. The resident microbial community in the oral cavity plays a crucial role in regulating various metabolic and immune functions, such as taste perception, food digestion, and the detoxification of pathogens [4]; disruption of which can cause systemic inflammation. Nonetheless, the impact of microbial communities on human disease models, particularly obesity, is underrepresented in literature. Several animal model studies have shown the impact of bacterial endotoxins on adipose tissue function and insulin resistance [8, 9]; establishing a potential link between periodontal disease and risk of obesity [10, 11]. Since the oral cavity serves as a pathway to the intestines and peripheral blood, it can potentially influence gut microbiota, which in turn regulates various systemic functions. Additionally, a suggested mechanism involves the regulation of taste perception through the production of acids and metabolites by microbial species, affecting the host’s metabolic cycle [12]. Similarly, dysregulation of saccharolytic and proteolytic/amino acid-degrading bacteria may also lead to changes in the oral microenvironment, possibly influencing pathogenicity [13].

The past decade has witnessed a dramatic increase in the prevalence of obesity in both adults and children from the state of Kuwait. Approximately 50% of the Kuwaiti adolescents are reported to be either obese or overweight, accounting for 54.3% in boys, and 44.6% in girls [14]. In our prior research on the oral microbiome of Kuwaiti adolescents, grouped according to their body mass index (BMI) status, we identified notable alterations in core microbial taxa among the study groups. We found that bacterial species, such as Porphyromonas, were uniquely clustered in obese individuals, Campylobacter in those who were overweight, and Haemophilus in individuals with normal weight. This suggests a potential role of microbes in influencing obesity. Therefore, in this study, we intend to further explore the composition and diversity of salivary microbiota in a larger group of Kuwaiti adolescents (n = 62) and assess its influence on obesity. This will be achieved through an extensive in-silico analysis to identify key oral microbial risk markers and their unique metabolite profiles linked to obesity.

The participants were aged between 17 and 19 years, representing late adolescence, consisting of 39 females and 23 males. The study participants were classified as normal weight (n = 27), underweight (n = 7), overweight (n = 14) and obese (n = 14) based on their body mass index (BMI- kg/m2) as shown in Table 1. Obesity was defined as BMI for age z-scores ≥ 95th percentile according to the World Health Organization protocol. Each participant provided 3 ml of saliva sample following an overnight fast as described previously [15–17]. Participants with prior major illness and/or who were taking medication or supplements known to influence body composition were excluded from the study. The study protocol was approved by the ethical review committee of Committee at Dasman Diabetes Institute and the Ministry of Health in Kuwait. Prior to the commencement of the study, written informed consent was secured from the adolescent’s parents or legal guardians, along with assent from the adolescents. The research adhered to the EQUATOR network’s reporting guidelines [18].

Library preparation and DNA sequencing

Microbial DNA was extracted from saliva samples using Purelink Microbiome DNA purification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Waltham, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of 5 ng/µL of DNA was amplified using primers targeting the bacterial 16 S rRNA V3 and V4 regions. PCR reaction was carried out using KAPA HiFi HotStart Ready Mix PCR mix kit (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, USA), and the resulting product was confirmed on a Bioanalyzer using high sensitivity DNA chip (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, USA), purified using AMPure XP beads (Agilent, USA) and bound to dual indices using the Nextera XT Index Kit (Illumina Inc., San Diego, USA), following the manufacturers’ recommendations. Normalized libraries will be multiplexed up to 24 samples and paired-end sequencing will be carried out on the MiSeq platform (Illumina Inc., USA).

Raw sequencing data were saved in FASTQ format. The sequence data obtained were analyzed using QIIME2 (Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology) version 2022.8 and Microbiome analyst package [19]. Using QIIME2, the forward and reverse reads of the same sample were first joined. The paired reads were demultiplexed and quality filtered at Q-score of 25. High-quality amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were obtained by using the Divisive Amplicon Denoising Algorithm-2 (DADA2) algorithm. The taxonomy profile analysis was performed against the Greengenes v13.8 reference database [20], and transformed into relative abundance at the phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species levels. After this, using Greengenes reference database, open-reference operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were picked out from the non-chimeric sequences at 97% similarity. The most abundant read from each OTU was selected as the representative read for that OTU. The taxonomy associated with the Greengenes database to which OTUs matched was assigned to OTUs.

To test two-group differences in the percentage of analyzable read numbers between normal, underweight, overweight, and obese adolescents, we calculated p-values using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The OTU table was filtered to remove the OTUs containing < 10 counts in all samples and were transformed into relative abundances at the phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species levels. The intra-sample (alpha) and the inter-sample (beta) diversity analyses were performed as described elsewhere. The alpha diversity was calculated using three different measures: Observed, Chao1, Abundance-based Coverage Estimator (ACE), Shannon index; Simpson index; and Fisher statistics and phylogenetic diversity, performed as the references cited for each. The bacterial genus/spp. data were provided. Beta diversity (Bray-Curtis distances) was calculated using the UniFrac and Bray-Curtis distances matrices, generating the two-dimensional Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) plots. Weighted and un-weighted UniFrac distance matrices were used to derive the beta diversity of the samples, which data could be accessed. Non-parametric Mann-Whitney/Kruskal-Wallace tests were used to determine the statistical significance of α-diversity measures, and permutational MANOVA was used to determine the differences in β-diversity. A post hoc power analysis based on observed differences in alpha diversity (ACE index and Shannon) indicated that the sample size of 62 participants across four BMI-defined groups provided sufficient statistical power (≥ 80%) to detect moderate to large effect sizes at a significance level of 0.05.

Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) analysis was performed to identify the bacteria for which the relative abundance was significantly increased or decreased in each phenotypic category. LEfSe algorithm using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted p-value cutoff of 0.05 and the logarithmic LDA score cutoff of 2.0. The LEfSe bar plots were created using the MicrobiomeAnalyst package. In all analyses, p-values were corrected for Benjamini-Hochberg FDR. Random Forest analysis was applied to the 16 S rRNA data using machine learning methods developed by using the MicrobiomeAnalyst package to identify the most significant microbial features. Features with a minimum prevalence of 10% across samples were included, and those with > 0.005 accuracy were considered significant. Data were further transformed to cantered log ratio (CLR) before applying the Random Forest classification algorithm.

Microbial metagenomic functional predictions

Phylogenetic investigation of communities by reconstructing unobserved states-2 (PICRUSt2) software to predict the microbial content from each gut microbe sample and functionally annotate the data. The results were further analyzed using the Microbiomeanalyst package. To investigate the metabolic network of the predicted organisms, we used the MetaCyc database, which contains data regarding chemical compounds, reactions, enzymes, and metabolic pathways that have been experimentally validated. The correlation between microbial communities and their metabolites was performed using a Microbiome analyst package.

Results

Impact of body mass index on salivary microbiome diversity and composition in Kuwaiti adolescents

A total of 72,57,824 original sequences were obtained from 62 adolescents. The average number of sequences per sample was 1,18,980, which generated 51,24,354 reads, with an average of 512,435 reads per sample. For quality filtering, 20% of reads across all samples were removed, and data were tested by quality control procedures.

The analysis of the salivary microbiome in Kuwaiti adolescents, categorized based on body mass index (BMI) into normal, underweight, overweight, and obese groups, revealed significant variations in microbial diversity and composition. Alpha diversity, as measured by ACE, Fisher, Chao1, and Shannon indices (Fig. 1A), showed a significant reduction in diversity in overweight and obese groups compared to the normal BMI group. Beta diversity analysis using weighted UniFrac distances and MDS (Fig. 1E) illustrated distinct clustering patterns, indicating differences in microbial community composition across BMI categories, though some overlap was observed (Fig. 1E). Further supported by Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA), which demonstrated clear group separation, suggesting that BMI influences overall gut microbial community structure (Fig. 1F). The relative abundance of different bacterial phyla highlighted Firmicutes as the dominant phylum across all groups, with noticeable shifts in the proportion of Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteria with increasing BMI (Fig. 1G). Specifically, the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio appeared to increase in overweight and obese groups, suggesting a potential link between microbiome composition and obesity. An absolute abundance of data further supported these observations. Firmicutes showed a marked increase in overweight and obese adolescents, while Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria displayed a relative decrease (Fig. 1H-M). Actinobacteria, TM7, and Fusobacteria also exhibited variations across BMI categories, indicating that shifts in less dominant phyla might contribute to overall microbiome changes associated with BMI (Fig. 1G). Bacteroidetes were enriched in underweight and overweight individuals, while Proteobacteria were elevated in the obese group. These findings highlight the presence of BMI-specific microbial signatures, which may serve as potential biomarkers for metabolic health classification using machine learning approaches. These results suggest that salivary microbiome diversity and composition in Kuwaiti adolescents are influenced by BMI, with distinct microbial signatures associated with normal, underweight, overweight, and obese individuals. Dynamic changes in microbial composition on genus and species level based on BMI

To further explore BMI-associated alterations in gut microbiota, we analyzed taxonomic profiles at the phylum, genus, and species levels across all groups. At the phylum level, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes dominated across all BMI categories, with notable shifts in the proportions of Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria (Fig. 2A). These trends were further supported by taxonomic analyses at the order, class, and family levels (Supplementary Fig. S1–S3), which revealed BMI-associated shifts in the relative abundance of key microbial groups such as Lactobacillales, Bacteroidia, Clostridia, Streptococcaceae, and Prevotellaceae. These changes reflect broad compositional differences associated with nutritional status. The phylum, genus and species-level analysis of the salivary microbiome in Kuwaiti adolescents across different BMI categories revealed distinct microbial compositions and shifts associated with body mass index. At the genus level, Streptococcus emerged as the most dominant genus across all BMI groups, followed by Prevotella, Veillonella, Neisseria, and Porphyromonas (Fig. 2B). However, noticeable differences in the relative abundances of these genera were observed among BMI categories. Overweight and obese groups showed an increased relative abundance of Prevotella and Veillonella, while the normal BMI group exhibited higher proportions of Neisseria and Porphyromonas. The underweight group presented a more balanced distribution across the top genera, though Streptococcus remained dominant. At the species level, the relative abundance analysis highlighted Prevotella_melaninogenica, Veillonella_dispar, and Veillonella_parvula as prevalent species in overweight and obese adolescents, suggesting an association of these species with increased BMI (Fig. 2C). Conversely, species like Neisseria_subflava and Rothia_mucilaginosa were more abundant in normal-weight adolescents, indicating a potential protective or homeostatic role in maintaining a balanced salivary microbiome (Fig. 2D). The underweight group exhibited a higher relative abundance of Prevotella_pallens and Haemophilus_parainfluenzae, while Bulleidia_moorei appeared in consistent proportions across all BMI categories. Moreover, a considerable proportion of microbial reads remained unassigned at both genus and species levels, suggesting the presence of yet uncharacterized taxa within the salivary microbiome of this population. These findings underscore the complexity of the salivary microbiome and its potential link to the body mass index in adolescents, highlighting specific genera and species that may serve as biomarkers for metabolic health or dysbiosis.

Correlation network among the differentially abundant microbiota at species level

The microbial correlation network analysis reveals intricate interactions between salivary microbial species across different BMI categories (Normal, Underweight, Overweight, and Obese) (Fig. 3). In this network, nodes represent specific microbial species. At the same time, the edges illustrate their correlations, red lines indicate positive correlations, and green lines indicate negative correlations. Prevotella melaninogenica, a dominant species particularly in overweight and obese groups, exhibited multiple positive correlations with species such as Veillonella parvula and Veillonella dispar, both of which are also more abundant in higher BMI categories. This suggests a cooperative relationship among these species, potentially contributing to microbiome shifts associated with increased body weight. Additionally, species such as Neisseria subflava and Rothia mucilaginosa, which are more prevalent in normal-weight individuals, showed several negative correlations with obesity-associated taxa like Prevotella pallens and Haemophilus parainfluenzae. These negative interactions suggest competitive exclusion, where beneficial species in normal-weight individuals might inhibit the growth of obesity-associated microbes. Additionally, species like Bulleidia moorei and Aggregatibacter segnis demonstrated mixed correlations across BMI categories, indicating complex roles that may vary depending on the host’s metabolic status. The network also highlights that the microbial interactions are more densely connected in overweight and obese individuals (indicated by larger purple and black segments in the nodes), indicating that BMI affects not only the quantity of certain microbes but also their ecological interactions within the salivary microbiome. Consistent with these findings, functional correlation analysis (Supplementary Fig. S4) revealed significant associations between key microbial species and predicted metabolic pathways, suggesting that BMI-linked microbial shifts may influence host metabolism through altered microbial function. Differential changes in salivary microbiome biomarkers across BMI categories

The Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) Effect Size (LEfSe) results show distinct microbial biomarkers that differentiate the salivary microbiome composition among Kuwaiti adolescents across normal, underweight, overweight, and obese BMI categories. In comparing normal-weight and underweight individuals, several taxa were identified as significant biomarkers. Underweight adolescents exhibited an enrichment of g_Leptotrichia, g_Cardiobacterium, s_rimae, and o_Rickettsiales, indicating potential microbial signatures associated with lower BMI. Conversely, normal-weight individuals showed a significant abundance of f_Pasteurellaceae, o_CW040, g_Abiotrophia, f_Gemellaceae, and g_Granulicatella. These taxa might contribute to maintaining microbial homeostasis in normal-weight adolescents (Fig. 4A). Overweight adolescents showed significant enrichment in g_Butyrivibrio, s_Durum, s_Rimae, s_Influenzae, s_Mucosae, and s_Delbrueckii, suggesting shifts in microbial composition that may correlate with increased BMI (Fig. 4B). In contrast, normal-weight adolescents were characterized by a higher abundance of s_Helveticus, g_Treponema, s_Intermedia, and g_Selenomonas. These findings suggest that specific bacterial taxa are modulated by weight gain and may play a role in the salivary microbiome’s response to metabolic alterations. Obese adolescents displayed a marked increase in several species, including s_Melaninogenica, s_Moorei, s_Nigrescens, s_Orale, g_Paludibacter, s_parahaemolyticus, o_RF39, f_Comamonadaceae, and g_Granulicatella. These taxa could be key indicators of dysbiosis associated with obesity. On the other hand, normal-weight adolescents had a higher abundance of g_Streptococcus, g_Staphylococcus, g_Kingella, g_Dentocariosa, f_Gemellaceae, and s_Mucilaginosa, suggesting these microbes might contribute to a healthier salivary ecosystem (Fig. 4C). The phylogenetic tree provides a comprehensive overview of the taxonomic relationships among the significant biomarkers identified in the LEfSe analysis (Fig. 4D). Taxa enriched in obese individuals, such as Prevotella melaninogenica and Bulleidia moorei, are clustered within the Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes phyla, respectively, suggesting their role in obesity-associated microbial shifts. In contrast, taxa associated with normal-weight individuals, such as Streptococcus and Gemellaceae, are found within the Firmicutes phylum, indicating their potential protective role against weight-related dysbiosis. Additionally, species like g_Leptotrichia and g_Cardiobacterium, enriched in underweight individuals, are positioned within the Fusobacteria and Proteobacteria phyla, respectively, further underscoring the microbial distinctions across BMI categories. These findings underscore notable changes in the salivary microbiome associated with BMI, pinpointing specific microbial taxa that could act as potential indicators for metabolic health and obesity in adolescents. In the comparison between underweight and normal-weight adolescents, significant correlations were observed between specific taxa and functional enzymes. For example, Inositol 2-dehydrogenase and tRNA-methyltransferase were positively correlated with g_Leptotrichia and g_Cardiobacterium, both enriched in underweight individuals. Conversely, enzymes such as Pyruvate oxidase and Glycogen phosphorylase were negatively associated with underweight-associated taxa but positively linked with taxa abundant in normal-weight individuals like g_Granulicatella and g_Abiotrophia. These findings suggest that energy metabolism and biosynthetic pathways are downregulated in underweight individuals (Fig. 5C). Overweight individuals exhibited significant functional alterations compared to normal-weight peers. Enzymes like Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, Alcohol dehydrogenase, and Superoxide reductase were positively correlated with overweight-associated taxa such as g_Butyrivibrio and s_durum. These enzymes are involved in glucose metabolism and oxidative stress, suggesting that overweight individuals may experience increased oxidative stress and altered carbohydrate metabolism. Conversely, normal-weight-associated taxa such as g_Treponema and g_Selenomonas were negatively correlated with these enzymes, indicating a potential protective functional profile in the normal-weight group (Fig. 5D). Obese adolescents demonstrated pronounced alterations in microbial functional potential. Enzymes like Alcohol dehydrogenase, Superoxide reductase, Glucose-2-dehydrogenase, and Glycogen phosphorylase showed strong positive correlations with obesity-associated taxa such as s_moorei, s_orale, and s_nigrescens (Fig. 5E). These enzymes are critical in carbohydrate metabolism and redox regulation, suggesting a metabolic shift favoring energy storage and oxidative stress in obese individuals. In contrast, enzymes such as Glutathione peroxidase, Pyruvate oxidase, and Pyruvate synthase were negatively correlated with obesity-associated taxa but positively associated with normal-weight-associated taxa like s_mucilaginosa and g_Streptococcus. This indicates that normal-weight individuals may have enhanced antioxidant capacity and more balanced energy metabolism (Fig. 5E).

Although salivary enzymes do not directly participate in metabolic pathways like those in the liver or muscle, emerging evidence supports their role as non-invasive biomarkers of systemic metabolic status. Recent studies have demonstrated that salivary αamylase activity (sAAa) is inversely correlated with measures of adiposity and insulin resistance in both children and adults for example, in Qatari adults, higher sAAa was associated with significantly reduced odds of obesity after adjusting for metabolic covariates [11]. Furthermore, a large study from Qatar Biobank found a negative association between sAAa and BMI, waist and hip circumference, particularly among women [21]. More mechanistic insight comes from a 2025 study in overweight women of reproductive age, which showed that elevated sAAa was linked to reduced visceral fat accumulation and mediated decreases in the triglyceride-glucose index [22]. Complementary genetic research has revealed that copy number variation in the AMY1 gene strongly influences salivary amylase activity, which in turn is associated with metabolic disease status [23]. Thus, while these enzymes are not directly metabolizing nutrients systemically, their levels in saliva reflect physiological processes tied to energy balance, adiposity, and metabolic health.

Interestingly, these enzyme-related patterns align with our microbiome-based functional predictions, where taxa enriched in overweight and obese individuals were associated with elevated expression of carbohydrate- and redox-related enzymes, alongside increased levels of predicted metabolites involved in energy storage, oxidative stress, and inflammation. In contrast, normal-weight individuals exhibited a microbial and enzymatic profile linked to antioxidant activity and balanced energy metabolism, accompanied by predicted metabolites with potentially protective metabolic roles. This convergence of salivary enzyme activity, microbial functional capacity, and predicted metabolite shifts highlights the interconnected nature of oral microbiota and host metabolic health, suggesting that the salivary ecosystem may serve as a valuable window into systemic physiological states across BMI categories.

These findings highlight significant functional shifts in the salivary microbiome associated with BMI in Kuwaiti adolescents. Alterations in carbohydrate metabolism, oxidative stress response, and biosynthetic pathways were prominent in overweight and obese individuals, suggesting a potential role of the salivary microbiome in metabolic health and disease. The distinct enzyme-taxa correlations underscore the complex interplay between microbial composition and functional capacity, providing insights into the microbiome’s role in obesity and undernutrition.

Discussion

Obesity is a major public health concern in Kuwait, with earlier national data indicating that a substantial proportion of adolescents were classified as overweight or obese [24]. While the salivary microbiome has been examined in different populations worldwide, it has not been thoroughly researched in this region [25]. The presented study provides a comprehensive insight into the relationship between salivary microbiome composition and varying BMI in Kuwaiti adolescents based on microbial diversity, taxonomic composition, co-occurrence networks, and key metabolic and functional profiles. Our analysis shows a distinct association between salivary microbial diversity and varying BMI categories such as normal, underweight, overweight, and obese. We observed an inverse relationship between oral bacterial diversity and obesity as depicted by alpha diversity indices, indicating a significant reduction in microbial diversity in overweight and obese individuals compared to normal-weight adolescents. Likewise, beta diversity analysis further confirmed distinct clustering patterns between BMI groups suggesting compositional shifts in microbial communities. These observations are consistent with previous studies linking reduced microbial diversity to metabolic disorders, including obesity [26, 27].

At the phylum level, Firmicutes dominated Kuwaiti adolescents across all BMI groups, with an increasing Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio in overweight and obese individuals. This shift mirrors general patterns observed in gut microbiome studies, where a higher Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio has been associated with obesity [28]. At the genus level, we observed a relative abundance of Prevotella and Veillonella in overweight and obese groups, while Neisseria and Porphyromonas were more prevalent in normal-weight individuals. These genera are known to influence oral and systemic health, with some associated with inflammatory responses that could contribute to metabolic dysfunction [29, 30].

Species-level analysis of the salivary microbiome in Kuwaiti adolescents identified several taxa that may serve as potential biomarkers for BMI-related metabolic states. Obese individuals showed higher abundances of Prevotella melaninogenica, Veillonella dispar, and Veillonella parvula, suggesting their possible role in obesity-associated microbial dysbiosis. In contrast, Neisseria subflava and Rothia mucilaginosa were more abundant in normal-weight individuals, potentially reflecting a healthier oral microbial profile. While some of these associations have been observed in previous microbiome studies, particularly at the genus level or in non-salivary niches, our findings provide additional insight into species-level variations within a Middle Eastern adolescent population an underrepresented group in microbiome research. Prevotella melaninogenica, an anaerobic species commonly found in the oral and upper respiratory tract, has been associated with various inflammatory and autoimmune conditions, including ankylosing spondylitis [31], Sjogren syndrome [32], and oral conditions such as dental caries [33], lichen planus [34], periodontitis [35], including oral squamous cell carcinoma [36]. Veillonella parvula has been implicated in conditions such as meningitis, osteomyelitis, and endocarditis, while Veillonella dispar has been linked to prosthetic joint infections and bloodstream infections. Neisseria subflava has been reported in cases of post-operative meningitis [37], and Rothia mucilaginosa plays a complex role in respiratory disease, with evidence supporting both pro- and anti-inflammatory effects [38, 39]. Recent studies have demonstrated oral microbiota alterations in relation to obesity and body composition in both children and adults, including shifts in subgingival and salivary microbiota linked to metabolic markers and inflammatory profiles [11, 40–42]. Our findings build on this growing body of evidence by providing population-specific insights into salivary microbial dysbiosis in overweight and obese adolescents from the Middle East. These results underscore the potential of species-level salivary profiling to uncover microbiome-based indicators of metabolic risk in youth.

We further carried out correlation network analysis to provide further insights into the microbial interactions underpinning BMI-related differences. Obese and overweight groups exhibited more densely connected networks, suggesting altered microbial dynamics and potential dysbiosis. Positive correlations among obesity-associated species, such as Prevotella melaninogenica and Veillonella dispar, indicate cooperative interactions that may promote the proliferation of pro-inflammatory microbes. In contrast, negative correlations between health-associated taxa like Neisseria subflava and obesity-associated microbes suggest competitive exclusion, where beneficial species may inhibit the growth of harmful taxa. These findings underscore the importance of microbial interactions in shaping the salivary microbiome and its relationship with metabolic health.

LEfSe analysis further revealed distinct microbial taxa that differentiate BMI categories. Underweight adolescents were characterized by an enrichment of Leptotrichia and Cardiobacterium, while overweight individuals showed higher abundances of Butyrivibrio and Haemophilus influenzae. Although Leptotrichia and Cardiobacterium are part of the normal flora, their beneficial effects are poorly understood, and they are generally considered to be opportunistic and pathogenic [43, 44]. The predominance of Butyrivibrio in the saliva of overweight individuals may be linked to a high dietary fiber intake that harnesses the beneficial role of Butyrivibrio in butyrate synthesis, promoting overall gut-oral microbial balance and anti-inflammatory effects [45, 46]. A lower abundance of Butyrivibrio has also been previously associated with the development of Type 2 diabetes [45]. Further in-depth studies need to be carried out to understand the role that Haemophilus influenzae plays in metabolic health. Obese adolescents exhibited a marked increase in Prevotella melaninogenica, Bulleidia moorei, and Paludibacter, highlighting potential biomarkers of obesity. Conversely, taxa such as Streptococcus, Gemellaceae, and Rothia mucilaginosa were enriched in normal-weight individuals, suggesting their role in maintaining a balanced salivary microbiome. These differential signatures provide valuable insights into the taxonomic shifts associated with BMI and may inform future diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

We further observed significant differences in microbial metabolic pathways across different BMI categories using functional prediction analysis. Overweight and obese adolescents exhibited increased activity of enzymes involved in carbohydrate metabolism, such as Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, Pyruvate oxidase, and Glycogen phosphorylase. Although salivary enzymes do not directly regulate carbohydrate storage like hepatic enzymes, previous studies suggest that their expression patterns can reflect host metabolic states, including energy intake and adiposity levels [11, 21–23]. These changes suggest a shift toward enhanced energy production and storage, consistent with metabolic profiles observed in obesity. Additionally, elevated levels of oxidative stress-related enzymes, including Superoxide reductase and Glutathione peroxidase, in overweight and obese individuals point to increased oxidative stress, a known contributor to metabolic disorders.

Conversely, normal-weight adolescents displayed higher activity of enzymes associated with antioxidant defenses and balanced energy metabolism, such as Pyruvate synthase and tRNA-methyl transferase. Underweight individuals showed reduced activity of key metabolic enzymes, reflecting potential deficiencies in energy metabolism and biosynthetic pathways. The correlation analysis between specific taxa and functional enzymes further supports these findings. Obesity-associated taxa, such as Prevotella melaninogenica and Veillonella dispar, were positively correlated with carbohydrate metabolism and oxidative stress enzymes, suggesting their involvement in metabolic dysregulation. In contrast, health-associated taxa like Neisseria subflava and Rothia mucilaginosa were linked to antioxidant and homeostatic functions, underscoring the role of specific microbes in maintaining metabolic balance. The presence of unassigned taxa highlights the complexity of the salivary microbiome and suggests the existence of yet unidentified microbes that may play critical roles in metabolic health.

Our study provides valuable insights into the relationship between the oral microbiota and obesity in Kuwaiti adolescents. The strength of our study lies in its comprehensive analysis of microbial biomarkers and their potential association with obesity and weight homeostasis, particularly during adolescence. However, several limitations must be acknowledged, which point the way toward future research directions. One primary limitation of the study is the small number of individuals in each subclass. Stratifying participants into various subgroups based on factors such as BMI categories, age, or lifestyle practices leads to reduced statistical power. Secondly, the interplay between BMI and the oral microbiome also raises an important question: Does a change in BMI influence the composition of the oral microbiome, or could alterations in the oral microbiota contribute to changes in weight? The current study design does not allow for definitive conclusions regarding causality. Longitudinal studies and interventional trials are needed to determine the directionality of this relationship.

In conclusion, this study highlights the intricate relationship between the salivary microbiome, microbial function, and BMI in Kuwaiti adolescents. The observed taxonomic and functional shifts suggest that the salivary microbiome may play a critical role in metabolic health and could serve as a non-invasive biomarker for obesity and related metabolic disorders. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies to establish causal relationships and explore the potential of microbiome-targeted interventions for preventing and managing obesity. Additionally, the identification of unassigned taxa emphasizes the need for deeper metagenomic sequencing to uncover novel microbes that may influence metabolic health.
