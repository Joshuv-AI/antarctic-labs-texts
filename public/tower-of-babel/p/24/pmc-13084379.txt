Bilevel Internal Model Control-Desired Dynamic Equation-PID Control Strategy for Superheated Steam Regulation in Combined Heat and Power unit
Authors: Jingyu Zhao, Chengbo Dai, Donghai Li, Yanjun Ding, Bin Tian, Gangyao Yan, Yali Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Superheated steam temperature (SST) regulation in large coal-fired combined heat and power (CHP) units becomes increasingly challenging under deep peak-shaving. Wide-range load variations and multisource disturbances require strong disturbance rejection, whereas industrial distributed control systems typically retain PID-oriented implementations with limited computational capability. Motivated by these, this paper proposes a bilevel IMC-DDE-PID control framework for SST regulation. In the inner loop, a desired dynamic equation (DDE)-based tuning rule shapes the plant dynamics to follow a prescribed desired closed-loop model without requiring a precise process model and partially attenuates input-side disturbances. The residual disturbance is then handled by an outer internal model control (IMC) layer built upon the desired model as an embedded internal model. Comprehensive simulations are conducted on seven benchmark SISO plants and on identified SST models at multiple loads, including scenarios with large load variations and cross-load model variation, to evaluate tracking, disturbance rejection, and control variation. Finally, field tests on an in-service 660 MW CHP unit under deep peak-shaving validate the practicality of the proposed method, reducing the SST fluctuation range by 7 °C.

Introduction

Power-plant control is entering a more demanding operating regime as electricity demand continues to grow, while generation mixes become increasingly variable. The IEA projects that global electricity demand will grow at close to 4% annually through 2027, driven by electrification, industrial expansion, cooling demand, and data-center growth. Although this transition reduces emissions, the intermittency of wind and solar increases short-term balancing requirements and places stricter demands on dispatchable heat and power (CHP) units to deliver fast, reliable load-following and AGC performance under frequent operating-point changes.

Within CHP units, safe-range regulation of the superheated steam temperature (SST) becomes particularly challenging during deep peak-shaving and fast load ramps. SST must be maintained within a narrow band around its set point to ensure both safety and efficiency. Industrial practice and prior studies commonly report an allowable deviation on the order of a few degrees. , Excessive SST can accelerate thermal stress and may increase the risk of pipeline damage, while excessively low SST can reduce the power generation efficiency of downstream steam turbines. Consequently, strengthening the SST control loop is a practical enabler for wider-range load adjustment and more secure renewable integration in modern power systems.

Facing SST regulation in coal-fired units, the combination of lag-dominant high-order thermal dynamics, operating-point nonlinearity, and multisource disturbances makes the conventional PI strategy increasingly inadequate. Cao et al. reported that the fixed PID settings commonly adopted in plants become mismatched as the operating point shifts, leading to degraded regulation quality. Accordingly, many enhanced control architectures have been investigated. One major stream extends PID-type designs to cope with load variations, including gain-scheduled PID designs and adaptive or intelligent PID variants. Zhang et al. used a cascade SST control structure with a neuro-PID controller. Wang et al. introduced a leading-signal correction scheme using upstream reheater temperatures to accelerate the final-stage response. Yu et al. employed load-dependent parameter adjustment to reduce temperature deviation and response latency during fast load ramps. A second stream leverages advanced control, such as MPC/DMC/GPC (often combined with feedforward compensation or constrained optimization), to explicitly handle multivariable interactions and actuator constraints in coordinated temperature control. Representative efforts include the stable fuzzy MPC (SFMPC) proposed by Wu et al., the neuro-fuzzy-network-based nonlinear GPC (NFGPC) developed by Liu et al., and the computationally efficient nonlinear MPC with integral action proposed by Fan et al. for wide-load operation of ultrasupercritical units under various disturbances. These efforts demonstrate promising results in simulations and plant tests; however, widespread adoption in in-service units remains limited.

Two practical barriers are most frequently encountered in field deployment: (i) Many advanced algorithms increase computational and engineering complexity, making integration, maintenance, and online commissioning on DCS platforms nontrivial, especially under strict reliability and cybersecurity constraints. (ii) Performance is often sensitive to model quality or online adaptation, whereas SST dynamics and disturbance paths vary significantly with load. Meanwhile, reliable model acquisition, particularly via open-loop identification tests, is costly and operationally risky in operating units. ,

Internal Model Control (IMC) is an intuitive and practically appealing control architecture with a highly transparent tuning philosophy, and it has been successfully used in many industrial applications, especially in the process industry. , By embedding a plant model explicitly in the controller, IMC constructs the nominal control action through a filtered model inverse, while the feedback acts on the plant–model mismatch to compensate for disturbances and modeling errors. A major advantage of IMC is that the performance–robustness trade-off is shaped explicitly by a low-pass filter whose time constant often serves as the primary tuning knob.

Beyond the standard IMC structure, IMC-based PID realizations and tuning rules have been developed for diverse process classes. Wang et al. derived IMC-PID tuning for stable/unstable dead-time processes via pole–zero conversion, using an imaginary first-order filter or a series lead–lag compensator with set point weighting, with the tuning parameter computed from the process time constant and dead time. Kumar and Padma provided IMC-based PID tuning for integrating processes and evaluated performance using IAE/ITAE and total variation. Irshad and Ali established λ-selection rules for time-delayed inverse-response processes to satisfy a prescribed maximum sensitivity Ms∈[1.4, 2.0]. Hu et al. further derived IMC high-order PID tuning by approximating the IMC controller as a PID series compensator with a single parameter λ chosen under robustness and ITAE criteria.

Fractional-order extensions have been introduced within the IMC/PID framework to impose fractional closed-loop specifications. Maâmar and Rachid developed IMC-PID-fractional-filter controllers in which an integer PID is cascaded with a fractional filter. Li et al. designed a maximum-sensitivity-based fractional IMC-PID controller for noninteger order systems with time delay, and the resulting controller takes the form of a FOPID cascaded with a filter. For nonlinear temperature-control applications, Pachauri et al. designed and modified an FO-IMC-PID controller and optimized its parameters using a water-cycle algorithm. In addition, FO-IMC controllers have been approximated by a modified PID with a cascaded fractional-order filter for practical realization.

Despite the rich IMC-based design spectrum, a common limitation remains: their performance critically depends on the validity of the embedded model and inversion across operating conditions. When model perturbations are pronounced, or the plant exhibits noninvertible elements (e.g., significant dead time, nonminimum-phase zeros, unstable factors, or strong operating-point dependence), accurate inversion is either infeasible or must be heavily approximated. Consequently, disturbance attenuation and tracking performance may degrade rapidly as the internal model mismatch grows, often leading to repeated identification and retuning in operating units.

To address these limitations, the model-independent desired dynamics-based approach offers a practical alternative. It reshapes the original system into an explicitly parametrized expected closed-loop dynamic that is stable and readily invertible, enabling subsequent IMC design. Within this line of research, active disturbance rejection control (ADRC) has been widely applied to process and aerospace systems. − In addition, the desired-dynamics-equation-based PID (DDE-PID) controller has also shown promising performance. To this end, this paper proposes a composite strategy for SST regulation. The main contributions are summarized as follows:1.A DCS-friendly bilevel IMC-DDE-PID framework is developed, where the desired dynamic model is reused as the IMC internal nominal model, avoiding open-loop or closed-loop system identification.2.By deriving the equivalent frequency-domain transfer relation of the input-disturbance channel, we systematically analyzed the influence of controller parameters on tracking and disturbance-rejection performance and quantitatively elucidated the mechanism by which the proposed method improves disturbance attenuation.3.The proposed IMC-DDE-PID method has been applied to an in-service 660 MW CHP field test, and the operating data confirms its feasibility in reducing temperature deviation under load changes.

Model Identification

The superheated steam temperature (SST) system is of a distributed-parameter nature. For controller design, it is therefore convenient to approximate the SST dynamics around a given operating point by a low-order lumped transfer-function model. In this study, we focus on the SISO channel from the second-stage desuperheating valve opening u(t) to the outlet steam temperature y(t). The SST models of an in-service 660 MW power plant in China are depicted as , GP(s)=Kl(Tls+1)4 1

Historical operating data were extracted at three typical load conditions of the unit, namely, about 30, 60, and 100% of rated power. For each condition, a time interval was selected in which the boiler load was quasi-stationary while the second-stage desuperheating valve exhibited sufficient movement. The data were recorded with a sampling period of Δt = 3 s. The optimization problem (eq ) is solved in MATLAB by the function lsqnonlin, which implements a trust-region Gauss–Newton algorithm for nonlinear least-squares problems.

IMC-DDE-PID Control Design

Based on the identified SST models in Section , the secondary desuperheating loop can be approximated around each operating point by a stable, strictly proper SISO transfer function: Y(s)=GP(s)[U(s)+Din(s)] 4 where U(s) is the valve command, Y(s) is the secondary steam temperature, and D in(s) denotes equivalent input-side disturbances.

To enhance dynamic performance under limited DCS computation, a bilevel control scheme is adopted (Figure ): the inner loop shapes the plant dynamics via DDE-PID, while the outer loop applies IMC using the shaped dynamics as the internal model. R(s) denotes the reference signal of the steam temperature. V(s) denotes the output of IMC and the set point of the inner DDE-PID loop. Yd (s) denotes the desired trajectory.

Notation

Lowercase letters denote time-domain signals (u(t), y(t), d in(t), r(t), v(t), and y d (t)), whereas the corresponding uppercase letters (U(s), Y(s), D in(s), R(s), V(s), and Y d (s)) denote their Laplace-domain counterparts. For brevity, the explicit arguments (t) and (s) may be omitted. Subscripts/superscripts follow a unified convention: “com” indicates the inner-loop shaped dynamics, “in” denotes the input-side disturbance channel, “out” denotes the output-side disturbance, and “eq” denotes an equivalent representation. In particular, T in(s), G p,com(s), and D out,eq(s) describe the inner-loop, whereas T d,in(s), T r (s), and T d,out(s) denote the overall bilevel system. The reference model used in the IMC layer is denoted by the desired dynamic model, G d (s). The IMC controller is denoted by G IMC(s).

This composite structure offers several salient features that are particularly relevant to SST control:1.Two-layer disturbance rejection: The inner DDE-PID loop shapes the SST dynamics to a desired dynamic model, while the outer IMC loop further compensates the equivalent output disturbance. Remark 1

Parameterizing the inner loop by ω d and l provides an explicit link between controller settings and closed-loop dynamics: a larger ω d aims at a faster response, while an excessively large ω d inevitably increases the deviation between the actual closed loop and the desired model Gd (s). The model-matching index introduced in Section quantifies this deviation and is used to select ω d and l such that the response is sufficiently fast, while the mismatch remains acceptable.

Remark 2

If a first-order desired model is used instead of eq , the same procedure yields an inner controller that reduces to a 2-DOF PI structure. The proposed framework, therefore, encompasses both PID-type and PI-type inner loops.

In summary, the DDE-PID inner loop shapes the original plant Gp (s) into an equivalent closed-loop dynamic that closely follows the desired model Gd (s). Input-side disturbances of D in(s) are partially attenuated by this loop, while the residual disturbances and model mismatch are handled by the outer IMC layer discussed next.

Model-matching index:

To explicitly quantify the quality of inner-loop shaping, the compensated plant is parametrized as a multiplicative perturbation of the desired model: Gp,com(s)≜Gd(s)[1+Δ(s)] 26 where Δ­(s) denotes the model mismatch induced by the DDE–PID design. When Δ­(s) = 0, the inner closed loop exactly matches the prescribed dynamics Gd (s).

The model-matching performance of the inner loop is characterized by the frequency-domain index: EΔ≜∥Δ(jω)∥∞=supω∈Ω|Gp,com(jω)Gd(jω)−1| 27

This index measures the maximum relative error between the compensated plant and the desired model over the frequency range Ω. Smaller values of E Δ indicate that the DDE inner loop shapes the plant more accurately into Gd (s).

Figure a shows E Δ as a function of the desired bandwidth ω d . A clear minimum occurs around ω d = 0.02: both slower and faster bandwidths lead to a larger mismatch. If the inner DDE-PID loop is well tuned such that |Δ­(s)| ≤ 1, then the closed-loop channels can be approximated by Tr(s)≈Q(s),Td,in(s)≈[1−Q(s)]Tin(s) 30

These relations reveal a two-degree-of-freedom interpretation of the IMC–DDE–PID scheme. The IMC filter Q(s) directly shapes the reference channel Tr (s), while the complementary factor 1 – Q(s) shapes the residual disturbance channel T in(s). In other words, the DDE parameters encode inner disturbance attenuation, while the IMC parameter λ adjusts the trade-off between tracking speed and disturbance rejection. This two-channel structure forms the analytical basis for the disturbance-rejection and tracking performance analyses in Section .

Superheated Steam Temperature Process

To further assess the engineering relevance of the proposed method, we then conducted the dynamic simulation on the superheater steam temperature (SST) loop of a large coal-fired power plant, using the model identified in Section .

The outlet temperature of the high-temperature superheater is the controlled variable, and the spray-water valve command is taken as the manipulated input. The active-power signal is used to construct a normalized load-related disturbance, representing the effect of deep peak-shaving on the superheater inlet conditions. The model at 60% load is selected as the nominal plant. On this model, controllers are tuned: for a standard IMC controller λ = 50; for a DDE-PID controller ω d = 0.015, k = 0.15, l = −0.0048; for the proposed controller ω d = 0.015, k = 0.15, l = −0.0048, λ = 50. To make the comparison clear, three simulation scenarios are considered.

Scenario A: Nominal Condition

The controllers tuned at 60% load are first tested on the corresponding nominal model, as shown in Figure . At about 70 min, a 10 °C step change is applied to the temperature set point to evaluate tracking performance. At approximately 130 and 200 min, load-related step disturbances with different magnitudes are introduced to assess disturbance rejection. The proposed IMC–DDE–PID controller achieves a faster tracking response while simultaneously reducing the impact of disturbances on the outlet temperature.

Under the nominal operating condition, the desired dynamics model Gd (s) is well matched with the DDE–PID inner-loop design so that the shaped closed-loop behavior closely follows the prescribed dynamics. The observed improvements in tracking and disturbance rejection in Figure can be interpreted using the indices derived in Section , namely, the disturbance-rejection index Λdy and the tracking index Λry. In particular, Λdy and Λry are jointly determined by the outer-loop IMC tuning parameters and the inner-loop DDE–PID parameters. This bilevel parametrization provides additional tuning degrees of freedom compared with a single-layer controller, enabling better results in both tracking and disturbance rejection performance.

Scenario B evaluates the controllers under realistic deep peak-shaving conditions, where large load variations induce significant input-side disturbances. The same three controllers, tuned at 60% load, are tested on the nominal 60%-load SST model. A time-varying disturbance signal is constructed from measured active-power data recorded during an actual deep peak-shaving operation and is normalized and injected as a load-related disturbance d in at the process input.

Owing to the strong load fluctuations, both the DDE–PID and the standard IMC controller exhibit noticeable temperature oscillations and larger deviations from the set point. In contrast, the proposed controller keeps the outlet temperature within a narrower band and recovers more quickly after major load changes.

The peak-to-peak fluctuation is defined as ΔT pp = max­(T) – min­(T) to quantitatively characterize the control performance. The statistical results show that the IMC method yields a fluctuation range of 19 °C, and the DDE–PID method reduces it to 13.65 °C, whereas the proposed method further tightens the range to 7.19 °C. This corresponds to a reduction of 47.3% relative to DDE–PID and 62.2% relative to IMC, demonstrating substantially enhanced disturbance attenuation.

This improvement is consistent with the frequency-domain interpretation in Section : the inner DDE–PID shaping reduces the effective input-disturbance path T in(s) by enforcing prescribed inner closed-loop dynamics, and the outer IMC further suppresses the residual equivalent output disturbance through the disturbance channel T d,out(s). Consequently, the overall input-disturbance-to-output transfer T d,in(s)= T d,out(s)T in(s) is reduced in a multiplicative manner, which directly explains the significantly tightened temperature fluctuation band.

Scenario C investigates the robustness of the controllers to model the variation caused by deep peak-shaving. The controllers tuned at 60% load are directly applied to the identified SST models at 30% and 90% load without any retuning. For each operating point, a 10 °C step change in the outlet temperature set point and a small load-related disturbance are imposed. Owing to the pronounced model mismatch across different load conditions, the disturbance attenuation capability of the standard IMC controller deteriorates. At 30% load (Figure a), the SST disturbance amplitude under the standard IMC reaches 6.4 °C, whereas the proposed method suppresses it to 2.5 °C, corresponding to a 60.9% reduction. At 90% load (Figure b), the disturbance amplitude further increases to 14.6 °C with the standard IMC; the DDE–PID reduces it to 9.7 °C, while the proposed method achieves 6.9 °C, representing reductions of 52.7 and 28.9%, respectively. This trend is expected from IMC algebra: the standard IMC relies on an explicit model inverse, and when the operating point changes, the effective process gain/time constants and delay characteristics deviate from the nominal internal model, enlarging the mismatch term and deteriorating T d,out(s), which manifests as larger temperature oscillations. In contrast, the proposed strategy fixes the inner-loop target dynamics through ω d and the observer bandwidth k, so that the IMC layer operates on the shaped equivalent plant G p,com(s) that stays closer to the desired model across loads. As a result, under load-induced nonlinear variations of the plant, a single set of controller parameters can cover a wider operating range with improved robustness using the proposed strategy.

Field Application

Building on the satisfactory results obtained from theoretical analysis and simulations, the proposed IMC-DDE-PID composite control method was implemented in the superheated steam temperature control of an in-service 660 MW combined heat and power (CHP) unit in China. This unit participates in deep peak-shaving with an operating range of approximately 200–660 MW. The IMC-DDE-PID algorithm was implemented on the industrial control computer using its native programming language. The original controller is a PI controller whose parameters were tuned by experienced field engineers.

The proposed bilevel IMC–DDE–PID strategy is configured on the plant DCS using standard function blocks. The outer IMC layer is implemented as a supervisory module that generates a virtual set point for the inner loop. Specifically, the desired model Gd (s) and the IMC low-pass filter Q(s) are realized by cascaded low-order transfer-function blocks (e.g., first-order lag or lead-lag elements) and summation nodes. The inner DDE–PID loop is implemented in its equivalent 2-DOF PID form (eqs and ), which is directly compatible with standard DCS PID modules with set point weighting. The DDE-related shaping parameters (ω d , l, k) are used to compute KP , KI , K D, and the set point weighting coefficient b, and the observer-related gain k is proportional to ω d . Standard engineering protections (output saturation, rate limits, and antiwindup) are enabled in the PID module.

During the field tests, both the inner DDE–PID and the outer IMC layer are equipped with enable/disable logic, and bumpless transfer is ensured by tracking the manual/previous controller output and initializing the integrator/filter states to match the current valve command before switching. This avoids output jumps at switching instants and preserves operational safety.

The computational burden is low: each scan updates only a small number of low-order filter states and a conventional PID calculation. No online identification, numerical optimization, or matrix operations are required. Therefore, the proposed method can be readily deployed within typical DCS scan cycles for temperature-control loops.

Figures and report two typical field tests under load-increasing and load-decreasing conditions, respectively. Vertical dashed lines represent the switching between the original controller and the proposed method. In the load-increasing case shown in Figure , the steam temperature exhibits pronounced fluctuations, with peak values reaching about 605.4 °C and troughs down to 591.2 °C, i.e., a peak-to-peak variation exceeding 14 °C under the original PI controller (blue curve). After switching to the proposed controller (magenta curve), the temperature oscillations are visibly attenuated: the peak-to-peak value decreased to approximately 8–10 °C. The quantitative indices over the respective operating intervals are summarized in Table : the mean absolute error drops from 3.11 to 1.82 °C, and the standard deviation decreases from 1.51 to 0.98 °C. This improvement is consistent with the proposed disturbance-channel shaping mechanism: load changes inject disturbances through multiple pathways (fuel/air dynamics, flue-gas temperature, and heat storage), which can be effectively represented by an input-side disturbance d in. The inner DDE–PID stage reshapes the closed-loop dynamics toward Gd (s) and attenuates the dominant disturbance component, while the outer IMC filter suppresses the remaining equivalent output disturbance without amplifying the measurement noise.

Conclusions

This work developed and validated an IMC-DDE-PID composite control framework for superheated steam temperature (SST) regulation in large coal-fired units operating under deep peak-shaving. The proposed structure integrates (i) a DDE-based desired-dynamics shaping layer that standardizes the effective closed-loop behavior and (ii) an IMC layer that reuses the shaped expected model as the internal nominal model. This design preserves industrial PID-oriented implementability while reducing the dependence on high-fidelity open-loop identification. A practical design procedure is then summarized after investigating the influences of tuning parameters on the control performance and stability.

Simulation studies on seven benchmark SISO plants (covering high-order, lag, and instability) and identified SST models across multiple loads show that the proposed controller consistently improves tracking and disturbance rejection compared with baseline controllers. In the deep peak-shaving SST simulation, the proposed method tightens the peak-to-peak fluctuation from 19.0 °C (IMC) and 13.65 °C (DDE–PID) to 7.19 °C, corresponding to reductions of 62.2% and 47.3%, respectively. Field tests on an in-service 660 MW CHP unit further quantify the benefit under realistic peak-shaving. Switching from the original controller to the proposed method reduces ΔT pp from 18.2 to 9.2 °C (49.5%) for load increasing and from 17.5 to 10.5 °C (40%) for load decreasing. The mean absolute error drops from 3.11 to 1.82 °C, and the standard deviation decreases from 1.51 to 0.98 °C. These results indicate that IMC-DDE-PID can effectively tighten the SST fluctuation band under peak-shaving. The successful field deployment on an in-service 660 MW unit indicates a strong potential for practical adoption in power plants. This potential becomes increasingly relevant as deep peak-shaving becomes routine with a higher penetration of renewable energy.
