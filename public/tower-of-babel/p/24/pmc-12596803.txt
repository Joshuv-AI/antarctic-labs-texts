Diagnosis and surgery for cervical synovial cysts: A review
Authors: Nancy E. Epstein, Kyla Agulnick, Marc A. Agulnick
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Most patients with cervical synovial cysts (CSC) present with radiculopathy and/or myelopathy. MR studies are the gold standard for diagnosing CSC, and typically show hypointense T1/hyperintense T2 lesions, with occasional cyst-wall enhancement and additional cyst-wall calcification. Surgery typically warrants focal cyst resection/ decompression with/without an instrumented fusion. Here, we reviewed the diagnosis/treatment of a 76-year-old male with a CSC, and C5-C6/C6-C7 , and C7-T1 stenosis. We further provided a select review of the literature.Methods: For 3 months, a 76-year-old-male experienced progressive bilateral/arm pain (i.e., radiculopathy), and one week of increased right upper/right lower extremity weakness with loss of balance (i.e., myelopathy). The MR with/without contrast and non contrast CT studies documented moderate C5-C6/C6-C7 stenosis and a large, likely synovial cyst filling the right-side of the spinal canal at the C7-T1 level (i.e, 12 mm x 9 mmx 19 mm).Results: The patient urgently underwent excision of the large right C7-T1 synovial cyst, along with a C6-T2 laminectomy for stenosis, and a C4-T4 instrumented fusion. At 4-postoperative months, the patient was neurologically intact. The repeated X-rays performed at 2, 6, and 12 weeks postoperatively documented maintained alignment, while the MR scan confirmed adequate cord decompression without myelomalacia.Conclusion: A 76-year-old male with increased right-sided myeloradiculopathy, successfully underwent resection of a right-sided C7-T1 synovial cyst filling the spinal canal, a C6-T2 laminectomy for stenosis, and a C4-T4 instrumented fusion. Four months postoperatively, the patient was neurologically intact, with postoperative X-rays showing stable alignment. Further, the follow-up MR documented no residual cord/root compression, or myelomalacia. Here, we have provided a review of this case and select literature regarding the diagnosis and surgery for CSC.

INTRODUCTION

Cervical synovial cysts (CSC) represent 0.65-2.6% of all spinal synovial cysts, and most frequently occur at the C7-T1 followed by the C1-C2, C5-C6, and C2-C3 levels [Table 1].[4,7-9,11,12,15] Most patients present with axial pain (77.1%), unilateral radiculopathy, and/or myelopathy (94.3%).[1,3,4,7,9,11,12,14,15] MR imaging, the gold standard for diagnosing CSC, best shows the location and full extent of cord/nerve root compression.[1-16] On MR, CSC lesions are hypointense on T1WI, hyperintense on T2 WI, with cyst walls occasionally enhancing with contrast. Alternatively, CT studies may show cyst-wall calcification without providing any cord/root detail.[9,11,15] Surgery rarely requires cyst resection/decompression alone, as most patients warrant instrumented fusions to address instability (i.e., particularly for transitional-level C1-C2 or C7-T1 lesions).[2-7,9,10,12,13-15] Here, a 76-year-old-male successfully underwent a right-sided C7-T1 CSC excision, a C6-T2 laminectomy to decompress stenosis, and a C4-T4 instrumented fusion [Figures 1-9]. Here, we have additionally provided a select review of the literature establishing the diagnosis of a CSC and reviewing the surgical options.

Case Presentation

A 76-year-old-male presented with 3-months of bilateral shoulder/arm pain, and one week of markedly increased right arm/hand (C6, C7, C8; Grade 3-4/5) and right iliopsoas weakness (Grade 4/5) with gait instability [Table 1]. The preoperative X-rays, MR (with/without contrast) and CT studies documented an adequate cervical lordosis, a C7-T1 grade I anterolisthesis, a likely right-sided C7-T1 synovial cyst (12 x 9 x 19 mm), and C6-T2 stenosis [Figure 1-7]. Surgery included excision of the right C7-T1 synovial cyst, a C6-T2 laminectomy for decompression of stenosis, and a C4-T4 lateral mass -pedicle/screw fusion. Intraoperatively, the right-sided C7-T1 synovial cyst markedly compressed the right side of cord and C8 nerve root. The capsule, tough/ tenacious and containing a relatively small amount of ballotable intrinsic cyst fluid, was readily dissected away from the dura. The ossified yellow ligament contributing to the C6-T2 stenosis was also easily removed. The intraoperative neurological monitoring potentials (IONM: SEP/MEP/EMG) showed no significant changes. X-rays, 2, 6, and 12 weeks postoperatively confirmed adequate localization of the instrumentation (i.e., lateral mass screws C4-6, and pedicle screws T1-T4) [Figure 8]. Four months postoperatively, the patient was neurologically intact. The follow-up non contrast MR documented full cord/root decompression without any high cord signal or myelomalacia [Figure 9].

Incidence and Etiology of CSC

Yang et al. (2023) reported a 0.65-2.6% incidence of cervical CSC, while Yoo et al. (2024) documented the frequency of their locations in the cervical (55%), thoracic (42%), and lumbar spine (31%) [Table 1].[15,16] Further Mak et al. (2019) attributed the etiology of CSC extrusions to progressive/ acquired degenerative defects within the facet joint capsules largely attributed to repeated hypermobility and/or trauma resulting in the extrusion of synovial hyperplastic tissue through degenerated facet joint capsules.[11]

Locations of CSC: Most at C7-T1, Followed by the C1-C2, and Rarely C5-C6 and C2-C3 Levels

Overlapping series revealed that most CSC occurred, in descending order mostly at the C7-T1 followed by the C1-C2 level, and only rarely at the C5-C6, and C2-C3 levels [Table 1].[4,5,9,10,11,12,15] CSC predominantly are found at C7-T1 succeeded by the C1-C2 level (i.e., Epstein 1 case C7-T1 (1993), Peng et al. (2025) 79 cases mostly at C7-T1 or C1-C2, Lunardi et al. (1999) 22 cases at various levels/their specific case at C7-T1, Lyons et al. (2011) 35 various cases, and Yang et al. (2023) 3 cases at C7-T1).[5,9,10,12,15] Although Corredor et al. (2015) observed that most CSC occur at C7-T1, they presented 6 cases of C5-C6 CSC from the literature and added their own 7th C5-C6 case.[4] Mak et al., (2019) also operated on 7 patients with C7-T1 CSC, but additionally presented one case at the C2-C3 level.[11]

Classical Clinical Presentation of C7-T1 and C1-C2 CSC: Pain, Cervical Radiculopathy and/or Myelopathy

Multiple studies reported that patients with C1-C2 CSC exhibited myelopathy alone (i.e. cannot demonstrate radiculopathy due to location), while those with C7-T1 CSC could present with combinations of unilateral radiculopathy and/or myelopathy [Table 1].[3,4,7,11,12,15] Chibbaro et al. (2020) 4 patients with C1-C2 CSC, and Peng et al. (2025) C1-C2 CSC patients all had myelopathy.[3,12] Although many patients with C7-T1 CSC have radiculopathy (i.e., Yang et al. (2023) 3 C7-T1 CSC, and Peng et al. (2025) C7-T1 CSC subset of 79 patients), Kido et al. had 4 C7-T1 patients who exhibited myelopathy alone.[7,12,15] Further, Corredor et al. (2015) C5-C6 CSC (i.e., 3.3 x 5.3 mm lesion) patient and 6 others with C5-C6 CSC from the literature all had radiculopathy.[4] Additionally, Mak et al., (2019) had 1 patient with a CSC at C1-C2 with myelopathy, and 5 patients with CSC at C7-T1 levels with radiculopathy only.[11]

Rare Decompressions Alone vs. More Patients Requiring Decompession/Instrumented Fusions for Unstable Typically Transitional Level C1-C2 and C7-T1 CSC

Rarely, CSC patients may be managed with decompressions alone whereas most with transitional C1-C2 and C7-T1 level lesions require decompressions with instrumented fusions [Table 1].[2,4,5,9,10,15] Two case studies, respectively involving 47 and 48-year-old patients with C7-T1 CSC, with adequate preservation of the ipsilateral facet joint and stability on dynamic X-rays, were successfully managed with laminectomy alone.[5,9] Alternatively, for Bydon et al. (2014) 3 CSC at C1-C2 (17.6%) and 14 in the subaxial cervical spine (82.3%), only one patient was successfully managed with a decompression alone, while 16 patients required additional fusions.[2] Patients with CSC from three other series were also effectively treated with decompressions/instrumented fusions (i.e., Lyons et al. (2011) 3 cases, Corredor et al. (2015) 7 cases, and Yang et al. (2023) 3 cases (C7-T1)). Note, fusions were largely performed for CSC occurring at transitional levels due to either documented or anticipated future instability.[4,10,15]

Unlikely Comparable Efficacy of Decompressions vs. Decompression/Fusion for CSC Including for C1-C2 and C7-T1 Transitional Levels

It is unlikely, given sufficiently powered, complete, and accurate data, that 3 CSC studies could demonstrate the comparable efficacy of decompressions alone vs. decompressions/fusions for CSC (i.e., even including transitional C1-C2 and C7-T1 level lesions) [Table 1].[2,7,12] When Bydon et al. (2014) reviewed over 54 articles involving 101 CSC patients (i.e. 41 cases (40.6%) at C1-C2 vs. 60 cases (59.4%) of subaxial CSC), they found, irrespective of the CSC level, comparable outcomes following 52 decompressions alone vs. 49 decompressions with fusions.[2] In Kido et al., (2023) review, 75 of 139 CSC at the T7-T1 level were successfully treated with hemilaminectomy alone (47 patients) vs. laminectomy/ posterior fusion (28 patients).[7] Peng et al. (2025) similarly found that for 79 CSC, mostly located at the C1-C2 or C7-T1 levels, that surgical decompressions vs. decompressions accompanied by fusions were equally successful.[12]

Increased Risks of Minimally Invasive Surgery (MIS) for Lumbar Synovial Cysts Should be Equally Applicable to Cervical Synovial Cysts

Epstein’s 2024 review of MIS vs. open procedures performed for lumbar synovial cysts (LSC) documented more adverse events (AE) with MIS LSC surgery (i.e., cerebrospinal fluid leaks/dural tears, traction-related neural injuries, hematomas, and adhesive arachnoiditis); typically, these AE were under-reported in the literature [Table 1].[6] Comparable surgical errors would likely also occur with minimally invasive surgery (MIS) performed for CSC. More likely than not, there is great safety when operating on CSF utilizing open surgical procedures (i.e., better visualization/safer maneuverability to remove/decompress tough, thick, tenacious, and often calcified CSC densely adherent to the underlying dura). Further, three underpowered reviews of different MIS surgical decompressive techniques with/without fusions performed for removing CSC did not sufficiently counter the superiority of open surgery for CSC (i.e., Ruetten et al. (2018) 7 subaxial “full endoscopic uniportal” procedures/no fusions, Chibbaro et al. (2020) 4 C1-C2 CSC “posterolateral facet sparing intradural approaches/no fusion”, and Sanchez et al. (2021) 13 CSC and 22 LSC “posterior tubular microsurgical decompressions” with 37.1% primary and 3 secondary fusions) [Table 1].[3,13,14]

MR: “Gold Standard” for Diagnosing CSC While CT Rarely Shows Calcified Capsules

MR scans are the “gold standard” for diagnosing CSC, best demonstrating CSC causing spinal cord and/or nerve root compression. Additionally, they may demonstrate other classical features of CSS including; hypo/isointense T1WI, hyperintense T2 WI, with often marked enhancement of cyst capsules [Table 1].[4,9,10,11] Additionally, CT studies will occasionally demonstrate calcification of the CSC capsular rim, but usually fail to provide any “useful” cord and/or nerve root definition.[9] When Corredor et al. (2015) presented their single case of a C5-C6 CSC, the MR demonstrated an extradural lenticular cystic lesion measuring 3.3 AP (Anterior/Posterior) x 5.3 mm TD (Transverse Diameter) causing cord/C6 root compression.[4] MR scans in Mak et al., (2019) 6 patients with 1 CSC at C2-C3 and 5 at C7-T1 showed cysts were hypointense on T1 WI, and hyperintense on T2 WI, while just one showed capsular enhancement; further 2 CSC caused root while 4 contributed to cord compression (i.e. measured an average of 5.3 mm in AP x 7.3 MM in TD.[11]

Histological Confirmation of CSC

Several studies obtained histological confirmation of CSC [Table 1].[3,4] These included Corredor et al. (2015) CSC at the C5-C6 level,[4] and Chibbaro et al. (2020)[4] CSC at the C1-C2 level (i.e., all sent for histological diagnosis).[3,4] Multiple other studies similarly documented that operative specimens were submitted to pathology for histological confirmation of benign CSC.

Risk of Acute or Subacute Spontaneous Hemorrhages into CSC

Two studies demonstrated the risk for subacute and/or spontaneous hemorrhages into CSC precipitating acute myelopathic/radiculopathic deficits often warranting immediate and/or timely surgical intervention [Table 1].[1,8] Attwell et al. (2014) discussed the acute onset of cord compromise in patients experiencing spontaneous hemorrhages into CSC.[1] Additionally, Lee et al. (2024) presented a 58-year-old male with the subacute onset over 5 months of tingling/ numbness extending from the shoulder into the right arm accompanied by chest pain attributed to the subactue spontaneous hemorrhage into a right C7-T1 CSC; following surgical resection of the CSC, these symptoms resolved.[8]

CONCLUSION

Most patients with CSC at the C7-T1, C1-2, C5-C6, and C2-3 levels (i.e., in descending order) best documented on MR studies, present with radiculopathy and/or myelopathy. Operative intervention rarely just warrants decompressions alone, but more likely requires fusions to address documented or future anticipated instability particularly for transitional level (i.e., C7-T1 and C1-C2) CSC.
