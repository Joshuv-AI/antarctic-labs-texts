A study of serum brain-derived neurotrophic factor level in individuals with obsessive compulsive disorder and their first-degree relatives as compared to the healthy population
Authors: Shaily Mina, Rupam Dhiman, Prakamya Singal, Sukanya Gangopadhyay, Pankaj Verma, Shivani Kathuria
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:The nosological tradition in psychiatry defines diagnostic criteria for disorders based on expert consensus than objective biological markers reflecting underlying neurobiological correlates. Endophenotypes have been researched as heritable biological markers that can be quantified and defined to represent intermediate measures of a psychiatric illness. In obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), various putative biomarkers such as neuropsychological, neurophysiological, neuroradiological, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), etc., have been explored.Aim:The study aimed to compare levels of serum BDNF in individuals with OCD and their unaffected first-degree relatives (FDR) with healthy controls (HC).Methods:This cross-sectional study compared serum BDNF levels in medication-free/naive individuals with OCD (n = 30) to their FDR (n = 30) and age-sex matched HC (n = 30). Intergroup comparison was done using analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post-hoc Tukey’s test. Correlation analysis was conducted to find the relationship of sociodemographic and clinical correlates to serum BDNF as well as dimensional subtypes of OCD.Results:No significant difference in BDNF levels was observed between OCD and HC (P = 0.13) but a significantly higher level was found in the FDR group compared to age-sex matched HC (P = 0.02).Conclusion:BDNF levels may have a complex interplay influencing the genetic inheritance and clinical manifestations of OCD. Further research is required before considering it a viable biomarker.

INTRODUCTION

The lifetime prevalence of obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is currently estimated at 2 − 3%, and the prevalence in their first-degree relatives (FDR) is estimated to range between 10 and 11%.[1] The high lifetime prevalence and adolescence onset of illness add significantly to the years of disability and distress to an individual’s life as the disorder is a relapsing-remitting disease.[2] There is evidence of strong family history of the disorder among adults with heritability estimates ranging between 27% and 47%.[3] The studies of OCD probands and their relatives cumulatively provide strong evidence that some but possibly not all forms of OCD are familial.[4] Thus, there is compelling desire to find a common etiological factor for OCD. Unfortunately, no consistent genetic factor has been found to achieve genome-wide significance.

The nosological tradition in psychiatry defines diagnostic criteria for disorders based on expert consensus rather than through objective biological markers founded on underlying biological correlates. This leaves room for subjective interpretation of symptoms, clubbing of etiologically dissimilar disorders together under one category based on clinical presentation and lack of clarity in explaining the differences in treatment response within various symptom dimensions of OCD. In order to address these issues, the US National Institute of Mental Health launched the Research Domain Criteria initiative that focuses on integrating genomics and circuits into behavior and clinical presentation. Currently, diagnostic research is shifting towards the identification of biomarkers for better understanding of psychiatric illness.

An endophenotype is a variable that lies somewhere in a causal chain linking an etiologic factor and psychiatric illnesses. If the disorder is heritable, then so is the endophenotype, and they have a shared genetic basis, as indicated, for example, by genetic correlations in twin studies. It can be a risk factor for a disorder even if the person is not currently symptomatic. They are quantifiable biological markers that co-segregate with a psychiatric illness and found in non-affected family members at a higher rate than the general population.[3] OCD is likely to arise from multiple causal chains, with each having their own endophenotypes. A number of studies have explored such as endophenotypes for OCD including neuropsychological and cognitive markers (Go/No-Go test, Wisconsin card sorting test, etc.),[5] neuroradiological (structural and functional abnormalities in cortico-striatal-thalamic circuit), etc.[6] The role of candidate genes has been explored to study the genetic underpinnings of the disease.[78]

Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), a protein molecule expressed in neurons and glial cells, has been found to be altered in multiple psychiatric disorders. It plays a regulatory role in the neural functions involved in stress response like the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, glucocorticoid regulation, etc., and influences neural plasticity in the brain.[9] A meta-analysis found an association between low BDNF levels and anxiety disorders; however, the association was largely owing to OCD.[10] Wang and colleagues reported lower levels among both drug naïve and medicated OCD groups compared to controls but no significant difference was reported between the two subsets of OCD.[11] However, studies are reporting contrary findings of higher BDNF levels in OCD patients in comparison with healthy controls (HC).[1213] The evidence linking BDNF with anxiety like clinical presentation is not clear and riddled with conflicting reports from animal and human studies.[14] Nevertheless, changes in BDNF levels seem to be involved in the pathogenesis of OCD and could serve as a potential biomarker. Several studies have reported an association of BDNF Val66Met polymorphism to OCD, and a recent meta-analysis concludes that female Val carriers of the gene may have an increased susceptibility to OCD.[7] Since the gene regulates BDNF protein secretion and thereby its serum levels, the BDNF levels in biological relatives particularly FDR of OCD can further reflect the potential endophenotypic role that it may subserve.

Very few studies have investigated BDNF levels in unaffected FDR of OCD patients, our study aimed to compare the levels of peripheral serum BDNF among OCD patients and their FDR with comparable HC in the Indian population. We hypothesized that patients who have been diagnosed with OCD would have lower BDNF levels compared with HC and that FDR of patients with OCD would express intermediary BDNF levels. We also assessed the sociodemographic and clinical correlates of serum BDNF levels in individuals with OCD.

METHODOLOGY

Study participants

Using convenience-based sampling, thirty individuals with OCD with their FDR were included in this cross-sectional study along with 30 age and gender-matched HC at the outpatient department (OPD) of psychiatry of a tertiary care center in north India. Individuals aged more than 18 years old fulfilling the diagnostic criteria of OCD as per diagnostic and statistical manual (DSM) 5 and not been on any pharmacological or non-pharmacological treatment for OCD in the previous 3 months were included. FDR of individuals with OCD were included if they were related to the patient in the capacity of a sibling, parent, or child and were aged more than 18 years old. HC were recruited from amongst the hospital staff and attendants or non-biological relatives of patients attending the hospital and were matched to individuals with OCD. Participants were excluded if they had a significant history of any psychiatric illness in FDR or HC groups. Participants were also excluded if there was any unstable medical illness. All evaluations were conducted by a psychiatrist using clinical judgment. All participants were recruited after providing written informed consent.

Study instruments

A semi-structured case record sheet was utilized to collect data on sociodemographic and clinical details.

Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS): It is the gold standard measure in the assessment of OCD.[15] It is a clinician-administered 10-item rating scale with scoring for both obsessions and compulsions based on the five-point Likert scale. Scores range from 0 (no symptoms) to 4 (severe symptoms) and the total score ranges from 0 to 40. The scale is reliable (test–retest stability r = 0.85) with adequate consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.91). Dimensional Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (DY-BOCS): It is a self-report checklist comprising six symptoms’ dimensions: aggression, sexual/religious, symmetry/ordering/arrangement, contamination/washing, hoarding, and miscellaneous. Severity for each dimension is measured on three ordinal scales with six anchor points focusing on symptom frequency (0–5), amount of distress caused (0–5), and degree of interference with functioning (0–5) during the previous week. The individual’s overall level of current impairment due to OC symptoms is scored on a scale ranging from “none” (0 points) to “severe” (15 points). The total global score is obtained by combining the sum of the global severity scores for frequency, distress, and interference and the impairment score, yielding a maximum total global severity score of 30. The scale has excellent psychometric properties with high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha range = 0.89-0.99).[16]

Serum BDNF: Blood sample (2 ml) was collected after assessment in anticoagulant-free tubes and serum was separated. Serum was stored at -20°C until analysis. Serum BDNF levels were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) technique as per the manufacturer’s instructions (Elabscience BDNF test Kit Catalog No: E-EL-H0010 Product size: 96T/48T/24T/96T*5). After the color reaction with the substrate, the optical density was recorded at 450-nm wavelength with an automated ELISA reader. The absorbance at 450 nm was converted to picograms per milliliter (pg/mL) for BDNF.[17]

Statistical analysis

The analysis was done using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS version 21.0). Categorical variables are presented in frequency (percentage), and continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR). Intergroup comparison of categorical variables was done using Chi-square test and continuous variables were done using analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post-hoc Tukey’s honest significant difference test mentioning the mean difference (MD) and its 95% confidence interval (95%CI) between the groups. For correlation between continuous variables, Pearson’s correlation was used and for correlation between continuous and categorical variables, point-biserial correlation was used. A “p” value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical aspects

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and permission was obtained from the institutional ethics committee.

RESULTS

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

Thirty participants in each group of OCD, unaffected FDR, and HC were included in the study. The mean age was similar amongst the groups {OCD: 27 ± 9.62 years, FDR: 30.9 ± 15.39 years, HC: 27.23 ± 9.62 years, F(2,87) =1.02, P = 0.36}, and all the groups had similar gender distribution with 17 (56.7%) males in each group (P = 1.00).

The median illness duration was 24 (IQR = 27) months with a wide variety of obsessive-compulsive symptoms. The mean scores of total, obsessive subscale, and compulsive subscale of Y-BOCS were 26.7 ± 7.39, 13.9 ± 3.87, and 12.8 ± 5.44. The grading of illness severity based on the total Y-BOCS score suggested that all participants had moderate to extremely severe illness {Moderate (16-23): n = 13 (43.33%); Severe (24-31): n = 6 (20%); Extremely severe (32-40): n = 11 (36.67%)}. Almost half of the patients had previously taken treatment for OCD (46.7%). The mean scores for the dimensions of frequency, distress, and interference were 3.83 ± 0.98, 3.63 ± 0.99, and 3.93 ± 0.91, respectively, and the mean scores for impairment were 10.70 ± 2.52. The mean total global severity score for DY-BOCS was 22.1 ± 4.36.

Serum BDNF levels

The mean serum BDNF levels were found to be significantly different among the three groups {OCD: 1381.13 ± 332.27 pg/mL; FDR: 1522.23 ± 273.04 pg/mL; HC: 1328.47 ± 230.99 pg/mL; F(2,87)=3.79, P = 0.02}. On further analysis with post-hoc Tukey’s honest significance difference test, FDR were found to have a higher BDNF level than HC {MD = 193.76, 95%CI = 20.24 to 367.29, P = 0.02) while no statistical difference was seen between FDR and OCD patients (MD = 141.10, 95%CI = -32.42 to 314.62, P = 0.13) or OCD and HC patients (MD = 52.67, 95%CI = -120.86 to 226.19, P = 0.75) as illustrated in Figure 1.

On comparing medication-free OCD patients to drug-naive individuals, the serum BDNF levels were comparable (1362.31 ± 315.91 pg/mL vs 1402.64 ± 360.82 pg/mL, respectively, P = 0.74). Similarly, there was no difference among the genders for serum BDNF levels in OCD patients (male: 1441.71 ± 294.71 pg/mL, female: 1301.92 ± 372.85 pg/mL, P = 0.26).

Correlation between serum BDNF levels and clinical and demographic characteristics

No significant correlation was found between the serum BDNF levels to age (r = 0.06, P = 0.54) or gender (r = -0.06, P = 0.57) in the overall sample.

No significant correlation was found between the serum BDNF levels to the patient’s demographic and clinical profile including age (r = 0.01, P = 0.95), gender (r = -0.21, P = 0.26), illness duration (r = 0.03, P = 0.85), history of past treatment (r = -0.06, P = 0.74), Y-BOCS total score (r = 0.22, P = 0.23), Y-BOCS compulsion subscale score (r = 0.06, P = 0.75), and illness severity (r = 0.26, P = 0.16). Serum BDNF was marginally correlated to Y-BOCS obsession subscale scores (r = 0.34, P = 0.05) and total global severity score of DY-BOCS (r = 0.34, P = 0.06) as illustrated in Figure 2. The serum BDNF levels were not significantly different among various dimensional subtypes of OCD (r = -0.18, P = 0.33). BDNF levels were found to be having significant mild positive correlation with Y-BOCS obsession subscale item of time occupied by obsessive thoughts (r = 0.37, P = 0.04) and interference with obsessive thoughts (r = 0.37, P = 0.04).

DISCUSSION

In the present study, while a significant difference was observed in the serum BDNF levels between the three groups, no difference was observed between patients with OCD and HC. This is in contrast to previous studies which reported significant differences between the two groups. However, note must be made of the conflicting reports in the literature with few studies reporting lower levels of peripheral BDNF levels in OCD patients compared to HC[11181920] while few reporting higher levels.[1213] Reason for the discrepancy in the BDNF levels in literature could be because most of the studies failed to control for confounding factors (age, sex, co-morbid medical, or psychiatric illness) or measured BDNF levels in the plasma, which tends to have lower levels compared to serum due to differences in their constitution. The current study did control for age, gender, and co-morbidity amongst the groups and measured serum BDNF levels overcoming few of these confounding factors present in previous research.

Various hypotheses have been suggested to explain higher BDNF levels in OCD attributing it to be mediated through damaging effects of the HPA axis hyperactivity on brain tissue, through glutamatergic hyperactivity seen in OCD patients or via dysfunction in the neurotrophin expression.[1321] Studies have also suggested that depressive patients have higher levels of BDNF.[22] It is possible that the presence of mood symptoms in the current sample resulted in not observing the lower levels as reported in previous literature. However, mood was assessed only for the presence of depressive disorder through clinical interview rather than using any specific scale, thus limiting this possible explanation. The effects of medication taken by OCD patients should also be taken into account, because studies on depression have indicated that the serum BDNF levels could be altered with a good response to the drug treatment.[22] About half of our OCD patients were medication-free, whereas the patients enrolled in the previous studies were mostly drug-naïve. Length of drug treatment has been reported to be positively associated with peripheral BDNF levels in the drug-treated patients.[11] However, on comparing medication-free to drug-naive OCD individuals, we did not find any difference in serum BDNF levels between the groups similar to few previous studies.[23] The presence of insight into symptoms can also impact BDNF levels. Manarte and colleagues found that OCD patients with poor insight had lower BDNF values than those of the good-insight and control groups while there was no difference in levels amongst good-insight and control groups.

In patients with OCD, we found no significant association between peripheral BDNF levels and age, gender, illness severity, previous medication use, or illness duration. Previous literature also reports of similar findings in OCD patients.[11121824]

We did not found any significant association between symptom dimensions and BDNF levels unlike previous literature.[19] However, the small sample size in the current study with too many categories of dimensional variants in the sample could have contributed to this finding.

The present study found significant positive mild correlation between BDNF levels and time occupied by obsessive thoughts as well as interference with obsessive thoughts. However, it should be noted that BDNF is a growth factor that plays many critical functions in the central nervous system and may be associated with not just a variety of neuropsychiatric illnesses including depression, dementia, and neurodegenerative disorders but also with other conditions such as age, smoking, drinking, fasting, time of sampling, physical activity, and living in an urban environment.[1025] The possibility cannot be omitted in the current study that some of these factors may have been responsible for this association leading to spurious findings. We could not find previous literature specifically reporting such a finding.

The heritability of a disorder is supposedly linked with its endophenotype sharing the genetic basis. Since they can exist as a surrogate marker even if the person is not currently symptomatic or in an non-affected family member, their potential role as an identifiable risk factor becomes much more important.[3] Studies have postulated BDNF gene as a potential endophenotype in OCD.[7824] The BDNF protein secretion that is reflected by serum levels is regulated by this gene and thus can be indicative of the gene indirectly serving as biomarker. The present study found significantly higher levels of serum BDNF in the FDR compared to HC group. This can reflect the potential endophenotypic role that it may subserve. However, the wide CI may be affecting the findings. Similar results have been replicated in unaffected FDR of other psychiatric disorders like bipolar disorder and depression.[262728] The current findings also add to the previous speculations made in these studies that a compensatory or protective effect of elevated BDNF levels could exist in individuals at high risk of OCD.[12] The current findings are merely an indication towards conducting more studies to further elucidate the phenomenon before considering it as a viable entity for portraying as an endophenotype.

If the disorder is heritable, then so is the endophenotype, and they have a shared genetic basis, as indicated, for example, by genetic correlations in twin studies. It can be a risk factor for a disorder even if the person is not currently symptomatic. They are quantifiable biological markers that co-segregate with a psychiatric illness and are found in non-affected family members at a higher rate than the general population.[3]

The current study is the first study comparing the peripheral BDNF levels in OCD patients with their unaffected FDR and HC in the Indian population. Our results may be viewed in light of well-documented inconsistencies in BDNF gene as well as peripheral BDNF level distributions across diverse populations and conditions.[1025] We assessed serum levels of BDNF which addresses constitutional differences in separate physiological compartments. Another strength of the study is control for various confounding factors like age, gender, and co-morbidities although the sample size is small. The effect of treatment on BDNF levels was also assessed as the sample comprised of medication-free as well as drug-naive population. However, no significant effect of past treatment was found on serum BDNF levels. Further, studying unmedicated patients can offer the advantage of observing the true picture of patients presenting to psychiatry clinics without excluding the enrolment of patients with more severe symptomatology.

The study is limited in its generalizability due to the small sample size, too much-varied symptom dimensions, and being from a single centre. Nevertheless, it lays the ground for future exploration into the role of BDNF as a predictive marker in high-risk individuals with a family history of the disorder. Lastly, an analysis of the single nucleotide polymorphisms of BDNF in specific brain structures was not possible, which could add to the body of evidence supporting a protective role of BDNF signaling pathways.

CONCLUSION

The findings in the current study did not support our hypotheses but rather contrasted with prior findings of decreased BDNF levels in patients with OCD. The presence of higher BDNF levels in FDR adds to a more complex understanding of the possible influence of neurotrophins on the genetic inheritance and clinical manifestations of OCD. Further studies are needed to elucidate the association between BDNF gene expression and phenotypic expression, familial loading, and therapeutic response in a larger subset of OCD population of Indian origin.
