Narrative exposure therapy for the treatment of trauma-related symptoms among adolescent survivors of sex trafficking: A pilot study
Authors: Sheeba Shamsudeen, Preeti Gupta, Neha Sayeed, Sanjay K. Munda
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:India is home to 14 million survivors of human trafficking, with most being trafficked for sexual exploitation. Trafficking constitutes crimes that violate the rights of survivors, and despite its psychological consequences, there is little evidence-based guidance to meet the needs of these individuals.Aim:The current study aimed to examine the outcome of narrative exposure therapy (KIDNET) among adolescent survivors of sex trafficking in improving trauma-related symptoms, along with psychological distress, dissociation, depression, and anxiety.Methods:A pre-post study design was used with 20 adolescent female survivors of trafficking aged 13 to 17 years, with ten participants in the KIDNET group and ten participants in a waitlist control group. Trafficking Victim Identification Tool and Standard Progressive Matrices were administered as screening measures. The Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale for Children and Adolescents, Kessler’s Psychological Distress Scale, Shutdown Dissociation Scale, Patient Health Questionnaire, and Beck Anxiety Inventory were administered as outcome measures. Fourteen sessions of KIDNET were conducted for the clinical group, while sessions were conducted for the control group post delivery of intervention for the clinical group.Results:Findings suggest an overall improvement in the severity of trauma-related symptoms in the KIDNET group, with significant improvement in psychological distress, depression, and anxiety.Conclusion:Results indicate that KIDNET may be a promising and acceptable treatment for adolescent survivors of sex trafficking, and this intervention module may be safely delivered in further randomised controlled trials to ensure that the holistic needs of this vulnerable group are appropriately addressed.

INTRODUCTION

Trafficking of human beings, especially children and adolescents, is a form of modern-day slavery requiring an all-inclusive approach to address the complexity of the problem.[1] The “United Nations Protocol to Prevent, Suppress and Punish Trafficking in Persons” defines trafficking as the “recruitment, transportation, transfer, harbouring or receipt of persons, by means of threat or the use of force or other forms of coercion, of abduction, of fraud, of deception, of the abuse of power or of a position of vulnerability or of giving or receiving of payments or benefits to achieve the consent of a person having control over another person, for the purpose of exploitation. Exploitation shall include, at a minimum, the exploitation of the prostitution of others or other forms of sexual exploitation, forced labour or services, slavery or practices like slavery, servitude or the removal of organs”.[2]

Trafficked individuals typically experience multiple traumatic events, including physical abuse, sexual abuse, and threats to themselves and their families. Usually involving a multitude of abuse and exploitation, sex trafficking results in devastating mental health problems for child and adolescent survivors and may include clinically diagnosable illnesses and personal mental health struggles.[3] A significant concern for children forced into trafficking is the impact of these experiences on their overall development, especially the harm caused to their psychological and emotional development. These experiences increase an adolescent’s risk for mental health issues such as PTSD, depression, anxiety, guilt, shame, anxiety, and loss of self-esteem.[4] Additionally, individuals who have survived trafficking have shown increased rates of anxiety and stress disorders, dissociation, personality disorders, suicidal ideation, poor interpersonal relationships, and somatic symptoms.[5]

There is currently only a small body of published research on the health consequences of human trafficking, with minimal research on its mental health consequences. Trauma-informed services are a crucial part of the recovery of survivors as treatment is guided by trauma-informed care, which plays an essential role in accommodating the vulnerability of trauma survivors.[6] Trauma-focused approaches have in common that they focus on traumatic memories using cognitive or exposure-based interventions. Independent evaluations based on rationale and effect have indicated that narrative exposure therapy (NET) is probably advantageous for the treatment of traumatised individuals in comparison to other trauma-focused approaches.[7]

Because of the trauma memories, survivors may suffer from various trauma-related symptoms, leading to difficulties in daily functioning and social life. Symptom reduction and restoration of functionality may require the traumatic experiences to be oriented in the appropriate context of time and place in their autobiography, that is, trauma-focused remembering.[8] NET is based on the principles of prolonged exposure therapy (activation and modification of fear networks), autobiographical memory, and testimony therapy. It aims to complete the survivor’s autobiography by linking hot (sensory-bodily-emotional) and cold (episodic) memories of events, contextualizing the experience of their significant traumas. It has been built on the theory of dual representation of traumatic memories. In NET, the individual constructs a chronological narrative of their life story focusing on the traumatic experiences, while fragmented reports of these experiences are transformed into a coherent narrative.[7] During this process, painful emotions are analyzed, facilitating emotional recovery from the trauma. Emotional processing theories support the use of exposure therapy and suggest that habituation of emotional responses is the mechanism that improves trauma-related symptoms.[9]

The existing long-term treatments for trauma-related outcomes have difficulties of access, high rates of dropouts, financial limitations, and displacement during reintegrating back into society.[10] To meet the needs of this target group, a short-term standardized therapy is required, where there is scarce access to human and monetary resources.[11] Considering this, short-term treatments can help decrease immediate symptoms and may be more accessible for survivors of human trafficking. Additionally, a brief treatment model minimizes therapy having to end prematurely. NET and KIDNET apply in varied cultures since they are based on the oral narrative tradition, a common element in many cultures.[8] KIDNET allows the survivor and therapist to compare the atrocities against objective, agreed-upon principles of how human beings should behave toward each other. It has emerged as an evidence-based treatment for patients in low- and middle-income countries exposed to multiple traumatic stressors. It has been most used for multiple traumas resulting from domestic, sexual, or organised violence or abuse, war, or natural disasters, therefore making it an appropriate intervention in this study for survivors of sex trafficking.[12]

Thus, the aim of the present study was to assess the outcomes of Narrative Exposure Therapy for Traumatized Children and Adolescents (KIDNET) on trauma symptomatology, psychological distress, dissociation, depression, and anxiety among adolescent female survivors of sex trafficking. The objectives of the study were to assess and compare trauma symptomatology, psychological distress, dissociation, depression, and anxiety from baseline to postassessment among adolescent female survivors of human trafficking receiving KIDNET and with those in a waitlist control group. The study also aimed to attain feedback from the participants regarding their experiences in therapy.

METHODS

The Institutional Ethics Committee at the Central Institute of Psychiatry, Ranchi, approved the study (IEC/CIP/2020-21/632, dt. 29/09/2021). The study was registered and approved by the Clinical Trials Registry – India (ICMR – NIMS) on 01/11/2021, with CTRI Reg. No. CTRI/2021/11/037706. Permission was sought from the Child Welfare Committee and the Integrated Child Protection Scheme, Ranchi, to conduct the study. The present research is a prospective study with a pre-post intervention design and was conducted at a shelter home for girls in Ranchi run by the Jharkhand State Resource Centre under the Department of Women, Child Development and Social Security, Govt. of Jharkhand. Purposive sampling technique was used according to specific inclusion and exclusion criteria. Participants were randomly allocated to either group using a single random-number sequence. The numbers were written in a series of sealed envelopes, and the envelope for each participant was opened immediately before commencement of allotment of participants to either of the two groups after the administration of the screening assessments. Additionally, in this present study, the participants were enquired about their experiences in therapy using a feedback-informed method which routinely sought feedback regarding therapeutic effects and participant outcomes.

Inclusion criteria included adolescents aged between 13 and 17 years, female, rescued from trafficking within the past 3 months and who are presently residing in the shelter homes, grade III intellectual capacity average (50th percentile) and above; they met the screening criteria for trafficking according to the Trafficking Victim Identification Tool, a score >22 on the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale, consent from a case worker/agency staff, and assent for the study from the adolescents. Participants with a history of organic disorders, substance dependence and harmful use, chronic or other significant medical or neurological conditions, and physical or sensory disability were excluded from the study.

Initially, 26 participants were selected, out of which two did not fulfil the inclusion and exclusion criteria due to below-average intelligence, and four did not complete the therapy program. Finally, 20 participants were selected for the present study. Random allocation was done with ten participants in the KIDNET group and ten in the waitlist control group. Assessments were conducted before and post intervention for the KIDNET group. In contrast, the measures were performed simultaneously for the waitlist control group but received the intervention after conducting postintervention assessments.

The validated Hindi-translated versions of the self-report measures of Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) and Beck’s Anxiety Inventory (BAI) were used, while the Kessler’s Psychological Distress Scale (K10) was translated in Hindi language. For this purpose, the scale was translated using the forward and backward translation methods with the original scale in English first translated in Hindi by two native speakers separately, that is, forward translation. The translated version was then back-translated, that is, reverse translation, into the original language by two separate translators with the same qualifications and characteristics as before. After assessing both forward- and reverse-translated versions, a final version of the scale was obtained. All the language experts who translated the scale have a doctorate in the respective language and are the holders of a permanent academic position in government academic institutions. The feedback-informed method was used at the end of each session to assess therapeutic progress and participant outcomes. This was conducted using an interview method to assess progress of participants and to monitor the treatment response by eliciting patient-reported outcomes over the course of therapy. The verbatim of the patients was recorded and categorized for each phase of treatment as outlined in Figure 1. All assessments and interventions were conducted and delivered by the first author, a licensed clinical psychologist, who was trained in NET.

Assessment tools

Sociodemographic and Clinical Data Sheet: A semistructured data sheet was drafted for this study, containing all sociodemographic and clinical details of the participants relevant to the study.

Trafficking Victim Identification Tool (TVIT): A semistructured interview tool, the TVIT contained items that addressed the participants’ human trafficking experiences. It was used to identify trafficking survivors and was developed to produce a validated screening tool for identifying trafficked persons and their experiences.[13]

Standard Progressive Matrices (SPM): This test assesses intelligence in adults and children using abstract reasoning tasks. It is meant for individuals aged 8–65 and focuses on evaluating an individual’s clear-thinking ability using both spatial and logical skills.[14]

Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale – Child/Adolescent Version (CAPS-CA): It is a semistructured tool that allows therapists to establish a current diagnosis of PTSD, make a prognosis of the evolution of PTSD, and assess the symptoms of PTSD. It determines trauma exposure and assesses the frequency and intensity of the 20 DSM-V-defined symptoms of PTSD.[15]

Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10): The K10 is a widely used self-report measure of psychological distress. It is intended to yield a global measure of the distress a person experienced in the most recent 4-week period.[16]

Shutdown Dissociation Scale (Shut-D): This is a 13-item measure of the physical signs of dissociation. It was included because it indicates the potential likelihood of dissociation and common signs and symptoms that must be managed and addressed during therapy.[17]

Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9): This measure has been used for screening, diagnosing, and measuring the severity of depression. It incorporates DSM–IV diagnostic criteria for depressive symptoms into a brief self-report tool. This tool is appropriate for individuals aged 12 and older.[18]

Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI): It is a self-report inventory to assess anxiety levels in adults and adolescents. It has also been used for individuals who have experienced traumas. The measure is usually used with individuals aged 17 to 80 years, but this measure has been used in peer-reviewed studies with younger adolescents aged 12 and older.[19]

Intervention

NET was initially conceived as a short-term treatment for traumatic stress disorders resulting from organized or family violence – multiple and continuous traumatic stressors. Its modified version, KIDNET, is specially designed for traumatized children and adolescents as, in children and adolescents, the memories of traumatic events may be encoded differently, partly at a somatic and sensory level and partly at an emotional level, as opposed to the verbally mediated level. KIDNET consists of 90–120 minutes duration of each session depending on the number of traumatic events experienced by the child/adolescent.

For the current study, KIDNET was delivered in 14 sessions, lasting 90 to 120 minutes over 5 weeks with 2–3 weekly sessions. NET is based on the theory of fear/trauma network and includes principles of CBT and testimony therapy. With its twofold focus, one goal of KIDNET is to reduce trauma symptoms, while the other is to reconstruct the autobiographical memory framework with a chronological narrative. KIDNET focuses on completing and integrating the initial fragments of the traumatic events, including the incident’s sensory, physiological, emotional, and cognitive experiences. KIDNET focuses on both these methods – habituation of emotional responses to reminders of traumatic events and constructing a narrative of traumatic events and their consequences.[20]

In KIDNET, an individual goes through their autobiography, where both traumatic and positive events are identified and understood within the broader context where they occurred. Therapy carries out a detailed exploration and exposure to traumatic events experienced chronologically. The exploration of these traumatic events uses different techniques, such as drawings and role-playing as children and adolescents are not as verbally expressive as adults and may require more time to establish trust.

The nature of the exposure to traumatic events in KIDNET[8] is different to that used in CBT and other forms of trauma-informed therapies. Here, the therapist guides exposure through the event in a more directive manner than is usual. Close attention is paid to the emotional, physiological, and behavioral responses that the survivor experiences during the exposure. These are then reflected to them through the renarration of the traumatic events while maintaining a dual focus on present and past experiences, resembling an “exposure conversation”.

Physical experiences occurring peritraumatically and during exposure are addressed with emphasis on cognitions, meanings, emotions, and sensory experiences. Unlike other trauma-based interventions, affect regulation is managed while the exposure occurs and not in a preliminary stabilisation phase of treatment. The contextual information is integrated into the trauma memory during exposure to complete the autobiographical memory.[8]

A detailed account of the individual’s autobiography is recorded by the therapist and is given to the adolescent when therapy is terminated. It provides a written testimony of the survivor’s experiences, including both positive and traumatic events within the context in which they occurred. Thus, the therapist bears witness to the adolescent’s story and facilitates them to express the events in the context of the survivor’s life story narrative. Statistical analysis

The Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) v. 25 was used for analyzing the data. Descriptive statistics were used to investigate the sociodemographic variables, whereas two-way ANOVA assessed and compared symptom improvement from baseline to postintervention assessment in the two groups. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Mental health outcomes

The findings of this study showed there was a significant difference in PTSD symptoms from baseline to postassessment among the KIDNET group as compared to the waitlist control group. Upon assessment of the overall mean PTSD scores [Table 2], scores in the KIDNET group reduced significantly as compared to the waitlist control group from baseline to postassessment (F = 12.47, P = 0.002), showing significant improvement in the KIDNET group on the overall PTSD outcomes. The subscale analysis showed a significant difference between the groups on intrusive memories (F = 5.88, P = 0.026) with a reduction in symptoms of intrusion in the KIDNET group. Analyzing the subscale of avoidance symptoms, the KIDNET group showed a statistically significant difference when compared to the control group (F = 20.94, P = 0.000). Similarly, the cognitions and mood symptoms subscale showed a significant difference between the two groups (F = 7.66, P = 0.013). While the arousal and reactivity symptoms subscale showed a significant difference between the KIDNET group and the waitlist control group (F = 8.82, P = 0.008), the distress/impairment subscale also showed a significant difference between the groups from baseline to postassessment (F = 9.30, P = 0.007). Hence, there was a statistically significant difference between the KIDNET participants and the waitlist control group participants on the measures of PTSD with median scores showing substantial reduction in most of the PTSD subscales including intrusive memories, avoidance, cognition and mood, arousal and reactivity, and distress/impairment as compared to the waitlist control group.

Participant-reported outcomes in the process of KIDNET

The feedback conducted during KIDNET sessions revealed that the research questions posed to participants about their experience in therapy elicited positive responses from most participants. One participant (Ms. SK, 14 years, sex trafficking survivor) reported change in perspective toward being in therapy and stated:

“The first session, I really did not want to be there. But I also realised I needed to be there. So, I sat, and I was as open as I could be because I didn’t want to feel the way I was feeling. I wanted to get better.”

SK’s decision to go against her tendency to avoid allowed her to process her experiences. Like her experience in beginning therapy, the other participants’ decision to commit to treatment enabled them to engage with the aspects of their traumatic experiences while appreciating inclusion for better outcomes.

During the initial stage of intervention, participants were assessed for both traumatic experiences and other stressors that they may have experienced within and outside the trafficking situation. Introducing the trauma checklist and psychoeducating them by explaining why it was helpful to know what trauma does to the individual, the family and the community informed the survivor about why they are suffering from distressing events and what can be done about this. Psychoeducation was continued by framing their experiences, increasing understanding of symptoms, and rationale for treatment.

After completing the assessment, trauma reactions were explained to the survivors in an open and transparent manner using the brain model. At the end of this session, Ms. SK stated:

“Everything I was feeling in my body, the fear, the anger, the sadness… I was becoming increasingly anxious. I was afraid. But this made sense. I understood now why it kept coming back even when I tried to push it away, day and night. Now I know why my mind keep bringing up these bad feelings like it was happening now. This makes it understandable… It’s in my mind, the fear is all there… it brings it back. But I get it now… it’s not happening now… It seems important to do this if I want it to go away…”

The lifeline exercise was aimed to organize the life experiences of the survivor as symbols chronologically along the timeline. The therapist placed a flower symbolizing Ms. SK’s birth at the beginning of the timeline, after which she explained about her family and placed flowers and stones for her pleasant and unpleasant memories. The therapist accompanied the process by empathically verbalizing what was being laid down, finding suitable labels for the symbols, ensuring the cold memories were activated, and adding contextual information. Figure 3 shows the timeline created by Ms. SK, and at the end of the session, she stated:

Lifeline of a sex trafficking survivor. Flowers symbolize positive events, and stones symbolize negative events (traumatic, stressful, or sad experiences)

“It was nice to start with. I never saw my birth as a positive thing. It gave me hope going on to place the flowers for my family and my brother… then the stones were there… then more flowers… They are all moments of my life. Putting this on the table and giving names for each… there was meaning for everything.”

During the middle phase of the narration and exposure, participants perceived it as overwhelming, as stated by Ms. SK:

“When I started to tell my story, I was interested in exploring all the details and was able to talk about it. But when I started speaking about the stones, I was terrified. I kept thinking, if we were really going to deal with this issue here…”

During this process, Ms. SK’s distress was heightened, and her arousal was rising. Her initial apprehension and disbelief suggested that trauma survivors may enter therapy with some degree of ambivalence about how they would engage with their psychological distress. She referred to how KIDNET was explained to her, which allowed her to understand what was expected and how exposure would be conducted to ensure reduction in fear-provoking associations.

During the emotional activation, the hot memories were detailed, explored, and connected to contextual cold facts. Arousal then gradually decreased, and reporting traumatic moments became increasingly tolerable for Ms. SK. Exploring her terrifying lifetime memories in a safe and empathetic encounter helped her experience a corrective relationship contact, and she felt emotionally supported.

“…But when it was explained to me how the process worked, I got it. It helped me understand and tolerate it. Slowly, I could talk about the memories, and the fear reduced.”

Further sessions explored all the stones placed by the survivors; the hot memories of the fear/trauma network were on various levels, including sensory, cognitive, emotional, and physiological elements. Ms. SK’s labeling of emotional states focused on staying in touch with her perceptions, as she stated in one narration:

“I am stuck in that room. It is locked. There are no windows. It is dark inside. I am so afraid. My legs won’t move. I can’t get up. Someone is laughing outside. A girl is crying and screaming somewhere. I can’t move my legs….”

The narration of the sensory experiences followed throughout the narration and exposure sessions, and the therapist noticed a rapid heightening of arousal. Ms. SK was brought to the present experiences of immobility, and comfort was provided to her as she sobbed. The emotions were labeled once she defreezed from the immobility, after which she continued to narrate the details of what happened next. The therapist detailed the fright stage during this threat, so she could understand her physical immobility. Further sessions focused on inviting Ms. SK to explore more details of the event, place herself at the beginning, and go through it again. This narration was then integrated into her existing narrative of what happened then, how she felt and how she feels now, and what she thinks about it today.

After the first emotional exposure, the following is an excerpt from Ms. SK’s narration in the session:

“My body was shaking. I was so scared. I felt the room was smaller. It was scary like my legs won’t move, and they were shaking. I was breathing so loudly, and my throat was dry. A few minutes later, I could feel it… in my legs and my throat… I wanted it to stop hurting… But with the help, very slowly, I spoke… I was back here… Not there anymore… The room was not dark. There was no one screaming. It didn’t smell dirty. It was going away… I could slowly move my legs a little bit now. I could see the room I was sitting in now… I drank some water, I could breathe…”

After some habituation had occurred, Ms. SK was able to make sense of her experiences. Similar to other survivors, she was able to identify her patterns of outbursts of anger and general anxiety and how they had originated in the traumatic experience. The detailed narration led to a thorough understanding of the survivor’s behavior during the event, leading to cognitive restructuring. The once hot memories were compared to the now cool memories, and the implications of this transition were discussed with the survivor. While reviewing her narration in the following sessions, Ms. SK stated:

“I went back to my room and sat there for hours. I was not crying or angry. I just sat there and realised where I am, how I am now… how I will be… I was so angry and would yell at everyone who tried to come close to me in the shelter home… I was angry at them. Now, I just sat and started thinking about it… I used to be so irritable, and my hands would feel shaky… but I was not scared now… not throwing things at others…”

Further sessions proceeded for each of the stones similarly, and continuous monitoring was done while keeping the cold memories of the incident with a here-and-now emphasis, with a specific focus on the present and cognitive and emotional reorganization.

During the last session, when enquired about the changes noticed during the short-term treatment of KIDNET, Ms. SK stated:

“…I saw differences in myself. Like I was dealing with things. I was not just praying for it to go away like before. I was actually trying to get better. And in these three weeks, I noticed I am not avoiding people like before. I am not ignoring my feelings in those situations…. I think back to what happened. I am thinking about it and trying to move on… It does not make me feel scared now… my hands are not shaking… my legs feel strong… it doesn’t smell dirty now… there is light…”

In the last phase of therapy, written narratives of the participants’ experiences were reread to them to complete the testimony and habituation process. This was done step-by-step, having details filled in and completing an entire narration, including all the traumatic events of the survivor’s life in chronological order. When asked about Ms. SK’s feedback after listening to her testimony, she stated:

“It is strange, listening to my own words. I said all this… how it has changed from the first day… So strange… thinking how the way I see it has changed… I can’t imagine I used to feel like this… It has made me different… to see where I go… with anything that happens in life…I am stronger…”

DISCUSSION

Survivors of sex trafficking experience multiple forms of abuse and suffer from a wide range of mental health consequences. Little evidence is available regarding effective treatments to meet the complex needs of this highly vulnerable group. Thus, it is imperative to explore and assess the ability of trafficking survivors to engage in psychological therapy. The present pilot study examined the effect of KIDNET in improving trauma-related symptoms, psychological distress, dissociation, depression, and anxiety in adolescent survivors of sex trafficking. This study shows preliminary evidence for KIDNET, which can be used with this vulnerable population as they may continue to experience uncertainty and instability due to social displacement. These results are promising and suggest that KIDNET may be a viable treatment for survivors of sex trafficking.

The findings of this study showed there was a substantial reduction in PTSD symptoms in the KIDNET participants from the beginning up till the end of treatment. Although the intervention was effective overall, the mean PTSD scores show in the moderate range at the end of therapy. However, all participants had scored in the severe range before intervention was started. The subscale analysis revealed that the participants showed a significant difference between the two groups in all the PTSD domains with a reduction in scores on items of intrusion symptoms, avoidance, arousal and reactivity, and impairment/distress.

There was a decrease in the severity of PTSD symptoms in this study, which may indicate that KIDNET, which is built on a theory that is supported by evidence-based cognitive research[21] in child development, is a suitable intervention to be used among adolescents suffering from PTSD symptoms. Additionally, as a short-term intervention that can be applied on a large scale and provides testimonies, it makes KIDNET a suitable treatment modality in the humanitarian field.

A retrospective audit conducted among trafficking victims using NET showed a reduction in PTSD severity scores post treatment, along with a significant decrease in general distress.[22] Another recent study that compared NET with a waitlist control in adult survivors of trafficking indicated a substantial reduction in PTSD and anxiety symptoms post treatment in the NET group but not in the waitlist group.[23] These findings can substantiate that KIDNET can be used effectively with children and adolescents who have been rescued from human trafficking. However, modifications are required to ensure that the holistic needs of this vulnerable group are appropriately addressed.

Apart from PTSD symptoms, this study assessed psychological distress, dissociative experiences, depression, and anxiety symptoms among the participants. Several hypothetical constructs have been posited to explain that various comorbid psychiatric symptoms occur along with PTSD symptoms.[24] In PTSD, aside from trauma exposure, re-experiencing symptoms (for example, nightmares and flashbacks) and many of the other symptoms, such as hyperarousal, avoidance, and numbing, overlap with other mental disorders, such as anxiety disorders, panic disorder, dissociative disorders, and depression.[25] This study found that a significant difference was seen in psychological distress, depressive symptoms, and anxiety from pre- to postintervention between the two groups with a reduction in scores at the two time points in the KIDNET group.

An intervention study using NET conducted with survivors of natural disasters showed that general mental distress scores were significantly reduced with NET and positively affected survivors’ perceived social support by reducing distress.[26] Concerning depression and general anxiety symptoms, NET has shown that when anxiety-provoking implicit memories relate to the episodic context, the autobiographical memory is rebuilt and thus allows for the reduction of anxiety.[8] Studies using NET with comorbid PTSD and other severe mental illnesses have shown a reduction in depressive and anxiety symptoms along with a slight improvement in quality of life.[27]

Our study found no significant difference in dissociative symptoms from baseline to postassessment between the KIDNET and control groups. Previous studies[28] have shown that NET is statistically superior to controlled comparisons with medium effect sizes, but our study did not reflect this at any time point. However, in line with another study,[29] it was seen that NET might be less effective in treating dissociative symptoms when compared to PTSD symptoms. As NET is based on the theoretical and neurobiological foundations of psychological trauma and was specifically developed to treat PTSD symptoms related to memory in the context of trauma, this substantiates its applications to target the symptom manifestations of overall only trauma-related disorders. Studies assessing improvement in dissociation symptoms post delivery of NET have shown mixed results. While a few studies revealed that KIDNET drastically reduced symptoms of dissociation, others had fewer compelling results, possibly due to limited follow-up data.[232628] This may be because some studies indicate that KIDNET showed better, more stable, and improving results at the follow-up compared to the post-treatment assessment.

The feedback interview was conducted after each session with the participants eliciting their insights regarding their experiences throughout the course of therapy. The findings suggest that participants expressed appreciation toward the opportunity for inclusion in treatment irrespective of initial doubts and scepticism regarding its outcome. Participants reported that the process of therapy contributed to reduce feelings of isolation and increased perceived social support. The explanations of trauma reactions during the psychoeducation process aided in a better understanding of participants’ symptoms, leading to legitimization of their experiences. The findings also suggest that participants reported feeling hopeful and being able to give meaning to their experiences with the lifeline exercise.

During the middle phases of the intervention, narration was followed by exposure which was perceived as challenging and overwhelming experience for participants. However, when the sessions focused on ensuring that fear-provoking associations do not overwhelm them using the techniques of KIDNET, this challenge was eventually overcome by participants. With respect to the exposure and habituation process, exploring the lifetime memories in a safe and empathetic space helped the participants experience emotional support. Participants reported that the sensory re-experiencing and labeling of emotional states aided them to focus on staying in touch with the perceptions. Findings also suggest that after habituation, participants reported being able to make sense of the trauma and put meaning to it and identify their behavioral patterns and the origins in the traumatic event. When written narratives from the previous sessions were reread to the survivors to complete their testimony and habituation process, participants reported reduced trauma reactions and change in their outlook with a renewed sense of personal growth.

Limitations

The results of the present study are preliminary and can be valuable, given the lack of existing evidence about the benefits of treatment in trafficking survivors. The findings of this study may point to consider conducting further RCTs to explore the effectiveness of KIDNET in treating this population. Being a pilot study and not an in-depth study, the present study is limited by design and may not provide adequate data to draw conclusions. It may not be adequate to serve judgement regarding the tolerability of the intervention, and further large-scale studies are required to assert its efficacy. Additionally, there may be a plausible bias by probable influence of respondents’ expectations, thereby affecting the representativeness of the sample. This study also used a feedback-informed method, and as there are limited guidelines available to apply mixed methods in pilot feasibility studies, the comprehensive understanding of implications for future trials may be limited.

Although KIDNET showed reductions in PTSD and other psychological symptoms, some clinical issues emerged with this group that can be addressed in further exploration in future research. The survivors of trafficking in this study were quite heterogeneous in their trauma experiences. While some reported a long trauma history with various experiences of abuse before being trafficked, others had more time-limited trauma exposure. This study has other limitations, including a lack of follow-up assessment to assess the maintenance of symptom reduction. The self-report measure of K-10 was translated in Hindi, but the further process of conducting validity and reliability of the scale was conducted. The impact of guilt and shame in the maintenance of PTSD symptoms arose as a possible feature during intervention, but it was not evaluated as an outcome measure. Considering all these aspects, the findings of the present study may be impacted and a more comprehensive identification of related barriers to treating this population may be considered in future studies. This study may benefit from further research to adapt NET protocols to ascertain other psychosocial factors, including positive psychology variables such as resilience, post-traumatic growth, and psychological well-being.

CONCLUSION

Adolescents trafficked for sexual exploitation are likely to experience multiple traumatic events and are at risk for trauma-related outcomes and other associated psychological issues. As little is known about the effectiveness of treatments for sex trafficking survivors, this pilot study found KIDNET to be a well-tolerated treatment to reduce experiences of trauma-related outcomes and associated psychopathology. As such, the findings of the study show favorable outcomes in symptom reduction in the KIDNET group as compared to the waitlist control group. Scores of PTSD and its domains showed a significant difference from baseline to postintervention in the KIDNET group. Additionally, significant differences were also seen in the groups in the domains of psychological distress, depression, and anxiety symptoms. Participant feedback outcomes reflected positive experiences of individuals throughout the course of therapy. The results of this study may indicate that KIDNET may be considered as a suggested treatment for trauma survivors, specifically those rescued from human trafficking. The findings also indicate a need for full-scale RCTs and inclusion of associated variables that may interfere with positive outcomes. Measures to overcome challenges faced by participants during therapy can ensure that the holistic needs of survivors are met by considering these factors to facilitate optimal engagement in psychological interventions.
