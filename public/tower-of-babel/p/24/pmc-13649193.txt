Moral Injury and Mental Health Consequences in Active-duty Soldiers: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Cross-sectional Surveys
Authors: Madhubanti Sinha, Jay Kumar Ranjan, Chandra Uday Singh, Mahesh A. Tripathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Moral injury (MI), defined as perceived betrayals or violations of one’s moral values, causes psychological distress in active-duty soldiers (ADS), and it is associated with various mental health (MH) issues such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), anxiety, and depression. Although some quantitative reviews have examined the association between MI and MH outcomes in the military population, primarily among veterans, none of the studies focused specifically on ADS. This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted to address the research gap.Methods:We reviewed published articles from six databases, namely, PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Taylor and Francis, and JSTOR up to July 29, 2025, following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines.Results:The retrieved articles underwent a systematic selection process using pre-specified inclusion and exclusion criteria, resulting in 12 potentially eligible studies. Seven cross-sectional studies examined the association between MI and PTSD, representing a total sample of 4,370 ADS. Pooled effect measures showed that MI and its dimensions, such as betrayal, transgression toward self, and transgression toward others, were positively correlated with PTSD. Similarly, MI and its domains were significantly positively associated with anxiety and depression in ADS.Conclusion:The present findings should be interpreted with caution, as this review synthesis included a limited number of studies and was conducted mainly in Western countries. Future studies should address the cross-cultural component of MI to understand how MI manifests in non-Western military contexts, where cultural and religious values may shape moral reasoning differently.

INTRODUCTION

Moral injury (MI) occurs when people suffer severe psychological distress as a result of actions or inaction that violate their deeply rooted moral beliefs and values.[1] This inaction leads individuals to feel betrayed, emotionally injured, and unable to reconcile the gap between what they expect and what they observe.[2] Over time, a persistent violation or neglect of the moral framework can lead to guilt, shame, anger, and reduced self-confidence, ultimately damaging mental health (MH).[3]

MIs are a major concern for active-duty soldiers (ADS) as they are working in morally complex and high-risk environments where decisions often have life-or-death implications.[4] Unlike other professions, the intensity and immediacy of military operations make soldiers uniquely vulnerable to moral challenges that may be contrary to their deeply held beliefs and values.[1] Fifty-two to ninety per cent of active US soldiers experience some symptoms related to MI,[56] and it is also common among ADS from other parts of the globe.[789]

Moral damage can develop through three primary pathways, namely self-inflicted MI resulting from one’s own actions (perpetration; MI-S), exposure to others’ moral transgressions (witnessing; MI-O), and experiences of betrayal by trusted leaders or institutions (betrayal; MI-B).[1210] Each path can elicit different emotional and psychological responses, including guilt, shame, anger, and a deep sense of betrayal, all of which undermine self-confidence, trust in others, and confidence in authority.[211] In the military service context, unresolved moral conflicts not only affect MH[12] but can also undermine unit cohesion and long-term well-being in soldiers.[4]

Many empirical studies reported adverse MH consequences, such as heightened symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD),[51314] depression, and anxiety[5615] are experienced by veterans and ADS due to MI. In addition, MI has been identified as a risk factor for increased suicidal thoughts and behaviors.[161718]

There are many attempts have been made to review and synthesize the reviews related to MI systematically and associated MH consequences in military personnel, especially in veterans.[61920] However, no systematic literature study has been carried out among ADS. To fill this research gap, a systematic literature review is conducted to synthesize the findings regarding MI and associated MH consequences in ADS.

METHODS

Protocol preregistration

Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, this systematic review was conducted. The study protocol is registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews with registration number CRD420251111434.

Literature sources and search strategy

We systematically search the articles in five major databases, namely PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Taylor and Francis, and JSTOR up to July 29, 2025. The Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) guidelines were used to create the search string for this systematic review.[21] The following string of keywords was used for database search:

(“moral injur*” OR “moral transgression” OR betrayal OR blame) AND (“mental health” OR wellbeing OR anxiety OR depression OR hostility) AND (“military” OR army OR navy OR air force OR soldier OR “special forces” OR “military personnel” OR sailor OR airmen).

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria for the present systematic review were as follows: (a) empirical studies examining the relationship between MI and MH consequences in active-duty military personnel; (b) studies that assessed MI using standardized scales; (c) studies that measured PTSD, anxiety, or depression as outcome variables; and (d) articles published in the English language. Studies were excluded if they focused on veterans, healthcare professionals, or other occupational groups. Review articles, theoretical papers, and non-empirical studies were also excluded from the present review.

Data extraction

The search strings were used independently by two authors to identify potentially relevant articles across the selected databases. Duplicate records were identified and removed using Zotero reference management software.[22] Titles and abstracts of the remaining records were screened against predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Further, full texts were retrieved only for studies that provided information on MI and its associated MH consequences in ADS. The two authors independently reviewed the full texts, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer until consensus was reached. Data from eligible studies were systematically extracted in a structured Excel spreadsheet, including author(s), country, study design, characteristics of the sample, and outcome variables, particularly correlation coefficients between MI and depression, anxiety, and PTSD.

Risk of bias

The risk of bias (RoB) for the included studies was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale.[23] This instrument is specifically designed to assess the methodological quality of cross-sectional studies included in systematic reviews. The tool consisted of six items assessing the sample characteristics, assessing exposures and outcomes, and confounding factors. Using this tool, studies are classified as low (7–9 stars), medium (4–6 stars), or high (0–3 stars) ROB. The use of this tool provides a structured, transparent, and standardized approach for assessing the quality of the cross-sectional evidence included in this review.

Data synthesis

All meta-analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [24] with the meta[25] and dmetar[26] packages. A random-effects model was employed for the meta-analysis. The metacor() function from the meta package was used to pool correlation coefficients, which required the generic input of correlation values and corresponding sample sizes from each included study.[25] Most of the studies[67132728293031] reported direct correlation coefficients between MI and its dimensions (e.g., betrayal, transgression) with PTSD, depression, and anxiety. For studies that reported standardized beta coefficients[3233] these values were treated as equivalent to correlation coefficients, as standardized path coefficients from causal to endogenous variables can be interpreted as correlation estimates.[34] In the case of Easterbrook et al.,[35] odd ratios were converted to Cohen’s d, which were subsequently transformed into correlation coefficients. Similarly, for a study, Zasiekina et al.[36] raw data provided in supplementary materials were used to compute correlations of MI with depression and anxiety. Further, forest plots were generated using the forest() function. Publication bias was assessed visually and statistically, with Egger’s test implemented through the regtest() function.[26]

RESULTS

Study selection

Our comprehensive search yielded 632 results that were identified from five electronic databases; among them, 229 studies were duplicate records [Figure 1]. Further, on the basis of title or abstract search, a total of 40 articles were identified that have the potential to provide MI and related MH consequences in ADS. Further, after full-text review of 40 articles, 19 studies were excluded, as MI was not measured in eleven studies, six studies did not measure correlational aspects of MI with depression, anxiety, or PTSD, and two studies were intervention-based. Finally, 12 studies were included in the present study.

Study characteristics

The current meta-analyses comprise 12 studies, and most of the studies were from North America (k = 8; USA and Canada), one study each from Australia, Germany, Ukraine, and Israel. Commonly employed instruments for measuring MI were the MI Events Scale (MIES;[37]), the MI Symptom Scale–Military Version (MISS-M;[38]). The MIES is an 11-item self-report scale that evaluates potentially morally injurious events as indicators of MI. It consists of three subscales, namely, MI-S, MI-O, and MI-B.[37] The MISS-M assesses MI symptoms along with spiritual indicators of MI.[38] It is a multidimensional scale; however, only total scores have been used to evaluate MI symptoms in two of the included studies.[636] MH was measured using the PTSD Checklist (PCL-5/PCL-M;[3940]), Patient Health Questionnaire-9,[41] Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale-7,[42] State-Trait Anger Expression Inventory-2,[43] etc. Table 1 presents a comprehensive overview of the characteristics of the included studies.

Moral injury and mental health consequences

Moral injury and post-traumatic stress disorder as mental health consequences

Table 2 shows the pooled correlation coefficients for the relationship between MI and its dimensions and PTSD. A total of 7 studies comprising 4,370 participants were included to examine the relationship between total MI and PTSD. A significant positive association was found between MI and PTSD (pooled r = 0.47, P < 0.01). Further, five studies with a total of 1621 participants assessed the association between MI-B and PTSD. The meta-analysis revealed a significant positive association between MI-B and PTSD (pooled r = 0.26, P = 0.02). Furthermore, eight studies comprising 5473 participants evaluated the link between MI-O and PTSD. A significant positive association between MI-O and PTSD has been found (pooled r = 0.18, P < 0.01). Finally, seven studies (n = 5440) investigated the relationship between MI-S and PTSD and yielded a significant association (pooled r = 0.18, P < 0.05) [Figure 2]. A substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 86.76%–95.2%) was observed across studies measuring the relationship of MI and its dimensions with PTSD. However, the significant variability calls for further investigation of possible moderators that may affect the relationship between MI and PTSD.

Moral injury and depression as mental health consequences

MI has been consistently associated with elevated depressive symptoms among military personnel. However, only six studies included in the present review examined the relationship between MI and depressive symptoms. Of these, four assessed overall MI, one study focused on MI-B, and two studies each examined MI-S and MI-O, which limited the feasibility of conducting a meta-analysis across MI dimensions.

In a robust sample of 2941 participants, Easterbrook et al.[35] reported that MI significantly increased the risk of developing depression among ADS (odds ratio [OR] = 1.79). At the subdimension level, both MI-S (OR = 1.12) and MI-O (OR = 1.62) were associated with higher odds of depression. Consistent with this pattern, all three cross-sectional studies also demonstrated significant positive associations between MI and depressive symptoms, with correlations ranging from 0.21 to 0.57.[63036] Furthermore, Bryan et al.[13] reported that MI-S (r = 0.167, P < 0.01), MI-O (r = 0.176, P < 0.01), and MI-B (r = 0.255, P < 0.01) were significantly correlated with depression. Presseau et al.[32] used a path-analysis approach and found that MI-S had a direct effect on depression (B = 0.26, P < 0.01), whereas MI-O did not contribute to depression (B = 0.02, P > 0.05).

MI and anxiety as MH consequences

A total of six studies reported findings on the association between MI and anxiety. Among these, four studies examined total MI, three assessed MI-S and MI-O each, and one study focused on MI-B; therefore, meta-analysis was not conducted for these association conditions. In a sample of 2941 ADS, Easterbrook et al.[35] also demonstrated that MI significantly increased the risk of generalized anxiety (OR = 1.80), and subdimension-wise analysis showed that MI-S (OR = 1.09) and MI-O (OR = 1.74) were associated with higher generalized anxiety risk. Further, Plouffe et al.[30] and Volk and Koenig et al.[6] have also found a significant association between MI and anxiety, with correlation coefficients of 0.31 and 0.32, respectively. In contrast, Zasiekina et al.[36] did not find a significant correlation between MI and anxiety (r = −0.108, P > 0.05). Furthermore, Bryan et al.[13] reported that MI-S (r = 0.124, P < 0.01), MI-O (r = 0.138, P < 0.01), and MI-B (r = 0.219, P < 0.01) were all associated with increased anxiety symptoms. Using a path-analysis approach, Presseau et al.[32] found that MI-S significantly predicted anxiety (B = 0.28, P < 0.05), whereas MI-O was not a significant predictor within the structural model (B = 0.06, P > 0.05).

Publication bias

The funnel chart provides a visual representation of potential publication bias in pooled correlation estimates between MI and related MH outcomes in ADS. Asymmetry in the funnel plot further indicates the presence of potential publication bias [Figure 3]. Egger’s regression test[44] conducted using the regtest() function from the dmetar package, was employed to assess the symmetry of the funnel plot. The intercept of the regression model (β0 = 0.16) was statistically significant (t = 2.20, P = 0.05), indicating the presence of publication bias in studies examining the association between MI and PTSD among ADS. However, we were unable to assess funnel plot asymmetry for studies examining correlations between MI and anxiety or depression, as few studies were only available in each of these symptom domains.

DISCUSSION

The present systematic review and meta-analysis provide an integrated understanding of the MH consequences of MIs in ADS. This is one of the few attempts to synthesize quantitatively the existing evidence on how MI contributes to MH outcomes in ADS. Given the high operational demands[45] and repeated exposure to morally demanding situations,[114] our findings highlight MI as one of the risk factors for poor MH in ADS.

The findings of the present meta-analysis reveal that MI and its dimensions are strongly correlated with PTSD. The present findings are aligned with the systematic review of Koenig et al.,[46] and previous meta-analytical studies of Williamson et al.[47] and Griffin et al.[48] have also found that MI is significantly associated with PTSD, although these studies were conducted in mixed occupational professionals. Although Williamson et al.,[47] have not found any difference between military and non-military participants in the relationship between MI and PTSD.

The present study also found that MI in most of the included studies was a significant association of MI and its dimensions with depression and anxiety, except for a few of the results.[31] The present findings are aligned with previous literature, where the relationship between MI and negative MH-related outcomes is consistently reported. For instance, Griffin et al.[48] in a study involving mixed occupational groups, a significant association was found between MI and these MH conditions. Similarly, Levi-Belz et al.,[49] observed strong associations between dimensions of MI and depression among UK veterans. Likewise, Williamson et al.,[47] in their meta-analytical review, reported that MI was significantly associated with depression and anxiety among mixed samples, including both veterans and non-military participants. Furthermore, in a systematic review, Koenig et al.[46] also stated that MI is significantly associated with PTSD, depression, and anxiety, both in ADS and veterans. In contrast, a meta-analytical review by Bryan et al.[50] reported only a very small association between MI and both depression and anxiety. However, it is also important to mention that a greater number of studies are carried out among ADS, a population that generally has greater access to MH services within military settings. They often encounter MI symptoms in more acute forms, influenced by immediate exposure to morally complex situations and strict military hierarchies; however, systemic barriers, stigma and fear of career repercussions often inhibit help-seeking. Moreover, in military service contexts, career advancement and promotions are closely linked to overall fitness, including MH. Consequently, fear of career consequences may deter soldiers from openly disclosing psychological distress, leading to socially desirable responses during assessments. Therefore, Levi-Belz et al.[51] highlighted the moderating role of self-disclosure in the relationship between MI and MH consequences in ADS.

There are a few reasons that can be attributed to the lower association of MI with depression and anxiety reported in ADS. Some studies argued about the transdiagnostic aspect of PTSD and MI, as their symptom domains overlap. However, some previous studies have controlled the severity of symptoms of PTSD, even though the presence of MI remains an important risk factor for depression, anxiety, and suicide,[1417] and these studies further support the notion that MI is a separate syndrome from PTSD. Furthermore, Currier et al.,[52] controlled the effect of combat exposure even though MI was significantly associated with PTSD, depression, and suicidality, and further concluded that only MI imposes transdiagnostic risks for the occurrence of different MH problems. Therefore, recent methodological reviews emphasize that although some symptom domains of MI and PTSD are overlapping,[53] but distinct constructs, as MI uniquely involves spiritual and existential distress that may not be captured by traditional PTSD measures.[48]

According to existing literature, this meta-analysis study is one of the first to synthesize findings of the relationship between MI and its associated dimensions and different MH outcomes in ADS. However, there are some limitations. First, the present study is based on 12 cross-sectional studies, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, most of the studies included were carried out in Western countries, including the United States, which makes the applicability of results to the Asian, African, and other low- and middle-income countries’ military population uncertain. Therefore, in future, studies should address the cross-cultural component of MI to understand how MI manifests in non-Western contexts, where cultural and religious values may shape moral reasoning differently. In addition, the present findings showed a high degree of heterogeneity across the domains of MI and its associated dimensions with PTSD; however, the underlying sources of heterogeneity could not be fully investigated due to the limited number of studies available in the different association conditions. The included studies likely vary methodologically, with differences in sample characteristics, measurement tools, and design, which may weaken the comparability and broader applicability of the findings.

The present study has both theoretical and applied implications. First, the findings underline the importance of recognizing MI and associated MH consequences in ADS; therefore, MI, along with the MH status of soldiers, should be routinely monitored. Second, the moral and ethical dimensions of trauma should be incorporated in targeted trauma-focused psychological interventions to treat adverse MH outcomes among ADS. Therefore, institutional policies may be designed to provide for the basic screening and implementation of training modules on MI, especially pre-deployment and post-deployment in particularly vulnerable areas.

CONCLUSION

This systematic review and meta-analysis show that MIs and their dimensions are strongly associated with PTSD, depression, and anxiety in ADS. The consistently high level of heterogeneity suggests that contextual factors such as deployment type, length of service, military culture, and access to MH services may affect the relationship between MI and MH outcomes. The findings further highlight the clinical relevance of assessment of MI as a routine MH evaluation in military settings and suggest that components of MI should be incorporated and optimized in trauma-focused psychological interventions for soldiers.
