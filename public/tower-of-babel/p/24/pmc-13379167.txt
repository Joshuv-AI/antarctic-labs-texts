Breast Ultrasound Texture Features of Family History of Breast Cancer and Contraceptive Use in Nigerian Younger Women
Authors: Akwa Egom Erim, Akpama Egwu Egong, Precious Onwugamba, Chimezie G. Akwaeze, Immanuel Atauyo Akpauso, Ernest Usang Ekpo, Stephen Clement Ovai, Nneoyi Onen Egbe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The impact of family history of breast cancer (FHBC) on the texture features of the breast is poorly understood. We sought to examine the effect of FHBC on the image texture features of breast sonograms of young asymptomatic women.Methods:This study estimated texture features in breast sonograms with ImageJ software in a cohort of 360 young asymptomatic women aged 19–29 years. The sonographic assessment was performed using a digital color Doppler ultrasonic diagnostic instruments (Xuzhou Kaixin Electronic Instrument: Model – DCU7; SN-2331531). Texture analysis of each region of interest was performed using the gray-level co-occurrence matrix plugin of the ImageJ software. An independent samples t-test was used to compare the texture features of the breast in women with FHBC and the control.Results:Tissue homogeneity and uniformity were significantly lower in women with FHBC (P < 0.05), while tissue complexity (entropy) was significantly higher in subjects with FHBC (P = 0.02). Correlation and contrast were statistically the same between these groups contraceptive use showed no statistical association with breast image texture features (P > 0.05).Conclusion:In this study, breast tissue complexity, homogeneity, and uniformity were affected by FHBC. Breast texture features should be considered when assessing risk in women with FHBC.

INTRODUCTION

Breast cancer has been reported in young women in their 20s and 30s and those who have premature birth at a late age or have no breastfeeding experience.[1] Breast cancers identified at age below 40 years are known as early-onset breast cancer.[2] The incidence of early-onset breast cancer is becoming alarming, having incidence at 1.4% in women younger than 35 years and 0.43% in those <30 years.[3] This rise in breast cancer in this subpopulation has been linked to the underrepresentation of young women in breast cancer care programs among other factors.[2] For instance, the age-specific screening in mammography in developing economies excludes very young women due to high breast density, which reduces sensitivity to breast cancer detection. Early-onset breast cancer is worrisome and raises concerns due to poor prognosis, its aggressive nature, and poor survivorship.[2] From evidence, breast cancer in young women is the foremost among causes of cancer-related mortality in women younger than 40 years, having a cumulative risk of about 0.61% before 40 years.[4]

Mammography is the standard breast cancer screening tools for characterizing breast lesion. However, it is known for having poor sensitivity to breast cancer, especially in young women with dense breasts.[56] Over the years, breast density has been an important factor in breast cancer risk stratification and prediction. However, this marker has yielded misleading screening intervals for different risk categories, reducing reliance on it for screening regimens.[7]

The efficacy of ultrasound as an adjunct to mammography and screening tool in younger women is becoming more evident, with significant improvement in biopsy guidance and breast cancer staging.[2] Ultrasound examination has the potential to discriminate between fibrous and glandular tissue[8910] and makes it easier to estimate the proportion of glandular tissue and visualization of breast malignancies more efficiently than mammography[11] in the detection of lesions in younger women with dense breasts, as well as in pregnant and lactating women.[7] Additional 10% of recall for further investigations, after supplemental care with ultrasound screening, have been reported.[1213]

Artificial intelligence has provided opportunities to extract and model information within radiological images invisible to the human eye to predict the risk of breast cancer and support clinical decision-making.[1415161718] The radiomics signature, which leverages tissue texture information contained within an image, could indicate tissue disturbances and disease process and characterize lesions within a mammographic image.[15] Breast texture features such as entropy, uniformity, and homogeneity are important metrics in breast cancer risk stratification, as they provide useful information on the textural characteristics of the breast tissue, which can be tailored to identify the subset of women with a higher propensity for breast cancer.[19] This has triggered a growing interest in the utilization of tissue texture in improving personalized breast care, as these features can detail tissue information beyond masking from dense breast to indicate subtle changes and estimate the risk for breast cancer induction.[20]

It is important to understand the roles of patient characteristics in breast cancer indices when planning measures toward mitigating its incidence and improving outcomes. Patients’ characteristics, such as family history of breast cancer (FHBC) and contraceptive use, have been underpinned in breast cancer incidence and treatment. The FHBC and contraceptive use have been mentioned among factors influencing breast cancer incidence, especially in young women. However, the impact of FHBC and contraceptive use on breast ultrasound texture features in younger asymptomatic women in Nigeria is poorly understood. This study assessed the impact of FHBC and contraceptive use on breast ultrasound texture features of younger Nigerian women.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This research utilized a prospective cross-sectional analytical study involving a cohort of asymptomatic young women between 19 and 30 years of age. Women who fulfilled the study’s eligibility requirements based on age and the absence of symptoms relating to breast conditions and those with FHBC were recruited into the study and were provided with a consent document outlining their rights and potential benefits as study participants. The study randomly selected participants from the cohort into three groups: FHBC/contraceptive use, and control (without FHBC) each group having a total of 90 each, totaling 360 young and asymptomatic women. Those in the study diagnosed with lumps or any pathology were excluded from the study. The study also excluded pregnant or lactating women and those with any previous history of breast pathology and surgery. A sonographer with 15 years of experience performed the ultrasound assessment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was initiated after approval from the review board of the Department of Radiography and Radiological Science, University of Calabar (study reference number IRB# UC/ERCA/24/061). A written informed consent was obtained from the study subjects.

Procedure for data collection

Ultrasound procedure and assessment

A digital color Doppler ultrasonic diagnostic instruments (Xuzhou Kaixin Electronic Instrument: Model – DCU7; SN-2331531) fitted with a linear probe of 7.5 MHz was used to obtain sonograms of the women’s breasts. Each breast (right/left) was scanned in both sagittal and axial planes across the quadrants of the breast, and images demonstrating properly the fatty and fibroglandular components of the breast were frozen, saved, and transferred to an external hard disk drive. Subjects’ ages and FHBC were also obtained.

Image texture analysis

The breast images were uploaded to the ImageJ interface [Figure 1], and the bit depth was changed to eight bits to optimize the gray appearance. The gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) image texture plugin, which handles image texture estimation, was downloaded and added to the ImageJ toolbox. A rectangular region of interest (ROI) was selected within the glandular component of the breast tissue for processing and analysis. The image was normalized to take care of inhomogeneity, denoising, and gray-level quantization. For the estimation of texture analysis, the ROI was maintained at 100 mm by 100 mm for a consistent and accurate output. The GLCM plugin was selected, and from it, all the texture parameters were checked and determined. A spreadsheet was automatically generated carrying values of the parameters for every breast sonogram assessed with ImageJ. The image was loaded on three-dimensional (3D) texture and displayed on a grayscale using dots to render the image of the ROI in a 3D format [Figure 2]. Image was also looked at on a lookup table using a spectrum of color to distinguish and highlight different texture patterns in breast tissue [Figure 3]. The GLCM texture features were quantified and the angular second moment (ASM); contrast, correlation, and inverse difference moment (IDM) were estimated for the breast ultrasound images.

Statistical analysis

The relationship between age and breast image texture parameters was determined using Pearson’s correlation. Independent samples t-test was utilized to compare the breast image texture parameters between those with and without a FHBC as well as the use of contraceptives. Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) version 27.0. Level of significance was set at P ≤ 0.05.

RESULTS

A summary of the results is presented in Table 1. No statistical relationship was seen between the image texture parameters and age (P > 0.05). Subject’s weight showed no significant correlation with texture parameters (P > 0.05) [Table 2]. Both right and left breast texture parameters demonstrated statistically similar values (P > 0.05) [Table 3]. Tissue homogeneity (ASM) and uniformity (IDM) was significantly lower in subjects with FHBC (P < 0.05), while tissue complexity (entropy) was significantly higher in subjects with a FHBC (P = 0.026). Correlation texture parameter and contrast were statistically even between women with FHBC and control [Table 4].

DISCUSSION

Breast texture parameters provide information on the structural characteristics of the breast tissue, which can be tailored to personalize breast care.[21] The findings in this study revealed that neither age nor weight was associated with breast tissue texture indices. However, there were contrasting results regarding the influence of demographic factors such as age and weight on breast tissue characteristics: The works of Granchi et al.,[22] indicated that age and other demographic factors could affect breast tissue texture features and density. The current yield could have been influenced by a number of factors, like sample size, sampling method, and image-processing software characteristics. Besides, this study included women within close age brackets. Similar tissue uniformity, homogeneity, complexity, and contrast were noticed between the right and left breasts. The symmetry provided in this result could present a viable metric for indicating the presence of a disturbance in one breast where there are dissimilar tissue texture values between both breasts. The results of Nemat et al.[23] noted that asymmetry in breast tissue texture could be a marker for malignancy. The outcome on breast texture asymmetry in this study could be helpful in providing a viable risk assessment performance if integrated into a risk prediction model, further emphasizing the diagnostic value of assessing bilateral symmetry in breast cancer detection.[242526]

These study findings also indicated that contraceptive use did not affect the homogeneity of tissue, texture difference, uniformity, and tissue complexity [Table 5]. These results were consistent with the findings of Menon et al.,[27] which suggested that hormonal factors, including contraceptive use, might not significantly alter breast tissue texture. This adds to the understanding that while hormones are known to affect breast tissue, their impact on GLCM-derived texture indices may not be pronounced or detectable using current analysis techniques. It was observed that some breast tissue texture features differed statistically between young women with FHBC and those without it. Women with a FHBC showed distinctly lower breast texture uniformity and homogeneity. On the contrary, tissue entropy was revealed to be significantly higher in individuals with a FHBC, indicating greater complexity in their breast tissue texture. A similar research indicated higher entropy and tissue inhomogeneity, which implies a higher propensity for breast cancer induction.[28] These findings suggest that individuals with a FHBC may have underlying structural differences in their breast tissue, which could represent an increased risk for developing breast cancer.

The results of a systematic review and meta-analysis by Schopf et al.[29] confirm image texture to be a viable tool in assessing the risk of malignancy, yielding a better outcome during mammographic screening. These findings align with the understanding that a FHBC often correlates with a higher genetic predisposition to the disease. Genetic mutations, such as those in the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes, are known to significantly increase the risk of breast cancer and are often inherited. Entropy was found to be higher in malignant lesion by Moon et al.,[30] indicating more complex and heterogeneous tissue structures compared to benign tissues. The observed textural differences in individuals with a FHBC could be used to improve the predictive value of breast texture with a larger cohort size. While Liang et al.’s[31] study focused on identifying textural features of breast ultrasound images to differentiate between malignant and benign lesions, utilizing texture features such as mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis in classifying breast lesions; this current research extends this approach to evaluating breast tissue in nonmass and asymptomatic individuals with information on FHBC.

Similarly, the work of Sadeghi-Naini et al.[32] explored the efficacy of quantitative ultrasound (QUS) spectral parametric maps combined with texture-analysis techniques (using the GLCM) to differentiate between benign and malignant breast lesions. Their study concluded that QUS texture analysis could facilitate the early characterization of breast lesions by providing insights into the heterogeneous microstructure of tissue. This current study’s findings resonate with this conclusion, suggesting that similar textural analysis techniques could be applied not just to lesions but to the broader breast tissue to identify individuals at higher risk due to FHBC. This could lead to more a targeted and personalized screening protocol.

Eom and Ye[3] also contributed to the understanding of breast tissue texture by analyzing the texture characteristics of normal breast tissue and benign and malignant tumors using ultrasonography. Their use of the GLCM algorithm to extract features such as cluster prominence, entropy, homogeneity, sum variance, and information measure of correlation proved effective in classifying breast tissue types. The current study’s findings observed in women with FHBC mirror an observation from Eom and Ye[3] on the importance of complexity in tissue texture as a distinguishing and viable indicator of subtle breast changes. This suggests that integrating these texture parameters into computer-aided diagnostic systems could enhance the early detection of cancer in genetically predisposed individuals.

Overall, the analysis indicates that while demographic factors and contraceptive use did not significantly influence breast tissue texture in this sample, FHBC is associated with notable changes in texture indices, demonstrating lower uniformity and homogeneity, with higher tissue randomness in breast tissue texture. It may serve as early biomarkers of such genetic risks, offering a noninvasive way to monitor individuals with FHBC cancer more closely.

The study’s outcome leans on existing opinions, which suggests that tissue texture randomness signals structural differences that indicate a propensity for breast malignancy.[30] This current research is in line with previous studies that have explored the relationship between breast tissue texture and breast cancer risk: In a study conducted by Yoo et al.,[33] variations in tumor textural differences have been linked with genetic composition and phenotypes.

This research evaluated, for the first time, the texture parameter of breast sonogram using the ImageJ software (version 1.54p, Bethesda, Maryland, USA) in young asymptomatic women and provided baseline values for breast glandular tissue texture features. It demonstrated lower homogeneity and uniformity in subjects with FHBC than those without such history, whereas tissue randomness was higher with FHBC. Therefore, sonographic breast texture analysis may be a useful tool for identifying high-risk individuals, particularly those with a FHBC. These findings could also contribute to the existing body of knowledge by highlighting the complexity of breast texture analysis and its relationships with various factors. One key limitation to this study is the relatively small sample size, with a narrow age range (19–24 years) and limited variability in other demographic factors. This homogeneity may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations with more diverse age ranges, body characteristics, and backgrounds. A larger and more varied sample could provide more robust results.

CONCLUSION

In this study, breast tissue complexity, homogeneity, and uniformity were affected by FHBC. Women with an FHBC demonstrated lower tissue homogeneity and uniformity and higher tissue complexity compared to the control group. Breast texture features should be considered when assessing risk in women with FHBC. These findings could also contribute to the existing body of knowledge by highlighting the complexity of breast texture analysis and its relationships with various factors while contraceptive use did not significantly affect breast tissue image texture. More detailed data on contraceptive use, such as the duration, type, or dosage of contraceptives used by participants, could provide a clearer understanding of its impact on breast tissue texture. A key benefit is the suggestion that ImageJ can be applied in a low-income environment to achieve texture-based studies in the absence of more expensive softwares.
