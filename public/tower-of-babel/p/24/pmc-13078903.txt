Natural variation links seedling hypocotyl growth with water loss in Arabidopsis
Authors: Gilor Kelly, Dvir Meiri, Shay Igon Hagbi, Davinder Sharma, Adi Doron‐Faigenboim, Aiman Egbaria, Eduard Belausov, Tamar Halaly‐Basha, Junjie Mei, Xuenan Zhang, Adi Yaaran, Atara Gal, Yair Yehoshua Zach, Alisdair R. Fernie, Wenxiu Ye, Yariv Brotman, Nir Sade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYPlants integrate environmental and internal cues such as light and sugar availability to coordinate growth and water‐use strategies. While hypocotyl elongation is widely used to study photo‐ and thermo‐morphogenesis, its connection to whole‐plant physiological traits remains unclear. Here, we uncover a negative correlation between hypocotyl length in young Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings and water loss in mature plants. Genome‐wide association mapping across diverse natural accessions identified two genes, HEAT SHOCK TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR A2 (HSFA2) and a 2‐oxoglutarate‐dependent dioxygenase‐like (2OGD‐like), that affect hypocotyl elongation. Both genes are preferentially expressed in guard cells and are associated with reduced hypocotyl growth and increased stomatal conductance. Loss‐of‐function mutants displayed elongated hypocotyls and reduced gas exchange, whereas inducible and guard cell‐specific expression of HSFA2 suppressed hypocotyl elongation and promoted stomatal opening. Guard cell transcriptomics and ChIP‐seq analyses further revealed that HSFA2 directly targets genes involved in sugar signaling, light responses, and actin reorganization, including SCAR3, a component of the WAVE‐ARP2/3 complex required for stomatal dynamics. Together, these findings establish a functional link between seedling growth and stomatal performance, expand the role of HSFA2 beyond heat responses, and position hypocotyl growth as a predictive platform for assessing plant water‐use traits.

INTRODUCTION

Plant developmental plasticity enables seedlings to continuously adjust growth and physiology in response to fluctuating environmental and internal cues. In darkness, seedlings elongate in search of light, transitioning from reliance on seed‐reserved carbon to autotrophic photosynthetic sugar production. Soon after germination, stomata emerge on the cotyledons (Kelly, Lugassi, et al., 2017; Smit et al., 2023), preparing the seedling for gas exchange once environmental conditions allow. Thus, early seedling architecture, particularly hypocotyl length, reflects the integration of metabolic status, light perception, and growth optimization.

Light and sugars are central regulators of hypocotyl elongation. Light restricts elongation through activation of blue‐ and red‐light photoreceptors, cryptochromes (CRY) and phytochromes (PHY), respectively (Cai & Huq, 2025; Wang, 2005). These photoreceptors modulate the antagonistic activities of PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTORS (PIFs) and ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL 5 (HY5), key transcriptional regulators of photomorphogenesis (Leivar & Monte, 2014; Liebers & Pfannschmidt, 2024; Toledo‐Ortiz et al., 2014; Wang, Liu, et al., 2022). Under low‐light or shade conditions, PIFs promote elongation by activating genes involved in auxin biosynthesis, cell wall remodeling, and cellular expansion. Conversely, HY5 accumulates under light, suppresses PIF activity, and promotes photomorphogenic development while restricting hypocotyl growth (Arya et al., 2024; Burko et al., 2020).

Sugars further modulate hypocotyl elongation by interacting with light signaling pathways. Sucrose and glucose stimulate elongation through PIF4‐mediated auxin production (Lilley et al., 2012; Simon et al., 2018; Stewart et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2016). Elevated expression of the sugar sensor HEXOKINASE1 (HXK1) mimics high sugar availability and enhances hypocotyl length up to threefold in a PIF4‐dependent manner (Kelly et al., 2021). Remarkably, guard cell‐specific expression of HXK1 is sufficient to trigger this elongation response, underscoring the systemic influence of guard cell sugar signaling on seedling development. Guard cells also perceive light cues that affect hypocotyl growth. Guard cell‐specific expression of PIF4 or HY5 drives opposing effects on hypocotyl elongation, suggesting the existence of mobile signals that communicate guard cell status to distal tissues (Kelly et al., 2021, 2023). Interestingly, guard cell expression of HXK1, PIF4, and HY5 alters both hypocotyl elongation and stomatal behavior in mature leaves. Guard cell HXK1 and PIF4 promote stomatal closure, whereas HY5 promotes opening, reinforcing their antagonistic functions. Light‐responsive pathways therefore appear to coordinate developmental growth with stomatal performance. For example, phyB and cry1 mutants exhibit elongated hypocotyls together with reduced stomatal apertures (‘long/low’), whereas cop1, det1, and pif4 mutants display short hypocotyls and elevated stomatal conductance (‘short/high’) (Chen et al., 2021; Fernando & Schroeder, 2015; Kelly et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2022; Lau & Deng, 2012; Wang et al., 2010). Despite these observations, this inverse relationship between early elongation and mature‐leaf stomatal performance has not been systematically explored, and the contribution of sugar signaling to this coordination remains unclear.

Intrigued by this correlation, we leveraged natural variation across approximately 300 Arabidopsis thaliana accessions grown with or without sucrose to dissect the genetic basis of hypocotyl elongation and its association with stomatal behavior. Genome‐wide association mapping identified two loci of particular interest: HEAT SHOCK TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR A2 (HSFA2), a central regulator of heat responses (Haider et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2018), and an uncharacterized 2‐oxoglutarate‐dependent dioxygenase‐like gene (2OGD‐like). Both genes influence hypocotyl elongation and exert marked effects on stomatal conductance. Guard cell‐specific transcriptomics and ChIP‐seq analyses reveal that HSFA2 regulates genes associated with circadian rhythm, photosynthesis, MAPK signaling, and actin reorganization, highlighting its contribution to guard cell function. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that early seedling growth traits can predict water‐use characteristics at later developmental stages, establishing a conceptual framework that links developmental plasticity with physiological performance through natural genetic variation.

RESULTS

Genetic basis underlying hypocotyl growth in Arabidopsis

To uncover genetic factors regulating hypocotyl growth under 1% sucrose (Suc) treatment, we conducted a growth assay using a panel of 308 natural Arabidopsis accessions previously described by Wu et al. (2018). Hypocotyl length was measured in 8‐day‐old seedlings grown under standard conditions (0.5× MS medium) and in the presence of Suc. Under control conditions, hypocotyl lengths varied widely, ranging from 1.45 mm in the shortest accessions to 6.49 mm in the longest (Figure 1A). In response to Suc, hypocotyl lengths ranged from 1.62 to 8.1 mm (Figure 1B). Most accessions displayed a significant increase in hypocotyl elongation in response to Suc compared with control conditions (Figure 1C; Table S1). This observation is consistent with previous reports showing that Suc promotes hypocotyl elongation in wild‐type seedlings (Lilley et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2011; Sairanen et al., 2012; Simon et al., 2018; Stewart et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2016). However, a subset of accessions showed insensitivity to Suc, exhibiting similar hypocotyl lengths under both conditions, while a few accessions displayed reduced hypocotyl length in response to Suc (Table S1). Overall, the 308 accessions demonstrated a broad phenotypic spectrum for hypocotyl length under both control and Suc conditions, highlighting the high plasticity within this panel (Figure 1).

To identify genetic determinants of hypocotyl growth, we performed genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) using two independent datasets: hypocotyl length under control conditions and hypocotyl length in the presence of 1% Suc. GWAS was conducted using the 250K SNP chip (Horton et al., 2012), identifying 5 and 10 significant SNP‐trait associations (LOD ≥5.3) in the control and Suc datasets, respectively. Among the loci identified (12 in control and 19 in Suc; best SNP LOD >5), only one locus was common to both treatments, suggesting that Suc induces a partially distinct genetic response. This shared locus corresponded to AUXIN RESISTANT1 (AXR1; AT1G05180). It is well established that auxin signaling influences hypocotyl elongation (Balcerowicz et al., 2014; Procko et al., 2016). Analysis of the axr1‐3 mutant confirmed impaired hypocotyl elongation under our experimental conditions, consistent with previous studies (Figure S1). The identification of AXR1 validates the robustness of our GWAS approach and demonstrates its Applicability in uncovering genetic regulators of hypocotyl growth.

Identification and experimental validation of a heat‐shock transcription factor involved in hypocotyl growth under sucrose

GWAS analysis identified several loci potentially involved in regulating hypocotyl growth under sucrose conditions (Figure 2). A significant QTL on chromosome 2 containing HEAT SHOCK TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR A2 (HSFA2; AT2G26150) was detected (Figure 2A). Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis within the 11‐kb region surrounding HSFA2 revealed five associated SNPs (m68680–m68684). Among these, four SNPs (m68680–m68683) showed strong LD (r 2 >0.80, P < 0.0001), and three were located within the HSFA2 coding sequence. Notably, SNP m68681 was identified as the lead SNP (Figure 2B,C; Figure S2). The strong LD among these variants, together with their localization within HSFA2, points to this gene as the likely causal factor underlying the observed variation in hypocotyl elongation (Figure S2).

Haplotype analysis of HSFA2 revealed four distinct haplotypes (Figure 2D). Accessions in clusters I and II (34 accessions) displayed significantly longer hypocotyls and shared haplotype SNPs C, T, and G. In contrast, clusters III and IV (254 accessions) exhibited average hypocotyl lengths and carried haplotype SNPs T, A, and A/G at the same positions. Notably, SNP m68682 results in a threonine‐to‐methionine substitution that distinguishes clusters I and II from clusters III–IV and is significantly associated with hypocotyl length (P = 9.399 × 10−5; Figure 2B,D,E). To test whether the 32 accessions in cluster I (Figure 2D) primarily drive the observed phenotype, we repeated the GWAS after excluding these accessions. This exclusion led to a significant reduction in the LOD score compared with randomly removing any other set of 32 accessions (P = 0.013758; Figure S3). These results indicate that genetic variation distinguishing cluster I from the other haplotypes underlies the association between the HSFA2 locus and hypocotyl elongation.

A database search using ePlant (see Materials and Methods) indicated that HSFA2 expression is higher in hypocotyls than in cotyledons of young seedlings (Figure 2F). To validate its functional role, we analyzed the T‐DNA insertion mutant (hsfa2, SALK_008978) and found that hsfa2 seedlings displayed longer hypocotyls under sucrose treatment (1–4%), with a 22–26% increase compared with WT, whereas no difference was observed under control conditions (Figure 2G). In contrast, transgenic seedlings expressing HSFA2 under a β‐estradiol‐inducible promoter (pES‐HSFA2) exhibited reduced hypocotyl length following β‐estradiol treatment (Figure 2H). Sucrose supplementation significantly induced HSFA2 expression within 4 h (Figure 2I), indicating that HSFA2 is sucrose responsive. A similar expression pattern was observed following glucose treatment, further supporting the role of HSFA2 as a sugar‐responsive regulator (Figure S8). Together, these findings suggest that in young seedlings, HSFA2 acts to limit hypocotyl elongation in response to elevated sugar levels.

Identification of a gene similar to 2‐oxoglutarate‐dependent dioxygenase involved in hypocotyl growth under normal growth conditions

GWAS conducted under control conditions identified a locus on chromosome 1 (Figure 3A). This locus contains four genes encoding members of the 2‐oxoglutarate (2OGD) and Fe(II)‐dependent oxygenase superfamily: AT1G06620, AT1G06640, AT1G06645, and AT1G06650 (Figure S6). LD analysis within a 23‐kb region of this QTL revealed a stronger LD pattern among SNPs m3262–m3275 compared with surrounding loci (Figure 3C; Figure S6B). Within this region, two sub‐regions exhibited distinct LD characteristics. The first sub‐region (R1) included SNPs m3262–m3265, which were in high LD with each other (r 2 >0.90, P < 0.0001) and with the lead SNP m3267 (r 2 >0.80, P < 0.0001), located between AT1G06640 and AT1G06645 (Figure 3C; Figure S6B). The second sub‐region (R2) encompassed SNPs m3271–m3275, which showed strong internal LD (r 2 >0.90, P < 0.0001) but weaker LD with the lead SNP (r 2 >0.40, P < 0.0001; Figure 3C). Because R1 exhibited stronger LD with the lead SNP, we focused on AT1G06640 (hereafter referred to as 2OGD‐like) for further analysis.

Haplotype analysis of AT1G06640 identified three distinct haplotypes (Figure 3D). Accessions in haplotype group H1 (247 accessions) displayed average hypocotyl lengths and shared SNPs T, G, and T. In contrast, haplotype group H2 (37 accessions), associated with elongated hypocotyls, carried SNPs C, A, and C at the same positions (P = 0.001093; Figure 3D). These sequence differences were significantly associated with variation in hypocotyl length (Figure 3E). Database analyses further indicated that AT1G06640 expression is higher in hypocotyls than in cotyledons (Figure 3F).

Functional validation using a T‐DNA insertion mutant (2ogd‐like) confirmed its involvement in hypocotyl growth regulation (Figure 3G). In contrast to the hsfa2 mutant, which affected hypocotyl length only under sucrose‐supplemented conditions (Figure 2G), the 2ogd‐like mutant influenced hypocotyl elongation under both control and sucrose treatments. Under all tested conditions, 2ogd‐like mutants displayed significantly elongated hypocotyls compared with the wild type (Figure 3G). Specifically, hypocotyl length in the mutant increased by 26, 24, and 33% under control, 1, and 2% sucrose conditions, respectively, relative to WT (Figure 3G). At 4% sucrose, WT seedlings exhibited strongly repressed hypocotyl growth, whereas 2ogd‐like mutants showed only a moderate reduction, maintaining hypocotyls approximately 150% longer than WT (Figure 3G). Furthermore, sucrose treatment of WT seedlings for 4 h induced 2OGD‐like expression (Figure 3H), consistent with a sucrose‐responsive role observed for HSFA2 (Figure 2I). Collectively, these findings suggest that although 2OGD‐like was initially identified under control conditions, it functions as a genetic factor that restricts hypocotyl elongation under both basal and sucrose‐treated conditions.

HSFA2, 2OGD‐like, and stomatal movement

We have previously demonstrated that guard cells play a pivotal role in regulating hypocotyl elongation (Kelly et al., 2021, 2023). Specifically, guard cell‐specific expression of HY5, PIF4, and HXK1 influences both hypocotyl elongation and stomatal conductance, with these factors generating signals that are exported from guard cells to affect other tissues involved in elongation (Kelly et al., 2013, 2019, 2021, 2023). Intrigued by this stomata‐to‐hypocotyl connection, we investigated whether HSFA2 and 2OGD‐like also influence stomatal movement in addition to hypocotyl growth. Database analyses revealed preferential expression of HSFA2 in guard cells compared with mesophyll cells (Figure 4A1). In addition, HSFA2 expression was higher in epidermal peels, which include epidermal and guard cells, than in whole leaves (Figure 4A2). Sucrose treatment further enhanced HSFA2 expression specifically in guard cells (Figure 4A3) (Bates et al., 2012). This guard cell‐enriched and sucrose‐responsive expression pattern suggests a potential role for HSFA2 in stomatal regulation. To test this hypothesis, we performed thermal imaging and gas‐exchange analyses on the hsfa2 mutant and accessions randomly selected from the haplotype group associated with long hypocotyls (H‐long; Figure 2D, cluster I). It is well established that stomatal closure and reduced transpiration result in increased leaf temperature (Merlot et al., 2002). Infrared thermal imaging revealed significantly higher leaf temperatures in the hsfa2 mutant and H‐long accessions compared with WT, indicating reduced stomatal conductance (Figure 4B). This observation was corroborated by direct measurements of stomatal conductance (gs), which showed significantly lower values in the hsfa2 mutant and H‐long accessions relative to WT (Figure 4C). Net photosynthesis rates were also reduced in the hsfa2 mutant, while no significant differences in biomass accumulation were observed (Figure S4) (Nishizawa et al., 2006). Conversely, transgenic plants expressing HSFA2 under a β‐estradiol‐inducible promoter (pES‐HSFA2) exhibited elevated HSFA2 expression and increased g s following β‐estradiol treatment (Figure 4D). Whereas WT plants maintained g s values of approximately 0.2 mol H2O m−2 sec−1, pES‐HSFA2 plants reached values as high as 0.35 mol H2O m−2 sec−1 (Figure 4D). Together, these findings indicate that HSFA2 modulates stomatal conductance in addition to its established role in heat stress responses.

Following the identification of HSFA2 as a factor influencing both hypocotyl growth and stomatal movement, we next examined whether 2OGD‐like, which is associated with elongated hypocotyls (Figure 3), exhibits a similar pattern. Database searches indicated preferential expression of 2OGD‐like in guard cells (ePlant), with higher expression levels in epidermal peels than in whole leaves and in guard cells compared with mesophyll cells (Figure 4E). This expression profile suggested that 2OGD‐like may also participate in stomatal regulation. To test this possibility, we analyzed the 2ogd‐like mutant and five accessions randomly selected from the haplotype group associated with elongated hypocotyls (Figure 3D, H2; H‐long‐2OGD‐like). While the 2ogd‐like mutant exhibited leaf temperatures comparable to WT, the H‐long‐2OGD‐like accessions displayed significantly higher temperatures (Figure 4F). Consistent with this observation, g s was reduced in both the 2ogd‐like mutant and the H‐long‐2OGD‐like accessions relative to WT (Figure 4G). These results suggest that 2OGD‐like contributes to both hypocotyl growth during early development and stomatal conductance in mature plants.

Finally, to evaluate the broader relationship between hypocotyl elongation and stomatal conductance, we measured stomatal conductance in the 40 Arabidopsis accessions with the longest hypocotyls and compared them with the 40 accessions exhibiting the shortest hypocotyls (Figure 4H). Accessions with longer hypocotyls displayed significantly lower stomatal conductance than those with shorter hypocotyls (Figures 1A and 4H), further supporting the inverse relationship between early elongation and mature stomatal performance.

Guard cell‐ specific molecular responses of HSFA2

Given the role of HSFA2 in modulating hypocotyl elongation and stomatal conductance, we sought to identify genes directly regulated by HSFA2 in guard cells. To this end, we performed guard cell transcriptome analysis in WT and hsfa2 mutant plants to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). A substantial proportion of DEGs in hsfa2 guard cells were associated with starch and sucrose metabolism, photosynthesis (carbon fixation, Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle, Rubisco), general metabolic pathways, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, and circadian clock‐related processes (Figure 5A; Table S3).

Light‐related genes involved in circadian rhythm regulation, including PSEUDO‐RESPONSE REGULATOR (PRR) 3, PRR5, PRR7, GIGANTEA, TIMING OF CAB EXPRESSION 1 (TOC1), EARLY FLOWERING 3 (ELF3), and CRY2, were differentially expressed in guard cells of the hsfa2 mutant compared with WT (Figure 5B). Notably, PRR5 displayed a pronounced increase in expression in the mutant, collectively suggesting that stomatal light responses change in the absence of HSFA2.

Collectively, HSFA2 was (i) identified through screening of a light‐responsive developmental trait (hypocotyl growth; Figure 2), (ii) shown to modulate stomatal conductance (Figure 4), and (iii) found to affect the expression of light‐responsive genes in guard cells (Figure 5). In addition, HSFA2 influenced the expression of photosynthesis‐related genes, including PHOSPHOENOLPYRUVATE CARBOXYKINASE 1 (PEPCK1), LIGHT HARVESTING COMPLEX GENE 2 (LHCA2), PHOTOSYSTEM II SUBUNIT P‐1 (PSBP‐1), PHOTOSYNTHETIC ELECTRON TRANSFER C (PGR1/PETC), PHOTOSYSTEM II SUBUNIT S (PSBS), and PSAN (a subunit of photosystem I), further linking HSFA2 to carbon‐related processes within guard cells (Figure 5C). MAPK signaling was also significantly altered in hsfa2 guard cells, including genes involved in abiotic stress responses, particularly ABA signaling (e.g., WRKY33, WRKY22, OST1, PYL8, SnRK2.2, and ABI2; Figure 5D).

To further explore the HSFA2 regulatory network in guard cells and its response to sucrose, we generated plants expressing HSFA2 specifically in guard cells in the hsfa2 mutant background (KST1pro:HSFA2‐GFP/hsfa2) using the well‐established guard cell‐specific promoter KST1 (Kelly, Lugassi, et al., 2017; Plesch et al., 2001). GFP fluorescence confirmed that HSFA2 expression was restricted to the epidermis, predominantly enriched in guard cells (Figure 6A). Measurement of hypocotyl length revealed that guard cell‐specific HSFA2 expression partially rescued the elongated hypocotyl phenotype of the hsfa2 mutant (Figure 6B). In addition, stomatal conductance was elevated in KST1pro:HSFA2‐GFP/hsfa2 plants compared with hsfa2 (Figure 6C), consistent with the effects observed upon inducible HSFA2 expression (Figures 2H and 4D).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP‐seq) was performed following exposure of KST1pro:HSFA2‐GFP/hsfa2 plants to high sucrose for 3 h, identifying 36 putative direct HSFA2 target genes (Table S2). Notably, HSFA2 itself was identified as a direct target (Figure 6D), suggesting an auto‐activation response under sucrose treatment, similar to its behavior during heat stress (Liu et al., 2013; Wang & Ren, 2024). Seven genes were consistently detected in both datasets, guard cell transcriptomics of hsfa2 and ChIP‐seq analysis of guard cell‐specific HSFA2 under sucrose (Tables S2 and S3; Figure 6E), indicating that these genes likely represent direct HSFA2 targets in guard cells. These included MAPKKK21 (AT4G36950), ACCUMULATION AND REPLICATION OF CHLOROPLASTS 3 (ARC3; AT1G75010), SCAR3 (AT1G29170), AROMATIC AND NEUTRAL TRANSPORTER 1 (ANT1; AT3G11900), BRUNO‐LIKE1 (BRN1; AT4G03110), a stress‐responsive gene (AT2G32500, referred to here as SR), and HSFA2 itself (Figure 6F).

Functional characterization of the corresponding mutants (ant1, sr, scar3, brn1, mapkkk21, and arc3) revealed that ant1, sr, and scar3 mutants displayed elongated hypocotyls (Figure 6G), consistent with their reduced expression in hsfa2 guard cells (Figure 6F). In agreement with the elongated hypocotyl phenotype, these mutants also exhibited reduced stomatal conductance (Figure S7). Additionally, arc3 and mapkkk21 mutants displayed reduced g s values. The SCAR3/WAVE2 complex, known to regulate stomatal movement, interacts with multiple components involved in ABA signaling, brassinosteroid responses, and actin reorganization, including ARP3, ARPC2A, ARPC3, ABI1, ABI2, ABI3, ABI4, ABI1‐like1, ABIL2, ABIL3, ABIL4, and BIN2 (Figure 6H). Taken together, these findings support a role for HSFA2 in linking light signaling, stomatal function, and developmental processes such as hypocotyl elongation, and provide mechanistic insight into this link.

DISCUSSION

Negative correlation between hypocotyl elongation and stomatal movement

In this study, we report the existence of a novel correlation between hypocotyl elongation in the seedlings stage and the rate of water loss at mature stages of growth. Long hypocotyls are associated with lower stomatal conductance (long/low), while short hypocotyls have the opposite response of high stomatal conductance (short/high). This relationship was evident across the natural variation panel, in the mutants identified, and in the haplotypes associated with these mutants (Figures 1A and 2, 3, 4; Figure S5). In the hsfa2 mutant and its associated haplotypes, hypocotyls were elongated compared with WT (Figure 2D,G; Figure S5), whereas water loss was significantly reduced (Figure 4B,C). Conversely, inducible overexpression of HSFA2 resulted in suppressed hypocotyl elongation (Figure 2H) and increased stomatal conductance (Figure 4D). Although this long/low‐short/high relationship has not previously been directly addressed, it can be inferred from multiple independent studies. For example, mutants in phytochrome and cryptochrome photoreceptors (phyB, phyA, phyAphyB, cry1, and cry1cry2) exhibit elongated hypocotyls together with reduced stomatal aperture (long/low) (Chory et al., 1996; Eckert & Kaldenhoff, 2000; Kelly et al., 2023; Mao et al., 2005; Wang et al., 2010), whereas overexpression of PHYB and CRY1 produces the opposite phenotype (short/high) (Mao et al., 2005; Wang et al., 2010). Similarly, mutants in CONSTITUTIVE PHOTOMORPHOGENIC 1 (cop1), DE‐ETIOLATED 1 (det1), and PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTOR 4 (pif4) display short hypocotyls and open stomata (Fernando & Schroeder, 2015; Kelly et al., 2021, 2023; Moazzam‐Jazi et al., 2018).

Within our dataset, this inverse relationship was further supported by HSFA2‐related downstream mutants (sr, ant1, and scar3), which exhibited elongated hypocotyls together with reduced stomatal conductance (Figure 6G; Figure S7). Notably, the correlation became particularly evident when gene expression was manipulated specifically in guard cells. For instance, guard cell‐specific expression of the sugar sensor HXK1, which promotes stomatal closure, also resulted in a threefold increase in hypocotyl length (long/low). Although the pif4 mutant displays short hypocotyls and elevated transpiration (short/high), guard cell‐specific expression of PIF4 in the mutant background restores both transpiration and hypocotyl elongation, re‐establishing the long/low phenotype (Kelly et al., 2021, 2023). Similarly, guard cell‐specific HY5 activity in the hy5 mutant reinforces the inverse correlation between hypocotyl length and stomatal conductance (Kelly et al., 2021, 2023). In the present study, guard cell‐specific expression of HSFA2 in the hsfa2 mutant background restored both hypocotyl length and stomatal conductance toward WT levels (Figure 6B,C). Together, these observations indicate that guard cell genetic manipulation is sufficient to generate this inverse relationship, supporting the existence of long‐distance signals originating in stomata that influence distal growth responses.

It is important to note that hypocotyl elongation in this study was quantified under relatively low light intensity, conditions under which exogenous sucrose is expected to exert a stronger effect on elongation via PIF‐mediated signaling than at higher light intensities. Such conditions may amplify natural variation in sugar perception and downstream responses, and individual accessions may therefore exhibit context‐dependent behavior. Nevertheless, the inverse correlation between hypocotyl length and stomatal conductance was consistently observed across multiple datasets and genetic contexts, and appeared particularly pronounced when gene expression was modified specifically in guard cells. This supports the notion that guard cell‐derived signals can reinforce the developmental‐physiological coupling between seedling growth and stomatal behavior.

The observed inverse relationship between hypocotyl elongation and water loss may reflect a trade‐off that balances growth with water‐use efficiency. Elongated hypocotyls are characteristic of low‐light environments, often associated with competition and limited resources, where reduced water loss may confer an advantage. In contrast, short hypocotyls and open stomata are characteristic of high‐light environments in which maximizing photosynthesis and gas exchange becomes favorable. Recent work showing that HSFB1 affects plant growth and drought tolerance, with hsfb1 mutants displaying elongated hypocotyls similar to hsfa2 (Zheng et al., 2025), further supports this link between growth and water‐use strategies. Additionally, PIF4 has been shown to bind the G‐box of the HSFA2 promoter to promote basal thermotolerance (Yang et al., 2022), suggesting that light‐ and heat‐responsive pathways converge on shared regulatory nodes. The long/low‐short/high relationship likely reflects integration of light, sugar, and hormonal pathways, all of which influence both stomatal behavior and hypocotyl elongation. For example, ABA not only regulates stomatal closure but also modulates cell elongation (Emenecker et al., 2023; Hayashi et al., 2014; Lorrai et al., 2018). Likewise, HY5‐ and PIF4‐mediated light signaling coordinates growth and gas exchange responses (Gao et al., 2015, 2018; Kelly et al., 2023). Together, these interconnected pathways enable plants to dynamically adjust developmental growth and physiological performance to changing environmental conditions, optimizing survival and competitive fitness across developmental stages.

Molecular factors linking seedling growth with stomatal conductance

In this study, we found that changes in HSFA2 levels affect stomatal movement. The hsfa2 mutant and its associated haplotypes exhibited reduced stomatal conductance, whereas elevated expression of HSFA2 promoted stomatal opening (Figures 4C,D and 6C). In general, heat stress responses are associated with stomatal opening, a phenomenon demonstrated across multiple species, including trees (Diao et al., 2024; Marchin et al., 2022; Urban et al., 2017a, 2017b). In citrus, for example, transpiration increased up to fivefold in heat‐tolerant Carrizo citrange (Poncirus trifoliata L. Raf. × Citrus sinensis L. Osb.) plants under elevated temperature (Zandalinas et al., 2016). These observations suggest that basal HSFA2 levels may influence stomatal aperture and water loss even under non‐stress conditions.

HSFA2 has been implicated in diverse abiotic stress responses beyond heat, including drought (Andrási et al., 2021; Banti et al., 2010; Jiang et al., 2018; Kotak et al., 2007; Lim et al., 2021; Nishizawa et al., 2006; Roychoudhury, 2021). For example, hsfb1 mutants exhibit increased drought sensitivity under normal temperatures, consistent with enhanced water loss, whereas OE‐HSFB1 lines show the opposite phenotype (Zheng et al., 2025). Our data indicate that higher basal levels of HSFA2 are associated with increased water loss, positioning HSFA2 as a link between heat stress responses and stomatal opening, potentially facilitating leaf cooling under elevated temperatures. ChIP‐seq analysis revealed that HSFA2 binds its own promoter (Figure 6D), indicating an autoregulatory mechanism. Such feedback regulation may fine‐tune HSFA2 activity and ensure balanced stress responses, consistent with regulatory circuits described for other heat‐shock factors (Zheng et al., 2025). A link between heat stress memory (HSM) and sugar accumulation has recently been proposed as part of short‐term heat priming mechanisms. Following heat priming, glucose accumulation contributes to sustaining HSM (Olas et al., 2021; Sharma et al., 2019). Moreover, glucose activates TARGET OF RAPAMYCIN (TOR), which regulates HSFA1 and HSFA2 via E2Fa in shoot apical meristems (Sharma et al., 2022; Staacke et al., 2025). Collectively, these findings suggest that sugars accumulate under mild heat stress and may function as an energetic and signaling reservoir for subsequent stress responses. Our results are consistent with this conclusion. Elevated sugar levels (sucrose and glucose) induced HSFA2 expression (Figure 2I; Figure S8) and promoted HSFA2 binding to target loci, including autoactivation (Figure 6). Further, our data shows that guard cells are capable of triggering this response in a spatially specific manner, influencing developmental traits such as hypocotyl elongation through defined genetic hubs, including SCAR3 (Figure 6H). The SCAR3/WAVE complex interacts with components of ABA signaling, including ABI1, ABI3, ABI4, and ABI‐like‐1 (Waese et al., 2017). Notably, ABI1 contributes to heat priming through activation of BES1 within the brassinosteroid pathway (Albertos et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2022). The interaction between HSFA2 and SCAR3 under elevated sugar conditions therefore suggests potential crosstalk between sugar, ABA, and brassinosteroid signaling pathways in guard cells. SCAR3 also interacts with BRASSINOSTEROID‐INSENSITIVE 2 (BIN2), which represses BES1 under normal temperatures but is modulated by ABI1 during heat exposure (Albertos et al., 2022). Additionally, ACTIN‐RELATED PROTEINS (ARPs), including ARP3, physically interact with the SCAR/WAVE‐ARP2/3 complex (Yanagisawa et al., 2013; Zhang, Liu, et al., 2008), further linking HSFA2‐dependent responses to cytoskeletal remodeling processes required for stomatal dynamics. Mutations in ARPC2 cause ABA insensitivity and defective stomatal closure (Jiang et al., 2012), and the SCAR/WAVE‐Arp2/3 complex regulates dark‐induced reorganization of guard cell actin filaments during stomatal closure (Isner et al., 2017). Together, these observations provide additional insight into how HSFA2 may influence stomatal responses through SCAR3 and ARP‐dependent mechanisms, extending its functional scope beyond classical heat stress regulation.

HSFA2 also binds to BRN1, ANT1, AT2G32500 (here referred to as Stress Responsive, SR), MAPKKK21, and ARC3 in guard cells under sugar treatment. ANT1, an aromatic and neutral amino acid transporter capable of mediating auxin transport (Chen et al., 2001), exhibited reduced expression in the hsfa2 mutant. Consistently, the ant1 mutant displayed elongated hypocotyls similar to hsfa2 and scar3, suggesting that HSFA2‐dependent ANT1 expression contributes to maintaining proper auxin distribution and growth coordination. SR likewise emerged as a direct HSFA2 target whose transcript levels were reduced in hsfa2 guard cells, and its mutant exhibited elongated hypocotyls. Together with SCAR3, ANT1 and SR define a subset of HSFA2‐responsive mediators whose reduced expression correlates with increased hypocotyl elongation, supporting the existence of a guard cell‐centered module integrating developmental and physiological processes. In line with their elongated hypocotyl phenotypes, these mutants also displayed reduced stomatal conductance (Figure S7), further reinforcing the inverse relationship between early elongation growth and later stomatal behavior.

BRN1 transcript levels were significantly reduced in the stomata of the hsfa2 mutant. BRN1 binds specific 3′‐UTR elements of target mRNAs and represses translation (Kim et al., 2013); for example, BRN1/BRN2 bind the 3′‐UTR of the flowering regulator SOC1 to promote mRNA degradation (Kim et al., 2013). ARC3 encodes a chloroplast division factor required for proper chloroplast replication. Notably, ARC3 transcripts are enriched in guard cells and downregulated by ABA (Table S7), suggesting a stress‐associated role in guard cell chloroplast dynamics. In agreement, arc3 mutants displayed altered stomatal conductance under our conditions, while hypocotyl length remained comparable to WT. Direct HSFA2 binding to ARC3 therefore suggests a potential role for HSFA2 in chloroplast‐related processes within guard cells.

MAPKKK21 emerged as another HSFA2‐responsive gene with documented roles in ABA‐associated signaling, similar to MAPKKK18 (Lin et al., 2021). Although mapkkk21 mutants did not exhibit altered hypocotyl elongation, differences in stomatal conductance were observed, consistent with a possible contribution to stomatal regulation downstream of HSFA2. Together, these observations suggest that HSFA2‐responsive genes may differentially influence developmental and physiological traits depending on context. Altogether, these findings indicate that stomatal HSFA2 participates in integrating developmental, hormonal, and stress‐related pathways through multiple downstream components. While SCAR3, ANT1, and SR appear more closely associated with hypocotyl growth, additional HSFA2‐responsive genes such as BRN1, ARC3, and MAPKKK21 may primarily contribute to stomatal behavior.

A dual role for the 2OGD‐like in hypocotyl growth and stomatal movement

In addition to HSFA2, our GWAS identified a genetic locus on chromosome 1 corresponding to a gene annotated as a 2‐oxoglutarate (2OG) and Fe(II)‐dependent dioxygenase‐like protein (2OGD‐like), which appears to restrict hypocotyl elongation (Figure 3D,E,G). This gene has not previously been characterized. Nevertheless, haplotype analysis together with mutant validation supports its involvement in both hypocotyl growth and stomatal movement. The 2ogd‐like mutant consistently exhibited elongated hypocotyls, with approximately 25–30% increases in length relative to wild type (Figure 3G; Figure S5). Similar to hsfa2, the 2ogd‐like mutant displayed reduced stomatal conductance in our assays, and accessions carrying the long hypocotyl haplotype showed comparable behavior (Figures 3D and 4F,G). These findings suggest that 2OGD‐like normally acts to limit hypocotyl elongation while promoting stomatal opening, aligning with the long/low versus short/high framework described above.

The 2OG dioxygenase superfamily encompasses a broad range of enzymes, many of which participate in hormone biosynthesis or the modification of secondary metabolites that influence growth and stress responses (Han et al., 2023; Kawai et al., 2014; Wang, Abid, et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2025). Several 2OG dioxygenases catalyze key steps in gibberellin biosynthesis and catabolism, thereby directly affecting cell elongation (Lange et al., 2020). Others contribute to the production of specialized metabolites such as flavonoids (Wang et al., 2021; Wang, Liu, et al., 2022) or furanocoumarins, as recently demonstrated in citrus (Goldenberg et al., 2025). It is therefore reasonable to assume that 2OGD‐like participates in the synthesis or modification of a metabolite that coordinates growth with stomatal function.

Publicly available expression data indicate that 2OGD‐like is highly expressed in guard cells (Figure 4E), resembling the expression pattern of HSFA2. This guard cell enrichment raises the possibility that 2OGD‐like influences stomatal behavior through metabolic modulation within guard cells. Notably, both sucrose and glucose treatments induced 2OGD‐like expression in wild‐type seedlings (Figure 3H; Figure S8), and the 2ogd‐like mutant displayed partial insensitivity to sucrose‐mediated growth repression, retaining substantially longer hypocotyls than WT even at 4% sucrose (Figure 3G). These observations suggest that 2OGD‐like may participate in a sugar‐responsive pathway linking metabolic status to growth regulation. Future biochemical and metabolic profiling of the 2ogd‐like mutant, together with detailed guard cell physiological analyses, will be necessary to define the molecular pathways through which this gene influences hypocotyl elongation and stomatal conductance.

CONCLUSION

Our study demonstrates that natural variation in hypocotyl growth among Arabidopsis accessions is associated with differences in stomatal conductance at mature stages, revealing an inverse relationship between seedling hypocotyl elongation and water‐use characteristics. Collectively, these findings establish this inverse correlation as a quantitative trait influenced by natural genetic variation that can predict water‐use performance at later developmental stages. Through a GWAS‐based approach, we identified candidate genes underlying this relationship, highlighting hypocotyl growth assays as a robust and scalable platform for uncovering genetic determinants of plant water‐use efficiency. Importantly, this correlation becomes more pronounced when gene expression is specifically manipulated in guard cells, underscoring the contribution of guard cell‐derived signals in coupling early developmental growth with later stomatal behavior. Further examination of these genetic factors will deepen our understanding of how developmental and physiological traits are integrated and may provide new avenues for improving stomatal regulation, whole‐plant transpiration, and drought‐related performance.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant material

Diverse collection of 308 A. thaliana natural accessions previously described (Horton et al., 2012; Li et al., 2010) was used to measure hypocotyl length for GWAS with existing SNP data (Table S1). The hsfa2 (Salk_008978), 2ogd‐like (At1g06640, Salk_041759), axr1‐3 (CS3075), mapkkk21 (Salk_018714), Scar3 (a‐ Salk_073641, b‐ Salk_036994, c‐ Salk_051959), brn1 (Salk_041205), arc3 (Salk_057144), ant1 (a‐ SALK_046627, b‐ Salk_090394), and At2g32500 (Salk_134850) mutants were obtained from the Arabidopsis Information Resource (ABRC) stock (https://abrc.osu.edu). T‐DNA single mutant lines were genotyped by primers designed using the SIGnAL primer design tool (http://signal.salk.edu/tdnaprimers.2.html) powered by the Genome Express Browser Server (GEBD; Table S5). The pES‐HSFA2 vector (HSFA2 under the control of an estradiol‐ dependent promoter) was kindly provided by the lab of Prof Salma Balazadeh (Georg‐August‐Universität Göttingen in Germany).

Plasmid construction, and generation of transgenic KST1pro:HSFA2‐GFP/hsfa2 plants

The GCHSFA2‐GFP construct used in this study was assembled using the GoldenBraid (GB) modular cloning system (Figure S9; Sarrion‐Perdigones et al., 2013). The guard cell‐specific partial promoter KST1 ppro (Kelly, Lugassi, et al., 2017) was synthesized by GeneArt (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Regensburg, Germany) and cloned into the pUPD (1) vector. The HSFA2‐GFP coding sequence was designed by fusing the AtHSFA2 gene (At2g26150) to GFP via an alanine linker, synthesized by GeneArt, and cloned into pUPD (2). The OCS terminator pUPD (3) was generously provided by the laboratory of Diego Orzaez (Universitat Politècnica de València, Valencia, Spain). The pUPD's (1–3) constructs were subcloned into the Alpha1 vector. A transcriptional unit containing the 35S promoter, the Basta resistance gene, and the OCS terminator was synthesized and cloned into the Alpha2 vector. Final assembly into the Omega1 vector was carried out according to the GB protocol, using T4 DNA ligase and either BsaI‐HF or BsmBI enzymes (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). The assembled plasmid (Figure S9) was introduced into Escherichia coli DH5α heat‐shock competent cells. Colonies were selected on appropriate antibiotic media supplemented with X‐gal and verified by PCR and Sanger sequencing. Subsequently, 100 ng of the verified plasmid was transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 by heat shock. Arabidopsis transformation was performed using the floral‐dip method (Clough & Bent, 1998), and screening of positive plants was performed on half Murashige and Skoog (0.5× MS; Duchefa Biochemie, Haarlem, The Netherlands) medium supplemented with 3% sucrose (pH 5.8, 0.8% plant agar) and 20 mg L−1 Basta (Duchefa).

Growth conditions

For plants growing in soil, a potting mix containing (w/w) 70% peat, 30% perlite, supplemented with slow‐release fertilizer (Even‐Ari 7611, Israel) was used, and the plants were kept in a walk‐in growth chamber (10 h light/14 h dark photoperiod, 150 μmol m−2 sec−1).

Hypocotyl growth assay

For the hypocotyl growth assay, seeds were surface sterilized and sown onto Petri plates (9 mm diameter) containing 0.5× MS medium as a control (pH 5.8, 0.8% plant agar) or 0.5× MS with 1% sucrose (Suc; Duchefa), as indicated for each experiment. Seeds were cold‐treated at 4°C in darkness for 3 days before they were transferred to a growth chamber (16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod at 22°C–23°C). Light intensity, measured by Apogee MQ‐200 quantum meter (Apogee Instruments, Logan, UT, USA), was kept at 40 μmol m−2 sec−1. Unless mentioned otherwise, seedlings were imaged 8 days after the transfer to the growth room. To determine hypocotyl length, images were analyzed using the ImageJ software (http://rsb.info.nih.gov/ij/) fit‐line tool, following size calibration. A detailed list of hypocotyl lengths for all natural accessions under different treatments used for the GWAS analysis is provided in Table S1.

Genome‐wide association mapping, calculation of heritability, haplotype, and LD analysis

Following the methodology of Wu et al. (2018), we mapped hypocotyl length to genetic loci. The R packages EMMAX (Efficient Mixed‐Model Association eXpedited; Kang et al., 2010) and GAPIT (Genomic Association and Prediction Integrated Tool, version 23‐May‐18; Lipka et al., 2012) were used to create the Associations. A mixed linear model containing fixed and random effects characterized the population structure using the first three principal components (Q matrix; Price et al., 2006) to incorporate this information together with the VanRaden kinship matrix (Eu‐Ahsunthornwattana et al., 2014) as fixed and random effects, respectively (method = ‘MLM’). Hypocotyl length measurements from ~300 distinct plant accessions were mapped to loci in the A. thaliana genome using the GAPIT function and 199 455 SNP markers with minor allele frequency >1% obtained using Affymetrix GeneChip Array 6.0 (TAIR version 9; Horton et al., 2012; Li et al., 2010) using PCA.total = 3, model = ‘MLM’, SNP.fraction = 1.0 (all other parameters were set to default). The logarithm of odds (LOD) threshold was set to 5.3 (−log10(1/N) with N the number of SNPs). The resulting SNPs with LOD ≥5.3 were assigned to the same group if the genomic distance between them was less than 10 kb, and the genes within the respective groups were considered as candidate genes. Broad‐sense heritability (H2) was defined by the proportion of the total variance explained by the genetic variance according to Fusari et al. (2017) using the lmer function and obtaining the variances by the function VarCorr from lme4 (v1.1–23; Bates et al., 2015).

For haplotype analysis, the distance between haplotypes was calculated from the SNPs by the dist. gene function from the ape (v5.3; Paradis & Schliep, 2019) package (method = ‘pairwise’, pairwise.deletion = FALSE, variance = FALSE). Distances were clustered by the hclust function (method = ‘ward.D’), and the tree was cut by cutree (h = 0.00001) from the stats package (v3.6.2). To test for statistical relation between haplotypes and hypocotyl lengths, anova (anova from the stats package, v3.6.2) was performed with false discovery rate (FDR) correction (P.adjust with method = ‘BH’). For LD analysis, the P‐values were taken from the GWAS results file for the respective mass feature (LOD = −log10(P‐value)). Standardized LD, r 2 values were calculated via the function r2fast from the GenABEL package (v1.8‐0; Aulchenko et al., 2007). Expression analysis for genes of interest was conducted within the eFP browser (Winter et al., 2007) using the data set of Schmid et al. (2005). The scripts can be found at https://www.github.com/tnaake/GWAS_arabidopsis_seed.

Thermal imaging

Thermal images of 3‐week‐old plants were obtained from the FLIR T660 IR thermal camera (FLIR, Wilsonville, OR, USA). Following image acquisition, leaf temperature was quantified using the FLIR designated software according to the manufacturer instructions.

Gas‐exchange analysis

Stomatal conductance was measured on fully expanded leaves of plants grown under controlled chamber conditions using an LI‐600 leaf porometer (LI‐COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA). Measurements were performed on the abaxial leaf surface under steady‐state conditions. For β‐estradiol treatments, mature plants were sprayed with 0.05% (v/v) TWEEN20 (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with 10 μM β‐estradiol (Sigma‐Aldrich), dissolved in ethanol. Control plants were sprayed with an equivalent concentration of ethanol without β‐estradiol. Stomatal conductance was measured 4 h after treatment.

Net photosynthesis was measured using a LI‐COR Li‐6800 portable photosynthesis system (LI‐COR Biosciences). Measurements were performed on fully expanded leaves under controlled chamber conditions. For steady‐state measurements, photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) was set to 150 μmol m−2 sec−1 and reference CO2 concentration was maintained at 400 μmol mol−1. For light‐response analyses, net photosynthesis was recorded across increasing PPFD levels of 50, 100, 200, 300, 500, 700, 900, 1200, and 1400 μmol m−2 sec−1, while maintaining CO2 concentration at 400 μmol mol−1.

Microarray database processing and analysis

For the publicly available data of comparing guard cell expression in response to Suc, expression in guard cell compares with mesophyll and in epidermal peel compare to whole leaf (Figure 4A,E), data was obtained from the ePlant website (https://bar.utoronto.ca/eplant) (Schmid et al., 2005), and following the data generated by Bates et al. (2012) for Figure 4A3.

Expression data for AtHSFA2, At1g06640, and additional candidate genes identified in GWAS analysis and displayed in Figure S8 were obtained from the NASCarrays microarray database (Craigon et al., 2004), Experiment No. 593. A heat‐map diagram was computed using ClusViz tool (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/clustvis/) (Metsalu & Vilo, 2015) based on the expression data obtained from the NASCArray database. The accession number and the Affimetrix probe ID number for each gene are listed in Table S6. Gene accession numbers were assigned according to the information in the TAIR database (http://www.arabidopsis.org/).

RNA extraction cDNA preparation and quantitative real‐time PCR

To assay gene expression following the application of sugar, plants were grown on sterile culture plates containing 0.5 × MS medium (0.5× MS; Duchefa Biochemie, The Netherlands) for 3 weeks. On the morning of the experiment, 2 h after the lights switched on, sucrose was added, absorbed in the medium to a final concentration of 4% for 4 h, following the procedure described in Kelly, Sade, et al. (2017). Then, two leaves were collected from each plant for quantitative real‐time PCR experiment. For RNA extracted from seedlings, seedlings were grown horizontal in conditions as described in the ‘hypocotyl‐growth assay’ section of Material and Methods. At least 40 seedlings were collected for each sample, snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted using the Logspin method (Yaffe et al., 2012). The RNA was treated with RQ1‐DNase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions, to degrade any residual DNA. The purity of all RNA samples was assessed by 260/280 and 260/230 nm absorbance ratios. For the preparation of cDNA, total RNA (1 μg) was taken for reverse‐transcription PCR using qscript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Quanta BioSciences, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions Quantitative PCR was performed using the PikoReal 96 Real‐Time PCR System (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA) and qPCRBIO SyGreen Blue Mix Hi‐ROX kit by PCR Biosystems (London, UK). The melting point was determined for each sample to validate the specificity of the primers. The Arabidopsis AtTIP41 (accession no. At4g34270) was used as a reference for the normalization of cDNA amounts. The primers used for the amplification procedure are listed in Table S4.

Guard cell transcriptome analysis

WT and hsfa2 plants were grown in soil for about 35 days before taken for guard cell transcriptome analysis. Guard cell protoplast isolation was performed as described previously (Ueno et al., 2005; Ye et al., 2015). RNA extraction, transcriptome sequencing and analysis were performed as described in Guo et al. (2025). DEGs identified from the guard cell transcriptome comparison between hsfa2 and WT are listed in Table S3.

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and pathway enrichment

Differential‐expression (DE) analysis was performed with the DESeq2 package (Love et al., 2014) of the R software. Transcripts that were more than 2‐fold differentially expressed with an adjusted P‐value of no more than 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. GO and pathway‐enrichment analyses were performed using KOBAS program (Xie et al., 2011), to determine significant annotated processes of the main biological functions. The KEGG PATHWAY (www.genome.jp/kegg/), BioCyc (https://biocyc.org) and GO databases were used with multiple testing correction of FDR (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). The threshold was set to FDR with a corrected P‐value of less than 0.05. The Scientific and Research Plot Tool (SRplot) was used to analyze the results obtain from enrichment analysis (Tang et al., 2023). The heat‐map diagrams were generated using ClusViz tool. Venn diagrams were generated using the Venny 2.1 online tool (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing

ChIP assays were performed as described by Saleh et al. (2008). Briefly, seedlings were grown in MS plates for 3 weeks. Following 3 h exposure to 4% sucrose, the seedlings were then infiltrated with a 1% formaldehyde solution under vacuum for cross‐linking. The reaction was quenched by adding glycine to a final concentration of 125 mM, followed by vacuum application. The cross‐linked grains were ground into a fine powder, and nuclei were isolated. Chromatin was then extracted, sonicated, and precleared according to Saleh et al. (2008). For immunoprecipitation, XFlag‐trap agarose beads (GTA‐20, ChromoTek, Planegg, Germany) were added, and DNA‐protein complexes were eluted using 500 μl of elution buffer (1% SDS, 100 mM NaHCO3). Reverse cross‐linking was performed in the presence of 200 mM NaCl. Proteins were digested with proteinase K, and DNA was purified using phenol‐chloroform extraction. ChIP‐seq was conducted at the Crown Genomics Institute, Nancy and Stephen Grand Israel National Center for Personalized Medicine, Weizmann Institute of Science (https://g‐incpm.weizmann.ac.il/units/CrownGenomics/about). Adapters were trimmed using cutadapt, and reads shorter than 40 nucleotides were discarded (cutadapt option –m 30). Processed reads were uniquely aligned to the reference genome using bowtie (version 1.0.0) (Langmead et al., 2009). Bound regions were identified using MACS2 (version 2.0.10.20131216) (Zhang, Mallery, et al., 2008), and gene assignments for genomic regions were performed using GREAT (McLean et al., 2010). ChIP enrichment statistics for genome features were obtained using CEAS (version 1.0.2) (Shin et al., 2009).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the JMP 14 software program. Box plots were prepared using the Graph Builder tool. Means were compared using Student's t‐test, Tukey's HSD test, or Dunnett's method, as described for each experiment. Means were considered to be significantly different at P < 0.05.
