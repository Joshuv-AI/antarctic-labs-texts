Gut microbial remodeling as an ecological strategy for host metabolic management under environmental stress on the Xizang Plateau
Authors: Cheng Pan, Yangzong Zhaxi, Junru Pan, Cisang Zhuoma, Xiaofeng Luo, Wenrui Ye, Pan Tan, Haiyan Li, Zhenzhen Zhang, Deji Quzong, Duoji Ouzhu, Qiongla, Tianzeng Song, Wangsheng Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The adaptive responses of gut bacteria and host metabolism in Xizang sheep to the decline in forage quality and the extremehigh-altitude environment remain largely unclear. This study utilized Xizang sheep as a model, and employed 16S rRNA sequencing, metabolomics, and RT-qPCR to investigate changes in the composition and functional pathways of the gut bacteria, as well as hepatic and muscular metabolism, in response to these stressors. The results indicated that in response to these stressors, the complexity of the gut microbial network in Xizang sheep was reduced, yet the overall positive correlations within the community were enhanced, accompanied by a significant increase in the abundance of Fibrobacterota and Lachnoclostridium, while the abundance of Bacteroides decreased. Furthermore, metabolomic analysis based on gene co-expression network (WGCNA) revealed that the glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway in the liver was suppressed, while the lysine degradation pathway was activated in muscle. These findings reveal the physiological adaptations of the gut bacteria, liver, and muscle of Xizang sheep in response to seasonal variations in grass quality at high altitudes. This research provides a theoretical framework for understanding ruminant adaptation to high-altitude environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05423-8.

Introduction

The gut bacteria plays a critical role in regulating nutrient absorption [1], energy metabolism [2], immune function [3], and ecological adaptation [4]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that its composition and function are regulated by various factors, including host genetic background (such as sex [5], age [6], and genetic composition [7]), as well as environmental factors (such as dietary structure [8], husbandry practices [9], and habitat characteristics [10]). Among these, diet serves as a primary external factor and is an indispensable in modulating the structure and function of the gut bacteria. In grazing ruminants, the nutritional composition and availability of pasture grasses fluctuate with periodic changes in environmental conditions [11]. For instance, in the Xizang Plateau ecosystem, summer pastures are nutrient-rich, whereas their nutritional value significantly declines during the transitional phase, presenting the host with dynamic changes in nutrient supply [12, 13]. Such fluctuations in resources not only impact the composition and function of the gut bacterial community but also pose challenges to the host’s metabolic homeostasis.

The liver functions as the central metabolic regulatory organ of the body, governing the conversion and homeostatic balance of various energy substrates including gluconeogenesis [14], fatty acid oxidation [15], and amino acid metabolism [16]. Concurrently, skeletal muscle, the largest metabolically active tissue, dynamically adjusts to fluctuations in energy demands by regulating protein turnover [17], remodeling muscle fiber types [18], and modulating mitochondrial biogenesis and function [19]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the liver and skeletal muscle play pivotal roles in environmental adaptation across multiple species. For instance, in goats [20], sheep [21] and yaks [22], metabolic organ plasticity has been shown to be closely associated with environmental challenges such as high-altitude conditions and thermal stress.However, existing research has predominantly focused on isolated changes within individual organs, lacking a systematic analysis of the synergistic regulatory mechanisms among multiple organs, especially research on Xizang sheep.

The recent proposal of the ‘gut-organ axis’ concept has provided a novel theoretical framework for understanding systemic host-microbiome interactions [23]. Research indicates that the gut microbiome can influence the physiological functions of distal organs via the circulatory system or neural pathways through signaling molecules such as short-chain fatty acids [24], secondary bile acids [25], and tryptophan metabolites [26]. For instance, the gut-liver axis and gut-muscle axis are increasingly recognized as pivotal links regulating immune modulation, growth and development, meat quality traits, and adaptive capacity to environmental stressors in ruminants [27–29]. While the potential roles of gut-liver and gut-muscle axes in ruminants are being progressively uncovered, the synergistic responses mediated by gut-liver and gut-muscle axes in Xizang sheep exposed to high-altitude environmental stress have yet to be fully elucidated.

Therefore, we hypothesize that Xizang sheep may adapt to seasonal variations in high-altitude environments through the coordinated regulation of the gut-liver/muscle axis. This study employs 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing and metabolomics techniques to describe the synchronous changes in gut bacterial communities, liver metabolism, and muscle metabolism of Xizang sheep during the grazing season. The results provide theoretical insights for understanding the adaptive responses of plateau ruminants and the sustainable development of animal husbandry in high-altitude areas.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and sampling

This experiment was conducted from June to November 2023 in Maqian Village, Bango County, Xizang Autonomous Region, China. During the summer period (June 1 – August 31), 6 healthy Sewa sheep (Xizang sheep) approximately one year of age and with similar body weights were selected and assigned to the summer group (S). During the autumn period (September 1 – November 30), another six healthy Xizang sheep of similar age and body weight were selected and assigned to the autumn group (A). All sheep were sourced from the same farm in Bangor County, Xizang Autonomous Region. All detailed grazing management and body weight change data for the animals are provided in the reference literature [30]. Prior to slaughter, all sheep were fasted for 12 h and then euthanized by intravenous injection of sodium pentobarbital (> 30 mg/kg) following anesthesia, with supplemental doses administered as needed to ensure a painless death. According to the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for Euthanasia [31], sodium pentobarbital injection is one of the internationally recognized and widely accepted methods for animal euthanasia, and it has been employed in numerous studies involving sheep [32]. Samples of the longissimus dorsi muscle (between the 13th and 14th ribs), liver tissue (margin of the right lobe of the liver), and colon contents were collected, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored in an ultra-low temperature freezer at -80 °C. The slaughter process strictly followed the guidelines and regulations approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Southwest University of Science and Technology (Approval No.: L2022014).

Forage sample collection and chemical composition analysis

The simulated grazing method was employed to collect grass samples, with all forage clipped at a fixed stubble height of 5–6 cm from the ground surface. In the main grazing area of the sheep flock, 10 points were randomly selected (with a distance of more than 10 m between sampling sites). The pasture is primarily composed of Kobresia, Stipa purpurea, and Leontopodium. The collected forage was dried in an oven at 60 °C for 24 h until a constant weight was achieved, and then ground in a mill and sieved through a 1 mm sieve for further analysis. The contents of crude protein, crude fat (EE), crude ash (CA), Ca, and P were measured using AOAC methods [33].

DNA extraction

The whole genome DNA was extracted from the colonic contents of Xizang sheep using the CTAB method, and its concentration and purity were assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Based on the results, the DNA was diluted to 1 ng/µL with sterile water and re-evaluated for purity and concentration via gel electrophoresis. The V3–V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR using primers B341F and B806R (B341F: CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG, B806R: GGACTACHVGGGTATCTAAT). The PCR reaction was carried out with 15 µL of Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs), 2 µM of forward and reverse primers, and approximately 10 ng of template DNA. The thermal cycling program consisted of an initial denaturation at 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 50 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min.

16S rRNA amplicon sequencing and analysis

The 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing from the colon contents of Xizang sheep was conducted by Wuhan MetWare Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, Hubei, China) using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). To obtain high-quality sequence data, the raw sequencing reads were first assembled and filtered. Subsequently, the Deblur algorithm was applied for sequence denoising, generating amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Mothur software (v1.48) was used to perform taxonomic annotation of the representative ASV sequences. Microbial community composition and structure were analyzed at multiple taxonomic levels, including phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species. To identify the shared and unique ASVs among different samples and to assess species richness and evenness within samples, the following α-diversity indices were calculated: Shannon index, Simpson index, Chao1 index, ACE index, Observed ASVs, and PD whole tree. Based on the weighted UniFrac distance of ASV abundances, β-diversity analysis was performed using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) to evaluate intergroup differences. In addition, the t-test was employed to analyze differences in microbial composition and community structure between the two groups. Finally, the functional potential of the microbial communities was assessed using the phylogenetic investigation of communities by reconstruction of unobserved states (Tax4Fun2). Furthermore, the association between bacteria and key metabolites was also evaluated using Spearman’s correlation coefficient.

Construction of microecological networks

Using the corAndPvalue function from the WGCNA package in R, microbial abundance data were read from a CSV file and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients (r) along with their statistical significance (P-values) were calculated between taxonomic units (ASVs). P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Only significant correlations with |r| ≥ 0.6 and FDR < 0.001 were retained, while non-significant edges were set to zero. A weighted undirected network was then constructed based on the filtered correlation matrix using the igraph package, and isolated nodes were removed. Nodes with taxonomic information were matched at the kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, and genus levels, and these annotations were assigned as node attributes. Finally, a GraphML file was exported for network visualization using Gephi.

Metabolomics detection and analysis

The liver and muscle tissue samples stored at -80 °C were placed on ice (all subsequent operations were performed on ice). After thoroughly grinding the samples in liquid nitrogen it was followed by accurately weighing 20 mg (± 1 mg) of each sample and transfered it to correspondingly labeled centrifuge tubes. In the tubes, 400 µL of a 70% methanol aqueous solution containing an internal standard was added and thencentrifuged at 1500 r/min for 5 min, and allowed to stand in an ice bath for 15 min. A second centrifuge was done at 4 °C and 12,000 r/min for 10 min, then transfered 300 µL of the supernatant to another correspondingly labeled centrifuge tube, and store at -20 °C for 30 min. A last centrifuge was done at 4 °C and 12,000 r/min for 3 min, then aspirate 200 µL of the supernatant and transfered it to an insert in an autosampler vial for subsequent machine analysis. Data visualization analysis was performed using the Metware Cloud platform (https://cloud.metware.cn). The criteria for screening differentially accumulated metabolites were as follows: (1) Absolute value of log₂(Fold Change) > 1; (2) Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) value > 1; (3) False Discovery Rate (FDR)-adjusted P < 0.05 based on Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method was used for multiple testing correction to control the FDR.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and real-time quantitative PCR

Liver and muscle tissue samples were processed strictly according to standard operating procedures. Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara Bio Inc., Dalian, China) and subjected to quality assessment. Qualified RNA samples were treated with the PrimeScript™ RT Kit (including gDNA Eraser) for genomic DNA removal and subsequent cDNA synthesis through reverse transcription. The quantitative analysis of target gene mRNA expression levels was performed using real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR). The reaction system was based on TB Green Premix Ex Taq™ II (Tli RNaseH Plus) reagent, and amplification and detection were carried out on the CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The relative expression levels of the target genes were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. The sequences of the gene-specific primers used are listed in Table S1.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

The WGCNA package in R software was used to construct the WGCNA co-expression network. Outlier samples in WGCNA can affect the analysis of module results; therefore, outlier samples were removed prior to WGCNA. First, the correlation coefficients among metabolites were calculated, and co-expression modules were constructed using the identified metabolites. To determine whether metabolites exhibit similar expression patterns, an appropriate soft threshold was selected [34] to ensure that the connectivity between metabolites in the network follows a scale-free topology. Subsequently, the co-expression network was constructed using the selected soft threshold [35], and metabolites were clustered into modules based on their expression patterns. Metabolites with similar expression patterns were grouped into the same module, with different colors representing distinct modules. Module-trait associations were calculated and visualized as a heatmap. Modules were considered significantly associated with phenotypic traits at P < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Unless otherwise specified, all statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 21.0 software. Inter-group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test. Statistical significance was defined as follows: P < 0.05 was considered significant, and P < 0.01 was considered highly significant.

Results

Alterations in colonic bacterial composition and function

We established a research model to investigate the seasonal adaptability of natural grazing (Fig. 1). All sheep were raised under free-range conditions on alpine pastures at an elevation of approximately 4,750 m, with unrestricted access to forage and water throughout the study. The levels of crude protein (CP), crude fat (EE), crude ash (CA), and P in the summer forage were significantly higher than those observed in the autumn group (P < 0.05). In contrast, the Ca content was significantly lower in the summer group compared to the autumn group (P < 0.05, Table S2).

To investigate the effects of declining in forage quality on the structure and function of gut bacteria in Xizang sheep in a high-altitude environment, we conducted 16S rRNA sequencing on intestinal content samples. Microbial ecological network analysis revealed that, compared to the S group, the number of nodes in the A group decreased to 580, the number of edges dropped to 561, and the average degree declined to 1.93. This indicates a reduction in microbial interconnections and a decrease in community complexity under these conditions (Fig. 2B-C). Meanwhile, compared to the S group, the proportion of positive correlation edges in the A group increased (81.64% vs. 79.93%), while the proportion of negative correlation edges decreased (18.36% vs. 20.07%, Fig. 2B-C).

Alpha diversity analysis showed no significant differences between the A group and the S group concerning theChao1, observed_ASV, PD_whole_tree, and ACE indices (P > 0.05, Fig. 3A). However, the Shannon index was significantly higher in the A group compared to the S group (P < 0.01, Fig. 3A), and the Simpson index was also significantly higher in the A group (P < 0.05, Fig. 3A). Beta diversity analysis revealed a significant separation between the A group and the S group (P < 0.05, Fig. 3B). At the phylum level, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were the dominant microbial groups in both groups (Fig. 3C), with the F/B ratio exhibiting an increasing trend in the A group (Table S3). At the genus level, the relative abundance of Bacteroides, a genus involved in secondary bile acid formation [36], was lower in the A group compared to S group (Fig. 3D, Table S4). The results of LEfSe analysis for identifying biomarkers between the two groups revealed that, at the genus level, the candidate signature taxa in the S group included Ruminococcus and Bacteroides, while those in the A group included Fibrobacter and Monoglobus (Figure. S1). Subsequently, using T-test analysis to screen for significantly different bacteria at the phylum and genus levels between the two groups, we found that the relative abundance of Fibrobacterota at the phylum level was significantly higher in the A group (P < 0.01, Fig. 3E). At the genus level, the relative abundances of Lachnoclostridium, Angelakisella, Dorea, and Tyzzerella were also significantly increased in the A group (P < 0.05, Fig. 3E). Furthermore, functional analysis using Tax4Fun2 showed that compared to the S group, the Adipocytokine signaling pathway and cAMP signaling pathway, which are involved in regulating energy metabolism [37, 38], were significantly upregulated at KEGG level 3 in the A group (P < 0.05, Fig. 3F).

The analysis of the hepatic metabolomic dataset from Xizang sheep revealed that, with the β parameter set to 8 (Fig. 4A), a total of 3,269 metabolites were clustered into 23 modules (Fig. 4B). The module-trait association analysis demonstrated that a significant positive correlation between the salmon module and AL (R² ≥ 0.7, P < 0.05) (Fig. 4C). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated significant enrichment of the module’s eigemetabolites in Glycerophospholipid metabolism, Linoleic acid metabolism, and alpha-Linolenic acid metabolism (P < 0.05, Fig. 4D). Notably, within the Glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway, the metabolites triacylglycerol and monoacylglycerol were significantly downregulated in the AL group (P < 0.05, Fig. 4E). Additionally, the integration analysis between the eigemetabolites of the salmon module and DEMs identified five overlapping metabolites (Fig. 4F), which included two significantly upregulated and three significantly downregulated metabolites (Fig. 4G).

Screening and functional analysis of key metabolites in the muscle

Analysis of the muscle metabolomic dataset from Xizang sheep indicated that when the β value was set to 6 (Fig. 5A), a total of 2,329 metabolites were categorized into 14 distinct modules (Fig. 5B). Module-trait association analysis revealed that the red module exhibited a significant positive correlation with AL (R² ≥ 0.7, P < 0.05) and the blue module showed a significant negative correlation with AL (R² ≥ 0.7, P < 0.5) (Fig. 5C). KEGG enrichment analysis showed that characteristic metabolites within both the red and blue modules were significantly enriched in pathways including Terpenoid backbone biosynthesis, Glutathione metabolism, and Lysine degradation (P < 0.05, Fig. 5D). Specifically, within the Lysine degradation pathway, the metabolites Pipecolic acid and Succinic acid were significantly upregulated in the AL group (P < 0.05, Fig. 5E). Furthermore, we analyzed the overlap between the eigemetabolites of the red and blue modules and DEMs, identifying 28 and 189 overlapping metabolites respectively (Fig. 5F).

Discussion

The structure and function of the gut bacteria are dynamically regulated by the nutritional environment [39]. Previous studies have demonstrated that nutrient deprivation can significantly reshape the composition of microbial communities [40–42]. For instance, in lambs under conditions of solid feed deficiency, the Simpson Index of rumen bacteria decreases, accompanied by reductions in both the number of nodes and edges in the microbial co-occurrence network, indicating a simplification of microbial interaction networks [43]. In this study, the gut bacteria of Xizang sheep may adopt similar adaptive strategies. Specifically, under conditions of forage nutrient deficiency, the number of nodes, edges, and average connectivity in the gut microbial network were all reduced, suggesting a decreased complexity in interspecies interactions, which is consistent with the findings reported by Yu et al. [43]. Furthermore, correlation analysis of the gut bacteria revealed that the proportion of positive associations increased to 81.64%, while negative associations decreased to 18.36%, indicating a shift in microbial survival strategy from competition-dominated to cooperation-dominated.

The colonization and shaping of key gut microbes play a crucial role in host environmental adaptation. A previous study on Hulunbuir grazing sheep demonstrated that the combination of feed restriction and cold stress triggered adaptive remodeling of rectal bacterial communities, thereby improving energy acquisition from poor forage [44]. This finding is consistent with the adaptive patterns observed in Xizang sheep in the present study. Under conditions of forage nutrient deficiency, the F/B ratio in the gut bacteria of Xizang sheep was significantly increased. This finding is consistent with the observation in forest musk deer (Moschus berezovskii), where the F/B ratio is higher during the withered grass season compared to the herbaceous season [45]. A gut bacteria characterized by a high F/B ratio has been associated with enhanced fermentation efficiency, enabling the host to extract more energy from limited dietary resources [46]. This suggests that an elevated F/B ratio may represent a conserved microbial strategy employed by ruminants to cope with nutritional scarcity in forage. Furthermore, this study revealed a significant increase in the relative abundance of Fibrobacterota, known for cellulose degradation capacity [47], indicating an adaptive response of the gut bacteria to low-quality forage. Concurrently, at the genus level, the relative abundances of Lachnoclostridium and Dorea, both involved in carbohydrate fermentation [48, 49], were also significantly increased. This further supports the notion that the gut microbial community enhances its capacity for carbohydrate fermentation under nutrient-limited conditions, thereby maximizing energy harvest from fibrous plant material.

The liver is a crucial metabolic organ for the environmental adaptation of Xizang sheep. In response to the decline in pasture quality, the expression levels of key intermediate metabolites TAG and MAG in the glycerophospholipid metabolic pathway in Xizang sheep liver were significantly downregulated. This indicates that Xizang sheep adapt to seasonal environmental changes on the plateau by suppressing glycerophospholipid metabolism. Under the hypoxic conditions of high altitude, host fatty acid metabolism faces significant challenges. The complete oxidation of fatty acids is highly dependent on oxygen availability. Numerous studies have demonstrated that animals often respond to hypoxic stress by downregulating fatty acid metabolic pathways [50, 51]. This adaptive strategy aligns with the findings of the present study, which indicate that the linoleic acid metabolism and alpha-Linolenic acid metabolism pathways are significantly enriched in the liver of Xizang sheep. This suggests that Xizang sheep may adapt to the high-altitude hypoxic environment by modulating the metabolic pathways of polyunsaturated fatty acids to regulate membrane fluidity. Additionally, the PPAR signaling pathway is crucial for processes such as metabolism, lipolysis, and lipogenesis [52, 53]). Under winter grazing conditions, differentially expressed genes in the PPAR signaling pathway in yak liver tissue were significantly enriched, with genes involved in lipogenesis (e.g., SCD, FASN, and FADS2), the rate-limiting step of fatty acid uptake (e.g., SLC27A2), and fatty acid oxidation (e.g., CPT1C) showing downregulated expression [54]. This phenomenon is consistent with the findings of the present study, which indicate that genes involved in key steps of fatty acid oxidation in the liver of Xizang sheep, such as ACADL, ACOX1, and CPT2, are significantly downregulated. This downregulation suggests that fatty acid metabolism is suppressed in the liver of Xizang sheep.

Muscle, as a primary energy metabolism organ involved in energy metabolism, is shown in this study to be associated with host adaptation to high-altitude environments. One of the final products of lysine degradation is acetyl-CoA, which can enter the TCA cycle to generate ATP [55, 56]. When the quality of pasture declines, the expression levels of piperine and succinate, an intermediate metabolite in the tricarboxylic acid cycle, are significantly upregulated in Xizang sheep muscle. This suggests that the lysine degradation pathway is activated, which may provide additional carbon sources and energy support for sustained muscle contraction and high energy demands in high-altitude environments. Succinate serves not only as an intermediate in energy metabolism but also as a crucial signaling molecule that can stabilize HIF-2α [57]. This stabilization transcriptionally activates a series of genes essential for hypoxia adaptation [58–60]. In this study, we found that HIF-2α in the muscle of Xizang sheep is activated due to the decline in forage quality. This activation leads to the upregulation of VEGF, which is involved in iron metabolism and angiogenesis [61], and EPO,which plays a role in involved in the regulation of erythropoiesis [62]. These adaptations facilitate the sheep’s response to environmental changes.

Existing studies have suggested that the metabolic functions of the liver, muscle, and gut bacteria play significant roles in adaptation to high-altitude environments [63–67]. However, these studies have primarily focused on individual organs. In recent years, the multi-axis synergistic mechanism of the gut–organ axis has garnered increasing attention in ruminants [27]. Based on this, we propose the hypothesis that the microbiome–gut–liver/muscle axis is essential for Xiang sheep in adapting to high-altitude environmental changes. Regarding the gut–liver axis, previous studies have shown that Bacteroides uniformis can ameliorate host glycolipid metabolism disorders by regulating bile acid metabolism, a process dependent on the gut–liver axis [68]. In this study, we observed that a decline in forage quality significantly reduced the abundance of Bacteroides involved in primary bile acid metabolism, while simultaneously inhibiting the glycerophospholipid metabolic pathway in the liver. Therefore, we hypothesize that the reduced abundance of Bacteroides may attenuate gut–liver signaling related to bile acid metabolism [69], thereby suppressing hepatic lipid metabolism. This may be one of the strategies employed by the Xizang sheep to actively reduce energy consumption in response to the decline in forage quality. Regarding the gut–muscle axis, previous studies have demonstrated that an optimized gut bacteria composition can enhance muscle mass, endurance, and antioxidant capacity in mice [70, 71], and that bacterial utilization of lactate contributes to improved exercise performance [72]. In this study, we found an increase in the abundance of Fibrobacter within the gut bacteria of Xizang sheep, accompanied by a significant upregulation of pipecolic acid and the TCA cycle intermediate succinate in muscle [73]. We hypothesize that Fibrobacter may promote the production of propionate and supplement the tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediate succinate, providing energy substrates for muscle.

Conclusion

In summary, this study reveals the comprehensive metabolic adaptation strategies of Xizang sheep in response to seasonal declines in forage quality on the plateau. The reduction in forage quality decreases the complexity of the gut microbial co-occurrence network while enhancing positive interspecies synergies among bacteria. Concurrently, it increases the gut F/B ratio and enriches Fibrobacterota, which are cellulose-degrading bacteria, thereby improving the host’s efficiency in degrading fibrous forage substrates and capturing energy. At the metabolic level, the glycerophospholipid metabolic pathway in the liver is suppressed, with significant downregulation of key fatty acid β-oxidation genes, including ACADL, ACOX1, and CPT2. In muscle, levels of pipecolic acid and the TCA cycle intermediate succinate are significantly upregulated, accompanied by the stabilization of HIF-2α and the upregulation of VEGF and EPO. Furthermore, this study proposes a synergistic regulatory hypothesis involving the gut-liver axis and the gut-muscle axis. Collectively, our findings preliminarily elucidate the adaptive metabolic changes in gut bacterial communities, liver, and muscle of Xizang sheep across different grazing seasons, providing a reference for understanding gut-organ axis interactions in ruminant adaptation to high-altitude environments.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the free‑grazing regime precluded the recording of individual feed intake. Second, the microbial characterization relied solely on 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing of bacterial communities, with no coverage of anaerobic fungi or ciliates, and we did not evaluate key markers of intestinal barrier integrity or epithelial transport pathways. Furthermore, the uniform 12‑hour pre‑slaughter fasting may have perturbed the metabolic signatures of liver and muscle tissues. Future studies should integrate metagenomic approaches, supplement data on anaerobic fungi and protozoa, and combine assessments of intestinal barrier morphology and epithelial transport factors to validate the regulatory pathways of the gut–organ axis.
