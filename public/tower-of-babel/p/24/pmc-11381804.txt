Hong Kong's first human case of B virus: Feeding monkeys, a risky practice!
Authors: Amogh Verma, Amna Zaheer, Kiran Bisht, Abhay M Gaidhane, Mahalaqua Nazli Khatib, Sanjit Sah, Ganesh Bushi, Ranjana Sah, Anil Kumar
Published: 2024

The recent identification of Hong Kong's first human case of the B virus, or Herpes simiae, marks a critical juncture in public health surveillance and response [1,2]. Identified by the Centre for Health Protection (CHP) of the Department of Health (DH), this case involved a 37-year-old man who was admitted to Yan Chai Hospital on March 21, 2024, after exhibiting symptoms of fever and altered consciousness. Subsequent tests confirmed the presence of B virus in his cerebrospinal fluid, necessitating intensive care due to the severity of his condition.

The B virus, primarily carried by rhesus (Macaca mulatta) and cynomolgus (Macaca fascicularis) monkeys, members of the macaque genus that are prevalent in Hong Kong's natural habitats, poses a significant zoonotic threat [3]. Transmission occurs primarily through direct contact with the bodily fluids of infected monkeys, such as saliva and urine, or through bites and scratches (Fig. 1) [4]. The patient in question encountered wild monkeys during a visit to Kam Shan Country Park, where he sustained injuries, underscoring the potential for zoonotic spillover in human-wildlife interactions. Historically, human infections with the B virus have been rare but severe, with documented cases mainly among individuals with occupational exposure to macaques, such as laboratory workers and veterinarians, totaling approximately 50 cases globally since the identification of this virus, with a mortality rate of approximately 40 % [4]. This highlights the critical need for heightened awareness and preventive measures in at-risk populations.

Transmission cycle and clinical manifestations of B virus (Herpes simiae) infection, including self-care measures.

Symptoms of B virus infection initially mimic flu-like illnesses, including fever, muscle aches, and fatigue [5]. As the infection progresses, it can lead to severe neurological complications, including encephalitis, highlighting its rapid onset and potentially fatal consequences if untreated. Prompt medical intervention, typically involving antiviral therapies such as acyclovir, is crucial for managing severe cases and improving outcomes [4,5]. Tourists often offer food to wild monkeys at popular sites, such as Kam Shan Country Park, unaware of the potential health risks associated with such practices. This behavior not only disrupts natural animal behavior, but also increases the risk of zoonotic disease transmission, as exemplified by this recent case. Preventive strategies recommended by the CHP emphasize avoiding contact with wild monkeys and refraining from feeding them. In cases of injury from monkey bites or scratches, immediate medical attention and thorough wound care with soap, water, and disinfectants are essential to mitigate the risk of infection. Public awareness campaigns play a pivotal role in educating communities about the dangers posed by zoonotic diseases and the necessary precautions to prevent their transmission.

In conclusion, the emergence of the B virus in Hong Kong serves as a poignant reminder of the dynamic interplay between human activity and wildlife health. As urbanization encroaches on natural habitats, interactions between humans and wildlife increase, increasing the risk of zoonotic disease transmission. Through concerted efforts in surveillance, prevention, and public education, health authorities can mitigate risks, protect community well-being, and advance global health security in an increasingly interconnected world.
