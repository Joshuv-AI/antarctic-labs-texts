A stress-induced PI3P complex controls autophagy in erythroid precursors
Authors: Pooja Roy, Blake A. Rose, Suhita Ray, Venkatasai R. Dogiparthi, Meg A. Schaefer, Cheryl L. Stucky, Suyong Choi, Nicholas T. Woods, Kyle J. Hewitt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySamd14 coordinates stem cell factor/Kit receptor signaling and is crucial for the survival of erythroid precursors in anemia contexts. Here, we show that Samd14 is needed to maintain balanced autophagy in erythroid precursors following acute anemia. Autophagy-associated gene signatures and protein levels are deregulated when erythropoiesis accelerates in acute anemia. Mechanistically, Samd14 interacts via its SAM domain with phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PI3P), an integral component of endosomal and autophagic membranes. Pharmacologic inhibition of VPS34, the class III PI 3-kinase that generates PI3P, impaired erythroid differentiation specifically in stress contexts. Loss of Samd14 shifted the sensitivity of erythroid precursors to VPS34 inhibition, where higher doses were required to block differentiation. Given the critical roles of autophagy in normal differentiation, Samd14’s stress-dependent activation and roles in autophagy suggest that this mechanism is needed to maintain progenitor levels and balance the production of healthy, mature red blood cells in anemia.

Introduction

During normal red blood cell differentiation, cell size decreases, and organelles are cleared to streamline hemoglobin production. Intracellular components such as mitochondria and ribosomes are recycled by the well-known process of autophagy.1,2 Autophagy-related genes and protein expression are elevated throughout erythroid cell commitment to control cell size and maintain protein quality control.3,4 The autophagy machinery is influenced by cytokine signaling pathways, including stem cell factor (SCF)/Kit that regulate aspects of cell differentiation including self-renewal and survival.5,6,7 Among the important steps, the PI-3-kinase vacuolar protein sorting-34 (VPS34) enzyme produces phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PI3P) where it accumulates to initiate autophagosome membranes.8 Autophagy mechanisms play a role in red cell disorders including β-thalassemia and myelodysplastic syndrome.2,4,9

While autophagy is required for the terminal stages of normal erythropoiesis, it is also a critical mechanism of cellular responses to stress.5 To wit, how do red blood cell progenitors integrate autophagy mechanisms required for normal erythropoiesis with stress-dependent changes in environmental conditions (e.g., in acute anemia)? The accelerated erythropoiesis needed to restore homeostasis in anemia is carried out by a unique population of stress erythroid progenitor (SEP) cells.10 After lineage commitment by stress signals, SEPs undergo a period of rapid expansion characterized by a requirement for unique signaling cues.11,12 SCF/Kit and downstream activation of ERK and AKT/mTOR are critical mediators of SEP expansion and activity.13,14,15 Following treatment with the red blood cell-lysing chemical phenylhydrazine (PHZ), mice bearing loss-of-function alleles of the Kit receptor have severe delays in SEP colony forming activity.11 Kit and mTOR signaling, along with autophagy, are integrated into processes of erythroid progenitor activation, expansion and differentiation to relieve acute anemia.13

Anemia is common, but knowledge about anemia recovery and key mechanisms involved in balancing the distinct requirements of “stress erythropoiesis” are incomplete.10 Prior work identified GATA factor-dependent transcriptional activation of genes predicted to control acute responses to anemia.16,17,18 The sterile alpha motif (SAM)-containing protein-14 (Samd14) is anemia-regulated via an intronic GATA factor-regulated enhancer.16 Samd14 enhancer knockout mice (S14ΔEnh/ΔEnh) fail to recover from severe anemia caused by PHZ injection.16 Samd14 boosts the erythroid system’s potential for regeneration.19 Samd14 has a conserved SAM, which is required for anemia-dependent SCF/Kit signaling, survival, and progenitor colony-forming activity.15,20 SAMs provide interfaces for molecular interactions. SAMs often self-interact or can form SAM-SAM oligomers and polymers, and SAMs can bind DNA, RNA, and lipids.21 While the SAM domain of Samd14 is required for stress progenitor cell activity and cell signaling downstream of Kit activation, heterologous proteins interacting with this domain or other molecular interactors are not known.

In this study, we investigated the process of autophagy in SEPs and the role of Samd14. We identified a SAM-dependent interaction between Samd14 and the autophagy-associated PI3P phospholipid. In both mouse and human erythropoiesis, the PI3P-producing enzyme VPS34 promotes differentiation. We link the Samd14-PI3P molecular association with a need to rebalance deregulated autophagy during expansion and differentiation of SEPs.

Results

Molecular attributes of autophagy are Samd14-sensitive in stress erythroid progenitors

A phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate lipid interacts with the Samd14 SAM

SAMs in SAM-containing proteins play widely varying roles in cell biology, including autophagy, but no known SAM-dependent protein-protein interactions of Samd14 have been detected.20,26 As approximately 40% of SAMs self-associate,27 we tested whether Samd14 self-associates via its SAM. Mouse GATA1-and Samd14-null G1E cells were co-infected with retrovirus containing FLAG-tagged and HA-tagged Samd14 (Figure 3A). Immunoprecipitation (IP) with anti-HA antibody followed by western blotting with anti-FLAG antibody revealed that HA-tagged Samd14 failed to pull down the FLAG-tagged Samd14 (Figure 3B). Instead, abundant FLAG-tagged Samd14 remained in the IP supernatant (Figure 3C). This holds true even when lysis conditions varied from 1% NP-40 to 0.1% NP-40 (Figure 3D). Phosphoinositides represent potential molecular interactors with the Samd14-SAM.28 Sequence alignment of Samd14-SAM from different vertebrate species revealed an evolutionarily conserved polybasic motif (PBM) located at the C-terminal end of its SAM domain (Figure 4A). PBMs are a cluster of basic amino acids that many proteins utilize to engage in electrostatic interactions with the inositol head group of phosphoinositide.30,31 AlphaFold predicted a solvent side PBM in the 5th alpha helix of Samd14’s SAM (Figure 4B). We hypothesized that a SAM-dependent Samd14 interaction with phosphoinositides facilitates erythroid progenitor cell activity in anemia recovery. To test this, we performed a protein-lipid overlay blot using affinity purified protein lysates. G1E cells (lacking endogenous Samd14 expression) were retrovirally infected with expression constructs containing HA-tagged full length Samd14, SAM deleted (S14ΔSAM), capping protein binding (CPB) deleted (S14ΔCPB), both domains deleted (S14ΔCS) or a point mutation in the putative polybasic motif (S14-R381Q) (Figure 4C). Full length Samd14 binds PI3P with high specificity, while also binding at lower affinity to PI4P and PI5P (Figure 4D). The Samd14-PI3P interaction was undetectable using the SAM-deleted Samd14 or the SAM/CPB-deleted Samd14. However, the molecular interaction was maintained using the CPB-deleted Samd14 (Figure 4D). While prior reports revealed a CP-PI3P interaction that controls actin assembly,32,33 our results show that Samd14 interacts independently with PI3P and CP through distinct domains. A CP-PI3P interaction cannot facilitate Samd14’s interaction with PI3P. The Samd14 PBM-mutant (S14R381Q) protein bound PI3P at much lower affinity (Figure 4D). To quantify the Samd14-PI3P interaction, we conducted a proximity ligation assay (PLA) in cells expressing HA-tagged Samd14 (Figure 4E). Compared to full length Samd14, the number of PLA puncta was 3.3- and 1.9-fold lower in cells expressing S14ΔSAM or S14R381Q, respectively (Figure 4F). To test the importance of the Samd14-PI3P interaction in erythroid progenitor activity, we expressed full length or deleted/mutant Samd14 in embryonic day 14.5 (E14.5) fetal liver cells and conducted colony formation (CFU) assays. As previously reported, Samd14’s SAM is needed to promote progenitor activity.15 Cells expressing S14R381Q formed 1.9-fold fewer burst forming unit-erythroid colonies (p = 0.01) compared to cells expressing full length Samd14 (Figure 4G). The PI3P-producing VPS34 enzyme is needed for early- and late-stage erythroid differentiation

PI3P is a relatively low-abundant lipid moiety in cells localized to early endosomes and in autophagic membranes.34 Our discovery that Samd14 binds specifically to PI3P and not PI(3,5)P2 in the PIP strip assay hints at Samd14 localization to early endosomes and eventual release as they transition into late endosomes. Another pool of PI3P localizes within autophagic membranes.35 If Samd14 localized to endosome/autophagosome membranes, then autophagy induced by serum starvation may relocate Samd14 to associate with intracellular organelles. To test this, we performed subcellular fractionation of crude organelles after serum starvation in human erythroleukemia (HEL) cells (Figure S1A). We detected an increase in the relative amount of SAMD14 present in the organelle compartment 4 h post serum-starvation (Figure S1B). LAMP2, RAB7, RAB5, LC3, CAPZB and GAPDH were used to validate separation of the organelle fraction from the cytosol (Figures S1C and S1D). To test whether this phenomenon depends on PI3P synthesis, which is catalyzed by the VPS34 enzyme, we treated cells with the VPS34 inhibitor SAR405.36 Inhibiting PI3P synthesis prevented Samd14 localization to organelles (Figures S1E and S1F).

To test the role of PI3P and VPS34 activity in erythropoiesis, primary erythroid precursors derived from WT mouse E14.5 fetal liver were treated with SAR405 for 72 h (Figure 5A). SAR405 treated fetal liver cells grew slower (Figure 5B) and cell pellets were paler in color compared to DMSO-treated controls (Figure 5C). Cell surface markers CD71 and TER119 were used to track erythropoiesis over 3 days in culture37 (Figure 5D). VPS34 inhibition decreased the percentage of committed erythroid cells (CD71highTER119-) by 1.5-fold (p = 0.001, dose 5 μM) commensurate with increased percentages of maturing erythroid cells (CD71+TER119+) by 1.3-fold (p = 0.05) (Figure 5E). Strikingly, mature erythroid cells (CD71low/negTER119+) decreased by 2.2-fold (p = 0.01) (Figure 5E). R3 erythroblasts of SAR405 treated groups contained a higher fraction of Kit positive cells (5.1-fold, p = 0.001) (Figure 5F). SAR405-treated cells expressed 2.5-fold more Gata2 mRNA, and 2.3-fold and 1.9-fold lower globin (Hba-a2) and Glycophorin A (Gypa) mRNA compared to controls, respectively, consistent with a less differentiated culture (Figure 5G). We next evaluated whether VPS34 inhibition could alter the kinetics of human erythropoiesis (Figure S2A). Primary human CD34+ cells were treated with SAR405 during erythroid differentiation and evaluated by flow cytometry using CD71 and CD235a cell surface markers (Figure S2B). Treatment with 5 μM SAR405 for 8 days reduced the percentage of CD71high CD235a+ cells in the culture by 2.3-fold (p = 0.001) compared to controls (Figure S2C), indicating that SAR405 treatment inhibited differentiation. SAR405 treated cultures had fewer CD71high CD235a+ cells at day 12 of differentiation as well (percentage 1.8-fold lower, p = 0.001) (Figures S2D and S2E). Later stages of maturation were quantified after 19 days of differentiation (Figure 6A). Erythroblast stages were marked by CD235a+ cells that progressively lose cell surface expression of CD49d (or α4 integrin) and gain band 3 at later stages (Figure 6B).38 As with earlier time points, SAR405 treated cultures contained fewer CD235a+ cells vs. controls (Figure 6C). In the CD235a+ population, we observed the following changes in SAR405 treated cells vs. controls: (1) an increase in the proportion of cells in population I (band3−CD49dhigh); (2) a decrease in population II (band3+CD49dhigh), and III (band3+CD49dlow/med); (3) an accumulation of cells in population IV (band3+CD49d−), (Figure 6D); and (4) fewer viable CD235a+ enucleated cells which stained negative for the membrane-permeable DNA dye DRAQ5 (Figures 6E and 6F). The Samd14-PI3P complex modulates erythropoiesis and autophagy in mouse stress progenitor cells

We asked whether there were unique requirements for PI3P in the context of stress erythropoiesis. WT and Samd14flox/flox mice crossed to a hematopoietic specific driver of Cre recombinase (S14-CKO) were treated with PHZ for three days prior to isolation and ex vivo culture of splenic erythroid precursors (Figure 7A). SAR405 treated cells were paler compared to DMSO-treated controls (Figure 7B). SAR405 treated cultures had a lower frequency of CD71+TER119+ cells than control populations and less differentiated CD71+TER119- cells (Figure 7C). To quantify autophagy flux in the context of SAR405-mediated inhibition of VPS34, we expressed the GFP-LC3-RFP fluorescent probe in splenic stress erythroid precursor cells (Figure 8E).39 This probe measures autophagic flux using a GFP-tagged LC3 protein (with an internal RFP control for expression levels) whose fluorescence is quenched by lysosomal pH (Figure 8F). The GFP/RFP median fluorescence intensity (MFI) signal ratio is inversely proportional to the rate of autophagic flux (Figure 8G). As expected, the GFP/RFP signal ratio was lowest (high autophagic flux) in the mature R4 (CD71low/negTER119+) cells, which indicates that autophagy flux increases during erythropoiesis (Figure 8H). Across all stages of erythroid maturation, SAR405 treatment decreased autophagy flux (Figure 8H). Samd14-deficient samples exhibited increased autophagic flux in the immature CD71+TER119- erythroid population compared to WT controls (Figure 8I). Autophagic flux changes through stages of erythropoiesis and is mediated by VPS34 activity and Samd14 expression.

Discussion

The identification of a lipid interacting partner for the Samd14-SAM domain has resolved a puzzling gap in knowledge regarding Samd14 molecular mechanisms to restore homeostasis in anemia. The PI3P lipid moiety serves as a docking site for Samd14 and other proteins which are capable of both promoting and opposing the tightly regulated process of autophagy.8 Prior reports show that PI3P interacts with another Samd14-interacting protein partner, the actin capping protein (CP) complex, to control actin polymerization.33 Our data show that Samd14 interacts with both PI3P and all three subunits of the CP complex as a multi-subunit complex at endosome/autophagosome membranes (Capzα1, Capzα2, and Capzβ).20 As PI3P is a spatial coordinator of signaling molecules and proteins involved in membrane formation,45 and Samd14 loss alters autophagy flux, this suggests that the PI3P-Samd14-CP complex plays a direct role in autophagy.

Autophagy is closely coupled with cell signaling pathways controlled by Samd14. As a pro-proliferation and survival pathway, the SCF/Kit receptor tyrosine kinase (which is Samd14-regulated) activates PI3K and AKT signaling, which generally leads to less autophagy in cells.6,46,47 Oncogenic Kit mutations have also been shown to induce autophagy in leukemic cells.48 While direct evidence for PI3P-binding effector proteins (other than Samd14) in Kit signaling is lacking in the literature, PI3P can control signaling through epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR).49 PI3P influences the duration of downstream signaling through the MAPK pathway.50 Our phospho-proteomics data, and other evidence linking the Kit signaling facilitator Samd14 and its interacting protein/lipid to autophagy, support the idea that SCF/Kit signaling suppresses autophagy in erythropoietic contexts. Autophagy responses to pro-recovery and pro-differentiation signaling pathways are essential for terminal differentiation in erythropoiesis, and in stress contexts. While mutations to Samd14 have not to-date been linked to human blood disorders or hematologic malignancy, Samd14 expression is deregulated in other cancer contexts.22 Our findings here that Samd14 is anemia-responsive in chronic sickle cell anemia mice suggests a general model in which Samd14 is anemia-activated in progenitor cells to promote RTK signaling and block autophagy. More work is needed to evaluate the consequences of Samd14 loss- or gain-of-function in other hematologic disorders.

Cells initiate autophagy responses during stress without causing cell death. Pro- and anti-autophagy mechanisms are needed to control this balance.51 Both excessive and ineffective autophagy can cause anemia.1 Samd14 is one of several autophagy regulators previously linked to erythropoiesis in normal and stress contexts, in which targeted deletion in mice yields anemia phenotypes. Other examples include the ATG4 protein, which controls autophagosome fusion in late, differentiating erythroblasts,52 ULK1 which is needed for the effective clearance of mitochondria in reticulocytes,53 and FIP200 which is an autophagy-promoting protein that is needed during embryonic development for selective autophagy.54 Transcriptional control of autophagy has been well established to occur downstream of the GATA1 transcription factor.3,55 Autophagy-related proteins were upregulated in erythroid progenitors after acute anemia, several of which were downregulated at the RNA level, suggesting potential feedback loops. The distinct requirements for Samd14 expression in the context of red blood cell regeneration led us to propose a model in which GATA factor-mediated Samd14 upregulation acts as a brake on excessive autophagy in acute anemia, thereby promoting cell and organismal survival. Our findings add to a body of knowledge surrounding autophagic control in erythropoiesis, implicating the GATA factor-regulated Samd14 protein as a relevant modulator of autophagy during stress erythropoiesis.

Limitations of the study

Our study used female mice for ex vivo experiments since males are more sensitive to PHZ-induced death due to hemolytic anemia. While female stress progenitor cells have more robust expansion capacity for use in ex vivo culturing experiments, the study design may potentially lose valuable information regarding sex as a biological variable. Our study analyzed hematopoietic and erythropoietic progenitors isolated from spleen in anemia stress, as well as fetal liver and bone marrow progenitors. While conclusions can be extrapolated to indicate stress vs. steady state conditions, it is important to note that the different anatomical sites (bone vs. spleen) may confound interpretation. Erythroid progenitors from non-anemic mouse spleens are rare and do not grow ex vivo in the same culture conditions (likely due to quiescence) and are therefore exceedingly difficult to transfect and study in direct comparison to cells isolated from PHZ-treated spleens. Due to current technical limitations, our study did not address whether the Samd14-PI3P interaction is needed for autophagy in vivo. Among the exciting next steps, it is important to assess whether the PI3P-interacting SAM is required for Samd14-mediated autophagy, and whether/how autophagy mechanisms are distinct in stress vs. steady state erythropoiesis.

Experimental model and study participant details

G1E and HEL 92.1.7 cell culture

Gata1-null Erythroid (G1E) cells (from the Mitch J. Weiss lab) were maintained at 0.5×106 cells/mL density in Iscove’s DMEM medium supplemented with 15% FBS, 0.5% mSCF-conditioned medium from Chinese Hamster Ovary cells, 0.1 mM monothioglycerol (Sigma) and 2 U/mL erythropoietin (Amgen). The Samd14 intronic enhancer was knocked out in cells (G1E-ΔEnh) by TALEN-mediated deletion. HEL 92.1.7 (ATCC) cells were grown in RPMI 1640 supplemented with10% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin and maintained at a density of 0.5×106 cells/mL.

Human CD34+ erythroid culture

Peripheral blood CD34+ cells were obtained from Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center. Cells were cultured using a four-phase differentiation protocol over a period of 19 days (Lee et al., 2015). An initial expansion phase (day 0-4) was followed by three successive differentiation phases: differentiation I (day 5-12), differentiation II (day 13-17), and differentiation III (day 17-19). During the expansion phase cells were cultured in serum-free expansion media (StemSpan SFEM) supplemented with CC100 cytokine cocktail (StemCell Technologies). The basal media for erythroid differentiation contained Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium, 15% FBS, 2 mM glutamine, 1% BSA, 1.5% penicillin–streptomycin, 500 μg/mL human holo transferrin (Sigma-Aldrich) and 10 μg/mL recombinant human insulin (Sigma-Aldrich). The basal media was supplemented with 1 μM Dexamethasone, 1 μM β-estradiol, 5 ng/ml rhIL-3, 100 ng/mL rhSCF, and 6 U/mL rhEpo for differentiation I, with 50 ng/mL rhSCF and 6 U/mL rhEpo for differentiation II, and with 2 U/mL rhEpo for differentiation III. Cultures were maintained at 0.5 - 1×106 cells/mL.

Mice

All animals used in this study were maintained on a C57BL/6 genetic background. Samd14-Enhancer knockout (S14ΔEnh/ΔEnh) mice were generated by site-directed TALEN deletion (Hewitt et al., 2017). Age and sex matched wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 mice (Jackson Laboratory strain 000664) served as controls for experiments using S14ΔEnh/ΔEnh mice. Samd14 conditional knockout (S14-CKO) mice was generated by crossing Samd14 floxed (Samd14fl/fl) mice (Schaefer et al., 2023) to the hematopoietic-specific Vav-Cre line (Jackson Laboratory strain 008610). Age and sex matched Samd14fl/flCre- littermates served as controls for experiments using S14-CKO mice. All mice used to isolate primary cells for ex vivo studies were females aged between 8 to 12 weeks. Spleens from Berkeley Sickle Cell Disease mice (HbSS) were provided by the Cheryl L. Stucky lab. All animal experiments met the ethical standards set by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Nebraska Medical Center.

Mouse primary cell culture

To induce hemolytic anemia, 8- to 12-week old female mice (WT, S14-CKO and S14ΔEnh/ΔEnh) were subcutaneously injected with a single dose (100 mg/kg) of phenylhydrazine (Sigma Aldrich) freshly resuspended in sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS). After 72 hours, spleens or bone marrow (femur and tibia) harvested from WT, S14-CKO mice or S14ΔEnh/ΔEnh mice were dissociated in PBS supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Similarly fetal liver was harvested from day 14.5 WT and S14-CKO embryos. Single-cell suspensions were obtained by passing cells through a 35 μm nylon filter. Cells were labeled with biotin-conjugated antibodies against lineage markers of T-cells (anti-mouse CD34ε; clone 145–2 C11), macrophages (anti-mouse CD11b; clone M1/70), B-cells (anti-mouse CD19; clone 6D5 and anti-mouse CD45R/B220; clone RA3-6B2), granulocytes (anti-mouse Gr-1; clone RB6-C5) and erythrocytes (anti-mouse Ter119). Erythroid precursors were enriched by negative selection of biotinylated cells using MojoSort streptavidin-conjugated magnetic nanobeads (Biolegend). The resultant lineage depleted cells were cultured in erythroid expansion medium: StemPro-34 (Invitrogen) containing 2 mM L-glutamine, 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin, 0.1 mM monothioglycerol, 1 μM dexamethasone, 0.5 U/mL erythropoietin, 1% mSCF Chinese Hamster Ovary cell conditioned medium, and SAR405/DMSO. Cells were maintained at a density of 0.5×106 cells/mL for 72 hours (fetal liver and spleen-derived erythroid cultures) or 96 hours (bone marrow-derived erythroid cultures).

Method details

Plasmid constructs

HA-tagged full-length Samd14, Samd14 lacking the SAM domain (S14ΔSAM), Samd14 lacking the capping protein binding (CPB) domain (Samd14 ΔCPB), and Samd14 lacking both SAM and CPB domains (S14ΔCS) (Ray et al., 2022) were cloned into mammalian expression plasmid pMSCV-Puro-IRES-GFP (Addgene #21654) using BglII and EcoRI restriction digestion sites. To generate Samd14 lacking the polybasic basic motif (S14R381Q), the full length Samd14 construct was mutated at arginine position 381 to glutamine using site-directed mutagenesis. Flag-tagged full length Samd14 cloned into pMSCV-Puro-IRES-mCherry was purchased from Vector Builder. All retroviral MSCV plasmids as well as the pMRX-IP-GFP-LC3-RFP (Addgene #84573) were packaged with pCL-Eco (Addgene #12371) in 293T cells using calcium phosphate transfection.

Phospho-proteomics

WT and (S14ΔEnh/ΔEnh) spleens were manually dissociated 3 days post-PHZ injection. Kit+ cells were isolated with biotin-conjugated anti-mouse CD117 (Kit) antibody and MojoSort streptavidin-conjugated magnetic nanobeads (Biolegend). 107 Kit+ cells per condition (N=3 mice per genotype) were serum-starved for 2 hours in 1% BSA/IMDM at 37°C and subsequently treated with 10 ng/ml SCF or vehicle (PBS) for 25 mins. 100 μg of protein was alkylated by addition of 8 μl 0.5M IAM in water for 40 min and acetone precipitated overnight at -20°C. Trypsin digested protein lysates were checked for extent of digestion >95% and TMT labeled according to the TMTpro Mass Tag Labeling Reagents and Kits User Guide. Labeled peptides were phosphopeptide enriched on titanium beads. Elution from the titanium beads was carried out with 5% ammonium hydroxide and 5% pyrrolidine. Phosphopeptide fractions were loaded onto a 75 μm x 2 cm Acclaim PepMap 100 C18 trapping column in 1.5% acetonitrile, 0.05% TFA at a flow rate of 5 μl/min using a RSLC nano Ultimate 3000 chromatographic system (Thermo Scientific). The samples were run twice using 2 different sets of 3 compensation voltages: -40, -60, -80 and -50, -70, -90. FAIMS-CID-MSA MS2 (in the ion trap MS) with RTS SPS MS3 (in the Orbitrap MS). Raw mass spectrometry data was analyzed using Proteome Discoverer 2.4 (ThermoFisher). Identified phospho-sites and abundances were compared using RokaiXplorer against the reference mouse proteome.

SAM dimerization assay

For SAM-SAM dimerization assay, we generated G1E-ΔEnh cells expressing both HA-tagged and Flag-tagged full length Samd14. A second experimental group comprised cells expressing HA-tagged SAM-deleted Samd14 together with Flag-tagged full length Samd14. Negative controls included cells expressing either HA-tagged Samd14 alone or Flag-tagged Samd14 alone or the empty vector. Full length or mutant Samd14 expression was achieved by retroviral spinoculation (2600 rpm for 90 minutes at 30 °C) of 1×106 G1E-ΔEnh cells with 100 μL viral supernatant, polybrene (8 μg/ml) and HEPES buffer (10 μg/ml). Cells were maintained at 0.5×106 cells/mL density for 48 hours after which either GFP+ or mCherry+ or double positive cells were sorted in a FACSAria (BD Life Sciences) and expanded for up to a week. For HA pulldown experiments, 3 × 107 sorted cells expressing different Samd14 constructs were harvested and lysed for an hour in 1% or 0.1% NP-40 lysis buffer (150 nM NaCl, 50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 2 mM DTT, 0.2 mM PMSF, 20 μg/ml leupeptin) at 4 °C. Lysates were incubated with HA-agarose beads (Pierce) overnight, washed and boiled with SDS lysis buffer. Recombinant protein expression was detected by western blotting using anti-rabbit HA (Cell Signaling Technology; 1:2000) and anti-rabbit DYKDDDDK tag antibody (Cell Signaling Technology; 1:4000).

PIP strip

G1E-ΔEnh cells were infected with retrovirus to express HA-Samd14 (full length), HA-S14ΔSAM (missing SAM domain), HA-S14R381Q (PBM mutant), HA-S14ΔCPB (missing capping protein binding domain), HA-S14ΔCS (missing both capping protein binding domain and SAM domain) or empty vector. After sorting GFP+ cells, HA-tagged recombinant proteins were immunoprecipitated with HA-agarose beads (Pierce). Once HA-tagged proteins were eluted from the bead with synthetic HA peptide (Pierce #26184), samples were passed through Vivaspin500 10kDa MWCO columns (Cytiva #28932225) to remove 1.1 kDa HA peptide while retaining the 50-55 kDa Samd14 protein. To control for correct completion of steps involved in this assay, we spotted approximately 150 ng protein and 0.5 uL of primary and secondary antibody directly onto open areas of the dry membrane. Spots were allowed to dry completely before blocking the membrane with 1% non-fat dry milk in TBST. Membranes were incubated with 2 μg/mL purified protein in 1% milk/TBST followed by incubation with anti-rabbit SAMD14 primary antibody (1:2000) and anti-rabbit HRP conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000). The blots were probed with ECL substrate and developed using X-ray films.

Proximity ligation assay (PLA) and confocal microscopy

PLA was performed using the Duolink PLA kit (Sigma Aldrich). FACS purified GFP+ G1E-ΔEnh cells expressing recombinant SAMD14 constructs were cytospun onto charged slides at 800g for 5 minutes at low acceleration such that each spot had 50,000 cells. The slides were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 minutes at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 minutes and stained according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Duolink PLA kit). We used primary antibodies against mouse PI3P (Echelon Biosciences; 1:300) and rabbit HA-tag (Cell Signaling Technology; 1:1200). Images were acquired by confocal laser scanning microscopy (Zeiss 880) and PLA puncta was quantified using the ImageJ plugin Andy’s Algorithm (Law et al., 2017).

Colony formation assay

Fetal liver isolated from day 14.5 S14-CKO embryos were dissociated in PBS containing 2% FBS and passed through a 35 μm nylon filter to obtain single-cell suspensions. Cells were labeled with biotin-conjugated antibodies against lineage markers of T-cells (anti-mouse CD34ε; clone 145–2 C11), macrophages (anti-mouse CD11b; clone M1/70), B-cells (anti-mouse CD19; clone 6D5 and anti-mouse CD45R/B220; clone RA3-6B2), granulocytes (anti-mouse Gr-1; clone RB6-C5) and erythrocytes (anti-mouse Ter119). Erythroid precursors were enriched by negative selection of biotinylated cells using MojoSort streptavidin-conjugated magnetic nanobeads (Biolegend). Post lineage depletion, fetal liver erythroblasts were retrovirally infected to express either empty vector (EV), Samd14, S14ΔSAM or S14R381Q constructs. For retroviral infections, 100 μL viral supernatant was added to 1×106 lineage depleted cells along with polybrene (8 μg/ml) and HEPES buffer (10 μl/ml) in a 12-well plate. Cells were spinoculated at 2600 rpm for 90 minutes at 30 °C and thereafter maintained at a density of 0.5×106 cells/mL. The cells were cultured in erythroid expansion medium: StemPro-34 (Invitrogen) containing 2 mM L-glutamine, 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin, 0.1 mM monothioglycerol, 1 μM dexamethasone, 0.5 U/mL erythropoietin, and 1% mSCF Chinese Hamster Ovary cell conditioned medium. After 40-42 hours of culture, GFP+ cells were purified using FACS. Sorted GFP+ cells were mixed with Methocult M3434 (STEMCELL Technologies) containing Epo, SCF, IL-3 and IL-6 and plated in 3 replicate wells of a 12-well plate (5 × 103 cells in 0.5 mL). BFU-E colonies were counted 6 days after culturing.

Subcellular fractionation

HEL 92.1.7 cells (ATCC) were grown in full media (RPMI 1640, 10% FBS, 1% Pen/Strep) for 24 hours. Cells were starved in RPMI 1640 for 4 hours prior to fractionation. SAR405 treated cells (Selleck Chemicals #S7682) were grown for 24 hours in full media supplemented at 1μM SAR405 or an equal volume of DMSO. This was followed by 4-hour starvation in RPMI 1640 alone containing SAR405 or DMSO. Cells were harvested using the Minute™ Plasma Membrane/Protein Isolation and Cell Fractionation Kit (Invent Biotechnologies, Plymouth, MN). Sample fractions were lysed for 30 minutes on ice in a mild lysis buffer (50mM Tris-HCl, 150mM NaCl, 1mM EDTA, 1% Triton-X, pH 7.5) in presence of protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche cOmplete™, Mini, EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail). Samples were centrifuged at max speed for 10 minutes at 4°C and supernatant was collected for BCA protein quantification assay (Pierce #23227). Equal amounts of protein were loaded in an SDS-PAGE and analyzed by western blotting.

Autophagy flux assay

The pMRX-IP-GFP-LC3-RFP construct (Addgene #84573) was packaged into retroviral particles with pCL-Eco (Addgene #12371). Lineage-depleted splenic stress erythroid precursor cells were retrovirally infected to express the GFP-LC3-RFP fluorescent probe. 100 μL of viral supernatant was added to 1×106 cells along with polybrene (8 μg/mL) and HEPES buffer (10 μL/mL). Cells were spinoculated at 2600 rpm for 90 minutes at 30 °C and thereafter maintained in erythroid expansion media at a density of 0.5×106 cells/mL. Approximately 16-18 hours post spinoculation, half the media was replaced with 2X SAR405-supplemented media. After another 48 hours in culture, autophagy flux was measured using flow cytometry.

Flow cytometry

Mouse primary cells (fetal liver, spleen and bone marrow) were stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies (all from BioLegend) specific to surface proteins CD71, TER119 and Kit for 45 minutes to an hour on ice. Human CD34+ cells were stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies specific to surface proteins CD71 (BioLegend), CD235a (BioLegend), CD49d (BioLegend) and Band3 (American Research Products) for 45 minutes on ice. For enucleation assay, antibody-labeled cells were washed with PBS and stained with DRAQ5 (BioLegend) for 15 minutes at room temperature. Cells were washed once with PBS, resuspended with the viability dye DAPI (Invitrogen) and analyzed with BD LSRII Y/G or BD LSRFortessa (BD Life Sciences). Data analysis was performed with FlowJo 10.10.0.

Western blotting

Protein lysates were prepared by boiling in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) lysis buffer (25mM Tris pH 6.8, 2% β-mercaptoethanol, 3% SDS, 5% bromophenol blue, and 5% glycerol) for 10 minutes at 95 °C. Proteins were resolved by SDS- PAGE and detected with Pierce ECL Western blotting substrate (Thermo Scientific) using X-ray film processor. Primary antibodies used are as follows: anti-Samd14 (Hewitt lab), anti-HA-Tag (Cell Signaling Technology), anti-Flag DYKDDDDK Tag (Cell Signaling Technology), anti-CapZ-β (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti-LC3A/B (Cell Signaling Technology), anti-β-Tubulin (Cell Signaling Technology), anti-β-Actin (Cell Signaling Technology), anti-phospho-AMPKα (Thr172) (Cell Signaling Technology), anti-AMPKα (Cell Signaling Technology), anti-phospho-eIF2α (Ser51) (Cell Signaling Technology), anti-eIF2α (Cell Signaling Technology), anti-GAPDH (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti-Rab5 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti-Rab7 (Cell Signaling Technology), anti-LAMP2 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Secondary antibodies include goat-anti-mouse-IgG-HRP (Jackson ImmunoResearch) and goat-anti-rabbit-IgG-HRP (Jackson ImmunoResearch).

Quantitative PCR

RNA was extracted from 1 to 2 × 106 cells with 1 mL TRIzol (ThermoFisher). For cDNA synthesis, 1 μg of RNA was mixed with 200 ng oligo(dT) primers and 50 ng random hexamer primers and incubated at 68 °C for 10 minutes. Reverse transcription was carried out using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (NEB) in the presence of 10 mM DTT, RNasin (Promega), and 0.5 mM dNTPs at 42 °C for 1 hour, followed by heat inactivation at 98 °C for 5 minutes. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed on a QuantStudio 3 system (Applied Biosystems) using 2 μl of cDNA, Power SYBR Green Master Mix (ThermoFisher), and 200 nM gene-specific primers on the Quant Studio 3 real time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). Relative expression levels were determined from a standard curve generated with serial dilutions of control cDNA. Expression levels were normalized to 18S rRNA. Control reactions lacking reverse transcriptase were included to confirm the absence of genomic DNA contamination.

Quantification and statistical analysis

For quantification of PLA signal, confocal images were processed using an automated macro plugin in ImageJ called Andy’s Algorithm (Law et al., 2017). Briefly, nuclei in each image field were segmented using the DAPI channel to create a mask for individual cell counting. PLA puncta were identified through automated thresholding (with Foci Gaussian Blur set at 1 and Foci Maxima Value set at 10). For every image analyzed, the total number of PLA signals was normalized to the number of nuclei to get an estimate for mean PLA puncta per cell. Identical thresholding parameters were applied to all images across different experimental groups to ensure consistency. All statistical analysis in this study was performed using either the two-sided Student’s t-test or one-way/two-way ANOVA as appropriate on GraphPad Prism 10.4.0. Data are reported as mean ± standard deviation or mean ± standard error of mean as indicated in figure legends. P values ≤ 0.05 were considered significant. Exact N numbers are indicated in the figure legends.
