Endothelial cell-specific knockout of ATG5 ameliorates inflammation and renal fibrosis by regulating pyroptosis
Authors: Yu Huang, Shu Wen, Li Chen, Yujiu Gao, Shufan Xu, Chen Fang, Jian Tang, Yue Yuan, Ying Feng, Shinan Ma, Shan Luo, Qinghong Zhang, Xiuyan Shi, Xuzhi Ruan, Xingrong Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesATG5, a key regulator of autophagy-associated inflammation, is markedly upregulated under inflammatory conditions. In this study, we observed significantly elevated ATG5 expression in the kidneys of patients with renal fibrosis and in mouse kidneys after unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO), particularly in renal tubules and glomeruli. Recent studies suggest that enhanced ATG5-mediated autophagy in tubular epithelial cells targets the NF-κB pathway to alleviate renal injury. The glomerulus is a dense capillary network primarily composed of endothelial cells, which represent a key immune cell population in the kidney. However, whether endothelial ATG5 exerts a function similar to that of tubular ATG5 during the progression of renal fibrosis remains unclear.MethodsFirst, Sirius red staining, immunohistochemistry staining and other and pathological methods were used to analyze the expression of ATG5 in relation to the progression of renal fibrosis in patients and UUO model mice. We next observed the effects of endothelial-specific knockout of ATG5 in mice with renal fibrosis induced by the UUO model by ultrasound image assessment, HE staining, and Masson staining. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were isolated, and their regulation of inflammation and promotion of renal fibrosis were analyzed by overexpression and knockdown of ATG5 using in vitro synthetic mRNA and siRNA interference techniques. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), qPCR and Western blotting were used to determine the effect of endothelial ATG5 on promoting inflammation-dependent pyroptosis in vitro and in vivo. Finally, the expression of GSDMD, a key pyroptosis gene, was examined in the kidneys of clinical renal fiber patients by immunohistochemistry staining.ResultsWe successfully generated endothelial-specific ATG5 knockout (Tek-Cre ATG5 KO) mice and found that endothelial cell deletion of ATG5 attenuated UUO-induced renal fibrosis. Mechanistically, endothelial-specific ATG5 knockout suppressed pyroptosis and the release of inflammatory factors in the UUO model, primarily by inhibiting GSDMD expression and its cleavage into the N-GSDMD fragment during canonical NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Electron microscopy results showed that ATG5 mainly caused pyroptosis independent of plasma membrane pores in endothelial cells. Conditioned medium from human umbilical vein endothelial cells with ATG5 knockdown inhibited the expression of the key fibrotic marker TGF-β1 in renal tubular epithelial HK-2 cells. Consistent results were obtained when HK-2 cells were cocultured with serum from UUO mice with endothelial-specific ATG5 knockout. Furthermore, GSDMD expression was positively correlated with the degree of fibrosis in patients with renal fibrosis.ConclusionsCollectively, our findings demonstrate that ATG5 modulates the inflammatory response by mediating pyroptosis and exacerbates UUO-induced renal fibrosis in endothelial cells, which is in striking contrast to its role in renal tubular epithelial cells. These results highlight the cell-type-specific functions of ATG5 during renal fibrogenesis. Furthermore, our study provides a novel theoretical basis for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies against renal fibrosis in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00011-026-02272-z.

Introduction

The prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) is increasing worldwide, and obstructive renal injury is the most important common cause of kidney fibrosis [1, 2]. Renal fibrosis arises from a multitude of pathological processes, including maladaptive repair and excessive inflammation triggered by chronic renal insults. Accumulating evidence indicates that, in response to persistent and prolonged injury, not only immune cells but also resident renal parenchymal cells (e.g., renal tubular cells and endothelial cells) actively shape the inflammatory response through the secretion of diverse proinflammatory cytokines [3]. These cytokines subsequently drive leukocyte recruitment into the kidney. While this cascade constitutes a physiological host response, unresolved and excessive inflammation drives progressive renal fibrosis and the eventual development of chronic renal failure [4, 5]. Glomerular endothelial cells undergo injury and cell death in the setting of both acute and chronic insults [6]. Notably, multiple studies have demonstrated that sustained endothelial injury leads to pyroptosis, which is accompanied by elevated secretion of cytokines and profibrotic factors [7, 8], thereby implicating glomerular endothelial cells as key mediators of inflammation and fibrosis in renal disease. Accordingly, dissecting the regulatory role of glomerular endothelial cells in shaping the inflammatory microenvironment may facilitate the development of innovative therapeutic approaches for the treatment of renal fibrosis. Pyroptosis is a type of programmed cell death associated with inflammation that is driven by caspase-1, caspase-4, and caspase-5 in humans (caspase-1 and caspase-11 in mice) following infection or cellular damage [9]. Pyroptosis involves cell swelling and rupture and is driven by GSDMD-mediated pore formation [10, 11]. As an innate immune response, pyroptosis was previously thought to be limited to immune cells. Recent studies have shown that pyroptosis is associated with chronic kidney diseases [12] and occurs mainly in renal tubular epithelial cells [13]. The glomerulus consists of a highly specialized capillary network predominantly composed of endothelial cells, which serve as crucial renal-resident immune sentinels. Nevertheless, whether glomerular endothelial cells undergo pyroptosis in response to chronic inflammatory insults and thereby contribute to the progression of renal fibrosis remains largely unexplored.

ATG5 has been reported to mediate activation of the Nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain, leucine-rich repeat and pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammatory body in Parkinson’s disease patients, thereby exacerbating disease progression [14]. Some studies have shown that ATG5 regulates NLRP3, Interluekin-1beta (IL-1β), and TGF-β1 in renal tubular epithelial cells to slow the progression of renal fibrosis in a UUO mouse model [15]. More notably, ATG5 exhibits divergent activation or inhibition patterns in distinct hepatic cell types during the same pathological process, such as liver fibrogenesis [16]. ATG5 expression is upregulated in hepatic parenchymal cells but downregulated in hepatic stellate cells, indicating that ATG5 may exert cell-type-specific functions [17]. Although previous studies have associated global autophagy inhibition with reduced pyroptosis during renal fibrosis [18, 19], the cell-type-specific mechanisms, particularly the role of endothelial autophagy, remain completely uncharacterized. Endothelial cells serve as key immunoregulatory components in the kidney. However, whether endothelial ATG5 modulates renal fibrosis under inflammatory conditions, akin to tubular ATG5, has yet to be determined.

In the present study, we demonstrate that endothelial cell-specific knockout of ATG5 alleviates renal fibrosis progression by regulating the NLRP3/GSDMD signaling pathway mediated by pyroptosis. Our findings indicate that ATG5 exerts distinct functional roles in glomerular endothelial cells compared with renal tubular epithelial cells during the pathogenesis of renal fibrosis, suggesting that therapeutic intervention for renal fibrosis warrants a more precise strategy to modulate the same molecular target in a cell-type-specific manner.

Materials and methods

Patient samples

Twenty-two primary CKD patients with renal fibrosis (glomerular filtration rate [eGFR]: 10.92–58.52 mL/min/1.73 m2) verified by renal biopsy were enrolled in the Department of Nephrology of Taihe Hospital, Hubei University of Medicine (Shiyan, China). Three patients with Estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (eGFR) > 110 mL/min/1.73 m2 and almost no lesions verified by renal biopsy were enrolled as controls. The following exclusion criteria were applied: polycystic kidney disease, pregnancy, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), renal cancer, and recent immunosuppressive therapy. Kidney biopsies were obtained from these patients for subsequent Masson trichrome staining or Sirius red staining. Ethics approval was granted by the Committees for Ethical Review of China Taihe Hospital (Shiyan, China, 2023KS32).

Mice

All animal experiments in this study were performed at Hubei University of Medicine and were recognized and approved by the experimental Animal Ethics Committee of the Animal Center of Hubei Medical University, China (Ethical Approval No. 2019-079). All experimental animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Littermates of wild-type (WT) and floxed ATG5 (ATG5fl/fl), B6. Cg-Tg(Tek-cre)12F1v/J (Tek-Cre+) and Tek-Cre ATG5-knockout (KO) mice were generated by crossbreeding Tek-Cre+∷ATG5fl/+ and ATG5fl/+ mice (purchased from Cyagen Biosciences Co., Ltd), respectively. Genotyping was performed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using transgene-specific primers, and the primers are shown in Additional file 1: Table S1. Male mice between 8 and 10 weeks of age were used in the present study.

For the UUO model, the mice underwent ligation of the left ureter with silk sutures, and sham-operated mice were used as controls. Control mice received the same dose of dimethyl sulfoxide. Bilateral renal tissue (obstructed and contralateral kidneys) and blood were extracted from mice (n ≥ 6 in each group, half female and half male) on the 3rd, 7th and 14th day after UUO modeling for further studies. Anesthesia for all experiments was administered using inhaled isoflurane, and euthanasia was performed by cervical dislocation following anesthesia. All control and KO mice were strict littermates and cohoused.

Ultrasound image assessment

The mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane (1.5 gas flow) (RWD Life Science Co., Ltd., R510-22-8) and placed on an electric heating pad to prevent hypothermia; abdominal hair was removed as previously described. The animals were positioned supine, and heart rate and body temperature were monitored while the heart rate was maintained at ≥ 400. Images of renal sagittal sections were obtained using the VisualSonics Vevo2100 Imaging System with a 400D scan head (FUJIFILM VisualSonics Inc., Toronto, ON). The structures of the left and right kidneys were examined by B-MODE imaging. Each ultrasound was completed within 10–15 min; the animals were monitored until they fully recovered from the anesthesia. Ultrasound images were analyzed using VevoLab 4.1 software provided by VisualSonics (Toronto, ON).

Histology, immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin-embedded mouse kidney sections were prepared by a conventional procedure. The tissue was placed in 10% formalin fixing solution for approximately 1–3 days. Once the tissue’s protein was fully denatured and solidified, it was embedded and fixed using paraffin wax. Subsequently, the tissue was sliced with a paraffin microtome, and the slice thickness was about 5 μm.

The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, Masson trichrome and Sirius red to determine interstitial volume expansion (blue staining or red staining). Quantification of the fibrotic area was carried out by using Image Pro-Plus v. 6.0 software (Bethesda, MD, USA). Masson’s trichrome staining was utilized to visualize the overall collagen deposition and spatial distribution within renal tissues, while Sirius red staining under polarized light specifically enhances the detection of mature collagen fibrils (particularly types I and III) through its birefringent properties. This dual approach provides complementary information: Masson staining offers superior histological context for fibrotic architecture, whereas Sirius red enables quantitative assessment of collagen cross-linking density—a critical indicator of fibrosis progression [20].

The kidney tissue was embedded in paraffin and cut into 5-µm sections for H&E, immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence. For IHC and IF, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies against ATG5 (1∶200, 10181-2-AP, Proteintech), Cluster of Differentiation 31 (CD31, 1∶1200, 11265-1-AP, Proteintech), Collagen I (1∶100, 14695-1-AP, Proteintech), NLRP3 (1∶30, A12694, ABclonal), apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC, 1∶300, DF6304, Affinity), GSDMD (1∶50, AF4012, Affinity), and IL-1β (1∶50, 12242 S, CST) at 4 °C overnight and then with HRP-conjugated, CY3-conjugated or FITC-conjugated (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) secondary antibodies for approximately 1 h. The sections of IHC were examined under a fluorescence microscope (Leica, DMI6000B). The sections of IF were examined under a Confocal Microscope (Zeiss, LSM510 META).

Cell culture and treatment

Human proximal tubular cells (HK-2 cells) were purchased from the American Tissue Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (GIBCO BRL, Rockville, MD, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 U/ml penicillin, 100 mg/ml streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. After being seeded into six-well plates for 24 h, HK-2 cells were placed in normal medium in the absence or presence of Dulbecco’s Hanks Medium (Dulbecco’s Hanks MediumDulbecco’s Hanks MediumDulbecco’s Hanks MediumDHM, 50, 100, or 200 mm) for 24–48 h.

Serum from different groups of mice was collected by means of eyeball blood collection, whole blood was left at room temperature for 1 h, centrifuged at 3000 revolutions per minute for 20 min to separate the serum. The upper serum was carefully aspirated, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C. It was used to treat renal tubular epithelial HK-2 cells for indirect co-culture.

Isolation, culture and identification of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs)

HUVECs were isolated from the umbilical cord that was separated from the placenta. The blood in the umbilical vein was rinsed with 1× HEPES solution. Then, 0.1% collagenase type II (Sigma, USA) was infused into the umbilical vein, which was digested for 15 min at 37 °C. The solution containing endothelial cells was rinsed with an appropriate amount of 1×HEPES solution. This entire procedure was performed with sterile techniques and was completed within 3 h. The cells obtained from the solution were cultured in M199 (ScienCell, USA) supplemented with 5% FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin, 100 mg/ml streptomycin, and 1% endothelial cell growth supplement (ScienCell, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 and 95% O2. The culture medium was refreshed every 2–3 days. HUVECs at passages three to six were used for this study.

After silencing or overexpressing ATG5 in HUVEC cells, HK-2 cells were cultured with the supernatant: HUVECs were transfected with ATG5-siRNA or ATG5-FLAG overexpression plasmid (FLAG-Vector as blank control) for 24 h, followed by pretreated with PMA (500 ng/ml) for 12 h and LPS (2 ug/mL) for 6 h, after which the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. In the co-culture experiment, HUVECs were divided into four groups: NC-siRNA, ATG5-siRNA, NC-siRNA + LPS+PMA, and ATG5-siRNA + LPS+PMA, or Vector, ATG5-OE, Vector + LPS+PMA, and ATG5-OE + LPS+PMA. After 24 h of treatment, the supernatants of HUVECs were collected for subsequent experiments. Four Wells of HK-2 cells were spread in 12-well plates in advance, and the supernatants collected above were added to HK-2 cells, respectively, and cultured for 24 h and 36 h for subsequent experiments.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT–PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells and tissues by TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized using the Superscript II kit for qRT–PCR (Tiangen Biotech). qRT–PCR was conducted on a 10 µl reaction mixture containing 50 nM forward and reverse primers, SYBR Green reaction mix (QIAGEN Bioinformatics) and 3.2 nM template. The system used for qRT-PCR is the CFX Connect Fluorescent Quantitative PCR Detection System (BIO-RAD). The primer sequences for quantitative analysis are listed in Table S2. Fold changes were calculated using the delta–delta Ct method. Three biological replicates were performed for each experiment.

Renal function

The levels of serum creatinine (Scr) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) were measured by ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). Additionally, mouse urine protein levels were measured to assess renal function using Urine Protein Quantitative Test Kits. The data are expressed as millimoles of albumin per liter urine.

Western blot analysis

Cells from each group were collected and lysed in lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). The proteins were extracted and separated by SDS–PAGE and then transferred onto PVDF membranes. After being blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature (RT), the membranes were incubated with antibodies against ATG5 (1:1000, 10181-2-AP, Proteintech), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphatedehy drogenase (GAPDH, 1:2000, AF5009, Beyotime), β-tubulin (1:10000, Abs830032, Absin), NLRP3 (1:1000, A12694, ABclonal), IL-1β (1:1000, 12242 S, CST), Sequestosome 1/SQSTM1 (p62, 1:1000, 5114s, CST), Light Chain 3 I/II (LC3I/II, 1:1000, 14600-1-AP, Proteintech), and GSDMD (1:500, AF4012, Affinity) at 4 °C overnight. After being washed 3 times with TBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1000, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) at RT for 1 h. Then, the membranes were washed 3 times with TBST and imaged with a gel imaging system (Bio-Rad, California, USA).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

HUVECs or mouse kidney tissue were fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde and 3% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS) overnight at 4 °C, postfixed with 1% OsO4 for 1.5 h and rinsed twice with water for 5–10 min at RT. The samples were then dehydrated through an ascending graded series of ethanol at RT (25% for 10 min, 50%, 75%, 95%, 100% at 20 min each) and then twice with 100% acetone for 20 min. The samples were then infiltrated with increasing concentrations of hydrophilic resin and araldite 502 as follows: 30 min in 100% acetone: resin (1:1) at RT, 24 h 100% acetone: resin (1:6) at RT, 20 min in fresh resin at RT, 30 min in fresh resin at 40 ℃, and 1 h in fresh resin at 45 ℃. Then, the resin-infiltrated tissue was transferred into molds containing fresh resin and polymerized at 60 ℃ for at least 24 h. Semithin (1 μm thickness) sections were obtained before ultrathin sections (gold interference color) were collected onto copper grids. Each grid was stained with uranyl acetate and examined using a Joel 1010 transmission electron microscope and Gatan micrograph software.

In vitro synthesis of ATG5 mRNA and HUVECs transfection

ATG5 mRNA was synthesized using previously described methods [21]. RNA transfection was performed with TransIT-mRNA (Mirus). The RNA was diluted in Opti-MEM basal medium (Gibco), and the Boost reagent and TransIT-mRNA were then added sequentially. After 2 min of incubation at RT, the RNA-lipid complexes were administered to the medium in culture plates and placed in the incubator. ATG5 expression was analyzed 12 h later by immunofluorescence, and RFP-mRNA was used as a control.

RNA interference

ATG5 and GSDMD siRNA oligonucleotides were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The siRNA target sequences used in this study are shown in Table S3. All siRNAs were synthesized by RiboBio (RiboBio, China) and were transfected at a final concentration of 10 nM.

Statistical analysis

Numerical results are presented as the mean ± SD or dot plots. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 19.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0). The normality of data distribution for each experimental group was first assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Based on the outcome:

For comparisons between two groups of normally distributed data, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied.

For comparisons among three or more groups of normally distributed data, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. If the ANOVA indicated a significant main effect (P < 0.05), post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test to correct for multiple comparisons. For data sets that did not meet the assumption of normality, non-parametric tests were employed: the Mann-Whitney U test for two-group comparisons, or the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple-group comparisons. A two-tailed P value less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

ATG5 expression is significantly increased in patients with renal fibrosis and in UUO mice

To investigate the role of ATG5 in the pathogenesis of CKD, we first performed IHC and Sirius red staining on kidney biopsy specimens from patients with renal fibrosis (Fig. 1A). ATG5 expression was markedly elevated in fibrotic biopsies and correlated positively with the extent of fibrosis, as assessed by Sirius red staining. Concurrently, patient eGFR data were collected, and correlation analysis revealed a negative relationship between ATG5 expression and eGFR levels (Fig. 1A). We next examined ATG5 expression dynamics during renal fibrosis progression in the UUO mouse model (Fig. 1A and B). IHC showed that ATG5 levels at days 3, 7, and 14 post-UUO were higher than those in the sham group, and its expression positively correlated with the structural changes and degree of renal fibrosis, as indicated by H&E and Masson staining—particularly in renal tubules and glomeruli (Fig. 1B-D; Suppl. Figure 1). These findings in UUO mice were consistent with the pathological observations in human renal fibrosis, further supporting a positive correlation between ATG5 expression and fibrotic degree.

Additionally, immunofluorescence (IF) revealed that ATG5 was upregulated in the kidneys of UUO mice and was predominantly expressed in endothelial cells (Suppl. Figure. 1 F). Recent studies have reported that tubular upregulation of ATG5-mediated autophagy targets NF-κB signaling to protect against renal injury [22]. The glomerulus is a rich capillary network composed mainly of endothelial cells. However, whether ATG5 expressed by endothelial cells—an important immune cell population in the kidney—plays a role in renal fibrosis similar to that of tubular ATG5 remains unclear.

Tek-Cre ATG5 KO mice were established

To clarify the role of ATG5 in glomeruli, we first established mice with Tek-Cre ATG5 KO in the endothelial cells of kidneys. The breeding protocol is shown in Fig. 2A. Male mice (ATG5f/f) were crossed with female Tek-Cre transgenic mice (Tek-Cre ATG5+/+). Following first-generation production, heterozygous female offspring (Tek-CreATG5f/+) were crossed to produce littermate mice of Tek-Cre ATG5+/+, Tek-Cre ATG5f/f (Tek-Cre ATG5 KO) and ATG5f/f. The genotype of Tek-Cre ATG5 KO mice was characterized by (1) amplification of the 700-bp DNA fragment floxed allele; (2) deficient amplification of the 350-bp DNA fragment WT allele; and (3) amplification of the 481-bp DNA fragment of the Cre gene (Fig. 2B). Immunohistochemistry revealed that ATG5 protein levels in the kidneys of ATG5f/f mice were lower than those in Tek-Cre ATG5+/+ mice (Fig. 2C), and we used Tek-Cre ATG5+/+ (Ctl) mice as control mice.

To verify endothelial cell-specific ATG5 knockout, we examined ATG5 and CD31 expression in the thoracic aorta and kidney of Ctl and Tek-Cre ATG5 KO mice by IHC and assessed their co-localization by IF. In the kidneys of Tek-Cre ATG5 KO mice, ATG5 and CD31 were co-localized, whereas no ATG5 expression was detected on CD31-labeled endothelial cells in the thoracic aorta (Fig. 2C-E). In UUO Ctl mice, IHC revealed increased ATG5 expression at days 7 and 14 post-surgery compared with the sham group, particularly in glomeruli. In contrast, ATG5 expression was undetectable in the glomeruli of UUO Tek-Cre ATG5 KO mice (Fig. 2F). In addition to large vessels, glomerular capillaries are the most abundant vascular structures in the kidney. We therefore examined whether endothelial-specific ATG5 knockout affects glomerular number or size. H&E staining indicated that endothelial cell-specific ATG5 deficiency did not alter glomerular number or size (Fig. 2G). Collectively, these results confirm successful generation of endothelial cell-specific ATG5 knockout (Tek-Cre ATG5 KO) mice.

Endothelial cell-specific ATG5 deletion attenuates UUO-induced renal fibrosis

WT, Tek-Cre- ATG5f/f, Tek-Cre ATG5+/+(Ctl) and Tek-Cre ATG5 KO littermate mice were subjected to UUO for 3, 7 and 14 days. Compared with those in the Ctl group, the Tek-Cre ATG5 KO mice exhibited significantly lower levels of urinary protein, serum creatinine, and blood urea nitrogen, with more pronounced differences at 7 and 14 days. However, there was no significant difference between the WT and Tek-Cre ATG5+/+(Ctl) groups or the Tek-Cre- ATG5f/f and Tek Cre ATG5 KO groups, which further indicated that Tek-Cre ATG5+/+(Ctl) mice were appropriate as a control. These findings suggest that endothelial-specific deletion of ATG5 delays UUO-induced renal dysfunction in mice (Suppl. Figure 2 A-C). Therefore, in subsequent experiments, we only examined UUO mice from each group at 7 and 14 days’ post-surgery. Ultrasound imaging and direct renal inspection revealed that Tek-Cre ATG5 KO mice exhibited markedly attenuated UUO-induced hydronephrosis at days 7 and 14 (Fig. 3A-C). Masson staining and Sirius red staining were used to assess the degree of renal interstitial fibrosis induced by the UUO. The results showed that renal fibrosis was more severe in the Ctl group than in the Tek-Cre ATG5 KO group (Fig. 3D-H). Kidney sections were stained for Collagen-I, which indicated that the degree of fibrosis was attenuated in Tek-Cre ATG5 KO mice (Fig. 3I, J). Notably, the difference in collagen-I expression was observed not in the glomeruli but in the renal tubules, suggesting that ATG5 may regulate inflammatory factors secreted by glomerular endothelial cells, thereby promoting renal tubular fibrosis. Collectively, these results indicate that ATG5 expression in renal endothelial cells accelerates UUO-induced kidney fibrosis.

ATG5 deficiency inhibits cytokine production in endothelial cells

To investigate the potential mechanisms underlying the role of endothelial ATG5 in renal fibrosis, we performed RNA-seq analysis of Tek-Cre ATG5⁺/⁺ (Ctl) and Tek-Cre ATG5 KO (KO) mice of kidney tissue at 7 days post-UUO surgery. Heat-map analysis highlighted obvious differences (> 1.5 fold) in the gene expression profiles of Ctl mice vs. KO mice, and ingenuity pathway analysis annotated the molecular and cellular functions of the differentially expressed genes (using a P < 0.05 cutoff) (Suppl. Figure 3 A, B). The DEGs were predominantly involved in immune responses, TGF-β/Smad, and Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction-related pathways (Suppl. Figure 3 C, D).

The UUO model mainly induces an acute inflammatory response that leads to renal fibrosis, and endothelial cells have immunomodulatory functions [23]. Studies have shown that ATG5 can regulate inflammation and affect the development of a variety of diseases [24, 25]. Therefore, we next validated the significantly differentially expressed genes in NF-kappa B signaling pathway (e.g., Interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-1β and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)) identified by RNA-seq using qPCR (Fig. 4A, and Suppl. Figure 4A). IL-1β and IL-6 are mostly activated by the NLRP3 and ASC inflammasomes [26]. STRING analysis indicates ATG5 regulates inflammatory responses via NF-κB targets such as NLRP3 and GSDMD (Suppl. Figure 4B). Therefore, we next examined the regulation of inflammasome-related gene NLRP3, ASC and GSDMD expression in Ctl and Tek-Cre ATG5 KO mouse kidneys by qPCR and IHC. The expression levels of NLRP3, ASC and GSDMD in Tek-Cre ATG5 KO mice were significantly lower than those in the control group after 7 d and 14 d of UUO (Suppl. Figure 4 C-D, Figs. 4B and C and 5A). These results show that ATG5 deficiency inhibits cytokine production in endothelial cells.

ATG5 induces inflammation by promoting pyroptosis in HUVECs and mouse kidneys in a UUO model

NLRP3 inflammasome activation typically induces pyroptosis, and our results showed that the key pyroptosis gene GSDMD was significantly lower in Tek-Cre ATG5 KO mice than in the control group after 7 d and 14 d of UUO (Fig. 5A, B, and Suppl. Figure 4B, C). We have detected the localization of IL-1β, NLRP3, GSDMD, ASC and CD31 in renal tissue of mice at 7 days and 14 days after UUO by IF, and found that IL-1β, NLRP3 and CD31 were co-localized in glomeruli and renal tubules. In addition, we found that ASC and GSDMD were distributed in the renal tubular region, which may be due to the dynamic activation of inflammasomes. The distribution of inflammation may change with time and stage of inflammation, and eventually accumulate in the renal tubular area (Suppl. Figure 5 and Suppl. Figure 6).

To analyze whether endothelial ATG5 promotes renal fibrosis via regulating its pyroptosis, we isolated and identified HUVECs (Suppl. Figure 7). RNA interference was used to reduce ATG5 expression in HUVECs. First, we screened the activity of ATG5 siRNA1-3 in HUVECs and found that siRNA1 and siRNA2 had better inhibitory effects (Fig. 6A). Then, we used combinations of siRNA1 and siRNA2 to interfere with ATG5 expression and found significant inhibition in HUVECs (Fig. 6B-C). The Western blotting experiment revealed that LPS could significantly increase the expression of ATG5 in HUVECs (Fig. 6D). Silencing ATG5 in HUVECs inhibited Lipopolysaccharides (LPS) + Positive Mental Attitude (PMA)-induced expression of NLRP3, GSDMD and the downstream inflammatory factors IL-1β and IL-6 (Fig. 6E-F and Suppl. Figure 8 A), as well as the posttranslational cleavage of GSDMD protein and the expression of autophagy-associated proteins (p62 and LC3I/II) induced by LPS + PMA (Fig. 6F). We also found that the combination of SiATG5 and LPS + PMA led to a significant decrease in pyroptotic cells (Suppl. Figure 8B), and then we evaluated HUVECs by TEM to identify any intracellular structures that were characteristic of pyroptosis. SiRNA-treated HUVECs showed the expected normal characteristics of endothelial cells, such as cytoplasm containing well-defined organelles and an intact nuclear envelope (Fig. 6G). We found an interesting phenomenon in which knockdown of ATG5 in endothelial cells induced the plasma membrane to be characterized by numerous pores and large disruptions, but classic pyroptosis did not induce numerous pores in the plasma membrane and the membrane pores of organelles, and these pores were most prominent in cells treated with the combination of siRNA ATG5 and LPS + PMA (Fig. 6G). Finally, we detected pyroptosis in the kidneys of UUO-treated mice by TEM and observed a similar phenomenon as in HUVECs (Fig. 6H). The membranes and organelles were intact in the kidneys of mice in the sham-operated group, whereas the perforated organelle membranes were more obvious in control-operated mice compared with the Tek-Cre ATG5 KO mice, and many organelles were not structurally intact, although the cell membranes in both groups were largely intact (Fig. 6H). To determine whether ATG5 in endothelial cells induces inflammation by promoting pyroptosis, ATG5 was overexpressed or GSDMD was knocked down in HUVECs by the synthesis of mRNA or siRNA in vitro (Suppl. Figure 9 A-C). Interfering with GSDMD expression inhibited the expression of the inflammatory factor IL-1β in endothelial cells with ATG5 overexpression (Suppl. Figure 9D), which suggests that ATG5-induced inflammation is dependent on GSDMD. Collectively, these findings reveal that ATG5 in endothelial cells promotes pyroptosis-induced inflammation independent of plasma membrane pores in HUVECs and mouse kidneys in a UUO model.

The above results indicate that specific knockout of endothelial ATG5 reduces UUO-induced endothelial cell pyroptosis, thereby delaying the progression of renal fibrosis. Interestingly, fibrosis and collagen deposition occur mainly in the renal tubules, suggesting that ATG5 regulates the crosstalk between endothelial cells and tubular cells. This raises the key question: how does endothelial ATG5 promote pyroptosis to increase renal tubular fibrosis? Studies have shown that Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in renal tubular epithelial cells is the main cause of renal tubular fibrosis. Studies have found that TGFβ1 is a key regulator of EMT [27]. Meanwhile, the RNA-seq results show that endothelial ATG5 regulates the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway (Suppl. Figures 3 C). Therefore, we used endothelial cell culture supernatant and serum from the UUO model to perform indirect coculture experiments with renal tubular epithelial HK-2 cells and found that the supernatant of vascular endothelial cells with ATG5 knockdown downregulated TGF-β1 expression in HK-2 cells induced by LPS and PMA compared with control supernatant. In the absence of LPS + PMA induction, culture supernatant containing ATG5, which interferes with endothelial cells, did not affect TGF-β1 expression in HK-2 cells (Fig. 7A, and Suppl. Figure 10 A). Compared with that of control mice, the serum of Tek-Cre ATG5 KO mice decreased TGF-β1 expression in the UUO model, and there were no differences between the sham groups (Fig. 7B, and Suppl. Figure 10B).

To analyze whether ATG5-dependent pyroptosis promoted EMT in renal tubular epithelial cells, HK-2 cells were treated with supernatant from endothelial cells with ATG5 overexpression and GSDMD interference. The results showed that ATG5-dependent expression of the key pyroptosis gene GSDMD in endothelial cells promoted TGF-β1 expression in HK-2 cells (Suppl. Figure 11 A). Next, we investigated whether endothelial ATG5 promotes pyroptosis and induces EMT in HK-2 cells in an NLRP3-dependent manner, we treated endothelial cells with the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 in combination with LPS and PMA. The results demonstrated that ATG5 knockdown suppressed LPS + PMA-induced endothelial pyroptosis. MCC950 treatment also attenuated LPS + PMA-induced pyroptosis; however, the combination of MCC950 and ATG5 knockdown did not yield an obvious additive inhibitory effect (Fig. 7C). Subsequently, we collected the supernatants from each treatment group and incubated them with HK-2 cells. IF analysis revealed that TGF-β1 and Collagen I expression in HK-2 cells treated with supernatants from the ATG5 knockdown plus LPS + PMA group was significantly lower than that in the control group. Notably, the combination of MCC950, ATG5 knockdown, and LPS + PMA did not further suppress TGF-β1 and Collagen I expression in HK-2 cells (Fig. 7D). Conversely, ATG5 overexpression promoted LPS + PMA induced endothelial pyroptosis, while MCC950 attenuated the degree of pyroptosis. After HK-2 cells were treated with the supernatants of the corresponding HUVECs, IF staining showed that the expressions of TGF-β1 and Collagen I in HK-2 cells treated with the supernatants of the ATG5 overexpression plus LPS + PMA group were significantly higher than those in the control group. However, the combination of MCC950, ATG5 overexpression and LPS + PMA further inhibited the expression of TGF-β1 and collagen I in HK-2 cells (Suppl. Figure 11B-C). Collectively, these findings indicate that endothelial ATG5 promotes EMT transition in HK-2 cells through the regulation of NLRP3. Last, we found that the expression of GSDMD in the kidneys of patients with renal fibrosis was positively correlated with the degree of fibrosis (Fig. 7E). These results suggest that ATG5 in endothelial cells promotes renal tubular epithelial cell fibrosis by upregulating TGF-β1 through pyroptosis (Fig. 7F). These results suggest that ATG5 induces inflammation by promoting pyroptosis in HUVECs and mouse kidneys in a UUO model.

Discussion

Kidney fibrosis is the hallmark of chronic kidney disease progression. However, no effective treatment currently exists to inhibit renal fibrosis, largely due to an incomplete understanding of the mechanisms underlying kidney dysfunction. Immune cell infiltration and elevated cytokine expression have been observed in the kidneys of patients with CKD [28, 29]. Blocking cytokine activation by targeting autophagy-mediated inflammation has shown some benefit in patients with kidney disease [30, 31]. Nonetheless, the upstream regulatory network governing cytokine activation remains to be elucidated. While previous studies have suggested a correlation, our endothelial-specific KO model provides direct causal evidence for this axis, and uncovers a previously unrecognized intercellular signaling mechanism involving tubular cells. Here, we first analyzed the relationship between the expression of the autophagy-related gene ATG5 and the degree of renal fibrosis. The results showed that ATG5 expression was low in normal human kidney tissues but elevated in patients with severe fibrosis. In the UUO-induced renal fibrosis mouse model, with the aggravation of renal fibrosis, the expression of ATG5 increased, and the increase in expression was mainly distributed in renal tubules and glomeruli. ATG5 in renal tubular epithelial cells has been shown to inhibit inflammation and regulate the progression of renal fibrosis [32]. However, the role of autophagy may vary depending on the cell type and disease stage. For example, autophagy activation in proximal tubular epithelial cell (PTECs) during acute kidney injury (AKI) is renoprotective [33], whereas autophagy induction in leukocytes promotes cell survival and exacerbates inflammation [34]. These findings suggest that autophagy exerts distinct functions in immune cells versus other cell types. The glomerulus is a rich capillary network in the kidney that is mainly composed of endothelial cells with immunoregulatory functions. Therefore, we hypothesize that ATG5, a key autophagy-related gene, plays distinct roles in regulating renal fibrosis in glomerular endothelial cells versus glomerular epithelial cells.

Unlike previous studies, we used endothelial cell-specific knockout mice—rather than global genetic knockout or proximal tubular epithelial cell-specific knockout approaches to directly determine the role of glomerular endothelial cell autophagy in fibrogenesis. Interestingly, our results revealed that ATG5 in glomerular endothelial cells and renal tubular epithelial cells plays an opposite role in the development of renal fibrosis. Endothelial cell-specific knockout of ATG5 can delay the progression of UUO-induced renal fibrosis in mice.

Renal fibrosis caused by ureteral obstruction is closely related to inflammation [29, 30]. Given that endothelial cells possess immunoregulatory functions [35], we hypothesized ATG5 may promote renal fibrosis by regulating inflammation. To test this, we examined the effect of ATG5 on the expression of inflammatory factors in endothelial cells. Compared with control mice, endothelial cell-specific knockout of ATG5 in UUO model mice resulted in reduced expression of inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and IL-1β in kidney tissue. Notably, most of these inflammatory responses are mediated by the NLRP3 (NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3) inflammasome. Recent studies have shown that NLRP3 regulates multiple inflammation-related diseases by activating pyroptosis [36]. Pyroptosis is a distinct form of lytic programmed cell death characterized by cell swelling, membrane rupture, release of intracellular contents, and potent proinflammatory effects. Inflammasomes are macromolecules complexes composed of multiple proteins, and their formation initiates the cleavage of procaspase-1, resulting in the activation of gasdermin D and the maturation and release of interleukin-1β and IL-18, leading to pyroptosis [37]. Pyroptosis can be classified into canonical and noncanonical pathways, with the NLRP3 inflammasome serving as the most critical initiator [38]. Recent studies have demonstrated that pyroptosis contributes to the development of UUO-induced renal fibrosis [39, 40], and that NLRP3 knockout inhibits pyroptosis and delays UUO-induced fibrosis [41]. Furthermore, ATG5 has been reported to regulate pyroptosis in both the neovascularization of oxygen-induced retinopathy in mice and in Leydig cells [42, 43]. These findings suggest that ATG5 in endothelial cells promotes renal fibrogenesis by regulating pyroptosis and thereby inducing inflammation.

In the UUO mouse model, we found that endothelial cell-specific knockout of ATG5 attenuated pyroptosis and the release of inflammatory factors such as IL-1β, primarily by inhibiting GSDMD expression and its cleavage into N-GSDMD fragments during canonical NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Electron microscopy revealed that ATG5 primarily induced pyroptosis independently of plasma membrane pores, instead affecting organelles in human and murine endothelial cells. A recent study reported that although N-GSDMD is required for IL-1β secretion in NLRP3-activated human and murine neutrophils, N-GSDMD localizes to the membranes of numerous intracellular organelles rather than the plasma membrane [37]. Moreover, genetic analysis of ATG7-deficient cells indicated that neutrophils secrete IL-1β via an autophagy-dependent mechanism. These findings reveal fundamental differences in GSDMD trafficking between neutrophils and macrophages that underlie neutrophil-specific functions during inflammasome activation. Although our study showed that ATG5-deficient cells secreted IL-1β and induced pyroptosis within intracellular organelles, whether this process depends on ATG5-induced autophagy and why it causes intracellular organelle membrane perforation require further investigation.

Deletion of ATG5 from glomerular endothelial cells alleviated UUO-induced renal fibrosis, with the differences primarily involving tubular fibrosis. How does endothelial ATG5 promote tubular fibrosis? Studies have shown that EMT in renal tubular epithelial cells is a major contributor to the development of renal tubular fibrosis [44, 45]. Therefore, we performed indirect coculture experiments using culture supernatants from HUVECs and serum from UUO model mice with HK-2 renal tubular epithelial cells. We found that the supernatant of cells with ATG5 knockdown or knockout could downregulate TGF-β1 expression compared with that of control cells. TGF-β1 is a key gene in the occurrence of EMT and one of the main factors contributing to fibrogenesis [27]. Our results further demonstrated that ATG5 upregulates the expression of the inflammatory factor IL-1β by regulating GSDMD expression and its cleavage into N-GSDMD. In endothelial cells, ATG5 promotes renal tubular epithelial cell fibrosis by upregulating TGF-β1 through pyroptosis. Analysis of clinical samples from patients with renal fibrosis revealed that GSDMD expression was positively correlated with fibrosis. GSDMD expression was mainly concentrated in the glomeruli and tubules. Whether abnormal autophagy caused by ATG5 in endothelial cells promotes renal fibrosis in a pyroptosis-dependent manner warrants further in vivo investigation.

Our results indicate that ATG5 exerts distinct functions in glomerular endothelial cells versus renal tubular epithelial cells during renal fibrosis development. Moreover, this is the first study to demonstrate a correlation between ATG5 and pyroptosis in the context of renal fibrosis. Although other related markers, such as IL-18, could provide additional insights, the consistent alterations along this defined axis strongly support our conclusion that endothelial pyroptosis is a key pathogenic mechanism.

Here, we investigated the mechanism by which the key autophagy gene ATG5 in endothelial cells induced inflammasomes activation and explored its role in inflammatory kidney diseases, aiming to identify new therapeutic targets for the prevention and treatment of these conditions and subsequent end-stage renal disease. We report that endothelial cell-specific knockout of ATG5 delays the progression of renal fibrosis by inhibiting the expression of the key pyroptosis protein GSDMD and its cleavage to generate N-GSDMD. N-GSDMD perforates endothelial cell organelles, promotes the release of the inflammatory factor IL-1β, aggravates EMT in renal tubular epithelial cells, and ultimately leads to renal fibrosis. This process represents a major contributor to immune activation and kidney disease development via the ATG5/NLRP3/GSDMD axis (Fig. 7E). ATG5 exerts distinct functions in glomerular endothelial cells versus renal tubular epithelial cells during renal fibrosis development. Moreover, this is the first study to demonstrate a correlation between ATG5 and pyroptosis in the context of renal fibrosis. Although other related markers, such as IL-18, could provide additional insights, the consistent alterations along this defined axis strongly support our conclusion that endothelial pyroptosis is a key pathogenic mechanism.

Although our findings indicate that endothelial ATG5 deletion ameliorates renal fibrosis by inhibiting pyroptosis, several limitations warrant consideration. First, while our genetic knockout model provides robust loss-of-function validation, complementary gain-of-function rescue experiments are necessary to formally establish causality. Second, our analysis was restricted to the 7- to 14-day time window, which captures the acute-to-fibrotic transition; extended time courses are required to delineate the role of ATG5 in the maintenance and resolution of chronic fibrosis. Third, despite the rigorous validation enabled by our targeted mechanistic approach, broader transcriptomic profiling is lacking. Future application of single-cell or spatial transcriptomics to endothelial-specific ATG5-deficient tissues would help define the comprehensive regulatory networks governed by ATG5. Fourth, it remains ambiguous whether ATG5 functions through canonical autophagy or autophagy-independent pathways. Resolving this question will require autophagic flux assays and rescue experiments using autophagy-specific ATG5 mutants. Finally, although our in vivo and ex vivo conditioned medium experiments strongly suggest a paracrine signaling axis from endothelial cells to the tubular epithelium, direct visualization of this crosstalk within the intact renal microenvironment has not been achieved. Advanced co-culture systems or in vivo biosensors will be essential to provide definitive visual evidence of this intercellular communication.

In summary, our results demonstrate that ATG5 exerts distinct functions across different cell types within the same tissue. This finding implies that targeting ATG5 for the treatment of renal fibrosis cannot be achieved by simply using broad inhibitors or activators; rather, cell-specific activators or inhibitors need to be developed to achieve precise, targeted therapy. Given the role of ATG5 in endothelial cells in renal fibrosis, our findings provide novel mechanistic insights into the induction of pyroptosis and the progression of renal fibrosis. Collectively, our work represents a conceptual advance in the understanding of targeted drug development for disease treatment. Modulating ATG5-mediated pyroptosis specifically in endothelial cells may represent a promising strategy to prevent the progression of renal fibrosis.

Conclusion

In summary, ATG5 exhibits cell type–dependent functions within the same tissue, arguing against the use of broad-spectrum ATG5 inhibitors or activators for treating renal fibrosis. Instead, cell-specific targeting strategies are required for precision therapy. Given the pathogenic role of endothelial ATG5 in promoting pyroptosis and renal fibrosis, our findings offer mechanistic insights and a conceptual framework for developing cell-specific therapeutics. Modulating endothelial ATG5-mediated pyroptosis thus represents a promising avenue to prevent renal fibrosis progression.
