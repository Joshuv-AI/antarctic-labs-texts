Identifying subjective life expectancy risk factors in physically active and inactive middle-aged and older adults using machine learning models
Authors: Jian Yang, Zhihui Li, Ming Wu, Yuan Zhang, Bianjiang Zhang, Huiyu Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhysical activity is a key focus in the field of public health, and subjective life expectancy is closely associated with individuals’ physical and psychological well-being. This study aimed to identify the risk factors for subjective life expectancy among middle-aged and older adults with active and inactive physical activity levels, and to provide an evidence base for developing differentiated health intervention strategies. MethodsBased on data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) 2018 survey, a total of 10,945 participants were included. Five machine learning models, including Random Forest (RF), Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), were separately constructed for the active and inactive groups. To reduce bias caused by class imbalance, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied to generate synthetic samples for the minority class. The dataset was split into a training set (70%) and a testing set (30%), and ten-fold cross-validation combined with grid search was employed to optimize hyperparameters, ensuring both robustness and generalizability of the models. Model performance was evaluated using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score.ResultsThe active group (4,707 men and 4,885 women) had a mean age of 59.76 years, while the inactive group (662 men and 691 women) had a mean age of 63.00 years. The Support Vector Machine (SVM) model achieved the best performance in the inactive group (AUC: 0.797; accuracy: 0.722; sensitivity: 0.747), whereas the Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) model achieved the best performance in the active group (AUC: 0.775; accuracy: 0.745; specificity: 0.814). Feature importance analysis indicated that “age” was the most important variable in the Support Vector Machine (SVM) model, while “perceived health” was the most important variable in the Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) model.ConclusionMachine learning methods can effectively identify key risk factors influencing subjective life expectancy among middle-aged and older adults, and provide valuable guidance for targeted health management strategies tailored to populations with different levels of physical activity.

Introduction

According to the World Population Ageing: 1950–2050, published by the United Nations, it is estimated that by 2050, the global population over the age of 65 will account for 16–22% of the total population, suggesting that population ageing is a global crisis [1]. Among them, China will face even more severe challenges, the General Research Report of Chinese Strategy for Dealing with Population Aging points out that it is expected that the number of elderly people in China in 2050 will reach 483 million people, accounting for 34.1% of the population, making China one of the fastest aging countries in the world [2].For people in the late stages of the life course, the time of their future life expectancy is significantly more important as they face the dilemmas of aging due to retirement, increased health problems, and other factors [3]. Subjective life expectancy, as a subjective measure that quantifies the perceived range of one’s remaining years, is a key element in understanding an individual’s future behavioral choices and in making decisions about organizing the rest of one’s life in terms of work, leisure, etc [4]. In addition to reflecting an individual’s level of physical and mental health, as well as being an important predictor of true mortality [5], subjective life expectancy will be an indispensable basis for decision-making in response to aging. Therefore, focusing on the subjective life expectancy of middle-aged and elderly people in China will help individuals make reasonable behaviors and decisions in the second half of their life course from a micro perspective. It will provide scientific evidence to cope with the crisis of population aging and build a healthy aging society from a macro perspective.

Previous studies have found that health behaviors are important predictors of subjective life expectancy, such as smoking and drinking are associated with poorer subjective life expectancy [6]. Physical activity, as one form of health behavior, reduces the risk of death by 20–30% in adults who achieve recommended levels of physical activity [7].Evidence has shown that physical activity levels are bi-directionally predictive of subjective life expectancy and that physical activity is a causal variable of subjective life expectancy [8]. Therefore, increasing physical activity and maintaining regular physical activity may be a positive step to improve subjective life expectancy in middle-aged and older adults. Numerous studies have revealed the positive benefits of engaging in physically active behaviors, but the negative effects of physical inactivity cannot be ignored. Physical inactivity is now a major public health problem, ranked by the World Health Organization as the fourth leading risk factor for death worldwide [9]. Studies have shown that if the trend of physical inactivity remains unchanged, nearly half a billion new cases of NCDs are expected to be diagnosed by 2030, with direct healthcare costs amounting to 520 billion international dollars [10]. Therefore, it is relevant to focus on the subjective life expectancy of middle-aged and older people with different levels of physical activity.

Machine learning algorithms can handle nonlinear relationships between complex features and demonstrate more powerful modeling capabilities compared to traditional research methods. The algorithm is able to learn autonomously from multidimensional data and effectively generalize identified patterns from the training set to the clinical test set, and has demonstrated superior generalization capabilities in previous studies [11]. It is currently widely used to construct predictive models for depression, bullying risk, and mental health [12–15]. Unfortunately, past studies have more often used traditional linear regression methods to explore the influencing factors of subjective life expectancy in the context of Western cultures [6], and it has not yet been any machine learning algorithms to develop predictive models tailored to the different physical activity categories of the Chinese population. In view of this, this study plans to construct subjective life expectancy risk factor models for the physically inactive group as well as the active group of middle-aged and older adults using five machine learning algorithms, namely, Random Forest, Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machines, XGBoost, and LightGBM, using a large sample of nationwide data. By constructing predictive models for different groups, the subjective life expectancy risk indicators of different subgroups can be captured more accurately, with the aim of providing targeted insights for specific populations.

Methods

Data sources

The data for this study were derived from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) database, initiated and managed by Peking University. This large-scale survey contains extensive demographic information, along with a wide range of indicators encompassing medical, psychological, and sociological domains. The present study utilized data collected in the 2018 wave of the survey. The investigation was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all study subjects signed an informed consent form. A total of 19,816 participants were enrolled in CHARLS-2018, and 10,945 valid samples were finally retained. Those excluded met the following criteria: (1) age less than 45 years; (2) missing or incomplete demographic information; (3) missing or incomplete physical and mental health information; and (4) missing or incomplete lifestyle information. The remaining participants were categorized into physically active and physically inactive groups based on their physical activity levels. The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) was used in the CHARLS questionnaire to measure the participants’ physical activity levels during the past week [16]. The questionnaire categorized physical activity into three levels: high-intensity (heavy lifting, fast cycling, etc.), moderate-intensity (brisk walking, regular cycling, etc.), and low-intensity (walking, etc.), and recorded the length of time spent each day engaging in physical activity of different intensities, which was subsequently measured using the Metabolic Equivalent (MET) unit of physical activity intensity. In conjunction with the IPAQ criteria [17], weekly physical activity levels below 600 METs were recorded as the physically inactive group, and those greater than or equal to 600 METs were recorded as the physically active group.

Data collection

Assessment of subjective life expectancy

The questionnaire used a “probabilistic estimation method” to assess subjects’ subjective life expectancy, which better captures uncertainty [18]. The questionnaire asked about the likelihood of surviving beyond 10 years based on the age of the subject (e.g., asking about the likelihood of surviving to age 75 for subjects younger than 65, 85 for subjects younger than 75, and so on, until older adults greater than or equal to 100 are asked about the likelihood of surviving to age 115). Subjects who answered the question as almost impossible and unlikely were considered to be in the risk group, and those who answered as likely, very likely, and simply certain were considered to be in the non-risk group.

Assessment of covariates

Based on the structure of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) questionnaire and informed by previous research [6, 19–23], the covariates in this study were categorized into three dimensions: sociodemographic characteristics, behavioral characteristics, and physical, mental, and cognitive health characteristics. The sociodemographic characteristics included gender, age, place of residence, education level, marriage, pension insurance, and medical insurance. Gender was categorized as male or female, age was based on actual age, residence was categorized as urban or rural or urban-rural, education level was categorized as elementary school and below or junior high school or high school and junior college or university and above, marriage was categorized as married, separated/divorced/unmarried, and widowed, and pension insurance and medical insurance were both with or without. Behavioral characteristics included chess playing, fitness, socializing with friends, alcohol consumption, and sleep duration. Chess and fitness were categorized as participation or non-participation, socializing with friends and drinking were categorized as yes or no, and sleep duration was the actual time spent sleeping at night. The physical, mental, and cognitive health characteristics included perceived health, physical pain, physical disability, intellectual disability, visual disability, depression, life satisfaction, satisfaction with the child, and falls. Specifically, perceived health, physical pain, life satisfaction, and satisfaction with the child are all scored on a five-point scale, with the exception of physical pain, where higher scores represent better outcomes. Physical disability, intellectual disability, visual disability, depression, and falls were all scored as yes or no, depression was assessed using the 10-item short form of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10). The total score ranges from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms.

Model construction and evaluation

The study used five machine learning models to identify subjective life expectancy risk factors in the physically active and inactive groups of middle-aged and older adults, namely Random Forest (RF), Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), XGBoost, and LightGBM models.

RF as an integrated learning method makes predictions by constructing multiple decision trees. The training data for each tree is randomly selected from the original data, and ultimately, RF combines the results of all decision trees to make predictions using majority voting or averaging. This method can effectively reduce overfitting and improve the stability and accuracy of the model, and is particularly suitable for complex, high-dimensional data.

LR is a widely used classification model that is particularly suitable for binary classification problems. By building a mathematical model, input features are mapped to a probability value that represents the likelihood of an event occurring. The core of the model is the use of a logistic function that restricts the predictions to between 0 and 1. Logistic regression not only provides classification results, but also reveals the extent to which individual features influence the results, making it highly interpretable.

SVM is a supervised learning algorithm that can be used to classify both linear and nonlinear data. It classifies data by finding an optimal decision boundary and thus realizing the classification. The boundary not only requires the ability to classify different categories of data, but also maximizes the spacing between the different categories as much as possible. SVM performs well with high dimensional data and complex data, especially when the number of samples is small, and can effectively avoid the overfitting problem.

XGBoost is an efficient gradient boosting method that improves prediction accuracy by iteratively optimizing existing models. In each iteration, the new model is adjusted according to the errors of the previous model, thus continuously improving the prediction results. XGBoost not only excels in accuracy, but also optimizes the computational speed, which is especially suitable for handling large-scale datasets. Due to its efficiency and accuracy, XGBoost is often widely used in various machine learning competitions.

LightGBM is another gradient boosting method that has higher computational efficiency when processing large-scale data compared to XGBoost. LightGBM speeds up training and is able to handle larger datasets by adopting a more efficient decision tree growth strategy and a finer feature splitting method. Despite its faster computational speed, it still copes well with complex data while maintaining high accuracy, and especially excels in high-dimensional data scenarios [24].

Considering the substantial difference in sample sizes between the two groups, the study employed the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) to address potential bias. This method generates synthetic samples for the minority class through linear interpolation between existing minority instances, thereby mitigating the issue of class imbalance and enhancing the model’s recall rate for the minority class [25]. The dataset was divided into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%) in order to prevent model overfitting problems as well as to assess the model generalization ability. All algorithms were validated using 10-fold cross-validation to ensure stability, along with a grid search to determine the optimal hyperparameter configuration for each model. The optimal parameters for each model in the inactive group were: random forest {max_depth: 5, min_samples_split: 10, n_estimators: 200}, logistic regression {C: 0.1, penalty: l2}, and support vector machine {C: 0.1, gamma: scale, kernel: rbf}, XGBoost {learning_rate: 0.01, max_depth: 3, n_estimators: 200, subsample: 1.0}, LightGBM {learning_rate: 0.01, n_estimators: 200,num_leaves: 63} The optimal parameters for each model in the active group were: random forest {max_depth: 10, min_samples_split: 10,n_estimators: 200}, logistic regression {C: 0.1, penalty: l2}, support vector machine {C: 0.1, gamma: auto, kernel: rbf}, XGBoost {learning_rate: 0.1, max_depth: 3, n_estimators: 200, subsample: 1.0}, LightGBM {learning_rate: 0.01, n_estimators: 200, num_leaves: 31}.

The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score metrics were used to compare the performance of the two groups of models. Among them, the ROC curve was used to evaluate the model’s classification performance at different thresholds, while the AUC value represents the model’s overall discriminative ability. The closer the AUC value is to 1 (ranging from 0.5 to 1), the better the model’s classification performance. Accuracy is the proportion of correctly predicted samples to the total samples and applies to the case of category balance. It ranges from 0 to 1, the closer to 1 the better, but can be misleading when categories are not balanced. Sensitivity measures the model’s ability to recognize positive categories, with values closer to 1 indicating fewer underreporting. Specificity measures the model’s ability to recognize negative categories, with values closer to 1 indicating fewer false positives. The F1-score is a reconciled average that combines precision and recall for unbalanced datasets. It ranges from 0 to 1, with values closer to 1 indicating better model performance [26].

Shapley’s additive interpretation (SHAP) method was used to visualize the model outputs and interpret the best machine learning models for the physically active and inactive groups respectively. This method is based on game theory and can break the traditional machine learning “black box” feature to further explain the output of the machine learning model. The SHAP value can be used to explain the unique contribution of each predictor variable to the model, with higher values indicating more positive contributions to the model and vice versa.

Statistical analysis

The machine learning process was run on the Jupyter Notebook platform with Python version 3.9.13. Descriptive statistics were performed using STATA 16.0. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test and expressed as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were compared using the t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test and expressed as means and standard deviations. P < 0.05 represents statistical significance.

The evaluation criteria not only considered performance on the testing dataset but also accounted for the performance gap between the training and testing datasets to ensure model generalizability and stability.

For the inactive group, the SVM model demonstrated a minimal difference of 0.026 between the training dataset AUC (0.823) and the testing dataset AUC (0.797), indicating acceptable generalization performance. In addition, it achieved the highest or near-highest sensitivity and F1-score among all models in the testing dataset, with no apparent signs of overfitting. Overall, its performance was stable and balanced (Table 2).

Importance of model features

The SHAP value feature plot enables a more intuitive demonstration of the relationship between the best performing model (SVM for the inactive group and LightGBM for the active group) and the features of the data. The X-axis represents the SHAP value, with larger values representing a greater influence of the factor on the model output. Red color indicates high feature values and blue color indicates low feature values. To explain the model output results in more detail, the top five important features of the two groups were output separately, drawing on previous studies as well as the model feature importance scores [27]. Discussion

Middle-aged and older adults in the physically inactive group reported a significantly higher subjective life expectancy risk rate (38.8%) than those in the active group (26.8%). It may indicate that high physical activity was a protective factor for subjective life expectancy in middle-aged and older adults, which is consistent with the results of previous studies [8]. Focusing on physical activity and subjective life expectancy in middle-aged and older adults is an important public health issue. Previous studies have focused more on the relationship between the two, while the present study builds on this foundation to further provide a customized prediction model for subjective life expectancy in physically active and inactive middle-aged and older adults. In the present study, five machine learning algorithms RF, LR, SVM, XGBoost, and LightGBM were employed to construct predictive models for the risk of subjective life expectancy among physically active and inactive middle-aged and older adults. Based on multiple performance metrics, including the AUC, F1 score, accuracy, and specificity, the SVM was ultimately identified as the optimal algorithm for the inactive group, while LightGBM was determined to be the optimal choice for the active group. This phenomenon may be attributed to differences in how the models handle nonlinear relationships and feature importance within the data. LightGBM, a tree-based gradient boosting approach, can effectively capture complex nonlinear relationships through its hierarchical structure, which may explain its superior predictive performance in the active group, a subgroup typically characterized by richer nonlinear feature patterns [28]. In contrast, the SVM exhibited better performance in the inactive group, likely due to its ability to delineate class boundaries effectively in high-dimensional, complex feature spaces [29]. Moreover, substantial differences in feature distributions between the active and inactive groups may necessitate distinct model selection and training strategies. Cross-validation and hyperparameter tuning were applied to identify the most suitable model for each subgroup, underscoring the personalized and group-specific nature of model selection.

In prior research, machine learning approaches have been more frequently applied for prediction rather than for the identification of key risk factors. The primary objective of machine learning is to optimize predictive accuracy by training models to detect patterns in data, rather than to directly infer causal relationships. By contrast, traditional statistical methods are grounded in predefined model structures and assumptions, employing techniques such as regression analysis and hypothesis testing to determine which variables exert significant effects on outcomes, thus emphasizing causal inference. Machine learning methods, on the other hand, can uncover intricate associations between features and prediction outcomes. For instance, algorithms (SVM) optimize classification boundaries to separate different categories of data. While these models do not directly perform causal inference, feature importance assessments (e.g., SHAP values) can reveal which predictors play a pivotal role in the modeling process. Accordingly, machine learning can serve a dual role: not only predicting outcomes but also identifying potentially influential variables within complex, high-dimensional datasets. Although such approaches cannot substitute for causal explanations, they offer a novel avenue for uncovering latent risk factors in multifaceted data environments.

In order to better visualize the internal decision-making process of the two machine learning models, the SHAP values were generated in this study. It was found that for the subjective life expectancy risk prediction model for middle-aged and elderly people in the inactive group, the top five largest features were age (0.150), perceived health (0.143), physical pain (0.130), depression (0.068), and life satisfaction (0.041). Compared to the first five features of the active group: perceived health (0.566), age (0.240), level of education (0.182), pension insurance (0.135), and physical pain (0.128), perceived health, age, and physical pain were common influences in both groups. Interestingly, age and perceived health ranked first and second in feature importance for both groups. This not only supports the robustness of the optimal algorithm’s results but also highlights differences in subjective life expectancy risk between active and inactive older adults.

Age is the most important feature in the SVM model, and previous studies have confirmed that biomedical factors such as age, gender, and personal health history are risk factors for subjective life expectancy [6]. The present study further found that age plays the most important role among such factors. Age serves as the most direct determinant of aging and mortality. As it increases, it progressively approaches the threshold at which middle-aged and older adults perceive their life expectancy, making it the primary risk factor among physically inactive individuals in this population. However, in the LightGBM model, perceived health is the most important feature. It has been found that the more physically active individuals are, the more they are concerned about their health and have a higher perception of health [30]. From a theoretical perspective, the health belief model suggests that every individual has an internal drive to stay healthy, and when individuals perceive the threat of disease they will adopt healthy behaviors such as physical activity to maintain the continuity of the individual’s life [31]. Therefore, perceived health became the first influencing factor for middle-aged and older adults in the active group, perhaps as an externalized manifestation of middle-aged and older adults’ own internal behavioral beliefs.

Physical pain is the third most important feature of SVM and the fifth most important feature of LightGBM. Physical pain is an adverse factor that leads to depression and induces mental health problems [32, 33]. Pain and depression have been found to be associated with higher risk of death and lower subjective survival probability (SSP), and high pain status significantly reduces subjective life expectancy in middle-aged and older adults [34]. In addition, physical pain will also affect an individual’s cognitive abilities. One study revealed that pain interferes with attention, cognitive function, and executive function [35]. In this context, the interference with subjective life expectancy, which is an individual’s cognitive judgment of his or her remaining time, will bear the brunt of the interference [36]. It is worth noting that depression is the fourth most important feature in the SVM model. The risk accumulation model proposes that the risk of occurrence of adverse events has a cumulative effect and that there is a risk association between adverse events [37]. There is a close association between pain and depression, and the two may have a mutually reinforcing negative effect, which in turn increases the risk of subjective life expectancy in middle-aged and older adults. However, it is important to note that depression was not among the top five important features of the active group, and past research has shown that physical activity is one of the interventions to improve depression [38], which has been demonstrated in several groups such as college students, pregnant women, and older adults [39–41]. Thus, the high level of physical activity in the active group may have objectively counteracted the adverse risks posed by depression to them.

While life satisfaction ranks as the fifth most important protective factor in the SVM model’s SHAP analysis, it does not appear among the top five features in the LightGBM model. This discrepancy may reflect inherently higher life satisfaction among active middle-aged and older adults, causing it to be outweighed by other factors in the importance ranking. Individuals in the inactive group were more likely to have physical and mental health problems such as depression and physical pain, and thus the odds of life satisfaction being output as a strong correlate of depression and pain may have increased. It should not be overlooked that in the LightGBM model, education level and pension insurance are the third and fourth most important features, respectively, but these features do not appear in the SVM model. The common feature is that for the active group, the first five important features of the elderly in the inactive group focus on the physical and mental health dimensions, while in the active group there are also socio-demographic factors in addition to the physical and mental health dimensions. This may suggest that physical and mental health issues are at the forefront of subjective life expectancy considerations for physically inactive middle-aged and older adults, whereas active middle-aged and older adults may have incorporated more non-“top” factors because the physical and mental health issues they face are less prominent.

Although the present study identified key feature factors associated with subjective life expectancy among middle-aged and older adults across different physical activity groups, its cross-sectional design limits investigation of temporal patterns, particularly trends in subjective life expectancy over time. Subjective life expectancy may be shaped by the long-term influence of multiple factors, including socioeconomic status, healthcare security systems, and lifestyle behaviors [42, 43]. Conducting trend analyses could elucidate the patterns of change in subjective life expectancy and uncover the underlying health dynamics driving these changes. However, the use of cross-sectional data imposes inherent limitations on the direct assessment of such temporal trends. Future research could adopt longitudinal study designs to systematically explore the dynamic changes in subjective life expectancy and its determinants across different subpopulations, thereby providing a more robust evidence base for long-term policy formulation. Moreover, our findings underscore the critical importance of subjective healthy life expectancy as both a public health metric and a key indicator in healthy aging research. Unlike subjective life expectancy, subjective healthy life expectancy not only reflects individuals’ judgments regarding the length of life but also integrates their subjective evaluation of the duration of healthy living, thus offering a more comprehensive indicator of older adults’ quality of life and health expectations. Consequently, future research that seeks to advance understanding of subjective life expectancy should also consider the potential value of incorporating subjective healthy life expectancy into the analytical framework. Finally, because this study relied on self-reported data, the findings may be affected by subjective reporting bias. Future investigations should incorporate more objective indicators to strengthen the validity and reliability of outcome assessment.

Conclusions

In this study, five machine learning models were developed using the CHARLS database to identify subjective life expectancy risk factors in physically active and inactive middle-aged and older adults. Among them, SVM and LightGBM are the best performing algorithms for the two groups, respectively. Age, perceived health, physical pain, depression, and life satisfaction are the most influential features in the SVM group, and perceived health, age, education level, pension insurance, and physical pain are the most influential features in the LightGBM group.
