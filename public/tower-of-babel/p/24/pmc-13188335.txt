Teacher-student relationships and social support as drivers of learning and behaviour in Malaysian gifted students
Authors: Fonny Dameaty Hutagalung, Nur Eva, Sumaia Mohammed Zaid, Mohd Hakimi bin Zainal Abidin, Kamelia Syafiera Binti Ahmad Kamarul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study examined the relationships among parental involvement, teacher-student relationships, child/adolescent social support, self-regulated learning, and behavioural outcomes (strengths and difficulties) among Malaysian gifted students. Specifically, the study investigated whether teacher-student relationships mediate the relationship between parental involvement and student outcomes and whether child/adolescent social support moderates the relationship between parental involvement and self-regulated learning and behavioural outcomes.MethodologyA total of 308 gifted students aged 10–17 years participated in this study, including 152 males and 156 females. Participants were enrolled in a Malaysian residential programme for gifted students. Data were analysed using correlation, mediation, and moderation analyses through the PROCESS macro.FindingsThe results indicated that parental involvement was positively associated with self-regulated learning and behavioural outcomes. Teacher-student relationships partially mediated the relationship between parental involvement and behavioural outcomes but did not mediate the relationship between parental involvement and self-regulated learning. Child/adolescent social support was positively related to self-regulated learning; however, its moderating effect on the relationship between parental involvement and student outcomes was limited.ConclusionThese findings highlight the complex interplay of family, school, and social factors in shaping the academic and behavioural development of gifted students. The study provides insights for educators and parents in fostering balanced parental engagement and supportive learning environments to promote the holistic development of gifted learners.Keywords: Parental involvement, Child/adolescent social support, Self-regulated learning, Behavioural outcomes, Teacher-student relationships

Background

Gifted students are commonly defined as individuals who demonstrate exceptional cognitive abilities or the potential for high performance in intellectual, creative, or academic domains [2, 3]. Although gifted learners often display advanced intellectual capabilities, their development extends beyond cognitive achievement and includes important social and emotional dimensions. These students frequently encounter unique psychosocial challenges in educational environments, including difficulties in peer relationships, heightened emotional sensitivity, and frustration with instructional practices that do not match their advanced learning needs [51]. Previous studies have reported mixed developmental outcomes among gifted students. While some demonstrate strong psychosocial resilience and adaptability, others experience emotional vulnerability, including social isolation, perfectionism, or reduced motivation due to repetitive or insufficiently challenging academic tasks [36, 52, 64, 78]. Recent research also suggests that social and motivational factors, such as peer support, grit, and resilience, play an important role in shaping gifted students’ academic persistence and psychological adjustment [16, 46]. These findings highlight the importance of examining the social environments that support gifted students’ development. Consequently, understanding the contextual factors that support both the academic and socio-emotional development of gifted students remains an important area of research.

Parental involvement and student development

Parental involvement has consistently been identified as a critical factor in shaping children’s academic and psychological development. Drawing on ecological systems theory and self-determination perspectives, parents contribute to their children’s development by providing emotional encouragement, learning resources, and guidance that foster competence and autonomy [9, 25]. Empirical evidence indicates that supportive parental involvement is associated with higher academic achievement, improved social competence, increased motivation, and better emotional well-being among students [31, 68]. Eccles and Harold [17] parental involvement framework further suggests that supportive parenting practices enhance students’ motivation and self-regulation by promoting positive academic beliefs and adaptive learning behaviours. Recent research also indicates that parental support and autonomy-supportive parenting practices contribute significantly to the development of students’ self-regulated learning strategies and academic engagement [49]. For gifted learners in particular, parental support plays a crucial role in nurturing advanced abilities while also helping them cope with academic pressure and socio-emotional challenges. However, the influence of parents does not occur in isolation; teachers and school environments also play a significant role in shaping gifted students’ developmental outcomes [33, 34].

Teacher-student relationships in gifted education

Within school settings, teacher-student relationships represent a central element influencing students’ emotional security and academic engagement. Drawing on attachment theory and contemporary educational models, supportive interactions with teachers provide students with a sense of trust, belonging, and guidance that facilitates both academic and socio-emotional development [29, 66]. For gifted students, positive teacher–student relationships are particularly important because teachers often serve as key figures in identifying talent, providing appropriate learning challenges, and supporting advanced learning needs. Research has shown that gifted students who experience supportive and responsive relationships with their teachers tend to demonstrate higher levels of motivation, engagement, and resilience [34, 40]. Conversely, conflictual or unsupportive relationships may contribute to disengagement and underachievement. Importantly, teacher–student relationships may function as a mediating mechanism through which external influences, such as parental involvement affect students’ learning behaviours. When parents actively support their children’s education, students may develop more positive attitudes toward school, which can strengthen their relationships with teachers and subsequently enhance their academic engagement and learning regulation [61].

A theoretical distinction may also help explain how teacher-student relationships influence different developmental outcomes among gifted students. Behavioural outcomes, such as emotional adjustment, social behaviour, and classroom conduct, are closely linked to students’ interactions with significant adults in the school environment. Supportive teacher-student relationships can provide emotional guidance, behavioural expectations, and a sense of security that help students regulate their behaviour and adapt to school norms [61]. In contrast, self-regulated learning primarily involves internal cognitive and metacognitive processes, including planning, monitoring, and evaluating learning strategies [50, 81]. Although positive teacher relationships may contribute to students’ motivation and engagement, the development of self-regulated learning often depends more strongly on individual learning strategies, metacognitive awareness, and personal motivation. Consequently, teacher-student relationships may have a stronger influence on behavioural outcomes than on students’ self-regulated learning processes.

Social support in educational contexts

In addition to parental and teacher influences, social support from the broader educational environment plays a crucial role in students’ psychological well-being and academic functioning. Social support refers to the perception that individuals are valued, cared for, and assisted by members of their social network [10]. In school contexts, social support typically originates from parents, teachers, and peers, each contributing distinct forms of emotional, informational, and instrumental assistance. Peer social support is particularly significant during the school years, as peers provide opportunities for social comparison, emotional validation, and collaborative learning experiences [71]. Recent studies have further demonstrated that peer support plays a significant role in strengthening students’ academic resilience, persistence, and engagement in learning activities [16, 63]. Longitudinal research indicates that supportive peer environments can foster motivational characteristics such as grit and perseverance, which are essential for sustained academic performance. For gifted students, peer relationships may be especially complex. While intellectually stimulating peer interactions can enhance engagement and academic motivation, social isolation or peer misunderstanding may negatively affect emotional well-being and self-confidence [13, 46] [13],Neihart et al., 2016). Consequently, strong social support networks may serve as protective factors that reduce stress, anxiety, and behavioural difficulties among gifted students [23, 38].

From a socio-ecological perspective, social support may also influence how environmental factors affect students’ developmental outcomes. According to the stress-buffering hypothesis, supportive social networks can reduce the negative impact of stress and enhance individuals’ coping capacity [10]. In educational settings, peer and teacher support can strengthen students’ confidence, emotional resilience, and engagement in learning activities. Recent studies have shown that perceived social support from family, peers, and teachers is associated with lower academic stress and better psychological adjustment among students [14]. For example, research indicates that peer and teacher support can enhance students’ coping abilities, reduce feelings of helplessness, and improve academic engagement and achievement. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that social support contributes to students’ psychological well-being by buffering the negative effects of stress and anxiety [77]. For gifted students who may experience social isolation or high academic expectations, supportive peer relationships can therefore play a particularly important role in reinforcing the positive effects of parental involvement on motivation and academic adjustment. Consequently, social support may function as a moderating factor that influences the strength of the relationship between parental involvement and students’ learning and behavioural outcomes.

Beyond its direct influence on student well-being, social support may also function as a moderating factor that shapes the strength of relationships among developmental variables. Moderation occurs when the magnitude or direction of a relationship between two variables depends on the level of a third variable [27]. In educational settings, social support can buffer the negative effects of stress while amplifying positive developmental processes. For example, students who receive strong peer and teacher support tend to demonstrate greater resilience, academic persistence, and engagement when facing challenging learning situations [16, 63]. Among gifted learners, social support may strengthen the positive effects of parental involvement on learning behaviours by reinforcing students’ confidence and motivation. Conversely, limited social support may weaken the beneficial impact of parental involvement, particularly when students feel socially isolated or misunderstood within their school environment.

Self-regulated learning in gifted students

Self-regulated learning (SRL) represents a key construct influencing gifted students’ academic success. SRL refers to the processes through which learners actively regulate their cognition, motivation, and behaviour in order to achieve academic goals [81]. These processes include goal setting, strategic planning, self-monitoring, and self-reflection, which enable students to manage their learning activities effectively and adapt their strategies when encountering challenges [55]. For gifted students, SRL is particularly important because their advanced cognitive abilities often require them to engage with complex learning tasks and independent study. Efklides [18] Metacognitive and Affective Model of SRL further highlights the dynamic interaction between cognitive processes, metacognitive monitoring, and emotional regulation in shaping learning outcomes.

In addition to individual cognitive factors, research indicates that supportive learning environments play a crucial role in fostering students’ self-regulated learning skills. Family support, teacher guidance, and positive peer relationships can promote motivation, persistence, and adaptive learning strategies that facilitate the development of SRL [50]. However, despite their high academic potential, gifted learners may still encounter difficulties in regulating their learning. Factors such as boredom, insufficient academic challenge, perfectionism, or socio-emotional stress can hinder the effective use of self-regulation strategies [11, 82]. Moreover, emotional challenges such as low self-confidence or social isolation may further disrupt gifted students’ ability to regulate their learning processes effectively [22].

Behavioural outcomes in gifted students

Gifted students’ behavioural outcomes often reflect a combination of advanced cognitive abilities and heightened emotional sensitivity. While many gifted learners demonstrate strong problem-solving skills, creativity, and leadership potential, they may also experience challenges in emotional regulation, social adjustment, or stress management [6, 73]. Research suggests that these behavioural patterns are influenced by the interaction of individual abilities with environmental support systems. When educational environments fail to meet gifted students’ intellectual and emotional needs, students may experience disengagement, frustration, or behavioural difficulties [73]. Therefore, examining the contextual factors that influence gifted students’ behavioural and learning outcomes is essential for designing effective educational interventions.

Taken together, the literature suggests that the development of gifted students is shaped by a complex interaction of family, school, and social influences. Parental involvement provides foundational support for motivation and learning, while teacher–student relationships may translate this support into positive academic experiences. At the same time, social support from peers and teachers may strengthen or weaken these relationships by moderating students’ emotional and motivational responses. Self-regulated learning represents a key mechanism through which these contextual factors influence students’ academic and behavioural outcomes. However, limited research has examined these relationships simultaneously within the Malaysian context. Addressing this gap may provide important insights into how educational and social environments can better support the holistic development of gifted students.

Despite growing international attention to gifted education, empirical research within Southeast Asian contexts remains relatively limited, particularly in examining the psychosocial development of gifted learners. In Malaysia, initiatives in gifted education have primarily focused on identifying high-ability students and supporting their cognitive development, most notably through programmes implemented by the PERMATApintar National Gifted Centre at Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia. However, comparatively fewer studies have examined the social, emotional, and behavioural aspects of gifted students’ development within the Malaysian educational context. Existing Malaysian research has largely concentrated on issues such as talent identification, academic performance, and programme implementation, while providing limited empirical investigation into the broader psychosocial factors influencing gifted students’ development [1, 4].

This research gap is particularly important in Malaysia because many gifted students participate in specialised enrichment or residential programmes where the nature of parental involvement, teacher-student relationships, and peer support may differ from that of traditional schooling environments. In such contexts, students often face increased academic expectations and social adjustment challenges, making psychosocial factors such as social support and self-regulated learning particularly important for their academic engagement and behavioural outcomes. Moreover, Malaysia’s multicultural educational environment may shape how family involvement and teacher support influence students’ learning behaviours and emotional adjustment. Therefore, the present study addresses this gap by examining how parental involvement, teacher-student relationships, and social support influence gifted students’ self-regulated learning and behavioural outcomes within the Malaysian context.

This study seeks to answer the following research questions:

RQ1: How does parental involvement relate to self-regulated learning and behavioural outcomes (strengths and difficulties) among Malaysian gifted students?

By examining these factors, the study aims to offer meaningful contributions that could inform policymaking, educational practices, and support systems, ultimately fostering an environment that promotes the holistic development and success of gifted children in Malaysia.

Method

Participants

A total of 308 gifted students (152 males and 156 females) from GENIUS@Pintar, the Malaysian Gifted and Talented Centre at Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (UKM), participated in this study. GENIUS@Pintar is a fully boarding school for students who achieve an IQ score of 130 and above, selected through a rigorous process. The programme is open to students from all over Malaysia through an online application system, as well as nominations by school counsellors and teachers. The selection process consists of three stages.

In the first stage, applicants sit for an IQ screening test adapted from the Malaysian Screening Test. Students who meet the required threshold are then invited to participate in a selection camp. During the second stage, students are assessed and observed on various abilities, including leadership, problem-solving, and participation in structured activities. In the final stage, shortlisted students undergo a one-month trial stay at the GENIUS@Pintar boarding school to evaluate their independence and adaptability. Only students who successfully complete all stages are formally admitted into the programme and identified as Gifted and Talented.

The age of participants ranged from 10 to 17 years old. Students were selected using a simple random sampling method from the entire student population at the centre. All the students had an IQ score of over 130, determined through standardised UKM intelligence testing, qualifying them as gifted. To recruit participants, researchers collaborated closely with the centre’s teachers. The teachers played a key role in facilitating the distribution of the questionnaires, which were administered during class time to ensure high participation rates. Consent forms were provided to both students and their parents, detailing the study’s purpose and assuring confidentiality of responses. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary, and students were free to withdraw at any stage.

Instruments

The main variables in this study include parental involvement in academic achievement, teacher-student relationships, child/adolescent social support, self-regulated learning, and behavioural outcomes. These variables were measured using a series of standardised questionnaires. All the questionnaires were administered in English. The questionnaires were compiled into a single booklet and distributed to the participants. The booklet included sections on demographic data, as well as instruments designed to measure teacher-student relationships, child/adolescent social support, parental involvement, self-regulated learning, and behavioural strengths and difficulties among gifted children. Each of these questionnaires is elaborated upon in the following sections.

Teacher-student relationships questionnaire

This questionnaire was used to assess the quality of interactions between teachers and students in the classroom. Students were asked to rate their relationship with their teachers. The instrument was developed by Pianta and Nimetz [54] and consists of 15 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale. It has been shown to predict students’ classroom behaviour, school retention, and academic outcomes. One example item is, “I share an affectionate, warm relationship with this child.” Participants respond using a Likert scale ranging from 1 (definitely does not apply) to 5 (definitely applies). The authors reported excellent psychometric properties across multiple studies and samples, including internal consistency from 0.86 to 0.89. The Teacher-student relationships Questionnaire demonstrated adequate construct validity and convergent validity [54]. In the current study, the reliability was 0.74.

Child and adolescent social support scale

The Children and Adolescents Social Support Scale, developed by Malecki et al. [43], measures perceived social support in children and adolescents from grades 3 to 12. In this study, researchers used a 12-item subscale to assess support from classmates (e.g., “My classmates help me when I am struggling with schoolwork,” with responses on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The authors of this scale reported test–retest correlations over an 8-week interval, with coefficients of 0.70 for the total scale and ranging from 0.60 to 0.76 for the subscales. This scale also demonstrated adequate construct validity as it effectively captures the distinct dimensions of social support from different resources [41]. In the current study, the reliability coefficient was 0.93.

Parental involvement in academic achievement questionnaire

Parental involvement was assessed using a questionnaire grounded in Epstein’s (2002) model of parental engagement. Students were asked to report their perceptions of their parents’ involvement in supporting their academic learning. The instrument consisted of 30 items representing six dimensions of parental involvement: communication, parenting, learning at home, volunteering, decision-making, and collaboration with the community. Because the original framework was developed to assess parental practices, several items were adapted for student-report to reflect students’ perceptions of their parents’ behaviours. For example, students were asked to indicate the extent to which statements such as “My parents maintain effective communication with teachers at school” described their parents’ involvement. Responses were measured using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Previous research has demonstrated that parental involvement measures based on Epstein’s framework show good predictive validity, as higher levels of parental involvement are associated with improved academic outcomes among students [20]. The internal consistency of the Parental Involvement in Academic Achievement Questionnaire is high, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.75 to 0.90 across the six dimensions. In the current study, the reliability of this questionnaire was 0.95.

Self-regulated learning scale

The Self-Regulated Learning Scale, developed by Zimmerman and Schunk [83], is designed to assess students’ capacity to manage and direct their own learning processes. The instrument evaluates core components of self-regulated learning, including goal setting, self-monitoring, strategic action, and self-reflection. It provides insight into the extent to which learners plan, regulate, and evaluate their learning activities, thereby supporting the development of effective study behaviours and promoting improved academic performance. One example item is, “I plan out projects that I want to complete.” Respondents rate their agreement on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Studies have confirmed the factorial validity of the scale, demonstrating that its items measure distinct yet related dimensions of self-regulated learning. The SRL scale has demonstrated high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.70 to 0.90 across its different subscales [83]. In the current study, the reliability was 0.79.

Strengths and difficulties questionnaire

The Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) is a brief behavioural screening questionnaire. It was developed by Goodman [24]. It consists of 25 items divided into five scales: emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity/inattention, peer relationship problems, and prosocial behaviour. One of the items is “Considerate of other people's feelings.” Respondents rated their responses on a Likert scale from 1 (not true) to 3 (certainly true). The SDQ has been demonstrated to possess strong construct validity and convergent validity across various populations. It has been found to have acceptable internal consistency, with Cronbach's alpha values ranging from 0.70 to 0.80 for most subscales, indicating satisfactory reliability [24]. In the current study, the reliability was 0.67.

Procedures

This study was reviewed and approved by the Universiti Malaya Medical Centre–Medical Research Ethics Committee (UMMC-MREC), Universiti Malaya (Ref. No. UM.TNC2/UMREC_2544). Prior to data collection, official permission was also obtained from the PERMATA Pintar National Gifted Centre, UKM, to collect data from the students. These approvals ensured that the study complied with ethical standards and safeguarded the rights and well-being of all participants.

All participants were informed that their participation was voluntary, that their data would be kept confidential, and that the information collected would be used solely for research purposes. They were also assured of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without any negative consequences. As the participants were under 18 years of age, written informed consent was obtained from both the participants and their parents or legal guardians. The consent form clearly outlined the study’s purpose, procedures, and measures taken to ensure confidentiality.

The five questionnaires were collated and distributed to the study sample in paper form. Each participant received a booklet containing all five questionnaires, stapled together for ease of handling. Detailed instructions were provided for each questionnaire to ensure participants understood how to complete them accurately. These instructions were both written on the questionnaires and verbally explained by the researchers to ensure clarity and consistency. The students were given a quiet environment to complete the questionnaires and were allowed to ask questions if they needed further clarification.

On average, the students took between 30 and 55 min to complete the questionnaires. Researchers monitored the completion process to provide support and ensure that the students adhered to the instructions. Upon completion, the questionnaires were collected and securely stored to maintain confidentiality. The data collected were then entered into a secure database for analysis. This rigorous process ensured the reliability and validity of the data collected for the study.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were analysed using SPSS version 26 to summarise demographic characteristics and questionnaire responses. Prior to the primary analyses, data were screened for accuracy, missing values, normality, outliers, and reliability. Missing data were inspected, and cases with more than a small proportion of missing responses were excluded pairwise/listwise (state which you used). Internal consistency of each scale and subscale was examined using Cronbach’s alpha. Multiple regression analyses were conducted using the PROCESS macro (Version 4) in SPSS to examine the proposed mediation and moderation effects. Mediation models tested whether teacher–student relationships and child/adolescent social support mediated the association between parental involvement, self-regulated learning, and behavioural outcomes. Moderation models tested whether child/adolescent social support moderated these associations. All continuous predictor variables were mean-centred before analysis. Standard errors were estimated using bootstrap procedures with 5,000 resamples.

Results

Demographic data

The participants in this study comprised 152 males (49.4%) and 156 females (50.6%). Most participants were between 15 and 17 years old (66.8%), while 33.1% were aged 10–14 years. Given the relatively broad age range, participants were categorised into two developmental groups: 10–14 years (early to mid-adolescence) and 15–17 years (late adolescence), see Table 1. This classification reflects developmental stages commonly discussed in the literature and aligns with the Malaysian secondary school system structure. All participants were enrolled in a gifted boarding school and were fully engaged in formal education. Prior to conducting inferential analyses, the dataset was screened for missing values and outliers. A total of 16 cases were removed, resulting in 292 valid cases used for the mediation and moderation analyses.

Relationship between parental involvement, self-regulated learning, and behavioural outcomes

Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to examine the relationships among the study variables. The results revealed a strong positive correlation between parental involvement and self-regulated learning (r = 0.52, p < 0.001). A similarly strong positive relationship was observed between self-regulated learning and child/adolescent social support (r = 0.52, p < 0.001). In addition, moderate positive correlations were found between parental involvement and child/adolescent social support (r = 0.46, p < 0.001), parental involvement and teacher–student relationships (r = 0.36, p < 0.001), self-regulated learning and behavioural outcomes (r = 0.38, p < 0.001), and behavioural outcomes and teacher–student relationships (r = 0.40, p < 0.001). Weak positive correlations were observed between parental involvement and behavioural outcomes (r = 0.25, p < 0.001), self-regulated learning and teacher–student relationships (r = 0.24, p < 0.001), and child/adolescent social support and teacher–student relationships (r = 0.14, p < 0.05). No significant relationship was found between child/adolescent social support and behavioural outcomes.

Mediation model: self-regulated learning

A mediation analysis was conducted using PROCESS to examine whether teacher-student relationships mediate the relationship between parental involvement and (a) self-regulated learning and (b) behavioural outcomes. Figure 1 illustrates the mediation analysis examining the role of the teacher-student relationship in the relationship between parental involvement and self-regulated learning.

Mediation path model of the relationship between parental involvement and self-regulated learning through the teacher-student relationship

The findings revealed that parental involvement was positively and significantly associated with teacher-student relationships (Path a), indicating that higher parental involvement was associated with more positive and supportive teacher-student relationships. Parental involvement was also positively and significantly associated with self-regulated learning (direct effect, Path c′), indicating that higher parental involvement predicted higher levels of students’ self-regulated learning. However, teacher-student relationships were not significantly associated with self-regulated learning (Path b), indicating that variations in teacher-student relationships did not significantly predict changes in self-regulated learning when parental involvement was controlled.

The indirect effect was not significant, as the bootstrap confidence interval included zero. These results indicate that teacher-student relationships did not mediate the relationship between parental involvement and self-regulated learning (see Table 2).

Moderation model: self-regulated learning

Moderation analyses were conducted using PROCESS Model 1 (Hayes, 2022) to examine whether child/adolescent social support (CASS) moderates the relationship between parental involvement and (a) self-regulated learning and (b) behavioural outcomes. CASS was treated as a categorical moderator with three levels: Level 1 (low social support), Level 2 (moderate social support), and Level 3 (high social support). The low social support group (Level 1) was used as the reference category.

The regression model predicting self-regulated learning was significant and explained 37.4% of the variance. Parental involvement positively and significantly predicted self-regulated learning, indicating that higher levels of parental involvement were associated with higher levels of students’ self-regulated learning. Child/adolescent social support also showed significant positive main effects, such that students with moderate and high levels of social support reported higher self-regulated learning compared to those with low social support.

The interaction between parental involvement and moderate social support was significant and negative (B = − 0.12, p = 0.046), indicating that the positive relationship between parental involvement and self-regulated learning was weaker among students with moderate social support compared to those with low social support. In contrast, the interaction between parental involvement and high social support was not significant, suggesting that high levels of social support did not significantly alter this relationship (see Table 4).

Discussion

The current study aimed to investigate the mediating role of teacher–student relationships in the association between parental involvement and both self-regulated learning and behavioural outcomes, as well as the moderating role of child/adolescent social support.

Summary of key findings

The findings of this study revealed that parental involvement was positively associated with both self-regulated learning and behavioural outcomes among Malaysian gifted students, indicating that higher parental involvement contributes to more adaptive learning behaviours and more positive behavioural adjustment. Furthermore, child/adolescent social support showed significant positive main effects on self-regulated learning, with higher levels of social support associated with higher self-regulated learning. However, the interaction analysis indicated that moderate levels of social support weakened the positive relationship between parental involvement and self-regulated learning, suggesting a buffering or compensatory effect. In contrast, social support did not significantly moderate the relationship between parental involvement and behavioural outcomes.

Relationships among study variables

The results indicated a significant relationship between self-regulated learning and child/adolescent social support. These findings support the theoretical importance of self-regulation skills for effective learning and performance across different contexts [53]. Studies have shown a significant relationship between self-regulated learning and child/adolescent social support, emphasising the impact of supportive environments on the development of self-regulation in students [67]. Additionally, research has highlighted the interplay between self-regulated learning and externally regulated learning during adolescence, indicating a decrease in both forms of regulation over time, as well as their association with teacher and parental behaviours, such as autonomy support and achievement pressure [48]. Furthermore, the COVID-19 pandemic has provided insights into self-regulated learning in online environments, highlighting disparities in self-regulation scores among different student subgroups and emphasising the importance of interventions to create a supportive online learning environment for all students [5].

On the other hand, the lack of correlation between child/adolescent social support and behavioural outcomes among Malaysian gifted children suggests that the relationship between social support and behavioural outcomes may be more complex and influenced by additional contextual factors. The research on child and adolescent well-being indicates that while social support plays a crucial role in mental health outcomes, there seems to be a lack of direct correlation between social support and behavioural outcomes [7, 12]. This suggests that the relationship may be weak or influenced by additional contextual factors, highlighting the complexity of factors influencing child and adolescent well-being [65]. Understanding this independence can guide future research directions by emphasising the need to explore additional variables that may impact behavioural outcomes beyond social support alone.

Mediating role of teacher-student relationships

The results demonstrated a robust direct effect of parental involvement on the teacher-student relationships, indicating that higher levels of parental involvement are associated with stronger teacher-student relationships. This finding aligns with existing literature suggesting that when parents are actively engaged in their children's education, teachers perceive these students more positively, which in turn fosters a supportive and collaborative relationship [28, 56]. Similarly, parental involvement significantly predicts self-regulated learning, supporting the notion that students benefit directly from their parents' engagement in their educational processes. These results are consistent with theories and empirical studies that highlight the positive impact of parental involvement on students' academic behaviours, motivation, and autonomy in learning [44, 57].

Interestingly, the direct effect of the teacher-student relationships on self-regulated learning among Malaysian gifted students was found to be non-significant. This result suggests that, contrary to some prior research that emphasises the importance of teacher-student rapport in promoting self-regulatory behaviours [15, 72], in this specific context, the teacher-student relationships does not have a statistically significant direct impact on self-regulated learning of Malaysian gifted students when parental involvement is accounted for. This could be attributed to the possibility that the influence of the teacher-student relationships might be more indirect or context-specific, varying significantly across different educational settings and student populations.

In addition, the indirect effect of parental involvement on self-regulated learning through the teacher-student relationships was also found to be non-significant, as the 95% bootstrap confidence interval included zero. This indicates that the teacher-student relationships do not serve as a mediating variable in the relationship between parental involvement and self-regulated learning among Malaysian gifted students. One possible explanation could be that the benefits of parental involvement are direct and multifaceted, influencing various aspects of a student's academic life and learning strategies independently of their relationship with teachers [68].

Additionally, the results showed that parental involvement has a significant predictive value for the teacher-student relationships. This suggests that increased parental involvement is associated with more positive teacher-student relationships. This finding aligns with previous research, which emphasises the importance of parental engagement in fostering a supportive educational environment that enhances teacher perceptions and interactions with students [8, 75].

Furthermore, the results demonstrate that the teacher-student relationships significantly influence students' behavioural outcomes among Malaysian gifted students. This indicates that better teacher-student relationships are associated with an increase in positive behavioural outcomes and a decrease in adverse behavioural outcomes. These findings are consistent with prior studies that emphasise the critical role of teacher-student relationships in promoting student well-being and adaptive behaviours [58, 74]. Additionally, the direct effect of parental involvement on behavioural outcomes among Malaysian gifted students was significant. This suggests that higher levels of parental involvement were significantly associated with students’ behavioural outcomes, suggesting that parental engagement is related to students’ emotional and behavioural functioning. This finding underscores the multifaceted influence of parental engagement, which extends beyond academic support to encompass behavioural and emotional aspects of student development [69].

Crucially, the mediation analysis reveals that the teacher-student relationships partially mediate the relationship between parental involvement and behavioural outcomes among Malaysian gifted students. The significant indirect effect suggests that a portion of the influence of parental involvement on student behaviour operates through its impact on the teacher-student relationships. This mediation effect underscores the importance of cultivating strong teacher-student relationships as a mechanism through which parental involvement can lead to improved behavioural outcomes for students [39].

Moderating role of child/adolescent social support

The present study also aimed to explore the impact of parental involvement on self-regulated learning among Malaysian gifted students, and to determine whether this relationship is moderated by child/adolescent social support. The findings indicated that the overall model was significant, suggesting a meaningful relationship among the variables.

Parental involvement was found to be a significant positive predictor of self-regulated learning, indicating that higher levels of parental involvement were associated with better self-regulated learning outcomes. This finding aligns with previous research showing that parental involvement plays a crucial role in supporting students’ motivation, autonomy, and learning behaviours [70, 76]. Parents who are actively engaged in their children's education provide support, guidance, and resources that facilitate the development of self-regulation skills [45].

Child/adolescent social support was also a significant positive predictor of self-regulated learning, with students reporting higher levels of self-regulation when they experienced greater social support. This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that support from peers, teachers, and the broader environment enhances students’ ability to manage learning demands, maintain motivation, and apply effective learning strategies [19].

The interaction between parental involvement and child/adolescent social support was partially significant. Specifically, the interaction between parental involvement and moderate social support (compared to low social support) was statistically significant and negative, indicating that the positive relationship between parental involvement and self-regulated learning was weaker at moderate levels of social support. In contrast, the interaction with high social support was not significant, suggesting that high levels of social support did not alter this relationship. Therefore, the moderating role of child/adolescent social support should be interpreted cautiously.

This finding suggests that the influence of parental involvement on self-regulated learning may vary depending on the level of social support available to students. Moderate levels of social support may reduce the relative influence of parental involvement on self-regulated learning. In contrast, at high levels of social support, students may already benefit from a strong support system, which limits the additional contribution of parental involvement.

In addition, parental involvement was positively associated with behavioural outcomes among Malaysian gifted students, indicating that higher parental involvement was linked to more positive behavioural outcomes (i.e., greater strengths and fewer difficulties). However, this relationship should be interpreted with caution, as the quality and type of parental involvement may influence outcomes differently. Parental involvement can range from supportive and autonomy-enhancing practices to more controlling or intrusive behaviours. Previous research suggests that excessive parental control may contribute to negative emotional and behavioural outcomes, including increased anxiety, pressure, and reduced autonomy [35, 57, 59]. For gifted students, who often face heightened expectations, such forms of involvement may lead to increased stress and potential behavioural difficulties.

In the case of Malaysian gifted students, parental involvement may not always align with fostering independence. Instead, some parents might adopt a highly structured, performance-oriented approach, emphasising academic success over emotional and social well-being. While this kind of involvement can lead to strengths, such as academic achievement or goal-oriented behaviour it can also exacerbate difficulties like anxiety, perfectionism, or behavioural problems when students feel overwhelmed by expectations. When parental involvement is characterised by excessive control, students may experience a decline in self-regulation, a crucial component for navigating challenges and academic pressures [82].

This finding suggests that while parental involvement encourages academic strengths, it may simultaneously impose emotional and behavioural strains on gifted students. The challenges of high parental expectations may intensify feelings of inadequacy, leading to behavioural difficulties, even when the child is performing well academically. These difficulties may include internalising behaviours, such as anxiety, or externalising ones, such as frustration and rebelliousness [68].

Contrary to expectations, neither child/adolescent social support (at a moderate level or higher) showed significant main effects on behavioural outcomes. This finding suggests that, independently, the levels of social support from peers, teachers, and the community do not have a direct impact on the behavioural outcomes measured in this study. This might indicate that other factors, such as the quality of the support or the specific types of interactions, are more critical in influencing behavioural outcomes [62].

The interaction terms between parental involvement and moderate social support (compared to low social support) and between parental involvement and high social support (compared to low social support) were not statistically significant, indicating that child/adolescent social support does not significantly moderate the relationship between parental involvement and behavioural outcomes. This suggests that the level of social support does not alter the impact of parental involvement on behavioural outcomes. These findings are consistent with some previous research that highlights the complex and multifaceted nature of social support and its interactions with other variables [30, 42].

The conditional effects analysis further elaborated on these findings by showing that the effect of parental involvement on behavioural outcomes is significant only when child/adolescent social support is at the highest level. This may suggest that the association between parental involvement and behavioural outcomes varies across levels of social support. One possible explanation is that in high-support environments, the influence of parental involvement, whether positive or negative, is more noticeable and can either mitigate or exacerbate existing behavioural tendencies [26].

An additional consideration in interpreting the findings relates to the broader conceptualisation of giftedness beyond cognitive ability. Giftedness has historically been associated with high intelligence or IQ; however, contemporary research emphasises that cognitive ability alone does not fully explain gifted students’ academic, social, and emotional development. Modern theoretical models suggest that gifted development results from the interaction of cognitive abilities with environmental and intrapersonal factors. For example, Renzulli’s three-ring model highlights the combined roles of above-average ability, creativity, and task commitment [60], while Gagné’s Differentiated Model of Giftedness and Talent proposes that natural abilities require supportive environmental and personal catalysts to develop into observable talent [21]. Increasingly, researchers have also highlighted the importance of emotional intelligence (EQ), which involves understanding and managing emotions, demonstrating empathy, and coping with stress. Studies show that emotional competencies play a significant role in how gifted students manage academic pressure, regulate behaviour, and adjust socially within school environments [47, 79]. Moreover, recent research indicates that gifted students often experience asynchronous development, where advanced cognitive abilities coexist with uneven social-emotional development, highlighting the importance of considering emotional and social factors alongside intellectual ability in understanding gifted students’ outcomes.

An important contextual factor when interpreting the findings of this study is that the participants were enrolled in a boarding school programme for gifted students. Boarding school environments may influence the nature and frequency of parental involvement because students typically reside on campus and therefore have less frequent daily interaction with their parents compared with students attending day schools. Research indicates that residential schooling can alter traditional patterns of parent–child interaction and may reduce opportunities for direct parental supervision and involvement in daily learning activities [37, 80]. In such settings, students often rely more heavily on school-based relationships with teachers and peers, while parental involvement may occur through indirect forms such as communication, emotional support, and guidance during school breaks. Despite these structural differences, parental support remains an important factor in students’ academic engagement and well-being [28, 32]. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted within the context of gifted residential education, and caution should be exercised when generalising the results to students in traditional day-school environments.

Research also suggests that boarding school contexts can shift students’ social dynamics, increasing the importance of peer and teacher relationships in shaping developmental outcomes while altering patterns of parental interaction [37]. Consequently, some dimensions of parental involvement, such as learning at home or direct supervision of academic work, may occur less frequently in residential settings, whereas communication and emotional encouragement may become more prominent forms of parental engagement.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow causal inferences to be drawn from the observed relationships. Second, the sample consisted of gifted students aged 10–17 years, spanning the early to late stages of adolescence. Although age grouping was applied, developmental differences may still have influenced the findings. In addition, students were nested within classes and a single school context; however, class-level identifiers were not available, and potential clustering effects could not be modelled statistically. Some scales produced Cronbach’s alpha values below the conventional 0.70 threshold, suggesting reduced internal consistency for specific measures. This should be taken into consideration when interpreting the findings.

Third, the reliance on self-report questionnaires may introduce social desirability bias, particularly for constructs such as self-regulated learning (SRL) and behavioural outcomes. In a high-achieving gifted school context, students may over-report positive behaviours (e.g., self-regulation) and under-report behavioural difficulties to present themselves in a favourable way. This tendency may have inflated the observed relationships between parental involvement and positive student outcomes. Therefore, these associations should be interpreted with caution, as they may represent the upper range of the true effects. Future research should address this limitation by using multi-informant approaches, such as incorporating teacher or parent reports, to provide a more balanced and reliable assessment of these constructs.

Finally, the study was conducted in one Malaysian gifted education setting with a relatively small and academically homogeneous sample; therefore, the findings should be generalised with caution. Future research should employ longitudinal and multi-level designs, use larger and more diverse samples, and incorporate additional contextual and individual factors (e.g., teacher characteristics, school climate, and student attributes) to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the processes examined.

Conclusion

The study emphasises the importance of parental involvement for self-regulated learning and behaviour in Malaysian gifted students. It directly improves self-regulation and teacher-student relationships, although these relationships do not significantly mediate learning outcomes. However, teacher-student relationships partially mediate the effect of parental involvement on behaviour, highlighting their significance. Social support moderates the relationship between parental involvement and self-regulated learning, but not behaviour, indicating that it amplifies academic benefits but has a less significant impact on behaviour. Overall, parental involvement, supportive teacher-student relationships, and social support are crucial for the academic, behavioural, and social development of gifted students. Future research should adopt longitudinal designs, including more diverse samples, and explore additional variables that moderate and mediate the relationship. Investigating the qualitative aspects of parental involvement and social support can further enhance understanding, enabling educators, policymakers, and parents to better support gifted children in reaching their full potential across multiple domains.
