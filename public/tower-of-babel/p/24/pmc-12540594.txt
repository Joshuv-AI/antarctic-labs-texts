Distribution patterns and origin of potentially toxic elements in soils of the Vanadzor urban area (Armenia)
Authors: Gevorg Tepanosyan, Tatevik Yenokyan, Zhenya Poghosyan, Astghik Gevorgyan, Lilit Sahakyan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Urban soils preserve important information about the processes conditioning the pollution levels of urban areas. Thus, geochemical characterization of the urban areas is essential for improving environmental quality and reducing potential risks. The results of this study showed that Cu, Pb, Zn and Cr in soils of Vanadzor city exhibited similar spatial distribution patterns. The higher contents of these elements were located close to industrial and residential areas suggesting their predominant anthropogenic origin. In addition, these elements display EF above moderate level. Three geochemical associations unveiled by k-means clustering and the interrelationship of the studied elements within these geochemical associations revealed by the hierarchical clustering showed that Cr, Cu, Zn, Pb are linked to Ca minerals or Fe oxy/hydroxides. The results of the hierarchical clustering of the subdataset (upper 25th quartile (> 75%) of the Ca database) revealed that the contents of Cr, Zn, Cu, Pb are lined to Fe, whereas the clustering of soil samples having > 75% contents of Cr, Zn, Cu, Pb revealed a link between these elements and Ca suggesting their fixation by Ca minerals. These findings inferred the existing differences in behavior of Cr, Zn, Cu, and Pb sorption by Ca minerals and Fe oxide-hydroxide in various areas of the city territory. Non-carcinogenic health risks to children were detected for Fe (three sites) and Cu (one site). For Pb, adverse health effects to children were observed in the main residential part of the city, whereas for adults, a precautionary risk level was identified in these areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10653-025-02827-9.

Introduction

The alteration of the chemical composition of soils in urban areas and the pollution with potentially toxic elements (PTEs) are considered as one of the main ecological problems related to humans (Dong et al., 2020; Long et al., 2021). The interest in the factors influencing the enrichment of these elements in the urban environment is increasing due to the high levels of pollution and PTEs-associated human health risks (Adewumi & Ogundele, 2024; Binner et al., 2023; Ordóñez et al., 2003; Siddig et al., 2025). High contents of PTEs, which act as co-factors for the occurrence of various diseases, can adversely affect human health, leading to disorders in various organ systems, cardiovascular disease and cancer (skin, stomach, kidneys, lungs) (Alengebawy et al., 2021; Christoforidis & Stamatis, 2009; Li et al., 2004; Rahman et al., 2021; Verma et al., 2020). Children are considered a particularly vulnerable group due to their active digestive system and sensitivity to hemoglobin, which allows them to absorb PTEs more rapidly (Li et al., 2004; Rahman et al., 2021).

In the urban environment, soils act as the main sink for PTEs, and soil studies are crucial for a comprehensive understanding of the quality of an urban environment (Dytłow & Górka-Kostrubiec, 2021; Long et al., 2021). The soil environment reflects long-term pollution and allows the identification of pollution sources altering the chemical composition of an urban environment, which is important for controlling emissions and reducing or preventing negative impacts on human health (Long et al., 2021). The contents of PTEs in the urban soils are usually controlled by both natural (geogenic, soil formation, weathering etc.) and anthropogenic (vehicular emissions, industrial emissions, coal combustion, construction, sewage sludge, waste disposal etc.) processes (Bi et al., 2018; Dytłow & Górka-Kostrubiec, 2021; Wei & Yang, 2010; Zhang et al., 2019). The combined outcome of these processes is the formation of multi-elemental intensive anomalies of PTEs in urban soils of many cities. The high contents of Cu, Pb, and Zn in the urban environment are related to the traffic emissions, brake pads, wear and tear of automobile parts (Morera-Gomez et al., 2021; Rahman et al., 2021) due to their use as vulcanized materials in automobile tires and as components of motor oil (Han & Lu, 2017; Yu et al., 2021). High contents of Pb are also related to the historical use of leaded gasoline (Binner et al., 2023; De Silva et al., 2021). The potential sources of Cu include corrosion of construction materials made of copper alloys (electrical rods, pipes), non-ferrous metal smelting, fertilizers, phosphoric, nitric, and sulfuric acids production, and ceramics (De Vivo et al., 2018). Emissions of Pb, Cu, Zn are also associated with coal combustion (Binner et al., 2023). Mn, Fe, V, Ba, Ca, K, and Cr are mainly linked to natural processes and local geological characteristics of the area (Cicchella et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2021). However, it should be emphasized that the development of the urban environment implies that these elements can be introduced into the urban environment from anthropogenic activities. For example, as a result of intensive construction activities in some urban areas, significant amounts of Ca can be released into the environment (Greinert et al., 2024; Jorat et al., 2020). Calcium accumulated in urban soils may alter natural environmental features and lead to the formation of relatively alkaline conditions, which in turn change the natural balance of the geochemical migration of PTEs.

In environmental geochemical investigations of urban areas, the study of PTEs spatial distribution patterns, pollution levels, and revealing their relationship with each other is carried out using various statistical methods and different geochemical indices (Brenko et al., 2024; Thiombane et al., 2019). Compositional data analyses combined with the geospatial mapping can provide an insight on the spatial patterns of geochemical processes, facilitate the identification of PTE origin, allow the detection of geochemical anomalies and reasons for their occurrence (Argyraki & Kelepertzis, 2014; De Vivo et al., 2018). Approaches of compositional data analysis aim to open closed systems of geochemical data (Filzmoser et al., 2018; Greenacre, 2021; He et al., 2022) and reveal hidden patterns in the underlying data structure (Banríon et al., 2023; Sadeghi et al., 2024; Shaukat et al., 2016; Tepanosyan et al., 2024). The application of dimension reduction methods such as principal component analysis (PCA) allows to reduce the number of variables, group them into representative vectors, and identify the relationships between these vectors, while preserving important information (Reiman et al., 2008; Sikakwe et al., 2020). Another important approach in geochemical studies is Enrichment Factor (EF), which is used to estimate the pollution by PTEs and identify their origin. This method is widely accepted and is based on the normalization of the observed element using reference element (i.e. Fe, Al, Ti, etc.) (Bern et al., 2019; Reimann & De Caritat, 2005; Selvaggi et al., 2019).

The city of Vanadzor was one of Armenia’s major industrial centers (Vanadzor Municipality, 2017a) and has undergone significant socio-economic transformations in recent decades, including the shift from heavy industry to light and processing industries. Previous environmental geochemical studies from the Vanadzor city (Sahakyan et al., 2019; Tepanosyan et al., 2024) targeted only a few elements (i.e. Pb, Hg). In particular, Tepanosyan et al. (2024) identified anomalous Pb levels near industrial areas and old garages. In the case of Hg, even if existed some anthropogenic influence, no pollution was detected in the city territory (Sahakyan et al., 2019). Therefore, the industrial history of the city, as well as the changes in its socio-economic conditions and current development may have an essential reflection on the natural geochemical characteristics of a broader list of elements and their levels in the urban environment, highlighting the need for a comprehensive study on the contents and spatial distribution patterns of some PTEs (Cr, V, Ti, Ca, K, Zr, Sr, Rb, Zn, Cu, Fe, Mn, Pb, Ba) in the soils of Vanadzor, unveil their geochemical associations, and identify their potential origin. The results obtained within this study can provide science-based information about the geochemical characteristics of city soil, their pollution levels by PTEs and areas where potential negative consequences for human health can be expected. In addition, the results of this study are an important component for decision-making in urban development and environmental quality improvement processes.

Materials and methods

Sampling site

Vanadzor (40° 48′ 46″ N, 44° 29′ 18″ E) is the third largest city in the Republic of Armenia. The city covers an area of 26 km2, with a population of 76.030 (ArmStat, 2023). The city is located in the northern part of the country at an absolute altitude of 1350 m a.s.l. (ArmStat, 2023), and is surrounded by the Bazum and Pambak mountain ranges. The geological basis is represented by the Eocene-Quaternary volcanic, volcanic-sedimentary, effusive, intrusive, and fractured clastic formations represented by tuff breccias, various types of tuffs, and quartz porphyries. In the area of the city, stone-gravel deposits are present (Stepanyan et al., 1985). The climate of the city is temperate. Winters are relatively mild, with constant snow cover. Average air temperature in January is − 3.1 °C, while in July, it is 18 °C. The average absolute humidity is 8.16 g/m3 and the average annual precipitation is 557 mm, of which 60–70% falls as rain in the spring and summer months (Vanadzor Municipality, 2017b). Since 1978, 27 large industrial enterprises have been operating in the city, including “Chemical Combine”, “Vanadzor Thermal Power Plant”, “Automatica”, “Autogenmash”, factories for lighting equipment, chemical fibers, and precision machinery (Vanadzor Municipality, 2017a). After the 1988 Spitak earthquake, several factories were destroyed, and some stopped operating. Heavy industry was then replaced by light and processing industries during the last decades, which are now the leading sectors in the city (Vanadzor Municipality, 2020). Currently, there are more than 40 industrial and manufacturing enterprises (mechanical engineering, chemical industry, light industry, shoe and leather goods production) (Vanadzor Municipality, 2017a).

Soil sampling

The geochemical soil survey of Vanadzor was carried out in July–August, 2016. Sampling was done with a M 1:25,000 scale using a 0.25 × 0.25 km grid (16 samples per 1 km2). In total, 355 samples were collected with stainless steel spade (Fig. 1) from the upper 5 cm of the soil layer (Johnson et al., 2011; Saet et al., 1990). At each sampling site, 3–5 subsamples were randomly taken and were mixed thoroughly to obtain a bulk sample․ Samples (0.8–1.5 kg) were stored in plastic bags and transported to the laboratory. The samples were air-dried at room temperature (< 20 °C), then crushed with a porcelain mortar and pestle, and sieved through a 2 mm sieve. Approximately 5 g of homogenized soil was grounded in an agate mortar and stored in sealed bags until analyses.Fig. 1Spatial distribution of soil sampling sites

Analytic methods and QA/QC

The total contents of Cr, V, Ti, Ca, K, Zr, Sr, Rb, Zn, Cu, Fe, Mn, Pb, and Ba in the soil samples were determined by X-ray fluorescence spectrometry (Olympus Innov-X-5000 (USA)) according to EPA method 6200 (US EPA, 2007). The QA/QC of the chemical analysis was done by estimating the Relative percent difference (RPD%) of duplicate samples and the Percent difference (PD%) (Cicchella et al., 2008; US EPA, 2007) of the certified reference materials (NIST 2711a and NIST 2710a). The results showed that the accuracy and precision of the studied elements were in range 1.1–15.7% and 0.4–6.3%, respectively.

Enrichment factor (EF)

The enrichment factor (EF) was calculated to evaluate the degree of an anthropogenic origin of elements in the soil. The EF was calculated with Ti as the reference element using the following equation (Lu et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2014; Zheng et al., 2010):1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$EFi = \frac{{Cn_{i sample} /C_{Ti} }}{{Cn_{i background} /C_{ Ti background} }}$$\end{document}EFi=Cnisample/CTiCnibackground/CTibackgroundwhere Cn(i sample) represents the content of the ith element in soil, CTi is the content of Ti in the soil sample, as a reference element, Cn(i background) is the background content of the ith element in Vanadzor’s soil (Saghatelyan et al. 2018), and CTi background is the background content of Ti in the Vanadzor’s soil. The value of EF < 2 indicates deficiency to minimal enrichment; 2 ≤ EF < 5: moderate enrichment; 5 ≤ EF < 20: significant enrichment; 20 ≤ EF < 40: very high enrichment; and EF ≥ 40: extremely high enrichment (Lu et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2014; Zheng et al., 2010). According to Lu et al. (2009) an EF > 10 indicates that the contents of element has a dominant anthropogenic origin.

Results and discussion

PTEs contents and statistical description

The descriptive statistics of the studied elements (Cr, V, Ti, Ca, K, Zr, Sr, Rb, Zn, Cu, Fe, Mn, Ba, and Pb) are presented in Table 1. A comparison of the mean and median contents in urban soils showed that the mean Zn, Cu, and Pb contents exceed the median by 1․5, 1․8, and 1․8 times, respectively, indicating right-skewed distributions of these elements. This is confirmed by skewness values of these elements different than 0. According to the results of the Shapiro–Wilk test, the studied elements did not follow a normal or lognormal distribution, which indicates the presence of anomalous values in the dataset. This suggests that the elemental contents exhibit a heterogeneous origin, possibly originating from multiple, distinct environmental conditions or anthropogenic sources dispersed across the territory of the city of Vanadzor. The absence of a normal or lognormal distribution further reflects the heterogeneous nature of the dataset, implying that the spatial and compositional variability of the elements is conditioned by complex, non-uniform processes rather than a single, consistent source or mechanism.

Among the studied elements, Cu, Pb and Zn showed high (> 60%) coefficients of variation (CV) of 405%, 293% and 207%, respectively․ These CV values indicate the significant anthropogenic input and the presence of potential pollution sources of these elements in the soils of Vanadzor city.

Figure 2 shows that outliers and extreme values were detected for all the studied PTEs. Positive outliers were recorded for all elements, while negative outliers were recorded for V, Ti, K, Zr, Sr, Rb, Cu, Fe, and Mn. The map of the spatial distribution of multivariate outliers (Fig. 3) showed that the outliers were recorded in 40 samples, most of which are located near the industrial areas of the city characterized by a relatively high density of road network. These results suggested that the natural chemical composition of soil is significantly altered in these areas.Fig. 3Spatial distribution of multivariate outliers of the studied elements.

Spatial distribution of multivariate outliers of the studied elements.

The median contents of the studied elements in the soils of Vanadzor were compared with other two largest cities of Armenia: Yerevan and Gyumri. The contents in Vanadzor are very close to those in Yerevan. In particular, the median contents of Cu and Ba observed in Vanadzor were 1.08 and 1.11 times higher, respectively, while median contents of Cr, V, Ti, Zn, Fe, Mn, and Pb were 1.80, 1.15, 1.15, 1.09, 1.43, 1.33, and 1.53 times lower, respectively (Tepanosyan et al., 2016). Median content of Cr in Vanadzor’s soils exceeded that in Gyumri by 5.78 times. For the other elements, the observed values were very close to each other (the median contents of Ti, Zn, and Cu exceeded those in Gyumri by 1.22, 1.09 and 1.22 times, respectively, and were 1.20, 1.08, 1.10 and 1.08 times lower for Fe, Mn, Pb and Ba, respectively) (Tepanosyan et al., 2017).

The mean contents of the studied elements in the soils of Vanadzor were also compared with the same values observed in different cities of the world (SM Table 1). The comparison showed, that the mean contents of Cu, Zn and Pb in Vanadzor exceeded those in Dhaka (Bangladesh) by 3.33, 3.44 and 4.25 times (Rahman et al., 2021), in Dunedin (New Zealand) by 4․32, 1․85 and 3․75 times (Turnbull et al., 2019). The sources of these elements in the cities are considered to be a traffic emissions, industry, the mechanical abrasion of vehicle parts, the destruction of old building paints, brake wear and tear (Rahman et al., 2021; Turnbull et al., 2019)․ In Madrid (Spain) (De Miguel et al., 2007), the low contents of these elements are associated with the gradual phase-out of leaded gasoline and the modernization of the vehicle fleet. The mean contents of K, Ti, Ca and Fe in soils of Vanadzor also showed higher values compared to Dunedin (New Zealand) by 14․4, 2․98, 14․3, 1․01 times (Turnbull et al., 2019), Madrid (Spain) by 9․51, 16․3, 2․54 և 3․15 times (De Miguel et al., 2007), Salerno (Italy) by 3,3, 3․53, 10․5 և 1․16 times (Cicchella et al., 2020), Galway (Ireland) by 13․0, 2․15, 1․75 և 1․57 times (Zhang, 2006), Welshpool by 46․7, 9․54, 11․6 և 1․41 times (Rate, 2014), and Dhaka (Bangladesh) by 1․56, 1․32, 2․66 և 1․27 times (Rahman et al., 2021), respectively. Compared to Athens (Greece), the mean contents of K, Ti and Fe in Vanadzor are 16.8, 16.3 and 1.12 times higher, while Ca contents are 1.92 times lower (Argyraki & Kelepertzis, 2014). The mean Mn content of Vanadzor is 3.09 and 2.41 times higher compared to Welshpool and Athens, respectively, but it is close to that in other cities (Argyraki & Kelepertzis, 2014; Rate, 2014). The contents of K, Fe, Ti, Ca, Mn in Athens (Greece), Salerno (Italy), Dunedin (New Zealand), and Tehran (Iran) were associated with detritic lithology, clay minerals, aluminosilicate minerals and their weathering, carbonate-rich sediments and alluvial soils (Argyraki & Kelepertzis, 2014; Cicchella et al., 2020; Dehghani et al., 2017; Turnbull et al., 2019). In Vanadzor, they are spatially distributed in soil layer developed on delluvial and elluvial deposits, boulder and pebble sediments mixed with sandy loam (SM Fig. 1) which were significantly altered during decades long industrial history of the city.

However, lower mean contents of some PTEs were also observed in soils of Vanadzor compared to other cities, such as Cr in Turin (Italy) (5.56 times), Zr (2.15 times) and Rb (2.46 times) in Dhaka (Bangladesh), Ba (3.67 times) in Galway (Ireland) (3.29 times) and Dunedin (New Zealand) (1.50 times) (Padoan et al., 2017; Rahman et al., 2021; Turnbull et al., 2019; Zhang, 2006). These differences can be attributed to both local geology and anthropogenic features. In particular, the excess content of Cr in Turin (Italy) is due to the occurrence of serpentine rocks (Padoan et al., 2017).

Spatial distribution patterns of PTEs and EF levels

The spatial distribution maps of the studied elements showed (SM Fig. 2) that the spatial distribution patterns of some of the studied PTEs had similarities. In particular, the contents of the studied elements, excluding Rb, Sr, Zr, Ca, and K, that fall within the fourth quartile of the data distribution (i.e., the upper 25th percentile, > 75%) were predominantly concentrated in the city center towards the northeastern and southeastern part of the city and this distribution was spatially correlated with the area close to the industrial and residential parts of the city. Meanwhile, less than 25% contents of the studied elements are distributed in the northwestern part of the city, close to the residential area. In contrast, more than 75% contents of Zr, Sr, Rb, Ca and K are located in the northwestern and northeastern parts of the city. Analogous patterns of spatial distribution of elements were also observed in the studies conducted in Yerevan (Tepanosyan et al., 2016). In both Yerevan and Vanadzor, > 75% contents of Pb, Zn, Cu and Cr were spatially distributed in densely populated and industrial areas, which may indicate a predominant anthropogenic origin of these elements. Moreover, Pb, Zn, Cu are known as the tracers of the urban environment under heavy anthropogenic impact (Johnson et al., 2011).

The contents of elements > 50% occupied an area of 12․8–16․5 km2 (51.2–66.0% of the city territory), while less than 50% occupied an area of 9.3–13.1 km2 (37.2–52.4% of the city territory). Relatively larger areas within the > 50% interval was observed for Cu, Pb, Fe, Cr and Zn, with 16․5 km2 (66.0%), 16․3 km2 (65.2%), 15․8 km2 (63.2%), 15․4 km2 (61.6%) and 15․3 km2 (61.2%), respectively. Meanwhile, within the < 50% interval, relatively larger areas were observed for K, Mn, Sr, Ti and Ba, with 13․1 km2 (52.4%), 12․4 km2 (49.6%), 12․0 km2 (48.0%), 11․9 km2 (47.6%), and 11․9 km2 (47.6%), respectively. Larger areas with relatively higher (> 75%) contents were detected for Cu, Pb, Zn, Cr with 8․47 km2 (33.9%), 7․87 km2 (31.5%), 7․44 km2 (29.8%), 7․24 km2 (29.0%), respectively.

The EF was calculated for all elements (Pb, Zn, Cu, V, Ba, Cr, Sr, Rb, Zr, Fe, Mn) except for Ca and K, due to a lack of background values for these elements (Saghatelyan et al. 2018). The minimum, maximum and mean EF values in soil and the percentage of samples at each EF level are presented in the SM Table 2. The mean EF values showed the following decreasing patterns: Pb > Zn > Cu > > Ba > Cr > Sr,Rb > Fe > V > Zr > Mn. Minimal enrichment level was observed in all samples for V and Zr, and in the majority of samples for Sr, Rb, Fe and Mn (93.0%, 99.7%, 98.3% and 99.7%, respectively). Moderate level of enrichment was observed for Sr, Rb, Fe, and Mn in 7.04%, 0.28%, 1.69% and 0.28% of the samples, respectively. Zn, Cu, and Pb were detected at all levels of enrichment. In particular, the EF values of Zn, Cu and Pb were higher than moderate level (> 5) by 14.4%, 8.60%, and 24.7% of the samples, respectively. Geochemical associations and potential sources of the studied elements in Vanadzor soil

Geochemical associations of the studied element in the soils of the city of Vanadzor were identified using PCA and k-means clustering. According to the PCA results (SM Table 3), the first three components explained 83.5% of the total variance, with the first component (PC1) accounted for 57.8%, the second (PC2) for 14.5%, and the third (PC3) for 10.9%.

As shown in Fig. 5, the studied elements formed three groups of geochemical associations: Group I: V, Ti, Mn, Fe, and Ba; Group II: Ca, K, Zr, Sr, and Rb; Group III: Cr, Zn, Cu, and Pb.Fig. 5Geochemical associations of the studied elements

Geochemical associations of the studied elements

The soil samples representing the geochemical association of Group I are distributed throughout the city, except in the northwestern residential area, where the Group II predominates. The presence of Fe and Mn in this group suggested the dominance of the natural processes within the group, since these elements form some of the most common soil components (Fe–Mn oxy/hydroxides). In addition Fe–Mn oxy/hydroxides are known for their high surface absorption capacity for certain metals, including V, Ti and Ba (Kabata-Pendias, 2011; Manceau & Combes, 1988). At the same time, the observed link between Fe and Ti may also be attributed to the presence of certain Fe-bearing minerals (i.e. ilmenite) in the studied soils (Izawa et al., 2019). Therefore, this suggests that the soil accumulation of elements in areas where this association was identified is predominantly influenced by natural processes.

The geochemical association of Ca, K, Zr, Sr, and Rb, has a clear spatial separation and is spatially located in the northwestern part of the city, where the geological base is mainly represented by diluvial and eluvial deposits and boulder and pebble sediments mixed with sandy loams (SM Fig. 1). Among the elements in this association, the Ca-Sr and K-Rb are known as naturally occurring geochemical pairs, and the presence of Zr in soils is mainly derived from parent rocks and tends to form stable compounds with soil components (Kabata-Pendias, 2011; Shahid et al., 2013). Moreover, the area occupied by the Group II is characterized by the absence of old industrial units and deficiency to minimal enrichment of Zr, Sr and Rb. Considering these patterns and the fact that these elements also showed relatively low CV (< 60%), it can be suggested that the elements within this geochemical association also have a natural origin.

The geochemical association of Group III is represented by Cr, Zn, Cu, and Pb, and is mainly spatially correlated with the area from the city center to the east where industrial zones and various factories are located. At the same time, all of the PTEs in this group showed relatively high CV (> 60%) and intensive anomalies. Moreover, EF value for Cr, Zn, Cu, and Pb exceeded moderate level (1․41%, 14.4%, 8.60%, 24.7% of all samples, respectively). Thus, these findings inferred the anthropogenic origin of these elements in Group III. It is worth mentioning that soil samples presented in Groups I and III are spatially integrated and do not have a clear separation. Therefore, to understand the relationships of the studied elements within each group, unveil possible underlying processes conditioning their interrelationship, as well as understand the link between major elements and those (i.e. Cr, Cu., Zn, and Pb) with predominant anthropogenic origin, a hierarchical clustering was performed using the subdataset of each group (SM Fig. 4). The results showed that Pb, Cr, Zn, and Cu were represented in the following clusters (at the rescaled distance of 15): Ca, Sr, Pb, Cu, Ba, Cr, Zn (Group I); V, Ti, Fe, Mn, Cu, Zn, Pb, Cr (Group II); Cr, Pb, Cu, Ba, Fe, Zn (Group III). It was found that the anthropogenic association of elements in the Group I is together with the pair of Ca-Sr, suggesting the link of Cr, Cu, Pb, and Zn to Ca minerals. In the other two cases, the link between the Cr, Cu, Zn, and Pb is stronger with Fe inferring the dominant role of Fe–Mn oxy/hydroxides in the metal absorption and complexation processes. This difference in the link between Cr, Cu, Zn, and Pb with major elements can be explained by the comparatively high contents of Ca in Group II, which can result in different soil pH values (increasing soil pH from neutral to alkaline), thus changing the mobility and absorption features of Cu, Zn, and Pb.

Considering that the geochemical associations of elements in soil may be influenced by distinct geochemical or anthropogenic processes underlying specific enrichment mechanisms, the fourth quartile of the data distribution (i.e., the upper 25th percentile, > 75%) for Ca (natural origin) and Cr, Pb, Zn, Cu (anthropogenic origin) was analyzed separately.

Particularly, as shown in Fig. 5, the distribution of Ca, which plays a significant role in the formation of the Group II, corresponds to the areas with > 75% Ca contents. Therefore, samples with > 75% Ca contents were separated and hierarchical clustering was performed for these soil samples. According to the results (SM Fig. 5), three clusters were identified (at the rescaled distance of 5), while Cr, Pb, Zn, Cu were grouped together with Fe and Ba which is in line with the clustering results of Group II.

The clustering of the upper 25th percentile contents of Cr, Pb, Zn, Cu result showed that (SM Fig. 6) Cu, Pb, Zn and Cr are grouped with Ca, indicating that relatively high contents of these elements are fixed by Ca minerals (Kabata-Pendias, 2011). Similar results were obtained for Pb in previous study (Tepanosyan et al., 2024), where in Gyumri and Vanadzor Pb was complexed with Ca contents in the soil. The distribution map shows that the points with > 75% contents of all four elements are located in an area stretching from the city center toward the northeast and southeast (SM Fig. 7). This area correlates with the historical part of the city, which has been subject to a long-term anthropogenic impact, as well as being rich in former and current industrial unites and characterized by a dense residential network.

Human health risk posed by PTEs in Vanadzor soils

The results of the non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risk assessment are presented in Table 2. Among the studied elements, non-carcinogenic risk to children (HQ > 1) was detected for Cu, Pb, and Fe. A medium level of risk for Cu was observed at only one sampling site, whereas for Fe, it was observed at three sampling sites. A precautionary level of risk was identified in 4% of cases (14 sampling sites) for Cu and in 99.1% of cases (347 sampling sites) for Fe. In the case of Pb, three levels of risk were detected for children: 43.7% of cases (153 sampling sites) were at the precautionary level, 48.9% (171 sampling sites) at the medium level, and 7.4% (26 sampling sites) at the high level (Fig. 6). For adults, risk was detected only for Pb. No risk was observed in 43.4% of cases (152 sampling sites), while a precautionary level of risk was identified in 53.7% of cases (188 sampling sites), a medium level in 2.6% of cases (9 sampling sites), and a high level of risk at one sampling site.Fig. 6Adults and children non-carcinogenic risk level distribution in the city of Vanadzor

Adults and children non-carcinogenic risk level distribution in the city of Vanadzor

In the case of carcinogenic risk, the CR exceeded 10−6 for Cr mean and maximum values and for Pb only for maximum value (Table 2). Moreover, a precautionary level of risk was detected for both elements. For Cr, this level was observed in 99.7% of cases (349 sampling sites), while for Pb, it was detected in 21.7% of cases (76 sampling sites).

Figure 6 clearly indicates that the highest levels of risk for both adults and children are found in areas characterized by the presence of the old garages and decomposed car scrap metal. However, these areas are not typically used by children for play or regular activity. As a result, the actual risk to children is presented in areas under medium risk level, which are more commonly located in residential parts of the city. This distinction should be carefully considered during decision-making processes, particularly when prioritizing locations for the implementation of risk reduction measures.

Conclusions

The results of the data analysis showed that Cu, Pb, Zn, and Cr exhibited relatively high CV (> 60%), suggesting the influence of potential anthropogenic sources in the urban area. This was supported by EF values, which exceeded 10 for Cu, Pb, and Zn in 2.25% (8 samples), 5.91% (21 samples), and 1.97% (7 samples) of all samples, respectively, indicating a predominantly anthropogenic origin of these metals at those sampling sites. Three groups of samples were revealed which are represented by: 1) V, Ti, Mn, Fe, Ba; 2) Ca, K, Zr, Sr, Rb; and 3) Cr, Zn, Cu, Pb. Group I and III overlapping with each other, included sampling sites located close to the industrial areas and are distributed throughout the city territory. However, EF of elements presented in Group I indicated about the absence of pollution suggesting the potential natural origin of this geochemical association. Group II is spatially separated from two other groups and has no pollution levels detected suggesting a natural origin of Ca, K, Zr, Sr, Rb. The elements in Group III display very high to extremely high pollution levels, indicating their predominantly anthropogenic origin. In addition, it was found that contents of Cr, Zn, Cu, and Pb in the upper 25th percentile are linked to the Ca suggesting the affinity of these elements to Ca minerals. In contrast, in near-natural sites where Group II is located these elements are found to be linked to Fe. Human health risk assessment showed that Pb is a primary element posing adverse health effects to children. The results obtained in this study serve as a foundation for improved urban environmental management and science-based policymaking aimed at protecting soil ecosystems and selecting optimal remediation measures for contaminated sites to reduce potential health risks.
