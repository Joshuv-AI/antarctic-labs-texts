An integrated genetic approach prioritizes closely linked candidate genes underlying an open-field behavior QTL on chicken chromosome 1
Authors: Aoi Ido, Takayuki Ochiai, Momoe Terashima, Akira Ishikawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough numerous QTL associated with complex economic and behavioral traits in chickens have been identified across the genome, the specific functional variants underlying these QTL remain poorly understood. We previously identified a QTL region associated with open-field (OF) behavior on chicken chromosome 1 in an F2 population derived from a cross between Oh-Shamo (OSM), a native Japanese chicken breed with aggressive behavior, and the White Leghorn T line (WL-T), which exhibits docile behavior. In this study, we aimed to identify candidate genes for this OF QTL using an integrated genetic approach combining QTL remapping, transcriptome analysis, haplotype frequency analysis, and association analysis. QTL remapping in the F2 population using the first principal component (PC1) scores derived from 4 OF traits affected by this QTL revealed a complex logarithm of odds (LOD) profile, identifying a 95% confidence interval (CI) spanning approximately 23 Mb and containing 297 genes. RNA sequencing of F2 individuals with extreme PC1 scores, followed by expression validation using reverse transcription quantitative PCR in the parental and F1 populations, reduced the number of candidate genes from 297 to 13. Subsequent haplotype frequency and correlation analyses prioritized G2 and S-phase expressed protein 1 (GTSE1) as the strongest candidate gene underlying the OF QTL region on chromosome 1, with IQ motif and ubiquitin domain containing (IQUB), and inner mitochondrial membrane peptidase subunit 2 (IMMP2L) representing additional candidate genes. Furthermore, multiple regression analysis suggested that these 3 genetically linked genes are associated with variation in OF behavior. These findings demonstrate the utility of an integrated genetic approach for prioritizing closely linked candidate genes within a broad QTL region exhibiting a complex LOD profile and provide new insights into the genetic architecture of OF behavior in chickens.Keywords: Open-field behavior, QTL, Candidate gene, Integrated genetic analysis

Introduction

Most economically important traits in poultry, such as body weight, breast muscle weight, egg size, and behavioral traits, are quantitative in nature. In chickens, behavioral traits such as fearfulness and aggressiveness significantly affect meat and egg production and also raise animal welfare concerns (e.g., Grams et al., 2015; Hedlund et al., 2019; Hedlund and Jensen, 2021; Ishikawa et al., 2023). These traits are influenced by multiple genetic loci, known as QTL, as well as environmental factors and their interactions. QTL can be identified either through genome-wide linkage mapping in crossbred populations, such as F2 populations, or through genome-wide association studies (GWAS), which exploit linkage disequilibrium in outbred populations (Miles and Wayne, 2008). To date, numerous QTL associated with quantitative traits have been mapped across the poultry genome (e.g., Goto and Tsudzuki, 2017; Ishikawa et al., 2020, 2021; Hu et al., 2022; Velasco et al., 2024).

However, several factors hinder the identification of causal genes underlying mapped QTL (reviewed by Mackay, 2001; Albert et al., 2015; Ishikawa, 2017; Visscher et al., 2017; Qi et al., 2025). First, individual loci often have relatively small phenotypic effects (< 10% of phenotypic variance). A recent study demonstrated that single-cell RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis failed to clarify the molecular mechanisms of causal QTL genes underlying mouse fear-related behavior (Chen et al., 2024). Second, genome-wide QTL mapping in F2 populations typically localizes loci to large chromosomal regions exceeding 10 Mb. Third, the confidence interval (CI) of each locus may contain hundreds of candidate genes. Fourth, many SNPs identified by GWAS reside in noncoding regions, complicating functional interpretation. Nevertheless, identifying the causal genes and variants underlying these loci is essential for elucidating the genetic architecture of economically important traits and for enabling molecular breeding in the poultry industry.

In recent years, it has become increasingly evident that a single analytical approach is insufficient to identify candidate genes from the hundreds present within a QTL CI. (Mackay, 2001; Ishikawa, 2026). Consequently, integrative strategies that combine multiple lines of evidence have been developed to narrow candidate gene lists to a manageable number (reviewed by Ishikawa, 2026). Indeed, Ochiai et al. (2023) successfully reduced the number of candidate genes for a QTL associated with open-field (OF) behavior to only 2 by applying an integrated genetic approach to the original QTL mapping population derived from a cross between two chicken breeds with contrasting behavioral phenotypes. This approach integrates QTL remapping, RNA-seq, reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), haplotype frequency analysis, association analysis, and conditional correlation analysis, as reviewed by Ishikawa (2026). Similarly, Furuta and Ishikawa (2026) applied this approach to a breast muscle weight QTL on chromosome 2 and reduced 329 genes within the CI to 2 strong candidate genes. Therefore, the present study applies this integrated genetic approach to identify candidate genes underlying a QTL affecting innate behavior in chickens.

Since the Heian period (794-1185 Common Era) in Japan, more than 45 native Japanese chicken breeds have been established in various local regions for ornamental purposes, crowing, and behavioral traits (Imamura et al., 2021). These breeds are also known for their excellent meat and egg quality. In recent decades, commercial Jidori chickens have been developed by crossbreeding selected Japanese breeds with Western chicken breeds (Imamura et al., 2021). For example, Oh-Shamo (OSM), a native Japanese breed originally developed for cockfighting, is characterized by strong aggression as well as excellent meat quality. Therefore, OSM is often used as a sire line in the production of commercial Jidori chickens. However, its aggressive behavior is frequently inherited by the resulting commercial Jidori chickens, leading to animal welfare concerns in the Japanese poultry industry (Ishikawa et al., 2023; Terashima et al., 2023). Thus, OSM provides a unique and valuable animal model for investigating the genetic architecture of behavioral traits.

The OF test is a widely used behavioral assay to evaluate fear responses in animals, including chickens (Faure et al., 1983; Forkman et al., 2007; Durosaro et al., 2022). It is an established quantitative method for assessing innate fear and anxiety-like behaviors, using indicators such as activity levels, exploratory behavior, and vocalizations in response to a novel environment. A previous study using OF tests in day-old chicks from 6 Japanese chicken breeds and 2 White Leghorn lines demonstrated that OSM chicks exhibit low sensitivity to innate fear, whereas White Leghorn T line (WL-T) shows high sensitivity (Terashima et al., 2023). Furthermore, Velasco et al. (2024) identified a major QTL on chicken chromosome 1 associated with OF activity in F2 chicks derived from a cross between OSM and WL-T.

In this study, we aimed to identify candidate genes underlying the OF behavior QTL on chromosome 1 detected in an F2 population derived from a cross between the aggressive OSM breed and the docile WL-T breed, using an integrative genetic approach proposed by Ishikawa (2026).

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Research Committee of Nagoya University, Japan, and were approved by the committee (approval numbers: AGR2019016, A240037, A250071).

Animals

All chicks of the OSM (16 males and 10 females) and WL-T (9 males and 12 females), as well as F1 (5 males and 5 females) and F2 (119 males and 146 females) generations used in this study, were produced in previous studies (Terashima et al., 2023; Velasco et al., 2024). In those studies, chicks at 1 day post-hatch were subjected to OF tests. Subsequently, body weight was measured, and blood, diencephalon (including the hypothalamus), adrenal glands, and liver were collected from each chick and stored at −80°C.

Open-field (OF) test

Data for OF behavior were obtained from 265 F2 chicks at 1 day post-hatch in a previous study (Velasco et al., 2024). Briefly, each chick was placed in an OF arena (54 × 79 × 30 cm), and its behavior was recorded for 10 min using a video camera. Behavioral data were analyzed using SMART software version 3.0 (Panlab, Harvard Apparatus, CA, USA). A total of 13 behavioral traits were extracted: number of entries into the center zone, latency to first entry into the center zone, total time spent in the center zone, distance traveled in the periphery zone, distance traveled in the center zone, total distance traveled, resting time (< 2.5 cm/s), slow movement time (2.5–15 cm/s), fast movement time (> 15 cm/s), mean speed, mean speed excluding resting time, maximum speed, and parallel index. Among these traits, only 4 traits were significantly affected by the OF behavior QTL on chromosome 1 at the genome-wide 5% significance threshold: distance traveled in the periphery zone, total distance traveled, resting time, and fast movement time.

The raw data for the 4 traits were previously adjusted for environmental factors (Velasco et al., 2024). Specifically, total distance was adjusted for sex and test day, whereas the other 3 traits were adjusted for test day only. In the present study, these adjusted data were used for all analyses.

Principal components analysis (PCA)

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on 265 F2 chickens using the 4 OF traits described above. The analysis was conducted using JMP Standard Edition version 19.1.0 (SAS Institute Japan, Tokyo, Japan) based on the correlation matrix. The first and second principal components (PC1 and PC2) were extracted, and their scores were calculated. Because PC1 explained the majority of the total variance, only PC1 scores were used as a quantitative trait for QTL remapping. In addition, PC1 scores were used to rank individuals and to select extreme individuals within each sex for subsequent analyses, following the selective genotyping strategy proposed by Darvasi and Soller (1994).

QTL remapping

QTL analysis was conducted on 265 F2 individuals using PC1 scores as a quantitative trait. A total of 2,109 SNP markers were developed by restriction-site associated DNA sequencing (RAD-seq) in a previous study (Velasco et al., 2024). QTL mapping was performed using the scanone function implemented in R/qtl version 4.3.1 (Broman and Sen, 2009), applying the Haley–Knott regression method. Logarithm of odds (LOD) scores were calculated at 1.1-centimorgan (cM) intervals. Genome-wide significance thresholds at the 1%, 5%, and 10% levels were determined by permutation testing with 10,000 permutations using scanone. The 95% CI of the LOD peak was estimated using the 1.8-LOD drop method. The proportion of phenotypic variance explained by each QTL, as well as additive and dominance effects, was estimated using the fitqtl function in R/qtl. Additive effects were defined as half of the phenotypic difference between the 2 homozygous genotypes, whereas dominance effects were calculated as the difference between the heterozygous phenotype and the mean of the 2 homozygous phenotypes. Phenotypic differences among the 3 genotypes at the SNP marker locus closest to the LOD peak were evaluated using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test implemented in JMP Standard Edition.

To detect 2 additive QTL on chromosome 1 and epistatic QTL across the genome, a 2-QTL genome scan was performed using the scantwo function in R/qtl at 2-cM intervals. Genome-wide 5% and 40% significance thresholds for the full and additive two-QTL models were determined by 500 permutation tests.

RNA-seq analysis

3 individuals with the highest PC1 scores and 3 individuals with the lowest PC1 scores were selected from the F2 population within each sex. These 6 extreme individuals within each sex used for RNA-seq were also included in the F2 extreme groups used for subsequent correlation and multiple regression analyses. The same RNA-seq individuals were included in the extreme F2 groups used for subsequent correlation and multiple regression analyses.

Total RNA was extracted from the diencephalon of each selected individual using TRI Reagent® (Cosmo Bio, Tokyo, Japan) and the chloroform–isopropanol precipitation method. RNA concentration was measured using a Quant-iTTM RNA Broad-Range (BR) Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan) with a Qubit® Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan). The diencephalon includes the hypothalamus, which plays a central role in the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis regulating stress and fear responses (Matteri et al., 2000).

RNA-seq and subsequent data analyses were performed by Rhelixa Inc. (Tokyo, Japan). RNA quality was assessed using a Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies), yielding RNA integrity numbers (RIN) ranging from 8.7 to 9.6. Sequencing libraries were prepared using a strand-specific protocol (dUTP method) with PCR amplification using indexed primers (Supplementary Table S1). Paired-end reads (150 bp) were generated using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Adapter sequences and low-quality bases (quality score < 20) were removed using Trimmomatic version 0.38 with the following parameters: ILLUMINACLIP:path/to/adapter.fa:2:30:10 LEADING:20 TRAILING:20 SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15MINLEN:36. Cleaned reads were aligned to the chicken reference genome RefSeq GRCg6a (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_000002315.5/) using HISAT2 version 2.1.0, resulting in average mapping rates of approximately 93% across the 12 extreme individuals. Raw read counts were normalized using the relative log expression (RLE) method implemented in DESeq2 version 1.24.0. Differential expression statistics, including log2(fold change), nominal P values, and Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted P values (false discovery rate; FDR), were calculated using DESeq2 by comparing the highest and lowest PC1 score groups within each sex. Because of the limited sample size, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were initially screened using fold-change criteria rather than FDR significance alone. Genes showing an absolute log2(fold change) greater than 0.26 (corresponding to a fold change > 1.2 or < 0.83) in either sex and exhibiting the same direction of expression change in both sexes were retained as DEGs for subsequent analyses. Furthermore, PCA based on the covariance matrix and correlation analysis based on transcripts per million (TPM)-normalized expression values were performed separately for males and females using the 6 extreme individuals within each sex to evaluate sample clustering and overall consistency among biological replicates.

RNA-seq data have been deposited in the DDBJ Sequence Read Archive under accession numbers DRR539903-DRR539908 (aishikawa-0020_Run_0001-0006) and DRR540139-DRR540144 (aishikawa-0021_Run_0001-0006).

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the diencephalon of OSM (5 males and 5 females), WL-T (5 males and 5 females), F1 (5 males and 5 females), the highest F2 group (9 males and 10 females), and the lowest F2 group (10 males and 10 females). cDNA was synthesized from 1.0 μg of total RNA using the PrimeScript® RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara Bio, Otsu, Japan). RT-qPCR was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq™ II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (Takara Bio, Otsu, Japan) on a StepOnePlusTM Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan). The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s; 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 5 s and annealing/extension at 60°C for 30 s; followed by a final extension at 60°C for 60 s.

To identify suitable internal reference genes among RNA polymerase Ⅱ subunit C1 (Pol Ⅱ), TATA-box binding protein (TBP), β-actin (ACTB), and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), amplification efficiencies of both target and reference genes were evaluated using relative standard curves generated from four 5-fold serial dilutions (10, 2, 0.4, and 0.08 ng/µL). Target genes with amplification efficiencies comparable to those of the reference genes were quantified using the 2^−ΔΔCt method.

The expression stability of candidate reference genes was further evaluated using the geNorm algorithm (Vandesompele et al., 2002). Only samples with complete Ct values for all candidate reference genes (OSM, WL-T, and F1 datasets) were included in this analysis. Raw Ct values were transformed into relative quantities (Q) using the equation Q = 2^−(Ct − Ct_min), and expression stability (M) values were calculated as the average pairwise variation based on log2-transformed expression ratios. As shown in Supplementary Table S2, TBP exhibited the highest stability (M = 0.53), followed by GAPDH (0.60), ACTB (0.69), and Pol II (0.86). All reference genes showed M values below 1.0, indicating acceptable expression stability under the experimental conditions. Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S3. All reactions were performed in triplicate.

To compare gene expression levels among OSM, WL-T, and F1 groups, the effects of group, sex, and their interaction were analyzed using 2-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's HSD test implemented in JMP Standard Edition. Similarly, differences between the highest and lowest F2 groups were analyzed using the same statistical model.

Haplotype determination

Diplotypes of the highest F2 group (9 males and 10 females) and the lowest F2 group (10 males and 10 females) were determined based on genotypes at 45 SNP marker loci located within the 95% CI of the QTL on chromosome 1. 3 diplotypes were defined for each candidate gene: homozygous for the OSM-derived haplotype, homozygous for the WL-T-derived haplotype, and heterozygous for the 2 haplotypes. Diplotype and haplotype frequencies were compared between the highest and lowest F2 groups using a 2-sided Fisher's exact test implemented in JMP Standard Edition. F2 individuals carrying recombinant haplotypes were excluded from the analysis.

Correlation analysis

Correlation analyses between gene expression levels and the 4 OF traits, as well as PC1 scores, were conducted using individuals from the highest F2 group (9 males and 10 females) and the lowest F2 group (10 males and 10 females). To adjust for diplotype effect, conditional correlations were estimated using linear models with diplotype included as a covariate in JMP Standard Edition.

Multiple regression analysis

Multiple regression analyses were conducted using individuals from the extreme F2 groups to evaluate the relationships between the expression levels of the final candidate genes and the 4 OF traits and PC1 scores in JMP Standard Edition. Gene expression levels and their interaction terms were included as explanatory variables in the model. To assess multicollinearity among the explanatory variables, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated, and all VIF values were below 2, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity.

Results

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Score and factor loading plots for PC1 and PC2 are shown in Fig. 1. Supplementary Table S4 summarizes the factor loadings, eigenvalues, and proportions of total variance explained by each PC. PC1 explained 94.4% of the total variance, whereas PC2 explained 5.0%. For PC1, factor loadings for 3 traits—distance in the periphery zone, total distance, and fast time—were positive, whereas resting time showed a negative loading. These consistent and strong loadings indicated that PC1 captures the shared variance across these locomotion-related traits. Thus, F2 chicks with higher PC1 scores exhibited greater locomotor activity, whereas those with lower PC1 scores showed more immobile behavior in the novel OF arena, suggesting that PC1 reflects an overall locomotor activity axis in this behavioral context. This pattern was also evident from the movement trajectories shown in Fig. 2A. Because PC1 accounted for the majority of the total variance (94.4%), only PC1 scores were used as a composite trait for subsequent QTL remapping.

QTL remapping

A significant QTL for PC1 scores (LOD = 4.26) was detected at 14.46 Mb on chromosome 1 (Fig. 2B), where the nearest SNP marker, c1m79, was located (Table 1). This QTL explained 7.13% of the phenotypic variance. The additive and dominance effects, expressed in SD units, were 0.43 ± 0.16 and −0.81 ± 0.23, respectively. At the c1m79 marker locus, F2 chicks homozygous for the OSM-derived allele (O/O) exhibited significantly higher PC1 scores than both heterozygotes (O/W) and homozygotes for the WL-T-derived allele (W/W) (P < 0.05, Tukey’s HSD test). However, no significant difference in PC1 scores was observed between O/W and W/W individuals. These results indicated that the OSM allele increases PC1 scores and acts in a recessive manner (Fig. 2C).

QTL analysis of the 4 OF traits identified a significant QTL at the same position (14.46 Mb) on chromosome 1, with peak LOD scores similar to that for PC1 (Fig. 2B and Table 1). The additive and dominance effects for 3 OF traits (total distance, distance in the periphery zone, and fast time) were positive, as observed for PC1, whereas the effect for resting time was negative (Table 1).

Based on the 95% CIs of all 5 traits, the QTL region was defined as a 23.16-Mb interval spanning from 7.33 Mb to 30.49 Mb. According to the chicken reference genome RefSeq GRCg6a, this interval contained 297 genes.

In the scantwo analysis, genome-wide 5% and 40% significance thresholds for the full models were estimated to be 12.6 and 10.4, respectively. No significant evidence for additional additive or epistatic QTL was detected within this region, even when applying the relaxed 40% significance threshold.

RNA-seq analysis

To identify DEGs among the 297 genes within the 95% CI, RNA-seq analysis was performed using the 3 highest and 3 lowest F2 individuals in each sex, ranked by PC1 scores. As shown in Supplementary Figure S1, PCA revealed clear separation between the highest and lowest groups in both sexes, and correlation analyses showed high overall consistency among 6 individuals within each sex.

Among the 297 genes, 46 were identified as DEGs between the highest and lowest groups and exhibited the same direction of expression change in both sexes (Table 2 and Supplementary Table S5). Of these 46 DEGs, 27 were upregulated, and 19 were downregulated in the lowest group. The magnitude of differential expression ranged from −3.14 to 3.30 in log2(fold change).

RT-qPCR analysis

To further narrow the list of candidate genes from the 46 DEGs identified by RNA-seq, RT-qPCR analysis was performed using OSM, WL-T, and their F1 individuals (n = 5 per sex per group). 2-way ANOVA revealed significant group differences in expression levels for 13 of the 46 genes (Supplementary Table S6). Among these, 3 genes (ENSGALG00000052297, ENSGALG00000008309, and ENSGALG00000036488) showed significant group × sex interactions. For these genes, expression levels differed significantly among groups in females (P < 0.001, 1-way ANOVA) and were higher in OSM than in WL-T, whereas no significant differences were observed in males.

For sex-combined data, the expression levels of 2 genes (ENSGALG00000043355 and ENSGALG00000033415) were significantly higher in OSM than in WL-T (Table 3). The F1 expression level of ENSGALG00000033415 was intermediate between the 2 parental breeds, whereas that of ENSGALG00000043355 did not differ from OSM. The F1 expression level of ENSGALG00000047845 tended to be higher than those of both parental breeds. Expression levels of the remaining 7 genes (ENSGALG00000054570, ENSGALG00000051514, LRRK2, GTSE1, IQUB, ENSGALG00000038090, and IMMP2L) tended to be lower in OSM than in WL-T. Overall, RT-qPCR analysis reduced the candidate gene list to 13 genes.

Discussion

In this study, the genomic location of the OF behavior QTL on chicken chromosome 1 was re-analyzed using PC1 scores summarizing phenotypic variation in the 4 OF traits affected by this QTL. PC1 can be primarily interpreted as representing an overall locomotor activity axis in the OF arena. As expected, QTL mapping based on PC1 was consistent with results for individual OF traits obtained in the present and previous studies (Velasco et al., 2024), with identical positions of the significant LOD peak and the 95% CI. In addition, the OSM-derived allele at this locus showed a recessive mode of inheritance.

Importantly, the use of PC1 offers practical and statistical advantages over analyzing each trait independently. First, by integrating highly correlated traits into a single variable, PC1 captures their shared variance and provides a more stable phenotypic measure than individual traits. Second, it enables a more robust ranking of individuals along a continuous behavioral axis, facilitating the selection of F2 individuals with extreme phenotypes for downstream analyses. Third, in our previous studies (Ishikawa et al., 2020; Ochiai et al., 2023; Furuta and Ishikawa, 2026), QTL mapping using PC1 improved localization and resulted in narrower CIs compared to analyses of individual traits. Although the improvement in resolution was modest in the present dataset, this approach remains useful for defining candidate regions and prioritizing genes within the QTL interval. Together, these points support the utility of PC1 as a biologically meaningful and statistically efficient composite phenotype for QTL remapping.

Because a single analysis is often insufficient to identify candidate genes for a QTL, multiple lines of evidence should be integrated (Ishikawa, 2026). Accordingly, the present study applied the hypothesis-free integrated genetic approach proposed by Ishikawa (2026). To maximize sensitivity at the discovery stage, we adopted a permissive screening strategy based on extreme phenotypic sampling and moderate fold-change thresholds, recognizing that this approach increases the likelihood of false-positive findings. Therefore, genes identified by RNA-seq were regarded as preliminary candidates rather than definitive causal genes and were subsequently evaluated using multiple independent approaches, including RT-qPCR validation, haplotype frequency analysis, association analysis, and conditional correlation analysis. Through this stepwise prioritization process, GTSE1, IQUB, and IMMP2L were identified as strong candidate genes. These findings further support the utility of this integrated framework, consistent with previous studies on the OF behavior QTL on chromosome 4 (Ochiai et al., 2023) and the breast muscle weight QTL on chromosome 2 (Furuta and Ishikawa, 2026), suggesting its applicability across different traits and genomic regions.

A potential limitation of this study is the possibility of reverse causality in the observed gene expression differences. Because brain tissues were collected after the OF test, the RNA-seq profiles may partly reflect acute transcriptional responses to locomotor activity or stress rather than solely causal mechanisms underlying behavioral variation. Although the QTL examined in this study was originally identified as a genetic locus associated with breed differences in OF locomotor activity, and additional evidence from RT-qPCR validation across independent populations, haplotype frequency comparison, and association analysis supports the involvement of the identified candidate genes, these findings cannot completely exclude the possibility that some of the observed expression differences are secondary consequences of behavioral or physiological responses. Therefore, further studies designed to distinguish causal from reactive expression changes will be required.

In the initial stage, RNA-seq analysis of diencephalic tissue from the 3 highest and 3 lowest F2 individuals of each sex reduced the number of candidate genes from 297 within the 95% CI to 46 DEGs. Because this QTL explained only approximately 7% of the phenotypic variance and the sample size was limited, relatively permissive fold-change thresholds (>1.2 and <0.83) were applied to reduce the risk of false negatives. Although this strategy inevitably increased the number of candidate genes retained after RNA-seq, it effectively reduced the search space from 297 genes to 46 DEGs, providing a practical starting point for the subsequent integrative analyses.

Several limitations of the present RNA-seq analysis should be considered. First, RNA-seq was performed using bulk diencephalic tissue, which contains a heterogeneous mixture of neuronal and non-neuronal cell types. Consequently, cell type-specific expression differences associated with OF behavior may have been masked. The diencephalon was selected because it includes the hypothalamus, a central component of the HPA axis involved in stress and fear responses (Matteri et al., 2000). Second, although single-cell RNA-seq may provide higher-resolution information on cell type-specific gene expression, recent evidence suggests that even this approach does not always clarify the molecular mechanisms underlying behavioral QTL genes (Chen et al., 2024). Finally, although adrenal gland samples were collected and preserved, they were not analyzed because our primary objective was to identify candidate genes within a brain region directly involved in behavioral regulation. Future analyses of adrenal gland tissues may provide additional insights into the physiological pathways linking the identified candidate genes to OF behavior.

Another limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size used for downstream analyses. F2 individuals were selected according to the selective genotyping strategy proposed by Darvasi and Soller (1994), which recommends sampling phenotypic extremes to maximize statistical efficiency. In total, 39 individuals (14.7% of the F2 population) were analyzed in the present study, exceeding the approximately 10% guideline suggested by those authors.

Notably, the most extreme individuals used for RNA-seq analysis were also included in subsequent analyses, resulting in partial sample overlap. This design may have inflated correlation coefficients and model performance estimates, and therefore the results of correlation and regression analyses should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, supplementary model evaluation indicated no evidence of problematic multicollinearity (all VIF values < 1.1), and diagnostic plots showed no obvious evidence of heteroscedasticity, systematic bias, or substantial departures from residual normality. Thus, although predictive performance may be somewhat optimistic because of the partial sample overlap, the fitted regression models satisfied standard diagnostic criteria. Moreover, the present study followed the selective genotyping framework of Darvasi and Soller (1994), which is designed to maximize statistical efficiency by sampling phenotypic extremes. Nevertheless, validation in larger or independent populations would further strengthen the robustness of the present findings.

To mitigate these limitations, multiple validation steps were incorporated into the analytical framework. DEGs identified by RNA-seq were subsequently evaluated using RT-qPCR, haplotype frequency analysis, association analysis, and conditional correlation analysis. Importantly, RT-qPCR validation was performed using parental and F1 individuals that were not included in the RNA-seq discovery phase, providing an independent line of evidence. Although further validation using independent populations and functional approaches will be required, the convergence of evidence across multiple analytical steps increases confidence in the prioritization of the candidate genes identified in this study.

Second, RT-qPCR reduced the number of candidate genes from 46 to 13 by confirming expression differences among OSM, WL-T, and F1 individuals. Because causal genes are expected to show expression differences among these groups, this step represents cross-population validation (Ishikawa, 2026). Notably, GTSE1, IQUB, and IMMP2L showed significant differences among groups. However, only the results of expression differences observed across OSM, WL-T, and F1 populations did not provide direct evidence of causality, as these populations are not segregating populations such as the F2. The 2 breeds are fixed for alternative QTL alleles, while the F1 population is fixed for heterozygous states across the genome, which may reflect trans-regulatory variation affecting OF behavior. The initial RNA-seq analysis using segregating extreme F2 individuals can partially mitigate trans-regulatory effects. Therefore, RT-qPCR results provide supportive evidence for consistent expression patterns across genetic backgrounds as a cross-population validation step.

Third, haplotype frequency analysis further reduced the number of candidate genes from 13 to 5. Because loci contributing to the QTL are expected to show different haplotype frequencies between the extreme F2 groups, this analysis helps exclude genes less likely to be associated with the phenotype. As haplotype frequency is independent of gene expression, it provides an independent line of evidence supporting candidate gene prioritization (Ishikawa, 2026). Based on this analysis, GTSE1, IQUB, and IMMP2L remained significant candidate genes.

Finally, correlation analyses indicated that GTSE1, IQUB, and IMMP2L were strong candidate genes. GTSE1 expression was consistently higher in the highest F2 group than in the lowest group and was correlated with all 4 OF traits and PC1 score. These correlations were maintained for 4 traits (excluding fast time) after adjustment for diplotype, suggesting a direct influence on OF behavior.

However, GTSE1 expression patterns differed between the parental breeds and the extreme F2 groups. The highest F2 group, which had a high frequency of OSM-derived haplotypes, exhibited higher GTSE1 expression than the lowest group, consistent with the RNA-seq results. Hence, GTSE1 expression would be expected to be higher in the OSM breed than in the WL-T breed. Contrary to this expectation, GTSE1 expression was higher in WL-T than in OSM. This discrepancy may reflect differences in genetic background, as alleles do not segregate at all loci within each breed, and other loci may be indirectly influencing GTSE1 expression in OSM and/or WL-T.

IMMP2L expression levels were higher in the highest F2 group than in the lowest group, consistent with the RNA-seq results. However, IMMP2L expression patterns differed between the parental breeds and the extreme F2 groups. Although IMMP2L expression was correlated with 4 OF traits in simple analyses, these correlations were not retained in conditional correlation analyses adjusted for diplotype, suggesting that its effects may be indirectly mediated by other genes.

In contrast, IQUB expression patterns were consistent between the parental breeds and the extreme F2 groups, as shown by RNA-seq and RT-qPCR analyses. Although IQUB expression was correlated with 3 OF traits (total distance, distance in the periphery zone, and the PC1 score) in simple correlation analyses, no correlations were retained after adjustment for diplotype, suggesting that IQUB is unlikely to directly influence OF behavior.

QTL analysis revealed no evidence for epistatic QTL within the 95% CI. In contrast, simple multiple regression analysis showed that all 3 genes (GTSE1, IQUB, and IMMP2L) were associated with most OF traits. Conditional regression analysis further indicated that GTSE1 was associated with 4 OF traits (excluding fast time), whereas IMMP2L was associated with 2 OF traits (distance in the periphery zone and resting time). These findings suggest that the 3 genes contribute largely independently to variation in OF behavior. Although GTSE1, IQUB, and IMMP2L are located in proximity to the 2 major LOD peaks observed within the QTL interval, the present data do not provide definitive evidence that the complex LOD profile reflects multiple functional QTL. Rather, the complex LOD pattern suggests the possibility of multiple linked loci within the interval. Additional fine-mapping studies, denser genotyping, recombinant analyses, and sequencing of the QTL interval will be required to resolve the underlying genetic architecture of this region. Collectively, the integrated analyses performed in this study identify GTSE1 as the strongest candidate gene for the chromosome 1 OF behavior QTL, with IMMP2L and IQUB representing additional candidate genes that warrant further investigation.

GTSE1 encodes a microtubule-localized protein expressed during the S and G2 phases of the cell cycle (Collavin et al., 2000). It is involved in microtubule stabilization, chromosome segregation, and negative regulation of the tumor suppressor p53 (Bendre et al., 2016; Monte et al., 2003). Because microtubule dynamics are critical for neurodevelopment and neural circuit formation, alterations in these processes can affect neural connectivity and plasticity (Baas et al., 2016; Matamoros and Baas, 2016). In addition, suppression of p53 activity by GTSE1 may influence neural progenitor proliferation and survival (Checler and Costa, 2014). Together, these functions strongly suggest that variation in GTSE1 expression plays a direct role in modulating neural circuit formation and function, thereby contributing to differences in OF behavior between the chicken breeds used in the present study.

IMMP2L encodes a peptidase localized to the inner mitochondrial membrane that processes mitochondrial precursor proteins by cleaving their targeting signal peptides (Lu et al., 2008). Human genetic studies have consistently implicated IMMP2L as a risk gene for neurodevelopmental disorders, including autism spectrum disorder (ASD), Gilles de la Tourette syndrome (GTS), and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) (Kunová et al., 2022; Lawther et al., 2023), supporting its role in neural function. In mice, deletion of Immp2l alters behavioral phenotypes, including auditory-stimulus-driven instrumental responses and increased amphetamine-induced locomotion activity (Lawther et al., 2023), indicating its involvement in neural circuit regulation. Consistent with these findings, the OF test results in the present study suggest that variation in IMMP2L expression may influence neural responsiveness to environmental stressors, thereby contributing to differences in OF activity between the chicken breeds used in the present study.

Although the function of IQUB has not been fully elucidated, recent studies suggest that it regulates axonemal microtubule function in cilia and flagella. IQUB is reported to act as an adaptor protein for radial spokes within the axoneme, thereby regulating the activity of motor proteins (Zhang et al., 2022). In both humans and mice, IQUB deficiency impairs sperm flagellar motility and causes male infertility, demonstrating its essential role in microtubule structure and movement (Hu et al., 2025). Beyond motility, primary cilia are increasingly recognized as organelles essential for signal reception and transmission in neurons (Suciu and Caspary, 2021), and ciliary dysfunction is associated with neurological disorders known as ciliopathies (Youn and Han, 2018). Therefore, variation in IQUB expression may be involved in neural ciliary function and signal transduction, potentially contributing to differences in OF behavior between the chicken breeds used in the present study.

To determine whether GTSE1, IQUB, and IMMP2L are the true causal genes underlying the OF behavior QTL on chicken chromosome 1, quantitative complementation tests using gene knockout chickens in a uniform genetic background are necessary (Ishikawa, 2026). Such knockout chickens can be generated using genome editing technologies such as CRISPR/Cas9. These approaches will provide direct evidence for causal relationships between candidate genes and behavioral phenotypes, further validating the effectiveness of the integrated genetic strategy applied in this study.

It should be noted that none of the individual analyses performed in the present study provides direct evidence for causal relationships between the identified candidate genes and the OF behavior QTL. Rather, the integrated framework applied here prioritizes candidate genes by evaluating the convergence of evidence across multiple complementary analyses and independent validation steps. This strategy may be particularly valuable for studies of indigenous and locally adapted chicken breeds, such as native Japanese chicken breeds, for which the large populations and extensive resources required for comprehensive multi-omics approaches are often unavailable. Therefore, the present framework provides a practical approach for narrowing candidate genes in limited-resource populations while preserving the unique genetic diversity of indigenous breeds.

Conclusion

In this study, we applied the integrated framework proposed by Ishikawa (2026), which combines QTL remapping, RNA-seq, RT-qPCR, haplotype frequency analysis, association analysis, and conditional correlation analysis. This approach prioritized GTSE1 as the strongest candidate gene underlying an OF behavior QTL on chicken chromosome 1, with IMMP2L and IQUB representing additional candidate genes. The results demonstrate that this integrated framework is effective for prioritizing closely linked candidate genes within a single QTL region exhibiting a complex LOD profile. These findings advance our understanding of the genetic architecture of behavioral traits in chickens and provide a foundation for future studies aimed at identifying the causal genes and variants underlying behavioral QTL.
