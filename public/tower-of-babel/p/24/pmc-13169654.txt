Integrated metabolome and transcriptome analyses reveal the role played by CaWRKY44 during anthocyanins accumulation in pepper fruits
Authors: Qiaoling Deng, Zhengcong Luo, Guoping Zhang, Yuping Bai, Wei Huang, Guining Fang, Xuewen Chen, Mengjuan Li, Qiaoli Xie, Minghua Deng, Junheng Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPepper is an important vegetable and widely cultivated across the world. Anthocyanins play a crucial role in determining the color and nutritional value of pepper. In this study, light effects on anthocyanins accumulation in purple peppers were analyzed via bagging and non-bagging treatments.ResultsTranscription factor CaWRKY44 was identified as playing a key role in anthocyanins synthesis according to the metabolome and transcriptome analysis. CaWRKY44 expression increased during the different pepper pericarp developmental stages and a promoter cis-element analysis identified light and hormone-responsive elements and protein-binding motifs. VIGS-mediated silencing of CaWRKY44 reduced anthocyanins accumulation, while transient overexpression enhanced pigment deposition. Furthermore, the silencing or overexpression of CaWRKY44 modulated the expression level of anthocyanins biosynthetic structural genes.ConclusionsOur results indicated that CaWRKY44 could directly bind to the W-box cis-element in the CaDFR promoter and activates its transcription, which subsequently drives anthocyanins biosynthesis. The results could lay the foundation of the regulatory mechanism controlling anthocyanins biosynthesis in pepper.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08691-8.Keywords: Pepper, Metabolome and transcriptome, Anthocyanins, WRKY transcription factor, Regulatory mechanism

Introduction

Anthocyanins are strongly associated with fruit quality and color, and they are water-soluble flavonoid secondary metabolite that are widely present in plants and have important economic value in the fields of food, medicine, and ornamental horticulture [1]. Purple pepper has a unique purple phenotype due to the large accumulation of anthocyanins in its pericarp. This characteristic means it has significant ornamental value and enhances its ability to resist and adapt to strong ultraviolet radiation, drought, low temperatures, and pest and disease infestations, which makes it an ideal research subject for analyzing the molecular mechanisms controlling pepper fruit color formation [2]. Foods rich in anthocyanins are highly favored by consumers due to their vibrant colors and multiple health benefits. Previous studies have confirmed that a dietary intake of anthocyanins can inhibit inflammatory signaling pathways by activating the endogenous antioxidant enzyme system, thereby reducing the risk of chronic diseases [3].

Anthocyanins biosynthesis is a branch pathway of the plant flavonoid pathway and consists of three stages that require the participation of a series of enzymes. This process occurs in three stages and is cooperatively catalyzed by a variety of enzymes. Key enzymes involved include PAL, C4H, CHS, CHI, F3H, F3’5’H, DFR and ANS [4]. The synthesis pathways for anthocyanins are generally well-characterized, but there only have few researches on the molecular mechanisms governing their regulatory control. Transcription factors can regulate the transcription level of downstream target genes by binding to specific cis-acting elements or exert regulatory functions by forming complexes with other proteins [5]. Anthocyanins accumulation is primarily regulated by the MYB, bHLH, and WDR transcription factors, which can form the MYB-bHLH-WD40 (MBW) complex [6]. In apple, MdMYB10 has a high homology with Arabidopsis AtPAP1 and can promote MdDFR expression, thereby promoting the accumulation of anthocyanins in fruit [7, 8]. Previous studies have also shown that FaMYB5 in strawberry regulates the accumulation of anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins by promoting the expression of F3’H and LAR promoters and interacts with FaEGL3 and FaLWD1/FaLWD1 to form an MBW complex, which has a regulatory role [9, 10]. In kiwifruit, WRKY44 synergistically enhances anthocyanins accumulation via MYBC1 by regulates the expression level of F3’H and F3’5’H [11]. MdWRKY10 promotes anthocyanin synthesis in fruit by binding to the promoters of MdF3’5’H and MdUFGT, thereby activating their transcription [12]. In Solanaceous vegetables, research on anthocyanins has primarily focused on eggplant, tomato, and pepper. In eggplant, SmMYB75 can bind to the promoter of SmCHS and activate its expression. Overexpression of SmMYB75 leads to increased accumulation of anthocyanins, resulting in a color change of callus tissues from green to purple [13], SmMYB44, SmMYB86, and SmMYB35 have also been demonstrated to be involved in anthocyanins biosynthesis in eggplant [14]. SlMYB7 directly represses the expression of SlANS by binding to its promoter, thereby functioning as a repressor in anthocyanins production [15]. In pepper, CaMYB113 acts as a transcriptional regulator by binding to the promoters of anthocyanins biosynthesis genes CaCHS and CaDFR, thereby modulating anthocyanins accumulation in cotyledons [16]. CaMYB5 can bind to the promoter of CaPAL and activate its expression, thereby promoting anthocyanins accumulation in pepper fruits [17], Silencing of CaWD40 led to a significant reduction in anthocyanins contents in pepper fruits [18].

Light is the main environmental factor which could affect the accumulation of anthocyanins, anthocyanins could protect plants from photooxidative damage [19]. The synthesis of anthocyanins in peppers is modulated by light intensity, quality, and photoperiod, yet the complex interplay of these factors remains unclear [20]. In tomatoes, strong light promotes anthocyanins accumulation by facilitating the assembly of the SlAN11-SlTT8-SlAN2 MBW complex, thereby upregulating key structural genes. Conversely, SlMYBL2 acts as a feedback inhibitor of this pathway [21]. CaHY5 regulates anthocyanins accumulation in the lower hypocotyls of pepper by directly controlling both biosynthesis and transport processes. It functions by binding to the promoters of crucial genes, including CaF3H, CaF3′5′H, CaDFR, CaANS, and CaGST [22]. Bagging of lychee fruits markedly suppresses anthocyanins accumulation in the peel. Conversely, upon bag removal and subsequent re-exposure to light, the expression of key anthocyanins biosynthetic genes—including LcCHS, LcDFR, LcANS, and LcUFGT is significantly upregulated [23]. High light significantly induces the accumulation of total anthocyanins in grape berry skins, along with the increased expression of genes such as CHS, CHI, F3H, F3′5′H, and DFR [24]. MdWRKY1 is activated by light and subsequently upregulates the long noncoding RNA MdLNC499. This activation promotes the transcription factor MdERF109, which then induces the expression of key structural genes in the anthocyanins biosynthesis pathway [25]. Currently, research on the regulation of anthocyanins synthesis in pepper fruits by light is relatively limited, and further exploration and investigation are still needed.

WRKY proteins participate in stress resistance and developmental regulation and indirectly modulate anthocyanins accumulation by influencing secondary metabolic pathways. CaWRKY22b positively regulates pepper resistance to bacterial wilt by modulating the expression of the JA pathway-responsive gene CaDEF1 [26]. CaWRKY40 is repressed by its salt tolerance homolog CaSTH2, which functions as a negative regulator involved in the immune response to reactive oxygen species accumulation and thermotolerance. CaSTH2 physically interacts with CaWRKY40, thereby preventing CaWRKY40 from activating immunity- or thermotolerance-related genes [27]. However, there have been relatively few reports on WRKY regulation of anthocyanins synthesis in pepper. This study integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses to explore the alterations in anthocyanins contents, gene expression profiles, and regulatory pathways in pepper fruits under bagging and normal light. A WRKY transcription factor, CaWRKY44, has been identified to regulate anthocyanins accumulation in pepper fruits. This finding provides a theoretical foundation for elucidating the mechanism of anthocyanins biosynthesis and for breeding pepper varieties with high anthocyanins contents.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatment

The material ‘CS03’ is a pepper cultivar that with purple leaves and fruits under normal lighting conditions, it was grown in the greenhouse (day at 30 ± 2 °C and night at 20 ± 2 °C) at Yunnan Agricultural University, Kunming, China. In order to study the light effects on anthocyanins accumulation in purple peppers, the fruits were bagged after pollination and grown under the same water and fertilizer management as the unbagged fruits. The bagged (ZSD) and unbagged (ZSL) fruits were sampled after 30 days. Ten fruits were mixed together for each sample, and there were three biological replicates for each treatment. All the samples were frozen and stored at − 80°C for further use.

Transcriptome analysis

Total RNA was isolated from the ZSD and ZSL fruit using Trizol (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The high quality RNA from the six samples (3 replicates × 2 treatments) was sent to Novogene (China) for cDNA library construction. The six cDNA libraries were sequenced on the Illumina Hiseq 4000 platform (paired-end, 150 bp) (Illumina Inc, San Diego, CA, USA). Raw sequencing were filtered for quality and directly mapped to the reference genome Zunla-1_Release_2.0 using the hisat2 program. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to evaluate biological replicate consistency. The counts of genes were performed using feature Counts, and differential expression analysis was performed with the DESeq R package (v1.18.0), significantly differentially expressed genes were defined as those with |log₂FC| > 1 and FDR < 0.05. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways analyses were performed using KOBAS 3.0 software, WGCNA was completed using the Metware Cloud (https://cloud.metware.cn) [28].

Anthocyanins detection

Freeze-dried samples were ground into a fine powder using a 30 Hz mill for 1.5 min and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Anthocyanins quantification was performed on the MetWare platform employing an AB Sciex QTRAP 6500 LC-MS/MS system. A 50 mg powder sample was extracted with 0.5 mL methanol/water/hydrochloric acid (500:500:1, v/v/v). After vortexing (5 min), ultrasonication (5 min), and centrifugation (12,000 g, 4 °C, 3 min), the residue was re-extracted once under the same conditions. The combined supernatants were pooled and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane (Anpel) prior to LC-MS/MS analysis. Sample extracts were analyzed using an UPLC-ESI-MS/MS system (ExionLC™ AD UPLC and QTRAP® 6500 + MS, Sciex) equipped with an ESI TurboIon-Spray interface operating in positive ion mode and controlled by Analyst 1.6.3 software (Sciex). Separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY BEH C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) at 40 °C with a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min and injection volume of 2 µL. The mobile phase consisted of water (0.1% formic acid, A) and methanol (0.1% formic acid, B). The gradient program was: 0 min, 95% A; 6 min, 50% A; 12 min, 5% A (held for 2 min); returned to 95% A at 14 min and held for 2 min.ESI source parameters were: temperature 550 °C; ion spray voltage 5500 V; curtain gas 35 psi. Anthocyanins were analyzed using scheduled multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) with optimized declustering potential and collision energy for each transition. Data acquisition and metabolite quantification were performed using Analyst 1.6.3 and Multiquant 3.0.3 software (Sciex), respectively [29].

Anthocyanins contents were detected by MetWare (http://www.metware.cn/) based on the AB Sciex QTRAP 6500 LC-MS/MS platform. Significantly different metabolites between comparative groups were identified by applying a threshold on absolute Log₂FC values (VIP ≥ 1 and Log2FC ≥ 1). These metabolites were annotated using the KEGG compound database and subsequently mapped to the KEGG Pathway database. Metabolite set enrichment analysis (MSEA) was conducted on pathways containing significantly regulated metabolites, with the statistical significance of enrichment determined by hypergeometric test p-values.

Gene expression analysis of CaWRKY44

Pepper pericarps, seeds, and placenta were sampled every 10 days from 10 to 60 days post-anthesis (DPA). Total RNA was extracted with a plant RNA kit (Huayueyang Biotechnology, Beijing), and cDNA synthesis using Hifair® III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Yisheng Biotechnology, Shanghai). Gene-specific primers for CaWRKY44 were designed via NCBI and SnapGene, synthesized by Beijing Qingke Biotechnology. RT-qPCR was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix (Yisheng Biotechnology) per manufacturer instructions, using 10 DPA tissues as controls. Relative expression was calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method, primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Subcellular localization

The CDS region in CaWRKY44 without a stop codon was amplified and inserted into the ScaI/BamHI sites in the pCambia1300 vector to generate the pCaWRKY44- Cambia1300-GFP construct. The pCambia1300- GFP, pCaWRKY44-Cambia1300- GFP, and a nuclear marker (mCherry) were transferred into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. Following 2–3 days of culture, leaf samples were analyzed using confocal laser scanning microscopy to determine the subcellular localization of CaWRKY44.

Transcriptional activity analysis

The CaWRKY44 CDS was cloned into pGBKT7 via NdeI/BamHI sites to generate pGBKT7::CaWRKY44. Yeast strain Y2H was co-transformed with either this construct, negative control (pGBKT7::00), or positive control (pCL1). Transformants were selected on SD/-Trp (pGBKT7::00, pGBKT7::CaWRKY44) or SD/-Leu (pCL1). After 3–5 days at 30 °C, colonies were replica-plated onto SD/-His/-Ade/X-α-Gal and incubated 1–2 days for color development. For transactivation assays, the effector pGreenII-62-SK-BD::CaWRKY44 was constructed by inserting CaWRKY44 CDS into BamHI/KpnI sites. This effector and the reporter (pGreenII-35 S-UAS-0800-LUC) were co-infiltrated into tobacco leaves. Luciferase activity was analyzed after 72 h using in vivo imaging and quantified via dual-luciferase assays (Beyotime Kit, Shanghai).

Anthocyanins contents determination

The anthocyanins contents were determined using a Plant Anthocyanin Content Assay kit (Beijing, China). The plant tissue was homogenized in 1 mL extraction buffer, incubated at 60℃ for 30 min with intermittent shaking, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was mixed with specific buffers (1:9, v/v) and quantified spectrophotometrically at 530 nm and 700 nm. The anthocyanins contents was calculated according to the instructions of the kit.

VIGS of CaWRKY44 in pepper fruit

A 300-bp fragment of CaWRKY44 was selected using the SGN VIGS tool and cloned into the EcoRI/BamHI sites of pTRV2 to generate pTRV2::CaWRKY44. Constructs (pTRV2::CaWRKY44, pTRV2::00, pTRV1::00) were cultured in LB medium supplemented with kanamycin and rifampicin. Bacterial suspensions were resuspended in infection buffer (MgCl₂, MES, AS) to OD₆₀₀ = 0.5–0.8. After agroinfiltration, pepper fruits were rebagged. Pericarp samples exhibiting the target phenotype were collected 10 days post-infection. The positive fruits were selected by qRT-PCR and further analyzed for anthocyanins pathway structural gene expression.

Transient overexpression of CaWRKY44 in pepper fruit

The transient overexpression vector pCambia1301::CaWRKY44 was generated as described in Sect. 2.6. Subsequently, qRT-PCR was employed to assess the relative expression levels of both CaWRKY44 and key structural genes within the anthocyanins biosynthesis pathway.

Yeast one-hybrid assay (Y1H)

The CDS region of CaWRKY44 was inserted into the EcoRI/XhoI sites in pB42AD to obtain the pB42AD::CaWRKY44 bait vector. The CaDFR promoter was then isolated from the pepper DNA and cloned into the same sites as pLacZi to create the trap vector. The Y1H assay was performed according to Cheng et al. [30].

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

The CaWRKY44 coding region was ligated into the pGEX-4T-1 vector at the EcoRI/SalI sites. Following this, the recombinant pGEX-4T-1::CaWRKY44 vector was introduced into E. coli strain BL21(DE3). An electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was carried out according to the protocol of Cheng et al. [30].

Dual luciferase assay

The promoter of CaDFR gene was cloned into the KpnI/BamHI sites in the pGreenII 0800-LUC vector, which generated the reporter construct CaDFR pro:LUC. The CDS of CaWRKY44 were inserted into the BamHI/EcoRI sites in the pGreenII 62-SK effector vector and the dual luciferase assay was performed according to Cheng et al. [30].

Results

Dynamic changes in the transcriptome between ZSD and ZSL

Transcriptomic analysis revealed differential gene expression between ZSD and ZSL pepper fruits (Fig. 1a). Principal component analysis (PCA) of the six biological replicates demonstrated high intra-group reproducibility while confirming significant inter-group transcriptional divergence (Fig. 1b). In the ZSD vs. ZSL comparison, 3,291 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, of which 1,316 were upregulated and 1,975 were downregulated (Table S2). A KEGG analysis of the DEGs revealed that biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, metabolic pathways, photosynthesis, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, carotenoid biosynthesis, and flavonoid biosynthesis genes had been enriched. Further analysis of the DEGs indicated that most of the structural genes involved in the anthocyanins synthesis pathways were downregulated in ZSD fruits. In the anthocyanins biosynthesis pathway, critical structure genes, such as PAL (Capana09g002200), C4H (Capana06g000272), CHS (Capana05g002274), F3H (Capana02g002586), and DFR (Capana02g002763), had significantly higher expressions in ZSL compared to ZSD. Conversely, UFGT (Capana10g001978) and ANS (Capana10g001357) expressions were upregulated in ZSD, which was opposite to the trend observed for most of the structural genes.

Transcriptome analysis of pepper fruits of ZSD and ZSL. a Phenotypes of pepper fruits of ZSD and ZSL; b PCA analysis of transcriptomic data; c Volcano plot of DGEs between ZSD and ZSL; d KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs between ZSD and ZSL

Variations in anthocyanins contents in the ZSD and ZSL fruits

Targeted metabolomics profiling of bagged ZSD and ZSL pepper fruits quantified anthocyanins dynamics. PCA revealed high inter-replicate consistency with significant metabolomic divergence between genotypes (Fig. 2a). KEGG enrichment confirmed differential metabolites primarily mapped to anthocyanins biosynthesis pathways (Fig. 2b). A total of 20 differentially accumulated anthocyanins were identified in the ZSD vs. ZSL comparison (4 upregulated and 16 downregulated). Among these 20 differentially accumulated anthocyanins, only six (naringenin, quercetin-3-O-glucoside, delphinidin-3-O-rutinoside, delphinidin-3-O-(6’’-O-tartaryl) glucoside, delphinidin-3-O-(2’’’-O-p-coumaroyl) rutinoside, and delphinidin-3-O-glucoside) exhibited relatively high contents, all of which accumulated higher in ZSL. The remaining 14 types, although present in differing amounts, all accumulated at low levels. Specifically, pelargonidin-3-O-glucoside, delphinidin-3-O-5-O-(6-O-coumaroyl)-diglucoside, pelargonidin-3-O-sambubioside, and cyanidin-3,5-O-diglucoside were almost not accumulated in ZSL, and only minimal accumulation was observed in ZSD (Table S3). Overall, all detected differentially accumulated anthocyanins metabolites accumulated at low levels in ZSD, indicating that anthocyanins primarily accumulate in pepper fruits under normal light conditions.

Identification of WGCNA modules associated with anthocyanins metabolism

To identify key regulators of anthocyanins metabolism, we constructed weighted gene co-expression networks (WGCNA) correlating DEGs with pigment contents in ZSD/ZSL fruits. These DEGs segregated into 38 expression modules (Fig. 3a). Six anthocyanins compounds showing significant content differences between ZSD and ZSL were selected for module–trait correlation analysis. A heatmap was generated to visualize the concentration of each compound across samples, as well as the correlation coefficients and corresponding p-values between modules and these anthocyanins (Fig. 3b). The heatmap shows that the ‘blue’ module had significantly positive correlations with delphinidin-3-O-glucoside, delphinidin-3-O-rutinoside, naringenin, quercetin-3-O-glucoside, delphinidin-3-O-(6’’-O-tartaryl) glucoside, and delphinidin-3-O-(2’’’-O-p-coumaroyl) rutinoside, with correlation coefficients of 0.98, 0.98, 0.99, 0.98, 0.97 and 0.97, respectively. The ‘blue’ module contained 5806 genes, including lots of genes involved in the anthocyanins synthesis pathway, such as PAL (Capana09g002200, Capana09g002190), C4H (Capana06g000272, Capana06g000273), CHS (Capana05g002274), F3H (Capana02g002586), F3’H (Capana03g000892), F3’5’H (Capana11g000195), and DFR (Capana02g002763). Transcription factors, such as MYB306, MYB1, MYB4, bHLH81, and WRKY44, were identified, and their expression showed they were potentially involved in anthocyanins biosynthesis (Fig. 3c). A co-expression analysis of these transcription factors with the structural genes revealed strong correlations among them. In particular, WRKY44 was highlighted as a crucial regulator of anthocyanins synthesis and it was subsequently designated as CaWRKY44.

Characterization of CaWRKY44

A WRKY transcription factor, CaWRKY44, was prioritized from the transcriptome data based on its strong association with anthocyanins accumulation. A subcellular localization analysis of CaWRKY44 (Fig. 4a), green fluorescence was detected across the cytoplasm, nucleus, and plasma membrane of tobacco cells. By contrast, the pCaWRKY44-Cambia1300-GFP and mCherry fusion protein fluorescences were predominantly localized in the nucleus, indicating that CaWRKY44 is a nuclear-localized protein. CaWRKY44 expression in pepper seeds, placenta, and pericarp was analyzed at 10–60 DPA and the results showed that it was present in all the tissues and that its expression was highest in the pericarp (Fig. 4b). The CaWRKY44 expression levels in the pericarp initially increased, then decreased, and reached a peak at 30 DPA. The transcriptional activation activity of CaWRKY44 was analyzed using the yeast two-hybrid system (Fig. 4c). The positive control, negative control, and pGBKT7::CaWRKY44 grew well on SD/-Trp deficient medium, indicating that the plasmids had been successfully transformed into yeast. However, only the positive control exhibited normal growth and turned blue on SD/-Trp/-His/-Ade/X-α-Gal deficient medium, whereas the negative control and pGBKT7::CaWRKY44 failed to grow properly. These results demonstrated that CaWRKY44 lacks transcriptional autoactivation activity. To further verify the CaWRKY44 transcriptional activation activity, its regulatory function on downstream reporter genes was analyzed through transient expression in N. benthamiana leaves. After co-injecting Reporter and Efector into tobacco leaves, the regulatory effect of Efector on Reporter was determined based on the results from the in vivo fluorescence imaging system and luciferase activity assays. The results showed that the fluorescence activity of the experimental group was significantly weaker than that of the control group (Fig. 4d). The LUC/REN ratio in the experimental group decreased 3.4-fold and was notably lower than that of the negative control (Fig. 4e). This indicated that CaWRKY44-GAL can bind to the USA sequence to inhibit the expression of downstream fluorescent genes.

Transient overexpression of CaWRKY44 increases anthocyanins accumulation in pepper pericarps

An overexpression vector, pCambia1301 (p1301)::CaWRKY44, was constructed and transiently transformed into pepper fruits via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. At 7 DPA, the needle-injected area of the positive fruits had a purple color (Fig. 5a). To elucidate the mechanism underlying the color change, RT-qPCR was conducted to quantify the expression of CaWRKY44 and key anthocyanins biosynthetic genes. The results revealed a significant upregulation of these genes in the treatment group compared to the control (Fig. 5b). Notably, the expression of structural genes, including CaPAL, Ca4CL, CaF3H, CaDFR, and CaANS, was markedly elevated in the peel of positively transformed fruits relative to the empty vector control (Fig. 5c). These findings suggested that CaWRKY44 may promote anthocyanins biosynthesis by upregulating the expression of these key genes.

CaWRKY44 silencing reduces anthocyanins accumulation in pepper

The PTRV2::CaWRKY44 silencing vector was constructed and used to infect pepper fruits. The injected areas of the fruits had not accumulated anthocyanins after 7 days incubation (Fig. 6a). A fluorescent quantitative PCR analysis revealed that the expression level of the CaWRKY44 in the positive fruits was lower than that in the control group (Fig. 6b). Furthermore, the anthocyanins contents (Fig. 6c) and the structural genes expression, such as Ca4CL, CaDFR, and CaPAL, were markedly reduced in the peel of positively transformed fruits (Fig. 6d). Collectively, the findings suggest that CaWRKY44 likely functions by transcriptionally regulating key structural genes within the anthocyanins biosynthetic pathway.

CaWRKY44 activates the CaDFR promoter

Overexpressing or silencing CaWRKY44 resulted in significant upregulation or downregulation, respectively, of structural genes related to anthocyanins biosynthesis compared to the control. A yeast one-hybrid assay was used to validate this result and investigate the regulatory effect of CaWRKY44 on downstream genes. The vectors PB42AD and PB42AD::CaWRKY44 were separately introduced into yeast cells containing different anthocyanins structural gene promoters and the results showed that the positive control yeast colonies turned blue, which indicated that the system was functioning correctly. In contrast to co-transformation with the empty PB42AD vector, yeast colonies co-transformed with PB42AD::CaWRKY44 and CaDFR developed a blue coloration, which showed that there was a physical interaction between the transcription factor CaWRKY44 and the CaDFR gene promoter (Fig. 7a). It was hypothesized that the W-box (TGAC) might serve as the binding site for CaWRKY44; therefore, the specific elements bound by CaWRKY44 to the CaDFR promoter of the anthocyanins synthesis gene were investigated. Biotin-labeled normal and mutated probes were designed by introducing mutations into the TGAC motif within approximately 2000 bp upstream of the CaDFR promoter. The EMSA results (Fig. 7b) revealed that there was a band shift when CaWRKY44 was incubated with the wild-type CaDFR promoter probe, which indicated that there was a protein-DNA interaction. By contrast, no band shift was detected when CaWRKY44 was incubated with the mutated probe, which confirmed that CaWRKY44 specifically binds to the W-box (TGAC) element. A dual luciferase assay was performed using N. benthamiana leaves to further validate the results. The CaDFR promoter sequence was cloned into the pGreenII 0800-LUC vector and the CaWRKY44 gene was inserted into the pGreenII 62-SK vector. These constructs were then co-infiltrated into tobacco leaves (Fig. 7c). The leaves were examined after 3 days using an in vivo fluorescence imaging system and by measuring the luciferase activities. The results demonstrated that CaWRKY44 enhanced the CaDFR promoter ability to drive reporter gene expression, which led to increased fluorescence signals in the experimental area of the tobacco leaves (Fig. 7d). The significantly elevated LUC/REN ratio, as revealed by enzyme activity assays, provides direct evidence that CaWRKY44 binds to the CaDFR promoter.

Discussion

Pepper fruits are rich in secondary metabolites, including capsaicinoids, carotenoids, vitamin C, vitamin E, and anthocyanins [31]. The synthesis of secondary metabolites is affected by environmental conditions, among which light signaling is one of the most critical environmental factors influencing the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites. Elucidating the regulatory mechanisms controlling carotenoid and anthocyanins synthesis networks in pepper could lead to improvements in pepper quality. In this study, a bagging treatment was applied to purple peppers and it significantly reduced the anthocyanins contents in the pericarp and the fruits became notably lighter. Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses were used to examine the changes in gene expression, anthocyanins accumulation profiles between ZSD and ZSL pepper fruits. The major differentially accumulated anthocyanins in ZSD and ZSL pepper fruits were delphinidin derivatives (delphinidin-3-O-rutinoside, delphinidin-3-O-(6’’-O-tartaryl) glucoside, delphinidin-3-O-glucoside, and delphinidin-3-O-(2’’’-O-p-coumaroyl) rutinoside) and flavonoids (quercetin-3-O-glucoside and naringenin). Previous studies have shown that delphinidin is the main coloring substance in purple pepper fruits [32]. In this study, the ZSL fruits were purple and had significantly greater delphinidin derivative levels (including delphinidin-3-O-rutinoside, delphinidin-3-O-(6’’-O-tartaryl) glucoside, delphinidin-3-O-glucoside, and delphinidin-3-O-(2’’’-O-p-coumaroyl) rutinoside), whereas the ZSD fruits showed minimal accumulation. This demonstrated that these anthocyanins compounds were the primary contributors to anthocyanins accumulation under light conditions and the key factors responsible for the purplish coloration of the fruits.

In this experiment, transcriptome analysis of bagged peppers and those under normal light revealed numerous light-related differentially expressed genes, including HY5 (Capana08g000273), PIF4 (Capana09g001161), COP1 (Capana12g002818), UVR3 (Capana03g001755), MYB1 (Capana10g001433), which may be involved in light response and anthocyanins synthesis. The HY5-MIR858a-MYBL2 regulatory loop reduces MYBL2 protein content through transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation, thereby enhancing the stability of the MBW complex and ultimately modulating light-induced anthocyanins synthesis [33]. Although PpHY5 in pear interacts with PpMYB10, it cannot activate transcription alone. Instead, it must form a heterodimeric complex with BBX16 to regulate PpMYB10 expression, thereby modulating anthocyanins biosynthesis [34]. In both apple and Arabidopsis, COP1 can directly interact with MYB transcription factors that regulate anthocyanins synthesis. Under dark conditions, AtCOP1 in Arabidopsis and MdCOP1 in apple directly interact with anthocyanins promoting transcription factors such as PAP1 and PAP2, as well as MdMYB1, respectively. Through the 26 S proteasome system, they promote the ubiquitination and degradation of these transcription factors, thereby suppressing anthocyanins biosynthesis [35, 36]. However, further research is still required to understand how the aforementioned genes participate in regulating anthocyanins biosynthesis in pepper, as well as their relationship with WRKY44 in the regulation of pepper anthocyanins synthesis.

A WRKY transcription factor, CaWRKY44, was identified by the integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis and was analyzed further to investigate the synthesis and regulatory mechanisms controlling anthocyanins in pepper fruits. CaWRKY44 was highly co-expressed with structural genes involved in the anthocyanins biosynthetic pathway. The CaWRKY44 expression level in the pericarp increased during the early developmental stages and decreased during the color transition period, which was consistent with the anthocyanins accumulation pattern and suggested that it was potentially involved in anthocyanins accumulation in pepper fruits. Light treatment and co-expression studies have demonstrated the involvement of CHS, DFR, CHI, EGL1, two MATE transporters, and two WRKY44 genes in anthocyanins biosynthesis [32]. Promoter analysis revealed multiple light-responsive elements in CaWRKY44, suggesting it functions as a rapid-response transcription factor downstream of light signaling to regulate anthocyanins biosynthesis. This role is consistent with its nuclear localization, indicating a primary function in modulating target gene transcription. The yeast autoactivation assay showed that CaWRKY44 had no self-activation activity, which suggests that it may coordinate anthocyanins synthesis through interactions with other proteins. In grapes, VvWRKY5 promotes jasmonic acid biosynthesis by interacting with VvMYBA1 and by positively regulating wound-induced anthocyanins accumulation in fruits [37]. Future studies could use the yeast two-hybrid system to further screen for proteins interacting with CaWRKY44, and to explore the regulatory network controlling transcription factor effects on the structural genes in pepper anthocyanins synthesis.

Overexpression of CaWRKY44 significantly upregulated its gene expression and that of the structural genes involved in the anthocyanins biosynthesis pathway, including CaPAL, Ca4CL, CaC4H, CaF3’5’H, CaF3H, CaDFR, and CaANS. Additionally, the anthocyanins contents in CaWRKY44-overexpressing pepper fruits were substantially higher than that in the control group. CaWRKY44 silencing reduced anthocyanins synthesis by downregulating pathway genes, underscoring the conserved function of WRKY factors; a parallel mechanism is observed in pear, where PpWRKY44 upregulates PpMYB10 under light to promote anthocyanins accumulation [38]; in eggplant, SmWRKY44 controls the anthocyanins accumulation by regulating the transcription of key genes in anthocyanins biosynthesis pathway [39]; and in Arabidopsis thaliana, overexpression of AtWRKY41 significantly increases the expression of AtMYB75 and AtMYB111 in leaves, thereby activating the anthocyanins biosynthesis [40]. Transient overexpression of VvWRKY5 in grapes resulted in significantly higher expression levels for VvDFR, VvCHS, and VvUFGT, thereby promoting a significant increase in anthocyanins at wound sites [37]. The results showed that CaWRKY44 plays a role in anthocyanins biosynthesis in pepper. Furthermore, this transcription factor may promote pericarp coloration by upregulating the expression of key structural genes in the anthocyanins biosynthetic pathway. DcWRKY15 positively regulates carnation coloration by binding to the DcCHS and DcF3H promoter [41]. In tea plants, CsWRKY31 and CsWRKY48 directly bind to the W-box [(C/T)TGAC(T/C)] elements within the promoters of CsLAR, CsDFR, and CsAOMT to suppress the activity of associated secondary metabolites [42]. The Y1H, EMSA, and LUC assays showed that CaWRKY44 can directly bind to the W-box (TGAC) element in the promoter region of the anthocyanins pathway structural gene CaDFR, which significantly activated its transcriptional activity and positively regulated the biosynthesis of anthocyanins in pepper fruits. The regulatory network mechanism controlling anthocyanins in pepper fruits is extremely complex, is influenced by multiple pathways, and involves multiple layers, including transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation. Future research should focus on exploring the interaction networks mediated by light signaling.

Conclusions

In this study, integrative metabolome and transcriptome analyses of ZSD and ZSL pepper fruits identified 3,291 differentially expressed genes and 20 differentially accumulated anthocyanins. This study demonstrates that CaWRKY44, a nuclear-localized transcription factor, acts as a positive regulator of anthocyanins biosynthesis. Silencing CaWRKY44 attenuated anthocyanins accumulation, while its transient overexpression enhanced pigmentation. Furthermore, Y1H, EMSA, and LUC assays confirmed that CaWRKY44 directly binds to W-box cis-elements in the CaDFR promoter, thereby activating its expression. Future research should focus on the environmental regulation of CaWRKY44 and the development of stable transformation systems for practical application.
