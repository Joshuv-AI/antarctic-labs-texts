Comments on: “Elevated Serum Amyloid A2 and A4 in Patients With Guillain–Barré Syndrome”
Authors: Josef Finsterer
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We were interested to read the article by Yao et al.1 describing a retrospective, controlled study investigating whether serum amyloid A (AA) subtypes AA1, AA2, and AA4 can serve as biomarkers of disease severity in patients with the demyelinating form of Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS). Although serum levels of AA2 and AA4 were elevated in GBS patients relative to healthy and diseased controls, they were not correlated with GBS disease severity as assessed by the GBS Disease Score (GBS-DS) or the Medical Research Council sum score.1 The study is noteworthy, but several points should be considered, as discussed below.

First, the blood sampling was not performed on the same day as the GBS-DS determination: the GBS-DS was collected on admission, whereas the blood samples were taken between diagnosis and the start of treatment.1 Therefore, if treatment was not started until a few days after admission, the AA values may not reflect the severity of the disease at admission. Since GBS is usually a progressive disease that worsens from onset until a nadir is reached a few days or even weeks after onset, the GBS-DS may have been worse at the time of blood sampling than at admission.

Second, the control group was defined as “other neurological disorders,” but 15 patients in this group had tension-type headache, 7 had “somatic symptoms and related disorders,” and 13 had peripheral vertigo. Only those with tension-type headache actually had a neurological disorder, and so the others should have been excluded from the control group. Nevertheless, it is surprising that AA2 and AA4 were elevated in this group relative to healthy controls, although the central or peripheral nervous system is hardly affected in these patients.

Third, only 23 out of 39 patients who underwent serum testing had a previous infection. What were the triggers of GBS in the remaining 16 patients? Were they vaccinated, did they have a history of previous surgery, trauma, or lymphoma, or was the infection subclinical in these patients? Were the AA1, AA2, and AA4 levels higher in the patients with a previous infection than in those without?

Fourth, since it is conceivable for AA levels to be elevated not only under pathological conditions with inflammatory stimuli but also due to medication, it is essential to include current medications in assessments of whether or not AA can truly serve as a biomarker of immune response. The conclusion of the index study that AA cannot serve as a biomarker of GBS disease severity might be at least partly attributable to the current medications of individual patients not being included in the analysis. Similarly, all comorbidities—especially inflammatory diseases—should have been included in the analysis, since immune-mediated disorders and inflammatory diseases in particular may be associated with elevated AA levels.2

Fifth, it is incomprehensible why not only GBS patients with the demyelinating subtype but also those with the axonal subtype were included in the study. Since AA is predominantly produced in Schwann cells, it is to be expected that AA is particularly high in demyelinating diseases, but not in diseases involving the degeneration of neurons.

Sixth, serum AA levels are correlated with body mass index (BMI),3 and so the lack of a correlation between AA levels and GBS-DS could be due to the BMI being omitted from the analysis.

In summary, conclusions about the potential use of serum AA as a biomarker of disease severity in GBS patients can only be drawn once all factors influencing serum AA levels are included in the type of analysis reported by Yao et al.1
