Anti-fibrotic effects of evogliptin in fibroblasts derived from post-burn hypertrophic scars
Authors: Youn Kyung Kee, June-Bum Kim, Ya Xin Zheng, Hui Song Cui, Yoon Soo Cho, So Young Joo, Yu Mi Ro, In Suk Kwak, Dong Hyun Kim, Cheong Hoon Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypertrophic scar (HTS) frequently occurs after burn injury, characterized by persistent fibroblast activation, overexpression of α-smooth muscle actin (αSMA) and extracellular matrix (ECM) components. Complete scarless regeneration remains incompletely understood. Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP4) is a cell-surface serine protease, its inhibitors (DPP4i) have been associated with fibrosis. However, the functional role of evogliptin in HTS formation remains unclear. This investigated its effects on fibrosis markers in hypertrophic scar fibroblasts (HTSFs). Human normal fibroblasts (HNFs) and HTSFs were isolated from normal skin and post-burn HTS tissues. DPP4 expression in HTS and normal skin tissues was detected using immunocytochemistry. mRNA and protein expression levels were determined via quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction and western blot, respectively. Evogliptin treatment in HTSFs and HNFs treated with transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β) both inhibited the expression of differentiation markers, including αSMA, TGF-β1, YAP1, TEAD1, and CTGF, thereby reducing fibroblast activation; ECM components, including type Ⅰ collagen, type Ⅲ collagen, and fibronectin; epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers, including snail1, slug, twist1, vimentin, and n-cadherin; and TGF-β-mediated signaling molecules, including SMAD2, SMAD3, TAK1, ERK, p38, JNK, AKT, and STAT3. Our results demonstrated that evogliptin exerts its anti-fibrotic activity by inhibiting fibroblast activation, EMT, and ECM synthesis in HTSFs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57929-w.Keywords: Evogliptin, Post-burn hypertrophic scar, Fibroblast, SMAD, MAPKSubject terms: Cell biology, Diseases, Medical research, Molecular biology

Introduction

Burn injuries constitute a form of acute trauma characterized by extensive and multifaceted complications. During rehabilitation, the high demands of patients are particularly pronounced across several domains, including physiological, psychosocial, health-related behavioral, and environmental needs1. Scar formation, particularly hypertrophic scarring (HTS), is the primary cause of these complications. The ideal outcome of wound healing is complete scarless regeneration; however, no pharmacological agent is currently available to achieve this outcome. Instead, only surgical interventions and limited adjunctive therapies aim to minimize HTS formation as much as possible2. Most post-burn wounds are superficial second-degree wounds that heal with inconspicuous, non-hypertrophic scars. Deep second- and third-degree burns, which damage the dermis, are associated with a significantly higher risk of HTS formation2. According to single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis of wound healing, fibroblasts exhibit the most prominent gene regulatory activity and serve as the primary drivers of scar formation3. Fibroblasts directionally proliferate and migrate to the wound site, where they express the differentiation marker α-smooth muscle actin (αSMA) to generate contractile forces that minimize wound surface area. Concurrently, they synthesize and remodel extracellular matrix (ECM) to facilitate tissue repair4. In HTS formation, fibroblasts undergo persistent activation and overexpress αSMA and ECM components5. Activated fibroblasts and myofibroblasts are phenotypic manifestations that are reversible under specific circumstances6. Previous studies have suggested that targeting activated fibroblasts to induce their transition to a quiescent state may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for HTS7.

Transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1) is widely recognized as the most potent factor in fibroblast activation and the most extensively studied8. TGF-β signaling cascades remain persistently activated in fibrotic tissues, encompassing both the canonical Sma- and Mad-related protein (SMAD)-dependent pathway, involving activation of SMAD2 and SMAD3, and SMAD-independent signaling, involving activation of transforming growth factor-β-activated kinase 1 (TAK1) and downstream mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), including Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), and p38 MAPK8,9. AKT and STAT3 are downstream effectors of TGF-β1 signaling pathways9,10. In hypertrophic scar fibroblasts (HTSFs), TGF-β1 exhibits markedly elevated activity, with mRNA expression levels sixfold higher than those in human normal fibroblasts (HNFs)11. Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1) is essential for fibroblast migration and differentiation4. When YAP1 is knocked down by siRNA or inhibited pharmacologically, fibroblast activation is effectively blocked12. Additionally, YAP1 and TGF-β1 interact synergistically to regulate fibroblast differentiation13. TGF-β1 can activate YAP1 through the SMAD2/3 pathway and MAPKs, and inhibition of its receptor suppresses YAP1 transcription14. YAP1 also promotes SMAD1 and SMAD3 expression15, and its inhibition may reduce SMAD2/3 expression and suppress TGF-β-induced fibroblast differentiation16. Previous studies have demonstrated that inhibition of YAP1 suppressed TGF-β1-induced activation of fibroblasts, leading to a significant reduction in scar thickness, total collagen deposition, and the type Ⅰ/Ⅲ collagen ratio, ultimately promoting scar-free skin formation17.

Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP4), also known as CD26, is a cell-surface serine protease whose inhibitors (DPP4i, liptins) have been used to treat diabetes18. To date, studies have confirmed that DPP4i affects wound healing and fibrosis, but these investigations have primarily focused on physiological scar formation19. For example, in the murine scar model, treatment with diprotin A reduced scar size and slowed wound healing compared to the control group18. Research on DPP4i for pathological HTS remains extremely limited, with only two diabetic-related studies: in vitro, linagliptin-mediated anti-fibrotic effects in high-glucose-induced HTSFs20; in vivo, sitagliptin treatment reduced the incidence of HTS and keloids in patients with diabetes21. In our experiment, evogliptin was selected, whose effect on HTS formation remains unclear. Evogliptin is a potent DPP4i whose inhibitory effect on enzyme activity increases in a dose-dependent manner. Following oral administration, inhibition of DPP4 activity exceeds 80% and is sustained for over 24 h22. In vitro experiments demonstrated that evogliptin exhibits a stronger inhibitory effect on DPP4 enzyme activity than sitagliptin23. The anti-fibrotic effects of evogliptin have been demonstrated in various tissues. In murine models of cardiac and renal fibrosis, evogliptin treatment significantly suppressed TGF-β1 and collagen expression24,25.

Our study aimed to evaluate the therapeutic potential of evogliptin as an add-on to HTSFs in non-diabetic individuals. In this context, we analyzed the changes in the expression of fibrosis- and differentiation-related markers in post-burn HTSFs treated with evogliptin. Furthermore, we investigated both SMAD-dependent and SMAD-independent signaling pathways underlying the antifibrotic effects of evogliptin.

Results

Reduced expression of DPP4 in the dermis of HTS tissue

According to our experiments, DPP4 expression was detected through both IHC staining (P < 0.01, Fig. 1a–c) and western blot analysis (P < 0.01, Fig. 1d), which revealed a significant reduction in the dermis of HTS tissue compared to the dermis of normal skin tissue. Additionally, DPP4 expression was significantly reduced at the mRNA level in HTSFs compared to HNFs (P < 0.01; Fig. 1e), which were isolated separately from HTS and normal tissues. When treated with TGF-β1, HNFs showed further reduction in DPP4 mRNA and protein expression (P < 0.01, Fig. 2c, d).

To evaluate the function of the DPP4 inhibitor, HTSFs and HNFs were treated for 2 days before functional assays. Firstly, to exclude the effects of cytotoxicity, we confirmed that treatment of 50, 100, and 150 µM evogliptin had no impact on cell viability (Fig. 3a). Furthermore, the protein expression of cytochrome C, Bid, and Bax, which act as apoptosis markers, was unaffected by evogliptin treatment (Fig. 3b–d).

Evogliptin treatment showed no statistically significant difference on cell proliferation and apoptosis in HTSFs and HNFs treated with TGF-β1. The activity of DPP4 was significantly inhibited by treatment with 50, 100, and 150 µM evogliptin compared with the untreated control group (P < 0.01, Fig. 1e). However, treatment with evogliptin at concentrations of 50, 100, and 150 µM significantly increased both mRNA and protein expression in HTSFs compared with the untreated control group (P < 0.01, Fig. 2a, b). In addition, DPP4 activity was markedly decreased by TGF-β1 treatment in HNFs, and evogliptin further reduced DPP4 activity in the TGF-β1-treated group (P < 0.01, Fig. 1f). Conversely, mRNA and protein expression were decreased in the TGF-β1-treated group compared with the control group, whereas evogliptin treatment at 50, 100, and 150 µM significantly increased mRNA and protein expression compared with the TGF-β1-treated group (P < 0.01, Fig. 2c, d).

Evogliptin inhibits the expression of differentiation markers and ECM components in HTSFs

In HTSFs, treatment with evogliptin at concentrations of 50, 100, and 150 µM for two days resulted in a significant decrease in TGF-β1 protein levels (P < 0.01, Fig. 4a). Additionally, evogliptin treatment significantly reduced the protein expression of YAP1 at 100 and 150 µM (P < 0.01, Fig. 4b), and the mRNA and protein expression of αSMA at 50, 100, and 150 µM (P < 0.01, Fig. 5a, b), both of which are activated by TGF-β1 and serve as differentiation markers of fibroblasts13. As downstream targets of YAP1 26, evogliptin treatment significantly reduced the protein expression of TEA domain (TEAD) and connective tissue growth factor (CTGF) at 150 µM (P < 0.01, Fig. 4c, d) and mRNA expression of CTGF at 50, 100, and 150 µM (P < 0.01, Fig. 4e) compared to the untreated group. mRNA and protein expression of ECM components, including collagen Ⅰ (P < 0.01, Fig. 5c, d), collagen Ⅲ (P < 0.01, Fig. 5e, f), and fibronectin (P < 0.01, Fig. 5g, h) showed significant decreases under evogliptin treatment at 100 and 150 µM compared to the untreated group.

These results indicated that the fibrotic characteristics of HTSFs were significantly suppressed following evogliptin treatment.

Evogliptin inhibits the expression of EMT markers in HTSFs

scRNA-Seq analysis revealed that HTSFs were enriched in transcripts associated with ECM organization, collagen formation, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)27. Therefore, based on our previous studies, we concluded that HTSFs exhibit an EMT phenotype, which indicates enhanced migratory capacity compared to HNFs28.

As representative EMT markers, mesenchymal markers showed significantly decreased mRNA and protein expression under evogliptin treatment. Compared with the untreated group, vimentin mRNA and protein levels were reduced at 50, 100, and 150 µM (P < 0.01, Fig. 6a, d). Corresponding reductions in n-cadherin mRNA and protein levels were observed at 100 and 150 µM (P < 0.01, Fig. 6b, e). Correspondingly, epidermal marker e-cadherin showed significant increases in mRNA and protein expression under evogliptin treatment at 50, 100, and 150 µM (P < 0.01; Fig. 6c, f) compared to the untreated group. Moreover, EMT transcription factors (EMT-TFs) were significantly decreased under evogliptin treatment compared to the untreated group, including decreased twist1 observed at 50, 100, and 150 µM (P < 0.01; Fig. 6g), decreased snail1 (P < 0.01, Fig. 6h), and slug (P < 0.01, Fig. 6i) observed at 100 and 150 µM.

Meanwhile, e-cadherin (CDH1) (c,f) was significantly induced in HTSFs compared to the untreated group. Fold changes were set to 1.0 for untreated HTSFs. **P < 0.01, vs. Untreated group. These results indicated that the migratory capacity of HTSFs was significantly suppressed following evogliptin treatment.

We treated HNFs with TGF-β1 to model the early phase of HTS formation and investigate associated signaling pathways. The results below indicate that evogliptin also exhibited significant anti-fibrotic activity in this model.

Similarly, protein expression of YAP1 at 50 and 150 µM (P < 0.01, Fig. 7a), TEAD1 at 150 µM (P < 0.01, Fig. 7b), and CTGF at 150 µM (P < 0.01, Fig. 7c) were downregulated in HNFs induced by TGF-β1 treatment.

Evogliptin inhibits the expression of SMAD-dependent and SMAD-independent signaling pathways in HNFs treated with TGF-β1

In HNFs, following evogliptin treatment at gradient concentrations, TGF-β1 treatment was set for 1 h to assess the expression levels of signaling pathway proteins. Upon TGF-β stimulation, our results showed significantly elevated phosphorylation levels of all target markers, including SMAD2, SMAD3, TAK1, ERK, p38, JNK, AKT, and STAT3.

In the TGF-β/SMAD-dependent signaling pathway, phosphorylated SMAD3 protein expression was significantly reduced under evogliptin treatment at concentrations of 100 and 150 µM compared to the TGF-β1-treated group (P < 0.01, Fig. 9a, b). However, phosphorylated SMAD2 protein expression did not show a statistically significant difference from that in the TGF-β1-treated group (data not shown). In the TGF-β/SMAD-independent signaling pathway, evogliptin treatment reduced the phosphorylated protein expression of AKT serine/threonine kinase (AKT) (P < 0.01, Fig. 9a, d) and p38 (P < 0.01, Fig. 9a, f) at concentrations of 50 µM; reduced JNK (P < 0.01, Fig. 9a, e), ERK2 (P < 0.01; Fig. 9a, h), and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) (P < 0.01, Fig. 9a, i) at concentrations of 50 and 100 µM; reduced ERK1 (P < 0.01, Fig. 9a, g) at concentrations of 50, 100, and 150 µM compared to the TGF-β1-treated group. Conversely, compared to the TGF-β1-treated group, evogliptin treatment significantly increased the phosphorylated protein expression of STAT3 (P < 0.01, Fig. 9a, h) at 150 µM, whereas phosphorylated TAK1 increased at 100 and 150 µM (P < 0.01, Fig. 9a, c).

Furthermore, treatment with TAK1 inhibitor (5Z)-7-Oxozeaenol resulted in significant decreases in phosphorylation levels of the downstream effector MAPKs, including JNK (P < 0.01, Fig. 9a, e), p38 (P < 0.01, Fig. 9a, f), ERK1 (P < 0.01, Fig. 9a, g), and ERK2 (P < 0.01, Fig. 9a, h) compared to the TGF-β1-treated group. These findings directly demonstrated that the inhibitor effectively suppressed the activation of these signaling pathways. However, the primary target, TAK1 (P < 0.01, Fig. 9a, c), showed significant upregulation in protein expression, contrasting with the suppressed signaling cascade. We hypothesize that this observation may result from negative feedback or a compensatory signaling pathway via the downstream factor STAT3, which also showed upregulation in our results (P < 0.01, Fig. 9a, i). Likewise, in melanoma, it has been previously reported that STAT3 could mediate activations of MAPKs and AKT through feedback, which could be inhibited by a STAT3 inhibitor29.

Discussion

DPP4 is a prototypical cell surface peptidase that functions as both an enzymatic catalyst and a receptor-binding protein. Our results showed that DPP4 protein expression and immunostaining intensity were significantly decreased in the dermis of HTS tissue compared with normal skin tissue. Given the elevated TGF-β1 expression in HTSFs compared to HNFs28, we observed that TGF-β1 treatment reduced DPP4 expression in HNFs. It has been shown that normal human skin contains a substantial population of DPP4 + fibroblasts30. In keloid tissue and keloid-derived fibroblasts (KFs), DPP4 was downregulated while TGF-β1 was upregulated; in mature scar and normal skin, DPP4 was highly expressed while TGF-β1 was reduced31. In T cells, exposure to TGF-β1 reduced DPP4 levels, whereas DPP4i induced TGF-β1 secretion32,33. In cancer cells, DPP4 knockdown reduced TGF-β1 expression, whereas TGF-β1 treatment suppressed DPP4 expression34,35. However, most studies generally accepted that DPP4 + fibroblasts were significantly more abundant in both the dermis and the epidermis of hypertrophic scars than in normal skin3,36. One hypothesis for this opposing expression may be the unavoidable post-burn care, such as long-term anti-inflammatory and steroid regimens, which may affect the expression landscape. Another hypothesis may be that the opposing expression are attributable to the distinct anatomical locations of obtained skin samples. Previous studies have demonstrated that human fibroblasts derived from different anatomic sites exhibit marked diversity in gene expression37. According to some studies, these differences were not dictated by the local microenvironment, but by cell‑intrinsic characteristics — fibroblast heterogeneity, which could affect dermal structure and wound healing18. It has also showed that DPP4 expression selectively marked most fibrosis‑promoting fibroblast lineages18.

In our experiment, following evogliptin treatment in both HTSFs and HNFs, it observed a significant decrease in DPP4 enzymatic activity. Paradoxically, it was accompanied by a concurrent increase in both protein and mRNA expression of DPP4. An inverse correlation between expression levels and inhibitory activity has been reported in other studies. In vitro, treatment of HNFs with other DPP4i (diprotin A and sitagliptin) for 24 h did not alter DPP4 expression30. In vivo, under sitagliptin treatment, mRNA expression of DPP4 was upregulated in the bone marrow, liver, and spleen, but unchanged or downregulated in the ventricles, kidneys, and pancreas38. Furthermore, DPP4i—including sitagliptin, linagliptin, and MK-0626—could increase the circulating levels of soluble DPP4 (sDPP4) in vivo39. Sitagliptin treatment induced excessive shedding of mDPP4 and delays its degradation, leading to increased levels of sDPP440. Under basal conditions, sDPP4 is released at low levels. Under external stimulation, it may lead to increased shedding of mDPP4, resulting in the accumulation of sDPP4, or decreased shedding of mDPP4, resulting in its accumulation on the cell surface41,42. In summary, we attributed the observed increase in mRNA and protein expression to the compensatory response.

DPP4 is known to interact with fibronectin, collagen Ⅰ, and collagen Ⅲ, which is mediated by amino acid residues independent of the catalytically active site42,43. Following separate injection of DPP4 + and DPP4- fibroblasts into the dorsal regions of mice, substantial deposition of labeled ECM was observed in DPP4 + grafts, whereas minimal to no ECM accumulation was detected in DPP4- grafts18. In wound healing, DPP4 knockdown has been shown to significantly downregulate the gene and protein expression of α-SMA, TGF-β1, and ECM components3,30. Researchers proposed that, via its endopeptidase activity, DPP4 cleaves the terminal end of fibronectin to promote scaffold formation and facilitate excessive ECM accumulation41,42,44. Therefore, most DPP4i demonstrated anti-fibrotic properties22. In HNFs, sitagliptin treatment reduced the expression of α-SMA, fibronectin, and type Ⅰ collagen, and decreased total collagen deposition, but failed to suppress collagen contractions that were observed in DPP4-knockdown fibroblasts3. In HTSFs under high glucose conditions, linagliptin reduced fibroblast migration and differentiation and suppressed ECM accumulation20.

In our study, evogliptin treatment reduced the expression of ECM components (including type Ⅰ collagen, type Ⅲ collagen, and fibronectin in HTSFs) and differentiation markers (including αSMA, TGF-β1, YAP1, TEAD1, and CTGF), thereby reducing fibroblast activation. In the liver, evogliptin exerted a suppressive effect on TGF-β-driven CTGF expression and cell differentiation45. During HTS formation, YAP remains in a non-phosphorylated conformation and translocates to the nucleus, where it interacts with TEAD as a potent transcriptional coactivator, directly engaging CTGF promoter to promote its expression26,46. Treatment with the YAP inhibitor suppressed the proliferation and collagen production of HTSFs and impeded their migratory capacity47.

EMT stands as a hallmark event in fibrosis. EMT can be induced by TGF-β1 and YAP1/TEAD1, thereby enhancing cell migration and invasion48. During cancer progression, such as prostate cancer, EMT facilitates the progression toward a more aggressive state48. We have previously identified the EMT phenotype of HTSFs as a key therapeutic target for HTS28,49. In our study, evogliptin significantly reduced the expression of EMT-TFs—including snail1, slug, and twist1, and mesenchymal markers vimentin and n-cadherin—while increasing the expression of epithelial marker e-cadherin.

DPP4 itself has been recognized as a promoter of cell adhesion and invasion41,42. A higher tumor stage is associated with increased malignancy, including invasive and metastatic capabilities, as well as a higher DPP4 expression35,50. DPP4 knockdown downregulated the expression of Snail and N-cadherin50; DPP4 overexpression induced EMT via TGF-β1 and upregulated the expression of e-cadherin51. This function is regulated through ECM modulation and is partially modulated by DPP4i3,42. Previous studies reported that sitagliptin did not affect EMT in thyroid cancer cells35, whereas evogliptin inhibited cell migration in liver cells and human umbilical vein endothelial cells45,52.

DPP4 knockdown has been shown to inhibit the TGF-β1 signaling pathway in cancer cells34,35. Treatment with Sitagliptin and Linagliptin may impede cancer progression, skin inflammation, and psoriasis by inhibiting the activation of the JNK, ERK, p38, and AKT signaling pathways50,53. However, in HNFs, sitagliptin treatment can’t significantly alter the phosphorylation levels of SMAD3 and ERK; moreover, proteomic analysis revealed no inhibition of any signaling molecules3,30. In our results regarding SMAD signaling, evogliptin treatment did not alter phosphorylated SMAD2 levels but reduced phosphorylated SMAD3 levels. In our results regarding non-SMAD signaling, evogliptin treatment induced an atypical biphasic response characterized by an initial suppression followed by an increase. By and large, the downward phases of these fibrotic signals exhibited drug-concentration-dependent responses, consistent with the objective of our study and demonstrating that evogliptin inhibited the TGF-β1 signaling pathways. Under evogliptin treatment, experiments in the liver and kidneys have demonstrated inhibition of the TGF-β/SMAD3 signaling pathway, leading to the suppression of fibrosis25,45.

On the other hand, the abnormally upregulated expressions were most prominent in TAK1, located at the upstream initiation site, and in STAT3, located at the downstream terminus site. The promoter of the human DPP4 gene is known to contain binding sites, including activator protein 1 (AP-1), epidermal growth factor receptor-transcription factor site, signal transducer and activator of transcription 1α (STAT1α), and nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB)54, that exhibit crosslink with TGF-β signaling pathways in fibroblasts55. The opposite trends in TAK1 and STAT3 upon high-concentration evogliptin were consistent with those upon the TAK1 inhibitor, which we both interpreted as a form of feedback regulation. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that STAT3 knockdown reduced DPP4 expression, whereas STAT3 overexpression enhanced DPP4 expression in pulmonary fibrosis56. So that the feedback may lead to a dose-dependent increase in both protein and mRNA expression of DPP4 across a range of evogliptin concentrations.

Furthermore, our study has several limitations. First, our findings were primarily derived from in vitro experiments using fibroblasts isolated from post-burn HTS, so that the observed anti-fibrotic effects of evogliptin may not fully recapitulate the complex cellular and biomechanical environment of scar tissue in vivo. Second, the number of donor samples were limited, which may affect the generalizability of the results. We cannot exclude the potential influence of inter-individual and anatomical site-specific differences. Third, although we focused on key pathways (SMAD and MAPK) downstream of TGF-β1, the precise molecular targets and potential off-target effects of evogliptin in scar fibroblasts were not comprehensively evaluated. Finally, concrete dose-response relationships, long-term treatment effects, and validation in animal models or clinical studies were beyond the scope of our work. Future studies addressing these issues will be essential to clarify the therapeutic potential of DPP4i for HTS treatment.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Fresh tissues were sectioned into pieces approximately 1 cm³ and fixed in 10% formalin at 4 °C to ensure proper preservation. These tissues then underwent stepwise processing, including dehydration, clearing, and paraffin infiltration, ultimately resulting in 3-µm-thick paraffin sections mounted on glass slides. The sections were dewaxed and quenched for endogenous peroxidase activity, then blocked and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody (DPP4, 1:300, catalog #67138S, Cell Signaling Technology). The following day, the sections were washed three times with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20, incubated with detection reagent and 3,3′-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) from the ImmPRESS™ Staining Polymer Kit (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA), and subsequently stained with hematoxylin (Cancer Diagnostics, Inc., Durham, USA). Finally, the slides were dehydrated and mounted to complete the process. Tissue images were captured using a light microscope (IX 70; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at 10× and 40× magnification. Data visualization was performed using ImageJ (Fiji, version 2.16.0; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), and Average Optical Density (AOD) values were calculated as quantitative outcomes. Normal tissue samples were used as controls to facilitate data normalization.

Fibroblast isolation and culture

Isolation and primary culture of the dermal fibroblasts from human skin tissues were performed as previously described28,49. Fresh tissues were cut into approximately 1 mm³ pieces to facilitate subsequent digestion. The tissues were washed three times with cold Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS; Biowest, Riverside, MO, USA). Subsequently, the tissues were incubated with Dispase II solution (1 unit/mL; Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) at 4 °C for 18 h. The following day, tissues were transferred to cold DPBS, and the epidermis and dermis layers were separated. The dermal portion of tissue was collected and incubated in collagenase type Ⅳ solution (500 units/mL; Gibco) at 37 °C for 1 h. The resulting cell suspension was retained, and fibroblasts were obtained by centrifugation at 1,200 rpm for 5 min, then transferred to T75 flasks (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). Fibroblasts were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Biowest, Riverside, MO, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Biowest), 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (Gibco), and Gentamicin Reagent (Gibco), under a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The cells were characterized by immunohistochemical staining for vimentin expression28,49.

Evogliptin treatment

When fibroblast confluence reached 50–60%, the culture medium was replaced with low-serum (0.05%) medium and maintained for 24 h. After starvation, fibroblasts were treated with evogliptin (Dong-A ST, Seoul, South Korea) at concentrations of 50, 100, and 150 µM dissolved with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The selection of evogliptin concentrations for this study was informed by prior literature focusing on anti-fibrotic effects, such as treatment with 100 µM evogliptin in Kupffer cells45, and treatment with 100–200 µM evogliptin in normal rat kidney fibroblasts25. An equal volume of DMSO was added to the control group.

Group HNFs were treated with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 (Peprotech, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA) following a 1-hour pre-treatment with evogliptin, whereas this step was omitted for group HTSFs. For HNFs, two extra TGF-β1-treated groups were treated with 30 µM LY 2,157,299 (TGF-β/SMAD inhibitor, Selleckchem, Houston, TX, USA) or 50 µM (5Z)-7-Oxozeaenol (TAK1 inhibitor, Cayman, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) to clarify whether TGF-β1 mediates the activation of SMAD and non-SMAD signaling pathways. After treatment with TGF-β1 for 1 h (for l signaling detection) or 24–48 h (for mRNA and protein detection), the cells were harvested using a cell dissociation solution (Cello, WELGENE). Cell images from each group were captured at the 48-hour time point after evogliptin treatment using a light microscope (IX70; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at 10x magnification.

DPP4 activity assay

After harvesting, HNFs and HTSFs from T75 flasks were seeded into 96-well black plate. All subsequent procedures were performed following the manufacturer’s protocol (ab204722; Abcam, Cambridge, UK). These fibroblasts were lysed with sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-free Chaps cell extract buffer (#9852, Cell Signaling Technology). Firstly, a gradient of 7-Amino-4-methylcoumarin (AMC) concentrations was prepared via serial dilution to establish a standard curve. The positive, negative, and background controls were prepared according to the kit protocol. All dilutions were adjusted to a 50 µL volume with assay buffer per well; subsequently, 10 µL of diluted DPP4 substrate was added and mixed. The samples were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Finally, the fluorescence was acquired at Ex/Em = 360/460 nm with Tecan Infinite M200 Pro microplate reader inder (Tecan Group Ltd., Menedorf, Switzerland), and subsequently analyzed through formula. Each unit DPP4 activity equals to the yield of AMC from hydrolyzation of DPP4 substrate.

Western blotting

Harvested cells and tissues were extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with PhosSTOP™ (Roche). The resulting mixture was centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 45 min at 4 °C to obtain the supernatant containing total protein. The samples were then mixed with 5× sample loading buffer (GenScript, Piscataway, NJ, USA) and boiled for 5 min. The resulting samples were stored at -80 °C. Protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and absorbance was measured at 595 nm.

Each sample was loaded with 15 µg of protein for gel electrophoresis, followed by electrotransfer of the separated proteins onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The resulting membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature and subsequently incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The antibodies were diluted to the concentrations listed in Table 3 with 3% skim milk as the diluent. The membranes were washed with 1× TBST (Tris-buffered saline containing Tween-20) and subsequently incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Merck Millipore; 1:2500; goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G [IgG] or goat anti-mouse IgG) at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, protein expression signals were visualized using a chemiluminescent substrate (ATTO, Tokyo, Japan) under a luminescent imaging system (WSE-6100, ATTO). In addition, the membranes could also be stripped using a stripping buffer (TaKaRa) and re-blocked to detect other proteins, such as total protein or the internal reference protein GAPDH. This procedure followed the principle of using primary and secondary antibodies from different host species to minimize interference. The obtained images were analyzed using ImageJ, normalized to GAPDH levels, and the control group was set to 1.0.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was performed in at least four independent replicates. Data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD), given the use of nonparametric statistical tests. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 24.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Differences among multiple groups were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. When significant differences were detected, pairwise comparisons between groups were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test. To control for false discovery rate due to multiple testing, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 or P < 0.01.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our experimental findings demonstrated that evogliptin attenuated the differentiation, EMT phenotype, and ECM synthesis capacity of HTSFs via both TGF-β1 SMAD-dependent and SMAD-independent signaling pathways. These effects collectively reduced fibrosis and supported the potential of evogliptin as an effective therapeutic agent for HTS.
