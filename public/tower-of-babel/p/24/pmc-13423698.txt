Ga-68 FAPI-04 PET/CT detection of repeatedly biopsy-negative poorly differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma: a case report
Authors: Zhipeng Cheng, Xiuxiu Chang, Yuying Lei, Na Liu, Jing Wu, Lifei Zhang, Aixia Sui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
False-negative endoscopic biopsy may delay the diagnosis of infiltrative gastric cancer, particularly in poorly differentiated or signet-ring cell phenotypes. We report a 56-year-old man with progressive abdominal distension, nausea, vomiting, weight loss, and epigastric pain. Three endoscopic biopsies performed before and after transfer to our hospital showed chronic inflammation without definite malignancy. F-18 FDG PET/CT demonstrated slightly increased and indistinct uptake compared with the liver in the thickened gastric antrum wall, whereas subsequent Ga-68 FAPI-04 PET/CT showed intense uptake with clearer lesion delineation. Because of progressive gastric outlet obstruction and persistent clinical suspicion, distal gastrectomy was performed for symptom relief and definitive diagnosis. Final histopathology confirmed poorly differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma with partial signet-ring cell morphology. The patient subsequently received postoperative XELOX chemotherapy and remained clinically stable at the 7-months follow-up. This case suggests that Ga-68 FAPI-04 PET/CT may serve as a problem-solving adjunct when repeated endoscopic biopsies are non-diagnostic but suspicion for infiltrative gastric cancer persists.

Introduction

Gastric cancer remains a major global health burden and is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage (1). Although endoscopy with tissue biopsy is the diagnostic standard, false-negative biopsy may occur when tumor infiltration is predominantly submucosal, scirrhous, poorly cohesive, or signet-ring cell in pattern (2, 3). In this setting, repeated superficial sampling may show only chronic inflammation despite persistent clinical and radiologic suspicion.

F-18 FDG PET/CT has variable sensitivity for gastric cancer, and lesion conspicuity may be limited in poorly differentiated, mucinous, or signet-ring cell carcinoma (4, 5). Ga-68 FAPI PET/CT targets fibroblast activation protein expressed by cancer-associated fibroblasts and may provide higher tumor-to-background contrast in

selected gastrointestinal malignancies (6–11). We report a repeatedly biopsy-negative but clinically suspicious case in which Ga-68 FAPI-04 PET/CT increased lesion conspicuity and supported multidisciplinary diagnostic-therapeutic planning.

Case presentation

Patient information and clinical findings

A 56-year-old man presented with a 2-months history of abdominal bloating and a 2-weeks history of persistent nausea and vomiting, accompanied by weight loss and epigastric pain. At an outside hospital, 2 consecutive endoscopic biopsies were interpreted as chronic gastritis. His symptoms progressed despite 4 weeks of standard treatment, including a proton pump inhibitor and gastric mucosal protective therapy, and gastrointestinal decompression with nasogastric nutritional support was required.

After transfer to our hospital, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy showed hyperplastic, erosive, and hemorrhagic mucosal changes in the gastric antrum. Repeat biopsy again demonstrated chronic inflammation without definite malignant cells (Figure 1). Because the patient had progressive obstructive symptoms and a persistent mismatch between benign biopsy results and clinical suspicion, a multidisciplinary consultation was performed. Diagnostic assessment

After written informed consent was obtained, the patient underwent F-18 FDG PET/CT followed by Ga-68 FAPI-04 PET/CT more than 48 h later. The total administered activity of F-18 FDG, calculated according to body weight, was 6.2 mCi (approximately 229 MBq), and imaging was performed 60 min after tracer injection. The total administered activity of Ga-68 FAPI-04, also calculated according to body weight, was 3.5 mCi (approximately 130 MBq), and PET/CT acquisition was also performed 60 min after injection. Both PET/CT examinations included non-contrast low-dose CT for attenuation correction and anatomical localization, without intravenous contrast administration.

Before F-18 FDG PET/CT, the patient was instructed to drink water for gastric distention; however, adequate gastric distention could not be achieved before Ga-68 FAPI-04 PET/CT because of poor tolerance. For semiquantitative analysis, the lesion was first localized on sagittal images, and a spherical 3-dimensional region of interest large enough to encompass the entire suspected tumor was placed on the corresponding axial images. SUVmax was automatically calculated by the postprocessing software. If multiple lesions were present, the lesion with the highest SUVmax was selected as the representative lesion.

F-18 FDG PET/CT showed gastric antral wall thickening with slightly increased but indistinct FDG uptake compared with the liver, with an SUVmax of 7.3 (Figure 2). In contrast, Ga-68 FAPI-04 PET/CT demonstrated intense uptake in the same gastric antrum wall, with clearer lesion delineation and an SUVmax of 19.7 (Figure 3). No distant abnormal tracer uptake suggestive of metastatic disease was identified.

Therapeutic intervention

Because of progressive gastric outlet obstruction, fluid imbalance, poor tolerance of oral intake, and persistent suspicion for malignancy, distal gastrectomy was performed primarily for symptom relief and definitive diagnosis. Although EUS, EUS-guided tissue acquisition, deep biopsy, or mucosal incision-assisted biopsy could have been considered after repeated negative mucosal biopsies, these procedures were not performed preoperatively in this patient because of rapidly progressive gastric outlet obstruction, persistent vomiting, poor tolerance of oral intake, and the need for timely therapeutic intervention. In addition, further repeated superficial biopsy was considered unlikely to change immediate management. Distal gastrectomy was therefore selected not only for symptom relief but also for definitive pathological diagnosis.

Pathological findings

Intraoperative frozen section analysis suggested invasive poorly differentiated carcinoma. Final histopathology of the gastrectomy specimen confirmed poorly differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma with partial signet-ring cell morphology, mixed Lauren type. The tumor invaded through the muscularis propria into the subserosal adipose tissue, corresponding to at least pT3 disease. Perineural invasion was present, whereas definite lymphovascular tumor emboli were not identified. The proximal resection margin was free of tumor; however, tumor infiltration was identified in the mucosal muscularis and submucosa of the distal resection margin. The omental tissue was negative for carcinoma. No regional lymph node metastasis was identified among 36 examined lymph nodes; however, carcinoma was noted in adjacent soft tissue in several nodal stations.

Immunohistochemistry showed CK7 positivity, CK20 negativity, partial MUC6 positivity, CD10 negativity, Villin negativity, HER2 negativity (score 0), p53 positivity, beta-catenin positivity, and a Ki-67 labeling index of approximately 40%. Neuroendocrine markers were negative, including synaptophysin, CD56, and chromogranin A. Mismatch repair protein expression was retained, including MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, and MSH6 (Figure 4).

Follow-up and outcomes

The patient recovered after surgery and received postoperative XELOX chemotherapy. At the 7-months postoperative follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable without newly reported severe treatment-related adverse symptoms.

Discussion

This case highlights an important diagnostic pitfall: repeated negative endoscopic forceps biopsies do not exclude infiltrative gastric cancer when clinical and imaging findings remain suspicious. Although endoscopic biopsy is the standard diagnostic method for gastric cancer, its yield depends on adequate sampling of tumor-involved mucosa. Poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma, signet-ring cell carcinoma, diffuse-type gastric cancer, and linitis plastica-like lesions may predominantly infiltrate the submucosa, muscularis propria, or stromal compartment, while the overlying mucosa shows only non-specific inflammatory or erosive changes. As a result, superficial biopsy specimens may be non-diagnostic (2, 3). In this patient, three endoscopic biopsies showed only chronic inflammation, but progressive gastric outlet obstruction, weight loss, epigastric pain, and gastric antral wall thickening remained highly discordant with a benign diagnosis. Final pathology confirmed poorly differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma with partial signet-ring cell morphology, subserosal invasion, and perineural invasion, supporting the likelihood of insufficient sampling by repeated superficial biopsies.

The value of Ga-68 FAPI-04 PET/CT in this case was not to replace histopathological confirmation, but to provide additional problem-solving information in a difficult biopsy-negative scenario. F-18 FDG PET/CT showed slightly increased but indistinct FDG uptake compared with the liver in the thickened gastric antrum wall, with limited lesion conspicuity. In contrast, Ga-68 FAPI-04 PET/CT demonstrated intense uptake in the same region, with a higher SUVmax and clearer lesion delineation. In the present case, Ga-68 FAPI-04 PET/CT influenced management by increasing confidence that the biopsy-negative antral wall thickening represented an infiltrative malignant process rather than non-specific inflammatory change. This additional imaging information helped the multidisciplinary team move from repeated non-diagnostic superficial biopsies toward definitive surgical intervention, particularly because the patient had progressive gastric outlet obstruction, poor oral intake, and fluid imbalance. Importantly, surgery was not performed solely because of FAPI-04 positivity; rather, it was based on progressive obstruction, poor oral intake, fluid imbalance, repeated non-diagnostic biopsies, and persistent clinical-radiologic concern. Therefore, FAPI-04 PET/CT served as a problem-solving adjunct within the clinical decision pathway rather than as a stand-alone diagnostic test.

The difference between FDG and FAPI-04 imaging may reflect their distinct biological targets. F-18 FDG uptake mainly reflects glucose metabolism and is variable in gastric cancer, depending on histological subtype, tumor cellularity, mucin content, and tumor biology (4, 5). Poorly differentiated, mucinous, or signet-ring cell gastric cancers may therefore show reduced or heterogeneous FDG uptake, lowering tumor-to-background contrast and obscuring lesion margins. By contrast, FAPI imaging targets fibroblast activation protein expressed by cancer-associated fibroblasts in the tumor stroma. Tumors with diffuse infiltration, desmoplastic reaction, or poorly cohesive histology may show prominent FAPI uptake even when FDG findings are equivocal. Several head-to-head studies have reported higher lesion detectability, tumor-to-background ratios, or clearer lesion delineation with FAPI PET/CT than with FDG PET/CT in gastric cancer (6–9). This may be particularly relevant in mucinous adenocarcinoma and signet-ring cell carcinoma, in which FAPI PET/CT has shown promising diagnostic value (10). The present case is consistent with these observations, although a single case cannot establish diagnostic superiority.

This case also suggests that the diagnostic strategy for suspected infiltrative gastric cancer should not rely only on repeated routine mucosal biopsies. When symptoms progress despite non-diagnostic biopsy results, further evaluation may include endoscopic ultrasonography, deep biopsy, mucosal incision-assisted biopsy, EUS-guided tissue acquisition, contrast-enhanced cross-sectional imaging, molecular imaging, and multidisciplinary reassessment (2, 3). In the present patient, preoperative EUS or EUS-guided deep biopsy was not performed because of progressive gastric outlet obstruction and the need for timely surgical management. In such real-world circumstances, Ga-68 FAPI-04 PET/CT may help strengthen diagnostic confidence and guide further intervention.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this is a single case report and cannot determine the diagnostic accuracy of Ga-68 FAPI-04 PET/CT in biopsy-negative gastric cancer. Second, FDG and FAPI-04 scans were not performed under identical gastric distention conditions, which may have influenced lesion conspicuity and SUV measurements. Therefore, the apparent advantage of FAPI-04 imaging should be interpreted cautiously. Third, FAPI uptake is not tumor-specific and may also occur in benign fibrotic, inflammatory, wound-healing, or reactive stromal processes (12, 13). In the stomach, chronic gastritis, ulcer-related fibrosis, post-inflammatory remodeling, or other benign fibroinflammatory processes may theoretically produce false-positive uptake. Therefore, FAPI-positive gastric wall uptake should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of cancer. It should be integrated with symptoms, endoscopic findings, CT morphology, biopsy results, and, when necessary, definitive histopathology. Finally, the follow-up period was short, limiting assessment of the prognostic or treatment-monitoring value of FAPI-04 PET/CT.

Conclusion

This case suggests that Ga-68 FAPI-04 PET/CT may have problem-solving imaging value in patients with repeatedly biopsy-negative but clinically suspected infiltrative gastric cancer. However, FAPI-positive uptake is not specific for malignancy, and the findings must be interpreted together with clinical presentation, endoscopy, cross-sectional imaging, and histopathological confirmation.
