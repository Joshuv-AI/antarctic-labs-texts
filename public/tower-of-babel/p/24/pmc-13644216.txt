Association between lactate-to-albumin ratio and the short-time mortality of patients with ischemic stroke in intensive care unit
Authors: Yiting Ning, Xuerong Jia, Zhiruo Song, Anyu Liao, Na Sai, Kangmo Huang, Wusheng Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with severe acute ischemic stroke (IS) often face poor outcomes and extended hospital stays. Early prognosis prediction aids in creating personalized treatment plans. The lactate-to-albumin ratio (LAR) is linked to poor outcomes in various illnesses, but its connection to all-cause mortality in ischemic stroke patients is not well understood. This study aims to investigate the prognostic value of LAR in these IS patients in intensive care unit.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study utilized MIMIC-IV (v2.2) data to identify intensive care unit (ICU) patients with acute IS. Lactate and albumin levels were measured upon admission, and LAR was calculated. The primary focus of our study was on 28-day all-cause mortality. Cox proportional hazards analysis and receiver operating characteristic curves assessed the link between LAR and mortality, while Kaplan–Meier analysis compared mortality rates between high and low LAR groups. Restricted cubic splines examined the nonlinear relationship between LAR and mortality.ResultsThe study analyzed 703 IS patients in the ICU, median age of 69.9 years: 387 men (55%) and 316 women (45%), finding a 28-day mortality rate of 30.6%. A significant correlation existed between LAR and mortality (adjusted HR: 1.24; 95% CI: 1.06–1.44; p = 0.006). The AUC of final multivariate model was 0.831 (95% CI: 0.800–0.862). Kaplan-Meier curves indicated higher mortality risk in the high-LAR group (log rank p < 0.001). LAR showed a nonlinear relationship with mortality (p for nonlinear < 0.001).ConclusionsThe LAR was significantly associated with the 28-day mortality in IS patients in the ICU, suggesting its potential utility as a prognostic biomarker.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-026-05148-1.

Introduction

Cerebrovascular diseases, particularly ischemic stroke (IS), remain a major global health burden, contributing substantially to disability, mortality, and economic costs [1]. However, the clinical outcomes of IS are often unsatisfactory [2]. Therefore, early identification of prognostic factors is essential for improving clinical management and guiding timely intervention.

During the acute phase of ischemic stroke, brain damage leads to a systemic inflammatory response, A combination of multiple blood markers may enhance the ability to predict long-term outcome following ischemic stroke [3]. Previous studies have confirmed that hematological markers and composite indicators reflecting the systemic inflammatory state are associated with the prognosis of IS patients, including many joint indicators such as neutrophil albumin ratio [4], hemoglobin-to-red cell distribution width ratio [5], neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) [6], triglyceride-glucose index [7], and RDW-to-albumin ratio [8]. Scholars are also working hard to identify easily accessible early hematological markers that can predict the prognosis of patients.

Ischemic stroke and the complications it induces are largely affected by oxidative stress and systemic inflammation [9]. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) is an enzyme found inside cells that serves as a marker for cell membrane damage, often caused by oxidative stress [10]. As a product of the catalytic metabolism by LDH, serum lactate reflects tissue hypoperfusion and cellular hypoxia levels, and can be used to foresee organ failure and mortality in critically ill patients [11]. However, many factors, such as physical exertion, tissue hypoxia and metabolic disorders can influence lactate levels [12]. Furthermore, previous investigations have demonstrated that albumin as extracellular antioxidant, immunomodulator, exhibits associations with the severity of inflammation, disease prognosis like IS [13] and mortality [14]. Nevertheless, the nutritional status of patients, chronic inflammation and liver cirrhosis can also influence albumin levels [15]. Therefore, making predictions solely based on serum lactate or albumin levels may have limitations (such as being interfered with by the state of metabolic diseases).

To overcome this limitation, some scholars have proposed LAR, which might reduce the interference or error caused by a single indicator. It might more accurately reflect the relationship with patient prognosis. Numerous investigations have confirmed the predictive value of the LAR in various clinical scenarios, including sepsis [16], acute pancreatitis [17], heart failure [18] and acute respiratory failure [19] but the association between LAR and mortality in patients with IS remains uncertain. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the association between the LAR upon admission and 28-day all-cause mortality in IS patients in ICU.

Methods

Study population and data source

Data were sourced from the MIMIC-IV (v2.2) database, which is an extensive and freely accessible collection of patient information from critical care unit admissions at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, spanning the period from 2008 to 2019. Following the training, one of our authors was allowed to access the database. (ID: 13238167) The study’s design and implementation adhered to applicable guidelines and regulations, such as the Helsinki Declaration. Inclusion criteria: (1) age > 18 years when entering ICU; (2) patients diagnosed with ischemic stroke according to the ICD-9 or ICD-10; (3) length of stay in ICU > 24 h. Exclusion criteria: (1) not the first intensive care unit( ICU) admission for the patient; (2) patients admitted with cerebral trauma, cirrhosis; (3) missing key medical records, especially the actual lactate and albumin records; (4) no follow-up data within 28 days.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed using Structured Query Language (SQL) and Navicat software (version 16). For the final cohort, we collected the following data: (1) demographics, such as age, gender, race; (2) the first result of lactate and albumin within the initial 24 h since ICU admission; (3) the average laboratory results on the first day of ICU admission (Red Blood Cells, White Blood Cells, red cell distribution width, Platelets, Serum Calcium, Serum Sodium, Serum Potassium); (4) comorbidities (myocardial infarct, congestive heart failure, dementia, chronic pulmonary disease, diabetes, rheumatic disease, renal disease, malignant cancer, fibrillation, hypertension, obesity); (5) severity of illness scores on the first day of ICU admission, which include Sequential Organ Failure (SOFA) [20], Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) [21], Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) [22]; (6) the mean values of vital signs during the first 24 h of ICU stay (Mean blood pressure, heart rate; respiratory rate); (7) Stroke-specific variables (Anterior circulation, Posterior circulation, antiplatelet, anticoagulation, reteplase, mechanical thrombectomy, hemorrhagic transformation).

LAR, the key factor of interest, was computed in the following way: LAR=lactate/albumin. Vital signs and treatments were considered in this study. The endpoint of our study was defined as all-cause mortality within 28 days. The MIMIC database records the death situations of patients. The date of death outside the hospital was obtained from the Social Security Administration Death Master File of the United States. To avoid bias introduced by statistical imputation and ensure data integrity, subjects without target data were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of both survivor and non-survivor samples were compared. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), and between-group differences were assessed using Student’s t-test or Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons between groups were performed using Pearson’s chisquare test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate.

Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the association between LAR and 28-day mortality. Covariates were preselected based on clinical knowledge, including demographics, comorbidities, severity scores, and stroke-specific variables. Four sequential models were constructed with stepwise adjustment. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals. Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank tests compared survival between high- and low-LAR groups.

Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis evaluated models predicting 28-day mortality in IS patients, calculating sensitivity, specificity, and AUC. The ROC curve using Delong test was employed to compare the AUC values of the combined model mainly constructed with LAR with those of traditional scoring systems such as SOFA, GCS, SAPS II, SIRS and OASIS. Incremental value was assessed using ΔAUC, net reclassification improvement (NRI), integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), and decision curve analysis (DCA).

X-tile software identified the optimal LAR cutoff, which was solely adopted for patient grouping in Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis implemented via the segmented R package was performed to explore the dose-response relationship between LAR and mortality; the four-knot Cox-based RCS model yielded the raw inflection point representing the nonlinear association, whose robustness was internally validated by 1000 bootstrap resamples to acquire the corresponding median inflection point for threshold verification.

The visreg R package was used to visualize the interaction between two continuous variables. Subgroup analyses assessed how LAR and dichotomized covariates affected primary outcomes. Statistical analysis and figures were created using R (version 4.4.2). A p-value below 0.05 indicates statistical significance.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 703 patients with ischemic stroke (IS) were included from MIMIC-IV (v2.2) database in the final analysis, of whom 215 (30.58%) died within 28 days of admission. Figure 1 shows details of the selection process employed in this study.

The LAR is an independent risk factor for 28-day mortality

Univariate Cox regression analysis showed that both higher LAR levels (HR: 1.33, 95% CI: 1.20–1.46, p < 0.001) and higher lactate levels (HR: 1.11, 95% CI: 1.07–1.16, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with increased 28-day all-cause mortality in IS patients. Furthermore, higher albumin levels are significantly associated with a lower 28-day all-cause mortality in IS patients. (HR: 0.71, 95% CI: 0.60–0.85, p < 0.001). The ROC analyses showed that the AUC value of the combined model was superior to SOFA [0.831 (95% CI: 0.800–0.862) vs. 0.519 (95% CI: 0.472–0.566), DeLong p < 0.001], GCS [0.831 (95% CI: 0.800–0.862) vs. 0.501 (95% CI: 0.466–0.536), DeLong p < 0.001], SAPSII [0.831 (95% CI: 0.800–0.862) vs. 0.672 (95% CI: 0.629–0.714), DeLong p < 0.001], OASIS [0.831 (95% CI: 0.800–0.862) vs. 0.680 (95% CI: 0.638–0.722), DeLong p < 0.001], and SIRS [0.831 (95% CI: 0.800–0.862) vs. 0.581 (95% CI: 0.538–0.624), DeLong p < 0.001].Table 2 presents adjusted analyses of LAR and28-day all-cause mortality in patients with IS using COX proportional risk models. LAR was associated with 28-day all-cause mortality in all models. After adjusting for all the covariates that were pre-selected based on clinical knowledge, LAR remained an independent predictor, (adjusted HR: 1.24; 95% CI: 1.06–1.44; p = 0.006). The ROC analyses showed that the AUC value of final multiple covariates combined model is 0.831 (95% CI: 0.800–0.862); (Fig. 2).

RCS analysis

The RCS analysis indicated a nonlinear relationship between LAR and 28-day all-cause mortality in IS patients (p for non-linearity < 0.001; Fig. 4). The raw inflection point identified from the original data was 3.397. After 1000 Bootstrap resampling validations, the median inflection point was 2.345(95%CI: 1.756–4.160) (Table 3). The relatively wide range of the 95%CI, which included the original inflection point, indicated a certain degree of fluctuation in the critical value of LAR. However, the overall threshold interval was stable and could serve as a reference for subsequent subgroup analyses or truncated grouping.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analysis was conducted to assess whether the association between LAR and 28-day mortality remained consistent across different subgroup (Fig. 5). When the stratified analysis was performed for age (≤ 65 or > 65), gender (male or female), race(white, black and other), congestive heart failure (yes or no), myocardial infarct (yes or no), chronic pulmonary disease (yes or no), diabetes (yes or no), hypertension (yes or no), the forest plot showed no significant interaction of LAR with each dichotomized covariates (p for interaction > 0.05, Fig. 5). Subgroup analyses were performed stratified by key clinical characteristics including gender, age, race, comorbidities, medication use, stroke circulation territory, and hemorrhagic transformation. As shown in Fig. 5, a significant association was observed between the LAR and the outcome event in the overall cohort (HR = 1.33, 95%CI: 1.20–1.46, p < 0.001).

Stratified analyses confirmed that the risk-increasing effect of the LAR on the outcome was consistent with the overall trend across all prespecified subgroups. Statistically significant associations were maintained in subgroups of female, older age (> 65 years), White race, presence of diabetes or hypertension, use of antiplatelet or reteplase therapy, anterior circulation stroke, and absence of hemorrhagic transformation (all p < 0.05). Conversely, the associations did not reach statistical significance in subgroups of Black race, anticoagulant use, and posterior circulation stroke, which may be attributed to limited sample size. Interaction tests revealed that all P values for interaction were greater than 0.05 (ranging from 0.077 to 0.793), indicating no significant heterogeneity in the association between the exposure factor and the outcome across subgroups. These findings demonstrate the robust and generalizability of the study conclusion.

Moreover, we generated a heat map (Fig. 6) to show how GCS, SIRS, and LAR interact and affect 28-day mortality. The color band illustrated how GCS, SIRS or LAR influenced mortality probability, with deeper yellow indicating a stronger positive correlation and deeper blue indicating a stronger negative correlation. It suggested that the association between LAR and 28-day mortality is affected by GCS or SIRS, and conversely, the association between SIRS (p for interaction = 0.001), GCS (p for interaction = 0.388) or SOFA (p for interaction = 0.017) and 28-day mortality is also influenced by LAR (Fig. 6).

Discussion

In this study, it was determined that the Lactate-to-Albumin Ratio (LAR), along with lactate and albumin levels, were associated with patient prognosis. Notably, LAR emerged as an independent predictor of all-cause mortality in patients with ischemic stroke (IS) within 28 days of hospital admission. Furthermore, the predictive capability of LAR for 28-day mortality in IS patients surpassed that of lactate or albumin when considered individually. Additionally, our findings indicated a non-linear relationship between LAR and mortality rate.

During the acute phase of ischemic stroke, brain damage leads to a systemic inflammatory response. Previous scholars have found that some complex blood indicators are related to the prognosis of IS patients, but these combined indicators focus more on the inflammatory status of patients but do not fully reflect their metabolic and nutritional status.

Our research found that compared with the 28-day survivors, the serum lactate levels of the 28-day non-survivors were significantly increased. Similar to our findings, previous literature has revealed that elevated blood lactate is associated with the mortality rate of critically ill patients [11], traumatic brain injury [23]. and those with severe sepsis [24]. We speculated that lactate levels may affect the prognosis of IS patients through the following mechanisms. During human stress after an acute ischemic stroke, the brain can increase its reliance on lactic acid to adapt to the new energy demands [25]. Additionally, by boosting the NAD+/NADH ratio, lactic acid and ketone bodies can impede glycolysis, leading to a shift in glucose metabolism towards the pentose phosphate pathway to regulate brain metabolism and enhance antioxidant defenses [26], which might be helpful in improving the prognosis of the patients. However, lactate concentration is affected by a range of factors, including liver dysfunction, conditions like diabetes, drugs, and nutritional status [12], which reduces the reliability of using lactate alone to predict the prognosis of patients.

Furthermore, our research found that albumin levels were related to prognosis. This was easy to understand because plasma albumin, as a negative acute phase protein, reflected the level of inflammation [27]. Hypoproteinemia is linked to poor stroke prognosis [28], mortality prediction [13] and abnormal body water balance in acute ischemic stroke [29]. Wang et al. pointed out that for AIS patients under 60 years old, the increase in serum albumin was dose-dependent on the reduction of the risk of hemorrhagic transformation [30]. Albumin may improve stroke prognosis by reducing oxidative damage [31], decreasing neutrophil adhesion and vascular leakage [32], and inhibiting platelet aggregation through various mechanisms [33]. However, the serum albumin level is affected by chronic diseases, nutritional support and inflammation [15], which may limit its prognostic value to some extent.

More importantly, our research confirmed that LAR was a prognostic predictor, and its predictive performance was superior to that of lactate and albumin. As mentioned above, lactic acid or albumin alone is susceptible to interference from factors such as chronic diseases and nutritional status, making it difficult to reflect the metabolic and inflammatory states of patients under acute stress. Previous studies have pointed out that compared with lactate or albumin alone, LAR is a superior indicator that can better reflect the horizontal tissue oxygenation status and inflammatory response of patients [17]. Similarly, Chen found that LAR was associated with the prognosis of patients with acute myocardial infarction [14]. Additionally, LAR can be used as an independent predictor of 28-day mortality in patients with respiratory distress syndrome [19]. Therefore, this suggests that LAR is expected to serve as a prognostic predictor for patients with IS. Of course, this also requires well-designed prospective trials to further verify our results.

Notably, the relationship between LAR and 28-day mortality rate was nonlinear. The inflection raw point identified from the original data was 3.397. The stability of this threshold has been verified through Bootstrap (500 times) or cross-validation, but it needs to be validated in future external datasets. Additionally, all thresholds in this study are exploratory statistical indicators rather than validated clinical cutoffs for patient risk stratification.

Limitations

Owing to its retrospective design and reliance on a single database, this single-center cohort study is limited in causal inference and inherently susceptible to potential selection bias arising from the non-random absence of lactate and albumin measurements. Concerns regarding missing value imputation were raised because repeated measurements of lactate and albumin at 6–24 h intervals were not consistently recorded for all patients. Therefore, this study focused on establishing the strong independent prognostic predictive value of baseline LAR. Despite multivariate adjustments and subgroup analyses, residual confounding may still exist. Additionally, the LAR measured for the first time after admission cannot reflect the impact of dynamic LAR changes on prognosis. Our data from MIMIC-IV (v2.2) cover inpatients from 2008 to 2019, and treatment plans during this period may have varied with medical advancements, potentially causing deviations in results. The database lacks imaging data, limiting analysis of intracranial changes in IS patients and their effects on mortality, which warrants further investigation. It should be noted that the conclusions of this study are applicable to the ischemic stroke population in the ICU, but may not be applicable to patients with mild symptoms or those not in the ICU. Furthermore, since the database did not provide the specific cause of death for the patients, the study adopted all-cause mortality instead of specific cause mortality.

Conclusions

Our results indicated that the LAR defined by the ratio of lactate to albumin was associated with the 28-day mortality rate of IS patients in the ICU after admission and was an independent risk factor. Therefore, the detection of LAR may help in the early identification of patients at high risk of death after IS in the ICU and guide the treatment and thereby improve the prognosis.
