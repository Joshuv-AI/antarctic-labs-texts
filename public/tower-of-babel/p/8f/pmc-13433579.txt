Integrated multi-omics analysis: exploring the molecular regulatory mechanisms of HIV infection and identifying the key gene WFDC2
Authors: Bingqing Yan, Tiantian Jiang, Yayun Wu, Renfei Zhu, Shifang Wang, Yihua Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although substantial progress has been made in the treatment of HIV infection, the regulatory mechanisms linking genetic variation, transcriptomic alterations, immune-cell changes, and post-transcriptional control remain poorly defined. A systematic multi-omics framework is needed to identify key molecular drivers and candidate biomarkers associated with HIV infection. We performed an integrated multi-omics analysis combining protein quantitative trait locus (cis-pQTL)–based summary-data Mendelian randomization (SMR) for 555 plasma proteins with multiple HIV-related genome-wide association study (GWAS) outcomes, followed by HEIDI testing to exclude linkage. In parallel, we conducted differential expression analyses of mRNA and miRNA from blood of healthy controls (n = 7), individuals with low viral load (n = 14), and individuals with high viral load (n = 16). Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was used to define co-expression modules, which were overlaid with SMR hits and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and further embedded into transcription-factor (TF) regulon networks. Plasma WFDC2 levels were quantified by ELISA in untreated HIV-infected, ART-treated, and control cohorts. Immune-cell deconvolution, gene set enrichment, and miRNA–mRNA network modeling were used to characterize the functional context of candidate genes.Lastly, HIV-associated cellular models were constructed, followed by functional validation experiments based on WFDC2 overexpression. Transcriptomic profiling revealed strong concordance between the HVL–CON and LVL–CON contrasts, identifying 803 commonly up-regulated and 1,108 commonly down-regulated genes. Integration of SMR results with DEGs highlighted eight overlapping candidate genes—including WFDC2—whose encoded proteins were supported by both genetic instruments and transcriptional deregulation. These genes were embedded in immune-related co-expression modules and TF regulons. Among them, WFDC2 emerged as a key candidate, showing consistent down-regulation at the mRNA and protein levels, extensive correlations with inferred immune-cell subsets, and co-expressed genes enriched for chromatin remodeling, antiviral defense, RNA processing, and translation. Plasma WFDC2 concentrations were reduced in untreated HIV-infected individuals and partially restored under ART compared with healthy controls. Differentially expressed miRNAs potentially targeting WFDC2 and its co-expressed partners suggested additional post-transcriptional regulation. This multi-omics framework delineates a layered network connecting genetic regulation, transcriptional changes, immune-cell shifts, and miRNA-mediated control in HIV infection. Within this network, WFDC2 stands out as a putative hub integrating immune modulation and RNA metabolic pathways, and may represent a promising biomarker and potential therapeutic targets in HIV.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-026-02197-1.

Introduction

Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS) is a chronic infectious disease caused by the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), primarily characterized by impaired immune system function with multi-organ involvement [1].Despite remarkable advances in antiretroviral therapy [2], HIV infection continues to pose substantial clinical and public health challenges [3]. While modern combination antiretroviral therapy (ART) can suppress plasma viremia to undetectable levels and dramatically reduce AIDS-related morbidity and mortality [4],profound immune system perturbations often persist, including incomplete CD4⁺ T-cell reconstitution [5], skewed immune-cell composition [6], chronic inflammation [7], and an elevated risk of non-AIDS comorbidities [8, 9]. The pathogenesis of HIV infection is intricate, involving multiple dimensions including virus-host cell interactions, immune regulatory network disruption, and aberrant gene expression [10]. To date, our understanding of its underlying molecular regulatory mechanisms remains incomplete.

With the advancement of high-throughput sequencing technologies, multi-omics analysis has emerged as a powerful tool for dissecting the molecular mechanisms of complex diseases [11, 12]. It enables systematic characterization of disease-associated molecular alterations across multiple layers, including the transcriptome, proteome, and epigenome. Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) can construct co-expression modules based on gene expression profiles, facilitating the identification of core modules and hub genes closely correlated with disease phenotypes [13, 14]. Summary-data-based Mendelian Randomization (SMR) analysis, when integrated with cis-expression quantitative trait locus (cis-eQTL) data, can effectively verify causal associations between gene expression and disease phenotypes while minimizing confounding effects [15]. Additionally, microRNAs (miRNAs), as crucial post-transcriptional regulators, modulate biological processes such as immune cell development and viral replication through targeted binding to mRNAs, and their regulatory roles in HIV infection have attracted increasing attention [16, 17].

Among the many host genes potentially involved in HIV pathogenesis, WFDC2 (WAP four-disulfide core domain protein 2) is particularly intriguing. WFDC2 encodes a secreted glycoprotein characterized by WAP-type disulfide core domains [18] and is best known as a biomarker in certain malignancies [19–21]. Emerging evidence also has demonstrated its role as a key factor in immune regulation and host defense [22].However, the expression characteristics and functional roles of this gene in HIV infection remain unclear. In this study, we integrated multi-omics data including transcriptomics, proteomics, and miRNAomics, combined with bioinformatic approaches such as WGCNA and SMR, to systematically dissect the molecular regulatory networks underlying HIV infection under different viral load states. We also screened and validated the function of the key regulatory gene WFDC2. This study aims to provide novel molecular targets for investigating HIV pathogenesis and to lay a theoretical foundation for optimizing clinical diagnostic strategies and therapeutic interventions.

Materials and methods

A schematic overview of the entire research process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

A schematic of the entire study process

Cis-pQTL instruments and HIV-related GWAS outcomes

Cis-pQTL summary statistics for 555 plasma proteins were obtained from a large-scale proteogenomic study [23]. This dataset was selected because of its large sample size, broad proteomic coverage, and suitability for summary-data Mendelian randomization analysis, thereby enabling more robust instrumental-variable-based prioritization of candidate proteins in relation to HIV-related GWAS outcomes. As this dataset was not derived from an HIV-specific population, it was used as a high-powered external reference resource for plasma protein genetic regulation rather than as a disease-matched cohort.

For each protein, we defined cis variants as single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) located within ± 1 Mb of the encoding gene. Only genome-wide significant cis-pQTLs (P < 1.8 × 10⁻⁹) were retained as candidate instrumental variables to ensure strong association with protein levels and reduce weak-instrument bias. Variant identifiers were harmonized across datasets using rsID and genomic coordinates.

GWAS summary statistics for the outcomes of interest were collected from publicly available repositories, including GWAS Catalog accessions (GCST90435531, GCST90435532, and GCST90041717) and FinnGen endpoints (FinnGen R12: HIV-parasitic infections (AB1), R12: HIV infection status (Z21, asymptomatic), and R12: HIV, NOS (AB1)). All datasets were formatted to a consistent genome build and aligned to matching variant identifiers. When necessary, liftover and allele harmonization were performed to ensure comparability across sources.

Summary-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) and linkage filtering

SMR was performed to test whether cis-pQTL-driven differences in protein abundance were associated with each outcome using GWAS summary statistics [24]. For each protein–outcome pair, we estimated the SMR effect and its standard error under the SMR framework. To distinguish pleiotropy from linkage, we applied the HEIDI test using additional cis variants in the locus; loci failing HEIDI were excluded based on a predefined HEIDI threshold. Multiple testing was controlled using false discovery rate (FDR) correction across tested protein–outcome pairs.

Transcriptomic data processing and differential expression analysis

Whole-blood mRNA and miRNA expression data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) dataset GSE76246, comprising controls (CON, n = 7), individuals with low viral load (LVL, n = 14), and individuals with high viral load (HVL, n = 16). Standard quality control procedures were implemented to exclude low-quality samples. Differential expression analysis was then performed using the limma package [25]. Two primary contrasts were defined: HVL versus CON (HVL–CON) and LVL versus CON (LVL-CON). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined by |log₂ fold change (log₂FC)| > 1 and adjusted P value < 0.05. In parallel, differentially expressed miRNAs were identified using similar criteria.

Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs

To characterize the biological functions associated with differential gene expression, upregulated and downregulated DEGs were analyzed separately using the clusterProfiler package [26]. Gene Ontology Biological Process (GO BP) enrichment analysis was performed, and terms with adjusted P < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Integration of SMR findings and expression changes

To identify genes with consistent regulatory evidence across molecular layers, integrative analyses were performed by combining differential expression results with SMR. Genes passing SMR significance criteria were intersected with the shared DEGs to prioritize candidates supported by both differential expression and genetic evidence.

Immune-cell deconvolution

To estimate immune-cell composition from bulk transcriptomic profiles, we applied CIBERSORT with the LM22 reference matrix [27], which characterizes expression signatures of 22 human hematopoietic cell subsets. Relative fractions of immune-cell types, including various T-cell, B-cell, NK-cell, monocyte, dendritic cell, macrophage, eosinophil, and mast cell populations, were inferred for each sample. Pairwise group comparisons (CON vs. LVL, CON vs. HVL, LVL vs. HVL) were conducted using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. P values were adjusted using Benjamini-Hochberg FDR control.

Co-expression network construction and module detection

Gene co-expression networks were constructed using Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) [28]. The soft-thresholding power was set to 22 to achieve approximate scale-free topology. Network construction was performed with a maximum block size of 30,000 genes. Dynamic tree cutting was applied to detect gene modules, with the parameters set as deepSplit = 4, detectCutHeight = 0.995, and minModuleSize = 50. Closely related modules were further merged using a mergeCutHeight of 0.2. Module eigengenes were then calculated, and their associations with clinical traits were evaluated to identify modules most relevant to HIV infection. To further assess the involvement of gene modules in disease-related expression changes, module-level differential expression analysis was performed by comparing average module expression between groups. Statistical significance was evaluated using wilcox test. Finally, to characterize the biological functions captured by the co-expression network, GO BP enrichment analysis was conducted for the key WGCNA modules of interest. Key WGCNA modules were subjected to network reconstruction by integrating gene co-expression relationships with curated protein–protein interaction (PPI) data. Module-specific co-expression matrices were generated, and PPI information was incorporated to refine network connections. TOM were calculated to assess intramodular connectivity and network structure. For each WGCNA module, intramodular connectivity was quantified by calculating module membership (kME), defined as the correlation between individual gene expression profiles and the corresponding module eigengene. Genes were ranked by kME values, and the top-ranked genes within each module were identified as candidate hub genes.

TF-target regulatory network inference

The TF-target regulatory network was derived in two steps. First, putative TF-gene links were generated by promoter motif scanning with motifmatchr(https://github.com/GreenleafLab/motifmatchr) on protein-coding gene promoters extracted from an Ensembl annotation database via ensembldb (using promoters/genes to obtain promoter coordinates and gene symbols). Second, XGBoost regression models were fitted separately for each target gene using 5-fold cross-validation and 100 boosting rounds. Model performance statistics were recorded from the cross-validation process. To improve robustness, feature-importance scores were extracted from each fold-specific model and then averaged across all folds. Feature importance was evaluated using the standard XGBoost metrics of Gain, Cover, and Frequency. In parallel, the correlation between each candidate TF and its target gene was calculated using Pearson correlation analysis. The final TF regulatory network was assembled by integrating motif support, averaged XGBoost feature importance, and TF-target expression correlation. TF-target pairs with higher averaged importance scores were considered more strongly supported candidate regulatory interactions.

WFDC2-immune cell correlation and pathway analysis

To evaluate associations between WFDC2 expression and immune-cell composition, WFDC2 transcript levels were correlated with inferred immune-cell fractions across all samples using Spearman correlation. Correlation P values and FDRs were computed for both raw WFDC2 expression and covariate-adjusted residuals. To evaluate pathway-level alterations associated with WFDC2, we applied two complementary strategies. First, samples were dichotomized into high- and low-WFDC2 groups based on an interquartile range (IQR)–based stratification, followed by differential expression analysis between groups. Second, a correlation-based approach was performed by quantifying the association between WFDC2 expression and each gene across samples to derive a continuous ranking metric. For both strategies, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted using GO BP gene sets, with genes ranked by the corresponding continuous statistics and enrichment significance evaluated using permutation-based testing. Pathways with adjusted P < 0.05 were considered significant, and shared pathways were defined as those significant in both analyses with consistent enrichment direction.

WFDC2 protein quantification by ELISA

Plasma concentrations of WFDC2 protein in healthy controls, treatment-naive HIV-infected individuals, and subjects receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART) were measured using a commercially available Human HE4 (epididymal protein 4) ELISA Kit (Catalog No.: E-EL-H6191, Elabscience). Samples were processed under standardized pre-analytical conditions, incubated in wells pre-coated with capture antibodies, and detected with enzyme-conjugated secondary antibodies. Absorbance was recorded at the recommended wavelength, and WFDC2 concentrations were derived from standard curves generated using serial dilutions of recombinant WFDC2. Group comparisons were conducted using appropriate two-sided tests depending on data distribution, with prespecified significance thresholds.In addition, inter-plate CVs for the ELISA assay were calculated using the formula: CV (%) = (SD / mean) × 100%.

ssmiRNA functional enrichment analysis and miRNA-mRNA network construction

Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed microRNAs (DE-miRNAs) was performed using miRNA Enrichment Analysis and Annotation (miEAA) implemented in the rbioapi package [29].

miRNA-mRNA regulatory interactions were constructed using the multiMiR package [30]. Briefly, predicted miRNA targets were retrieved with get_multimir function for all differentially expressed miRNAs, and target symbols were overlapped with the differentially expressed mRNA set. Finally, the interaction network was plotted using the igraph package.

Cell culture and experimental design

Human Jurkat cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂.

To assess the role of WFDC2 in the HIV-related cellular context, Jurkat cells were assigned to the following groups: control, infection, infection + WFDC2 overexpression (infection + OE), and infection + empty vector (infection + EV). Cells in the control group were maintained under the same culture conditions without infection. Cells in the infection group were exposed to the HIV-related treatment. For the overexpression experiments, Jurkat cells were transfected with a WFDC2 overexpression plasmid or the corresponding empty vector and then subjected to subsequent analyses.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from Jurkat cells using lysis buffer, and protein concentrations were measured using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes.

After blocking, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against gp120, gp41, p24, WFDC2, BCL-2, BAX, cleaved caspase-3, caspase-3, and β-actin, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence detection system and quantified by densitometric analysis using ImageJ software. The expression levels of gp120, gp41, p24, WFDC2, BCL-2, and BAX were normalized to β-actin. Cleaved caspase-3 was normalized to total caspase-3.

Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis assay

Cell apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection assay according to the standard protocol. Briefly, Jurkat cells were collected, washed with phosphate-buffered saline, and resuspended in binding buffer. Cells were then stained with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide.

Flow cytometric analysis was performed, and data were analyzed using flow cytometry software. A sequential gating strategy was applied. First, the main cell population was selected based on forward scatter height and side scatter height to exclude debris. Second, singlets were identified using SSC-A versus SSC-H. Apoptotic populations were then analyzed on Annexin V-FITC/PI dot plots. Viable cells were defined as Annexin V-/PI-, early apoptotic cells as Annexin V+/PI-, late apoptotic cells as Annexin V+/PI+, and necrotic cells as Annexin V-/PI+. The apoptotic rate was calculated as the sum of early and late apoptotic cells.

Statistical analysis

All statistical tests were two-sided, with significance defined as P < 0.05, and multiple testing was controlled primarily using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (BH-FDR). Immune cell fractions inferred by CIBERSORT were compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Correlation analysis was performed using spearman.

Results

Coordinated genetic and transcriptional changes in disease states

Integration of cis-pQTL-SMR analysis, differential expression analysis, and functional enrichment results revealed significant consistency between genetically regulated protein level changes and disease-related transcriptional changes (Fig. 2A). SMR analysis identified a set of candidate proteins significantly associated with multiple outcomes, exhibiting a stable pattern of genetic effect direction across different outcomes, suggesting that these molecules may be involved in a common causal pathway.

Differential expression analysis further showed a large number of common differentially expressed genes in high- and low-burden/state comparisons, with highly correlated magnitudes of change, indicating a high degree of consistency in overall transcriptional remodeling between the two states (Fig. 2B). Functional enrichment analysis of these common differentially expressed genes showed that downregulated genes were mainly concentrated in biological processes related to immune response regulation, inflammatory response, host defense, and cell activation, while upregulated genes were more involved in cell adhesion, extracellular matrix remodeling, development, and structural reconstruction, reflecting the molecular characteristics of coexisting immunosuppression and tissue remodeling (Fig. 2C).

Overlap analysis showed that common genes accounted for a major proportion in both upregulated and downregulated gene sets, suggesting that core molecular programs remain stable under different conditions (Fig. 2D). Joint analysis of SMR effects and expression change directions revealed that most candidate molecules showed a certain degree of consistency or systematic deviation between genetic effect direction and expression change direction, suggesting that they may influence protein levels through genetic regulation, thereby participating in the regulation of disease-related molecular networks (Fig. 2E).

Immune cell remodeling and module-level associations across disease severity

Comparative analyses revealed a progressive remodeling of peripheral immune cell composition across control, low-level, and high-level disease states, with multiple innate and adaptive immune cell subsets showing significant alterations between groups (Fig. 3A). These changes were accompanied by coordinated shifts in age- and sex-adjusted immune profiles, indicating that disease severity is associated with systematic immune reprogramming rather than isolated cell-type perturbations .

Network-based modeling identified several co-expression modules whose overall activity differed significantly between disease states and controls (Fig. 3B). In particular, distinct modules exhibited consistent upregulation or downregulation in high-level and low-level conditions relative to controls, suggesting graded molecular responses linked to disease burden. Module trait correlation analysis further demonstrated that these modules were closely associated with specific immune cell populations and clinical variables, supporting their biological relevance (Fig. 3C). Integrated network analysis revealed marked heterogeneity across modules, with some modules displaying dense, highly interconnected cores and others showing sparser organization (SupFigure 1). Incorporation of PPI information strengthened biologically plausible interactions and highlighted highly connected hub genes. These results suggest distinct regulatory architectures among key modules and underscore the value of integrated co-expression–PPI networks for prioritizing functionally relevant genes. kME analysis revealed distinct hub gene compositions across modules, with each module characterized by a specific set of highly connected genes. These hub genes exhibited strong coherence with their respective module eigengenes, indicating stable intramodular organization and suggesting potential key regulatory roles within each co-expression module (SupFigure 2).

Differential module activity analysis showed that a subset of modules displayed stronger and more significant changes in the high-level condition compared with the low-level condition, whereas other modules were shared between both comparisons (Fig. 3D-E, SupFigure 3). Collectively, these results indicate that disease progression is characterized by both shared and severity-specific immune network alterations, highlighting key molecular programs that may underlie immune dysregulation across disease stages.

Regulatory origins of core genes and transcription factor-target relationships

Module-level enrichment analysis demonstrated that disease-associated transcriptional programs are organized into distinct co-expression modules with specific biological functions, with immune-related pathways dominating modules linked to the disease state and metabolic or biosynthetic processes enriched in control-associated modules (Fig. 4A). These findings indicate a structured reprogramming of gene expression rather than diffuse pathway activation. Analysis of regulatory activity revealed systematic differences in the balance of positive and negative regulation across modules, supporting widespread rewiring of transcriptional control in disease compared with controls (Fig. 4B). This shift suggests that altered regulator activity contributes to coordinated module-level expression changes. By integrating module membership with gene prioritization, the origins of core genes were traced back to specific co-expression modules, demonstrating that these key genes are not randomly distributed but instead arise from a limited subset of disease-relevant modules (Fig. 4C). This result establishes a direct link between higher-order module organization and the identification of core disease genes. Further regulatory network analysis identified transcription factors that directly target these core genes, defining explicit transcription factor–target relationships in which the targets correspond to the core genes (Fig. 4D).

Association of WFDC2 with immune composition, pathway activity, and clinical levels

Correlation analysis revealed that WFDC2 expression was significantly associated with multiple immune cell populations, showing positive correlations with several T-cell and myeloid subsets and negative correlations with selected innate immune populations, with these relationships remaining robust after multiple-testing adjustment (Fig. 5A). This pattern indicates that WFDC2 is closely linked to immune compositional changes rather than isolated cell-type effects. Pathway-level analysis demonstrated that WFDC2-associated transcriptional signatures were strongly enriched for biological processes related to chromatin remodeling, antiviral defense, RNA processing, ribosome biogenesis, stress responses, and immune regulation, with consistent enrichment observed across both correlation-based GSEA and group-based GSEA comparisons (Fig. 5B). These results suggest that WFDC2 is embedded in coordinated molecular programs that integrate immune activation with fundamental transcriptional and translational control mechanisms. Successful overexpression of WFDC2 in Jurkat cells

To investigate the functional role of WFDC2, a WFDC2 overexpression model was generated in Jurkat cells. Western blot analysis demonstrated that WFDC2 protein expression was markedly increased in cells transfected with the WFDC2 overexpression construct compared with the corresponding control group (Fig. 7C-D).

WFDC2 modulated apoptosis-related proteins in infected Jurkat cells

To further explore the molecular changes associated with the anti-apoptotic effect of WFDC2, apoptosis-related proteins were analyzed by Western blot. As shown in Fig. 7E-F, WFDC2 overexpression significantly decreased BAX and cleaved caspase-3 levels and increased BCL-2 expression compared with infected cells, while the empty vector control showed no significant improvement. Together, these findings indicated that WFDC2 overexpression may protect Jurkat cells against infection-associated apoptosis by modulating the BCL-2/BAX/caspase-3 signaling axis.

WFDC2 overexpression attenuated apoptosis in infected Jurkat cells

To determine whether WFDC2 affects apoptosis in the HIV-related setting, Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry was performed. The gating strategy used for apoptosis analysis is shown in Fig. 7G. WFDC2 overexpression significantly reduced the apoptotic rate compared with the infection group, whereas empty vector transfection did not produce a comparable protective effect (Fig. 7H). These results suggested that WFDC2 overexpression alleviated infection-associated apoptosis in Jurkat cells.

Discussion

HIV infection is a complex pathological process involving viral replication, immune cell damage, and dysregulation of gene expression. Deciphering its molecular regulatory mechanisms is of great significance for developing novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets [31–34]. In this study, we integrated multi-omics data including transcriptomics, proteomics, and miRNAomics, combined with bioinformatic approaches such as weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR). We systematically characterized the molecular alterations associated with HIV infection under different viral load states, identified WFDC2 as a pivotal hub regulatory gene, and preliminarily validated its function and regulatory network. These findings provide new insights into the pathogenesis of HIV infection.

Immune cells serve as the primary targets of HIV infection and the core of immune defense, and their compositional changes directly impact disease progression [35–37]. Our study showed significant differences in the abundance of multiple immune cell subsets between people living with HIV and healthy controls. Notably, resting memory CD4+T cells were markedly downregulated in the high viral load (HVL) group (Fig. 3A), which is consistent with the well-established understanding that HIV primarily targets CD4+T cells [38–40]. Natural killer (NK) cells, as critical components of the innate immune system, can directly eliminate HIV-infected cells [41]. The significant reduction in NK cell abundance in the HVL group may impair the body’s capacity to clear the virus, thereby facilitating viral replication. Additionally, neutrophil abundance was elevated in the HVL group. Neutrophils can participate in immune pathological damage by releasing inflammatory factors, which may be associated with HIV infection-related chronic inflammatory responses [42, 43]. Collectively, these results demonstrate that HIV infection induces remodeling of immune cell composition [44], and this remodeling is closely linked to viral load [45]. The immune cell damage was milder in the low viral load (LVL) group, whereas it was more severe in the HVL group. These findings provide a basis for understanding the immunopathological mechanisms underlying HIV infection.

Differential expression analysis indicated that a large number of genes were differentially expressed between people living with HIV and the control group(Fig. 2B). Moreover, the gene expression changes exhibited a high degree of consistency between different viral load groups (r = 0.96), suggesting that these genes may be involved in the core pathological processes of HIV infection. Functional enrichment analysis (Fig. 2C) showed that the upregulated DEGs were mainly enriched in immune-related pathways such as regulation of innate immune response and viral defense response, indicating that the body initiates immune defense mechanisms to resist viral invasion following HIV infection [46]. In contrast, the downregulated DEGs (Fig. 2C) were primarily enriched in pathways including embryonic organ development and synapse assembly, which may be associated with cell function impairment induced by HIV infection [47].

WGCNA constructed 17 co-expression modules, among which Module 8 showed a strong negative correlation with HIV viral load(Fig. 3C). The genes in Module 8 were enriched in pathways such as antiviral defense and RNA processing, suggesting that Module 8 may represent a core functional module associated with HIV infection. Transcription factor regulon analysis revealed that several key transcription factors (e.g., NR2F1, IKZF1) were present in Module 8. These transcription factors participate in the HIV infection process by regulating the expression of their target genes. NR2F1 has been confirmed to be involved in immune cell differentiation [48, 49] and viral replication regulation [50, 51], while IKZF1 plays an important role in B cell development [52] and innate immune responses [53]. The enrichment of these transcription factors in Module 8 suggests that they may affect the progression of HIV infection by regulating the expression of genes within the module.

SMR analysis integrated with cis-protein quantitative trait locus (cis-pQTL) and HIV genome-wide association study (GWAS) data effectively excluded the interference of confounding factors and identified 8 hub genes (Fig. 2E) with causal associations with HIV infection. Among these genes, WFDC2 was significantly downregulated in people living with HIV, and this expression pattern was consistent with the direction of SMR signals, suggesting that WFDC2 may act as a key regulatory gene in HIV infection. As a member of the WAP domain-containing protein family, WFDC2 possesses innate immune functions such as antibacterial and anti-inflammatory activities [53–55]. Its downregulation in HIV infection may compromise the body’s innate immune defense capacity, thereby promoting viral replication. This is consistent with our finding that antiviral pathways were significantly downregulated in samples with high WFDC2 expression(Fig. 5B).

In this study, we verified the functional and clinical significance of WFDC2 in HIV infection from multiple dimensions. First, WFDC2 expression levels were significantly positively correlated with the abundance of multiple immune cell subsets (e.g., resting memory CD4⁺ T cells, NK cells) and significantly negatively correlated with neutrophil abundance(Fig. 5A). Nicole E. James et al. [56] clearly confirmed that WFDC2 expression is significantly positively correlated with the abundance of multiple immune cell subsets and significantly negatively correlated with neutrophil abundance through immune cell deconvolution analysis of the TCGA dataset. These results indicate that WFDC2 may be involved in the differentiation and functional regulation of immune cells, and its downregulation may lead to immune cell dysfunction, exacerbating the immune damage caused by HIV infection. Second, functional enrichment analysis showed that pathways such as antiviral defense and RNA processing were significantly downregulated in samples with high WFDC2 expression, suggesting that WFDC2 may participate in the pathological processes of HIV infection by regulating these pathways. The downregulation of WFDC2 may relieve the inhibition of these pathways, triggering the body’s antiviral defense response; however, excessive downregulation may lead to pathway dysfunction, which in turn promotes viral replication.

ELISA validation(Fig. 5C)demonstrated that plasma WFDC2 protein levels in untreated people living with HIV were significantly lower than those in healthy controls. Following antiretroviral therapy (ART), plasma WFDC2 levels slightly increased but remained below normal levels, indicating that WFDC2 expression levels are closely associated with HIV infection status and may serve as a potential biomarker for HIV infection. Furthermore, as a secreted protein, WFDC2 is readily detectable in plasma, which confers clinical applicability and highlights its potential utility for early diagnosis of HIV infection and evaluation of treatment efficacy.

As important post-transcriptional regulators, miRNAs play a key role in HIV infection [57, 58]. In this study, we identified multiple HIV infection-related differentially expressed miRNAs (DE-miRNAs), whose target genes were enriched in functional pathways such as innate immune response and apoptosis regulation [59, 60], . The constructed miRNA-mRNA regulatory network indicated that core DE-miRNAs such as miR-182-5p and miR-320a may target hub genes including WFDC2 to participate in HIV infection regulation. miR-182-5p was significantly upregulated in people living with HIV, and it may be involved in HIV infection regulation by inhibiting WFDC2 expression. Previous studies have shown that miR-182-5p can promote viral replication by regulating the expression of target genes [61]. Its enrichment in our study suggests that it may exacerbate immune damage induced by HIV infection by targeting WFDC2, providing new clues for understanding the post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms of HIV infection.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively limited, especially the small number of controls in the RNA-seq dataset, which may have reduced statistical power, increased variability, and affected the robustness and generalizability of the findings. Although our integrative multi-omics framework partially mitigates this issue by combining differential expression analysis, WGCNA, TF regulon analysis, SMR/pQTL-based prioritization, and experimental validation, this strategy does not fully overcome the limitations associated with a modest sample size. Second, the SMR analysis was based on plasma pQTL data, whereas the transcriptomic analyses were derived from peripheral blood cells. Although both are blood-derived, circulating protein abundance may not directly correspond to mRNA expression in blood cells because it can be influenced by secretion, protein turnover, systemic physiological states, and contributions from multiple tissues or cell types. Therefore, this cross-platform integration should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, although we provided bioinformatic evidence and in vitro validation supporting the potential role of WFDC2, we did not perform in vivo animal experiments or more detailed mechanistic studies to verify its regulatory mechanisms. This study is that the inferred TF-target relationships were derived from promoter motif scanning, XGBoost-based expression modeling, and TF-target correlation analysis without formal benchmarking against external curated regulatory resources such as ENCODE or ChIP-seq datasets, and should therefore be interpreted as putative rather than experimentally validated direct regulatory interactions. Finally, the interactions between WFDC2 and other hub genes were not investigated in depth, and the precise regulatory position of WFDC2 within the molecular network of HIV infection remains to be further clarified. Future studies will expand the sample size, and use cell experiments and animal models to thoroughly validate the function and regulatory mechanisms of WFDC2, and explore its feasibility as a therapeutic target for HIV infection.

Conclusion

In this study, through integrated multi-omics analysis, we systematically revealed the changes in immune cell composition and the molecular regulatory network associated with HIV infection under different viral load states, and identified WFDC2 as a key regulatory gene. WFDC2 was significantly downregulated in people living with HIV, and its expression levels were closely correlated with immune cell composition and antiviral pathways. Moreover, plasma WFDC2 protein levels may serve as a potential biomarker for HIV infection. In addition, DE-miRNAs such as miR-182-5p may participate in HIV infection regulation by targeting WFDC2. These findings provide new molecular targets for in-depth understanding of the pathogenesis of HIV infection, and lay a theoretical foundation for optimizing clinical diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.
