Unequal contributions in global allergy research: A comparative study of high-, low-, and middle-income countries☆
Authors: Andrés Noyola-Pérez, Sandra Nora Gonzalez-Diaz, Carlos Macouzet-Sanchez, Alejandra Macías-Weinnmann, Rosa I. Guzmán-Avilán, Cindy E. de Lira-Quezada, Ana K. Chavez-Ruiz, Mario Morais-Almeida, Byran Martin, Ignacio J. Ansotegui, Mariana Castillo-Rubio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionGlobal disparities in scientific research disproportionately favor high-income countries (HICs), limiting contributions from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In allergology, this inequity restricts diverse perspectives and constrains advancements in addressing allergic diseases, which disproportionately affects underserved populations.MethodsThis study analyzed the contributions of 41 allergy journals listed in the 2024 Journal Citation Reports. Data on publication volume, citation impact, article processing charges (APCs), and editorial board representation were collected. Countries were classified as HICs or LMICs based on World Bank rankings. Descriptive statistics and comparative analyses were used to evaluate the disparities between HICs and LMICs.ResultsLMICs accounted for only 18.6% of the total publications, compared to a median of 85% from HICs, and only 1 journal included a low-income country among its top contributors. Journals received contributions from a median of 8 HICs versus 2 LMICs. APCs presented a median cost of 3090 USD, posing a significant financial barrier. Geographic alignment was observed between editorial boards and contributing countries, further limiting LMIC representation.ConclusionSystemic barriers, including high APCs numbers and underrepresentation on editorial boards, limit the contribution in global allergology. Addressing these disparities requires reducing financial obstacles, expanding capacity-building initiatives, and ensuring equitable editorial representation. Creating inclusive research ecosystems can enhance global knowledge dissemination and improve outcomes for populations disproportionately affected by allergic diseases.

Introduction

Global disparities in scientific research have long been a concern, with high-income countries (HICs) contributing disproportionately to both the volume and impact of publications compared to low-and middle-income countries (LMICs).1 These inequities are evident in many fields, including allergology, in which the contributions of LMIC researchers remain underexplored, mainly through fewer publications from underrepresented regions, lower citation rates, and striking absence from top-tier journals.2 Such disparities diminish the diversity of research perspectives and constrain progress in understanding and addressing allergic diseases affecting populations worldwide. Addressing these inequities requires a thorough examination of the distribution of research output and structural barriers such as prohibitive publication costs, which perpetuate the underrepresentation of LMIC researchers. Materials and methods

We analyzed all the allergy journals listed in the 2024 Journal Citation Reports. We examined the top 10 contributing countries for each journal over the past 3 years to ensure a manageable dataset. Countries were classified as HICs or LMICs according to the World Bank Ranking.3 Two independent reviewers gathered details, such as total citations per journal, number of papers associated with each top-contributing country, impact factor, publisher information, geographic distribution, and article processing charges (APCs). Any discrepancies in the data extraction were resolved through discussion.

Descriptive statistics, including medians and interquartile ranges, described overall trends, and appropriate tests were applied to determine whether differences between HICs and LMICs contributions were statistically significant. These analyses focused on discerning patterns in publication volume, citation impact, and potential barriers that may limit the representation of LMICs in global allergology.

Results

Our analysis included 41 allergy journals, which had a median JIF of 2.8 (IQR 1.1–5.9) and a median total citation count of 2316 (IQR 403–2590). Article Processing Charges presented a median cost of 3090 USD (IQR 1744–4000). Geographic analysis revealed a marked imbalance in the contributions of HICs and LMICs. Journals received contributions from a median of 8 HICs (IQR, 6–9) compared to the 2 LMICs (IQR, 1–3). Notably, only 1 journal (2.4%) included a low-income country among its top 10 contributors.

Self-citation was prevalent, with 24 journals (58.5%) most frequently citing their own publications. Furthermore, 28 journals (68.3%) were affiliated with a specific organization, and in 23 of these cases (56.1%), the top-contributing country corresponded to the organization's home country, suggesting geographic alignment.

HICs contributed a median of 216 articles per journal (IQR 109–426), significantly exceeding the median of 44 (IQR 5–95) from LMICs (p < 0.001). On average, LMICs accounted for 18.6% of the total articles published per journal, compared with 85% of HICs. Discussion

These findings highlight significant disparities in global allergology research, with LMICs contributing far fewer publications than HICs do. Our analysis revealed that LMICs accounted for only 18% of the total articles per journal, with only 1 journal, including a low-income country, being among the top contributors. This underrepresentation stems from systemic barriers such as limited funding,4 insufficient research infrastructure,5 and fewer training opportunities6 in these regions. High APCs, with a median cost of 3090 USD, further exacerbate these challenges by placing high-impact publication venues out of reach for many LMIC researchers.7

In addition to funding limitations, editorial boards play a pivotal role as gatekeepers for scientific dissemination. Our analysis highlights a troubling pattern of geographic alignment, where the home country of a journal's editorial board often correlates with its top contributing country. This unequal representation limits the inclusion of diverse perspectives, particularly in regions in which allergic diseases may impose a significant health burden. Equitable representation on these boards is critical for addressing the needs of LMICs, particularly given that over 75% of the world's population resides in these regions.8 Studies across disciplines have shown that underrepresentation on editorial boards perpetuates regional biases in research priorities and funding, limiting the global impact of scientific advances.9, 10, 11

Addressing these disparities requires systemic changes to the research ecosystem. Waiving for or reducing APCs for LMIC researchers would lower financial barriers and enable wider participation in high-impact journals. Encouragingly, some publishers have begun offering discounted fees or open-access publishing options; however, such initiatives have remained unevenly implemented. However, as highlighted in recent studies, APC waiver programs often fail to address the systemic barriers faced by LMIC researchers,12 such as burdensome application processes and insufficient communication about eligibility criteria, further limiting their effectiveness in promoting equity. These efforts must be complemented by broader strategies to build research capacity and ensure equitable participation at every ecosystem level.

Investments in capacity building are another critical avenue. Training programs, research grants, and international collaborations can empower LMIC research teams with the skills and resources needed to meaningfully contribute to global allergology.13 Funding agencies have a unique opportunity to drive equity by incentivizing partnerships between HIC and LMIC researchers.14 Encouraging collaborations that strengthen local expertise and incorporate regional knowledge leads to research outcomes that are both groundbreaking and applicable worldwide.15

This study underscores the systemic challenges faced by LMIC researchers, from financial barriers to underrepresentation of their perspectives in editorial decisions. However, these disparities present opportunities for further transformation. Establishing a more inclusive allergology research ecosystem could provide new insights into allergic diseases, particularly in LMICs, where the burden of disease may be overlooked. By aligning research priorities with global health needs and fostering equitable collaboration, allergology can evolve into a field that serves populations worldwide more effectively.

Ultimately, addressing these imbalances is more than fairness; it is about advancing science. Including LMIC perspectives enriches the research landscape, ensuring that findings are attuned to the diverse cultural, environmental, and genetic factors that shape allergic diseases. An inclusive approach to research dissemination and collaboration can drive progress in prevention, diagnosis, and treatment strategies, particularly in underserved areas, where the need is greatest.

Conclusions

Addressing systemic inequities in global allergology research is essential for building a more inclusive and impactful scientific ecosystem. By reducing financial barriers, diversifying editorial boards, and promoting equitable collaborations, LMIC researchers can meaningfully contribute to the field. Such efforts not only enhance research diversity but also ensure that global health solutions are more representative and effective, particularly for underserved populations facing significant allergic disease burdens.
