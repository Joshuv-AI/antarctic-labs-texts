A cross-sectional study on discordances between Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR and BACTEC MGIT 960 system for assessing susceptibility for Mycobacterium tuberculosis: analysis of clinical specimens from patients with isoniazid-monoresistant, rifampicin-monoresistant, and multidrug-resistant tuberculosis
Authors: Kanphai Wongjarit, Pattama Torvorapanit, Suthidee Petsong, Ajcharaporn Sawatpanich, Waritta Sawaengdee, Panadda Dhepakson, Suwatchareeporn Rotcheewaphan, Gompol Suwanpimolkul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRapid molecular tests have facilitated early diagnosis and guided treatment of tuberculosis (TB) by providing faster results than the gold-standard phenotypic drug susceptibility testing (DST). Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR is an RDT available in Thailand that simultaneously detects Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) complex and mutations associated with isoniazid (INH) and rifampicin (RIF) resistance, enabling genotypic DST in various clinical specimens. However, discordance between the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR and phenotypic DST creates a decision-making dilemma.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted on clinical and microbiological data from patients with different types of tuberculosis (TB) at King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital (KCMH), a referral hospital for TB in Thailand. The study focused on patients with drug-susceptible TB (DS-TB), isoniazid mono-resistant TB (Hr-TB), rifampicin mono-resistant TB (RR-TB), and multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) from January 2018 to June 2023. The correlation between genotypic and phenotypic DST using the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR and BACTEC MGIT 960 assays, respectively, was investigated using clinical specimens and MTB clinical isolates recovered from specimens collected during the same period.Results836 MTB clinical isolates from 796 patients had genotypic and phenotypic DST results. 44 MTB (5.2%) clinical isolates had discordant results. There was a significant discordance between the assays in assessing INH (p-value < 0.001) and RIF susceptibility (p-value < 0.022). The Cohen’s kappa coefficient for assessing INH and RIF susceptibility was 0.837 and 0.772, respectively. The majority of INH susceptibility discordances (29/32, 90.6%) displayed genotypic susceptibility but phenotypic resistance. RIF susceptibility discordances (11/13, 84.6%) displayed genotypic resistance but phenotypic susceptibility. 52.2% were undertreated, 2.3% were overtreated, and 40.9% experienced no change in treatment in patients with genotypic-phenotypic discordant DST results. Undertreatment was due to false-negative genotypic DST. 6/11 RIF-susceptibility discordant isolates were susceptible by phenotypic DST; mutations in the rpoB gene were identified: Leu430Pro, Asp435Tyr, or Leu452Pro.ConclusionsThe Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR assay demonstrated high agreement with the BACTEC MGIT 960 assay; however, both assays yielded substantial discordance in resistance results. Most of these discordances were false-negative genotypic results for INH resistance, which could lead to undertreatment decisions. Furthermore, some instances of genotypic resistance but phenotypic susceptibility to RIF were associated with borderline rpoB mutations, highlighting the need for careful interpretation of discordant results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12941-026-00864-5.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the leading infectious causes of death, and drug-resistant tuberculosis (DR-TB) poses a major threat to Thailand and global TB control, with progress toward the World Health Organization (WHO) End TB Strategy falling behind targets [1, 2]. In 2023, an estimated 400,000 cases of multidrug- or rifampicin-resistant TB were reported worldwide, resulting in approximately 150,000 deaths, while only about 44% of affected individuals received appropriate treatment, highlighting substantial gaps in diagnosis and management [3]. To address this challenge, rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) have been developed and widely implemented for the early detection of Mycobacterium tuberculosis and drug-resistance mutations. These technologies include nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) targeting resistance to first-line drugs, rifampicin (RIF) and isoniazid (INH), and next-generation sequencing (NGS) approaches for comprehensive resistance profiling [2, 3]. The feasibility of RDTs has facilitated earlier diagnosis and guided treatment, with faster results compared to the gold-standard phenotypic drug susceptibility testing (DST), potentially resulting in better control of DR-TB [4–6].

Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR is an available RDT in Thailand that can simultaneously detect Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) complex and mutations associated with INH and RIF resistance in various clinical specimens [7]. However, discordance between the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR and phenotypic DST creates a decision-making dilemma.

Addressing the problematic phenotypic-genotypic discordance in INH and RIF susceptibility is imperative, yet the existing evidence on its impact on clinical decision-making remains limited [6, 8–10]. Furthermore, most of the existing literature primarily focuses on RIF discordance, especially in the Xpert MTB/RIF assay; however, it cannot detect INH resistance, a prevalent concern in Thailand [6, 11]. Additionally, most studies rely on the analysis of clinical isolates rather than directly comparing genotypic DST results from clinical specimens to phenotypic DST results. Therefore, our study aims to evaluate the correlation between genotypic and phenotypic DST using the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR and BACTEC MGIT 960 assays to assess INH and RIF resistance in various clinical specimens, and to assess the impact of discordant results on treatment decisions.

Materials and methods

Study population and Mycobacterium tuberculosis clinical isolates

A cross-sectional study was conducted on patients with drug-susceptible TB (DS-TB), isoniazid mono-resistant TB (Hr-TB), rifampicin mono-resistant TB (RR-TB), and multidrug- resistant TB (MDR-TB) at King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital (KCMH), a tuberculosis referral hospital in Thailand, from January 2018 to June 2023. Data on clinical characteristics, including gender, age at diagnosis, type of infection, and history of tuberculosis, were collected. Microbiological data, including type of specimen, AFB staining, Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR results (Seegene Technologies, Seoul, Korea), and phenotypic DST results by BACTEC MGIT 960 (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), tested as part of the routine service of our hospital, were reviewed.

A total of 1,641 MTB clinical isolates recovered from 1,373 patients with TB were initially included in the study, as shown in Fig. 1. Duplicated clinical isolates of the same specimen type from the same patient were excluded. Among these, 836 MTB clinical isolates from 796 patients with both genotypic and phenotypic DST results available were included in the analysis.

The correlation between genotypic and phenotypic DST was investigated using the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR and BACTEC MGIT 960, respectively. This analysis involved clinical specimens and MTB clinical isolates recovered from the specimens obtained in the same period, with no more than 4 weeks between collections. We further categorized comparisons as follows: concordant results, discordant results leading to undertreatment, discordant results leading to overtreatment, and discordant results with no effect on treatment decisions. Undertreatment was defined as first-line DS-TB regimens initially prescribed to patients based on genotypic DST results obtained from the clinical specimen, followed by a subsequent regimen change by physicians based on phenotypic DST results showing INH and/or RIF resistance. Over treatment was defined as DR-TB regimens initially prescribed based on genotypic DST results obtained from the clinical specimen, followed by a subsequent change in the regimen by physicians based on phenotypic DST results showing susceptibility to INH and/or RIF.

Genotypic susceptibility testing

The Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR assay is designed for the identification of M. tuberculosis (IS6110 and MPT64) and mutations associated with INH resistance (katG: S315I, S315N, and S315T; inhA promoter region: C-15T, T-8 C, and T-8 A) and RIF resistance (rpoB: codon 511–533) [7]. The assay was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions, with melting-curve analysis and interpretation conducted using Seegene Viewer software, version 2.0 (Seegene Technologies, Seoul, Korea).

Phenotypic susceptibility testing

The susceptibility of MTB to INH and RIF was assessed using culture-based phenotypic DST with the BACTEC MGIT 960 system, which acted as the reference method for this study. The MGIT 960 SIRE kit (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This kit contains the following concentrations of antibiotics: streptomycin (STM) at 1.0 µg/mL, INH at 0.1 µg/mL, RIF at 1.0 µg/mL, and ethambutol (EMB) at 5.0 µg/mL. INH and RIF susceptibility were interpreted based on clinical breakpoints using the WHO’s critical concentrations [12].

The clinical isolates were tested with a high-dose INH (0.4 µg/mL) using the BACTEC MGIT 960 INH 0.4 (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) for DST, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

For MTB clinical isolates selected for whole-genome sequencing (WGS), phenotypic DST was also performed using the broth microdilution method (BMD) with a custom SENSITITRE THAMYCO plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). This plate contained 12 antibiotics in twofold serial dilutions: RIF, INH, EMB, levofloxacin (LFX), moxifloxacin (MFX), bedaquiline (BDQ), linezolid (LZD), clofazimine (CFZ), kanamycin (KAN), amikacin (AMK), ethionamide (ETO), and capreomycin (CAP). The procedure was carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions. MIC results (mg/L) were interpreted using CLSI breakpoints and epidemiological cut-off values (ECOFF/ECVs) as defined by the CRyPTIC consortium [13, 14].

WGS and bioinformatics analysis

Only MTB clinical isolates exhibiting RIF-discordance, suspected of harboring borderline rpoB mutations (genotypic DST resistance/phenotypic DST susceptibility, GR/PS) between the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR and BACTEC MGIT 960 assays, were included in the analysis.

Genomic DNA was extracted from bacterial colonies using the ZymoBIOMICS DNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Genomic DNA libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. WGS was performed using a 2 × 150 paired-end configuration on the Illumina NovaSeq 1000 or NovaSeq 6000 platforms.

The FASTQ sequences were quality-assessed with FASTQC v0.11.9 [15], followed by adapter and quality trimming (Phred score > 30) using Trimmomatic v0.39 [16]. De novo assembly was conducted using Unicycler v0.5.1 [17], and the quality and completeness of the assembled genomes were assessed using QUAST v5.2.0 [18] and CheckM v1.2.3 [19] using M. tuberculosis H37Rv (NC_000962.3) as the reference genome. Only assemblies that have over 99% completeness and are free from contamination were selected for further analysis.

MTB identification, lineage determination, and susceptibility predictions were performed using TB-Profiler v6.6.0 [20]. Borderline rpoB mutations were defined according to the WHO catalogue of mutations in MTB and their association with drug resistance, including Leu430Pro, Asp435Tyr, His445Asn, His445Leu, His445Ser, Leu452Pro, and Ile491Phe (MTB numbering system) [21, 22].

Ethical statement

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University (COA No. 0761/2023) in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and by the Institutional Biosafety Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University (MDCU-IBC No. 045/2024). All participants provided written informed consent before any procedures were performed.

Statistical analysis

McNemar’s test formula was used to determine the sample size needed to obtain a representative sample for testing two dependent proportions. To evaluate the accuracy of INH and RIF resistance, we compared the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR and BACTEC MGIT 960 assays. Using the discordant test proportions of 0.0645 and 0.147 from a previous study [23], we calculated that this study would require 242 samples to detect a significant difference, with type I and type II error rates set at 0.05 and 0.2, respectively.

The accuracy and agreement between Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR and BACTEC MGIT 960 assays were assessed by McNemar’s test, Cohen’s kappa coefficient, and agreement rate. The diagnostic performance of the assays was assessed using sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows, version 29.0.1 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Patients and MTB clinical isolates

During the study period from January 2018 to June 2023, a total of 836 MTB clinical isolates from 796 patients with both genotypic and phenotypic DST results available were identified. Characteristics of patients and MTB clinical isolates are summarized in Supplemental Tables 1 and 2. Of the 836 MTB clinical isolates, 103 (12.3%) were INH-resistant isolates, 2 (0.2%) were RIF-resistant isolates, 23 (2.8%) were INH- and RIF-resistant isolates, and 708 (84.7%) were INH- and RIF-susceptible isolates, based on the BACTEC MGIT 960 assay.

Among these, 44 MTB clinical isolates showed discordant susceptibility results between the BACTEC MGIT 960 and the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR assays, as determined from the clinical specimen; the discordance rate was 5.2%. The discrepancies are outlined in Table 1, with a notable observation that the majority of INH susceptibility discordances (29 of 32, 90.6%) displayed genotypic susceptibility but phenotypic resistance. In contrast, RIF susceptibility discordances (11 of 13, 84.6%) displayed genotypic resistance but phenotypic susceptibility.

Comparison of assays for the detection of INH and RIF susceptibility

Among the 836 MTB clinical isolates with both genotypic and phenotypic DST results available for analysis, McNemar’s test revealed a statistically significant discordance between the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR and BACTEC MGIT 960 assays in assessing INH and RIF susceptibility, with p-values of < 0.001 and 0.022, respectively. The Cohen’s kappa coefficient indicated strong agreement between the assays: κ = 0.837 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.782–0.892) for INH susceptibility, signifying almost perfect agreement, and κ = 0.772 (95% CI 0.652–0.892) for RIF susceptibility, indicating substantial agreement. The agreement between the assays is summarized in Table 1.

Diagnostic performances of the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR assay compared to the BACTEC MGIT 960 assay for assessing INH and RIF susceptibility

The sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of the assay for detecting INH resistance were 77.0% (95% CI 69.1–83.7), 99.6% (95% CI 98.9–99.9), 97.0%, and 96.1%, respectively. Meanwhile, for detecting RIF resistance, the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were 92.0% (95% CI 77.3–98.6), 98.6% (95% CI 97.7–99.3), 67.6%, and 99.8%, respectively. Borderline rpoB mutations in RIF-discordant isolates

A total of 11 MTB clinical isolates that were RIF-discordant, exhibiting genotypic resistance but phenotypic susceptibility (GR/PS) on DST, were further investigated using WGS and BMD. Among these, 6 isolates harbored borderline rpoB mutations, including Leu430Pro (n = 3), Asp435Tyr (n = 1), and Leu452Pro (n = 2) according to the MTB numbering system. Three isolates showed no detectable rpoB mutations, while data for two isolates were unavailable due to poor growth on subculture. The majority (5 of 6) of the isolates with borderline rpoB mutations belonged to lineage 2.2.1 (Beijing sublineage), while one isolate belonged to lineage 2.1 (Proto-Beijing lineage). BMD testing for RIF indicated resistance in 3 of 6 isolates with borderline rpoB mutations, based on the CLSI M24S breakpoints (MIC ≥ 1 mg/L). The effect of discordant results on physicians’ treatment decisions

Among patients with genotypic-phenotypic discordant DST results, these discordant results led to under treatment in 52.2% (23 of 44), overtreatment in 2.3% (1 of 44), and no change in treatment in 45.5% (20 of 44). The impact of INH and RIF susceptibility discordance is summarized in Supplemental Fig. 1. Notably, 76.0% of INH-discordant cases exhibited genotypic susceptibility but phenotypic resistance (GS/PR), leading to a subsequent treatment change to the RZEL regimen based on phenotypic susceptibility results.

Discussion

In this study, to highlight the applicability to the real-world clinical setting, we assess the correlation between genotypic and phenotypic DST using clinical specimens, with a focus on its influence on treatment decisions. We compare results from the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR assay for clinical specimens with those from the BACTEC MGIT 960 assay for MTB clinical isolates recovered during the same period. A discordance prevalence of 5.2% was observed with statistically significant discrepancies between the two assays: p-value of < 0.001 and 0.022 for INH and RIF susceptibility, respectively. The agreements for the detection of INH and RIF resistance were perfect and in substantial agreement with k values of 0.837 and 0.772, respectively. Overall, the diagnostic performance was high, with sensitivity and specificity, comparable to the previous literature [23–25]. These findings support the assays’ diagnostic utility in guiding the treatment decision.

We identified a significant number of discrepancies in which there were discordant results: genotypic susceptibility to INH paired with phenotypic resistance. This finding aligns with studies conducted in Thailand by Chumpa et al. [23], and in Italy by Sali et al. [25]. Mutations located outside the katG and inhA promoter regions—such as those in oxyR-ahpC, fabG1, and furA—are not detected by the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR assay and may explain the discordant INH results [26, 27].

Unexpectedly, we also identified a subset of discordant results with genotypic resistance but phenotypic susceptibility to RIF, a type of discordance not previously reported in the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR assay [23–25]. The differences could be explained by variations in the defined study population and the prevalence of DR-TB. We further confirmed that this phenomenon is partly explained by borderline rpoB mutations—Leu430Pro, Asp435Tyr, and Leu452Pro—which are associated with low-level RIF resistance, as characterized by WGS and BMD. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating that the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR assay can detect these mutations, which phenotypic DST missed. This finding aligns with an issue previously addressed in the classic Xpert MTB/RIF and Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assays [11, 28–30]. Moreover, this underscores a key strength of the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR assay, as some borderline rpoB mutations, especially Leu452Pro, have also been reported to be missed by the Hain Genotype MTBDRplus V1 and the classic Xpert MTB/RIF assay [31].

Discordant results between RDT and phenotypic DST should be considered in clinical decision-making [6, 8]. Our study emphasizes the indispensability of both assays, as they cannot be replaced and should be used simultaneously at the time of tuberculosis diagnosis. Clinicians need to be vigilant about potential false-negative genotypic INH results initially reported. Treatment may need to be adjusted based on subsequent phenotypic susceptibility results, with consideration given to a 6-month (H)REZ-Lfx regimen, per the WHO recommendation [32]. Even though the sensitivity of 77.0% for detecting INH resistance with the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR assay was low, the PPV remained high as at 95.7–100%. This indicates that if the assay reveals INH resistance, clinicians can initially treat suspected Hr-TB patients with the RZEL regimen without relying on phenotypic DST results. In contrast to RIF susceptibility, the PPV was relatively low, ranging from 66.7 to 70%. Clinicians need to be aware of the limitations of relying solely on genotypic resistance for selecting treatment regimens, especially in areas with low prevalence of RIF resistance or in patients without risk factors for RR- or MDR-TB. Mutations associated with low-level RIF resistance should also be considered in certain cases of discordant RIF susceptibility results. Further WGS or BMD may be required to solve discordance before making a final treatment decision [31].

In this study, the prevalence of Hr- and MDR-TB was 12.1% and 2.6%, respectively. KatG mutations associated with high-level INH resistance were detected in 7.2% of the 834 clinical specimens. The prevalence of DR-TB and the katG gene mutation among TB patients was similar to the previous reports [1, 33–35]. In addition, most of the katG gene mutations (95.0%) detected by the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR assay conferred high-level INH resistance as determined by the BACTEC MGIT 960 INH 0.4 assay, with a sensitivity of 74.1% (95% CI 61.4–84.5) and a PPV of 93.0%, reflecting good predictive properties for high-level phenotypic resistance. However, the detection of the inhA gene mutation did not always confer the low-level resistance, with a relatively lower sensitivity of 50.8% (95% CI 38.3–63.4). To our knowledge, this study is the first direct comparisonof the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR assay and the BACTEC MGIT 960 assay for characterizing low- and high-level INH resistance.

The strength of our study lies in the comprehensive head-to-head analysis between the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR and the BACTEC MGIT 960 assay in an endemic country. We directly compared genotypic DST results from clinical specimens to phenotypic DST results, as performed in a real-world clinical setting. To date, this study is the largest to investigate the discrepancy between the two assays. We uncovered the discordant RIF susceptibility results coupled with the detection of borderline rpoB mutations associated with low-level resistance by WGS, which has not previously been documented in the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR assay.

Despite the robustness of this study, it has several limitations. Firstly, the retrospective nature of the study and its conduct during the COVID-19 pandemic era posed challenges in documenting patients’ outcomes, due to a considerable number of missing data and loss to follow-up. Secondly, WGS was not performed for all isolates, which may have led to some borderline rpoB mutations outside the rifampicin resistance-determining region (RRDR) being missed, and left the mechanism of INH discordance unexplained. Lastly, the study lacks a head-to-head comparison with widely used assays, such as Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra and Xpert MTB/XDR, for the analysis of clinical specimens.

Conclusions

In conclusion, these findings emphasize both the benefits and limitations of the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR assay as a rapid diagnostic test (RDT) for early diagnosis and prompt treatment of drug-resistant tuberculosis (DR-TB). While the Anyplex™ II MTB/MDR assay demonstrated strong alignment with the BACTEC MGIT 960 assay, significant discrepancies in isoniazid (INH) resistance and rifampicin (RIF) susceptibility highlight the necessity for careful interpretation of the results.
