Risk factor analysis and development of a prediction model for trismus after surgical treatment of buccal mucosa squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Liying Sun, Wei Liu, Lei Xu, Ziqiang Ma, Boyue Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBuccal mucosa squamous cell carcinoma (BMSCC) arises in a functionally complex anatomical region adjacent to the buccinator muscle, the anterior border of the masseter muscle, the pterygomandibular raphe, the retromolar region, and the pterygomandibular space. Tumor infiltration, extended resection, post-reconstruction scarring, and postoperative radiotherapy may affect the masticatory muscle complex and mandibular movement. Compared with other oral subsites, such as tongue and floor-of-mouth cancers, postoperative trismus after BMSCC has more pronounced subsite-specific characteristics. Evidence regarding independent risk factors and risk prediction tools for postoperative trismus in this subsite remains limited. This study aimed to identify independent risk factors for trismus at 12 months after surgery for BMSCC and to develop an integrated peri-treatment risk-prediction model.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed clinical data from 312 patients who underwent surgical treatment for BMSCC at our institution between January 2020 and June 2024. The primary outcome was trismus at 12 months after surgery, defined as a maximum interincisal opening (MIO) of < 35 mm. The collected data included demographic characteristics, exposure to risk factors, preoperative assessment parameters, surgery-related variables, postoperative pathological indicators, adjuvant treatment, and follow-up information. Two investigators independently extracted data using a prespecified electronic case report form; data consistency was assessed by repeated random sampling. Univariable analyses were used to describe associations between candidate factors and the outcome; candidate predictors for the main model were prespecified according to the number of events, clinical relevance, temporal sequence, and variable overlap. A multivariable logistic regression model was developed and evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, calibration curves, the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, bootstrap internal validation, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsAmong 312 patients, 98 (31.4%) developed trismus at 12 months after surgery, including 42 patients (42.9%) with mild trismus, 39 (39.8%) with moderate trismus, and 17 (17.3%) with severe trismus. Preoperative MIO was clinically comparable between the two groups (mean difference, -0.6 mm; 95% confidence interval (CI), -1.78 to 0.58; P = 0.312). Multivariable logistic regression showed that pathological depth of invasion (pDOI) ≥ 10 mm (odds ratio (OR) = 3.078, 95% CI, 1.756–5.392), subtotal or total masseter resection (OR = 4.288, 95% CI, 2.272–8.096), medial pterygoid muscle resection (OR = 2.680, 95% CI, 1.494–4.808), buccal mucosal defect area ≥ 15 cm² (OR = 2.402, 95% CI, 1.420–4.064), radiation dose ≥ 60 Gy (Gy) (OR = 2.252, 95% CI, 1.342–3.780), and masticatory muscle V50 ≥ 40% (percentage of masticatory muscle volume receiving ≥ 50 Gy; OR = 2.520, 95% CI, 1.368–4.644) were independent risk factors for postoperative trismus (all P < 0.05). The area under the curve (AUC) of the combined prediction model was 0.862 (95% CI, 0.820–0.904), and the bootstrap-corrected AUC was 0.848 (95% CI, 0.802–0.894). The Hosmer–Lemeshow test showed no evident lack of fit (χ² = 6.842, P = 0.554).ConclusionspDOI, masseter and medial pterygoid resection status, buccal mucosal defect area, radiation dose, and irradiated masticatory muscle volume were independent risk factors for trismus after surgical treatment of BMSCC. The integrated peri-treatment prediction model based on these factors demonstrated good discrimination and acceptable calibration in internal validation in this cohort and may serve as an adjunctive reference for identifying high-risk patients and developing function-preserving strategies. Its clinical applicability requires further validation in multicenter external cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09352-w.

Introduction

Buccal mucosa squamous cell carcinoma (BMSCC) is a common subtype of oral cancer, with a particularly high incidence in Asian populations, especially among individuals who chew betel quid [1]. The buccal mucosa is located on the lateral aspect of the oral vestibule and is adjacent to the buccinator muscle, the anterior border of the masseter muscle, the retromolar region, the pterygomandibular raphe, and the pterygomandibular space. When a tumor extends posteriorly or deeply, it often approaches or involves the masticatory muscle complex and related fascial structures [2]. Surgical resection is the mainstay of treatment for BMSCC. In patients with locally advanced disease or high-risk pathological features, postoperative radiotherapy or concurrent chemoradiotherapy may improve locoregional control but may also increase the risk of masticatory muscle fibrosis and scar contracture [3].

Previous studies of postoperative trismus have often combined multiple oral subsites, including tongue, floor-of-mouth, gingival, palatal, and buccal mucosa cancers, in a single analysis. Such mixed cohorts may be confounded by heterogeneity in tumor site, resection extent, reconstruction method, and radiotherapy target volume [4, 5]. Compared with tongue or floor-of-mouth cancers, BMSCC more frequently involves full-thickness buccinator resection, management of the retromolar region or pterygomandibular space, partial resection of the masseter or medial pterygoid muscle, and reconstruction of large buccal mucosal defects [6]. These treatment factors may directly alter mandibular movement pathways and increase the risk of postoperative trismus [7]. Therefore, analyzing BMSCC as a distinct entity may reduce heterogeneity across oral subsites and provide more directly applicable evidence for surgical extent planning, reconstruction strategy selection, radiotherapy plan optimization, and early rehabilitation in this patient population [8].

Trismus affects not only oral intake and speech but also oral hygiene, thereby increasing the risk of dental caries and periodontal disease. In severe cases, it may also interfere with subsequent oral examination and surveillance for tumor recurrence [9]. Previous studies have reported postoperative trismus rates of 5%–40% in patients with oral cancer, and its occurrence has been associated with tumor stage, surgical extent, and radiotherapy dose [10, 11]. However, systematic studies on risk factors for postoperative trismus specifically in BMSCC remain limited, and subsite-specific risk-stratification tools are lacking [12]. Because several key predictors are derived from surgery, pathology, and adjuvant treatment planning, the model developed in this study is intended as an integrated peri-treatment risk-assessment tool. Early identification of high-risk patients is clinically important for radiotherapy plan optimization, rehabilitation intensity planning, and follow-up planning. Therefore, this study retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of 312 patients who underwent surgery for BMSCC to identify independent risk factors for postoperative trismus and to develop a prediction model to support clinical decision-making.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This was a single-center retrospective study of patients who underwent surgical treatment for BMSCC at our institution between January 2020 and June 2024. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee; informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study. Data collection was completed on October 30, 2025.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pathologically confirmed BMSCC; (2) primary radical surgical treatment; (3) normal preoperative mouth opening (maximum interincisal opening [MIO] ≥ 35 mm); (4) complete data on the primary outcome and candidate predictors in the main model; and (5) postoperative follow-up of ≥ 12 months.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) previous oral and maxillofacial surgery or radiotherapy; (2) pre-existing trismus; (3) concurrent malignancy; (4) systemic disease affecting mouth-opening function, such as scleroderma, systemic lupus erythematosus, or other connective tissue diseases; (5) local recurrence or distant metastasis within 12 months after surgery; and (6) incomplete follow-up data or loss to follow-up, defined as unavailable maximum interincisal opening (MIO) measurement at the 12-month postoperative time point.

A total of 405 patients with BMSCC who underwent surgery were screened. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 312 patients were included in the final analysis. Ninety-three patients were excluded, including 18 with previous oral and maxillofacial surgery or radiotherapy, 14 with pre-existing trismus, 11 with concurrent malignancy or systemic disease affecting mouth-opening function, 22 with local recurrence or distant metastasis within 12 months after surgery, and 28 with incomplete follow-up data or loss to follow-up.

Preoperative assessment parameters

Preoperative assessment parameters included baseline mouth-opening function and tumor-related indicators.Mouth-opening function: MIO was measured with a vernier caliper as the distance between the incisal edges of the upper and lower central incisors [14]. Patients were seated with the head in a natural upright position and were instructed to open the mouth actively as widely as possible. Measurements were repeated three times, and the mean value was recorded as the preoperative MIO (mm).Tumor-related indicators: the primary tumor site was recorded as upper, middle, or lower buccal mucosa, with or without oral commissure or retromolar region involvement. The upper region was defined as buccal mucosa corresponding to the upper alveolar ridge; the middle region as buccal mucosa corresponding to the occlusal plane; and the lower region as buccal mucosa corresponding to the lower alveolar ridge. The T stage, N stage, M stage, and overall clinical stage were recorded according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system [15]. The maximum tumor diameter (cm) was measured on preoperative computed tomography (CT)/magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or physical examination. Radiological depth of invasion (rDOI, mm) was measured on axial contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MRI as the vertical distance from the tumor surface to the deepest point of invasion. Tumor-masticatory muscle relationships were jointly assessed by two head and neck radiologists using preoperative CT/MRI, and proximity to or involvement of the masseter, medial pterygoid, lateral pterygoid, and temporalis muscles was recorded. Proximity was defined as a tumor margin < 5 mm from the corresponding muscle without muscle signal or density destruction, abnormal intramuscular enhancement, or loss of the tumor-muscle interface. Involvement was defined as loss of the tumor-muscle interface, abnormal intramuscular enhancement, or direct muscle invasion suggested by preoperative imaging combined with intraoperative findings. Disagreements were resolved after review by a senior oral and maxillofacial head and neck oncologic surgeon. Radiological proximity or involvement was used to reflect preoperative anatomic risk and was not considered equivalent to preoperative mouth-opening dysfunction. Posterior tumor extension, including involvement of the retromolar trigone or pterygomandibular space, was also recorded.

Postoperative pathological indicators

Postoperative pathological indicators included pathological tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage, histological differentiation (well, moderate, poor, or undifferentiated; grading criteria according to the 5th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Head and Neck Tumours, 2022), actual tumor size (cm), and pathological depth of invasion (pDOI, mm). Tumor size and the vertical distance from the surface to the deepest invasion point were measured by pathologists on hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections with the largest tumor dimension. pDOI was used for final staging, whereas rDOI was used for preoperative assessment. Lymphovascular invasion (present/absent, defined as tumor cells identified microscopically within vascular or lymphatic lumens), perineural invasion (present/absent, maximum diameter of the involved nerve, classified as small-nerve invasion [diameter < 1 mm] or large-nerve invasion [diameter ≥ 1 mm]), lymph node metastasis status (number of positive lymph nodes/number of dissected nodes, presence of extranodal extension), and margin status (negative [> 5 mm], close [≥ 1 and ≤ 5 mm], or positive [< 1 mm or microscopic tumor cells at the margin]) were recorded.

Postoperative complications

Postoperative complications were recorded, including early complications within 30 days after surgery (wound infection, wound dehiscence, flap necrosis, hematoma, and salivary fistula) and late complications (hypertrophic scar, scar contracture, and osteoradionecrosis).

The primary outcome of this study was postoperative trismus, defined as MIO < 35 mm at 12 months after surgery. Severity was classified as mild trismus (30–34 mm), moderate trismus (20–29 mm), or severe trismus (< 20 mm). Secondary outcomes included the MIO decline rate, calculated as (preoperative MIO - MIO at 12 months after surgery)/preoperative MIO × 100%, and the number of patients requiring secondary surgical release, defined as patients who underwent scar-release surgery for trismus caused by scar contracture refractory to conservative treatment.

Data extraction and quality control

All data were extracted using a prespecified electronic case report form. Two uniformly trained oral and maxillofacial head and neck oncologic surgeons independently reviewed electronic medical records, operative records, anesthetic records, pathology reports, picture archiving and communication system (PACS) imaging data, radiotherapy planning systems, and outpatient follow-up records. Demographic information, exposure history, and comorbidities were obtained from admission records and preoperative assessment records; resection extent, masticatory muscle management, reconstruction method, and intraoperative parameters were obtained from operative and anesthetic records; pathological indicators were obtained from official pathology reports; radiotherapy dosimetric parameters were extracted from the treatment planning system by a radiation oncologist; and follow-up MIO and VSS scores were obtained from structured follow-up forms. Original measurements were preferentially used for continuous variables. If multiple records were available at the same time point, the record closest to the prespecified follow-up window with a complete measurement method was used.

After independent extraction, the two investigators cross-checked all data. Discrepant items were adjudicated by a third senior physician after review of the original records. To assess data reliability, 10% of the included cases (n = 31) were randomly selected for repeated extraction. The kappa coefficient for categorical variables was 0.86, and the intraclass correlation coefficient for continuous variables was 0.93. The discrepancy rate for repeated extraction was 3.8%, and all discrepant items were corrected after review. The primary outcome and main-model candidate predictors had no missing values among included cases. Any missing values in other descriptive variables were reported according to the original records and were not replaced by simple mean imputation. Baseline data, preoperative imaging variables, surgical variables, and pathological variables were extracted before final model development. Radiological proximity or involvement was determined using prespecified criteria, and disagreements were reviewed by a senior physician.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 and R version 4.2.0. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the independent-samples t test; Welch t test was used when variances were unequal. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages) and were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. For comparison of preoperative MIO, the mean difference and 95% confidence interval (CI) were reported in addition to the P value. A 5-mm difference was used as the reference threshold for a clinically meaningful baseline mouth-opening difference and did not replace statistical hypothesis testing. Statistical modeling was performed using complete-case analysis. The primary outcome and main-model candidate predictors had no missing values among the included cases; simple mean imputation was not performed for other descriptive variables.

Univariable analyses were used to describe the association between each candidate factor and postoperative trismus and were not used as the sole criterion for model entry. Before model development, the number of candidate predictors was limited according to the number of outcome events to control model complexity. In this study, 98 patients developed postoperative trismus. Based on the rule of approximately at least 10 events per candidate predictor, the number of candidate predictors in the main model was limited to nine. Candidate variables were selected according to clinical relevance, temporal sequence, interpretability, and degree of variable overlap. For highly overlapping variables, such as rDOI and pDOI, clinical T stage and pathological T stage, total radiation dose and dose category, and masticatory muscle Dmean and V50, the variable with stronger clinical interpretability or closer alignment with the intended model application time point was retained. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to assess multicollinearity among independent variables; VIF > 10 was considered indicative of severe multicollinearity.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent risk factors and to construct an integrated peri-treatment prediction model. The final model consisted of predictors from the main model that were statistically significant and clinically interpretable. A model including all nine candidate predictors was used for sensitivity analysis.

Radiotherapy-related variables were coded for the entire cohort in modeling and single-predictor ROC analyses: total radiation dose was recorded as 0 Gy for patients who did not receive postoperative radiotherapy, and masticatory muscle V50 was recorded as 0% for patients whose irradiation field did not include the masticatory muscles. This approach aligned radiotherapy dosimetric variables with the target population of the combined prediction model.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to evaluate model discrimination, and area under the curve (AUC) values with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. The optimal cutoff values and the corresponding sensitivity and specificity were determined. Model calibration was comprehensively evaluated using calibration curves, calibration intercept, calibration slope, Brier score, and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. A Hosmer–Lemeshow P value > 0.05 was interpreted only as showing no evident lack of fit and not as sufficient proof of model robustness or clinical reliability. Bootstrap resampling (1000 times) was used for internal validation and to estimate the optimism-corrected AUC. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to evaluate clinical net benefit. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics and incidence of trismus

Among 312 patients, 213 (68.3%) were men and 99 (31.7%) were women. Age ranged from 32 to 78 years, with a mean of 56.8 ± 10.2 years. At 12 months after surgery, 98 patients (31.4%) had trismus, including 42 (42.9%) with mild trismus, 39 (39.8%) with moderate trismus, and 17 (17.3%) with severe trismus. The remaining 214 patients (68.6%) did not develop trismus. The MIO decline rate was significantly higher in the trismus group than in the non-trismus group (38.0 ± 11.5% vs. 8.2 ± 5.6%; t = 24.356, P < 0.001). At the 12-month postoperative follow-up endpoint, all 98 patients with trismus had persistent functional impairment. During follow-up, 12 patients (12.2%) underwent scar-release surgery because of trismus caused by scar contracture refractory to conservative treatment.

Comparison of demographic characteristics and risk-factor exposure between the two groups

There were no statistically significant differences between the trismus and non-trismus groups in age, sex, or BMI distribution (P > 0.05). The proportion of heavy betel-quid chewers was significantly higher in the trismus group than in the non-trismus group (P < 0.05) (Table 1).

Comparison of preoperative assessment parameters between the two groups

Comparison of follow-up parameters between the two groups

The trismus group had significantly lower MIO at all postoperative time points and significantly higher VSS scores than the non-trismus group (P < 0.001). Each Vancouver Scar Scale (VSS) component score (pigmentation, thickness, pliability, and vascularity) was significantly higher in the trismus group at all postoperative time points (P < 0.05). Among patients with a VSS pliability score ≥ 3, Shore hardness was higher in the trismus group than in the non-trismus group (P < 0.05). The scar contracture band area at 12 months after surgery was larger in the trismus group (P < 0.001). Oral intake function, speech intelligibility score, and facial symmetry were all significantly worse in the trismus group (P < 0.05) (Table 8).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis of independent risk factors for postoperative trismus

Univariable analyses were used to describe associations between candidate risk factors and postoperative trismus. The main model included nine prespecified candidate predictors according to the number of events, clinical relevance, temporal sequence, intended model application time point, and variable overlap: pDOI ≥ 10 mm, subtotal or total masseter resection, medial pterygoid resection, buccal mucosal defect area ≥ 15 cm², perineural invasion, close/positive margin, wound infection, radiation dose ≥ 60 Gy, and masticatory muscle V50 ≥ 40%. The events-per-candidate-predictor ratio in the candidate model was 10.9, and the events-per-predictor ratio in the final nomogram model including six independent predictors was 16.3. The variance inflation factor (VIF) range of the candidate variables was 1.08–3.62, indicating no severe multicollinearity.

Multivariable logistic regression showed that pDOI ≥ 10 mm, subtotal or total masseter resection, medial pterygoid resection, buccal mucosal defect area ≥ 15 cm², radiation dose ≥ 60 Gy, and masticatory muscle V50 ≥ 40% were independent risk factors for postoperative trismus (P < 0.05) (Table 9). The sensitivity model including all nine candidate predictors showed that the direction and statistical significance of the main predictors were consistent. Perineural invasion, close/positive margin, and wound infection did not reach statistical significance after adjustment (Supplementary Table S1).

Development and evaluation of the prediction model

A prediction model was developed based on the six independent risk factors identified by multivariable logistic regression, and a nomogram was constructed (Fig. 1). The AUC of the combined prediction model was 0.862 (95% CI, 0.820–0.904). The Hosmer–Lemeshow test result was χ² = 6.842, P = 0.554, indicating no evident lack of fit; the calibration curve showed overall agreement between predicted probabilities and observed incidence. The calibration intercept was 0.02, the calibration slope was 0.91, and the Brier score was 0.147. Bootstrap internal validation (1000 repetitions) yielded a corrected AUC of 0.848 (95% CI, 0.802–0.894), with an estimated optimism of 0.014. These findings indicate that the model showed good discrimination and acceptable calibration in internal validation within this cohort, although external validation remains necessary. DCA showed that, across a threshold probability range of 0.10–0.70, the prediction model provided greater net benefit than both the treat-all and treat-none strategies, suggesting potential clinical applicability.

ROC curve analyses were performed for each independent risk factor. The AUC for pDOI in predicting trismus was 0.742 (95% CI, 0.684–0.800), with an optimal cutoff of 9.5 mm, sensitivity of 72.4%, and specificity of 68.2%. The AUC for masseter resection status was 0.689 (95% CI, 0.626–0.751), with subtotal or total resection as the optimal cutoff, sensitivity of 32.7%, and specificity of 90.7%. The AUC for medial pterygoid resection status was 0.611 (95% CI, 0.548–0.674), with any resection as the optimal cutoff, sensitivity of 36.7%, and specificity of 85.0%. The AUC for buccal mucosal defect area was 0.724 (95% CI, 0.664–0.784), with an optimal cutoff of 14.5 cm², sensitivity of 68.4%, and specificity of 70.6%. The AUC for total radiation dose was 0.658 (95% CI, 0.592–0.724), with an optimal cutoff of 59.5 Gy, sensitivity of 46.9%, and specificity of 79.4%. The AUC for masticatory muscle V50 was 0.663 (95% CI, 0.598–0.728), with an optimal cutoff of 38.5%, sensitivity of 30.6%, and specificity of 93.9%. The AUC of the combined prediction model was higher than those of all individual indicators (DeLong test, all P < 0.05) (Table 10; Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study evaluated BMSCC as a single oral cancer subsite rather than combining multiple oral cancer subsites in one analysis. This design was primarily based on the subsite-specific characteristics of BMSCC: its posterior and deep extension is closely related to the retromolar region, pterygomandibular space, anterior border of the masseter muscle, and medial pterygoid muscle. Tumor resection, buccal defect reconstruction, scar contracture, and radiotherapy target distribution may directly affect mandibular movement [16]. Therefore, risk factors for postoperative trismus in BMSCC may differ from those in other oral subsites, such as tongue and floor-of-mouth cancers. In this retrospective analysis of 312 patients, the incidence of trismus at 12 months after surgery was 31.4%, which is within the range reported in previous studies of head and neck tumors and oral cancer but higher than that in some mixed oral cancer cohorts [17, 18]. This difference may be attributable to the greater tendency of buccal mucosal cancers to approach or involve the masticatory muscle complex and the more frequent need for full-thickness buccinator resection and large soft-tissue defect reconstruction [19].

Preoperative anatomic risk is not equivalent to preoperative functional restriction. Although the trismus group had higher proportions of preoperative proximity to or involvement of the masseter and medial pterygoid muscles, the mean difference in preoperative MIO between the two groups was only − 0.6 mm, and the 95% CI remained within the 5-mm reference threshold for clinically meaningful difference, suggesting that baseline mouth-opening function was clinically comparable. Several explanations may account for this finding. First, this study excluded patients with pre-existing trismus, and all included patients had a preoperative MIO ≥ 35 mm [20]. Second, the term “proximity” in Table 3 mainly reflected the radiological spatial relationship between tumor and muscle and was not equivalent to true intramuscular invasion [21]. Third, even when focal muscle involvement was present, preoperative mandibular movement may have been maintained within the normal range through compensation by uninvolved muscles and soft tissues [22]. In contrast, postoperative trismus is more likely to be driven by the combined effects of muscle injury caused by oncologically safe resection, buccal scar contracture, reduced compliance of reconstructed tissue, and radiation-induced fibrosis [23]. Therefore, the preoperative tumor-muscle relationship should be interpreted as an anatomic indicator of subsequent treatment extent and postoperative functional impairment risk rather than as a direct surrogate for preoperative mouth-opening dysfunction.

Multivariable analysis showed that pDOI ≥ 10 mm was an independent risk factor for postoperative trismus (odds ratio [OR] = 3.078, 95% CI, 1.756–5.392). Depth of invasion is an important pathological indicator of tumor invasiveness and has been incorporated into the 8th edition of the AJCC TNM staging system [24]. Deeply invasive tumors are more likely to extend toward the retromolar region, pterygomandibular space, or masticatory space, thereby increasing the need for extended resection, masticatory muscle management, and complex reconstruction [25]. In this study, single-predictor ROC analysis of pDOI as a continuous variable yielded an AUC of 0.742, with an optimal cutoff of 9.5 mm, consistent with the pDOI ≥ 10 mm categorical threshold used in the multivariable model. Preoperative rDOI may still serve as an anatomic risk indicator before treatment planning, but pathological pDOI was prioritized in the final model to reduce overlap with preoperative imaging indicators and clinical T stage [26].

Subtotal or total masseter resection had the highest OR (4.288) and was the strongest risk factor identified in this study. As a principal elevator of the mandible, the masseter muscle is essential for maintaining normal mouth-opening function [27]. Previous studies have shown that the risk of postoperative trismus increases significantly when more than 50% of the masseter muscle volume is resected [28]. The results of this study are consistent with these observations and further indicate that medial pterygoid resection is also an independent risk factor. The medial pterygoid muscle participates in mandibular elevation and lateral movement, and its injury may alter mandibular motion patterns and aggravate mouth-opening difficulty [29]. Therefore, preserving masticatory muscle function as much as oncological safety permits should be an important consideration in surgical planning.

A buccal mucosal defect area ≥ 15 cm² was independently associated with postoperative trismus. A larger defect indicates more extensive surgical trauma and a more complex reconstruction requirement, and may be accompanied by more pronounced scar formation [30]. Follow-up data in this study showed that the incidence of hypertrophic scar was 77.6% and that of scar contracture was 73.5% in the trismus group, both significantly higher than those in the non-trismus group. Scar contracture is an important mechanism of trismus and is associated with excessive fibroblast proliferation and abnormal collagen deposition during wound healing [31]. Selecting appropriate reconstruction techniques, ensuring flap survival, and minimizing scar contracture are key steps in preventing postoperative trismus [32].

The present results support an independent association between radiotherapy-related factors and postoperative trismus. Radiation dose ≥ 60 Gy and masticatory muscle V50 ≥ 40% were both independent risk factors, consistent with the dose-dependent nature of radiation-induced fibrosis [33]. Radiotherapy can induce progressive fibrotic changes in muscle and connective tissue, which may manifest as irreversible trismus in severe cases [34]. Previous studies have suggested a tolerance dose of approximately 50 Gy for the masticatory muscles, above which the risk of trismus increases significantly [35]. In the present study, the optimal cutoff for masticatory muscle V50 coded across the entire cohort was 38.5%, suggesting that the irradiated volume and dose of the masticatory muscles should be reduced as much as feasible in radiotherapy planning. While maintaining tumor control, intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) and other precision radiotherapy techniques may help reduce masticatory muscle injury [36].

The prediction model constructed from the six independent risk factors had a combined AUC of 0.862, outperforming all individual indicators. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test result of P = 0.554 only suggested no obvious evidence of lack of fit in this sample and cannot by itself prove model robustness or clinical reliability. Model performance was also evaluated using the calibration curve, calibration intercept, calibration slope, Brier score, and bootstrap internal validation; the corrected AUC was 0.848, indicating a modest and acceptable degree of optimism. Because the model includes peri-treatment variables such as pDOI, surgical resection status, and radiotherapy planning parameters, it is mainly applicable for integrated risk stratification after postoperative pathological information and adjuvant treatment plans are available, rather than for purely preoperative prediction.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center retrospective study. Although a prespecified case report form, independent dual extraction, repeated random-sampling review, and consistency assessment were used, selection bias and information bias may still exist. Second, the model was developed based on 98 postoperative trismus events, and the risk of overfitting was reduced by limiting the number of candidate predictors, assessing VIF, and performing bootstrap internal validation; nevertheless, internal validation cannot replace independent external validation. Third, the Hosmer–Lemeshow test has limited statistical power. A P value > 0.05 indicates only that there is no evident lack of fit and does not prove that the model has sufficient robustness or general clinical applicability. Fourth, the follow-up endpoint was set at 12 months after surgery, and mouth-opening function may continue to improve or deteriorate over a longer period in some patients. Longer-term follow-up data would be helpful for a more comprehensive prognostic assessment. Fifth, this study did not include factors such as adherence to postoperative rehabilitation exercises, although early functional training plays an important role in preventing trismus. Future multicenter external cohorts are needed to validate the model calibration, discrimination, and clinical net benefit.

Conclusions

In conclusion, pDOI, masseter and medial pterygoid resection status, buccal mucosal defect area, radiation dose, and irradiated masticatory muscle volume are independent risk factors for trismus after surgical treatment of BMSCC. The prediction model constructed from these factors showed good discrimination and acceptable calibration in internal validation in this cohort and may serve as an adjunctive reference for identifying high-risk patients and developing function-preserving strategies. Its clinical applicability requires further validation in multicenter external cohorts.
