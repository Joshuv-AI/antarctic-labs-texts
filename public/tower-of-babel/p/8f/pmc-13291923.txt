Isolation of Metabolites With Antiviral Activity From the Brown Algae Dictyota fasciola and Dictyota mediterranea
Authors: Aikaterini Koutsaviti, Elsi‐Evangelia Andriotaki, Maria Harizani, Katarzyna Owczarek, Magdalena Pachota, Aleksandra Synowiec, Artur Szczepański, Paweł Botwina, Krzysztof Pyrć, Vassilios Roussis, Efstathia Ioannou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTwenty diterpenes (1–20) and a sesquiterpene (21) were isolated from Dictyota fasciola and Dictyota mediterranea collected from the Aegean Sea and evaluated as potential antiviral agents against a panel of viral pathogens. Among the isolated metabolites, six dolabellane diterpenes (1−4, 9, and 12) and one germacrane sesquiterpene (21) are new natural products. The evaluation of the antiviral activity of the isolated compounds was carried out against three viruses of different phylogenetic backgrounds that are characterized by high incidence in human population and cause significant medical threat and economic losses worldwide: herpes simplex virus (HSV‐1), human coronavirus (HCoV‐NL63), and Zika virus (ZIKV). The antiviral assays demonstrated that some compounds inhibited the viruses within a concentration range of 3–10 µg/mL. The maximal suppression of viral replication was 90% for HSV‐1, 99.99% for HCoV‐NL63, and 96.8% for ZIKV, highlighting the differential efficacy of the tested compounds against specific viruses. Mechanistic studies revealed that, depending on the compound, antiviral activity occurred either at the virus attachment stage or during a post‐entry replication stage. Among the tested compounds, metabolites 6–8, 13, 17, and 18 exhibited considerable antiviral activity against RNA (HCoV‐NL63, ZIKV) and DNA (HSV‐1) viruses.

Introduction

Over the last 6 decades, brown algae (Ochrophyta) of the family Dictyotaceae have been the subject of numerous studies. Members of this family exhibit a rich chemical profile and have yielded approx. 700 structurally diverse secondary metabolites [1]. Dictyotaceae comprises c. 35 genera with a wide geographic distribution in temperate, subtropical and tropical marine ecosystems around the world [2]. The genus Dictyota is considered one of the most important genera of Dictyotaceae, primarily due to the interesting chemical profile that characterizes the representatives of the genus, but also due to the significant ecological roles that members of this genus play in forming benthic communities in coastal ecosystems [3]. The genus Dictyota exhibits an almost cosmopolitan geographic distribution, mainly found in tropical and temperate waters, although a small number of representatives have adapted to cooler environments [4]. Members of this genus possess effective chemical defense mechanisms, with diterpenes being a major class of secondary metabolites that characterizes species of Dictyota [1, 5]. It is worth noting that more than 230 diterpenes have been isolated in total from Dictyota spp., including potential drug candidates, belonging to the classes of dolabellanes, dolastanes, xenicanes, and prenylated sesquiterpenes, among others [6, 7]. Representatives of these classes of compounds have shown a plethora of biological activities, such as antibacterial and antifungal, antiviral, antischistosomal, antifouling, cytotoxic, anti‐inflammatory, anti‐platelet, and anti‐coagulant [1, 4, 6, 7].

The need for new antiviral agents has become increasingly urgent with the rapid emergence of novel pathogens and the limitations of existing therapies. Current drugs are often narrow in scope, prone to resistance, and even unavailable for many high‐burden infections, while vaccines alone cannot provide timely or universal protection [8, 9]. Algae represent a promising source of structurally diverse and biologically active molecules, creating opportunities to develop broad‐spectrum, resistance‐resilient agents essential for outbreak preparedness and the management of chronic viral diseases.

Herpes simplex virus (HSV‐1) belongs to the dsDNA family Herpesviridae, which encompasses more than 100 viruses, 8 of which affect humans. They are widespread in humans and it is estimated that worldwide 3.8 billion people under the age of 50 (64% of the population in that age group) are infected with HSV‐1. The HSV‐1 infection is mainly associated with watery blisters in the oral mucosa, however it can also take a more severe course with potentially fatal viral encephalitis or stromal keratitis—an ocular disease, which is a leading cause of cornea‐derived blindness in developed countries.

Human coronavirus NL63 (HCoV‐NL63) belongs to the ssRNA(+) family Coronaviridae and causes respiratory tract disease of moderate severity. Although less pathogenic than the related SARS‐CoV and SARS‐CoV‐2, HCoV‐NL63 is responsible for a large number of infections worldwide and is the major etiological factor of croup in children.

Zika virus (ZIKV) is a mosquito‐borne ssRNA(+) flavivirus, usually causing a disease that is manifested with a set of unspecific symptoms, including fever, rash, headache, joint, and muscle pain. The large‐scale ZIKV outbreak that occurred in Brazil during 2015‐2016, with an estimated 1.3 million cases reported across South America, demonstrated that ZIKV infection can be also associated with severe neurological outcomes, most notably microcephaly in fetuses and Guillain–Barré syndrome in adults.

In the framework of our ongoing research on bioactive secondary metabolites from marine sources, we investigated the chemistry of two Dictyota spp. collected from the Aegean Sea and the potential inhibitory activity of the isolated compounds against three phylogenetically diverse viruses, namely HSV‐1, HCoV‐NL63, and ZIVK, that were selected in order to evaluate the compounds’ antiviral activity across a broad spectrum of human pathogens that collectively pose major medical and economic challenges worldwide. The two investigated species were collected from the rocky shores of Rhodes and Karpathos, two of the largest islands of the Dodecanese complex in the south‐eastern part of the Aegean Sea. Both algal species, collected in August, which is the hottest month of the year with the water temperature reaching up to 26°C–27°C, were found at relatively low depths in dense populations clean of epiphytes.

Results and Discussion

Specimens of the brown algae Dictyota fasciola and Dictyota mediterranea, collected from the island of Rhodes and the island of Karpathos, respectively, were exhaustively extracted with mixtures of CH2Cl2/MeOH, and the resulting organic residues were separately subjected to a series of chromatographic separations leading to the isolation of compounds 1−21 (Figure 1). In particular, eleven metabolites were isolated from D. fasciola (1, 3−5, 7, 8, 14, 17−20) and 20 from D. mediterranea (2−21). Among these, compounds 1−4, 9, 12, and 21 are new natural products, whereas the remaining 14 were identified as (1R*,2E,4R*,7E,11R*,12S*)‐12,18‐dihydroxydolabella‐2,7‐diene (5) [10], (1R*,2E,4R*,7E,11S*,12R*)‐18‐hydroxy‐2,7‐dolabelladiene (6) [11, 12], (1R*,2E,4R*,7E,10S*,11S*,12R*)‐10,18‐dihydroxy‐2,7‐dolabelladiene (7) [11], (1R*,2E,4R*,7E,10S*,11S*,12R*)‐10‐acetoxy‐18‐hydroxy‐2,7‐dolabelladiene (8) [11, 13], (1R,3E,7Z,11S,12S)‐dolabella‐3,7,18‐trien‐17‐oic acid (10) [14], (1R,3E,6R,7Z,11S,12S)‐dolabella‐3,7,18‐trien‐4,17‐olide (11) [14], (1R,3E,7E,11S,12S)‐14‐oxo‐3,7,18‐dolabellatriene (13) [15], dictyol E (14) [16], pachydictyol A (15) [17], isopachydictyol A (16) [18], dictyol C (17) [19], acetyldictyolal (18) [20], neodictyolactone (19) [20], and acetylsanadaol (20) [21]. Among them, compounds 10 and 11, previously isolated from the tracheophyte Trichilia trifolia, are reported for the first time from a marine source.

Chemical structures of compounds 1‐21 isolated from the brown algae Dictyota fasciola (1, 3−5, 7, 8, 14, 17−20), and Dictyota mediterranea (2−21).

Compounds 1 and 2, obtained as colorless oils, had the molecular formula C20H34O3 according to HR‐ESIMS measurements. In particular, sodium adduct ion peaks [M + Na]+ at m/z 345.2397 (δ ppm = −0.8690) and 345.2396 (δ ppm = −1.1586) were observed for compounds 1 and 2, respectively. Metabolites 1 and 2 exhibited similar structural features according to their 1H and 1 3C NMR spectra. Specifically, the 13C NMR spectra of 1 and 2, in combination with their HSQC‐DEPT and HMBC spectra, displayed signals for 20 carbon atoms corresponding to 4 quaternary carbons, 5 methines, 7 methylenes, and 4 methyls (Tables 1 and 2). Characteristic signals were displayed in the 13C NMR and 1H NMR spectra for three methyls on quaternary carbon atoms (δ C/H 21.3/1.10, 25.6/1.29, 26.1/1.29 for 1; δ C/H 20.5/1.08, 25.6/1.32, 26.0/1.31 for 2) and one methyl on a tertiary carbon (δ C/H 21.7/0.89 for 1; δ C/H 21.3/0.92 for 2), along with one oxygenated methine (δ C/H 79.6/3.93 for 1; δ C/H 72.8/4.06 for 2). Evident were also the signals for one 1,2‐disubstituted double bond (δ C/H 132.5/5.08, 139.8/5.45 for 1; δ C/H 132.4/5.14, 138.5/5.36 for 2), as well as one 1,1‐disubstituted double bond (δ C/H 111.8/5.00, 5.05, δ C 148.2 for 1; δ C/H 106.8/5.07, 5.13, δ C 149.2 for 2). The two double bonds accounted for two of the four unsaturation degrees; thus, the structures of 1 and 2 were assumed to be bicyclic. Thorough analysis of their HSQC‐DEPT, HMBC, and COSY spectra led to the determination of the same planar structure for 1 and 2, featuring the dolabellane skeleton (Figure 2a). Specifically, the COSY correlations of H‐2/H‐3, H‐3/H‐4, H‐4/H2‐5, H2‐5/H2‐6, H2‐6/H‐7, H2‐9/H2‐10, H2‐10/H‐11, and H‐4/H3‐16, in conjunction with the heteronuclear correlations of H3‐15 with C‐1, C‐2, and C‐11, of H2‐17 with C‐7, C‐8, and C‐9, and of H3‐16 with C‐3, C‐4, and C‐5 displayed in the HMBC spectra established the assignment of the 11‐membered ring for both compounds. Additionally, the observed COSY correlations of H2‐13/H2‐14 and the HMBC correlations of H3‐15 with C‐14, as well as of H‐11 and H2‐13 with C‐12 defined the assignment of the five‐membered ring. Moreover, the heteronuclear correlations of H3‐19 and H3‐20 with C‐12 and C‐18 indicated the presence of a hydroxy‐isopropyl moiety and fixed its position (Figure 2a). The fact that metabolites 1 and 2 shared the same planar structure suggested that they are stereoisomers. The relative configurations of the five stereogenic centers of 1 and the geometry of the 1,2‐disubstituted double bond were established on the basis of 1H NMR coupling constants, in combination with the analysis of a set of 1D‐NOE spectra. The coupling constant between H‐2 and H‐3 (J 2,3 = 15.9 Hz) defined the geometry of the disubstituted Δ2 double bond as E. The strong NOE interactions of H‐2/H3‐15 and H‐3/H‐11 established the trans fusion of the two rings. The NOE cross‐peak of H3‐19 with H‐11 determined the relative configuration of C‐12, namely the α‐orientation of the hydroxy‐isopropyl group and hence the β‐orientation of the hydroxyl group attached to C‐12. Moreover, the NOE correlations of H‐2/H‐4 and H‐3/H3‐16 determined the α orientation of the methyl at C‐4, while the NOE correlations of H‐11/H‐17a and H‐7/Η‐17b determined the β orientation of the hydroxyl group at C‐7 (Figure 2b). Thus, metabolite 1 was identified as (1R*,2E,4R*,7R*,11R*,12S*)‐7,12,18‐trihydroxy‐dolabella‐2,8(17)‐diene. Compound 2 exhibited similar NOE enhancements to 1, namely H‐2/H‐4, Η‐2/Η3‐15, Η‐3/Η‐11, Η‐3/Η3‐16, Η‐11/Η‐17a and Η‐11/Η3‐19, suggesting the same relative configurations at C‐1, C‐4, C‐11, and C‐12. In contrast, the fact that C‐7 in 2 resonated at significantly lower frequencies in comparison to 1 (δ C 79.6 for 1 vs. δ C 72.8 for 2), in combination with the coupling constant of H‐7 (brd, J = 4.2), was indicative of the β orientation of H‐7 and hence the S* configuration at C‐7. This hypothesis was further supported by the absence of a NOE enhancement between H‐7 and H‐17b but instead the presence of NOE cross‐peaks between H‐5β (δ H 1.38) and both H‐2 and H‐7, verifying the β orientation of H‐7 (Figure 2c). As such, compounds 1 and 2 were identified as C‐7 epimers.

Compounds 3 and 4 were isolated as colorless oils and according to their HR‐ESIMS data shared the molecular formula C20H34O4. The 13C NMR spectra of 3 and 4, in conjunction with the HSQC‐DEPT and HMBC experiments, showed the presence of 20 carbon signals attributed to 4 methyls, 7 methylenes, 5 methines, and 4 quaternary carbons (Tables 1 and 2). According to the COSY, HSQC‐DEPT, and HMBC data, 3 and 4 shared the same gross tri‐oxygenated dolabella‐2,8(17)‐diene structure (Figure 2a), similarly to compounds 1 and 2. However, the oxygenated tertiary carbon C‐7 of 3 and 4 resonated at significantly higher frequencies (δ C 92.4 for 3; δ C 86.8 for 4) compared to a typical oxygenated carbon (δ C 79.6 for 1; δ C 72.8 for 2). Additionally, the carbons directly attached to C‐7 were shifted to higher fields (δ C 26.8 and 145.3 for 3; δ C 26.7 and 145.8 for 4) compared to 1 (δ C 31.0 and 148.2) and 2 (δ C 29.5 and 149.2), suggesting the presence of a hydroperoxy functionality at C‐7 [22], a hypothesis that was also in compliance with the MS data. As in the case of 1 and 2, the relative stereochemistry of 3 and 4 was established on the basis of the data obtained from 1D‐NOE experiments and the measured 1H NMR coupling constants. The geometry of the Δ2 double bond was determined as E due to the large coupling constant between H‐2 and H‐3 (J 2,3 = 15.9 Hz), while the NOE correlations of H‐2/H‐4, Η‐2/Η3‐15, Η‐3/Η‐11, H‐3/Η3‐16, Η‐7/Η‐17b, H‐11/H‐17a and H‐11/Η3‐19 established the relative configurations of C‐1, C‐4, C‐7, C‐11, and C‐12 in 3 similarly to 1. In 4, as in the case of 2, the orientation of H‐7 was defined as β based on the coupling constant of H‐7 (dd, J = 4.2, 4.2), also supported by the absence of a NOE enhancement between H‐7 and H‐17b. Thus, compounds 3 and 4 were characterized as C‐7 epimers and the hydroperoxy analogues of 1 and 2 at C‐7, respectively.

Compound 9, isolated as colorless oil, had the molecular formula C20H32O according to HR‐ESIMS and NMR data. The spectroscopic data of 9 (Tables 1 and 2) suggested a diterpene featuring the dolabellane skeleton with 5 degrees of unsaturation. An isopropenyl group was evident by the signals of one 1,1‐disubstituted double bond (δ C/H 109.8/4.86, 5.04, δ C 149.6) and the heteronuclear correlations of the protons of the vinyl methyl H3‐19 and of the exomethylene protons H2‐20 with the olefinic C‐18 and the quaternary oxygenated C‐12 (δ C 86.3). Additionally, the presence of one trisubstituted and one 1,2‐disubstituted double bond was obvious according to the 1H NMR signals in conjunction with HSQC‐DEPT and HMBC data. The geometry of the Δ2 and Δ7 double bonds was in both cases defined as E based on the resonance of C‐17 at lower frequencies (δ C 16.2) and the coupling constant of H‐2/H‐3 (J 2,3 = 15.9 Hz), respectively, further supported by the observed NOE interactions. Thorough analysis of the hetero‐ and homonuclear interactions (HSQC‐DEPT, HMBC, and COSY) led to the assignment of the planar structure of metabolite 9 (Figure 3a), while the relative configurations of the four stereogenic centers of 9 were determined according to the observed NOESY cross‐peaks (Figure 3b). The NOE enhancements of H‐2/H3‐15 and H‐3/H‐11 suggested the trans fusion of the two rings, while the NOE correlations of H‐2/H‐4 and H‐3/H3‐16 determined the relative configuration of C‐4 and the α orientation of the methyl H3‐16. The β orientation of the isopropenyl group was proposed based on the chemical shift of H‐11 (δ H 1.63) in combination with the resonance of C‐10 at δ C 20.1 [23]. This hypothesis was further supported by the weak NOE correlation of H‐20b (δ H 4.86) with H2‐10. The natural origin of compounds 3 and 4 in the investigated organisms through enzymatic processes in the living organisms can not be unambiguously supported since they could potentially be artifacts of oxidation of the corresponding metabolites 1 and 2 during the extraction and/or chromatographic separations. Nonetheless, we have used similar experimental protocols in the past for the chemical study of other populations of Dictyota spp. but have not detected diterpenes bearing hydroperoxyl moieties.

Compound 12 was isolated as colorless oil with the molecular formula C20H30O, according to HR‐APCIMS and NMR data. The spectroscopic data of 12 (Tables 1 and 2) indicated a dolabellane diterpene with six degrees of unsaturation. The presence of an isopropenyl group was evident (δ C/H 111.0/4.60, 4.80, δ C 146.1, and δ C/H 23.1/1.50). Moreover, signals for two trisubstituted double bonds (δ C/H 125.8/5.32 and δ C 135.8; δ C/H 154.8/6.47 and δ C 144.0) were obvious in the 1H and 13C NMR spectra of 12 along with an aldehyde group (δ C/H 195.6/9.37). The hetero‐ and homonuclear correlations observed in the 2D NMR spectra verified the dolabellane carbocycle and led to the assignment of the gross structure of 12 (Figure 4a). Based on the NOE enhancements of H3‐15/H‐2β and H‐11/H‐2α, the fusion of the two rings was determined as trans (Figure 4b). The NOE cross‐peaks of H‐11/H‐12 defined the relative configuration of C‐12 and the β orientation of the isopropenyl group. The NOE enhancements of H3‐15/H‐3, H‐3/H‐5β and H3‐16/H‐5α determined the E geometry of the Δ3 trisubstituted double bond, whereas the geometry of the Δ7 double bond was identified as E on the basis of the strong NOE enhancement of H‐7/H‐17, further supported by the fact that the aldehydic proton resonated at relatively higher fields (δ Η 9.37) [24, 25].

Compound 21, isolated as colorless oil, had the molecular formula C17H26O2 according to its HR‐ESIMS data. Signals for 17 carbon atoms were observed in the HSQC‐DEPT and HMBC spectra, assigned to 3 non‐protonated carbons, 6 methines, 4 methylenes and 4 methyls (Table 3). Signals were evident for two methyls on quaternary carbon atoms (δ C/H 16.4/1.52 and 20.3/1.73), two methyls on a tertiary carbon (δ C/H 19.3/0.80 and 20.3/0.84), one 1,1‐disubstituted double bond (δ C/H 111.5/4.82, 5.01, δ C 144.0), one 1,2‐disubstituted double bond (δ C/H 132.9/5.08 and 135.8/5.52), one trisubstituted double bond (δ C/H 128.0/5.11 and δ C 138.8), one oxygen‐bearing methine (δ C/H 71.4/5.69), and one carbonyl functionality (δ C 169.3). Since the three double bonds and the carbonyl group accounted for four of the five degrees of unsaturation, metabolite 21 was determined as monocyclic. The interpretation of the observed COSY and HMBC correlations led to the elucidation of the planar structure of 21 (Figure 5a). Specifically, the COSY correlations of H‐1/H‐2 and H‐2/H‐3, as well as of H‐5/H‐6, H‐6/H‐7, H‐7/H2‐8, H2‐8/H2‐9, H‐7/H‐11, H‐11/H‐12 and H‐11/H‐13 established the two separate spin systems of 21 and in conjunction with the HMBC correlations of H3‐14 with C‐1, C‐9, and C‐10, as well as of H2‐15 with C‐3 and C‐5, led to the identification of 21 as a germacrane sesquiterpene. The HMBC correlations of H3‐17 and H‐2 to the carbonyl carbon (δ C 169.3) secured the position of the acetoxy group at C‐2. The geometry of the double bonds, as well as the relative configurations of the stereogenic centers of 21 were proposed on the basis of the measured 1H NMR coupling constants and the correlations observed in the NOESY spectrum. The coupling constant between H‐5 and H‐6 (J = 15.7 Hz) and the resonance of C‐14 at lower frequencies (δ C 16.4) determined the geometry of the Δ5 and Δ1,10 double bonds as E in both cases. The NOE interactions of H‐5/H‐3α, H‐3β/H‐2, H‐2/H3‐14 and H3‐14/H‐6 determined the α orientation of the acetoxy group, whereas the NOE enhancements of H‐5/H‐7 and H‐6/H3‐12 established the β orientation of the isopropyl group (Figure 5b).

Regarding the absolute configurations of the new dolabellanes isolated in the present investigation (1−4, 9, and 12), even though they were not experimentally determined, on the basis of a common biogenetic route they are expected to be the same as that of the co‐occurring metabolite 13, for which the absolute configuration has been previously established [15]. Specifically, the absolute configuration of C‐1 for compounds 1−4, 9, and 12 is expected to be R, further indicated by the fact that in all cases to date dolabellanes isolated from algae, liverworts and higher plants possess the same absolute configuration, while those isolated from soft corals are in an antipodal relationship [1, 14, 26].

Taking into account that several diterpenes isolated from brown algae of the genus Dictyota have exhibited noteworthy levels of antiviral activity [27, 28, 29, 30], compounds 1, 3–8, and 10–21 were evaluated as potential antiviral agents against representative members of the Herpesviridae, Coronaviridae, and Flaviviridae virus families, and in particular against herpes simplex virus (HSV‐1), human coronavirus NL63 (HCoV‐NL63), and Zika virus (ZIKV).

Initially, the cytotoxicity of the compounds was assessed using the XTT assay across a concentration range of 2.5–20 µg/mL. As shown in Figure 6, concentrations equal or higher than 15 µg/mL negatively affected cell viability. Antiviral activity was subsequently tested in Vero E6, LLC‐MK2, and Vero cells, which are permissive for HSV‐1, HCoV‐NL63, and ZIKV infection, respectively, with quantification performed by qPCR. Infection with HSV‐1 was effectively inhibited by compounds 6 and 17, which reduced viral replication by up to 6 log (99.9999%) at nontoxic concentrations, with IC50 values of 3 µg/mL. Infection with HCoV‐NL63 was inhibited by compounds 7, 8, and 13, resulting in up to 4 log (99.99%) reduction at nontoxic concentrations, with IC50 values of 3–5 µg/mL. For ZIKV, compounds 6 and 18 inhibited infection by up to 4 log (99.99%), with IC50 values of 9–11 µg/mL (Figure 6, Table 4).

To preliminary address the mechanisms underlying antiviral activity, functional assays were performed to assess (i) direct virus inactivation, (ii) cell protection, (iii) inhibition of viral attachment, and (iv) interference with late stages of the viral life cycle. Compounds were tested at 10 µg/mL. The majority of the bioactive compounds primarily inhibited the late stages of viral infection, although moderate effects on viral attachment were also observed (Figure 6). Notably, compound 8 was also able to directly inactivate HCoV‐NL63 virions.

Taken together, the results confirm that a number of the isolated compounds present inhibitory effects against viral infection, providing a valuable basis for further investigation using infectivity‐based assays that would determine the extent to which reductions in viral genome copies correlate with reductions in infectious virus production.

The antiviral activity described in this study was evaluated using RT‐qPCR quantification of viral genomes, which provides a sensitive measurement of viral replication but does not directly distinguish between infectious and non‐infectious viral particles. Therefore, the calculated IC50 values reflect inhibition of viral RNA accumulation rather than direct quantification of infectious virus yield. A further limitation is the absence of standard antiviral reference compounds which restricts direct benchmarking of potency. However, the primary objective of this study was the comparative evaluation of the isolated compounds, and the results should be interpreted within this context. In addition, the use of primate kidney‐derived cell lines (Vero E6, Vero, LLC‐MK2), which are highly permissive but lack full physiological relevance to human target tissues and, in the case of Vero cells, a functional interferon response, may influence the observed antiviral effects. Future studies employing infectivity‐based assays, such as plaque reduction or TCID50 analyses, as well as validation in more physiologically relevant human cell models, would be valuable to further substantiate the antiviral potential of these compounds.

Conclusions

The chemical investigation of two species of the brown algal genus Dictyota resulted in the isolation of 21 secondary metabolites (1–21). In particular, the organic extract of D. fasciola collected from the island of Rhodes afforded 11 metabolites (1, 3−5, 7, 8, 14, 17−20), whereas the organic extract of D. mediterranea collected from the island of Karpathos yielded 20 compounds (2−21). Among the isolated metabolites, 6 dolabellane diterpenes (1−4, 9, and 12) and 1 germacrane sesquiterpene (21) are new natural products. The evaluation of the antiviral activity of the isolated compounds was carried out against three viruses of different phylogenetic backgrounds with high incidence in human population: herpes simplex virus (HSV‐1), human coronavirus (HCoV‐NL63), and Zika virus (ZIKV). The results of the antiviral activity evaluation showed that some compounds moderately inhibited the viruses within a concentration range of 3–10 µg/mL. Mechanistic studies revealed that, depending on the compound, antiviral activity occurred either at the virus attachment stage or during a post‐entry replication stage. Among the tested compounds, metabolites 6–8, 13, 17, and 18 exhibited noteworthy antiviral activity against RNA (HCoV‐NL63, ZIKV) and DNA (HSV‐1) viruses, positioning them as preliminary leads for future investigations using infectivity‐based assays.

Experimental Section

General Experimental Procedures

NMR experiments were performed at room temperature on Bruker AC 200 and Bruker DRX 400 (Bruker BioSpin GmbH, Rheinstetten, Germany) and Varian 600 MHz (Varian, Inc. Palo Alto, CA, USA) spectrometers, using Bruker's or Varian's standard pulse sequences. The sample solutions, using CDCl3 or C6D6 as solvent, were transferred to 5 mm NMR tubes. Chemical shifts are expressed on a δ (ppm) scale referenced to residual CHCl3 and C6H6 at δ H 7.24 and 7.16 for proton, respectively, and to the central peak of the corresponding signal of the deuterated solvent 13CDCl3 and 13C6D6 at δ C 77.0 and 128.0 for carbon, respectively. Optical rotations were measured on an AUTOPOL III automatic polarimeter (RUDOLPH RESEARCH ANALYTICAL, USA) at 25°C at 589 nm with a 1 dm cell. UV spectra were recorded on a Shimadzu UV‐1900i UV–vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Europa GmbH, Duisburg, Germany), whereas IR spectra were obtained on a FTIR Bruker Alpha II spectrometer (Bruker Optik GmbH, Ettlingen, Germany). High‐resolution mass spectra were measured on a Thermo Scientific LTQ Orbitrap Velos mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany). Column chromatography separations were performed with Kieselgel 60 (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). HPLC separations were conducted on an Agilent 1100 liquid chromatography system equipped with refractive index detector (Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany) or a CECIL 1100 Series liquid chromatography pump (Cecil Instruments Ltd. Cambridge, UK) equipped with a GBC LC‐1240 refractive index detector (GBC Scientific Equipment, Braeside, VIC, Australia), using a Supelcosil SPLC‐Si 5 µm (250 × 10 mm, Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA), an Econosphere Silica 10u (250 × 10 mm, Grace, Columbia, MD, USA) or a Kromasil 100‐10‐SIL (250 × 10 mm, Akzonobel, Eka Chemicals AB, Separation Products, Bohus, Sweden) column. Thin layer chromatography (TLC) was performed with Kieselgel 60 F254 aluminum‐backed plates (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and spots were visualized after spraying with a solution of H2SO4 in MeOH (20%, v/v) followed by heating. The 3D structures of compounds 1, 2, 9, 12, and 21 were generated and optimized in HyperChem Professional 8.0.8 (Hypercube, Inc.).

Biological Material

Specimens of D. fasciola and D. mediterranea were collected from Plymiri bay at the island of Rhodes in August of 2009 and from Agios Theodoros bay at the island of Karpathos in August of 2013, respectively. Cells

Vero (ATCC: CCL‐81; RRID:CVCL_0059) and Vero E6 (ATCC: CRL‐1586; RRID:CVCL_0574)—African green monkey kidney epithelial cells were maintained in Dulbecco‐modified Eagle's medium (DMEM, high glucose, Corning) supplemented with 3% heat‐inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, Life Technologies), penicillin (100 U/mL), streptomycin (100 µg/mL), and ciprofloxacin (5 µg/mL) at 37°C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. LLC‐Mk2 cells (ATCC: CCL‐7; RRID:CVCL_3009)—Macaca mulatta kidney epithelial cells were maintained in minimal essential medium (MEM) (two parts Hanks' MEM and one part Earle's MEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Poland) supplemented with 3% heat‐inactivated fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Poland), penicillin (100 U/mL), streptomycin (100 µg/mL), and ciprofloxacin (5 µg/mL) at 37°C under 5% CO2.

Viruses

HSV‐1 strain 17+ (0104151v) was acquired from Public Health England. In infection experiments, HSV‐1 at TCID50 = 400/mL was applied on Vero E6 cells and incubated for two days at 37°C. HCoV‐NL63 isolate Amsterdam 1 was contributed by Dr. Lia van der Hoek from Amsterdam University Medical Center, the Netherlands. In infection experiments, HCoV‐NL63 at TCID50 = 400/mL was applied on LLC‐Mk2 cells and incubated for 5 days at 32°C. ZIKV strain H/PF/2013 was acquired from European Virus Archive [31]. In infection experiments, ZIKV at TCID50 = 400/mL was applied on Vero cells and incubated for 3 days at 37°C.

Virus stocks were generated by infecting fully confluent the respective virus‐permissive cells with the virus at TCID50 of 400/mL. Cells were cultured until the appearance of the cytopathic effect (CPE). The cultures were lysed by freezing at −80°C for at least 2 h, thawed, aliquoted, and stored at −80°C. Mock samples were produced in the same manner using uninfected cells. The virus titers were determined according to Reed and Muench formula [32]. The number of CPE‐positive wells was scored 2–5 days post‐infection (p.i.), as indicated above for each virus.

Antiviral Activity Assay

The compounds’ ability to inhibit virus replication was determined by infecting confluent monolayers of the respective virus‐permissive cells in the presence of selected compound concentrations or no compound as a control. Cells in 96‐well plates were overlaid with media containing the tested compounds and incubated for 30 min at 37°C. Subsequently, the medium was discarded, and cells were infected with the respective virus or mock‐infected for 2 h in the presence or absence of the potential inhibitor. Following the incubation, cells were washed thrice with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and overlaid with fresh media supplemented with the inhibitors or reference samples. Media samples for qPCR were collected 2–5 days p. i., as indicated above for each virus. Virus genetic material was isolated using Viral DNA/RNA isolation kit (A&A Biotechnology, Poland). For RNA viruses (HCoV‐NL63 and ZIKV) additional step of reverse transcription was carried out with High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermofisher Scientific, Poland), according to manufacturers’ protocols.

qPCR

To assess virus yield, DNA standards were subjected to qPCR along with the DNA or cDNA acquired from the isolated samples. qPCR was performed using Real‐time qPCR kit (RT‐PCR mix, A&A Biotechnology, Poland), virus‐specific primers and fluorescent probes (Table 5). The thermal profile was 3 min at 95°C followed by 37 cycles of 2 s at 95°C and 20 s at 60°C, and the reaction was conducted in a CFX96 Touch Real‐Time PCR Detection System (Bio‐Rad).

Cytotoxicity Assay

Confluent cells on 96‐well plates were washed with PBS, overlaid with medium supplemented with a range of compound concentrations, and further incubated for two‐five days at 37°C. Cell viability was examined using XTT Cell Viability Assay (Biological Industries, Poland), according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, the medium was removed, and each well received 50 µL of fresh standard medium mixed with 50 µL of activated XTT solution. Following a 2‐h incubation at 37°C, the supernatant was transferred to a new transparent 96‐well plate. The absorbance of the formazan product was then measured at 490 nm using a colorimeter (Tecan i‐control Infinite 200 Microplate Reader, version 1.5.14.0). Results were normalized to the control, with cell viability defined as 100%.

Statistical Analysis

Pre‐processing of data: For cytotoxicity assays (Figure 6a–c), raw values were normalized to DMSO‐treated control cells; the control condition was defined as 100%, and all other values were expressed relative to this reference. For infection experiments (Figure 6d–i), data were log10‐transformed and are presented as differences relative to DMSO‐treated, virus‐infected control samples.

Normality of data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and potential outliers were identified using Grubbs’ test. Data are presented as bar charts, with bars representing the mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Sample sizes were as follows: for cytotoxicity assays (Figure 6a–c), n ≥ 6; for antiviral efficiency assays (Figure 6d–f), n ≥ 3; and for functional assays (Figure 6g–i), n ≥ 4.

For statistical comparisons, datasets with normal distribution were analyzed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's multiple comparisons test. When data did not meet normality assumptions, the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's multiple comparisons test was applied. The significance threshold was set at α = 0.05. P values are reported as follows: 0.0332 (*), 0.0021 (**), 0.0002 (***), <0.0001 (****). GraphPad Prism 9 was used for statistical analysis.
