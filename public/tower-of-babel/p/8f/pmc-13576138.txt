A network analysis examining mental health issues, including anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder, in colorectal cancer patients
Authors: Yi Zhou, Wen Teng, Liang Luo, Linan Du, Qiuyu Gu, Ping Zhou, Haiying Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs the incidence of colorectal cancer increases, patients face heightened stress and mental health challenges associated diagnosis. Mental health disorders such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, and anxiety can greatly affect the overall physical and psychological health of these patients. This study aims to investigate the associations between anxiety, depression, and PTSD symptoms in colorectal cancer patients.MethodsThis study involved 312 colorectal cancer patients who completed a general information questionnaire, the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5), and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). Data were analyzed using network analysis in R.ResultsThe analysis identified depression as the key central node in the network. Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a common malignant tumor of the gastrointestinal tract, with approximately 1.9 million new cases worldwide in 2022 and about 904,000 related deaths [1]. Colorectal cancer is increasingly prevalent in China, with both the incidence and mortality rates showing a significant upward trend [2]. With recent advancements in diagnostic and treatment technologies, CRC patients now have improved long-term survival outcomes [3]. However, due to cancer diagnosis, treatment, financial burdens, and poor prognosis, colorectal cancer survivors often experience various psychological issues, including post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), anxiety, and depression [4, 5]. These psychological symptoms can decrease the quality of life and patients’ survival rates, leading to significant adverse effects [6, 7].

The prevalence of PTSD in colorectal cancer patients has been reported to range from 11.2% to 32.3% [5, 8]. PTSD primarily involves intrusive memories, avoidance behaviors, negative alterations in cognition and mood, and hyperarousal [9]. Prolonged psychological stress can lead to alterations in immune system function [10] and may result in adverse health outcomes, including self-harm and suicide [11].

Previous studies on PTSD often assessed severity using scale total scores, overlooking the complex interactions among symptoms [12]. Network analysis emphasizes the uniqueness of each PTSD symptom and the structure of relationships among symptoms [13]. In the network, symptom nodes are connected through edges, reflecting the strength of statistical associations between symptoms [14]. Its main advantage is the identification of the role of individual symptoms within the network structure. Furthermore, network analysis can identify bridging symptoms between different psychopathologies that show strong cross-network associations, thereby enhancing the understanding of comorbidity patterns [15].

Previous studies have examined mental health issues in various populations [16–18], such as the general public, veterans, and first responders, encompassing symptoms of anxiety, depression, and PTSD. In oncology settings, anxiety and depression among cancer patients have also been widely documented, and recent network analysis studies have provided insights into symptom interactions [19–22]. Our previous study [23] examined the network structure of PTSD symptoms among colorectal cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy. However, it focused exclusively on PTSD and did not account for comorbid anxiety and depression or cross-disorder symptom interactions.

Despite growing interest in symptom network analysis in oncology, research specifically investigating the cross-disorder network of PTSD, anxiety, and depression symptoms in colorectal cancer patients remains limited. Moreover, PTSD symptom networks may vary across different types of traumatic experiences [24], suggesting that findings from other populations or cancer types may not be directly generalizable to colorectal cancer patients.

Therefore, the present study aimed to examine the cross-disorder network of PTSD, anxiety, and depression symptoms among colorectal cancer patients and to identify central and bridge symptoms, as well as cross-disorder symptom interactions. Based on previous network studies in psychopathology, we hypothesized that certain symptoms would exhibit high centrality and that bridge symptoms would play a key role in connecting comorbid conditions.

Methods

Participants and procedures

Patients with colorectal cancer from a tertiary general hospital in Wuxi, Jiangsu Province, China, participated in this study between November 2023 and June 2024. All patients were confirmed to have colorectal cancer through pathological examination, had undergone surgical treatment, and were classified as TNM stages Ⅰ–Ⅲ. The present dataset was partially derived from the same recruitment source as our previous study [23]; however, the inclusion criteria and research objectives differed substantially between the two studies. Other inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years, with clear consciousness and basic reading comprehension; and (2) awareness of their diagnosis and medical condition. Patients were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) diagnosis of cancer for less than one month; (2) a history of mental illness; (3) exposure to significant traumatic events within the past six months (e.g., natural disasters or the death of a close relative); (4) presence of other malignancies; and (5) current participation in other intervention studies.

Participants were informed of the study purpose by the researchers prior to data collection. After obtaining informed consent, paper-based questionnaires were distributed. A standardized script was used to provide guidance and answer participants’ questions during questionnaire completion. Completed questionnaires were checked and collected immediately by the researchers. A total of 324 questionnaires were distributed, and 312 valid responses were collected, resulting in a valid response rate of 96.29%. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuxi No. 2 People’s Hospital (No. 2023Y-172), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Measures

Data were collected on the patients’ gender, age, education level, marital status, residence, monthly household income, tumor type, presence of enterostomy, cancer stage, treatment, and length of diagnosis.

To evaluate the severity of PTSD symptoms, we utilized the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) [25]. It consists of four dimensions, including intrusive symptoms, avoidance symptoms, negative alterations in mood and cognitions symptoms, and hyperarousal symptoms, comprising 20 items. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely). Total scores range from 0 to 80, with a cut-off score of 33 or higher indicating probable PTSD. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the PCL-5 in this study was 0.882.

To measure levels of anxiety and depression in patients, we employed the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [26], which consists of two subscales: anxiety and depression. The scale consists of 14 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 to 3. Scores for each subscale range from 0 to 21, with scores ≥ 8 indicating clinically significant symptoms. The Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.907 for anxiety subscale and 0.924 for the depression subscale. HADS-A and HADS-D were included as composite indicators of affective distress rather than symptom-level nodes in the network analysis.

In this study, PCL-5 was analyzed at the item level to capture the fine-grained symptom structure of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). In contrast, HADS was included at the subscale level (HADS-A and HADS-D), consistent with its original factorial structure and its common application as a dimensional measure of anxiety and depression in network analysis studies. Although this leads to differences in node granularity across instruments, such mixed-level approach has been widely adopted in previous psychometric network studies integrating symptom-level and dimensional constructs. This approach may introduce measurement heterogeneity and affect the magnitude and comparability of centrality estimates across nodes, particularly for HADS-A and HADS-D, which represent broader affective constructs rather than symptom-level nodes.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics for demographic characteristics, clinical data, and scale scores were conducted using SPSS 29.0 software. Various packages in R 4.4.1 software were used to conduct network analysis.

The qgraph package was used for EBICglasso regularization network estimation of PTSD, anxiety, and depression [27]. A default tuning parameter 0.5 was applied to the extended Bayesian information criterion to remove edges with relatively weak connections. The parameter aimed to achieve a sparser and more concise symptom network, facilitating a more straightforward interpretation of the results. In the network, shorter and thicker edges between network nodes imply closer and stronger associations. Positive correlations are depicted in blue, while negative correlations are represented in red.

The qgraph package calculated each node’s expected influence (EI) [28]. By incorporating positive and negative edges, the expected influence reflects the cumulative edge weights connecting a node to its adjacent nodes. A higher EI value reveals that the node is more important. The bridge expected influence (BEI) for each node was estimated using the networktools package [15], which assesses the interconnectedness of nodes across different communities. The 80th percentile of the BEI values served as the threshold, suggesting that a more substantial BEI value for a node correlates with a higher probability of activating the comorbidity network.

The bootnet package was used to determine the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for edge weights and the correlation stability (CS) through non-parametric bootstrapping with 1000 samples, assessing the precision and reliability of the network [29]. Narrower CIs with less overlap suggest a higher accuracy of the network edge weights. The CS should be at least 0.25, ideally exceeding 0.50; a higher CS signifies more excellent stability of the network centrality measures.

The NetworkComparisonTest package [30] was used to conduct network comparison analyses based on gender (male and female), length of diagnosis (≤ 6 months and > 6 months), and cancer stage (I/II and III). The study first examined the network structure and global strength variations and subsequently compared edge weight differences among networks that showed structural changes. The present study defined statistical significance as 0.05. There were no missing data in the present study.

Network structures

Figure 1A shows the network structure of PTSD, anxiety, and depression in colorectal cancer patients, comprising 22 nodes. Bootstrap analysis based on 1000 resampled networks indicated that the estimated network structure demonstrated acceptable stability and accuracy. The network consists of 104 non-zero edges (95% CI [97, 135]), with an average edge weight of 0.044 (95% CI [0.039, 0.045]) and a network density of 0.45 (95% CI [0.42, 0.58]). Most edges are positive (n = 95, 91.35%, shown in blue), while a smaller portion is negative (n = 9, 8.65%, shown in red). The connections between the two nodes are shorter, thicker, and darker in color, indicating stronger correlations. Associations within dimensions were generally stronger than those between dimensions.

Network structure diagrams. A Networks of PTSD, anxiety, and depression. B EI values of nodes. C BEI values of nodes. PTSD, post-traumatic stress disorder; HADS, Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale

The strongest edge was observed between avoidance of thoughts (P6) and avoidance of reminders (P7) (edge weight = 0.86, 95% CI [0.82, 0.90]), followed by the edges between hypervigilance (P17) and exaggerated startle response (P18) (edge weight = 0.67, 95% CI [0.60, 0.74]), anxiety and depression (edge weight = 0.56, 95% CI [0.49, 0.63]), intrusive thoughts (P1) and emotional cue reactivity (P4) (edge weight = 0.35, 95% CI [0.25, 0.44]), and self-blame or blame of others (P10) and irritability (P15) (edge weight = 0.33, 95% CI [0.23, 0.44]). All reported edges had 95% confidence intervals that did not include zero, indicating reliable associations. Figure 2A displays the bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals of edge weights, indicating that the strongest edges were estimated with relatively good precision.

Network centrality and bridge symptoms

Figure 1B presents the expected influence (EI) centrality of the network. The nodes with the highest EI values were depression, negative trauma-related emotions (P11), anxiety, emotional cue reactivity (P4), as well as restricted affect (P14). Conversely, the symptom with the lowest expected influence was self-destructive or reckless behavior (P16). Figure 1C illustrates the bridge expected influence (BEI) centrality of the network. The highest BEI values were found for depression, anxiety, loss of interest (P12), sleep disturbance (P20), and intrusive thoughts (P1). Figure 2B depicts the stability analysis of the centrality indices. The correlation stability (CS) coefficients for EI and BEI were 0.673 and 0.75, respectively. Both coefficients exceed 0.5, indicating good stability of the EI and BEI centrality estimates.

Network comparison

No significant differences were found in either network structures or global network strength between gender networks, between networks with a disease duration of ≤ 6 months and > 6 months, and between networks with cancer stages I/II and III. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as the limited statistical power of the subgroup analyses may have reduced the ability to detect potential network differences.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is among the first to examine cross-disorder symptom interactions among PTSD, anxiety, and depression using a network approach in Chinese colorectal cancer patients. The present study was conducted in the same hospital and during a similar recruitment period as our previous study [23]. Accordingly, the two studies partially overlapped in sample recruitment. However, the two studies differed substantially in their inclusion criteria and research focus. The previous study examined only PTSD symptom networks among colorectal cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy, whereas the current study focuses on postoperative patients with TNM stage I–III and adopted a cross-disorder network approach incorporating PTSD, anxiety, and depression symptoms. Therefore, the present study addressed a distinct research question and extended our previous work by exploring cross-disorder symptom interactions and bridge symptoms. These findings are also consistent with the growing body of psycho-oncological research highlighting the co-occurrence and interaction of PTSD, anxiety, and depression in cancer populations [31, 32].

Consistent with our theoretical expectations and previous network studies [23, 24, 33], our results showed the strongest edge between avoidance of thoughts (P6) and avoidance of reminders (P7). Depression, anxiety, loss of interest (P12), sleep disturbance (P20), and intrusive thoughts (P1) were identified as the primary bridge symptoms in the network. These bridge symptoms may play an important role in linking PTSD, anxiety, and depression symptoms among colorectal cancer patients. Early identification and intervention targeting these bridge symptoms may help reduce symptom interactions across disorders.

It is important to note that the present network combined item-level PTSD symptoms (PCL-5) with subscale-level measures of anxiety and depression (HADS-A and HADS-D). This difference in measurement granularity may have influenced the observed network structure, particularly the prominence of anxiety and depression nodes. Therefore, centrality estimates involving these broader constructs should be interpreted with caution.

No significant differences in network structure or global strength were observed across gender, disease duration, or tumor stage groups. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the limited statistical power of subgroup analyses may have reduced the ability to detect subtle network differences.

The strongest edges identified in the network were between avoidance of thoughts and avoidance of reminders. This finding is consistent with previous studies highlighting the central role of avoidance in the persistence of PTSD symptoms [14, 24, 34]. From a theoretical perspective, different forms of avoidance may mutually reinforce each other, as efforts to suppress trauma-related thoughts can increase sensitivity to external reminders, while avoidance of trauma-related cues may further prevent cognitive processing of the traumatic experience [35]. This reciprocal reinforcement may contribute to the persistence and rigidity of avoidance behaviors over time [19]. Clinically, this close association suggests that targeting one form of avoidance may have downstream effects on other avoidance-related symptoms, potentially weakening interconnected avoidance symptoms within the PTSD network [36]. In particular, exposure-based interventions, which directly reduce avoidance by facilitating gradual confrontation with trauma-related thoughts and cues, have been shown to be effective in reducing symptom severity and interrupting this maintenance cycle [37–40]. These findings support the early implementation of exposure-oriented or cognitive-behavioral strategies in trauma-exposed populations.

Depression was identified as both a bridge symptom and a highly central component of the network in this study. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies [21, 22, 41]. Clinically, depression may represent an important link between PTSD and broader psychological comorbidity [42]. Routine screening for depressive symptoms in colorectal cancer patients may facilitate early identification and intervention for comorbid psychological distress [41]. Additionally, loss of interest, another bridge symptom, reflects the close association between PTSD and depression [43]. In clinical settings, it may indicate emotional withdrawal and reduced engagement in treatment [44]. Behavioral activation strategies and cognitive behavior therapy (CBT) may help restore engagement in daily activities and reduce maladaptive emotional processes [45, 46]. While interventions for PTSD have primarily focused on trauma-focused therapies [47], future research directly targeting loss of interest may also be beneficial.

Anxiety, identified as a bridging symptom in this study, is consistent with previous findings [11, 48]. One possible explanation for the high centrality of anxiety may be its association with disease-related fears [49], such as concerns about treatment side effects, cancer metastasis, and disease recurrence [50]. These fears may strengthen the association between anxiety and PTSD symptoms. This finding supports integrating anxiety management into routine cancer care, including psychoeducation and cognitive-behavioral interventions [51]. Additionally, intrusive thoughts were identified as another bridge node within the network. Intrusive thoughts represent a maladaptive form of spontaneous cognition and are prevalent across various mental disorders [52]. Cognitive-behavioral therapy and exposure-based treatments have been shown to alleviate intrusive symptoms and reduce trauma-related distress [47, 51].

Sleep disturbance was also identified as a key bridge symptom, consistent with longitudinal evidence suggesting its association with subsequent increases in depression and anxiety within PTSD symptom networks over time [53]. It may also be associated with systemic immune dysregulation (e.g., C-reactive protein and interleukin-6), which may be related to psychological distress [54, 55]. Given its high prevalence and modifiability, sleep disturbance represents an important target for intervention, including sleep hygiene education and cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) [56, 57]. Additionally, sleep problems may serve as a practical entry point for early psychological screening. Overall, targeting bridge symptoms may provide a more efficient strategy for reducing psychological comorbidity in cancer patients.

In our study, self-destructive/reckless behavior showed the lowest centrality within the network. Previous research suggests that suicidal ideation may decrease in individuals experiencing cancer-related trauma [58]. The perceived threat associated with serious illness may alter the expression of self-destructive thoughts and behaviors. Although reckless behavior and suicidal tendencies differ in severity, studies have reported a strong association between them [11]. This may partially explain the relatively low centrality of reckless behavior observed in the present network.

Clinical implications

This study highlights the complex network relationships among PTSD, anxiety, and depression symptoms in colorectal cancer patients, emphasizing the potential importance of bridge symptoms such as depression, anxiety, loss of interest, sleep disturbances, and intrusive thoughts in symptom co-occurrence. Targeting these bridge symptoms through early psychological interventions may help reduce cross-disorder symptom interactions in clinical practice. For instance, cognitive reappraisal strategies and exposure-based therapies have been shown to alleviate anxiety and intrusive symptoms, while interventions targeting sleep problems may also contribute to improved psychological well-being. Therefore, comprehensive treatment approaches focusing on bridge symptoms may help improve mental health outcomes in colorectal cancer patients. Furthermore, given cultural perceptions surrounding mental disorders, implementing screening and preventive measures for sleep issues may facilitate earlier identification of psychological distress and support patients in managing mental health challenges more effectively.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding symptom relationships. Second, the use of self-report mearsures for PTSD, anxiety, and depression may have introduced recall and reporting biases. Third, the study was conducted exclusively among colorectal cancer patients in China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other cultural contexts and cancer populations. Fourth, the subgroup network comparison analyses may have been underpowered due to limited sample sizes within each subgroup, potentially reducing the ability to detect subtle network differences. Finally, the combination of item-level PTSD symptoms (PCL-5) and subscale-level anxiety and depression measures (HADS-A and HADS-D) resulted in unequal node granularity, introducing measurement heterogeneity that may have influenced centrality estimates and increased the prominence of broader constructs such as anxiety and depression. Therefore, centrality comparisons involving these nodes should be interpreted cautiously. Future studies should consider using uniform item-level measures across constructs, longitudinal designs, and structured clinical interviews, as well as conducting validation studies in more diverse cultural and cancer populations.

Conclusions

This study provides a preliminary insight into the network relationships among PTSD, anxiety, and depression in colorectal cancer patients in China. Consistent with our theoretical expectations, several symptoms demonstrated important bridge roles within the network. Depression, anxiety, loss of interest, sleep disturbance, and intrusive thoughts were identified as important bridge symptoms within the network, highlighting their potential relevance in cross-disorder symptom interactions. These findings suggest that interventions targeting bridge symptoms may contribute to improved psychological outcomes in colorectal cancer patients. Future longitudinal studies are needed to further clarify the temporal dynamics and clinical significance of these symptom relationships.
