Management and outcomes of solid pseudopapillary neoplasm of the pancreas: a systematic review
Authors: Arianna Pontrelli, Giovanna Di Meo, Francesco Paolo Prete, Belinda De Simone, Mario Testini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Solid pseudopapillary neoplasm of the pancreas (SPN-P) is a rare entity, accounting for about 0.9–2.7% of all exocrine and 5% of cystic pancreatic neoplasms. There is limited evidence on the clinical, surgical, and oncological characteristics of SPN-P, as well as surgical short- and long-term outcomes. A systematic review was conducted searching four databases (MEDLINE/PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus and Cochrane Library) from January 2004 to August 2024, adhering to the reporting standards for systematic reviews. Eligible studies of provided information on key clinical features of SPN-P, surgical management, and surgical and oncological outcomes were reviewed. A total of 1315 studies were identified, of which 54 met the inclusion criteria. Data from 1.888 SPN-P patients, comprising 1.617 women (85.6%) and 271 men (14.4%), with a mean age of 30, 7 years (range: 7–87 years) were collected. Of these patients, 1.882 underwent surgical intervention, and 1.869 received pancreatic resection. The most common postoperative complication was postoperative pancreatic fistula, which was more prevalent in cases of parenchyma-preserving surgery (23%). Other complications included wound infections, intra-abdominal collections, delayed gastric emptying, and pancreatic insufficiency. Surgical intervention remains the gold standard for the treatment of SPN-P, offering favourable oncological outcomes and an excellent prognosis. Nonetheless, further research is required to address unresolved aspects of SPN-P surgical management and optimize treatment protocols.

Introduction

SPN-P is a rare, low-grade pancreatic tumor, classified under the 2019 WHO guidelines. It accounts for approximately 0.9–2.7% of all exocrine pancreatic neoplasms and 5% of cystic pancreatic tumors [1]. Initially described by Frantz in 1959, SPN-P has been referred to by various names, including solid cystic tumor, papillary cystic tumor, Frantz tumor, and Hamoudi tumor [2–4]. SPN-P predominantly affects young women. In males, the disease manifests later, with a generally worse prognosis [2, 5]. Improved diagnostic techniques and increased awareness of pancreatic neoplasms have led to a rise in SPN-P diagnoses since 2020 [3]. Although specific risk factors remain unidentified, associations with familial adenomatous polyposis have been reported [6, 7]. The etiology and histogenesis of SPN-P remain unclear [8, 9]. Patients with SPN-P are often asymptomatic, with incidental detection during imaging or surgeries for unrelated conditions [2, 3, 10–13]. Surgery is the treatment of choice for SPN-P, with the approach determined by tumor size and location. Procedures include enucleation (EN), spleen-preserving distal pancreatectomy (DP), distal pancreatosplenectomy (DPS), central pancreatectomy (CP), pancreatoduodenectomy (PD), and duodenum-preserving cephalic pancreatectomy (DPCP) [2, 3]. Given the low-grade malignancy of SPN-P, parenchyma-preserving resections (PPR) such as EN, CP, and DPCP are considered effective oncological treatments. Recurrence occurs in 2–10% of cases, with predictive factors for recurrence and poor outcomes including male sex, positive margins (R1), lymph node (LN) involvement, perineural or vascular invasion, adjacent structure infiltration, high proliferation index, and undifferentiated carcinoma [14–16].

The aim of this paper is to systematically review the literature focusing on surgical approaches, postoperative complications, and outcomes in the management and treatment of SPN-P.

Materials and methods

This review adheres to the reporting standards for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) and is AMSTAR compliant [17, 18]. A protocol was developed a priori. Four large databases—MEDLINE/PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Cochrane Library—were searched, from January 2004 to August 2024, for studies examining the clinico-pathological characteristics, surgical management, and surgical and oncological outcomes of patients who were operated for SPN-P.

Literature search

The search strategy was developed to identify data from studies to answer the following research question:

(Patients): human participants with radiological and/or histological diagnoses of SPN-P undergoing pancreatic surgery for SPN-P

(Intervention): surgical resection of the pancreas

(Comparator): (none)

(Outcomes): preoperative investigations, intraoperative performance metrics, perioperative outcomes and complications, pathological features of resected SNP-P, follow-up outcomes, including oncological.

Free text terms used for search were: “solid pseudopapillary tumor”, combined with “pancreas”, “surgery” and “resect*”. The search was restricted to English-language articles. The reference lists of all retrieved and relevant publications identified were searched for further studies.

Selection of studies

Two independent reviewers (AP and GDM) examined the results of the literature search, removing duplicates. Two further reviewers (FPP and BDS) independently reviewed studies for eligibility in stages (in abstract form and, if appropriate, in full text) by verifying adherence to inclusion criteria and presence of the pre-established outcome measures.

Eligibility criteria

Prospective and retrospective studies involving human subjects, including comparative studies and case series studies were included where they offered data on any of the outcomes of interest: the type of surgical approach (open or minimally invasive), postoperative complications (e.g., POPF, DGE, abscesses or intra-abdominal collections, pancreatitis, pseudocysts, wound infection, pancreatic insufficiency, or death), and oncological outcomes (e.g. LN status, adjuvant therapy, recurrence, overall survival (OS), and mean follow-up). Studies providing data on multiple pancreatic neoplasms were included only if specific outcomes for SNP-P could be obtained. Systematic reviews, narrative reviews, commentaries, editorials, letters and case reports were excluded.

At each stage, reasons for excluding studies were documented. Disagreement regarding article selection was resolved by discussion and consensus or by consulting a third member of the review team (MT). All identified studies were saved in an EndNote (Clarivate, Ver. 9) database.

Available data on primary and secondary outcomes were independently extracted (AP and GDM) onto a dedicated form that had been trialed ahead of the study. Data were recorded onto two Microsoft Excel databases (Version 2019-Windows) that were then compared, reconciling disagreements.

Risk of bias analysis

Two researchers (FPP and AP) independently assessed the eligible studies for bias according to the Newcastle–Ottawa tool for observational studies [19]. A third author (MT) confirmed the final determination after discussion.

Data extraction and tabulation were performed using Microsoft Excel 365.

Quantitative synthesis of the included evidence and statistical analysis

Categorical variables were extracted as numerical values and reported as proportions. Where sufficient information was available, we attempted a subgroup analysis considering post-operative complications and type surgical procedure, considering major pancreatic resection and parenchymal-sparing resection, where studies offering data on the comparison between these two types of resection were identified among the included evidence. Where adequate numerical data were available from selected studies, these were pooled in a meta-analysis conducted using a dedicated review manager software (Revman version 5.4; Nordic Cochrane Center, Cochrane Collaboration, 2011). Only dichotomous data (e.g. POPF) were considered using a random effects model. We extracted the number of patients who experienced the outcome of interest, in order to estimate a relative risk (RR) and its 95% confidence interval (95% CI). When considering the association between procedure type and the occurrence of perioperative complications, these were evaluated by calculating the odds ratio (OR) and its corresponding 95% confidence interval (95% CI). The OR represents the likelihood of an event occurring in the Conventional Surgery (CS) group compared to the PPR group. To further illustrate the differences between these two procedures, the risk difference for perioperative complications was also calculated across the included studies. Both Cochrane’s Q test and Higgins’ I2 statistic were utilized to evaluate inter-study heterogeneity, with P-value < 0.1 or I2 > 50% considered indicative of significant heterogeneity. The choice between the random-effects and common-effects models was based on the level of heterogeneity observed in the included studies. The significance level was set at P ≤ 0.05, and all P-values were two-tailed.

The included studies spanned over 20 years, from 2004 to 2024. These were all retrospective, except one prospective study. The accrued evidence examined a total of 1.888 patients diagnosed with SPN-P, of whom 1.617 (85.6%) were women and 271 (14.4%) were men, with a mean age of 30.7 years (range: 7–87). The most frequently reported symptoms included abdominal pain, lower back pain, abdominal mass, nausea, vomiting, abdominal discomfort, and weight loss. Notably, approximately 34.7% of patients were asymptomatic.

In all patients radiological diagnosis was achieved via abdominal computed tomography (CT) scan, occasionally supplemented by abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) with Fine-Needle Aspiration Cytology (FNAC) or biopsy (FNAB) was not routinely performed, and was reserved to cases with uncertain radiological findings. Only 10.6% of patients underwent EUS-FNAC/FNAB, and among these, only 7.9% received a definitive preoperative diagnosis of SPN-P. In other cases, the preoperative cyto-histopathological diagnosis was either non-diagnostic or indicative of alternative pathologies, such as pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (pNETs) or lymphoma.

During imaging or surgical exploration, some cases of locally advanced SPN-P were identified, characterized by vascular or adjacent organ infiltration and, in rare instances, synchronous liver, peritoneal, or lung metastases (1 case) [43]. Among the 1.888 patients included in the review, 1.882 underwent surgery, and 1.869 received pancreatic resections (Table 1). In one case, an 87-year-old patient with SPN-P was not a surgical candidate due to advanced age rather than tumor unresectability. At a follow-up of 3.3 years, this patient remained alive [59]. Of the remaining 13 patients, 2 underwent surgical biopsy of the pancreatic lesion [56, 64], 1 underwent gastrojejunostomy [43], 1 underwent bilio-digestive anastomosis with a simultaneous pancreatic lesion biopsy [41], and 1 underwent palliative surgery not specified by the authors [55]. Additionally, surgical resection of recurrent disease was performed in six cases: 4 patients with liver recurrence, 1 with retroperitoneal recurrence and 1 with recurrence in the pancreatic head [14, 37, 70]. In all other cases, the disease was deemed unresectable. The review also analyzed the surgical approach. Minimally invasive surgery (MIS) was employed in 14.6% of cases, with its use increasing after 2014 (Table 1).

Of the studies analyzed, only 11 reported postoperative exocrine and/or endocrine insufficiency [26, 41, 42, 48, 52, 58, 61, 62, 66, 68, 71] (Table 2). Furthermore, few studies correlated the type of postoperative complications with the surgical procedure performed [28, 34, 43, 45, 47, 48, 58, 66]. The most common complication was POPF, with a higher prevalence in PPR (23%) compared to CS (14%). Conversely, intra-abdominal collections were more frequent in CSs (7.3% vs 2.7%). Discussion

SPN-P is a rare pancreatic tumor, accounting for approximately 0.9–2.7% of all exocrine pancreatic neoplasms and about 5% of cystic pancreatic neoplasms [1].

Surgical resection is the cornerstone of treatment for SPNs-P. The choice of surgical procedure is primarily based on tumor location and size. DP with spleen preservation is preferred for lesions in the pancreatic body or tail when feasible, while PD is tipically performed for tumors in the pancreatic head. For lesions in the pancreatic neck, other procedures such as DPCP, EN, and CP may be considered. Total pancreatectomy (TP) is generally reserved for multifocal SPNs. However, prophylactic TP is considered overly aggressive due to the high risk of associated morbidities, particularly endocrine and exocrine insufficiency [73]. Standard lymphadenectomy is typically recommended for tumors larger than 5 cm or those exhibiting high malignancy phenotypes [74]. Nevertheless, in this systematic review, it was unclear whether standard lymphadenectomy, as routinely performed for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), or simple LN sampling was undertaken. Given the low incidence of LN metastasis in SPN-P, routine lymphadenectomy may be unnecessary, although no consensus exists on this issue .

For locally advanced SPN-P, surgical resection is feasible in combination with vascular resections or removal of adjacent infiltrated organs, similar to approaches used in PDAC [75]. In our review, such complex procedures were performed in 1.3% and 1.0% of cases, respectively. In select patients with resectable metastatic disease, synchronous resection—including liver metastasectomy or lobectomy—was also performed (approximately 1.0% of cases), depending on the number and location of metastases. Both open and minimally invasive approaches, including laparoscopic and robotic techniques, are viable options for pancreatic surgery. MIS offers advantages such as reduced postoperative pain, smaller incisions, quicker recovery, and shorter hospital stays. However, in our review, only 14.6% of patients underwent MIS, highlighting its limited availability outside of high-volume, specialized centers.

Our analysis suggests that PPRs are associated with higher postoperative morbidity than CSs. Reported complication rates range from 25 to 70% after CP [76, 77], and 43–47% after EN [78, 79], compared to 30–50% after PD and 25–30% after DP [80]. The main contributor to these differences is the higher incidence of POPF following PPR [81], despite PPR being associated with shorter operative time and reduced intraoperative blood loss [82, 83].

SPN-P rarely causes bile duct or pancreatic duct obstruction, therefore, the surrounding pancreatic parenchyma is typically characterized by a soft pancreatic texture and a small-caliber main pancreatic duct, both of which are risk factors for POPF. Managing the pancreatic stump remains critical to minimizing postoperative complications [84].

Reported POPF rates vary widely: 0–63% after CP [76, 77], 27–50% after EN [78, 79], 25–30% after PD, and 15–25% after DP [80]. The higher rates in PPR may result from the presence of two pancreatic stumps after CP or proximity to the PPD in EN [81]. In our review, 13.6% of patients developed POPF, with a higher incidence in the PPR group (23%) compared to the CS group (14%). However, not all studies reported the correlation between surgical technique and POPF occurrence. Several techniques are under investigation to reduce the incidence of POPF, including the Blumgart anastomosis and various modifications of pancreaticogastrostomy [85]. Additionally, CS was associated with higher rates of abdominal collections (7.3%) and DGE (1.5%) compared to PPR (2.7% and 0%) [28, 34, 43, 45, 47, 48, 58, 66].

Late complications, such as exocrine and endocrine insufficiency, significantly impact patients’ quality of life and long-term nutritional status [86, 87]. These complications are less common after PPR due to greater preservation of functional pancreatic tissue. Nonetheless, outcomes depend on both the volume and quality of residual parenchyma. Yamada et al. suggest that preserving at least 50% of pancreatic parenchyma may maintain adequate gland function. Authors propose preoperative volumetric assessments to guide the transection plane and help prevent postoperative diabetes. This approach is especially applicable to resections not involving pancreatic-enteric anastomoses [88].

Our systematic review revealed challenges in evaluating the incidence of postoperative diabetes and exocrine insufficiency due to the limited detail provided in the literature. Correlating these complications with specific surgical approaches is essential, but current data remain insufficient. Considering the indolent behaviour of SPN-P and the favourable prognosis, PPR may be a suitable option for younger patients with good performance status, as they are more likely to tolerate complications and benefit from preserved pancreatic function. Literature supports excellent OS and long disease-free intervals for these patients, despite higher rates of comorbidities [89].

Nevertheless, as with PDAC surgery, outcomes in SPN-P surgery should be assessed according to benchmark values to objectively evaluate surgical performance. Maintaining results within established benchmarks is critical to ensuring high-quality care. Benchmarking in pancreatic surgery plays an essential role in optimizing outcomes, allowing for meaningful comparisons across centers and informing improvements in technique and perioperative management [90].

SPN-P is associated with favourable oncological outcomes, with an OS rate of approximately 98%. In this systematic review, only 5.1% of patients undergoing pancreatic resection for SPN-P were found to have positive LNs (pN +). This finding, however, does not adequately reflect the true rate of LN metastasis, as not all patients underwent lymphadenectomy. In cases where lymphadenectomy was performed, the reasons—such as evidence of invasive tumor characteristics or intraoperative lymphadenopathy—were often unspecified. Additionally, it was unclear whether standard lymphadenectomy, as performed for PDAC, or simple LN sampling was performed.

Identified risk factors for recurrence include large tumor size, R1 resection, tumor rupture, male sex, and young age [91]. Neoadjuvant therapy was rare, reported in one case, while 17 patients received adjuvant therapy. Among the 13 patients who did not undergo resection, most received palliative interventions, such as gastrointestinal bypass or debulking of recurrent disease.

Postoperative prognoses for SPN-P patients often exceeded five years, with survival ranging from six months to over 17 years [2]. PPR were performed in approximately 20% of patients in our review, reflecting the low malignancy grade and minimal metastatic tendency of SPN-P. PPRs, such as EN and CP, are recommended under specific conditions. For instance, EN is suitable for low-grade malignant tumors smaller than 2 cm that are located at least 2–3 mm from the main pancreatic duct. CP is indicated for body-neck lesions smaller than 5 cm. However, SPN-P presenting with signs of aggressiveness, such as vascular infiltration, is not suitable for PPR [13].

Conclusions

This systematic review confirms that SPN-P is a pancreatic neoplasm with an excellent prognosis. Surgical resection remains the primary treatment, tailored according to tumor size, location, and patient characteristics. While PPR offers the advantage of preserving pancreatic function, it carries a higher risk of complications such as POPF. MIS is effective but remain underutilized outside specialized centers. Further studies are needed to standardize practices, such as the extent of lymphadenectomy and the correlation between postoperative complications and surgical approaches. Future efforts should aim to optimize individualized surgical strategies while minimizing morbidity and ensuring long-term patient survival.
