A feasibility randomised controlled trial of patient education videos assessing multimedia design effects on engagement and learning in adolescents with scoliosis
Authors: Garett Van Oirschot, Ciarán Purcell, Alison Keogh, Ciara M Greene, Cailbhe Doherty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultimedia patient education is increasingly used in healthcare, particularly useful in under-resourced settings. While scientific content is well studied, limited research examines how design influences patient engagement. Scoliosis often lacks adequate resources, and integration of multimedia learning principles in this context remains underexplored.Objectives This triple-masked three-armed feasibility parallel randomised controlled trial (RCT) assessed the viability of examining how evidence-informed design principles affect knowledge, engagement, quality of life, anxiety, and physical activity among Irish community-based adolescents with scoliosis.Methods Forty-six participants aged 10–18 were randomised into usual care (UC), standard video education (TRAD), or videos incorporating multimedia learning principles (AUGM). Videos were delivered weekly online over six weeks. Primary outcomes: feasibility of recruitment, intervention adherence, outcome completion, satisfaction and adverse events. Secondary outcomes: knowledge (quizzes), engagement (YouTube analytics), quality of life (EQ-5D-Y-3 L, SRS-22r), anxiety (STAI-Ch), and physical activity (PAQ-Ch)—were evaluated at baseline, post-intervention and week 8.Results Of 112 respondents, 46 participants (mean age 14.8 ± 2.0, 86% female) consented and were randomised (UC: n = 16, AUGM: n = 15, TRAD: n = 15). Baseline completion was 93.5%, weekly adherence ranged from 77 to 97% and follow-up completion was 82.6%. No adverse events occurred. 80% were satisfied with the videos. Knowledge improved by 9% (± 15%) across all groups and improved more when engagement with the videos was higher (p=0.011). Dropouts in the AUGM group were lower (n = 1) vs. the UC or TRAD groups (n = 3 each).Conclusion This feasibility RCT demonstrated successful recruitment, retention, and outcome completion, supporting a fully powered RCT to evaluate multimedia design principles in scoliosis education.Practice implications Delivering an online educational intervention is feasible and expands reach to rural/remote patients with scoliosis and reduce travel burdens. Online education fosters learning, especially for those who engage more on the first viewing. Introduction

Patient education is a cornerstone of musculoskeletal care, equipping individuals with knowledge and skills to manage their condition effectively [1–10]. As healthcare delivery evolves, multimedia education offers a powerful, accessible medium for conveying information through interactive and engaging formats tailored to diverse populations [11]. It enhances learning by simplifying complex concepts, improving engagement [12],and—via digital formats—can overcome limitations like clinician shortages or geographical distance [13]. Children and adolescents with chronic musculoskeletal conditions, such as Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis (AIS), may be especially well-positioned to benefit from multimedia education. This population is typically more digitally literate and requires support for long-term self-management. Even in mild to moderate severity, AIS may require treatment and lifestyle changes, making high-quality, accessible education essential [14, 15].

Despite its advantages, most research on multimedia patient education prioritises content rather than design—such as format, layout, or style. Where design research does exist, it tends to focus on non-musculoskeletal conditions [16–29]. In musculoskeletal healthcare, available guidance is mostly derived from narrative advice [30–32] or non-randomised studies [15, 33]. Nevertheless, well-designed education could potentially bridge gaps in healthcare systems facing limited resources or remote locations [13]. Scoliosis services in public healthcare exemplify this challenge, with resource constraints and long waitlists [34–36]. Like many musculoskeletal services, they cannot rely solely on one-to-one consultations to deliver education [37]. In response, digital innovations in healthcare have expanded access to healthcare information. For scoliosis patients and families, whose information needs are well-documented [15, 38, 39], online multimedia education offers a scalable, cost-effective method [13, 40, 41] of delivering information at scale without needing physical proximity [42, 43]. Online delivery also enables tracking of the proxies of content engagement via affective (likes/shares), behavioural (views or watch time) and cognitive (test score) engagement dimensions, as measured in previous research using YouTube videos [44–46]. These capabilities can support more responsive and personalised education strategies.

Multimedia education has been extensively studied in the pedagogical literature, yielding evidence-based recommendations to designing materials that enhance engagement and learning [11, 12, 45, 46], perhaps the most prominent being the Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning [12] [Supplemental Appendix A]. These principles have informed healthcare education tools, such as mobile apps for heart failure clinics that prioritise engagement, user confidence, and navigability [47]. They have also guided graphic design for breast health screening and treatment materials to improve clarity, comprehension and acceptability [19]. Similarly, web-based animations for kidney donation education have applied these principles to ensure suitability, acceptability and feasibility [48].

These are just a sample of the many healthcare examples where design principles have improved healthcare education materials [16–29]. However, their application in musculoskeletal care remains limited. To date, only one randomised controlled trial (RCT) in adults has evaluated their impact on osteoarthritis-related pain [49], with other musculoskeletal areas relying primarily on narrative advice [30–32]. In paediatric musculoskeletal care, such design principles [12] have not undergone RCTs, leaving scoliosis healthcare without evidence-informed guidance on educational material design. This underscores the need to identify optimal design strategies for the underserved [34, 35, 50] AIS population.

Given the uncertainties around recruiting this age group from the community and ensuring consistent engagement with educational content over time, a feasibility study was warranted to help inform the design and delivery of a fully powered trial.

This feasibility RCT aimed to assess the practicality of conducting a three-armed parallel RCT comparing the effectiveness of multimedia educational videos- designed with and without evidence-informed principles- against a usual care control group for AIS. The objective was to evaluate whether incorporating evidence-informed design advice using enhanced graphics and narration (Supplemental Appendix A) improves engagement, knowledge translation and retention, and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) at post-treatment, 8 weeks, and 26 weeks, compared to videos resembling traditional online resources [51] and usual care. We hypothesise that evidence-informed design would yield superior outcomes across these measures in the full trial.

Using the RE-AIM framework (Reach, Efficacy, Adoption, Implementation and Maintenance) [52], this feasibility study examined recruitment, intervention engagement, adherence, and outcome completion at baseline and week 8.

Methods

This triple-masked three-armed feasibility parallel RCT was conducted in Ireland and pre-registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. The protocol is reported in detail elsewhere [53] and any deviations are transparently reported below. The study followed CONSORT feasibility guidelines [54], TIDieR [55] for intervention reporting and RoB-2 [56] for minimising bias. The RE-AIM framework [52] guided planning and evaluation.

The trial was conducted in community settings across Ireland. Ethical approval was granted by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the authors’ institution. Protocol deviations are transparently reported. Data are available at osf.io/5qmf4/. The trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov as NCT06090344.

The protocol [53] was amended to include all idiopathic scoliosis cases aged 10–18 years to aid with recruitment. Inclusion criteria were Cobb angle ≥ 10° on radiograph, ability to access online materials and complete surveys, and for minors, parent/guardian consent. Non-idiopathic scoliosis was excluded.

Two adults with lived AIS experience, a scoliosis-specialised physiotherapist, and two researchers from prior AIS studies [15, 57, 58] were consulted to inform study design and intervention content.

Recruitment occurred through three scoliosis healthcare clinics, three scoliosis advocacy groups, social media and word of mouth. The first author (a physiotherapist with 18 years’ experience) conducted screening and provided participant information. Eligible participants provided electronic consent/assent. Those aged 18 consented independently; younger participants required parental consent.

SurveyMonkey was used to collect demographic information. A protocol amendment added a variable for socio-economic context by recording enrolment in schools under Ireland’s Delivering Equality of Opportunity in Schools (DEIS) programme.

The second author randomised participants 1:1:1 using a computer-generated sequence and remained masked to allocation. Participants were informed the trial compared usual care with educational videos. Those in the usual care (UC) group were unmasked; those in the video arms were unaware whether they received the traditional (TRAD) or augmented (AUGM) video format. The fifth author, also masked, delivered the video intervention links.

The usual care group received no additional materials. Video intervention groups received six weekly 3–5 min multimedia videos based Scoliosis Research Society FAQ content [51], refined by patient and public involvement that provided more resources [59–62] along with relevant qualitative literature [14, 38, 39, 63]. Video content was broken down into: General Information, Diagnosis, Treatment Overview, Bracing, Surgery, Living with Scoliosis (Supplemental Appendix B).

Participants were informed about the nature of video content. Protocols for handling distress or mental health concerns were in place (Supplemental Appendix C).

Both groups received videos with identical scripts, sequencing, and core information. Content was localised for Irish-English conventions. Videos were recorded on iPhone13, edited using Final Cut Pro X [64] and GarageBand [65]. Delivery via private YouTube channels enabled tracking of watch time and views. Videos could be paused, rewatched, or skipped freely until Week 8.

The TRAD videos [66] visually and audibly resembled Scoliosis Research Society content [51]. The design principles found to be lacking in these videos were the principles of redundancy, spatial contiguity, temporal contiguity, modality, embodiment and generative activity (Supplemental Appendix A). They were incorporated into the AUGM videos [66] in accordance with the literature [11, 12, 30–32]. All videos were shared [67] with participants post-study.

In accordance with CONSORT [68, 69], primary feasibility outcomes included the number recruited, rejected, dropped out, adverse events and reasons for each as well as completion rates for the intervention and outcome measures. Intervention adherence was assessed through behavioural engagement metrics from YouTube analytics (video views, watch time, percentage watched). PROM completion rates were assessed at all time points.

PROMs were collected via SurveyMonkey, including an MCQ assessing cognitive engagement through knowledge of all video content at baseline and week 8. All questions were generated directly from the finalised video scripts, ensuring explicit alignment between delivered content and assessed knowledge. Questions focused on key factual concepts presented in the videos and were written at a fourth grade reading level, with readability corroborated using the Microsoft Word readability tool. Content validity was addressed through review by the scoliosis-specialised clinician who formed part of the patient and public involvement (PPI) component of the trial, with feedback focusing on relevance and appropriateness for an adolescent audience. Formal pilot testing and item-level psychometric analyses were not undertaken prior to this feasibility study. Shorter MCQs examining material from individual weekly videos were conducted immediately and one-week post-intervention. Participants were instructed to complete each knowledge test as soon as possible after viewing the corresponding video. Participant responses were monitored and reminders were sent twice weekly to minimise delay.

The RE-AIM framework [52] ensured this feasibility study evaluated reach (participation rates and demographics), effectiveness (knowledge scores, PROMs), adoption (online delivery), implementation (adherence to intervention, budget, adaptations), and maintenance (week 8 follow-up completion). The Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) schedule of enrolment, interventions, and assessments is available in Supplemental Appendix D.

Missing data were tracked weekly. Twice weekly reminder emails and mandatory fields in online forms helped ensure outcome completion.

The first author, masked to group allocation, analysed data using intention-to-treat principles. Ordinal and change scores used Friedman and Kruskal-Wallis tests. Generalised Estimating Equations (GEE) examined video design associations with knowledge and the watch time. A protocol update replaced ANOVA with MANOVA for continuous data.

Recruitment feasibility was informed by audit data from one healthcare site, estimated as 60 eligible participants/year. Additional sites and social media advocacy expanded reach.

Analyses were conducted using SPSS v29; power calculations for the full RCT used G*Power [70]. Data management complied with institutional and GDPR (EU 2016/679) policies.

YouTube analytics revealed strong engagement in both video groups. GEE analysis showed that higher behavioural engagement (e.g., watch time %) significantly predicted better knowledge scores (p = 0.011). However, group assignment alone did not significantly affect scores (p = 0.098).

Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

This feasibility RCT demonstrated that recruiting, randomising, and retaining adolescents with idiopathic scoliosis in an online educational study is viable. High adherence and completion rates, absence of adverse events and 80% participant satisfaction support the intervention’s acceptability and safety. The study fulfilled the RE-AIM framework criteria, confirming that a fully powered RCT is feasible and safe. Exceeding the 45-participant recruitment target within four months, suggests that a longer recruitment window and additional sites could expand reach in a definitive trial.

The educational materials were developed transparently and reported in a replicable format, supporting open science and enabling independent appraisal—an approach still uncommon in musculoskeletal research of educational interventions [33]. Making study materials accessible ensures that open science principles are prioritised and findings can be fully interrogated by fellow researchers, and maximises public benefit from publicly funded research.

Findings indicated that multimedia education supported knowledge retention in adolescents with scoliosis, with 73–93% weekly engagement and greater knowledge gains in video groups compared to usual care, with the AUGM group completing more outcomes and displaying fewer dropouts than the TRAD group. These results align with the Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning [12], which suggests structured content enhances engagement. However, while these principles are widely applied in education and commercial media, their use in healthcare remains underexplored. There are currently no standardized guidelines for optimizing engagement in digital health education, despite growing evidence that patients increasingly seek online resources to self-manage musculoskeletal conditions [71, 72]. This study contributes early evidence linking multimedia design and patient learning, warranting further research.

The high completion rate for primary outcomes (85%) and secondary outcomes (77–97%), was comparable completion rates for spinal fusion (78–93%) [73–76], juvenile idiopathic arthritis (66%-92%) [77–79] and anterior knee pain (67%-99% ) [80–85]. A 15% loss to follow-up was reasonable for this population, though future trials should include phone follow-ups to explore reasons for attrition.

The maintenance aspect of the RE-AIM framework was optimised by using weekly reminders to engage with the intervention and data collection. Intervention adherence was lowest during weeks focused on bracing and surgery (weeks 4–5). This may relate to participation fatigue with the study or prior participant experience within the two video intervention groups, where 89% had previous bracing and 44% had surgery or were pending surgery, potentially reducing perceived relevance of these videos.

In evaluating secondary outcomes related to the effectiveness aspect of the RE-AIM Framework, behavioural engagement was the only YouTube metric that showed a significant association with knowledge scores. Participants who watched a larger percentage of the videos attained higher quiz scores than those in the UC group, while those who watched less of the videos or skipped sections tended to score lower.

Although not statistically significant, the AUGM group showed a trend toward improved engagement and knowledge outcomes. Repeated video views did not improve quiz scores, but higher initial watch time was associated with better performance, suggesting that clear, structured content optimised for first viewing is crucial. This finding reinforces the importance of thoughtful multimedia design in health education.

Group assignment did not significantly affect overall knowledge, but topic-level analysis (Fig. 2) suggests certain questions may have favoured the AUGM group. STAI, PAQ, SRS-22r, and EQ-5D outcomes did not differ between groups, potentially due to insufficient power.

Potential limitations, attempts to address, future consideration

This study provides early insights into the application of Cognitive Theory of Multimedia [12] principles in musculoskeletal healthcare education, which has been underexplored [53]. However, it examined a specific adolescent scoliosis population, which may limit generalisability to other musculoskeletal conditions. Different populations may respond differently to multimedia interventions, and condition-specific frameworks may be needed. Public and patient involvement was critical to identifying appropriate content and should be replicated in future studies. A qualitative follow-up study will explore participants’ information needs in greater detail and demonstrate how education can be tailored to a specific population.

YouTube’s ability to report watch time, percentage viewed and repeat views offered pragmatic indicators of behavioural engagement with our educational videos as indirect proxies for engagement [44]. While these metrics do not directly measure attention, cognitive processing, or learning, they provide an objective and scalable method of capturing how participants interact with video-based interventions in real-world digital environments. Watch time or percentage viewed cannot confirm attention, as videos may be played passively or while multitasking. Similarly, repeat views do not necessarily indicate enhanced understanding, as evidenced by the trend observed where single views correlated more strongly to higher quiz score. This suggested that repeat viewing may not improve quiz scores and perhaps there was no advantage to watching more if the content was not unclear or misunderstood on the first viewing. However, more statistical power is needed and a full-scale trial could possibly find that repeat views indicate more interest or effort to seek a correct answer.

Knowledge quizzes were included to assess cognitive engagement by proxy but were not necessarily completed immediately after video viewing and are subject to memory variables as well. In educational literature, engagement encompasses affective, behavioural and cognitive components, many of which require observation or self-report, which were not feasible within this online design. To address these issues surrounding engagement tracking, future studies may consider embedded interactions like tasks or comprehension checks, or time-linked assessments to better evaluate engagement in real time. Eye-tracking technology may also have a role to play in providing real-time engagement data. Until such approaches are more widely available and scalable in digital health trials, platform analytics provide a practical proxy for preliminary indicators of educational engagement.

The MCQ instruments were study-specific and were not subjected to formal psychometric validation or pilot testing. As such, they should be interpreted as pragmatic indicators of knowledge engagement and retention rather than as validated measures of learning. Additionally, while the intervention and assessments were delivered simultaneously, this was in an open online environment that limited control over the timing of test completion relative to video viewing. This introduces potential recall decay or knowledge contamination through external information-seeking or discussion with others. This can be better assessed by tracking video engagement data against SurveyMonkey quiz completion data in a full-scale trial.

The omission of the STAI-CH State subscale for early participants limits interpretation of anxiety outcomes. The SRS-22r also omits elements like mobility and self-care [86], highlighting the importance of complementary tools like the EQ-5D-Y-3 L. However, EQ-5D-Y-3 L’s school-function measurement may be limited [87], it’s correlation with the SRS-22r has been found to be good for non-scoliotic populations [88] thus far. Proxy reporting potentially occurred with the EQ-5D-Y-3 L in this study but this has been found to yield similar results [89].

Given long wait times in scoliosis services [14, 34, 35], scalable digital education could provide early, accessible information—especially valuable in under-resourced or rural settings. Future work should examine strategies to improve scalability and personalisation. AI tools could streamline content development and updates [90–92]. Studies should also explore multimedia features like background music, animation styles, and virtual or augmented reality to further enhance engagement.

Conclusion

This feasibility RCT confirmed that recruiting, engaging, and retaining adolescents with scoliosis in an online educational study is achievable. Online data collection of knowledge and PROMs is also feasible. Initial results suggest that greater video engagement improves knowledge retention, and augmenting video design assists with participant retention. A fully powered trial is needed to evaluate whether multimedia design principles enhance knowledge, engagement, and quality of life. Further research should aim to optimise digital education for accessibility, impact, and scalability across diverse patient groups.
