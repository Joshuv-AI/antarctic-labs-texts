Rapid detection of Seneca Valley virus based on reverse transcription recombinase polymerase amplification and CRISPR-Cas13a
Authors: Yuhan Wu, Jiayao Su, Jiashan Sun, Zijian Li, Jingjing Yang, Changde Wu, Zhe Liu, Junyao Wang, Yixin Wang, Jiyu Liu, Lianmao Duan, Cong Wang, Guangshan Zhu, Xuexia Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe Seneca Valley virus (SVV) is an emerging novel etiological agent that triggers vesicular stomatitis in pigs. SVV leads to considerable financial detriment to the global swine industry. Availability of expeditious, simple, and precise detection techniques would aid in the diagnosis and control of Seneca Valley disease. In this study, we developed a novel SVV detection assay based on reverse transcription recombinase polymerase amplification (RT-RPA)/CRISPR-Cas13a. This technique can specifically identify SVV with no cross-reactivity with other viruses, such as the porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus, pseudorabies virus, classical swine fever virus, and foot-and-mouth disease virus. It boasts a detection threshold as sensitive as 2.43 copies/μL, matching the proficiency of reverse transcriptase quantitative PCR in clinical diagnostics. The streamlined RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a assay for SVV minimizes procedural complexity and the likelihood of contamination. Integrating this method with nucleic acid acquisition via nucleic acid releaser enables a turnaround time of only 40 min from sample acquisition to result determination, underscoring its suitability for on-site diagnostics and affirming its role as a robust instrument for the monitoring and detection of SVV.

Introduction

Seneca Valley virus (SVV) is an emerging picornavirus that causes vesicular diseases in pigs [1–4]. It is a non-enveloped virus belonging to the genus Senecavirus within the family Picornaviridae [5,6]. The viral genome is a single-stranded positive-sense RNA of approximately 7.2 kb in length, which contains a 5′ untranslated region (UTR), open reading frame (ORF), 3′ UTR, and polyA tail [7]. The ORF encodes a polyprotein that is subsequently cleaved into structural proteins VP1, VP2, VP3, and VP4 and non-structural proteins Lpro, 2A, 2B, 2C, 3A, 3B, 3C, and 3D [5].

SVV was first isolated from PER.C6 cells cultured with adenovirus-5 (Ad5)-based vectors in the United States in 2002 [5]. In 2007, SVV nucleic acids were detected for the first time using PCR in pigs with vesicular disease imported from Canada to Minnesota [4]. Another case associated with SVV and vesicular lesions was reported in Indiana, USA, in 2012 [8]. However, little research had been conducted regarding the virus until the end of 2014, possibly because the cases were only reported in North American countries. In 2015, the first report of SVV outside of North American countries came from China [1,9]. Notably, novel clinical signs, characterized by sudden death, have been observed in newborn pigs infected with this virus [1,9]. Since then, SVV infections have been reported in symptomatic pigs in Colombia [2], Thailand [3], Vietnam [10], Chile [11], and Mexico [12], causing significant economic losses to the global pig-farming industry. Pigs infected with SVV typically present with vesicles on the oral mucosa, snout, and feet, which often rupture and develop to ulcers or erosions [13,14]. Foot lesions commonly lead to lameness, accompanied by systemic symptoms such as reduced appetite, depression, and fever (body temperature usually ranging from 40.3°C to 40.8°C) [13,14]. Notably, infected newborn piglets primarily exhibit diarrhea, neurological disturbances, and sudden death. Mortality rates can reach 30%–70% within the first seven days after birth, resulting in substantial economic losses to the swine industry [1,9]. The clinical manifestations of vesicular lesions caused by SVV are extremely similar to those of other vesicular diseases such as foot-and-mouth disease (FMD), swine vesicular disease (SVD), vesicular stomatitis (VS), and vesicular exanthema of swine (VES), which may lead to disputes in diagnoses based solely on clinical signs [15,16]. Therefore, laboratory diagnostic methods are required to confirm the diagnosis. The development of rapid, sensitive, and accurate detection methods for SVV is critical to effectively control and prevent its transmission.

A variety of detection methods, including virological identification methods and serological assays, have been developed for SVV detection. Among these, reverse transcriptase quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), conventional PCR, and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay are widely used [15]. However, these methods require well-equipped laboratories, specialized instruments, and trained personnel, making them unsuitable for rapid on-site detection. Recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) is a promising technology for point-of-care testing owing to its ability to efficiently amplify target nucleic acid fragments under isothermal conditions using a simple and portable instrument [17–20]. However, there is growing concern regarding the disadvantages of RPA, namely its nonspecific amplification. RPA technology tolerates up to nine base mismatches, which, while broadening its application scope, compromises its specificity [21–23]. Some studies have attempted to address the issue of nonspecificity in RPA, such as by using SAMRS primers and adding 0.8 M betaine [24,25]. Combining RPA with Cas13a is also an effective means of reducing the nonspecific amplification of RPA. Cas13a, an RNA-guided RNA endonuclease, specifically recognizes and cleaves target RNA through clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat (CRISPR)-derived RNA (crRNA) with a base pair [26]. Following its activation by a target RNA, the Cas13a nuclease domain cleaves neighboring RNA molecules nonspecifically – an activity termed collateral cleavage. Detection of these small cleavage products provides a direct readout for the presence of the target RNA, forming the basis for highly sensitive diagnostic assays [26]. The combination of RPA and Cas13a not only reduces the nonspecific amplification of RPA through the RNA-guided cleavage ability of Cas13a, but also enhances the sensitivity of pathogen detection by Cas13a by leveraging the amplification capability of RPA [26]. Current, detection methods for various pathogens such as SARS-CoV-2 [27], monkeypox virus [28], and avian influenza virus [29] have been successfully established based on the RPA and CRISPR-Cas13a systems, demonstrating their significant role in advancing pathogen detection.

In this study, we established a method for the rapid detection of SVV based on RT-RPA and CRISPR-Cas13a and confirmed the effectiveness of this method in terms of specificity, sensitivity, portability, and rapid detection. The detection performance of this method in clinical pig samples was evaluated by referencing results against those of RT-qPCR.

Materials and methods

Viruses and clinical swine samples

The SVV strain CHhb17 (GenBank accession No. MG983756.1), porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV) strain CHsx1401 (GenBank accession No.KP861625.1), pseudorabies virus (PRV) strain ZJ01 (GenBank accession No. KM061380.1), and classical swine fever virus (CSFV) C strain were kindly provided by Dr. Jiangwei Song of Beijing Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences, Prof. Lei Zhou of China Agricultural University, Dr. Junxing Li of Zhejiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Dr. Qianyi Zhang of the China Institute of Veterinary Drugs Control, respectively. The foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) vaccine was purchased from China Animal Husbandry Industry Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). A total of 49 clinical samples, including 25 vesicular fluids, 24 lymphatic tissues, were collected from 2018 through 2019 on commercial pig farms in Shenyang City (123°23’36″E, 41°47’40.56″N), Fuxin City (121°38′16.09″N, 41°59′13.79″E), Jinzhou City (121°8′27.08″N, 41°7′30.51″E) in Liaoning province from pigs with suspected clinical signs. The vesicular fluids were collected from sows, and tissue samples were obtained from neonatal piglets that died acutely within the first four days after birth. The owners have verbally consented to the animal’s participation in the study when they send the samples for pathogen detection. The consent procedure was approved by Animal Experimentation and Laboratory Animal Welfare Committee of Shenyang Agricultural University (No. 2017121501). Additionally, 25 nasal swabs were obtained from piglets infected with SVV strain CHhb17 during a separate experiment conducted in our own laboratory. The consent procedure was approved by Animal Experimentation and Laboratory Animal Welfare Committee of Shenyang Agricultural University (No. SNLL25030501). PCR testing showed negative results for the following viruses: foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV), swine vesicular disease virus, vesicular stomatitis virus, vesicular exanthema of swine virus, porcine epidemic diarrhea virus, porcine rotavirus, porcine transmissible gastroenteritis virus, and porcine deltacoronavirus. The animal studies have adhered to ARRIVE guidelines and were approved by Animal Experimentation and Laboratory Animal Welfare Committee of Shenyang Agricultural University (No. 2017121501 and No. SNLL25030501). The study was conducted in accordance with the local legislation and institutional requirements.

Extraction of viral RNA

We used 250 μL of the SVV, PRRSV, CSFV, PRV-infected cell supernatants to extract nucleic acid with Trizol reagent as previously described (Magen Biotechnology, Guangzhou, China) [29]. For the FMD vaccine, deflocculation was first performed, which involved adding 1.1 mL of n-butanol solution to 10 mL of the vaccine, mixing thoroughly, and then allowing it to stand at 4 °C overnight. Afterward, 250 μL of the clear lower liquid was used for nucleic acid acquisition. The processing of clinical samples was performed as previously described [29]. The extracted RNA was stored in a −80 °C refrigerator for further RPA amplification.

RT-RPA assay

The RT-RPA assay was performed using a commercial kit (Amplification FutureBiotech Co., Ltd., Weifang, China) with a reaction volume of 50 μL. The amplification was developed as follows: 29.4 μL of Buffer A, 2.5 μL of Buffer B, 2 μL each of the forward and reverse primers, 5 μL of RNA, and 9.1 μL of DNase/RNase-free water. Note that the mixture excluding Buffer B was first added to resuspend the lyophilized beads, and then Buffer B was added to initiate the amplification process. Following a quick burst of vortexing and subsequent centrifugation, the reaction tubes was placed in a 42 °C-water bath for a 30-min reaction.

Preparation and purification of crRNA

CrRNAs were prepared as previously described [29]. A commercial kit was used to purify crRNAs according to the manufacturer’s instructions (NEB, Ipswich, MA, United States). Briefly, 100 μL of Cleanuo Binding Buffer was added to a 50 μL sample, followed by 150 μL of anhydrous ethanol, and then mixed thoroughly by gently tapping the tube wall. The mixture was then added to the center of the filter membrane on a spin column, placed in a collection tube, and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 1 min. A total of 500 μL of RNA Cleanup Wash Buffer were added to wash the crRNA. Subsequently, 10 μL of RNase-free ddH2O was used to elute the crRNA.

LwaCas13a collateral cleavage assay

The LwaCas13a collateral cleavage assay was performed with reaction volume of 20 μL, including, 45 nM LwaCas13a protein, 22.5 nM crRNA, 0.5 mM NTP Buffer, 30 U T7 RNA polymerase, 80 U RNase inhibitor, 2 μM RNase reporter (FAM-UUUUU-BHQ1), 2 μL of RPA product, and a detection buffer (0.6 M NaCl, 0.1 M MgCl2, 0.4 M Tris-HCl, pH 7.3). The reactions were performed using the QuantStudio 3 system (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, United States) under the following conditions: 5 min of incubation at 37 °C per cycle for a total of 12 cycles, with one fluorescence intensity reading collected at the end of each cycle.

One-step RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a-based detection

RT-RPA and LwaCas13a collateral cleavage assays were performed in the same tube to streamline the procedure and reduce risk. Firstly, the reaction mixture including 29.4 μL Buffer A, 2.5 μL Buffer B, 2 μL of each the forward and reverse primers were added to the resuspended lyophilized reaction beads. The resuspended liquid was divided evenly into two portions. Subsequently, detection buffer (0.6 M NaCl, 0.1 M MgCl2, 0.4 M Tris-HCl, pH 7.3), 0.5 mM NTP buffer, 80 U RNase inhibitor, and 2 μM RNase reporter (FAM-UUUUU-BHQ1) were incorporated into the bottom of the reaction tube, while 45 nM LwaCas13a protein, 22.5 nM crRNA, and 30 U T7 RNA polymerase are added to the cap of the tube. Following the addition of 2.5 μL of nucleic acid, the tube was positioned in a 42 °C water bath for RPA amplification. Thereafter, the tube was briefly centrifuged and placed in a QuantStudio 3 system to collect fluorescence signals every 5 min for 1 h.

RT-qPCR assay

RT-qPCR was performed using HiScript III U+ One Step qRT-PCR Probe 5×Master Mix (Vazyme) and primer and probe sequences, as described previously [30]. The reaction volume contained 4 μL U + One Step qRT-PCR Probe 5×Master Mix, 0.4 μL of 50×ROX Reference Dye 1, forward and reverse primers, 0.2 μL Taqman Probe, 1 μg RNA, and RNase free ddH2O, totaling 20 μL. The amplification conditions were as follows: reverse transcription at 55 °C for 15 min, followed by a pre-denaturation step at 95 °C for 30 s, then PCR amplification at 95 °C for 10 s, and 60 °C for 30 s, for a total of 45 cycles, with the fluorescence signals collected after each PCR final step.

Rapid nucleic acid acquisition with nucleic acid releaser

A sample lysis stock solution (3% Triton X-100, 10% glycerol, 2 mM DTT, 25 mM Na2HPO4, and 25 mM Tris-HCl; pH 7.8) was prepared from the working solution immediately before use. Specifically, the stock solution was diluted with DNase/RNase-free water at a ratio of 1:6, and the RNase inhibitor was simultaneously added at a dosage of 0.04 U/μL. Sterilized disposable cotton swabs were used to collect the SVV supernatant fluids, and then transferred to 100 μL of the lysis buffer. The swabs were then rotated and compressed to thoroughly dilute the sample in the lysis buffer, followed by a 5-min incubation at 25 °C. Afterward, the lysed samples were placed on ice until use.

Statistical analysis

Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro – Wilk test. Data conforming to normal distribution were analyzed by one-way ANOVA (parametric test), while non-normally distributed data were evaluated using the Kruskal – Wallis test (non-parametric alternative). All statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism (version 8.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Significance levels are denoted by asterisks: *p < 0.05, *** p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001.

Results

Screening of primer and crRNA

The full-length genomic sequences of SVV were downloaded from NCBI and uploaded to the platform (https://adapt.run/run/) for the design of primers and crRNAs. The top ten pairs of primers and crRNAs were selected and synthesized (Table 1). The process of the entire detection method is shown in Figure 1. RPA reactions were conducted using these ten primer pairs, and the samples amplified with primer pairs 1 and 9 did not exhibit distinct bands (data not shown). Purification of crRNA10 repeatedly failed to yield viable products. Therefore, a LwaCas13a collateral cleavage assay was performed using the remaining eight primer pairs and their crRNAs. The results showed that the fluorescence intensities of the eight primer pairs and their crRNAs increased rapidly after 5 min of cleavage (Figure 2 and 3(A)). However, only the primer pair F6/R6 with crRNA6 specifically detected SVV, whereas the negative controls and the two other viruses produced almost no fluorescence signals (Figure 2). Consequently, the primer pair F6/R6 with crRNA6 was found to be optimal and used in subsequent experiments. Specificity of stepwise RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a-based detection for SVV

Four porcine viruses (PRRSV, CSFV, FMDV, and PRV) were used to analyze the specificity of the detection method. Fluorescence kinetic assays indicated that the fluorescence values of SVV rapidly increased and were significantly higher than those of the other viruses after the reaction started, with a maximum difference of 7.6 × 104 fluorescence units, demonstrating that the established detection method could specifically detect SVV (Figure 3(B)).

Sensitivity of stepwise RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a-based detection for SVV

To assess the sensitivity of the RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a detection for SVV, the initial concentration of SVV RNA was set to 100 ng/μL followed by a 10-fold serial dilution, and the copy numbers at each dilution step were calculated using the following formula: RNA copies (copies/μL) = 6.02 × 1023 (copies/mol) × RNA concentration (ng/μL) × 10−9/molecular weight (g/mol). Based on previous experiment [29], concentrations of 2.43 × 102 copies/μL, 2.43 × 101 copies/μL, and 2.43 × 100copies/μL were selected for fluorescence kinetic analysis. As shown in Figure 3(C), all three concentration groups exhibited significant fluorescence after the reaction started, while the negative control showed almost no fluorescence, indicating that the detection limit of this method is 2.43 copies/μL.

One-step RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a detection of SVV

To further optimize the detection method, RPA amplification was integrated with the CRISPR-Cas13a cleavage reaction in a single tube. The fluorescence kinetics results showed that the optimized method produced a significant fluorescence signal when detecting SVV, differing from the negative control by 3 × 106 fluorescence units (Figure 4(A)). It specifically detected SVV without cross-reacting with PRRSV, PRV, CSFV, and FMDV (Figure 4(B)). Sensitivity results indicated that a fluorescence signal remained at 2.43 × 100 copies/μL, with a value 5 × 105 fluorescence units above the negative control, demonstrating that the detection limit of this method was 2.43 copies/μL, exhibiting comparable sensitivity to the stepwise method (Figure 4(C)). Clinical samples detected by one-step RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a and RT-qPCR

A total of 74 clinical samples were analyzed using both RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a and RT-qPCR. The RT-qPCR results showed that 46 of the 74 clinical samples were positive for SVV, with Ct values ranging from 13.33 to 35.27 (Figure 5(A), Supplementary Table S1 and Figure S1). The detection results of RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a for 74 samples revealed that 46 samples produced clear fluorescent signals (Figure 5(B,C)). Figure 5(A) illustrates the Ct values of clinical samples plotted against the fluorescence intensity readouts, showing that the two methods yielded consistent results for the detection of 74 clinical samples. One-step RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a-based detection of SVV with extraction-free nucleic acid releaser

To further optimize the method by simplifying the RNA acquisition workflow, extraction-free RNA acquisition was performed using a sample lysis solution, followed by one-step RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a detection (Figure 6). The results showed that the Cas13a enzyme recognized the SVV nucleic acid treated with the sample lysis solution and cleaved the probe to emit fluorescent signals, whereas the negative control produced almost no fluorescent signal, resulting in a difference of 1.3 × 106 units in fluorescence intensity between the two groups (Figure 7(A)). The specificity test results indicated that the PRRSV, PRV, CSFV, and FMDV samples treated with the sample lysis solution showed no fluorescence response, demonstrating that the established method could specifically detect SVV (Figure 7(B)). To further analyze the sensitivity of nucleic acid releaser combined with the RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a detection for SVV, the viral solution with a titer of 105 TCID50/μL was diluted by 10-fold serial dilutions down to 102 TCID50/μL, 101 TCID50/μL, and 100 TCID50/μL, and then subjected to extraction-free nucleic acid releaser. We subsequently proceeded to RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a detection. As illustrated in Figure 7(C), diluting the sample to 100 TCID50/μL elicited fluorescent readings, indicating that the sensitivity threshold of this method was 1 TCID50/μL. Discussion

SVV is an emerging virus that poses a serious threat to the global pig industry [1–4]. The virus can infect pigs of all age groups. The incidence rate is the highest in sows, exceeding 70%, while the mortality rate is highest in piglets, especially those aged 1–3 d, at approximately 30%–70% [15,16]. However, because the clinical symptoms are very similar to those of FMD, it is difficult to implement targeted control measures to swiftly contain epidemics, resulting in significant economic losses [15,16]. Currently, no commercial vaccines or effective therapeutic drugs are available for the prevention or treatment of SVV infections [31]. Therefore, it is necessary to establish simple, rapid, accurate, and reliable diagnostic methods for SVV that are fundamental to its prevention and control.

In the present study, we developed and validated a rapid SVV detection method based on RT-RPA and CRISPR-Cas13a. Initially, the complete genome sequences of representative SVV were downloaded from the NCBI GenBank database and ten pairs of RPA primers and crRNAs were designed. RPA and collateral cleavage assays were performed to comprehensively evaluate the primers and crRNAs with optimal signal intensities and specificities. We established an RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a-based method for detecting SVV with the selected primers and crRNA, which showed no cross-reaction with other porcine viruses, had a detection limit of 2.43 copies/μL, and performed comparably to RT-qPCR in detecting 74 clinical samples. Second, the RPA assay was integrated with the Cas13a cleavage reaction in a single tube to simplify the procedure and reduce the risk of contamination. The one-step method could also specifically detect SVV with a sensitivity comparable to that of the two-step method. Finally, a sample lysis solution was used to lyse the virus for nucleic acid acquisition, a process that was not only highly simple to perform, but also significantly reduced the time required. However, this method is only suitable for fluid-type samples and not for tissue samples (data not shown). In the future, we will continue to focus our efforts on this area.

Currently, isothermal amplification assays are often combined with CRISPR-Cas12a or CRISPR-Cas13a to detect various pathogens. The enzymes Cas12a and Cas13a each have their own strengths and weaknesses: (i) Cas12a is directed to target sequences by the T-rich PAM sequences (5′-TTN-3′) and can cleave both DNA and RNA molecules [32]. In contrast, Cas13a, cleaves RNA by identifying sequences containing protospacer flanking sites [33]. Thus, Cas13a imposes less stringent constraints on target sequences than Cas12a. (ii) In contrast, Cas13a has been shown to have stronger collateral activity [34]. (iii) The trans-cleavage activity of Cas12a can be activated by recognizing the DNA amplicon, whereas the activation of Cas13a’s trans-cleavage activity requires transcribed RNA from the amplified target DNA, which is a comparatively more cumbersome process. Following isothermal amplification, the typical requirement of opening the reaction vessel for Cas12a/Cas13a testing poses a risk of aerosol contamination. Integrating the two-step procedure into a seamless single-step process can effectively address this issue. However, in a one-step process, the cis-cleavage activity of Cas12a results in the cutting of the DNA target, impeding isothermal amplification, and consequently diminishing the efficiency of detection. Cas13a, on the other hand, has no effect on the DNA targets. Given this perspective, Cas13a is a more appropriate candidate for application in one-step reactions.

Various of SVV detection methods have been established based on different detection principles. A TaqMan-based RT-qPCR method targeting the VP1 gene has a detection limit of 1.3 × 101 copies/μL, capable of detecting and quantifying the RNA genome in porcine biological samples [35]. A SYBR green I-based RT-qPCR method targeting the VP4 gene can detect SVV nucleic acids at 6.4 × 101 copies/μL, which is significantly higher than the 6.4 × 103 copies/μL of gel electrophoresis [36]. Since these methods require a PCR thermal cycler, which incurs significant expenses for the technical apparatus and entails an extended operational timeframe, several investigations have formulated isothermal amplification strategies for the identification of SVV using reverse transcription-insulated isothermal PCR (RT-iiPCR), loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), and RPA. Zhang et al. developed a field-deployable RT-iiPCR method based on the 3D gene, with a detection limit of 30 TCID50/mL and a detection time of approximately 2 h [30]. An assay utilizing RT-LAMP was devised to detect SVV, with a sensitivity threshold of 1 TCID50/mL and a turnaround time of 1.5 h [37]. An RPA coupled with lateral flow dipstrip assay targeting the SVV VP1 gene was established, featuring a detection sensitivity down to 15 copies/μL and a reduced reaction time of 50 min [38]. In the same year, Wang et al. established a real-time fluorescent rapid RPA detection method based on the VP2 gene, with a detection limit of 101 TCID50/reaction, but the procedure requires at least 2.5 h [17]. In this study, a detection approach combining RT-RPA with CRISPR-Cas13a was developed, leveraging the 3D gene and achieving a sensitivity threshold of 2.43 copies/μL or 1 TCID50/μL (2.5 TCID50/reaction). The established method in this study has the following advantages over the isothermal amplification methods (RT-iiPCR, RT-LAMP, and RPA) mentioned above: (i) The RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a method demonstrates higher sensitivity, with a detection limit at least 10-fold lower than that of the aforementioned methods [17,30,37,38]. (ii) One-step RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a-based detection with nucleic acid releaser reduces the total detection time (from sample collection to result acquisition) to 40 min, whereas the RPA assay, the fastest among the aforementioned methods, still requires 50 min [38]. Additionally, in principle, the RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a method exhibits greater specificity compared to the aforementioned isothermal amplification methods, as only RPA amplification products specifically recognized by crRNA generate positive signals [39]. However, this has not yet been experimentally validated. It is important to note that this process is a double-edged sword: Cas13a’s off-target activity can potentially yield false-positive or false-negative results [39]. The established method demonstrates comparable sensitivity to that of RT-qPCR, while exhibiting lower device dependency and shorter detection time, making it more suitable for on-site rapid detection. However, compared to RT-qPCR, this method also has some limitations. First, while RT-qPCR enables highly precise quantification by monitoring fluorescence signals at each amplification cycle, RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a allows quantification based on fluorescence intensity but exhibits inferior accuracy and dynamic range compared to RT-qPCR. This limitation was evident in the sensitivity assay, where higher fluorescence signals were unexpectedly observed at lower template concentrations than at higher template concentrations (Figure 3(C) and 7(C)). Therefore, RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a method is currently more suitable for qualitative or semi-quantitative analysis [40]. Second, RT-qPCR can simultaneously detect multiple targets in a single reaction using different fluorescent dyes, whereas current CRISPR-based systems face significant technical challenges in achieving multiplex detection of various viruses or variants [41]. Third, RT-qPCR is a well-established and standardized technology with proven stability and reliability, whereas the stability, reproducibility, and interference resistance of RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a still require further validation with large-scale clinical samples.

Conclusions

In summary, this study established and validated a 3D gene-based RT-RPA/CRISPR-Cas13a detection method for SVV. This approach exhibits excellent sensitivity and specificity, enables rapid detection with minimal equipment reliance, and has strong potential for on-site testing applications. These features highlight its efficacy as a robust tool for surveillance and detection of SVV at border crossings, in the field, or on farms. Taking swab testing at a border post as an example: after collection, the swab is placed in sample lysis buffer for 5 min to release nucleic acids. The lysate is then transferred to a pre-aliquoted master-mix tube and incubated at 42 °C for 30 min in a portable isothermal heater. Following a brief centrifugation, the reaction is run at 37 °C for 5 min in a portable fluorescence reader, after which the result can be read immediately. Future optimization efforts could focus on (i) expanding the assay to tissue samples, (ii) lyophilizing the master mix for ambient-temperature shipment and storage, (iii) integrating the RPA and CRISPR-Cas13a steps into a single modular cartridge, and (iv) developing a smartphone app for real-time result display, thereby making the entire workflow even simpler and more field-friendly.
