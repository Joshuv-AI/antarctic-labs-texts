Emergency physicians’ perspectives on structural factors associated with mental health and burnout
Authors: Anish K. Agarwal, Rachel E. Gonzales, Devon Schroeder, Lauren Southwick, Raina M. Merchant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ImportanceThe well-being of emergency medicine (EM) physicians significantly impacts health care systems, and patients. Understanding the interplay of factors contributing to EM physicians’ mental health issues and burnout is essential for developing targeted support mechanisms.ObjectiveTo investigate the individual, environmental, and structural factors influencing the mental health and burnout experienced by EM physicians, thereby informing potential strategies for intervention and support.DesignA qualitative study using recorded and de-identified semi-structured interviews.Setting and participantsTwenty EM residents and faculty physicians at a large urban academic health system.MeasuresInterviews followed a semi-structured interview guide focusing on clinical work experiences and factors influencing well-being and mental health. Thematic analysis was applied. The study outlined underlying themes from the participants’ responses, utilizing a general inductive approach.ResultsFour primary themes emerged with subthemes: (1) Individual Coping – detailing strategies for managing stress during and outside shifts, including long-term adjustments; (2) Interpersonal Factors – examining the interactions with patients, peers, and family; (3) Environmental Factors – highlighting the challenges posed by patient boarding and workload; and (4) Systemic Issues – addressing stigma in mental health, confidentiality, and the strain from public health crises.ConclusionsThe study underscores the complex challenges impacting EM physicians’ mental health and career longevity. It highlights the need for comprehensive interventions that move beyond resilience training and build upon interpersonal support and systemic health system reforms. Fostering a culture of workplace safety and implementing targeted resources to manage capacity strain are essential approaches for supporting the well-being of EM physicians.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-026-01610-y.

Introduction

Emergency medicine (EM) physicians play a critical role for patients and communities within the broader context of health care delivery [1]. EM clinicians are at the forefront of providing a range of care, spanning minor issues to lifesaving critical care. The nature of this work, characterized by high-pressure environments, underscores the importance of understanding the factors that influence their well-being. Extensive literature has examined EM physicians’ challenges before and during the pandemic [2, 3]. Despite heightened attention to clinician well-being, there remains an opportunity to explore the nuanced interplay between clinical work, mental health, and health systems within this segment of the health care workforce.

Physicians, especially in EM, experience high rates of depression, anxiety, and acute stress [4–9]. Physician depression and anxiety decrease the quality of care, compromise patient safety, and are associated with alcoholism, a reduced sense of accomplishment, and erosion of relationships [10–13] Recent surveys, reveal high levels of stress (87%) and burnout (72%) among EM physicians, with 30% considering leaving the profession [14] Given the stigma in medicine, EM physicians, struggle to acknowledge and identify their own mental health symptoms, where a culture of stoicism and perceived heroism prevails [15–17] There is a need for research that can enhance the understanding of how EM physicians experience, recognize, and manage their mental health. These insights are needed to develop strategies that facilitate the identification of these issues and support physician mental health. This study aimed to deploy qualitative methods to identify key themes affecting the well-being and mental health of EM physicians.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study utilized semi-structured interviews of EM physicians at a large, urban academic health system. This project was reviewed by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Review Board and deemed exempt from review (#849556). The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies (COREQ) guidelines were used to report this study. [18].

Eligible participants were emergency physicians at a large urban academic health system. Recruitment messaging was sent using email listservs within the health system, including study information and a link to a screening survey. Participants were contacted by a research team member with no connection to clinical care. Interested participants were offered an interview conducted via a virtual platform. Participants provided verbal informed consent after the research staff detailed the study, protocol, and potential risks and benefits. Participants received a $50 gift card for their time.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted between March and September 2023 by a study team member with several years of experience in qualitative research (RG). The interviewer identifies as a Hispanic female with no clinical training or interaction with the participants outside the research study. Interviews were completed with only the interviewer and participant present. Participants completed a brief self-reported demographic questionnaire following the interview. Interviews were conducted using a semi-structured interview guide (Appendix) developed by the study team, consisting of experts in qualitative research, emergency care, and clinician well-being (AKA, RG, RM). Informed by the National Academy of Medicine’s well-being framework [19], the guide focused on participant experiences during clinical work, factors influencing well-being, and mental health care. It was designed to elicit a range of responses rather than quantifiable data. Interviews lasted approximately 40 min and were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Transcripts were de-identified and checked against audio recordings for accuracy.

Analysis

Transcripts and responses from the interview were analyzed using thematic analysis and a general inductive approach. Codes were developed, and research team members met to review initial codes, agree on coding priorities, and create a preliminary codebook (RG, DS). Once the codebook was created and reviewed, five transcripts were coded by two coders to iteratively revise the codebook, assess interrater reliability using Cohen’s kappa (K = 0.82), and develop consensus on the codebook (RG, DS). Coding discrepancies were identified and resolved among team members (RG, DS, AKA). Coders coded the remaining transcripts, with one double-coded to maintain reliability (RG, DS). No additional codes were added, indicating thematic saturation was achieved [20, 21] Thematic saturation was assessed at the level of themes rather than the individual codes; interviews continued until no new themes or conceptual insights emerged across successive transcripts. Following coding, members of the research team met to discuss findings and generate themes and subthemes (RG, DS, LS). Data management and qualitative analysis were conducted using NVivo 20.0 software (QSR International). Demographic data collected in the pre-interview questionnaire were summarized.

Research team reflexivity

The research team included a diverse set of individuals with varying professional and clinical backgrounds. Interviews with participants were conducted by a qualitative researcher (RG) with no clinical background, which minimized bias from contextual background and any dynamics related to hierarchy or role with the clinical environment. Two investigators (AKA, RM) are emergency medicine faculty at the study institution and participants were aware of this when consented. To mitigate potential social desirability bias, AKA and RM did not participate in recruitment or interviews, and all transcripts were de-identified prior to analysis. Coding and theme development involved the entire research team, allowing for reflexive discussion of how professional and clinical positionality may influence interpretation of the findings.

Results

In total, 20 EM physicians participated in a semi-structured interview (Table 1). Most participants were self-reported White, male, and had six or more years of clinical experience in EM. Participants shared various perspectives on the factors that intersect with clinical work and well-being. Guided by the socio-ecological model [22], qualitative coding was organized into four themes, each with subthemes: (1) Individual Coping, (2) Interpersonal factors, (3) Clinical Environmental Factors, and (4) Systemic Issues (Table 2).

Theme 1: individual coping

Participants reflected on how they identify, react, and cope with feelings of stress and other negative emotions caused by clinical work in the ED. These findings were categorized into the following subthemes.

During the shift

Participants identified numerous ways they handle feelings of stress during shifts. Many participants noted needing to “take a breather” during a change to cope with stress or needing to compartmentalize their emotions to “push through” clinical shifts.

Outside of shift

In discussing how participants responded to workplace stressors, many identified coping strategies that took place outside of the ED. These strategies include professional mental health support, physical exercise, and making time to rest after and between shifts.

Long term

Participants identified longer-term strategies to cope with workplace stress and negative emotions. Several participants identified the practical need to reduce their clinical hours as a high-impact strategy that they or their colleagues have used to respond to chronic EM related stress. Others noted that having variety related to their work, for example, attempting to work in multiple ED environments, helped to mitigate negative emotions and symptoms. Finally, participants discussed the implications facing EM physicians including financial losses, limited career growth, and feelings of duty towards their patients and peers.

Theme 2: interpersonal factors impacting clinical work

Participants shared how specific ED clinical interactions impacted them positively and negatively. These interactions were categorized into three subthemes.

Patient interactions

Patient interactions were discussed by many as being a positive influence on workplace well-being. Participants felt validated when they could help patients, with one participant sharing, “[When] we have a genuine, therapeutic interaction, I feel like those moments are really, really good.” Despite this, an overwhelming majority of participants discussed patient interactions as being an almost constant source of stress impacting them within the ED and emotionally staying with them beyond their clinical time. These interactions ranged in descriptions, with a majority being moments where patients were “yelling and cursing” at the ED team, to less obviously straining on the patient-clinician relationship when ED management did not align with the patients’ expectations of care, which was highlighted to be particularly stress-inducing. Participants also separately identified physical violence from patients to ED staff members as a different level of workplace violence that was unique to EM and normalized, “as part of the job.”

Peer interactions

Participants described peers as having both positive and negative impacts on their clinical work. Many noted that peers were an essential source of support during clinical work, including through small-talk venting to one another and “commiserating”. Having this support was a necessary aspect of the clinical environment for many and made work more “fun and enjoyable” when that source of support was noticeable at a site. Participants noted the importance of being physically located near peers when working to facilitate these moments. Participants also highlighted negative interactions with clinicians outside of EM, particularly consultants from other specialties. Many participants felt that they should be “on the same team” and that “disagreements about what should be done” for patients could contribute significantly to their stress.

Influence of family and home life

Participants identified family and home life as an essential interpersonal factor impacting their clinical work. Some participants identified their family and spending time with their family as a source of support in helping them manage stress. Others noted the clinical work’s impact on their family, including work anxieties and stressors, making them more “irritable”, “cranky”, and disconnected at home.

Theme 3: clinical environmental factors

Participants identified many aspects of the clinical environment that contributed to or detracted from their workplace well-being. These aspects were categorized into the following subthemes.

Boarding

The single major issue highlighted by essentially all participants. Half of the participants identified boarding as having a significant negative influence on their clinical experience, emotional health, and careers. Many felt that boarding in the ED forced them to practice beyond their training, dealing with cases not characteristic of emergency medicine. Others felt the impact of boarding in how many patients they cared for at one time, and how many had to wait to be seen, which also impacted their stress levels.

Workload

An overarching issue associated with boarding, but described as separate, was an ever-increasing workload in EM, which was inadequately supported or resourced. Participants described “having more and more put on EM, but without compensation or acknowledgement.” Participants highlighted the “nature of emergency medicine” as a stressful component of their role, as they have to juggle tasks for many patients in a given shift with other administrative work. As time progresses, for more senior participants, they note the overwhelming nature of “doing more with less.”

Leadership and administration response

Many participants felt frustrated with institutional responses to problems affecting well-being. Participants felt that resources supporting well-being through online training would be better spent on fixing the clinical system issues (e.g., boarding and workplace violence) driving work environment stressors.

Theme 4: systemic issues

Participants identified overarching systemic factors that affect health care and, by extension, their workplace well-being. Discussion of these factors has been categorized into two subthemes.

Mental health stigma

Many participants discussed the known and persistent stigma that exists around mental health, particularly for health care workers. Some participants noted that they felt they were expected to be “strong” and above needing professional support. Others discussed how this stigma affected their willingness to discuss their own mental health needs with their colleagues and administration, in large part due to concerns around licensing and the potential for mistakes to be blamed on their mental health.

Confidentiality within the Workplace

Participants discussed the idea of personal health information confidentiality as it related to mental health efforts led by their workplace. Some expressed a willingness to share their mental health data for such efforts, noting that doing so might “[lessen] stigma and taboo”. However, even when willing to share such data, most participants emphasized a need for safeguards such as HIPAA compliance, data de-identification, and other confidentiality protections. Nonetheless, many participants remained hesitant and voiced concerns about sharing mental health data with their workplace, including threats to licensing and vulnerability in potential malpractice cases.

Public health strain

Some participants discussed factors outside the health system affecting their well-being as emergency care providers. Some expressed that they felt they were at the forefront of several ongoing epidemics, including COVID-19, gun violence, and racism. Participants highlighted that while EM plays a critical role in extreme issues, multiple public health catastrophes’ overlapping and unrelenting nature is overwhelming and challenging to manage. The chronic stress from being a frontline provider to trauma and injustice weighed on participants and contributed to their feelings of workplace burnout, stress, and anxiety.

Discussion

Emergency medicine sits squarely in the intersection of high-stress and high-demand clinical environments. EM physicians confront a diverse set of individual and systemic challenges that can profoundly affect their mental health and career longevity. This qualitative study provides updated insights into key areas brought forward by EM physicians. The findings inform the ongoing evolution of health systems as they attempt to better support and sustain the clinical workforce, marking out priority areas for targeted attention and action.

Although presented as distinct themes, participants’ narratives revealed that individual, interpersonal, environmental, and systemic factors are deeply interconnected and mutually reinforcing. Individual coping strategies were shaped by systemic stigma and confidentiality concerns, while environmental pressures such as boarding intensified interpersonal conflict and constrained clinicians’ ability to practice in ways aligned with professional values. These interacting pathways suggest that burnout and mental health strain emerge not from isolated stressors, but from cumulative and reinforcing structural conditions embedded within care delivery.

Participants described a range of approaches that span the immediate in-the-moment responses to workplace violence to longer-term strategies aimed at mitigating chronic stress. Despite these strategies, there is a tension between self-care and duty to one’s career, and stigma surrounding the use of mental health resources. While systems are increasingly turning to employee assistance programs and digital mental health services [23] to support physicians, the challenge lies in ensuring these resources are available but easy to access and use to busy and often over extended physician workforce [16, 24] Additionally, participants describe the need to increase variation in practice, either by physical site, research, or other operational work. In the long term, health systems may benefit by supporting diversity in role (e.g., creating appropriately compensated time for clinicians to pursue complementary areas to EM) to combat burnout and sustain career longevity. Research is needed to understand how to build models that introduce diversity in practice and to create personalized methods that support individuals early and often.

Interpersonal relationships emerged as a key component impacting EM clinicians in positive, but mostly negative, ways. While some interactions with patients were found to be rewarding and provided a sense of purpose, and other peer-to-peer interactions were significant supporting factors despite chaotic and busy clinical environments, these were infrequent. Participants highlighted the well-described and historical tension between EM and other specialties [25]. Additionally, the rise of physical and verbal instances of workplace violence [26] or discordant patient expectations contributed to stress and emotional strain. To mitigate these pressures, there is a need to bolster the strong bonds within EM and bridge the gaps with other specialties. Actionable ways to do this include joint conferences, interdisciplinary operations, and social outings. Beyond this, at an institutional level, safeguarding the physical and psychological safety within EDs is imperative, calling for strategies visibly supported by leadership that reinforce internal cohesion and cross-disciplinary collaboration. Systems have begun larger screening processes for weapons using advanced metal detector technology and de-escalation training. In addition to these methods, hospital-level protocols to make reporting of violence the standard would help quantify the otherwise well-known problem, and system-level standardization in upholding a no-tolerance approach to keep health care workers safe.

The normalization of physical and verbal violence as “part of the job” represents a particularly troubling manifestation of moral injury. Participants described being compelled to accept unsafe working conditions as unavoidable, a process that erodes professional dignity and contributes to emotional numbing. This normalization may further suppress help-seeking behavior by reinforcing expectations of stoicism and endurance, with downstream consequences for career longevity and workforce retention.

The national spotlight on ED boarding [27–29] continues, and participants emphasized the dramatic impact boarding and the additional workload associated with it have on worsening burnout and personal emotional strain. Participants’ accounts of boarding align closely with moral injury theory, which describes psychological distress arising when individuals are unable to act in accordance with deeply held professional or ethical values. Boarding forces EM physicians to practice beyond their scope of training, manage inpatient-level care without appropriate resources, and witness preventable patient harm. These conditions threatened clinicians’ professional identity as emergency physicians and generated a sense of institutional betrayal, as participants perceived leadership responses as misaligned with the realities of frontline care.

Participants articulated a paradoxical relationship with mental health data sharing: a desire to reduce stigma through openness, coupled with profound fear of professional repercussions related to licensing, credentialing, and malpractice vulnerability. This double bind reflects broader structural constraints within medical regulation that discourage disclosure while simultaneously promoting wellness. Resolving this tension will require policy-level interventions beyond individual institutions, including reforms in licensing language and stronger protections for physicians seeking mental health care.

Participants were explicit in distinguishing meaningful institutional support from symbolic or performative wellness initiatives. Interventions perceived as ineffective included mandatory wellness modules and episodic programming that failed to address underlying drivers of distress. In contrast, participants emphasized the need for structural reforms, including reducing boarding through hospital-wide accountability, formal mechanisms for reporting and addressing workplace violence, protected confidentiality for mental health care, and greater flexibility in clinical workload and role diversification. These findings underscore that effective interventions must prioritize system redesign rather than individual resilience training.

Systemic issues emerged as overarching influences on physician well-being. The pervasive stigma surrounding mental health within health care settings was identified as a significant barrier to seeking support, perpetuating a culture of silence and reluctance to disclose personal struggles [16, 17, 30] Moreover, concerns about confidentiality and the consequences of disclosing mental health issues highlight the need for strong protective policies and support mechanisms to protect physicians’ privacy and mitigate professional repercussions. This begs the question of where and how to support physicians best. Research is needed to develop private and secure ways of reaching physicians and connecting them to mental health care that is trusted and accessible.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Participants were drawn from a single large urban academic health system, and perspectives may differ in community, rural, or non-academic emergency departments with different resource constraints, staffing models, and patient populations. The sample was predominantly male and attending-level, which may have influenced discussions of stigma, help-seeking, and professional identity. As a qualitative study, findings are not intended to be statistically generalizable but instead provide in-depth, context-specific insights. Nonetheless, academic emergency departments may represent settings where structural pressures such as boarding and workforce strain are particularly pronounced, offering valuable insight into how these forces manifest at their extremes.

Conclusion

This study offers contextual insights and perspectives from EM physicians into the complex interplay of factors shaping the well-being and career burnout in the field in the “post-pandemic world.” By elucidating individual coping mechanisms, interpersonal dynamics, clinical environmental challenges, and broader systemic influences, these themes underscore the need for personal, interpersonal, and structural change interventions. Interventions addressing these multifaceted challenges should prioritize systemic reforms, promote a culture of physical and physiological safety, and provide targeted resources to address capacity strain to sustain EM physicians.
