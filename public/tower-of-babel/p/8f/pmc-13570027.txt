Guideline-recommended post-operative intravesical chemotherapy and cystoscopic surveillance among patients with low- and intermediate-risk non-muscle invasive bladder cancer
Authors: Michael J. Shulman, Valerie S. Lee, Janise M. Roh, Marilyn L. Kwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionWe sought to characterize concordance of surveillance and treatment patterns in a large community-based healthcare system with the American Urologic Association/Society of Urologic Oncology (AUA/SUO) guideline for low- and intermediate-risk non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC). We expect the results of this study to improve the oncologic care of patients with NMIBC.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study of patients aged 18 years or older diagnosed with low- or intermediate-risk NMIBC between 1/1/2018 and 12/31/2022 and followed until 12/31/2024. The prevalence of single-dose post-operative intravesical chemotherapy instillations, cystoscopy procedures, and urine cytology tests were used to determine at, below, and above guideline-concordance.ResultsA total of 631 low-risk and 619 intermediate-risk NMIBC patients were identified. Prevalences of single-dose immediate postoperative intravesical chemotherapy were: 35.2% (low-risk) and 36.8% (intermediate-risk). In the low-risk NMIBC group, average guideline-concordance was 47.5% for cystoscopy and 82.3% for cytology. In the intermediate-risk group, average guideline-concordance was 54.1% for cystoscopy and 27.2% for cytology. Percent of cystoscopy procedures exceeding guideline-concordance was 30.5% and 14.5% in the low- and intermediate-risk NMIBC groups, respectively.DiscussionAlthough guideline-concordance of single-dose intravesical instillations, cystoscopy procedures, and cytology tests mirrored what is reported in the literature, the prevalence of cystoscopy and cytology tests exceeding guideline-concordance was notably less, which may be a feature of our integrated delivery and finance system.ConclusionUnderstanding the current landscape of concordance with the AUA/SUO guideline is necessary for every healthcare delivery system to optimize the oncologic care of patients with NMIBC.

Introduction

Bladder cancer is the sixth most commonly diagnosed cancer worldwide. 1 Among those newly diagnosed with bladder cancer, 75% have non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC). 2 Patients with NMIBC are at high risk of frequent cancer recurrences.3,4 For example, those with low-risk NMIBC have a 5-year incidence of recurrence between 20-30%, 4 which increases to 40-55% in those with intermediate-risk NMIBC.3,4 The American Urologic Association/Society of Urologic Oncology (AUA/SUO) guideline uses incidence of recurrence and progression to determine standards of care, as well as to design risk-adjusted surveillance frameworks to improve overall prognosis.2,5 The AUA/SUO guideline recommends frequent office-based cystoscopy and urine biomarkers to detect bladder cancer recurrences, imaging tests to identify urothelial cancers in the upper urinary tract, surgical procedures to resect bladder tumors, and intravesical immuno- or chemotherapies to prevent cancer recurrence and progression. Because of the resource intensity required for surveillance and treatment, NMIBC has the highest lifetime treatment cost of all cancers.6,7

Despite awareness of the AUA/SUO guideline for NMIBC, real-world surveillance and treatment practice patterns among U.S. urologists are highly variable, and risk-aligned surveillance is not the norm.8–10 In fact, the cost of surveillance for NMIBC has been driven higher over time by overutilization of resources due to lack of adherence to the guideline. 8 As a result, patients are exposed to increased financial burden and morbidity without improvement in cancer outcomes.8,11,12

The goals of this study were to characterize the concordance of single-dose intravesical chemotherapy within 24 hours of transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) and surveillance cystoscopy and urine cytology patterns with the AUA/SUO guideline in a contemporary cohort of NMIBC patients treated in a large, community-based healthcare system. We hypothesized that guideline-concordant treatment and surveillance testing were not consistently implemented in routine clinical practice. These results will provide a better understanding of current surveillance practices of NMIBC patients with the goal of optimizing cancer treatment and surveillance practices.

Methods

Design, study setting, and patient population

This was a retrospective cohort study using electronic health record (EHR) data from Kaiser Permanente Northern California (KPNC), a large community-based and integrated healthcare system. The Research Determination Committee for the Kaiser Permanente Northern California region determined the project does not meet the regulatory definition of research involving human subjects per 45 CFR 46.102(d). From the KPNC Cancer Registry, patients diagnosed aged 18 years or older with low- or intermediate-risk NMIBC were identified between 1/1/2018 and 12/31/2022. Patients diagnosed with papillary urothelial neoplasm of low malignant potential (PUNLMP) were identified from pathology reports and added to the low-risk group. The index date for all subjects was defined as the date of the first pathologically confirmed diagnosis. KPNC had no guideline or clinical pathways for the management of NMIBC during the study period. Management was at the discretion of treating physicians. Patients were followed until the earliest date of progression to high-risk NMIBC, disenrollment from the health plan, death, or end of study (12/31/2024).

Surveillance and treatment data

Cystoscopy and bladder tumor fulguration procedures and intravesical chemotherapy instillations performed anytime during surveillance were captured using Current Procedural Terminology codes or KPNC-specific procedure codes from the Virtual Data Warehouse. These data were further amended by using reasons for encounter documented in the EHR. Cytology tests, bladder biopsies with tumor fulguration, and TURBTs were identified from the pathology database. The AUA/SUO definitions of low-, intermediate-, and high-risk NMIBC were used to define the risk group of all initial and subsequent pathologic diagnoses (Table 1). 5 Cancer recurrence and progression were identified from EHR review. Study outcomes

The number of cystoscopy procedures and cytology tests completed each year after the index date for each patient was calculated and compared with the amended 2020 AUA/SUO guideline (Table 1).2,5 Specifically, concordance status for each patient per surveillance year was defined as “guideline-concordant” if the number of cystoscopy or cytology tests in any given surveillance year were within the guideline-recommended range, “below guideline-concordant” if the numbers were less than recommended, and “above guideline-concordant” if the numbers were higher than recommended. Cystoscopy and cytology percent concordance for the low- and intermediate-risk NMIBC groups in each surveillance year were calculated by dividing the number of patients in the concordant, below concordant, and above concordant groups by the total number of eligible patients. For the concordance analysis, the intermediate-risk group combined subjects initially diagnosed with intermediate-risk with those who progressed from low-to intermediate-risk during the study period. Patient eligibility was determined for each year of surveillance and was defined as the number of active patients in the health system available for surveillance with cystoscopy and urine cytology testing.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive baseline patient and tumor characteristics, single-dose intravesical chemotherapy at index date, surveillance duration, and oncologic outcomes were summarized for all patients and compared between low- and intermediate-risk groups using the Chi-square test. Observed concordance percentages with the AUA/SUO guideline were calculated. Descriptive statistics (mean with SD, median with IQR, mode) were used to describe the number of cystoscopy and cytology tests per treatment year for all patients. Analyses were performed using SAS EG 8.3.

Prevalence of single-dose immediate postoperative intravesical chemotherapy was 35.2% for low-risk and 36.8% for intermediate-risk NMIBC patients (p = .54). Gemcitabine was the most common administered single-dose post-operative chemotherapeutic agent. The median surveillance durations were similar at 4.4 and 4.1 years for low- and intermediate-risk NMIBC, respectively (p=.11). Frequencies of recurrence (22.7% low-versus 44.8% intermediate-risk, p<.001) and progression to high-risk disease (5.7% low versus 23.8% intermediate, p<.001) were significantly higher in the intermediate-risk group.

Average annual guideline-concordant six-year cystoscopy surveillance incidence for patients with low- and intermediate-risk NMIBC were 47.7% and 54.1%, respectively (Table 3). Concordance with cystoscopy less than 4 months following a new diagnosis of NMIBC was 60.9% and 49.9% in patients with low- and intermediate-risk NMIBC, respectively. Average annual below guideline-concordant surveillance cystoscopy incidence was 29.3% (low-risk) and 31.1% (intermediate-risk). Average annual above guideline-concordant cystoscopy incidence was 15.8% (low-risk) and 14.9% (intermediate-risk). Average annual guideline-concordant six-year cytology incidence for patients with low- and intermediate-risk NMIBC were 82.3% and 27.2%, respectively (Table 3). Discussion

We report on the current practice of single-dose intravesical chemotherapy after TURBT and six-year cystoscopy and urine cytology surveillance among patients with low- and intermediate-risk NMIBC in KPNC. Among KPNC patients with NMIBC, 35-37% received single-dose postoperative intravesical chemotherapy. The AUA/SUO guideline recommends single-dose postoperative instillation of intravesical chemotherapy within 24 hours of TURBT.2,5 The basis of this recommendation is the finding that single-dose post-operative gemcitabine reduces the risk of recurrence in those patients presenting with low-grade NMIBC by 35% (relative risk reduction) and 10-15% (absolute risk reduction) at 4 years. 13 In contemporary studies, despite the AUA/SUO guideline, 22-52% of patients with low-grade NMIBC receive single-dose postoperative intravesical chemotherapy.14,15 The pattern of use of immediate postoperative intravesical chemotherapy in KPNC is similar to published practice patterns. However, the 35-37% guideline-concordance with the perioperative instillation of intravesical chemotherapy guideline observed within KPNC did not consider judicious use, 16 which may explain why utilization was low.

Incidence of guideline-concordant cystoscopy procedure by surveillance year for six years after diagnosis among KPNC patients with low- and intermediate-risk NMIBC averaged 47.7% and 54.1%, respectively. These findings are consistent with the 54-55% rates of adherence to the cystoscopy surveillance guideline reported among patients with NMIBC in a large population-based Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results–linked Medicare database. 8 Concordance with the first cystoscopy after NMIBC diagnosis deserves special attention. The AUA/SUO recommends that the first surveillance cystoscopy occurs three to four months after initial diagnosis of NMIBC.2,5 This recommendation is based on the finding that a first surveillance cystoscopy four months after a new NMIBC diagnosis is associated with significantly improved oncologic and survival outcomes. 17 In our study, 60.9% and 49.9% completed a surveillance cystoscopy less than 4 months after an initial diagnosis of low- and intermediate-risk NMIBC, respectively, which is consistent with the heterogeneity in clinical practice observed in published studies. 17

The prevalence of cystoscopy procedures and cytology tests above guideline-concordance in KPNC is lower than in published studies. In KPNC, 30.5% (low-risk) and 14.5% (intermediate-risk) of cystoscopy procedures were performed above guideline-recommendation, which were notably less than the 75-79% excess procedure prevalence reported in published studies.8,12,18 Similarly, the 6.3% of urine cytology tests completed above guideline-recommendation among KPNC patients with intermediate-risk NMIBC is far less than the 44.8% to 54.9% prevalence of urine-based biomarkers completed above guideline-recommendation published in the literature. 8 We hypothesize that the lower prevalence of surveillance resource utilization in excess of guideline-concordance in KPNC is a feature of our integrated delivery and finance system in which clinicians do not have productivity-based incentives. 19

The absence of differences in annual median and mode values for cystoscopy procedures and cytology testing between low- and intermediate-risk NMIBC patients suggest that risk-adjusted surveillance frameworks may not have been employed in routine clinical practice in KPNC. Although there is no difference in the median number of surveillance testing between risk groups, a statistically significant difference was observed in the mean number of cystoscopy procedures and urine cytology testing. Since mean values are more strongly influenced by extremes in data than the median, the significant difference in means is reflective of highly variable surveillance practices. This study adds to the evidence base that real-world surveillance practice patterns are highly variable and not often risk-aligned.8–10

Variation in clinical practice patterns among urologists has been attributable to their perception of NMIBC recurrence risk and comfort level with risk-adaptation.4,8,9 Moreover, awareness or acceptance of AUA/SUO surveillance recommendations of low evidence strength by urologists is another important factor that may be responsible for the observed variation in clinical practice.4,8,9 For example, expert opinion is the basis of all AUA/SUO urine biomarker and cystoscopic surveillance recommendations for patients with low- and intermediate-risk NMIBC except for cystoscopy surveillance in patients with low-risk cancer (very low to low evidence strength).2,5 In fact, the impact is unknown of the framework of AUA/SUO guideline-recommended surveillance on the incidence rates of tumor recurrence and progression during follow up among patients with low- and intermediate-risk NMIBC. Because the surveillance guideline is largely based on expert opinion, urologists have latitude in formulating surveillance plans which may in turn contribute to the observed wide variation in clinical practice. Understanding the impact of the framework of AUA/SUO guideline-recommended surveillance on oncologic outcomes deserves further study.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective, hypothesis-generating study from a single institution, the broader applicability of our findings is limited. Next, because we relied on programmatic data abstraction and chart reviews, data quality was subject to variable documentation practices over time and across a large number of physicians. Moreover, the surveillance and treatment data were limited to single-dose intravesical chemotherapy, cystoscopy, and cytology completion rates. A more complete understanding of surveillance and treatment practices among patients with low- and intermediate-risk NMIBC in KPNC would include data on upper urinary tract imaging and utilization of induction and maintenance intravesical therapy, which will be included in future studies. While we were unable to examine the clinical applicability of our findings, in a future study, we plan to examine the association of AUA/SUO guideline-concordant treatment and surveillance with cancer recurrence and progression rates and the physician- and patient-level factors associated with under- and overutilization of surveillance resources. Thereafter, with a solid understanding of the factors associated with guideline adherence and the oncologic outcomes associated with guideline-concordant care, we plan to study implementation strategies to improve guideline adherence in a prospective cohort.

Conclusion

This study highlights the importance of understanding variation in treatment and surveillance practices of physicians treating patients with low- and intermediate-risk NMIBC. Knowing the current state of concordance with the AUA/SUO guideline is the first step in developing standardized risk-aligned treatment and surveillance frameworks with the goal of optimizing the oncologic care of patients with NMIBC.
