DPEP2 suppresses hyperinflammation via metabolic reprogramming of macrophages in sepsis
Authors: Wenchen Luo, Wei Xu, Qimeng Yin, Jieqiong Song, Jie Wang, Yian Guan, Jian Huang, Jian Yu, Kefeng Zou, Danfeng Jin, Chao Xu, Min Qiu, Zhixin Qiu, Jing Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis-induced excessive inflammation contributes to mortality, but restricting hyperinflammation in sepsis remains challenging. Here, we identify dipeptidase 2 (DPEP2) as an immunotherapeutic target in sepsis by integrating single-cell and bulk RNA sequencing data from septic patients. In patients with sepsis, peripheral monocytes/macrophages have reduced DPEP2 expression, with DPEP2 levels negatively correlating with inflammation severity, disease progression, and clinical outcomes. In vitro, Dpep2 knockdown enhances macrophage-mediated inflammation, while in septic mice in vivo, macrophage-specific Dpep2 loss decreases survival by exacerbating inflammation and organ damage. Mechanistically, sepsis-induced EGR1 represses Dpep2 transcription, leading to reduced DPEP2-mediated enzymatic cleavage of leukotriene D4 (LTD4). Increased LTD4 redirects the metabolic flux toward prostaglandin E2 overproduction, amplifying NF-κB activation and lipopolysaccharide-induced inflammatory cytokine production. Lastly, lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-mediated delivery of Dpep2 mRNA expression to monocytes/macrophages mitigates inflammation and organ damage in septic mice. Our findings thus suggest a protective function for DPEP2 in sepsis-induced hyperinflammation via immunometabolic regulation, and also present LNP-mediated Dpep2 mRNA delivery as a potential therapy for septic hyperinflammation.Subject terms: Antimicrobial responses, Systems analysis, Sepsis, Gene regulation in immune cells

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by dysregulated host immune responses to infection1,2, and is responsible for approximately 20% of global deaths annually3,4. Sepsis management relies on two pillars: early and appropriate antimicrobial therapy and organ support5. However, given the contribution of dysregulated immune responses to high mortality rates6,7, immunotherapy has emerged as the third pillar in sepsis treatment6. In particular, immunotherapy targeting excessive inflammatory responses during the early acute phase of sepsis has attracted considerable attention. Reliable biomarkers and immunotherapy targets are urgently needed to provide immunotherapeutic approaches for treating sepsis6.

In the early stage of sepsis, innate immune cells release pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF), interleukin (IL)−1β, and IL-68, leading to a life-threatening cytokine storm and excessive inflammation9. This intense inflammatory response correlates with increased cellular damage, thereby elevating the risk of organ dysfunction9. As part of the first line of defense, monocytes and macrophages are critical in initiating inflammatory responses and mediating tissue injury10. It is well documented that metabolic reprogramming in macrophages is a key prerequisite for Toll-like receptor (TLR)-mediated pro-inflammatory cytokine production11. Therefore, immunometabolism critically impacts macrophage function, thereby profoundly influencing the prognosis of sepsis11. Among the metabolic pathways, lipid metabolism is particularly important for maintaining host defense and regulating inflammation during sepsis12. Indeed, reprogramming lipid metabolism directs pro-inflammatory differentiation of macrophages13. Hence, targeting lipid metabolism-related genes in monocytes/macrophages may provide an immunotherapeutic strategy for sepsis.

RNA-based drugs, including messenger RNA (mRNA), can manipulate genes of interest, therefore significantly contributing to the development of immunotherapeutic approaches for treating sepsis14. Lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-mediated mRNA delivery systems are emerging as a therapeutic strategy due to their ability to transiently and precisely program immune cells with minimal adverse effects14,15. Thus, targeting immune cells with LNP-mediated mRNA delivery offers a potential treatment avenue for sepsis. However, there is a notable gap in the development of LNP-based mRNA delivery systems targeting specific genes for sepsis treatment. We and others have profiled the immune cell landscapes in sepsis, revealing substantial reprogramming of monocytes/macrophages. Unfortunately, the therapeutic targets and strategies remain elusive.

Here, we combine state-of-the-art techniques, including single-cell and bulk RNA profiling, in vitro and in vivo sepsis modeling, and LNP–based therapeutic delivery engineering to identify potential metabolic targets in monocytes/macrophages for sepsis treatment. We identify DPEP2 as an immunoregulatory factor in sepsis, with markedly reduced expression in peripheral monocytes/macrophages, correlating with heightened inflammation, disease progression, and poor outcomes. Loss of DPEP2 drives excessive macrophage-mediated inflammation by disrupting leukotriene D4 (LTD4) metabolism and increasing prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) production. Notably, macrophage-targeted delivery of Dpep2 mRNA improves survival in septic mice, supporting the translational potential of DPEP2-based gene therapy for sepsis management. Collectively, these findings identify DPEP2 as a regulator of monocyte/macrophage-mediated immune homeostasis in sepsis, highlighting its potential as both a prognostic biomarker and a therapeutic target.

Results

scRNA-seq and RNA-seq profiling identify immunometabolic reprogramming of monocytes associated with DPEP2 downregulation during sepsis

To explore the cellular landscape and underlying mechanism of immune response during sepsis, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) from five patients in the early acute phase of sepsis and five healthy controls were profiled with single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) using the 10× Genomics 5′ sequencing platform (dataset from our previous study16) and reanalyzed in this study (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). After removing doublets and filtering low-quality cells, 80,063 high-quality cells were retained for further analysis, with an average of 1,656 genes detected per cell. Five distinct cell types were identified based on canonical lineage markers: T cells (CD3D, CD3E), monocytes (VCAN, FCN1, CD14, LYZ), natural killer (NK) cells (GNLY, KLRF1), B cells (CD79A, MS4A1) and megakaryocytes (PF4, PPBP) (Fig. 1b, c). By comparing differentially expressed genes (DEG) between patients with sepsis and controls, substantial reprogramming of gene expression levels was detected in monocytes during sepsis (Fig. 1d). Moreover, monocyte populations were consistently enriched in patients with sepsis (Supplementary Fig. 3a). DEG analysis in monocytes via Metascape web-based platform17 revealed that top gene ontology (GO) processes are primarily related to the regulation of immune and inflammatory responses in patients with sepsis (Supplementary Fig. 3b).

Unsupervised clustering of monocytes revealed four distinct subclusters (Mono1–4) with divergent abundance patterns between patients with sepsis and healthy controls (Fig. 1e). We found that, compared with Mono2–4, Mono1 constituted the largest monocyte subset in both groups, and exhibited the most substantial alteration in sepsis compared to controls (Fig. 1f). Among the four monocyte subclusters, only Mono1 was reduced in sepsis, in stark contrast to the expansion of Mono2–4. To determine the monocyte subcluster most closely linked to sepsis-associated immune dysregulation, we performed GO enrichment analysis. The results showed that immune response and inflammation regulation terms were most specifically associated with Mono1, but not consistently observed in other subclusters (Fig. 1g). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further indicated that metabolic pathways were also active in Mono1 (Fig. 1h). This unique pattern drew our attention, as it may reflect a specific impairment of immunoregulatory or inflammatory monocytes in the early stage of sepsis, which might shed light on the early detection and therapy for sepsis. Pseudotime analysis indicated a gene expression trajectory for monocytes, with Mono1 representing a unique cell state during monocyte reprogramming from the control to the sepsis group (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Taken together, these findings highlight Mono1 as a downregulated yet immunometabolically active subcluster in sepsis, supporting its relevance for further mechanistic investigation.

To further characterize the Mono1 cluster signature identified by scRNA-seq and prioritize robust monocyte targets in sepsis, we performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis of PBMCs from five additional patients in the early acute phase of sepsis and five non-septic controls (Supplementary Tables 3, 4, RNA-seq1, original dataset). Within RNA-seq1, 1375 upregulated and 553 downregulated DEGs were identified between patients with sepsis and controls (Supplementary Fig. 3d, e). The integrated analysis of these two datasets identified ten overlapping genes (Fig. 1i). Considering that Mono1 is closely associated with immunometabolic regulation, and DPEP2’s established role in lipid metabolism18–20 along with its consistent downregulation in all patients with sepsis, we selected DPEP2 for further investigation (Fig. 1i). DPEP2 downregulation in patients with sepsis was also verified by quantitative PCR of PBMCs and scRNA-seq of monocytes (Fig. 1i). Additionally, scRNA-seq data and the Human Protein Atlas revealed enriched DPEP2 expression in monocytes/macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 3f, g), suggesting a unique role for DPEP2 downregulation in monocytes/macrophages response to sepsis. Given DPEP2’s function in lipid metabolism regulation, it was hypothesized that it might influence the response of Mono1 by regulating the monocytes/macrophages inflammatory responses through lipid metabolism in sepsis4.

DPEP2 downregulation in monocytes correlates with inflammation, disease severity and prognosis in patients with sepsis

To evaluate the clinical relevance of monocyte DPEP2 expression in sepsis, we further recruited 36 patients in the early acute phase of sepsis (abdominal infection origin was the most common type) and 36 volunteers free of infection (Supplementary Tables 5, 6, original dataset) and collected blood samples, followed by the detection of DPEP2 protein levels in monocytes using flow cytometry (Fig. 2a). DPEP2 expression was significantly lower in the CD11b+CD14+ monocytes, but not in CD3+ T cells, of patients with sepsis compared to controls (Fig. 2b). In patients with sepsis, the proportion of DPEP2+ monocytes negatively correlated with the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score (r = -0.6825), which monitors acute morbidity of sepsis and is a key criterion in diagnosing sepsis syndrome21, and the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation score version II (APACHE-II) (r = -0.6522), which is a gold standard for risk evaluation in critically ill patients22 (Fig. 2c, d). Additionally, the lower proportion of DPEP2+ monocytes correlated with higher serum concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines in patients, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF (r = −0.4389, −0.6100, −0.5313, and −0.3900, respectively; Fig. 2e–h), indicating a higher inflammatory response in septic patients with DPEP2 downregulation.

To explore the relationship between monocyte DPEP2 expression and the prognosis of patients with sepsis, 36 patients were followed up for one month after discharge, 10 of whom died. Sepsis survivors had a higher proportion of DPEP2+ monocytes than sepsis non-survivors (Fig. 2i). Based on the median percentage of DPEP2+ monocytes in patients with sepsis, those expressing higher levels of DPEP2 had a higher survival probability than those with low levels (Fig. 2j). Multivariate logistic analysis identified the proportion of DPEP2+ monocytes as a potential predictor of sepsis outcome (Fig. 2k). The clinical relevance between DPEP2 expression and sepsis-related death was further confirmed using publicly available RNA-seq data from the peripheral blood of 26 sepsis survivors and 9 sepsis non-survivors (GSE54514, RNA-seq2). DPEP2 expression was higher in sepsis survivors than sepsis non-survivors (Fig. 2l), and the DPEP2High group had a higher survival probability than the DPEP2Low group (Fig. 2m). Further multivariate logistic analysis of the GSE54514 dataset confirmed that DPEP2 expression serves as a prognostic marker of sepsis-related death (Fig. 2n).

Cumulatively, these results suggest that DPEP2 expression is downregulated in the monocytes of patients with sepsis and is associated with inflammation, severity, and prognosis.

DPEP2 negatively regulates pro-inflammatory cytokine production in macrophages

To validate the relationship between DPEP2 and inflammation, an in vitro cellular inflammatory model was developed using several TLR ligands. TLRs (excluding TLR3) can activate NF-κB upon recognizing the corresponding pathogen ligands during initiation and amplification of inflammation in macrophages23. All tested TLR ligands reduced Dpep2 mRNA expression (Pam3Csk4 for TLR1/2, lipopolysaccharide [LPS] for TLR4, R848 for TLR7/8, and poly(I:C) for TLR3) in RAW264.7 murine macrophage-like cell line. LPS elicited more pronounced alterations in Dpep2 expression relative to other TLR ligands (Fig. 3a). DPEP2/Dpep2 mRNA expression was also downregulated in human CD14+ monocytes derived from PBMCs of healthy individuals and mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM) after LPS stimulation (Fig. 3b, c). Stimulating RAW264.7 cells with varying LPS doses for different durations revealed a consistent decrease in DPEP2 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3d, e).

To characterize the transcriptome programs regulated by DPEP2 downregulation in macrophages, RNA-seq analysis was performed in RAW264.7 cells with shRNA-mediated Dpep2 knockdown (shDpep2 RAW264.7 cells) (RNA-seq3, original dataset). DEG analysis revealed extensive transcriptomic reprogramming after Dpep2 knockdown in RAW264.7 cells, with 485 upregulated and 502 downregulated DEGs (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 4a), which included key pro-inflammatory mediators, such as Il1a, Il1b, Il6, Tnfaip3, Ccl2, Ccl7, Ccl9, Ccl12 and Cd80 (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 4b). GSEA revealed a comprehensive upregulation of inflammatory pathways, including the inflammatory response, TNF signaling via NF-κB, IL-6-JAK-STAT3 signaling, and cytokine response, in shDpep2 RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 4c–h), suggesting that DPEP2 acts as a suppressor of macrophage inflammatory responses. Similarly, pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF) mRNA expression and protein secretion were increased in shDpep2 BMDMs and BMDMs derived from Dpep2-knockout (KO) mice, compared with shNT BMDMs or BMDMs derived from wild-type (WT) mice (Fig. 3h–k). These findings imply that DPEP2 deficiency promotes macrophage inflammatory responses.

The effect of DPEP2 on LPS-induced pro-inflammatory cytokine production was then evaluated. Following in vitro LPS stimulation, higher levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines were observed in Dpep2 suppressed RAW264.7 cells and BMDMs using shRNA (Fig. 3l and Supplementary Fig. 5a–c), and BMDMs derived from Dpep2 KO mice (Fig. 3m and Supplementary Fig. 5d). Additionally, decreases in mRNA and protein secretion levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines were observed in Dpep2-overexpressing BMDMs and RAW264.7 cells after LPS stimulation (Fig. 3n, o; Supplementary Fig. 5e, f).

Collectively, these data suggest that DPEP2 inhibits macrophage inflammatory responses.

Dpep2 knockout contributes to heightened inflammatory response, exacerbated organ injury, and reduced survival in septic mice

To further investigate the role of DPEP2 in inflammation in vivo, a sepsis mouse model was established via cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) (polymicrobial sepsis model) procedure in C57BL/6 mice24. DPEP2 levels were downregulated in the heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney tissues, as well as in the peripheral blood of septic mice (Fig. 4a). Additionally, the proportion of DPEP2+ macrophages was significantly lower in the spleen and lung of septic mice compared to sham mice (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 6). In peripheral blood, the proportions of both DPEP2+ macrophages and DPEP2+ monocytes were significantly reduced during sepsis (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 6).

To fully reveal the in vivo function of DPEP2 in sepsis development and prognosis, a CLP sepsis model was developed in Dpep2 KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 7a). The mortality was significantly higher in Dpep2 KO mice than in WT mice after CLP challenge (Fig. 4c). Compared to WT controls, Dpep2 KO septic mice exhibited higher levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF) in peripheral blood (Fig. 4d–f), greater inflammatory cell infiltration and structural lung damage (Fig. 4g), and elevated markers of hepatic and renal dysfunction, including alanine transaminase (ALT), blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine, compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 8a). These data suggest that DPEP2 regulates the acute inflammatory response, organ dysfunction and survival in sepsis in vivo.

To specifically investigate the role of macrophage DPEP2 in sepsis, we generated macrophage-specific Dpep2 knockout mice (Dpep2f/fLyz2Cre) (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c) by crossing loxP-flanked Dpep2 exon mice (Dpep2f/f) with transgenic Lyz2Cre mice. Dpep2f/fLyz2Cre mice displayed higher mortality than Dpep2f/f controls after CLP challenge (Fig. 4h). Consistently, Dpep2f/fLyz2Cre septic mice displayed higher systemic levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF in peripheral blood than Dpep2f/f septic mice (Fig. 4i–k). Enhanced inflammatory cell infiltration in lungs, more severe pulmonary structural damage, higher lung injury scores (Fig. 4l) and elevated pulmonary apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 8b) were also observed in Dpep2f/fLyz2Cre septic mice compared with Dpep2f/f septic mice. Additionally, the levels of liver, kidney and heart injury indicators, including ALT, BUN, creatinine and creatinine kinase myocardial band (CK-MB), were higher in Dpep2f/fLyz2Cre septic mice (Supplementary Fig. 8c). Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) staining levels—marker of neuroinflammation—were higher in the hippocampus of Dpep2f/fLyz2Cre mice than Dpep2f/f mice after CLP challenge (Supplementary Fig. 8d), suggesting that Dpep2 downregulation induces astrocytosis-related neuroinflammation during sepsis25.

These findings suggest a protective role of macrophage DPEP2 in suppressing excessive inflammation and tissue and organ injury, thus improving the survival rate of septic mice.

EGR1-mediated repression of DPEP2 disrupts LTD4 metabolism in LPS-induced macrophage inflammation

Given the consistent downregulation of DPEP2 mRNA in sepsis, we first examined its upstream transcriptional regulation. To this end, we analyzed the DPEP2 promoter region using the ENCODE database and identified a potential binding site for early growth response 1 (EGR1) (Fig. 5a), a key transcription factor implicated in macrophage-mediated inflammation. Notably, EGR1 expression is consistently upregulated in macrophages during sepsis and Macrophage Activation Syndrome26–28. Similarly, we found LPS exposure upregulated Egr1 expression in macrophages (Fig. 5c). EGR1 is known as a transcriptional activator or repressor according to the context of target genes29. LPS stimulation significantly reduced Dpep2 mRNA levels, while pharmacological inhibition or genetic ablation of EGR1 substantially attenuated this repression (Fig. 5d, e). These findings identify EGR1 as an inflammation-inducible transcriptional repressor responsible for suppressing DPEP2 expression in macrophages during sepsis (Fig. 5b).

To elucidate how DPEP2 modulates macrophage inflammation, we examined its role in arachidonic acid (AA)–derived eicosanoid metabolism, a core component of immunometabolic regulation. DPEP2 enzymatically converts LTD4 to leukotriene E4 (LTE4), thereby participating directly in cysteinyl leukotriene metabolism18–20. Cysteinyl leukotrienes (a class of eicosanoids), such as LTD4 and LTE4, are lipid inflammatory mediators synthesized from AA through the 5-lipoxygenase (5-LO) pathway. Upon activation of 5-LO, AA is converted to leukotriene A4 (LTA4) in monocytes and macrophages. LTA4 is converted by LTA4 hydrolase (LTA4H) into LTB4, or by LTC4 synthase (LTC4S) into Leukotriene C4 (LTC4). LTC4 can be converted to LTD4 and then metabolized by DPEP2 to produce LTE4 (Fig. 5f). Consistent with its established enzymatic role, shRNA-mediated Dpep2 knockdown in RAW264.7 cells significantly increased extracellular LTD4 levels (Fig. 5g) and decreased LTE4 (Fig. 5h), whereas overexpression of Dpep2 elicited the opposite effects (Fig. 5i, j). After LPS stimulation, we found similar results (Fig. 5g–j), indicating that DPEP2 retains its metabolic activity under infection conditions. Functionally, LTD4, not LTE4, increased the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Il1b, Il6, and Tnf) in LPS-stimulated macrophages (Fig. 5k), indicating that the LTD4 to LTE4 conversion directly modulates inflammatory output. Thus, DPEP2 suppresses macrophage inflammation, in part, by metabolically directing LTD4 to LTE4.

To confirm whether these effects require DPEP2 enzymatic activity, we identified the essential catalytic glutamate residue using structural conservation among mammalian DPEP enzymes. Human DPEP2 and DPEP1 possess a conserved dizinc catalytic center in their extracellular domains, coordinated by a glutamate residue (E188 in human DPEP2 and E141 in human DPEP1)30,31. Sequence alignment identified E190 as the corresponding residue in mouse DPEP2 (Supplementary Fig. 9). We therefore generated a Flag-tagged WT DPEP2 plasmid and the enzymatic mutant targeting this catalytic residue (E190Q) (Fig. 5l). Both constructs were expressed at comparable levels (Fig. 5m). However, while WT DPEP2 decreased LTD4 and increased LTE4 in the supernatant, the E190Q mutant completely abolished this metabolic activity (Fig. 5n, o), confirming that E190 is essential for the enzymatic conversion of LTD4 to LTE4.

Functionally, overexpression of WT DPEP2 markedly suppressed inflammatory cytokine expression in RAW264.7 cells, whereas the catalytically inactive E190Q mutant failed to confer anti-inflammatory effects (Fig. 5p–r). These results provide direct mechanistic evidence that the immunoregulatory function of DPEP2 strictly depends on its enzymatic activity and its capacity to reprogram eicosanoid metabolism.

DPEP2 modulates CysLTR2 signaling and redirects AA metabolic flux toward PGE2 to regulate LPS-induced macrophage inflammation

Cysteinyl leukotrienes act on cysteinyl leukotriene receptor 1 (CysLTR1) or cysteinyl leukotriene receptor 2 (CysLTR2), promoting inflammatory responses32–34. Therefore, DPEP2 downregulation was hypothesized to upregulate cysteinyl leukotrienes activity, promoting inflammatory responses in macrophages through CysLTR1 or CysLTR2. Indeed, LPS stimulation downregulated Cysltr1 expression, while upregulating Cysltr2 (Fig. 6a, b). Pretreatment of RAW264.7 cells with Zafirlukast, an inhibitor of CysLTR1, failed to suppress the enhanced LPS-induced inflammatory responses caused by Dpep2 knockdown (Fig. 6c). In contrast, pretreatment with the CysLTR2 inhibitor (HAMI3379) abrogated the enhanced inflammatory responses caused by Dpep2 knockdown in LPS-stimulated macrophages (Fig. 6d). These results suggest that the exacerbation of LPS-induced macrophage inflammatory responses by Dpep2 knockdown is partially mediated by increased LTD4 levels via the CysLTR2 pathway.

In addition to cysteinyl leukotrienes metabolism, DPEP2 deficiency also reshaped the AA metabolic network by diverting flux toward PGE2 synthesis. PGE2 (one of eicosanoids), a key mediator of inflammation, is also synthesized from AA via cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes and terminal prostaglandin E synthases (PTGES) (Fig. 5f). Upon 5-LO downregulation, AA increases shunting to PGE2 synthesis35–38. Within the RNA-seq3, Dpep2 knockdown decreased the expression of 5-LO-encoding gene Alox5 and LTC4S-encoding gene Ltc4s, while increasing the expression of COX-encoding gene Ptgs1 and PTGES-encoding gene Ptges (Fig. 6e), which was validated using quantitative PCR (Fig. 6f, g). Protein levels of 5-LO were also decreased in RAW264.7 cells with stable Dpep2 knockdown (Fig. 6h). Correspondingly, RAW264.7 cells secreted more PGE2 after Dpep2 knockdown (Fig. 6i), while Dpep2 overexpression decreased PGE2 secretion (Fig. 6j). Moreover, adding PGE2 to the culture medium increased LPS-induced pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, which was further amplified by combining PGE2 and LTD4 (Fig. 6k). Additionally, PGE2 and LTD4 abrogated the downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines in RAW264.7 cells overexpressing Dpep2 (Fig. 6l).

Together, these findings demonstrate that DPEP2 regulates macrophage inflammation through direct immunometabolic mechanisms, including (1) enzymatic conversion of LTD4 to LTE4, (2) modulation of CysLTR2-mediated signaling, and (3) reprogramming of AA metabolic flux toward PGE2 synthesis. Loss of DPEP2 reprograms these metabolic pathways, synergistically amplifying inflammation in LPS-induced macrophages.

DPEP2 suppresses LPS-induced inflammatory responses by mediating NF-κB signaling

GSEA analysis of RNA-seq3 data comparing shDpep2 and shNT RAW264.7 cells showed that PI3K/AKT, NF-κB, and MAPKs pathways were enriched in shDpep2 RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 7a–c). This finding was further confirmed by pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs after Dpep2 knockdown (Fig. 7d). LPS activates TLR4, which contributes to macrophage inflammatory cytokine production through activation of PI3K/AKT, NF-κB and MAPK pathways39,40. Therefore, the roles of these pathways in DPEP2-mediated inflammatory cytokine regulation were investigated in LPS-induced macrophages.

Cells were pretreated with chemical inhibitors specifically targeting PI3K/AKT (Lyz294002), NF-κB (JSH23), MAPKs/p38 (SB203580), MAPKs/ERK (U0126) and MAPKs/JNK (SP600125), followed by LPS stimulation. Hence, DPEP2 may inhibit inflammatory cytokine production in LPS-induced macrophages through NF-κB and MAPKs/p38 signaling pathways.

The activities of the NF-κB and MAPKs/p38 pathways were then evaluated by detecting essential components in these pathways. The phosphorylation levels of p65 and IKKα/β were downregulated in RAW264.7 cells overexpressing Dpep2 (Fig. 7g) and increased in shDpep2 RAW264.7 cells after LPS stimulation (Fig. 7h). However, no significant differences were observed in the phosphorylation levels of p38 after Dpep2 knockdown or overexpression (Fig. 7g, h). Correspondingly, in a dual-luciferase reporter assay, NF-κB luciferase reporter activity increased in shDpep2 RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 7i) and decreased in RAW264.7 cells overexpressing Dpep2 after LPS stimulation (Fig. 7j).

Next, RAW264.7 cells were treated with LTD4 and PGE2 to mimic the effect of Dpep2 knockdown on eicosanoid metabolism. Consistent with Dpep2 knockdown, LTD4 and PGE2 treatment induced the activation of the NF-κB pathway, as evidenced by increased p65 and IKKα/β phosphorylation in RAW264.7 cells after LPS stimulation (Fig. 7k). In contrast, LTD4 and PGE2 treatment did not affect p38 phosphorylation levels (Fig. 7k). Additionally, LTD4 and PGE2 partially reversed the decreased phosphorylation level of NF-κB pathway induced by Dpep2 overexpression in RAW264.7 cells after LPS stimulation (Fig. 7l). These results suggest that in LPS-induced septic macrophages, DPEP2 reduction triggers the NF-κB pathway-mediated inflammatory response, partly via LTD4 and PGE2 metabolism.

LNP-mediated DPEP2/Dpep2 mRNA delivery to monocytes/macrophages alleviates sepsis

Considering the protective role of DPEP2 in monocytes/macrophages during sepsis, the therapeutic potential of DPEP2 was evaluated by restoring monocytes/macrophages DPEP2 expression and activity. To address the current lack of DPEP2-targeted therapeutics, we developed an LNP-mediated gene delivery system specifically for DPEP2 modulation. LNP technology has demonstrated broad therapeutic potential across multiple disease areas including oncology, genetic disorders, and infectious diseases41,42. LNPs were specifically developed to deliver DPEP2/Dpep2 mRNA into monocytes/macrophages as illustrated in Fig. 8a. To this end, both human DPEP2 and mouse Dpep2 mRNAs were constructed (Supplementary Fig. 11a). After camouflaging with the hybrid liposomal membrane, the hydrodynamic diameter of the nanoformulation increased from 66.8 ± 0.42 nm to 142.6 ± 0.50 nm (Supplementary Fig. 11b), while the zeta potential decreased from 60.26 ± 5.56 mV to 27.97 ± 0.74 mV (Supplementary Fig. 11c). Injecting CLP or Sham mice (mice underwent the same procedures of CLP surgery without cecal puncture) with the LNPs did not induce toxic side effects based on peripheral blood pro-inflammatory cytokines, lung injury score, and serum biochemical markers (ALT, AST, BUN, creatinine and CK-MB) (Supplementary Fig. 11d–l). The efficiency of LNP-mediated mRNA delivery was evaluated in mouse macrophages. Following tail vein injection of LNP-Dpep2 (LNP encapsulating 20 μg Dpep2-mRNA) to mice, the proportion of DPEP2+ macrophages in the spleen increased significantly 12 h post-injection and subsided after 36 h of injection (Supplementary Fig. 11m). To directly assess the immunocyte tropism of the LNPs, we performed an in vivo experiment using Cre-reporter mice, which carry a LoxP-flanked STOP cassette controlling tdTomato expression (Supplementary Fig. 12a). After intravenous delivery of Cre mRNA via LNPs, mice were euthanized at 24 h, and splenic and peripheral blood cells were analyzed by flow cytometry. The results clearly demonstrated that the LNPs efficiently transfected both macrophages and monocytes in both the spleen and peripheral blood (Supplementary Fig. 12b, c). Consistent with lentivirus-mediated Dpep2 overexpression data, ex vivo administration of LNP-DPEP2/Dpep2 to both THP-1-derived human macrophages and RAW264.7 cells suppressed LPS-induced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels (Fig. 8b and Supplementary Fig. 11n).

To evaluate the protective effect of LNP-Dpep2 in vivo, CLP-induced septic mice were treated with LNP-Dpep2 via tail vein injection. The analysis included measuring inflammatory responses, organ injury indicators, lung histopathology, and survival rate (Fig. 8c). Considering the effect of LNP-Dpep2 lasted for about 36 h and the mortality of CLP-induced septic mice was highest within 48 h after CLP, LNP-Dpep2 was administered to septic mice every 24 h for three times (Fig. 8c). LNP-Dpep2 treatment significantly improved the survival rates of CLP-induced septic mice (Fig. 8d). Significant reductions were observed in IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF levels in the peripheral blood of LNP-Dpep2 treated CLP septic mice (Fig. 8e–g). Blood biochemistry revealed lower aspartate aminotransferase (AST), BUN, creatinine, and CK-MB levels in the peripheral blood of septic mice treated with LNP-Dpep2 compared to those treated with empty-loaded LNPs (Fig.8h-l), suggesting that LNP-Dpep2 exerts a protective effect on heart, liver, and kidney injury during sepsis. Additionally, LNP-Dpep2 treatment mitigated lung injury, pulmonary inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 8m), and cell apoptosis in the lungs in CLP septic mice (Supplementary Fig. 13b).

These results suggest that DPEP2 in monocytes/macrophages alleviates systemic inflammation, protects mice against sepsis, and can be targeted therapeutically via LNP delivery to monocytes/macrophages.

Discussion

Excessive inflammation in sepsis is primarily mediated by the release of pro-inflammatory mediators from multiple cell types43, and is characterized by cellular damage that results in microcirculatory disorder and organ damage44. Early death from sepsis (usually due to cardiovascular collapse and multi-organ dysfunction) is primarily driven by excessive inflammation43. In our study, integrative scRNA-seq and RNA-seq1 analyses of PBMCs from patients with sepsis revealed a consistent and marked downregulation of DPEP2 in circulating monocytes during the early acute phase of sepsis, and this reduction correlated with more severe systemic inflammation and increased mortality in a prospective patient cohort. These clinical findings indicate that decreased monocyte DPEP2 expression may contribute to the hyperinflammatory state observed in patients with sepsis. Consistently, in mouse CLP models we observed a similar decrease in DPEP2 expression in monocytes/macrophages, confirming that DPEP2 suppression is a conserved feature across species during acute sepsis.

Building on these clinical observations, we demonstrated through gain-of-function experiments that restoring DPEP2 expression in monocytes/macrophages is sufficient to attenuate inflammation. Using an LNP-based mRNA delivery system targeting monocytes/macrophages developed in this study, we achieved efficient and selective DPEP2 overexpression in monocytes/macrophages in vivo. Pharmaceutical agents, such as rmSLAMF7 protein45, perhexiline46, 5Z-7-OX47, saquinavir48 and losartan49, possess anti-inflammatory properties on macrophages that can increase survival of septic mice. However, their broad action and off-target effects can lead to potential systemic toxicity, limiting their application. Consequently, the primary focus in developing next-generation therapies for sepsis is on targeted genome-editing technologies and drug-delivery systems that minimize adverse effects on non-targeted cells50,51. In this study, a previously unreported LNP-mediated Dpep2 mRNA delivery system was developed that specifically upregulates DPEP2 expression in monocytes/macrophages in vivo. This system reduces inflammation, alleviates organ injury, and improves survival rates in mice during acute sepsis. The LNP-mediated Dpep2 mRNA delivery system may represent a promising approach for drug development in early acute sepsis treatment.

LNPs have recently been used as the delivery system for nucleic acid-based therapeutics, as evidenced by the clinical approval of three LNP-RNA formulations, patisiran, BNT162b2, and COVID-19 mRNA52,53, paving the way for developing additional LNP-mRNA-based therapeutics for future clinical translation. Interest in using nucleic acid-based therapeutics to treat sepsis is growing54. Two studies14,55 have explored LNP-mRNA therapeutics for sepsis management in immunocompromised murine models, both utilizing macrophage-targeted delivery to enhance macrophage-mediated pathogen clearance. However, no research has reported using LNP-based delivery systems to mitigate excessive inflammatory responses in sepsis treatment. A major strength of the LNP-mediated Dpep2 mRNA delivery system is its ability to significantly reduce inflammation, organ injury, and sepsis mortality in CLP-induced septic mice, with no detectable systemic adverse effects attributable to the mRNA or LNP, even after repeated treatments with monocytes/macrophages-targeted LNP-Dpep2. Another advantage of LNP-mediated Dpep2 mRNA delivery is the relatively short half-life of delivered mRNA. Since most fatalities occur within the first 48 h during the acute phase of sepsis in CLP-induced septic mice56, the LNP-mRNA system may offer a safer and more effective therapeutic alternative compared to continuously expressed viral vector systems. Furthermore, the critical time window for high mortality in the acute phase of patients with sepsis is usually within 72 h after diagnosis57, making LNP-mediated Dpep2 mRNA delivery attractive for broad clinical translation. Thus, in contrast to the recent study by Zhan Z., et al., which targeted upstream regulators of DPEP258, we demonstrate direct therapeutic targeting of DPEP2 using an LNP-mRNA system, thereby establishing a precise and clinically compatible strategy for restoring DPEP2 function in acute sepsis.

To generate translationally relevant evidence, we further developed a macrophage activation syndrome (MAS) model using human THP-1-derived macrophages, in which inflammatory cytokine levels closely mirrored those observed in patient samples. In this human-relevant system, LNP-mediated delivery of human DPEP2 mRNA significantly attenuated cytokine production, providing cross-species validation of our findings and underscoring the translational potential of this approach.

Mechanistically, our functional assays revealed that DPEP2 downregulation reshapes eicosanoid metabolism. Eicosanoids, bioactive lipids derived from AA, function as core regulators at the interface of metabolism and immunity—forming a central axis of immunometabolic control in inflammatory diseases and sepsis, as exemplified by PGE2, cysteinyl leukotrienes (LTC4/LTD4/LTE4), and lipoxins (LX)59–61. DPEP2 catalyzes the conversion of LTD4 to LTE418, and our findings reveal that DPEP2 downregulation disrupts cysteinyl leukotriene metabolism by impairing this conversion, resulting in a pronounced accumulation of LTD4 and consequently enhancing cysteinyl leukotrienes bioactivity20. Cysteinyl leukotrienes are pro-inflammatory mediators in allergic diseases62, while LTD4 primes macrophages to release more pro-inflammatory cytokines after LPS stimulation63. Thus, this metabolic shift directly amplifies inflammatory output during LPS stimulation. Cysteinyl leukotrienes exert their effects via CysLTR1 and CysLTR2, both of which are expressed by macrophages64. The roles of CysLTR1 and CysLTR2 in sepsis remain poorly understood. Our results identify CysLTR2, rather than CysLTR1, as the dominant receptor mediating LTD4-driven inflammation in LPS-stimulated macrophages. This observation aligns with reports that LPS preferentially induces CysLTR2 signaling in dendritic cells65 and that CysLTR2 activation contributes to inflammatory responses in platelets66 and microglia34. Furthermore, our study demonstrates that LTD4 enhances NF-κB phosphorylation in LPS-stimulated macrophages. This indicates that elevated LTD4 levels, resulting from impaired DPEP2 enzymatic activity, amplify NF-κB–dependent inflammatory signaling through CysLTR2 during sepsis.

Beyond cysteinyl leukotriene dysregulation, DPEP2 deficiency also reprograms AA metabolism toward enhanced PGE₂ synthesis. In this study, an increase in PGE2 levels was observed following Dpep2 knockdown, potentially attributed to reduced 5-LO expression in macrophages36–38. Cysteinyl leukotrienes and PGE2 are rapidly produced at inflammatory sites and facilitate pro-inflammatory macrophage activation67–69, and their coordinated dysregulation represents a broader collapse of lipid-based immunometabolic homeostasis. Moreover, combining LTD4 with PGE2 enhances inflammatory gene expression and macrophage inflammatory protein 1β secretion, triggering diverse macrophage inflammatory responses through NF-κB signaling in vascular inflammation33. Our study extends this concept to infection-induced inflammation by demonstrating that LTD4 and PGE2 synergistically amplify NF-κB activation and cytokine expression in LPS-stimulated macrophages. This metabolic synergy highlights how DPEP2 constrains inflammation not through a single pathway but by governing the global balance of the eicosanoid network, a central principle of immunometabolism. Importantly, catalytic-site mutagenesis experiments in our study provide direct mechanistic evidence that DPEP2’s immunoregulatory function is strictly dependent on its enzymatic activity. These findings definitively demonstrate that the anti-inflammatory role of DPEP2 is not due to structural effects or transcriptional associations but requires its enzymatic capacity to remodel eicosanoid metabolism. Together with our receptor-inhibition and lipid-quantification data, these results firmly establish DPEP2 as a bona fide enzymatic immunometabolic regulator. These results highlight DPEP2 and its enzymatic activity as promising therapeutic targets for restoring eicosanoid balance and mitigating excessive inflammation in sepsis.

Our study expands the current understanding of DPEP2 biology. While DPEP2 has been primarily implicated in regulating tumor-associated macrophages70 and viral myocarditis71, our clinical and mechanistic findings reveal a previously unrecognized protective role in bacterial sepsis, where it mitigates systemic inflammation. In contrast to Zhan Z. et al. recent demonstration of DPEP2’s role in macrophage-mediated intestinal inflammation and sepsis58, our study offers distinct and complementary contributions—including clinical validation, therapeutic innovation, and distinct mechanistic insights—that collectively advance the understanding and translational potential of DPEP2 in sepsis.

This study also has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, our experimental strategy primarily relied on in vitro human and murine macrophage systems, as well as a mouse model of sepsis. While these models provide valuable mechanistic insights, they remain inherently limited in capturing the full complexity and heterogeneity of human sepsis pathophysiology. Future studies incorporating human 3D organoid systems will enable a more physiologically faithful exploration of macrophage fate, functional responses, and the therapeutic potential of LNP-DPEP2. Second, the clinical cohort in this study, while providing consistent and biologically meaningful results, constitutes an initial dataset. Validation in larger and more diverse cohorts will be critical to robustly establish DPEP2 as a biomarker and to confirm the generalizability of its prognostic utility across broader clinical settings. Finally, our in vivo experiments were performed in juvenile mice, which do not fully recapitulate the immune, metabolic, and physiological landscape of older adults who most commonly develop sepsis. Future work employing adult or aged animal models will be essential to establish the translational robustness of DPEP2 as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target.

In summary, this study provides evidence that DPEP2 is an important negative regulator of sepsis-induced inflammation, primarily by suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine production through eicosanoid reprogramming. Our findings shed light on the regulatory role of DPEP2 in sepsis and uncover the immunotherapeutic potential of the LNP-mediated Dpep2 delivery system, which may support the development of immunotherapeutic strategies for sepsis and is encouraged for future clinical translation.

Methods

Study approval

All experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, China. The human cohort study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University [Number B2024-374R], and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400091373) (https://www.chictr.org.cn/index.html). Informed consent was obtained from all patients or individuals authorized by the participants, including consent to publish clinical information that could potentially identify individuals. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Fudan University [Number 202412032Z] and complied with the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals and the guidelines set by the Animal Review Committee of Fudan University.

Human participants

Patients in the early acute phase of sepsis were diagnosed with sepsis based on the Third International Consensus Definition for Sepsis72 and should be diagnosed within 24 h. Inclusion criteria for patients with sepsis were as following: 1) evidence of bacterial infection; 2) SOFA score ≥ 2; 3) written informed consent was obtainable. Eligible controls were individuals without evidence of infection who provided written informed consent. The exclusion criteria for patients and controls included: 1) participation in organ transplantation within one month; 2) active cancer; 3) human immunodeficiency virus infection/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome; 4) autoimmune diseases; 5) participation in other drug trials 30 days before enrollment; 6) parturient or breast-feeding women. The clinical characteristics of the participants are documented (Supplementary Tables 3–6).

Isolation PBMCs from whole blood

Human PBMCs were extracted from anticoagulated whole blood using density-gradient centrifugation73. Whole blood was diluted 1:1 with 1 × phosphate buffer saline (PBS; C10010500BT, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), layered onto Lymphoprep (07851, Stem Cell, Vancouver, Canada), and centrifuged at 800 × g for 30 min. The PBMC layer was isolated and erythrocytes were removed using red blood cell lysis buffer (00-4333-57, Thermo Fisher Scientific) on ice. PBMCs were washed and resuspended in PBS for subsequent assays.

scRNA-seq data and analysis

The scRNA-seq dataset for the PBMCs of patients in the early acute phase of sepsis and healthy controls was obtained from our previous study16 (Supplementary Tables 1, 2) and downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE217906). The scRNA-seq and V(D)J libraries were constructed using the 10 × Genomics Chromium Controller Instrument, Chromium Single Cell 5′ library and gel bead kit, and the V(D)J enrichment kit (10 × Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA). Cells were concentrated to approximately 1000 cells/µL and introduced into each channel to produce single-cell gel bead-in-emulsions (GEM). Following reverse transcription, the GEMs were disrupted, and the barcoded cDNA was isolated and amplified. The final libraries were quantified utilizing a Qubit High-Sensitivity DNA assay (Q33230, Thermo Fisher Scientific); their size distribution was evaluated using a High-Sensitivity DNA chip on a Bioanalyzer 2200 (Agilent). All libraries were sequenced on an Illumina sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with a 150-bp paired-end run. Fastp74 was employed with default settings to filter the adaptor sequences and remove low-quality reads, yielding clean data. Feature-barcode matrices were generated by aligning the reads to the human genome (GRCh38 Ensemble: version 91) using CellRanger v3.1.0. For cell normalization and regression, the Seurat package (version 3.1.4, https://satijalab.org/seurat/) was utilized to scale the data based on unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts and mitochondrial read percentages. Marker genes were identified using the graph-based clustering method and FindAllMarkers function, using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test algorithm and the following criteria: P adjusted <0.05. To identify DEGs, the FindMarkers Seurat function with the Wilcox rank sum test algorithm was used under the following criteria: lnFC > 0.25; P adjusted <0.05; min.pct > 0.1.

A single-cell trajectory analysis was performed utilizing Monocle2 (http://cole-trapnell-lab.github.io/monocle-release) with DDR-Tree and default parameters. Based on the pseudo-time analysis, branch expression analysis modeling (BEAM) was applied for branch fate-determined gene analysis. GO analysis elucidated the biological implications of the marker genes and DEGs. GO annotations were downloaded from NCBI, UniProt (http://www.uniprot.org/), and Gene Ontology (http://www.geneontology.org/) databases. GSEA was performed on a preranked gene list, with gene expression fold change as the ranking metric, using the GSEA desktop application75. Fisher’s exact test was used to identify significant GO categories, and false discovery rate (FDR) was used to correct the P-values. P adjusted <0.05 was considered significantly different.

Bulk RNA-seq data and analysis

We performed RNA-seq on PBMCs from five patients with sepsis and five controls (original dataset, RNA-seq1) (Supplementary Tables 3, 4) and analyzed publicly available bulk RNA-seq data from whole blood of sepsis survivors and non-survivors (GSE54514, RNA-seq2). Additionally, total RNA from RAW264.7 cells with DPEP2 deficiency by shRNA-mediated Dpep2 knockdown (shDpep2 RAW264.7 cells) and control RAW264.7 cells was also obtained for RNA-seq analysis (original dataset, RNA-seq3). Total RNA was extracted to construct libraries with the Illumina TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit. The library was sequenced using paired-end 150-bp reads. Raw FASTQ reads were processed using Trim Galore (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore/) and transcript quantification was conducted using Salmon software in quasi-mapping mode76. Salmon “quant” files were subsequently converted using the Tximport function. DEG analysis was performed between two groups using log2FoldChange values in the RNA-seq analysis, with a significance threshold set at P adjusted <0.05. The results were visualized using heatmaps and volcano plots and subjected to GSEA.

Mice and treatment

Eight- to ten-week-old mice of both sexes were used for all animal experiments, except for six-week-old mice that were used for BMDM isolation. All mice were bred on a C57BL/6 background and housed in a specific pathogen-free environment with access to food and water at an environmental temperature of 20–26 °C and a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. Relative humidity was around 40– 70%. All experimental and control animals were co-housed. C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Charles River (Nanjing, China, Stock number: 213). Dpep2 knockout mice (Dpep2 KO), Dpep2f/f mice, macrophage-specific Dpep2-deficient mice (Dpep2f/fLyz2Cre), and Cre-reporter mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Limited Liability Company (Nanjing, China, Stock numbers: T002363, T008962, T066441, and T003822, respectively). Dpep2 KO and Dpep2f/fLyz2Cre mice were generated from the same strain as the C57BL/6 J lineage. Dpep2f/fLyz2Cre mice were generated by crossing Dpep2f/f mice with Lyz2Cre mice.

Mice were randomly assigned to groups, and a CLP model was established45. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (4% for induction and 2% for maintenance). The abdominal cavity was opened under sterile conditions, the cecum was carefully exposed, and approximately 75% of the cecum was ligated using a 5-0 suture and punctured twice with a 20-gauge needle to expel a small amount of fecal matter into the abdominal cavity. The cecum was then repositioned, and the incision was closed with two layers of sutures. The sham-operated mice underwent the same procedures without cecal puncture. Mice intraperitoneally received 1 mL of 0.9% saline for fluid resuscitation and buprenorphine (0.05 mg/kg) for analgesia after the surgery.

For macrophage DPEP2 activation in vivo, an initial dose of LNP-Dpep2 (LNP encapsulating 20 μg Dpep2-mRNA) was injected intravenously via the tail vein 2 h after sepsis model induction, followed by one injection every 24 h for a total of three times. The control mice received the same volume of LNP without Dpep2-mRNA.

At specified time points, mice were euthanized via CO₂ inhalation until respiration came to a complete stop and reflexes in the animals’ paws could no longer be triggered. Then, samples were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (E672002, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), or stored at -80 °C. Whole blood samples were collected in serum separator tubes, allowed to coagulate for 2 h at room temperature, and then centrifuged at 1000 ×g for 20 min to collect sera.

BMDMs and human monocyte isolation

Murine BMDMs were generated as follows45: bone marrow cells were harvested by flushing the femurs of six-week-old WT or Dpep2 KO mice. After erythrocyte lysis with red blood cell lysis buffer (00-4333-57, Thermo Fisher Scientific), the bone marrow cells were cultured in BMDM differentiation medium (Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium [DMEM, C11995500BT, Gibco, CA, USA] containing 10% fetal bovine serum [FBS, FBSSR-01021-500, OriCell, Guangzhou, China] and 30% L929-conditioned supernatant) for 7 days. BMDMs were obtained, with a purity of > 90%, as confirmed using flow cytometry. Human monocytes were isolated from the PBMCs of healthy participants using the Human Monocyte Isolation Kit (19359, Stem Cell) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The presence of purified monocytes was confirmed using flow cytometry before further use.

THP-1 derived macrophages

THP-1 cells (TIB-202, ATCC) were differentiated into macrophages by treatment with 100 nM PMA (P1585, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 48 h, followed by 24 h rest in fresh Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 complete medium (C11875500BT, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Flow cytometry confirmed macrophage purity >90%.

Plasmid construction

The shRNA oligos of Dpep2 and Egr1 were ordered from GENEWIZ (China), and their sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 7. Oligo annealing was performed by mixing 5 μL of 100 μM forward and reverse oligos, followed by PCR: 95 °C for 10 min using PCR machine, and then slowly cooling to 4 °C. Next, 2 μg of the pLKO.1-puro plasmid77 (Addgene, 8453) was digested with 1 μL AgeI (R3552S, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), 1 μL EcoRI (R3101V, New England Biolabs), 2 μL CutSmart Buffer (B6004V, New England Biolabs), and ddH2O (total volume is 20 μL) at room temperature for 2 h. The digested pLKO.1-puro fragments were analyzed via agarose gel electrophoresis and purified using a DNA Purification Kit (DP103-03, Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). The annealed oligos and pLKO.1-puro fragments were ligated with T4 ligase (M0202S, New England Biolabs) at room temperature for 2 h. Next, 5 μL of the ligation mix was introduced into 50 μL of competent Stbl3 cells (TSC-C06, Tsingke Biotech, Beijing, China), incubated on ice for 30 min, heat-shocked at 42 °C for 50 s, and returned to ice for 2 min. Subsequently, 700 μL of SOC medium (B540119-0100, Sangon Biotech) was added and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. Successfully ligated plasmids were verified by sequencing (Sangon Biotech) and purified using a DNA Purification Kit (DP118, Tiangen Biotech). The Flag-tagged plasmids overexpressing Dpep2 and DPEP2 point mutants were constructed as follows: The full-length coding sequence (CDS) of mouse DPEP2 (NM_001301204.1) was amplified from RAW264.7 cell cDNA by PCR using PrimeSTAR Max DNA Polymerase Ver.2 (R047A, TakaraBio, Shiga, Japan), following the manufacturer’s instructions. To generate an N-terminally tagged construct, a Kozak–DPEP2–2×FLAG fragment was assembled by PCR fusion, ensuring the inclusion of a consensus Kozak sequence to enhance translational efficiency. The resulting fragment and the pLVX-puro lentiviral expression vector78 (Addgene, 125839) were digested with XbaI (R0145S, New England Biolabs) and XhoI (R0146S, New England Biolabs), purified, and ligated using T4 DNA ligase. Recombinant plasmids were transformed into Stbl3 competent cells selected on LB agar plates containing ampicillin (B540119-0500, Sangon Biotech), and verified by colony PCR and Sanger sequencing. DPEP2 point mutants were generated by overlapping-extension PCR using mutation-specific primers. The mutant PCR products were digested with XbaI and XhoI and cloned into the pLVX-puro vector using the same procedure as for the wild-type construct. All mutant plasmids were sequence-validated to confirm the presence of the intended nucleotide substitutions and to exclude unwanted secondary mutations.

Lentivirus packaging and infection

HEK-293T cells (CRL-11268, ATCC) were seeded in 100-mm dishes and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 1–2 days. Transfection was performed when the cells reached 100% confluence. The medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM 0.5–1 h before transfection. The transfection mixture was prepared by combining 5 μg of shRNA plasmid/pLVX-DPEP2-puro plasmid, 3.75 μg of psPAX2 packaging plasmid (Addgene, 12260), and 1.25 μg of pMD2.G envelope plasmid (Addgene, 12259) in 400 μL of serum-free DMEM. Next, 20 μL of LipoD293 In Vitro DNA Transfection Reagent (SL100668, SignaGen Laboratories, Rockville, MD, USA) diluted in 400 μL of serum-free DMEM was added to the mixture. After 10 min of incubation at room temperature, the mixture was added dropwise to the cells, and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After 12 h, the medium was replaced with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS without antibiotics, and the viral supernatants were harvested 48 h post-transfection, and passed through a 0.45-μm filter to remove cells. For the lentivirus infection, 2 mL of viral supernatant was used to transduce 5 × 106 RAW264.7 cells or BMDMs in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene (H8761, SignaGen Laboratories) for 24 h. At 48 h post-infection, RAW264.7 cells or BMDMs were selected using 5 μg/mL puromycin (sc-108071, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Damascus, MD, USA) for 7 days.

Cell culture and cell treatment

RAW264.7 murine macrophage-like cells (TIB-71, ATCC), BMDMs, and HEK-293T cells (CRL-11268, ATCC) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (15140122, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Human monocyte, THP-1 (TIB-202, ATCC) and L929 cells (CCL-1, ATCC) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin.

RAW264.7 cells were treated with TLR1/2 ligands (1 μg/mL Pam3Csk4; HY-P1180A, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), TLR7/8 ligands (1 μg/mL R848; HY-13740, MedChemExpress) and TLR3 ligands (1 μg/mL poly(I:C); HY-107202, MedChemExpress) for 12 h. RAW264.7 cells, human monocytes, and BMDMs were stimulated with LPS (L4391, Sigma) to establish an in vitro inflammatory model. RAW264.7 cells were treated with LTD4 (1 μM; 20310, Cayman, Ann Arbor, MI, USA), LTE4 (1 μM; 20410, Cayman) or PGE2 (1 μM; HY-101952, MedChemExpress) in vitro. RAW264.7 cells were treated with a EGR1 inhibitor (5 μM EGR-1-IN-1 (IT25); HY-163731, MedChemExpress), CysLTR1 inhibitor (5 μM Zafrilukast; HY-17492, MedChemExpress), CysLTR2 inhibitor (1 μM HAMI3379; HY-112248A MedChemExpress), NF-κB inhibitor (30 μM JSH-23; HY-13982, MedChemExpress), ERK inhibitor (1 μM U0126; HY-12031A, MedChemExpress), JNK inhibitor (25 μM SP600125; HY-12041, MedChemExpress), p38 MAPK inhibitor (30 μM SB203580; HY-10256, MedChemExpress), or AKT inhibitor (30 μM Ly294002; HY-10108, MedChemExpress), for 1 h and stimulated with LPS (1 μg/mL) for 12 h.

Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining and histological analysis

Mice were euthanized, and their lungs were removed and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 24 h. Tissues were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and stained with H&E. Slides were evaluated under a light microscope to assess the severity of lung injury79. Five random high-power fields were blindly scored independently for each slice. The lung injury score was based on leukocyte infiltration, alveolar edema, hyaline membranes, proteinaceous debris in the alveolar space and the thickness of the alveolar septa79.

Immunofluorescent staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed as follows25: 24 h after surgery, mice were anesthetized and subjected to transcardial perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde. The brains were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into 5 μm-thick sections. The paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated for antigen retrieval. The primary antibodies were: rabbit anti-ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba-1) antibody (1:1000; 019-19741, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical, Japan) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) Polyclonal antibody (1:200; 16825-1-AP, Proteintech, Chicago, IL, USA). Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan).

TUNEL staining

Paraffin-embedded lung tissue sections were subjected to TUNEL staining to assess cell apoptosis according to the manufacturer’s protocol (A111-01, Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated using the SteadyPure Universal RNA Extraction Kit (AG21017, Accurate Biotechnology, Guangzhou, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration of RNA was determined using a Thermo Scientific NanoDrop One/One C Microvolume UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Reverse transcription of RNA was performed using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (4368814, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 1 μg of total RNA. The mRNA levels of the target genes were assessed using the PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (4309155, Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a Bio-Rad CFX96 real-time system (Life Technologies). mRNA expression was quantified using the comparative Ct (2-ΔΔCt) method and normalized to β-actin expression. Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 8.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Cell culture media and mouse sera were collected, and cytokine production was measured using mouse TNF (EK282, MultiSciences, Hangzhou, China), IL-1β (EK201B, MultiSciences), IL-6 (EK206, MultiSciences), LTD4 (mI037877, mlbio, Shanghai, China), LTE4 (ml495212, mlbio), and PGE2 (mI037542, mlbio) ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Protein extraction and western blot

Cells and lung tissues were lysed with RIPA Buffer (R10010, Solarbio, Beijing, China) supplemented with a proteinase inhibitor cocktail (4693124001, Sigma). Equal amounts of protein (determined using BCA assay) were loaded onto 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gels, separated by electrophoresis, and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in Tris-buffered saline (pH 7.4) (PS105S, Epizyme Biomedical Technology, Shanghai, China) containing 0.1% Tween-20 (HY-Y1891, MedChemExpress) for 2 h and incubated with primary antibodies overnight against DPEP2 (1:1000; A15502, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), NF-κB p65 (1:1000; sc8008, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), Phospho-NF-κB p65 (1:3000; 3033 T, Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA, USA), IKKα (1:1000; sc7606, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), Phospho-IKKα/β (1:3000; 2697 T, Cell Signaling Technology), p38 MAPK (1:1000; sc7972, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), Phospho-p38 MAPK (1:3000; 4511 T, Cell Signaling Technology), 5-LO (1:1000; sc136195, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), Flag (1:3000; F1804, Sigma), β-Actin (1:3000; GB15003-100, Servicebio, Woburn, MA, USA). The membranes were washed the next day and incubated with Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (1:3000; M21002S, Abmart, Shanghai, China) or Goat Anti-Mouse IgG HRP (1:3000; M21001S, Abmart) for 1.5 h at room temperature. An ECL chemiluminescence kit (SB-WB004, Share-bio, Shanghai, China) and a ChemiDoc XRS+ chemiluminescence gel imaging system (BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA) were used to visualize the target protein bands.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions were prepared and stained in PBS with the following antibodies for 40 min on ice in the dark: APC anti-mouse/human CD11b Antibody (1:100, 101211, Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA), APC anti-human CD14 Antibody (1:20, 399205, Biolegend), PE anti-human CD11b Antibody (1:20, 393111, Biolegend), Pacific Blue anti-human CD3 Antibody (1:20, 300329, Biolegend), PE anti-mouse F4/80 Antibody (1:20, 123109, Biolegend), DPEP2 antibody (FITC) (1:40, orb187504, Biorbyt, Cambridge, UK). Flow cytometry was performed using a Thermo Attune NxT Flow Cytometer, and data were analyzed by Flowjo software.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 24-well plates (5 × 104 cells/well) and transfected when they reached 60–80% confluence. The cells were transfected with 500 μL of penicillin-streptomycin-free DMEM, 2 μL Advanced DNA RNA Transfection reagent (AD600025, Zeta Life, Menlo Park, CA, USA), 1 μg shDpep2 plasmid/Dpep2 overexpression plasmid/control vector plasmid, 0.75 μg pNFκB-luciferase reporter plasmid (pNFκB-luc) (D2206, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), and 0.25 μg internal control plasmid phRL-TK (D2760, Beyotime Biotechnology) per well. After 24 h of transfection, the medium was replaced with 200 μL of fresh medium, and the cells were incubated for an additional 48 h. The cells were then treated with 1 μg/mL LPS for 24 h, harvested, and lysed, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (FR201, TransGen, Beijing, China) on a Tecan Spark multimode microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to the Renilla luciferase activity.

Assessment of organ injury markers

Plasma levels of ALT, AST, creatinine, BUN, and CK-MB were measured using an automatic chemical analyzer (BS-240 Chemistry Analyzer; Mindray, Shenzhen, China).

Mouse Dpep2 and human DPEP2 mRNA construction

The mouse Dpep2 sequences were amplified by primer Dpep2_F (5′-AGAGAACCCGCCACCATGGCCTGGCATGGAGAGACATACT-3′) and Dpep2_R (5′-CGAGGCTCCAGCTCATCACACAATAAGGCCTAATAAG-3′). Amplified DNA sequences were inserted into the in vitro transcription (IVT) vector (6143, TakaraBio) with EcoRI/BamHI cutting sites using the Takara IVTpro mRNA synthesis system (638947, TakaraBio). The recombinant plasmid overexpressing Dpep2 was treated with the Hind III restriction enzyme (R0104S, New England Biolabs) at 37 °C for 1 h, and the linearized template plasmid was purified using a PCR Purification Kit (19106ES50, Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Linearized template plasmids were used for IVT of mRNA. Dpep2 mRNA was produced through an IVT process employing T7 RNA Polymerase (EP0111, Thermo Fisher Scientific), pyrophosphatase (EF0221, Thermo Fisher Scientific), RNase (R1253, Thermo Fisher Scientific), GTP (R0461, Thermo Fisher Scientific), CTP (R0451, Thermo Fisher Scientific), ATP (R0441, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and N1-Methylpseudouridine (N1-Mep-UTP, R0491SKB012, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Following transcription, the reaction mixture was treated with DNase I (18047019, Thermo Fisher Scientific), precipitated, and purified using lithium chloride (A10531-22, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Oligo dT purification was employed to further isolate mRNA using Oligo dT (K04N2901, Nanomicrotech Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China). The human DPEP2 mRNA was purchased from Tsingke Biotech (China). The purity was detected using an Agilent 4200 Fragment Analyzer system.

Construction and characterization of LNPs

Ethanol solutions of ionizable lipids (A500737-0005, Sangon Biotech), cholesterol (57-88-5, Adamas, Shanghai, China), 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphorylcholine (DSPC, S01005, AVT, Shanghai, China), and 1,2-dimyristoyl-rac-glycero-3-methoxypolyethylene glycol-2000 (DMG-PEG2000, O02005, AVT) were mixed at a molar ratio of 50:19.25:5:0.75. The lipid mixture was added dropwise to sodium acetate buffer (25 mM, pH 5.2) to prepare blank LNPs. The LNPs were dialyzed using a Slide-A-Lyzer MINI Dialysis Device (3.5 K molecular weight cutoff, Thermo Fisher Scientific). LNP/mRNA formulations were prepared by mixing blank LNPs and mRNA at a mass ratio of 10:1 (ionizable lipid/mRNA). The size, polydispersity index (PDI), and surface zeta potential of the LNPs were characterized using a Zetasizer Nano (Malvern Instruments, Malvern). The in vivo efficiency of LNP-Dpep2 was determined by flow cytometry. After tail injection of LNP-Dpep2, mice were euthanized at the indicated time, and the spleens were removed for flow cytometry to detect the proportion of DPEP2+F4/80+ macrophages14.

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous outcomes determined using the Shapiro‒Wilk test were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and assessed using two-sided Student’s t-test for two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Bonferroni multiple comparisons or two-way repeated measures ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test for three or more groups. Non-normally distributed data were reported as median with interquartile ranges (IQR) and assessed using the two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Kruskal‒Wallis test followed by the Dunn post hoc test. Categorical outcomes were compared using a two-sided Fisher’s exact test with low expected cell counts. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to analyze the effect of DPEP2 expression on the risk of sepsis-related death. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to assess the relationship between the percentage of DPEP2+ monocytes and clinical laboratory indicators. The lon-rank (Mantel Cox) test was used for survival analysis. The datasets were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA) or R software (version 4.2.1, R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.
