Impact of an interventional thirst bundle on thirst discomfort and dry mouth in nasogastric tube-fed patients: Quasi-experimental time-series study
Authors: Merin Mathew, Latha Sambatra, Sujatha R Kannappan, Timi Thomas, Priya J. Monteiro, Gincy Joseph
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Nasogastric (NG) tube feeding is commonly used for patients unable to eat orally but often causes thirst discomfort, dry mouth, reduced sensory satisfaction, and poor appetite. This study evaluated the efficacy of an interventional thirst bundle on thirst discomfort and dry mouth in patients receiving NG feeding.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A quasi-experimental time-series study was conducted from 2018 to 2020 at a 1000-bed tertiary care hospital in South Karnataka, India. Ninety adult patients receiving NG feeding and reporting thirst (score > 5) were selected using convenience sampling and randomly assigned to experimental (n = 45) and control (n = 45) groups via the simple random lottery method. The intervention included three cold lip swabs, a 2 ml sterile water buccal spray, and a lip moisturizer administered thrice daily for 2 days. Thirst and dry mouth were measured using validated tools. Data were analyzed using SPSS v. 23.0, with independent t-test, and repeated measures ANOVA.RESULTS:The experimental group showed significant reductions in thirst and dry mouth at all time points (P < 0.001). Mean thirst scores declined from 16.96 ± 2.81 to 2.58 ± 1.03, and dry mouth scores from 4.51 ± 0.59 to 0.49 ± 0.20. Control group scores remained high. ANOVA confirmed statistical significance (P < 0.001).CONCLUSION:The interventional thirst bundle effectively reduced thirst and dry mouth among NG-fed patients. The researchers recommend incorporating this simple, low-cost intervention into routine nursing care to improve patient comfort and hydration. Implementing this approach in other hospital settings could especially benefit patients who are unable to drink fluids or maintain oral moisture due to NG feeding.

Introduction

Malnutrition remains a prevalent and critical concern in healthcare, affecting up to 40–70% of hospitalized patients and significantly contributing to increased morbidity and mortality.[12] Despite its severe implications, malnutrition frequently goes unrecognized in clinical settings, resulting in prolonged periods of inadequate nutritional support during hospitalization.[3] Nasogastric tube feeding (NG tube feeding), a technique widely utilized to meet the nutritional requirements of patients who are unable to consume food orally, plays a crucial role in preventing malnutrition, particularly in acute and critical care settings.[4] It is a globally established method aimed at maintaining the nutritional and hydration status of critically ill and fluid-restricted individuals.[1]

According to the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), approximately 245,000 hospital stays in the United States involve NG tube feeding, with 15% of these cases concerning children and newborns and the remainder involving adults and the elderly. Additionally, about 30,700 patients were reported to be on home enteral nutrition.[5] Although NG tube feeding is frequently prescribed in adult acute care, there remains a limited understanding of patients’ personal experiences and needs throughout the therapy.[3] Healthcare providers acknowledge that NG tube feeding can be a distressing and unpleasant experience.[6]

Among the most commonly reported and distressing symptoms in NG tube-fed patients is thirst, which is often overlooked and inadequately managed by healthcare professionals.[7] The restriction of oral intake and NG tube feeding is essential for maintaining the nutritional status and hydration, while necessary for clinical reasons, often leads to significant discomforts such as thirst and dry mouth.[8910] These symptoms are poorly acknowledged in clinical care, partly due to limited clinician awareness, lack of systematic assessment tools, and insufficient evidence-based strategies for management. Left unaddressed, thirst can become a distressing symptom comparable to pain in its severity.[10]

A study conducted in South Karnataka highlighted that 49.3% of NG tube-fed patients found the experience highly distressing, with notable issues related to thirst (mean = 2.35) and dry mouth (mean = 2.75).[1] Furthermore, the thirst prevalence among critically ill patients with strict fluid restrictions ranges from 43.8% to 90%, with 69.3% of NG tube-fed and nil-by-mouth (NBM) patients experiencing significant thirst-related distress (mean score ± SD: 5.7 ± 2.5).[10] Similarly, in a study conducted in the Post Anaesthesia Care Unit (PACU), Schittek et al.[11] reported that dry mouth was the most commonly experienced early postoperative discomfort, affecting 35.4% of patients, with pain being the next most frequent complaint at 12.7%. These symptoms were also associated with other oral and pharyngeal discomforts, including dry mouth, bad taste and odor, dryness in the throat, and oral sensitivity, particularly in intensive care settings.[12]

Although prior research has thoroughly documented the physiological and emotional discomforts related to NG tube feeding, limited efforts have been made to develop and validate interventions to alleviate these specific symptoms. Recent literature has emphasized the need for patient-centered strategies. For instance, a systematic review by Cattani et al.[13] stresses the importance of targeted interventions to alleviate NG tube-related discomforts. Similarly, Volkert et al.[14] underscore the value of comprehensive nutritional care in enhancing quality of life among enteral nutrition recipients. Elia et al.[15] further highlight the necessity for clinicians to be adequately equipped to manage the complications and psychological burden of tube feeding.

Emerging evidence supports the use of nonpharmacological approaches, including alternative or distractive techniques, to alleviate thirst in patients under fluid restriction. Preabsorptive satiety methods have shown promise in providing symptomatic relief.[1617] One study demonstrated that a structured thirst bundle comprising a lip moisturizer, peppermint water mouthwash, and vitamin C spray significantly reduced thirst severity scores (3.52 ± 2.03) compared to the control group (5.74 ± 2.54) (P < 0.001). This intervention also led to a notable reduction in the severity of dry mouth symptoms.[18] In postoperative patients, the use of menthol-infused ice and ice popsicles has been identified as an effective method for reducing not only thirst and dry mouth but also unpleasant sensations such as bad taste and oral odor.[19] A systematic review by Garcia et al.[16] further highlights the effectiveness of simple, low-cost strategies such as frozen gauze, ice chips, cold water, and chewing gum in mitigating thirst and oral discomfort. Despite their proven efficacy, the application of these interventions remains limited in clinical practice, largely due to a lack of awareness among healthcare professionals.[16]

Given the high prevalence and intensity of thirst discomfort and dry mouth among NG tube-fed patients and the evident lack of routine clinical practices to address these symptoms, there is a compelling need to investigate practical, simple, cost-effective, and evidence-based strategies for relief. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of an “interventional thirst bundle” in mitigating thirst and dry mouth in patients receiving NG tube feeding. By addressing this underexplored aspect of patient care, the research seeks to improve clinical outcomes and enhance the comfort and overall care experience of individuals dependent on enteral nutrition in acute and critical care settings.

Material and Methods

Study design and setting

The study adopted a quasi-experimental time-series design with experimental and control groups. It was conducted in a 1000-bed tertiary care medical college hospital in a major city of South Karnataka, India, from 2018 to 2020.

Study participants and sampling

Sample size was calculated using a formula based on the estimation of proportion, considering a precision (‘d’) of 10%, an anticipated thirst proportion (‘P’) of 75%, 80% power, and a 95% confidence interval. Based on this required sample size of 45 participants per group, a total of 90 participants meeting the inclusion criteria were selected through convenience sampling and randomly assigned to experimental (n = 45) and control (n = 45) groups using the simple random lottery method.

Inclusion criteria were patients aged 18 years and above receiving NG feeding, capable of verbally reporting thirst intensity and discomfort, with a thirst intensity score greater than 5 and mild to moderate dry mouth. Exclusion criteria included patients on mechanical ventilation or tracheostomy, those unable to follow commands, immediate post-oral surgery patients, and individuals with open sores or desquamation of the mouth and lips, oral cancer, altered coughing/swallowing reflexes, nausea, or vomiting.

Data Collection tools and technique

The tool used in the present study comprised three parts:

Part A included sociodemographic and clinical information such as age, gender, weight, education, duration of NG tube placement, prior NG tube experience, intake, output, clinical diagnosis, and comorbidities. These data were collected using a sociodemographic proforma developed and validated by the researchers.

Part B was the Thirst Discomfort Assessment Scale, developed after an extensive literature review to assess thirst discomfort. It is a participant-reported rating scale with items scored from 0 (no thirst discomfort) to 3 (severe thirst discomfort). Total scores were categorized as 1–8 (mild), 9–16 (moderate), and 17–24 (severe) thirst discomfort. Content validity was established by 11 experts from the fields of medicine, dentistry, and nursing. Pretesting with 21 participants was conducted, and the scale’s reliability was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.79.

Part C was the Challacombe Clinical Oral Dryness Scale, a standardized tool developed by Challacombe et al. and Osailan et al.[2021] to quantify dry mouth severity on a scale of 1 (least severe) to 10 (most severe). Scores were categorized as 1–3 (mild), 4–6 (moderate), and 7–10 (severe) dry mouth. Permission to use the scale was obtained, and the researchers underwent training in its administration. Content validity for use in the current study was confirmed by 11 experts, and pretesting with 21 samples showed strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.84).

Data collection procedure

Participants with a thirst intensity score greater than 5 were included in the study. Preintervention thirst and dry mouth levels were assessed using the Thirst Discomfort Assessment Scale and the Challacombe Clinical Oral Dryness Scale, respectively. Sociodemographic data were collected using a researcher-developed proforma.

The experimental group received an interventional thirst bundle comprising three lip wipes with a wet cold swab, followed by spraying 2 mL of sterile distilled water into the buccal cavity using a metered spray device. The spray targeted the upper and lower gums and roof of the buccal cavity, guided by a dental mirror, and was completed with the application of a lip moisturizer. This intervention was administered three times a day over 2 consecutive days. The control group received routine care as per hospital policy. Post-test assessments were conducted 15 minutes after each intervention.

Ethical consideration

The present study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association, 2013).[22] Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee, and written permission from the hospital was secured prior to the commencement of the study. Informed consent was obtained from all participants after explaining the procedure, ensuring confidentiality, and emphasizing their right to withdraw at any time. Intervention was validated by the experts in the field.

Results

The analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0 software package by a qualified statistician with a significance level set at P < 0.05. The sociodemographic proforma containing selected demographic variables were analyzed using frequency and percentage. The efficacy of the interventional thirst bundle on thirst and dry mouth was assessed using independent sample t-test and repeated-measures ANOVA.

Sociodemographic profile

The majority of participants were under 48 years (55% experimental, 53% control); most were male (55% experimental, 67% control), and 39% were female, with 16 postmenopausal. The median weight was 63 kg, with 51% of the control group above this and the experimental group mostly below. NG tube remained for 5–6 days (experimental) and 3–4 days (control). Intake/output trends were similar. Comorbidities included hypertension and DM (54% experimental) and DM alone (49% control). Most had no hormonal imbalances. χ² test showed both groups were homogeneous (P > 0.05) across all sociodemographic variables [Table 1].

Severity of thirst discomfort among participants

Before the intervention, 56% of the experimental group and 71% of the control group had severe thirst discomfort, with no mild cases in either group. After the intervention, 100% of the experimental group reported only mild thirst discomfort, while the control group remained highly symptomatic, with 60% experiencing severe and 40% moderate thirst discomfort after routine care [Table 2].

Dry mouth among the experimental and control groups before and after the intervention

The preinterventional mean dry mouth score on Day 1 morning was 4.51 ± 0.59 in the experimental group and 3.91 ± 0.42 in the control group. The immediate postinterventional mean dry mouth score on Day 1 morning was 4.29 ± 0.59 for the experimental group and 3.91 ± 0.42 for the control group. However, by Day 2 evening, a substantial reduction was observed in the experimental group, with a mean score of 0.49 ± 0.20, in contrast to the persistent almost similar mean of 4.04 ± 0.45 in the control group [Figure 1].

Effectiveness of Interventional Thirst Bundle on thirst discomfort

An independent t-test was used to assess the effectiveness of the interventional thirst bundle between the experimental and control groups. The baseline mean thirst score was 16.96 ± 2.81, which is almost similar to the control group (16.96 ± 1.85). Comparative analysis of postinterventional mean thirst discomfort scores between the groups across six time points showed a statistically significant reduction. On Day 1 morning after the application of interventional thirst bundle, the experimental group had a mean score of 11.00 ± 1.15, while the control group had a significantly higher mean of 16.69 ± 1.85 (t = 18.379, P < 0.001). In the afternoon, the experimental group’s mean further reduced to 8.82 ± 1.24 versus 18.31 ± 1.47 in the control group (t = 32.948, P < 0.001). By the evening, the experimental group’s mean dropped to 5.91 ± 1.39, compared to 17.62 ± 1.48 in the control group (t = 38.604, P < 0.001).

On Day 2 morning, the experimental group’s score further declined to 4.64 ± 1.59, while the control group remained higher at 16.96 ± 2.53 (t = 27.732, P < 0.001). In the afternoon, the experimental group scored 4.29 ± 1.90, in contrast to 17.42 ± 1.85 in the control group (t = 33.180, P < 0.001). By Day 2 evening, the effect of the intervention was most pronounced, with the experimental group recording a low mean of 2.58 ± 1.03, whereas the control group remained high at 16.40 ± 1.97 (t = 31.561, P < 0.001) [Table 3]. These findings show a significant and progressive reduction in thirst discomfort scores in the experimental group at all time points, supporting the intervention’s efficacy.

Effectiveness of Interventional Thirst Bundle on dry mouth

An independent t-test analysis of postinterventional mean dry mouth scores between the experimental and control groups over 2 consecutive days revealed a significant reduction in symptom severity among participants receiving the interventional thirst bundle. On Day 1 morning after the application of interventional thirst bundle, the experimental group had a mean score of 4.29 ± 0.589, while the control group recorded a slightly lower mean of 3.91 ± 0.417. In the afternoon, no significant difference was observed between the experimental (3.87 ± 0.417) and control (3.80 ± 0.505) groups (t = 0.657, P = 0.513), indicating similar dry mouth severity at that time point.

By Day 1 evening onwards, a significant reduction was noted in the experimental group (2.89 ± 0.647) compared to the control group (4.04 ± 0.208), with the difference being highly significant (t = 11.397, P < 0.001). This downward trend continued on Day 2. In the morning, the experimental group recorded a mean score of 2.76 ± 0.435, significantly lower than the control group’s 4.33 ± 0.477 (t = 16.407, P < 0.001). In the afternoon, the experimental group showed a further reduction to 1.69 ± 0.793, while the control group remained high at 4.36 ± 0.484 (t = 19.260, P < 0.001). The most pronounced difference was observed by Day 2 evening, with the experimental group reporting a mean score of 0.49 ± 0.203, in stark contrast to the control group’s 4.02 ± 0.452 (t = 34.963, P < 0.001) [Table 4].

Discussion

This study evaluated thirst bundle efficacy on thirst discomfort and dry mouth in NG-fed patients. The study demonstrated a significant reduction in thirst discomfort and dry mouth among participants receiving the intervention. The mean baseline thirst discomfort score was identical in both groups (experimental: 16.96 ± 2.81; control: 16.96 ± 1.85). By day 2, the experimental group’s mean score dropped significantly to 2.58 ± 1.13, while the control group showed minimal change (16.4 ± 1.97), yielding a statistically significant difference (t = 31.561, P < 0.001).

Similarly, the mean dry mouth score in the experimental group decreased from 4.51 ± 0.59 to 0.49 ± 0.20. The control group showed 3.91 ± 0.42 at baseline and 4.04 ± 0.45 during day 2 evening postintervention; this marked a highly significant difference (t = 34.963, P < 0.001).

Repeated measures ANOVA confirmed a statistically significant overall effect (P ≤ 0.001), validating the efficacy of the thirst bundle in reducing thirst and dry mouth in nasogastric tube-fed patients.

In a prospective multicenter observational study, Saltnes-Lillegård et al.[23] identified thirst as the most prevalent symptom among ICU patients, affecting 66% on day 1 with the highest mean intensity score of 6.13 (95% CI: 5.7–6.56). Thirst remained the most common (64%) and intense symptom on day 7, with a mean score of 6.05 (95% CI: 5.81–6.3). Findings across multiple studies[162425] emphasize the critical need to address thirst and dry mouth in patients receiving NG feeding. Puntillo et al.,[25] in a single-blinded randomized trial across three ICUs, reported greater reductions in thirst intensity (2.3 NRS points) and thirst distress (1.8 NRS points) in the intervention group compared to usual care (0.6 and 0.4 points, respectively; P < 0.05). Moreover, usual care patients had a 1.9 times higher likelihood of experiencing dry mouth. Garcia et al.,[16] through a systematic review of studies from PubMed, LILACS, and CINAHL, confirmed the efficacy of simple, low-cost interventions like frozen gauze, ice chips, cold water, and chewing gum in relieving thirst and dry mouth.

Supporting these findings, a quasi-experimental study among 60 ICU patients showed significant improvements using an interventional bundle of sterile cold wet oral swabs and ice-cold water sprays administered in two 15-minute sessions. After the first session, thirst intensity in the experimental group dropped to 3.10 ± 0.75 versus 6.40 ± 0.85 in controls (P = 0.001). The second session sustained this effect (3.10 ± 0.75 vs. 6.70 ± 0.59; P = 0.001). Dry mouth scores similarly decreased (0.97 ± 1.24 to 0.37 ± 0.85), while controls remained high (2.60 ± 0.28 to 3.67 ± 0.84).[26] Another RCT involving 61 critically ill patients used vitamin C sprays, peppermint mouthwash, and a lip moisturiser over 3 days. The intervention group showed greater reductions in thirst intensity and oral mucosa discomfort (1.27 and 0.36 points) than controls (0.19 and 0.1 points).[27] Other studies corroborate these results.[28] These results are consistent with current study findings that have highlighted the physiological benefits of cold oral stimulation in triggering salivary flow and reducing xerostomia-related discomfort. Interventions like wet gauze and cold swab can help by increasing saliva production and keeping the oral mucosa moist. The consistent evidence across various studies demonstrates that implementing simple, affordable interventions, such as the interventional thirst bundle, can significantly improve patient comfort and satisfaction by alleviating the thirst and dry mouth in critical care settings. Despite this, the application of these interventions in practice remains limited, highlighting an area for improvement in clinical care protocols.

Limitations and recommendations

A quasi-experimental design was chosen as an RCT was unfeasible during the pilot. Although conducted in a medical college hospital serving multiple districts, data were collected from a single setting, limiting generalizability. Thirst discomfort was self-reported using a researcher-developed scale. Longitudinal studies are needed to assess long-term efficacy and safety. Evaluating the bundle’s effectiveness in other patient populations or specialty units could enhance understanding of its broader applicability.

Conclusion

The interventional thirst bundle, using ice chips, cold water, and swabs, stimulates sensory nerves, relieves thirst, soothes dry mouth, and promotes saliva offering a simple, low-cost, and practical, comforting addition to standard patient care protocols.

Ethical consideration

The present study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association, 2013).[22] Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee, and written permission from the hospital was secured prior to the commencement of the study. Informed consent was obtained from all participants after explaining the procedure, ensuring confidentiality, and emphasizing their right to withdraw at any time. Intervention was validated by the experts in the field.
