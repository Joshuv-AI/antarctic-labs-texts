Adapting enhanced recovery after surgery protocols for day-care surgeries under general anesthesia at a tertiary care ophthalmic setting
Authors: Sujata Rawlani, Priyanka P. Karnik, Shalini Subramanian, Raja Narsingrao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:To evaluate the feasibility and benefits of implementing Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols in elective ophthalmic day-care surgeries conducted under general anesthesia at the tertiary care ophthalmic center.Methods:This prospective observational study included 70 patients aged above 1 month undergoing elective ophthalmic surgery under general anesthesia. Key ERAS elements were adapted, including structured preoperative counselling, optimized fasting, multimodal analgesia, prophylaxis for postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), early oral intake, and use of the Aldrete score for discharge readiness. Pain was assessed using age-appropriate scoring systems like Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability (FLACC) and patient feedback was obtained using a validated questionnaire. Data were analyzed using SPSS.Results:Mean fasting durations were 7.04 ± 1.2 hours for solids and 3.02 ± 1.22 hours for clear liquids. A low-dose opioid regimen combined with paracetamol ensured effective pain control; only a small number of patients required rescue analgesia. The overall incidence of PONV was low (8.6%), and early oral fluid intake was well tolerated. Most participants reported high satisfaction with their perioperative experience, with 91.4% scoring 4 or 5 on a 5-point scale. No adverse events or delayed discharges were observed.Conclusion:Integrating ERAS principles into ophthalmic day-care anesthesia is feasible and associated with improved recovery, reduced discomfort, and high patient satisfaction. A structured, multidisciplinary approach tailored to the ambulatory context can enhance perioperative care without compromising safety.

Introduction

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols are combinations of peri-operative (Pre, Intra, And Post Operative), evidence-based strategies, which work together synergistically, to markedly improve the quality and the speed of recovery after surgery. ERAS adopts a multimodal approach, integrating evidence-based interventions within a single programmed, with collaborative involvement from the surgeon, anesthesiologist, nursing team, and the patient, to enhance the overall perioperative experience. ERAS is now well established across most surgical specialties; however, reports of its structured implementation in day-care settings remain limited. This may reflect variability in awareness and application of perioperative evidence-based practices, which together form the foundation of ERAS protocols. Once considered appropriate only for healthy individuals undergoing minor procedures, advances in surgical techniques and the availability of shorter-acting anesthetic agents have extended its applicability to a broader patient population and more complex operations.[1]

Despite its widespread adoption in major surgical specialties, ERAS protocols remain underutilized in ophthalmic anesthesia, particularly for procedures conducted under general anesthesia. Institutional practices have traditionally prioritized basic perioperative care, with limited focus on the structured recovery pathways. Given the growing volume and increasing complexity of ophthalmic surgeries performed in day-care settings, the integration of ERAS principles offers a timely opportunity to enhance recovery, minimize postoperative complications, and optimize perioperative efficiency. This study aims to highlight the feasibility and clinical applicability of implementing ERAS protocols in elective ophthalmic surgeries conducted under general anesthesia within a day-care setting.

Methods

This prospective observational study was conducted at a tertiary care ophthalmic center to evaluate the adaptation of ERAS protocols for elective ophthalmic day-care surgeries performed under general anesthesia. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (ERR No: SSF/IRB/0004, October 2024). Written informed consent was obtained from all adult participants. For patients under 18 years of age, consents were obtained from parents or legal guardians [Annexure 1], and an assent form was signed by children aged 7–18 years [Annexure 2].[2] Patients over 1 month of age, of either gender, scheduled for elective ophthalmic surgical procedures under general anesthesia for a period of 3 months were included. Patients under 1 month, with a full stomach, undergoing emergency procedures or examination under anesthesia (EUA), or unwilling to participate were excluded. This is because ERAS protocols are designed for elective, structured perioperative care. Neonates would require close post-operative monitoring/NICU care and emergency cases with full stomach would need modification of anesthesia plan at induction and recovery as well as anti-aspiration prophylaxis. Hence it was considered prudent to exclude these cases.

3. Anesthetic Protocol:In the operating room, standard monitors including ECG, pulse oximetry, non-invasive blood pressure, and temperature monitoring were applied. For all adult patients, an intravenous line was secured in the preoperative area and intravenous induction was performed. In children without an in-situ IV line, inhalational induction with sevoflurane was used. Pediatric patients were premedicated with intranasal midazolam (0.3 mg/kg), while in adults, intravenous midazolam (0.05 mg/kg) was administered after securing IV access. The remainder of the anesthetic protocol was kept uniform across both groups: induction with intravenous propofol (2 mg/kg), analgesia with fentanyl (1 µg/kg) at induction (no repeat doses intraoperatively), and neuromuscular blockade with atracurium (0.5 mg/kg). The airway was secured with either a laryngeal mask airway (LMA) or endotracheal tube, depending on surgical requirements. At the end of surgery, neuromuscular blockade was reversed with neostigmine and glycopyrrolate, followed by extubating once patients met standard recovery criteria.4. Multimodal Analgesia:All patients received a low dose opioid in the form of intravenous fentanyl (1 µg/kg) at induction and intravenous paracetamol 15 mg/kg post induction and injection diclofenac 1 mg/kg was reserved for rescue analgesia postoperatively. Oral paracetamol or NSAIDs were prescribed at discharge.5. Regional Anesthesia:Regional anesthesia techniques like peribulbar or subconjunctival blocks were used where indicated and their usage was documented.6. Maintenance of Normothermia:Temperature was monitored throughout the procedure, and normothermia was maintained using radiant warmers for all patients.7. PONV Prophylaxis:Antiemetic prophylaxis was provided through a dual-drug regimen using dexamethasone 0.1 mg/kg after induction (unless contraindicated, e.g., in diabetic patients) and ondansetron 0.1 mg/kg given prior to extubating.8. Intraoperative Fluid Management:Intraoperative fluid management involved administration of ringer lactate 10 mL/kg in the first hour and 2 mL/kg/hour thereafter. Statistical analysis

Data entry was done in MS Excel (version 10.0). Data were summarized and presented systematically in tabular form. Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS software. Variables with parametric distribution described as mean ± SD and standardized analyzed using the Chi-square test and those with non-parametric distribution described as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were described by taking percentages and analyzed using the Chi-square test. P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The final analysis included all 70 patients, comprising 39 males (55.7%) and 31 females (44.3%). The median age of the study population was 15 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 7.75–27), with patients ranging from 5 months to 76 years. Discussion

Day-care surgery is a growing model of healthcare delivery in developing countries, especially with the expansion of the private sector and increasing surgical volumes. While cataract surgeries rank among the most performed day-care procedures, development of subspecialties within ophthalmology, the demand for anesthesiologists with focused expertise.[56] These surgeries, although suitable for the day-care model, demand careful peri-operative planning to ensure early recovery and same-day discharge without compromising patient safety. By integrating multimodal, evidence-based perioperative strategies, ERAS supports faster physiological recovery and better overall patient outcomes.[7]

The concept of ERAS originated from the “fast-track” surgical pathways introduced over two decades ago with the goal of reducing surgical stress and expediting postoperative recovery. The term “fast-track surgery” was first introduced in the 1990s by Henrik Kehlet, a Danish colorectal surgeon, and was initially embraced in ambulatory surgical settings.[8] These early frameworks laid the foundation for what are now established as ERAS protocols. Although the present study selectively applied ERAS principles suited to the day-care ophthalmic context, a key strength lies in its integration of these elements across every phase of perioperative care.

Optimization of preoperative fasting is one such element that holds particular importance in ambulatory settings. In our study, while most patients adhered to institutional fasting guidelines, we observed a tendency toward excessive fasting for solids in a subset of patients. A deviation of up to 1 hour from the recommended fasting duration was considered acceptable; however, beyond this threshold, non-compliance was noted. This pattern is likely attributable to the inherent limitations of a day-care surgical setup, where patients are not admitted to the hospital a day prior to surgery, making real-time monitoring and reinforcement of fasting instructions more challenging. In contrast, an audit conducted by Rawlani et al.[9] at a tertiary care pediatric hospital demonstrated that with structured in-hospital interventions like counselling of caregivers, training nursing staff, and active involvement of surgeons showed improved fasting compliance significantly in children admitted the day prior to surgery. The findings from that study highlight the value of in-ward vigilance and multidisciplinary engagement, which may not be fully replicable in purely ambulatory care models such as ours.

Beyond fasting, preoperative counselling itself plays a pivotal role. It not only reduces anxiety but also helps patients set realistic expectations about surgery, understand anesthetic techniques, and become informed about postoperative recovery milestones. In a study involving 104 patients undergoing ambulatory surgery, Pereira et al.[10] demonstrated that comprehensive preoperative counselling led to a measurable reduction in patient anxiety and significantly enhanced satisfaction levels.

The increasing variability of ophthalmic procedures underscores the importance of tailoring anesthetic management to the type and duration of surgery. In our study, surgical durations ranged from shorter procedures such as pediatric cataracts, typically lasting around 35 minutes, to more prolonged interventions like mucous membrane grafting, which can extend up to 2 hours. The median surgical time across all cases was 51.5 min. This heterogeneity necessitates a thoughtful anesthesia plan, as not all ophthalmic procedures carry the same analgesic demands. For instance, while cataract surgery may involve minimal postoperative discomfort, procedures such as endonasal dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR) and mucous membrane grafts are inherently more painful and can impact early recovery and discharge readiness in a day-care setting.

To address this, the application of a multimodal analgesic strategy becomes essential within an ERAS framework. In our study, a low-dose opioid (fentanyl 1 µg/kg) was administered at induction for all patients, supplemented with intravenous paracetamol (15 mg/kg). Regional anesthesia techniques were also employed where appropriate. In a large retrospective analysis by Vuong et al.,[11] involving 1,689 patients undergoing mastectomy, the implementation of a multimodal analgesia protocol significantly increased the likelihood of successful same-day discharge. These findings highlight the value of balanced, multi-agent pain management that we successfully integrated even in the context of ambulatory ophthalmic surgery. Given that most ophthalmic procedures in our study were of moderate duration and not extensively invasive, postoperative pain was generally well controlled. In a small subset of patients in our study where VAS scores exceeded 7, additional analgesia in the form of intravenous diclofenac (1 mg/kg) was administered as per our rescue analgesic protocol. The patients were prescribed oral analgesics (NSAID’S and paracetamol) at discharge. The overall low pain burden and limited need for rescue medication further validate the effectiveness of the multimodal analgesic regimen employed in this study.

Patients receiving general anesthesia are prone to hypothermia (core temperature <36°C) due to impaired thermoregulation caused by anesthetic agents and exposure to cool operating room conditions. This risk persists even during brief procedures, such as those conducted in ambulatory settings.[12] Intraoperative temperature fluctuations both hypothermia and hyperthermia can delay discharge and negatively impact patient satisfaction.[1314] In our study, temperature was routinely monitored, and measures were undertaken to maintain normothermia and prevent either extreme.

Intraoperative fluid management is another crucial component of the ERAS pathway. The goal is to maintain euvolemia, as both under- and overhydration are associated with adverse outcomes such as nausea, vomiting, dizziness, and delayed recovery.[15] Considering the minimal blood loss and negligible third-space losses typically associated with ophthalmic procedures, we opted for a conservative approach that proved sufficient for maintaining fluid balance without compromising recovery.

PONV remains one of the most common and distressing complications following ambulatory anesthesia, particularly in ophthalmic patients and in the pediatric age group. In our study, the overall incidence of PONV was low, occurring in only 8.6% of patients. This favorable outcome may be attributed to several key components of our anesthetic protocol. Notably, the use of propofol for induction has been shown to reduce the incidence of PONV compared to volatile agents and facilitates smoother emergence from anesthesia, thereby accelerating recovery in day-care settings.[1617] In addition, a dual-agent antiemetic prophylaxis regimen was employed, consisting of intravenous dexamethasone (0.1 mg/kg) administered after induction and ondansetron (0.1 mg/kg) given at extubating. Dexamethasone is a potent glucocorticoid, possesses both anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties.[1819] Intravenous doses of 4–10 mg are recommended by ERAS guidelines for PONV prophylaxis, with higher doses (8–10 mg) also contributing to reduced postoperative pain and opioid requirements.[2021] Our findings support existing evidence that early and appropriate prophylaxis can significantly reduce postoperative discomfort, enable timely initiation of oral intake, and contribute to faster discharge in day-care surgical settings.

In the general surgical population, early initiation of oral intake has been associated with a reduction in postoperative complications and shorter hospital stays.[22] In alignment with this principle, our study encouraged the intake of clear liquids immediately after recovery from general anesthesia, once patients were fully conscious and able to maintain their airway reflexes independently. This approach is supported by evidence from a systematic review and meta-analysis by Kang et al.,[23] which concluded that early oral hydration (EOH) in the PACU not only contributed to a reduction in PONV but also improved patient satisfaction, supporting its incorporation into enhanced recovery strategies where clinically appropriate.

Patient satisfaction is a key quality indicator in ambulatory surgical care and it is closely linked to the efficiency of recovery and discharge processes. In such settings, cost-effectiveness is also a critical consideration. Delayed emergence from anesthesia can contribute to increased postoperative morbidity, operating room inefficiencies, and elevated healthcare costs. The application of structured recovery scoring systems such as the Aldrete score and its modifications has long been used to assess discharge readiness and optimize workflow in outpatient surgery.[4] These tools, however, may be influenced by variables such as patient age, gender, socioeconomic background, and the nature of the surgical procedure.

The overarching goal of ERAS protocols is to promote early physiological recovery, minimize perioperative stress, and ultimately enhance the overall patient experience. In our study, the implementation of ERAS principles in 70 patients undergoing ophthalmic surgeries under general anesthesia resulted in notably positive feedback. These findings reflect a high level of acceptance and comfort with the perioperative care pathway. Ensuring the long-term success of ERAS in ambulatory settings requires continuous auditing, high compliance with protocol elements and iterative improvements based on outcome monitoring. When consistently applied, ERAS can significantly improve patient outcomes, reduce variability in care, and drive sustainable advancements in perioperative practice.[5]

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that adapting evidence-based perioperative ERAS principles in a day-care ophthalmic surgical setting is both feasible and beneficial. By selectively implementing key ERAS components across all perioperative phases, we were able to facilitate faster recovery, minimize postoperative discomfort, and enhance the overall patient experience without compromising safety. Sustained success, however, depends on continued auditing, multidisciplinary engagement, and protocol adherence tailored to the ambulatory context.
