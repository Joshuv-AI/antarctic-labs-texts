The implementation gap: how Cuba's tuberculosis setback exposes a universal challenge for low-burden countries
Authors: Alina Martínez-Rodríguez, Alexander González-Díaz, María de Los Angeles León Venero, Carilda Peña García, María Eugenia Toledo Romaní
Published: 2026

The pursuit of tuberculosis (TB) elimination is often depicted as a final sprint. In 2024, 62 countries reported a low TB incidence (<10 cases per 100,000 population).1 This commentary addresses a crucial challenge: the inherent fragility of progress in these settings, where hard-won gains can prove unexpectedly vulnerable to reversal.

Cuba's recent experience demonstrates that even exemplary TB control does not guarantee a linear path. The nation's sustained high detection and cure rates,2 alongside low paediatric TB mortality,3 signal a historically strong surveillance programme. Yet these achievements masked a significant vulnerability: a persistent pre-pandemic diagnostic gap of 1.8–2.5 cases per 100,000 between modelled burden and notified cases.4 This gap represented a reservoir of undiagnosed transmission. The COVID-19 pandemic acted as a catalytic shock, exacerbating systemic weaknesses and triggering a resurgence.2

The consequence was a non-linear reversal. After declining from a peak incidence of 9.8 per 100,000 in 2010 to a low of 5.4 in 2021, WHO-estimated incidence surged to 7.7 in 2022 and reached 11.4 by 2024, exceeding the 2010 baseline.4 This reflects sustained transmission, with estimated TB deaths rising from 61 in 2021 to 98 in 2024.4 This resurgence unfolds within a complex socioeconomic context, threatened by an intensifying blockade, climate stressors, and a syndemic epidemiologic scenario.5

The Cuban case is a powerful exemplar, not an anomaly. It reveals a pattern of fragility that could be replicated among low-incidence countries, where pre-existing systemic weaknesses—such as unaddressed reservoirs of latent TB infection (LTBI)—interact with external shocks. Nations including Chile, Costa Rica,6 and specific Western European regions7 have experienced similar, parallel setbacks since 2020. These reversals underscore that the baseline strength of a TB programme is defined not by past success, but by its adaptive capacity and reach into vulnerable populations.

Moreover, socio-epidemiologic context dictates the primary threat. While Cuba historically benefited from low TB-HIV coinfection and limited drug resistance,4 countries such as Canada and the United States must manage challenges related to TB importation and large LTBI pools among foreign-born populations.8 This contrast affirms that “low incidence” is not a homogenous category, and the specific drivers of fragility must be diagnosed locally.

Unravelling these complexities requires a two-tiered research response. First, operational research is essential to identify the “what” and “where”,9 using subnational data to locate undiagnosed infections, map transmission hotspots, and evaluate targeted interventions like LTBI screening.

Yet knowing “what” works is insufficient. The Cuban example demonstrates the critical need for implementation research (IR)—the science of how to make interventions work in real-world, resource-constrained settings.10 IR allows for the diagnosis of local bottlenecks, engages communities, tests adaptive strategies, and measures fidelity during scale-up.

Through IR within Cuba's TB control program, an obstacle was identified among primary care physicians: deficiencies in knowledge related to LTBI diagnostic tests.11 This finding highlights a broader challenge in low-burden settings, where maintaining clinical competency requires sustained effort as part of a collaborative strategy between research and the program. Information and communication technologies offer an urgent, scalable solution for ongoing professional training. Furthermore, analysis reveals that TB/HIV coinfection significantly impacts mortality,12 with a high case fatality rate continuing to negatively affect overall trends.4

Therefore, Cuba's experience should be interpreted not as a unique failure, but as a cautionary signal for all low-burden settings. As the world recommits to the End TB Strategy amid funding challenges, the lesson that remains is that elimination is neither a steady descent nor a static endpoint. It is a dynamic, research-informed endeavour that demands vigilance against complacency, sustained investment, and a willingness to interrogate data beyond headline incidence figures. The asynchronous trends of incidence and mortality, alongside hidden infection reservoirs in Cuba and elsewhere, confirm that technical knowledge alone is insufficient. Progress is jeopardised by a deficit in the applied science of delivery.

Ultimately, the resurgence of TB indicates that the era of passive control is insufficient for the active pursuit of elimination. The requisite new paradigm is one where every low-incidence country is equipped to conduct its own IR—transforming local challenges into evidence for local solutions. Therefore, the sustainability of elimination will depend less on disease metrics and more on the robustness of the learning systems established to maintain it. Our task is not only to reach the goal, but to lay the foundations that make it permanent.
