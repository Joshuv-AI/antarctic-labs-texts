Neonatal osteogenesis imperfecta revealed by antenatal fractures: A case report
Authors: Chaymaa EL Messari, Anass Ayad, Mohamed Sellouti, Salahiddine Saghir, Rachid Abilkassem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteogenesis imperfecta (OI) is a rare genetic disorder of connective tissue, primarily caused by mutations in the COL1A1 and COL1A2 genes encoding type I collagen. We describe a male neonate diagnosed with OI after presenting with multiple antenatal and postnatal fractures. Prenatal ultrasound revealed intrauterine growth restriction and long-bone deformities. Postnatal clinical and radiological evaluations demonstrated diffuse osteopenia and multiple diaphyseal fractures. Genetic analysis identified a heterozygous COL1A2 (p.Gly358Ser) mutation consistent with type II OI. The patient was treated with intravenous zoledronic acid and showed was well tolerated. This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges associated with severe neonatal OI. Early recognition, genetic confirmation, and multidisciplinary management are essential to improving survival and quality of life. Novel approaches including anti-sclerostin antibodies, TGF-β inhibitors, and emerging gene-editing therapies offer promising perspectives for the future management of this condition.Keywords: Osteogenesis imperfecta, Antenatal fractures, Bisphosphonates, Zoledronic acid, Neonatal bone fragility

Introduction

Osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) is a rare heritable connective tissue disorder characterized by defective synthesis of type I collagen, resulting mainly from mutations in COL1A1 and COL1A2 [1]. Its incidence is estimated at 1 in 15,000 to 20,000 live births [2].

Clinically, OI is characterized by bone fragility, recurrent fractures, deformities, and growth retardation. Extra-skeletal manifestations may include dentinogenesis imperfecta, blue sclerae, hearing loss, and cardiopulmonary involvement [3].

The clinical spectrum ranges from mild forms to severe perinatal presentations. Antenatal detection of limb shortening, deformities, or fractures should raise suspicion of severe OI or other skeletal dysplasias.

The objective of this report is to highlight the diagnostic value of prenatal and postnatal imaging in osteogenesis imperfecta and to emphasize its contribution to early management and differential diagnosis.

Case presentation

A male neonate was delivered at term (39 weeks’ gestation) with Apgar scores of 9/10/10. Birth weight was 2550 g (1.5th percentile), length 42 cm (<1st percentile), and head circumference 34 cm (29th percentile). Amniotic fluid was clear, and there was no evidence of maternal infection. The 32-year-old mother (G7P4) had a complex obstetric history: two healthy children (G1-G2), one intrauterine fetal death at seven months due to anhydramnios (G3), one term delivery complicated by gestational diabetes (G4), and two spontaneous miscarriages (G5-G6).

The current pregnancy was complicated by intrauterine growth restriction and lower limb deformities, with femoral length at the third percentile at 27 weeks and overall fetal growth at the first percentile by 37 weeks (Fig. 1). The mother had a history of postpartum stroke after her third delivery, previously treated with anticoagulants. There was no consanguinity.

Prenatal ultrasound showing reduced femoral length and curved lower limbs.

At birth, the neonate was peripherally hypotonic but reactive. He exhibited intense pain on limb manipulation, with audible crepitus over the long bones of the left upper and lower extremities. The limbs were flaccid and tender to touch. The skull was soft with widened cranial sutures. Other systemic findings were unremarkable (Fig. 2).

Phenotypic features: shortened, deformed lower limbs and blue sclerae.

Radiographs revealed multiple non-displaced fractures of the upper third of both humeral diaphyses and the mid-third of the femoral shafts (Fig. 3). Cranio-cervical CT demonstrated turribrachycephaly (cranial index 130), severe osteopenia with extreme thinning of calvarial bones, widened sutures, and diffuse bone erosions. The odontoid process was poorly visualized, suggesting delayed ossification or partial agenesis. No craniosynostosis or intracranial abnormality was noted. Echocardiography revealed a small membranous ventricular septal defect.

Standard X-ray showing multiple diaphyseal fractures of the humeri and femora.

Laboratory findings were as follows: calcium 87 mg/L (normal range: 85-105 mg/L), phosphorus 53 mg/L (normal range: 45-65 mg/L), alkaline phosphatase 143 IU/L (normal range: 100-350 IU/L), and parathyroid hormone 24 pg/mL (normal range: 10-65 pg/mL). Genetic testing confirmed a COL1A2 (p.Gly358Ser) mutation, consistent with type II OI.

The patient initially received analgesic treatment with paracetamol, but due to persistently elevated EDIN (Neonatal Pain and Discomfort Scale) score, continuous pain management was escalated to intermittent morphine infusion. Morphine was administered under close monitoring, with progressive tapering as the patient’s pain improved. No withdrawal symptoms were observed during or after weaning.

The patient received calcium and vitamin D supplementation and was treated with intravenous zoledronic acid (0.05 mg every six months, first dose at day 42 of life). The treatment was well tolerated; transient post-infusion fever occurred without hypocalcemia.

Short-term follow-up showed no new fractures. An orthopedic intervention is planned to address the previously healed fractures and residual deformities. At the time of reporting, no analgesic medication was required, and the infant remained clinically stable.

Discussion

Osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) is a genetically heterogeneous disorder characterized by increased bone fragility resulting from quantitative or qualitative defects in type I collagen, most commonly due to mutations in the COL1A1 and COL1A2 genes [1]. The clinical spectrum is broad, ranging from mild forms to severe perinatal presentations, historically considered lethal in type II OI [2,3]. Prenatal imaging plays a crucial role in the early detection of severe forms of OI. Ultrasound findings such as limb shortening, bowing of long bones, intrauterine fractures, and decreased mineralization are highly suggestive but not pathognomonic. These abnormalities can be detected as early as the second trimester and are frequently associated with intrauterine growth restriction. However, ultrasound alone may not always allow precise characterization of skeletal dysplasias [10].

Fetal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can provide complementary information, particularly in evaluating skeletal abnormalities, thoracic volume, and lung development, which are essential for predicting postnatal respiratory outcomes. This multimodal imaging approach enhances diagnostic accuracy and helps guide perinatal management.

A major diagnostic challenge lies in differentiating OI from other skeletal dysplasias. Hypophosphatasia is characterized by defective bone mineralization associated with low serum alkaline phosphatase levels. Campomelic dysplasia typically presents with bowing of long bones, especially the lower limbs, along with characteristic scapular and pelvic anomalies. Thanatophoric dysplasia is distinguished by extreme micromelia, a narrow thorax, and characteristic shaped femurs.

In our case, the presence of multiple antenatal and postnatal fractures, diffuse osteopenia on radiographs, normal biochemical parameters, and identification of a pathogenic COL1A2 mutation strongly supported the diagnosis of osteogenesis imperfecta.

Although the identified mutation is typically associated with type II OI, the patient’s survival and relatively stable clinical course suggest a severe but non-lethal phenotype, closer to type III OI. This finding underscores the limitations of the traditional Sillence classification and highlights the complexity of genotype–phenotype correlations [[3], [4], [5]].

Postnatal imaging, including radiographs and computed tomography (CT), plays a key role in confirming the diagnosis and assessing disease severity. Radiographs typically reveal diffuse osteopenia, multiple fractures at different stages of healing, and bone deformities. CT imaging, particularly with three-dimensional reconstruction, provides detailed evaluation of cranial abnormalities such as turribrachycephaly, widened sutures, and calvarial thinning, as observed in our patient. These findings are valuable for both diagnosis and follow-up.

Recent studies have emphasized that early prenatal diagnosis allows better anticipation of neonatal complications and facilitates delivery planning in specialized centers. Timila et al. [6] reported improved outcomes in infants managed with an early multidisciplinary approach. Similarly, Carroll et al. [5] demonstrated that a significant proportion of infants with a prenatal diagnosis of OI survive beyond the neonatal period, challenging the traditional perception of universally lethal forms.

The management of neonatal OI requires a multidisciplinary approach involving neonatologists, radiologists, geneticists, and orthopedic specialists. Pain control is a major component of care, as neonates experience significant discomfort due to fractures. The EDIN (Neonatal Pain and Discomfort Scale) is a useful tool for assessing prolonged pain and guiding analgesic management. In our patient, analgesia required escalation to morphine, which was progressively tapered without withdrawal symptoms.

Bisphosphonates represent the cornerstone of medical treatment in moderate-to-severe OI. These agents inhibit osteoclast-mediated bone resorption, leading to increased bone mineral density and reduced fracture incidence. Glorieux et al. [7] first demonstrated the efficacy of intravenous pamidronate in improving clinical outcomes in children with OI. More recently, zoledronic acid has emerged as an effective alternative due to its higher potency and convenient dosing schedule [6,8].

However, it is important to emphasize that bisphosphonates do not correct the underlying genetic defect. Potential long-term adverse effects include delayed fracture healing, oversuppression of bone turnover, and rare cases of osteonecrosis. Therefore, careful monitoring is required during long-term treatment.

Despite therapeutic advances, OI remains a chronic condition associated with significant morbidity, including growth retardation, skeletal deformities, and recurrent fractures. Long-term prognosis depends on disease severity and the quality of multidisciplinary care.

Emerging therapies offer promising perspectives. Novel approaches such as anti-sclerostin antibodies, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) inhibitors, mesenchymal stem cell therapy, and gene-editing techniques are currently under investigation and may provide disease-modifying or curative options in the future [9].

In summary, this case underscores the pivotal role of prenatal and postnatal imaging in the diagnosis of osteogenesis imperfecta and highlights the importance of integrating radiological, clinical, and genetic data. Early diagnosis and timely multidisciplinary management can significantly improve outcomes, even in severe forms traditionally considered lethal. These findings reinforce the importance of early multidisciplinary management in improving survival, even in forms historically considered lethal.

Conclusion

This case emphasizes the key role of imaging in the early diagnosis of osteogenesis imperfecta, particularly in the prenatal setting. It also highlights the importance of correlating radiological findings with genetic and clinical data to establish an accurate diagnosis and guide management. Although bisphosphonates improve bone density and reduce fractures, they do not cure the disease. Emerging therapies may offer future disease-modifying strategies.
