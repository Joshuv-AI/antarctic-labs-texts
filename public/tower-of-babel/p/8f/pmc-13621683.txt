A one-week automated genome-wide optical pooled screen using OttoSeq
Authors: Bryce Kirby, Matteo Di Bernardo, Iain M. Cheeseman, Paul C. Blainey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Optical pooled screens (OPS) are bottlenecked by labor-intensive in situ sequencing and analysis protocols. Here, we present OttoSeq, an automated OPS platform combining the Otto2 fluid handling system with the Brieflow analysis pipeline. We utilize OttoSeq to complete a genome-wide cell painting screen in eight days, sampling 5,198,240 high-quality cells across 21,732 gene knockout perturbations (224 cells per gene) and interpreting 320 functional gene clusters.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-026-04172-1.

Background

Optical pooled screening (OPS) enables microscopy-based high-dimensional, single-cell phenotyping of CRISPR perturbation libraries. By reading out genetic barcodes through in situ sequencing by synthesis directly in fixed cells, OPS captures spatial and morphological information with high fidelity [1–3] and offers a scalable alternative to next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based approaches such as Perturb-seq [4–6]. However, two bottlenecks have restricted OPS to specialist laboratories. First, in situ sequencing requires 1–2 h of manual fluid handling per plate-base cycle, demanding weeks of expert hands-on labor. Second, the computational scripts most commonly used to process OPS data generalize to only a limited extent and require project-specific customization by experts [1–3, 7]. While an integrated analysis solution has been recently reported [8], it has not yet been benchmarked for rapid turnaround. Production and primary readout of an optical pooled screen at genome-wide scale has thus required many months of hands-on labor and analysis. Here we present OttoSeq, an integrated system that addresses both bottlenecks by pairing the Otto2 on-microscope automated sequencing system with Brieflow [8], a modular open-source analysis pipeline (Fig. 1a). To benchmark OPS throughput with OttoSeq, we completed a genome-wide cell painting screen in only eight days during which we generated cell painting data (20 × magnification), amplified barcodes, and performed both the automated Otto2 in situ sequencing readout and automated Brieflow data processing including MozzareLLM AI interpretation (Fig. 1b).Fig. 1Otto2: an automated on-microscope in situ sequencing system. a Schematic summary of the OttoSeq workflow. Results and discussion

To address the challenges posed by existing protocols calling for 10 + cycles of labor-intensive in situ sequencing (Fig. 1c), we constructed Otto2, a previously unreported, minimal-cost, high-fidelity positive displacement pump system designed specifically for rapid automated in situ sequencing in the six-well plate format (Fig. 1d, e). The system was designed to replicate manual in situ sequencing operations within a timeline comparable to human-operated benchmarks and produce base qualities sufficient for demultiplexing large-scale screens. Otto2 performs semi-parallelized dispensing and aspiration operations lasting seconds, arranged in optimized sequences addressed to individual wells. A single automated in situ cycle replaces 1 h and 30 min of manual operations per plate specified in previously reported protocols with < 10 min of optional inspections and reagent replacement [3, 7]. Otto2 is an on-microscope sequencer: tubing from the system hardware is threaded into a custom heating box integrated with the Cephla Squid + fluorescence microscope [9], holding plates at 45 °C for chemistry operations except for a final post-incorporation wash and addition of imaging buffer, which occur in each cycle at room temperature. To operate OttoSeq, the user loads a 3D-printed manifold into the wells of a six-well plate of cultured cells, positions it on the microscope, and secures dispensing and aspiration lines (Fig. 1g). Thus loaded, the plate is secure for an arbitrary number of sequencing and imaging cycles. By eliminating per-cycle manual fluid handling and by operating with the plate remaining on the microscope stage, Otto2 enables near-unattended and continuous operation.

Each plate was imaged at 20 × magnification in four spectral channels (Fig. 1f) for cell painting—Hoechst (DNA), COX4 (mitochondria), AGP (actin and Golgi), and Concanavalin A (endoplasmic reticulum)—an OPS-optimized variation of the original cell painting panel [10]. Following cell painting and barcode amplification (Methods), we loaded each of the two screening plates for 12 cycles of in situ sequencing using Otto2 at 4 × magnification (Fig. 1f). During each cycle, following 10 min of setup and quality control, the Otto2 system ran autonomously for ~ 2.5 h including imaging; these 10 min of hands-on time per cycle do not significantly interrupt continuous operation or add to runtime, and encompass reloading the system with fresh reagents, emptying waste as needed, and observing the first several steps of the automated operation to validate system performance. For more information, see Methods.

Performing analysis quickly enough to match the speed of automated data generation required an adaptable analysis pipeline. Brieflow, a modular open-source pipeline for OPS data, processed raw images [8]. Brieflow's modular architecture was essential to achieving the 8-day overall turnaround: because individual processing steps—segmentation, feature extraction, barcode calling, spatial merging, clustering—are implemented as independent, swappable modules, improvements could be deployed without modifying upstream or downstream steps. The genome-wide screen required on-the-fly adaptations to two modules during the screen: enhanced SBS cycle alignment to accommodate unexpectedly large inter-cycle plate position offsets, and a filtered analysis arm designed to efficiently subcluster on only bootstrap-significant perturbations given the scale of the library (Methods). These on-the-fly updates allowed the analysis pipeline to keep pace with automated hardware–even in the face of the unexpectedly large plate position offsets encountered in this run. Bug fixes and performance improvements (Additional file 1: Table S1) were merged back into the main codebase to improve the robustness of the pipeline for future users. Brieflow processed 13,210,438 barcoded cells and 15,647,674 phenotyped cells across two six-well plates, extracting 1,670 morphological features from each phenotyped cell. After spatial merging, deduplication, and quality filtering, 5,198,240 mapped cells were retained across 21,732 perturbations with a median of 224 cells per gene (Fig. 2a–c, Additional file 1: Table S2). A final challenge was biological interpretation, which typically requires weeks to months of manual literature curation. We applied MozzareLLM [8], an LLM-driven tool that generates biological summaries from perturbation cluster data within hours. Bootstrap significance testing identified 3,715 perturbations with significant morphological effects, which were organized into 320 clusters (Fig. 2d, Additional file 1: Table S3). MozzareLLM then assessed each cluster for biological coherence, assigning pathway confidence tiers—27 high, 168 medium, and 125 low—based on the convergence of known gene functions within each cluster. The 610 genes in high-confidence clusters (75% essential in HeLa) comprise three categories: 393 with established roles consistent with their cluster's dominant process, biologically validating the clustering; 152 whose morphological profiles place them in unexpected functional contexts, representing phenotypic associations warranting independent experimental validation; and 65 less-studied genes for which cluster assignment serves as a hypothesis for functional classification (Additional file 1: Table S4). Clusters mapping to ubiquitin–proteasome degradation (Cluster 6, Fig. 2e) and RNA Pol II transcription (Cluster 8, Fig. 2f) recover recently assigned functions of genes such as AKIRIN2, mediating nuclear proteasome import [11], and UBE3A, an Angelman syndrome-associated E3 ligase with known transcriptional regulatory roles [12] clustering with core Pol II machinery. A select few perturbations in these highlighted clusters (e.g. BBIP1, BRSK2, RELB, PLPP4, RHOV, UBE3A) were absent from previous HeLa essential gene CRISPR libraries [3], making them promising subjects for further investigation (Additional file 1: Tables S3, S4).

Conclusions

Although OPS offers high-content screening of genome-wide CRISPR libraries, it has previously been bottlenecked by library demultiplexing and analysis, with screens routinely requiring many weeks of data generation and months of analysis. Our screen required only eight days with two researchers and consisted largely of hands-off automated runtime, drastically reducing labor costs and user burden without increasing consumable costs (Additional file 1: Table S5). Several considerations remain that limit the presented technology as a sequencing system: on-scope sequencing presents difficulties unique to fluidic-imaging integration—including plate position offsets caused by tension on attached tubing tugging the plate during automated imaging operations involving microscope stage movement, material compatibility with epifluorescence imaging, and parallelization limited to one plate per microscope. Per-cycle Otto2 runtime is also ~ 50% longer than manual cycling. This longer runtime encompasses the time necessary to conduct sequential single-well fluidics operations and could be shortened by configuring fluidics systems that parallelize operations across wells. Such parallelization could present exciting opportunities; while the current 8-day timeline reflects continuous semi-unattended operation rather than faster per-cycle protocols, future systems that parallelize fluid handling across wells or temporally overlap chemistry with imaging could push step times below those of manual operation. On the computational side, further retooling of Brieflow to support additional configurations (e.g. 2 color chemistry) and to improve runtime are ongoing. Automated in situ sequencing with OttoSeq unlocks previously unattainable speed and throughput for high-content perturbation screening and suggests the possibility of routine future access to high-speed, low-cost perturbation screening for cell biologists, computational biologists, and biomedical researchers.

Methods

Library and transduction

The genome-wide CROPseq-multi CRISPR-KO library targeted 20,108 true knockout targets (union of GENCODE, RefSeq, and CHESS annotations along with 1,624 controls, see below) with 41,906 dual-guide constructs in the CROPseq-multi-puro-v2 vector [13]. Each gene was targeted by two dual-guide constructs carrying four unique CRISPick-selected guides paired by pick-sum ordering (guides 1 + 4 and 2 + 3) with random position assignment [14–16]. Controls comprised 600 intergenic-targeting constructs (paired guides targeting the same chromosome, 100–10,000 bp apart, with comparable on-target efficacy and specificity scores to gene-targeting guides), 200 nontargeting constructs, and 824 constructs targeting 412 olfactory receptor genes (which are not expressed in HeLa cells but provide cutting controls at gene-body loci). Each construct carried a pair of iBARs with a guaranteed Levenshtein edit distance of 3 across 12 sequencing cycles, enabling error-corrected barcode calling; observation of either iBAR alone is sufficient to identify a construct. Lentivirus was produced by transfecting HEK-293 T cells seeded at 15 × 10⁶ cells in a T175 cm2 flask in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin 30 h pre-transfection. Media was changed to 20 mL virus production medium (IMDM supplemented with 20% heat-inactivated FBS) 7–8 h pre-transfection. Cells were transfected with 20 µg plasmid library, 3.62 µg pCMV-VSV-G, 8.28 µg psPAX2, and 95.8 µL FuGENE 4 K in Opti-MEM to a final volume of 2 mL. Media was changed to 55 mL fresh virus production medium 16–17 h post-transfection. Viral supernatant was harvested at 48 h post-transfection, 25 mL fresh medium was added, and a second harvest was collected at 72 h. Supernatant was 0.45 µm filtered and stored at − 80 °C. For the screen, HeLa-TetR-Cas9 (A7) cells were transduced with the sgRNA library in CROPseq-multi-v2-Puro and selected with 2 μg/mL puromycin (Sigma Aldrich P45-12) for 4 days. A7 HeLa cells have been characterized previously [17] but were not further authenticated or tested for mycoplasma contamination during the screen timeline.

Cell culture

Media comprised DMEM GlutaMAX (Gibco 10,569,010) with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (MilliporeSigma F4135), 100 U/mL Penicillin, and 100 μg/mL Streptomycin (Gibco 15,140,122). Cells were thawed, added dropwise to warmed media, spun down at 400 g × 5 min, resuspended, and seeded at half-confluency into T225 cm2 culture flasks (Fisher Scientific 159,934) at scale sufficient for 1000 × gene-perturbation representation. Following a 48-h incubation, an exchange for media containing 2ug/mL doxycycline was performed to induce Cas9 activity. Following a 24-h incubation, cells were washed once with 1X PBS, passaged with TrypLE (Thermo Fisher Scientific 12,604,013), and seeded in two six-well plates (P06-1.5H-N) at a density of 275 K cells per well in 2 mL of media containing 2ug/mL doxycycline. Following a 56-h incubation (for 80 h total of doxycycline exposure), plates were washed once with 1X PBS, fixed in 4% formaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences 15,714) in 1X PBS (Ambion AM9625) for 30 min at room temperature, and washed three times with 1X PBS.

Cell painting

Plates were incubated with cell painting mix containing 1:5000 Hoechst (Thermo Fisher Scientific 62,249), 1:250 COX4 (ProteinTech CL488-11,242), 1:1000 Phalloidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific A34055), 1:7500 WGA (Thermo Fisher Scientific W32464), and 1:500 Concanavalin A (Thermo Fisher Scientific C21421) in 2.5% BSA (LCG 1900–0016) and 0.5% Tween-20 (VWR 100216–360) in 1X PBS (Ambion AM9625) for 45 min at room temperature and washed three times with 0.05% Tween-20 in 1X PBS. Imaging setup consisted of a Cephla Squid + microscope with a 5 line laser engine (405 nm, 470 nm, 550 nm, 640 nm, 730 nm), a motorized filter wheel, 850 nm laser autofocus and a camera with Sony IMX571 sensor (3.76 um pixel size, 4168 × 4168 square FOV). Excitation filter was Chroma ZET405/470/555/640/730 and dichroic was Semrock FF421/491/567/659/776-Di01. Plates were imaged in 4 channels per field of view (Hoechst: 405 nm excitation with Semrock FF01-441/511/593/684/817 emission filter, henceforth multiband emission filter, COX4: 470 nm excitation with Chroma ET525/50 m emission filter, AGP: 550 nm excitation with multiband emission filter, Concanavalin A: 640 nm excitation with multiband emission filter) using a 20 × objective (Olympus Plan Apo 20 ×/0.8) with no z-stacking at 2 × 2 binning.

T7-based barcode amplification

Barcode amplification utilizing the T7-IVT (PerturbView) in situ amplification protocol with the Crop-Seq Multi vector is described in detail elsewhere [12, 18]. Briefly, barcode transcripts were generated and converted to localized rolling-circle amplicons as follows. Cells were fixed in 4% (v/v) formaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences 15,714) in 1X PBS (Ambion AM9625) for 30 min at room temperature and washed twice with PBS. Cells were permeabilized in 70% ethanol for 30 min, washed three times with PBS-T (1X PBS + 0.05% Tween-20; VWR 100216–360), and decrosslinked in 0.1 M sodium bicarbonate and 0.3 M NaCl at 65 °C for 4 h followed by three PBS-T washes. All reactions were performed in heat-compatible imaging plates (Cellviz P96-1.5H-N). In-situ transcription was performed in T7 reaction buffer (NEB E2040L) containing 10 mM each NTP (NEB E2040L), 5 mM DTT (NEB E2040L), 0.4 U/µL RiboLock RNase inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific EO0384), and T7 RNA polymerase mix (NEB E2040L—M0255AVIAL) diluted to 0.2X relative to the manufacturer’s recommendation. Reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. Samples were washed three times with PBS-T and post-fixed in 3% formaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences 15,714) and 0.1% glutaraldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences 16,120) for 30 min, followed by quenching for 1 min in pH 8 0.2 M Tris–HCl (Thermo Fisher Scientific AM9855G) and three PBS-T washes. Reverse transcription was carried out in 1X RevertAid RT buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific EP0452) containing 250 µM dNTPs (NEB N0447L), 1 µM biotinylated reverse-transcription primer (Integrated DNA Technologies), 200 µg/mL recombinant albumin (NEB B9200S), 0.8 U/µL RiboLock RNase inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific EO0384), and 4.8 U/µL RevertAid H-minus Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific EP0452). Reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. Samples were washed twice with PBS-T and incubated for 15 min with 20 µg/mL streptavidin (NEB N7021S) and 100 µg/mL recombinant albumin (NEB B9200S) in PBS, followed by two PBS-T washes and postfixation in 3% formaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences 15,714) and 0.1% glutaraldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences 16,120) for 30 min. Padlock probe gap-fill and ligation were performed in 1X Ampligase buffer (Lucigen A3210K) containing 50 nM dNTPs (NEB N0447L), 0.1 µM each padlock probe (Integrated DNA Technologies), 200 µg/mL recombinant albumin (NEB B9200S), 0.4 U/µL RNase H (Enzymatics Y9220L), 0.02 U/µL TaqIT polymerase (Enzymatics P7620L), and 0.5 U/µL Ampligase (Lucigen A3210K). Samples were incubated in gap-fill and ligation solution at 37 °C for 5 min followed by 45 °C for 90 min, then washed twice with PBS-T. Rolling circle amplification (RCA) was performed in 1X Phi29 buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific EP0091) containing 250 µM dNTPs (NEB N0447L), 5% glycerol (MilliporeSigma G5516), 200 µg/mL recombinant albumin (NEB B9200S), and 1 U/µL Phi29 DNA polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific EP0091) for 12 h at 30 °C. Samples were washed twice with PBS-T, then 1 µM sequencing primers (Integrated DNA Technologies) were hybridized in 2X SSC (Ambion AM9763) with 10% (v/v) formamide (Thermo Fisher Scientific 4650) for 30 min at room temperature and washed twice with PBS-T prior to in situ sequencing.

Otto2 system assembly overview

The Otto2 system was constructed exclusively from off-the-shelf parts. Additional photographs of the microscope cage and Otto2 assembly are included as Additional file 2. The Otto2 system comprises the following: 3 multiselector valves (1 each for reagent selection, well dispensation selection, and well aspiration selection, IDEX MXX778-605), 1 standard flow eight-channel head peristaltic pump (Gilson F155006), and one 3/8″ 12 V normally closed solenoid valve (U.S. Solid). These components were wired for programmatic control to an Arduino Mega 2650 microcontroller; the solenoid valve was wired downstream of an optocoupler relay (CHENBO DC 5 V 1 Channel Relay Module Board Shield High/Low Level Trigger with Optocoupler). For a complete wiring diagram, refer to Additional file 1: Table S6. Cooling blocks (Fisher Scientific 14–955-220) included aluminum 50 mL conical inserts. Tubing was chemical-resistant 1/16″ OD tubing (McMaster-Carr 5583K51) for reagent and dispensing tubing, ⅛” OD tubing (McMaster-Carr 9117T8) for aspiration tubing, and 1.02 mm ID PVC tubing for pump tubing (Gilson F117938). Leur locks were used to secure 16-gauge blunt-end sample needles (McMaster-Carr 75165A753) to 1/16″ (McMaster-Carr P-836) and ⅛” (McMaster-Carr P-831) tubing.

Otto2 hardware assembly

Otto2 hardware assembly proceeded as follows: 1) Cooling blocks, peristaltic pump, multiselector valves, and solenoid valve, and Arduino microcontroller were positioned on a shelf immediately above the Squid microscope and plugged into wall outlets; 2) the pump and multiselector valves were wired to the Arduino microcontroller; 3) the solenoid valve was wired between the Arduino microcontroller and one 12 V 5 A power source utilizing the optocoupler unit (see Additional file 1: Table S6). Tubing was then used to construct the dispensing lines in the following fashion: 1) 4 1/16″ OD tubing sections long enough to reach to the bottom of the cooling block inserts were connected into the reagent multiselector sample ports 1–4; 6 1/16″ OD tubing sections long enough to reach the microscope cage were connected into the dispensing multiselector sample ports 2–7, with an addition short 1/16 OD tubing section connected into dispensing multiselector sample port 8; 2) a short 1/16″ OD tubing section was connected to the central ports of the reagent and dispensing multiselector valves; 3) a blunt end needle was secured on the other end of each of the 2 tubing sections from the previous step; 4) a 1.02 mm ID PVC tubing section was threaded through the channel of the peristaltic pump according to manufacturer instructions and secured onto the needle-terminating ends of both reagent and dispensation central port tubing sections, with the reagent valve positioned downstream of the flow direction and the dispensing valve positioned upstream of the flow direction. Tubing was then used to construct the aspirating lines in the following fashion: 1) 3/8″ ID heavy wall tubing was secured to a vacuum wall spigot on one end and a solenoid valve port on the other end; 2) 3/8″ ID heavy wall tubing was secured to the remaining solenoid valve port on one end and to the port of a 1L Erlenmeyer vacuum flask (United Scientific FG5340-1000) plugged with a 3/8″ drilled #8 bung (Fermentaholics) on the other; 3) 3/8″ OD vacuum tubing (JoyTube) was secured into the drilled bung of the vacuum flask and stepped down to 1/8″ OD aspiration tubing on the other, via tubing diameter adapters (Cole-Parmer EW-50108–99, Cole-Parmer UX-50108–81) and 1/8″ ID adapting tubing (JoyTube); 4) 1/8″ OD vacuum line tubing was connected into the central port of the aspiration multiselector valve; 5) 6 1/8″ OD tubing sections long enough to reach the microscope stage were connected into the aspirating multiselector sample ports 2–7. Note that sample port 1 on both dispensing and aspiration multi valve selectors are left unconnected, which is necessary for software operation.

Otto2 heating cage assembly

The Otto2 cage was constructed from cast acrylic sheets (McMaster-Carr) with ¼” sheets used for walls and ⅛” sheets used for the floor, cut to size with a laser cutter (Epilog Fusion Pro) and sealed using industrial acrylic cement followed by silicone sealant. One 2″ diameter circular hole for tubing and 2 3″ diameter circular holes for heating ducts were cut into the back panel of the cage via laser cutter prior to assembly. The cage lid was constructed from two 1/8″ acrylic panels connected via a 12″ acrylic piano hinge and secured to the cage body via strips of ¼” wide self-adhering magnetic tape, with the back lid panel containing 2 3″ diameter circular holes for heating ducts. All CAD files used to laser cut acrylic panels are made publicly available [19]. 6 steel legs (GeilSpace Stainless Steel Pipe 1/2″ × 8″) screwed into steel flanges (GeilSpace Stainless Steel Floor Flange, ½”) were used to secure the microscope cage bottom panel around the Squid microscope body such that the top of the bottom panel was flush with the bottom of the microscope stage. The cage was temperature controlled using two parallel heating systems (right/left), each consisting of a 600 W PTC fan heater (PTCYIDU 110 V-600W) secured with high-heat foil tape to 3″ supply and return ducts and upstream of a dedicated heating controller (Inkbird ITC-308).

Otto2 well manifold assembly

The well manifold was constructed [19] using a Formlabs 3 SLA resin printer with Black v4.1 resin (RS-F2-GPBK-41) and cured according to manufacturer instructions. 12 #6–32 flat-headed screws threaded with flange nuts, used to secure tubing needles, were screwed down into 12 matching-sized holes in the manifold surface until resistance was met. Tubing needles leading from the Otto2 assembly were placed into their respective holes in the manifold and screwed down; the 6 dispensing needles were lowered into the 6 center holes until contact with the needle pillars, and the 6 aspirating needles were placed in the 6 left-offset holes until contact with the needle pillars. The well manifold, once loaded with needles, was then installed onto the well plate by placing the manifold on top of the well plate and gently applying pressure.

Otto2 software and system configuration

Otto2 control software was written in Arduino, accessed via publicly available Arduino IDE and is available as described in “Availability of data and materials”. All low-level functions, high-level functions, and system variables are contained within the OttoFns subfolder. The codebase also contains two calibration scripts and a run wrapper. One-time calibration, encompassing setting total reagent line and dispensing line volumes, was conducted by loading one line at a time of OneTimeCalibrationScript.ino onto the microcontroller and following in-script instructions. Pre-run calibration, encompassing setting per-mL pump time and validating success of dispensing, aspiration, and reagent addition operations across wells, was conducted by loading one line at a time of PreRunCalibrationScript.ino onto the microcontroller and following in-script instructions. Once calibrations were complete, the in situ run wrapper (RunOtto2.ino) was loaded onto the microcontroller via the Arduino IDE. Automation was initiated and linked to imaging via the Squid IDE via minor modifications to the most recent Squid codebase (contact Squid for up-to-date instructions). Briefly, in situ cycling operations defined in the Otto2 control software proceed as follows: a) multi-selector reagent valve is set to the appropriate reagent (incorporation mix, cleavage mix, incorporation wash buffer, or imaging buffer); b) reagent is drawn up from chemistry reservoirs through a dynamically pre-set reagent multiselector valve and inline peristaltic pump via positive displacement action through a dynamically pre-set dispensing multiselector valve and into a selected plate well via dispensing tubing and needle; c) an aspiration multiselector valve is set to a specified well; d) the solenoid valve is opened and well-specific aspiration occurs through the aspiration needle and tubing. Operations occur in a semi-parallel fashion such that while well B is undergoing aspiration, well A is undergoing dispensing, followed by well C undergoing aspiration while well B is undergoing dispensing. Once dispensed, sequencing reagents were followed by an air bubble and then wash reagent to keep reagents unmixed. For line-by-line information on in situ cycling protocols, consult RunOtto2.ino in the Otto2 source code. Upon conclusion of cycling operations, imaging automatically begins.

Automated in situ sequencing

The first incorporation cycle was conducted manually to decouple barcode prep from automation functionality in a fashion similar to previous reports [2] but utilizing diluted incorporation mix to further reduce screen cost and offset the higher minimum reagent volumes necessary for automated function compared with the manual protocol. Briefly, incorporation mix (reagent 1) was extracted from thawed Illumina MiSeq Nano v2 kits (Illumina MS-103–1003); wells were incubated with 1.5X dilute incorporation reagent, prepared by mixing 2 parts incorporation reagent with 1 part 1X ThermoPol buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific 62,249), for 7.5 min on a flat-top thermal cycler (Thermo Fisher Scientific 4,484,078) at 45 C, then exchanged three times in PR2 buffer (Illumina MS-103–1003), then washed quickly three times with PR2, then incubated for 5 min at 45 C on a flat-top thermal cycler five times, then washed once in PR2, then incubated for 5 min in 2X SSC (Thermo Fisher Scientific AM9763) with 1:2000 Hoechst (Thermo Fisher Scientific 62,249). Automated fluid handling requires approximately 50% higher per-well reagent volumes than manual pipetting (~ 1.1 mL vs ~ 600 µL per well), owing to dead volume and surface-tension considerations in flat-plate geometry. However, because the incorporation mix is diluted 1.5 ×, total kit consumption is comparable to manual operation; a complete cost breakdown is provided in Additional file 1: Table S5. Otto2 system was calibrated by setting correct pump time for 1 mL dispensation and by validating successful dispensing, aspiration, and reagent addition operations. Once the heating box reached equilibrium at 45 C (15 min) and cooling blocks were cooled to 4 C, the plate was loaded with the manifold and positioned on the microscope stage. Needle ends of dispensing and aspiration tubing were loaded and secured into correct positions in manifold as previously described with one dispensation and aspiration line loaded per well. Per base cycle, 7.5 mL of refrigerated 1.5X diluted incorporation mix (prepared as previously described), 7.5 mL of refrigerated pure cleavage mix, and 50 mL of refrigerated 2X SSC with 1:2000 Hoechst were placed in separate 50 ml conicals in cold block inserts with respective 1/16″ OD reagent tubing secured into to the bottom of each conical. A bottle containing 300 mL of PR2 buffer was secured with 1/16″ OD reagent tubing in the same fashion. Waste was emptied as needed and the waste reservoir was re-sealed with the vacuum line and the bung. Cycle operation was accessed via serial connection with the microcontroller unit and launched via the Squid IDE, after which cycle operation was hands-off; the user observed two diagnostic dispensing and aspiration operations across wells at cycle onset and observed imaging collection to validate correct performance. Cycle operation sequence for each sequencing reagent following two diagnostic washes (1 mL/well) proceeded as follows, first for cleavage then incorporation: reagent addition (0.98 mL); 1 exchange wash (1 mL dilution pre-1 mL wash); 1 quick wash (0.75 mL); 1 increased volume wash (3 mL); 4 standard washes (1 mL). Reagent addition incubations were 7.5 min, cleavage wash incubations were 1 min, and incorporation wash incubations were 5 min. All washes were conducted with PR2. The cycle concluded with a 12 min incubation, a 2X addition (1 mL) of imaging buffer and a final addition of imaging buffer (3 mL). Otto2 system held temperature at 45 C for all operations through final 5 min wash and at room temperature for final 12 min incubation, imaging buffer operations, and imaging. Conclusion of an Otto2 sequencing cycle automatically triggered onset of imaging via custom integration in Squid source software. Images were collected on the Cephla Squid + using the aforementioned imaging setup in 5 channels per field of view (Hoechst: 405 nm excitation with multiband emission filter, G: 550 nm excitation with FF01-578/21 emission filter, T: 550 nm excitation with FF01-624/40 emission filter, A: 640 nm excitation with FF01-680/42 emission filter, C: 640 nm excitation with FF01-732/68 emission filter) using a 4 × Olympus objective (Olympus Plan Apo 4 ×/0.16) at 1 × 1 binning.

Data transfer and compute

Raw images were transferred from the microscope workstation to Google Cloud Storage using parallelized gsutil rsync (12 processes × 8 threads). Phenotype images uploaded in approximately 55 min per plate (~ 1 h 50 min total); SBS cycle images uploaded in approximately 2 h 48 min per plate (~ 5 h 36 min total across both plates). Images were downloaded from GCS to the compute instance in approximately 10 min for phenotype data and 13 min per SBS cycle, benefiting from GCS–GCP colocation. On GCP, Brieflow preprocessing completed in approximately 25 min per plate, phenotype segmentation and feature extraction required approximately 3 h per plate (~ 6 h 11 min total), SBS processing required approximately 3 h per plate (plate 1 required additional time due to rotation alignment debugging), spatial merging completed in approximately 18 min, aggregation (including bootstrapping) in approximately 4 h 19 min, and clustering across multiple resolutions in approximately 1 h 55 min. MozzareLLM interpretation of 320 clusters completed in approximately 1 h 41 min. Thus, total runtime was approximately 31 h, although this does not include parameter setting, which is done in user facing notebooks (which takes < 30 min per module). All computation was performed on a GCP c4d-standard-384 instance (384 vCPUs, 1,488 GB RAM). Complete stage-by-stage runtimes are provided in Additional file 1: Table S7 and a detailed analysis timeline in Additional file 1: Table S8.

Brieflow analysis pipeline

Brieflow (v1.4.6) [8] was used for all computational analysis. The genome-wide screen served as a stress test for the pipeline, and the resulting changes fell into two categories. First, general bug fixes and performance improvements — including parallelized SBS parameter search, corrected IC field image combination, multi-mode evaluation support, and a windowed alignment parameter — were merged back into Brieflow's main codebase (PR #177, 10 commits; Additional file 1: Table S1), directly benefiting future users. Second, screen-specific adaptations — rotation-aware SBS alignment and a parallel filtered clustering arm — were developed on a dedicated branch (Additional file 1: Table S9) and are described below.

SBS cycle alignment

Automated on-scope fluid handling introduced inter-cycle drift exceeding what standard translation-only registration could correct. To address this, we added a rotation-and-translation alignment method to Brieflow's SBS alignment module. For each cycle, the DAPI channel was used as a reference. A rough translational offset was first computed on a windowed center region via phase cross-correlation, then applied to the full image. A Laplacian of Gaussian filter (sigma = 3.0) was applied to enhance spot features, and rotation was estimated by phase cross-correlation in log-polar space. The rotation was applied to the original source image before a final translational offset was computed in the rotated coordinate frame. Rotations exceeding 5° were clamped to zero as likely spurious. For tiles where automatic registration failed due to large drift, manual coarse offsets could be specified per cycle and applied as a pre-alignment step before the automatic fine alignment. An alternative edge-based alignment using Canny edge detection (sigma = 2.0) on DAPI was also implemented for robustness to signal degradation over cycles. All alignment methods were added to the shared alignment module without modification to downstream barcode calling or feature extraction. We sequenced deeply enough to enable error correction mode, a feature of the sparse sampling in Levenshtein space which allows barcode matching within a distance of two bases.

Feature extraction, merging, and aggregation

Segmentation, feature extraction (1,670 CellProfiler-derived features [20] per cell), spatial merging, deduplication, and aggregation were performed using Brieflow's standard modules as previously described [8]. Bootstrap significance testing used 10,000 simulations per gene against intergenic non-targeting controls.

Clustering on filtered perturbations

To focus downstream analysis on biologically meaningful perturbations, bootstrap significance testing identified 3,715 perturbations with significant (at least one feature with z-score threshold of 0.3, FDR threshold of 0.5) morphological effects from 21,732 total perturbations (Additional file 1: Table S4). These were used to filter the aggregated feature table prior to dimensionality reduction. PHATE embedding (cosine distance) [21] and Leiden clustering [22] were run at multiple resolutions (10.0–15.0) and benchmarked against CORUM [23] and KEGG [24] annotations. Resolution 10.0 was selected because it maximized pathway-annotation enrichment, yielding 320 clusters of which 15 were CORUM-enriched and 8 KEGG-enriched (Additional file 1: Table S3).

MozzareLLM interpretation

Cluster gene lists and associated UniProt functional annotations were passed to MozzareLLM [8]. The screen context prompt was varied slightly to inform the LLM API calls of the markers used in the screen. MozzareLLM queried Claude Sonnet 4.5 (claude-sonnet-4–5–20250929) to generate structured biological interpretations for each of 320 clusters, including dominant biological processes, pathway assignments, and per-gene classifications (established function, novel role, or uncharacterized). Confidence was assigned as high, medium, or low based on the coherence of gene functions within each cluster (Additional file 1: Tables S3, S4).
