Fulfilling the potential of traditional, complementary and integrative medicine for global health: addressing structural inequities within the research ecosystem
Authors: Jon Adams, Amie Steel, Nils Gilman, Marcel Wrzesinski, Motlalepula Gilbert Matsabisa, Georg Seifert
Published: 2026

The latest WHO Traditional Medicine Strategy1 and second WHO Global Summit on Traditional Medicine highlight traditional, complementary, and integrative medicine (TCIM)—a diverse set of practices, products, and care systems—as a cornerstone of global health. TCIM provides essential yet often covert support to formal health systems for what is estimated to be up to 80% of the world's population, particularly in structurally marginalised populations and lower income countries.2 Amid mounting pressures, including ageing populations, rising chronic disease, and health workforce burnout, the need for rigorous TCIM research has never been more urgent.

These insights are not new. In the decades preceding and following the turn of the 21st century, TCIM attracted a surge of attention within health research and policy circles. Many academic institutions launched TCIM centres and professorships, increasing numbers of TCIM-dedicated articles and special issues appeared in top-tier medical journals, and prestigious institutions and organizations released strategic TCIM declarations and recommendations.3,4 Whether critical or supportive, heightened visibility marked this period as one of cautious optimism for those seeking inroads for TCIM into global health research.

Unfortunately, some decades on, progress has largely stalled. Only a few select TCIM, including acupuncture, yoga and plant medicines, have gained limited visibility in the research field, and the full potential of TCIM research for global health remains unrealised. TCIM continues to receive minimal research funding (<1% global research budget) relative to its widespread use,5 and interest from leading medical journals and academic institutions has dwindled.

Why has momentum not been sustained? Critics place the blame firmly with TCIM research itself. They argue TCIM research lacks rigor due to its absence of ‘gold standard’ randomized controlled trials (RCTs), resulting in evidence that fails to meet conventional scientific standards. Some contend ‘there is no alternative medicine [TCIM]’, only medicine supported or not by evidence.6

Limits to these critiques are well-established, noting they disregard TCIM features ill-fitted to RCT frameworks, the importance of culturally appropriate, patient-centred care, and the broader range of accepted practice evaluation methodologies. It is not our intention to revisit these arguments here. Instead, we highlight a number of structural biases inherent in the wider research ecosystem which disadvantage TCIM's research progress and which, importantly, lie beyond its control. Addressing structural inequities has evolving support across health practice and research, but such considerations regarding the wider research ecosystem itself are far more limited. To help elucidate these inequities we can return to the very markers of TCIM's lack of progress around funding, publishing and institutional standing.

TCIM receives disproportionately low research funding and, with few exceptions (e.g., NCCIH, NIH), lacks ring-fenced allocations. This results in TCIM researchers competing in funding schemes designed for biomedicine and subject to reviewers with little or no TCIM expertise, limiting the feasibility of large-scale, high-quality studies. Furthermore, many of TCIM's interventions are low-cost and non-patentable and so offer minimal commercial incentives, leaving it structurally marginalized within a market-driven research system.

Few universities maintain dedicated TCIM departments or research centres resulting in a relatively small, fragile and disconnected TCIM research workforce with limited career pathways. These gaps critically undermine current and future research capacity, as well as the infrastructure and workforce needed to deliver large-scale, high-quality TCIM studies.

World-leading medical journals continue to marginalize TCIM research, limiting its visibility and standing. This goes beyond English-language bias and more fundamentally reflects systemic factors—journal scope, editor and reviewer expertise—combined with ranking systems that severely limit where TCIM research is published and how it is valued.

Due to these structural biases, TCIM remains one of global health's most overlooked assets. This stifles innovation, limits high-impact research, and undermines efforts to deliver safe, effective care for all. In response, we require substantial TCIM-specific research investment, clear institutional pathways for TCIM scholarship, recognition of pluralistic evidence frameworks, and a shift away from entrenched cultural and epistemic hierarchies in publishing and academia. Only by addressing the full complexity of structural barriers can we unlock TCIM's potential to help strengthen health systems and improve global health outcomes.
