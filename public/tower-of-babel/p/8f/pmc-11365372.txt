Removal of surface-attached micro- and nanobubbles by ultrasonic cavitation in microfluidics
Authors: Zibo Ren, Peng Xu, Huan Han, Claus-Dieter Ohl, Zhigang Zuo, Shuhong Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Unlike their typical lack of response to external stresses, surface-attached micro- and nanobubbles with contact radii of 2 μm to 20 μm exhibit a unique response to strong ultrasonic fields in microfluidics.•At frequencies between 200 kHz and 2 MHz, the surface bubbles remain unresponsive. However, at 100 kHz to 200 kHz, they interact with cavitation bubbles generated in the ultrasonic field.•The surface bubbles merge with the cavitation bubbles, causing them to detach from the substrate and become free gaseous nuclei. This detachment occurs without leaving observable residues.•The study suggests that the mutual acoustic radiation force between the cavitation bubbles and surface bubbles is responsible for their directional migration and eventual merging.Keywords: Surface micro- and nanobubbles, Ultrasonic cavitation, Bubble dynamics, Secondary Bjerknes force, Microfluidics

Introduction

Cavitation, the growth and collapse of gas or vapor bubbles in a liquid due to pressure variations [1], exhibits a fascinating duality, particularly near solid surfaces. This phenomenon can be both a destructive force and a valuable tool. In engineering fields like shipbuilding, aerospace, and hydropower, cavitation can damage high-speed rotating components, such as propellers, turbine blades, etc., necessitating its mitigation [2], [3]. Conversely, cavitation effects underpin technologies like jet polishing in precision manufacturing [4], cavitation reactor in water purification [5], [6], and kidney stone fragmentation in medical treatment [7], [8], [9], demonstrating a significant practical value. Therefore, in-depth research on cavitation phenomena, particularly the mechanisms of cavitation near the liquid–solid interface, is crucial for both mitigating its negative effects and expanding its applications in cutting-edge technologies.

Cavitation inception, the critical pressure drop triggering vapor bubble formation, is the foundation for subsequent cavitation phenomena [1]. To explain the reasons for cavitation inception near solid walls, it is assumed that there are “weak points” on the liquid–solid interface in water, namely cavitation nuclei, which significantly reduce the pressure threshold of cavitation inception from the solid wall [10]. To explain the physical nature of cavitation nuclei at the liquid–solid interface, Harvey et al. [11] proposed the “crevice model” in 1944, which assumed that non-condensable gases such as air were trapped in microscopic imperfections on rough hydrophobic surfaces. These bubbles become unstable and explosively grow under significant local pressure reduction, leading to cavitation. While the crevice model successfully predicts inception pressures for rough surfaces [12], [13], [14], [15], it does not explain how cavitation occurs on smooth surfaces, posing a challenge for a complete understanding of cavitation nuclei and their inception.

In 1986, the invention of atomic force microscopy (AFM) [16] greatly promoted the exploration of the interface from the micro and nanoscale and brought hope for the observation of gaseous nuclei on smooth surfaces. In 2000, through AFM, researchers from China [17] and Japan [18] independently reported the observation of spherical-cap-shaped gaseous domains on immersed smooth substrates with a maximum height of about 100 nm and a maximum contact radius of microns. Due to its height on the nanometer scale, the above gaseous domain is named “surface nanobubble”. However, it should be noted that “nano” in the name refers only to the magnitude of its height. Such bubbles can be observed by optical microscopy from a top/bottom view because of their large lateral sizes [19], [20], [21]. To more accurately reflect the transverse dimensional characteristics of such bubbles, they are addressed as “surface micro- and nanobubbles” below in this paper.

Similar to the characteristics of the cavitation nuclei described in the crevice model, the surface micro- and nanobubbles contain non-condensable gases such as air [22] and can exist on various hydrophobic and hydrophilic surfaces with a lifespan of several days [23], [24]. Whether surface micro- and nanobubbles can become cavitation nuclei has aroused researchers’ interest. It has been found through experiments that the surface micro- and nanobubbles have a completely different stability from the gas nuclei trapped in the crevice [25], [26]. It is manifested that the surface micro- and nanobubbles can remain at their sizes and positions under pressure reduction [27], mechanical forces [20], [28], or strong shear flows [29], which could be related to the pinning of their contact lines [20], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34].

Discrepancies have been seen in the early experiments on cavitation inception from surface micro- and nanobubbles. In 2007, Borkent et al. [35] focused transient shockwaves with a negative pressure of −6 MPa on an immersed hydrophobic substrate, which was covered with surface micro- and nanobubble-like objects, as examined with AFM. However, the cavitation events from the wafers did not show a strong relationship with the distribution of the surface micro- and nanobubbles. In 2014, Berkelaar et al. [36] pointed out that the surface micro- and nanobubble-like objects may have been contaminated droplets induced by disposal syringes and needles. These findings highlight the challenges in definitively attributing cavitation inception to surface micro- and nanobubbles in early experiments.

It is worth noting related research by Yukisada et al. [37] where they generated surface micro- and nanobubbles on a hydrophobic test tube bottom using the ethanol–water exchange method. Then they employed a tube-arrest technique to induce “acceleration-induced cavitation” [38], [39], [40]. This technique utilizes tensile waves generated during impact to induce cavitation bubbles on the inner wall, suggesting the possibility of cavitation from surface micro- and nanobubbles. However, the difficulty in measuring and controlling the tensile waves in this setup makes it challenging to determine the cavitation threshold for the surface bubbles.

Ultrasound is another common method for manipulating surface micro- and nanobubbles. In 2011, Brotchie et al. [41] generated micro- and nanobubbles on the surface of highly ordered pyrolytic graphite, which was then treated in ultrasonic bath with a frequency of 515 kHz and an amplitude of about 0.12 MPa for about 35–40s, and was observed by in situ AFM measurements. It was found that the surface micro- and nanobubbles remained at their original places after ultrasonic treatment, however, with a slight increase in size. Because AFM measurements can only be performed before and after ultrasonic treatment, the responses of surface micro- and nanobubbles during ultrasonic treatment are still unclear.

Indirect evidence suggests a potential role for surface micro- and nanobubbles in ultrasonic cavitation. Belova et al. [42] deposited a 3 nm-thick hydrophobic layer on the substrate of smooth hydrophilic pure aluminum, and detected surface micro- and nanobubbles on the hydrophobic regions immersed in air-saturated water. Cavitation from the surface was induced by ultrasonics with a frequency of 20 kHz and a power density of 51.3 W/cm2. After a few to tens of minutes of ultrasonic treatment, the surface was detected with AFM and scanning electron microscopy respectively. It was found that the damage of the hydrophobic region was greater than that of the hydrophilic region, implying that surface micro- and nanobubbles may enhance cavitation damage to the substrate. Bussonnière et al. [43] added silica microparticles with random shapes to pure water and treated the suspension with high-intensity focused ultrasound at a frequency of 1.1 MHz, producing tensile stresses of about -2MPa. By high-speed imaging and pressure measurement, they found that the cavitation intensity was maintained after tens of minutes of ultrasonic treatment, and they speculated that nanoscale cavitation nuclei were generated continuously on the surface of the particles.

While these studies provide evidence for the possible involvement of surface micro- and nanobubbles in cavitation, a lack of direct observational evidence with high spatial and temporal resolution hinders a clear understanding of the conditions required to induce cavitation from these bubbles. Molecular dynamics simulations offer a valuable tool to study the behavior of surface micro- and nanobubbles [31], [32], [44]. Dockar et al. [45] predicted a lower cavitation threshold pressure for the surface micro- and nanobubbles in the experimental work by Borkent et al. [35], suggesting insufficient experimental pressure. They have also used theoretical models to predict the resonance frequency of surface micro- and nanobubbles under ultrasound, aligning with simulation results [46]. Additionally, Dockar et al. [47] employed simulations to predict the collapse of surface micro- and nanobubbles towards a wall induced by shockwaves. However, limitations in computational domain size (often less than 1 μm) and timescales (nanoseconds or picoseconds) make it difficult to experimentally verify these simulation results due to the demanding combination of spatial and temporal scales required.

In this paper, we address the challenges outlined above by investigating experimentally whether surface micro- and nanobubbles attached to smooth solid walls can become cavitation nuclei in a strong ultrasonic field. The following sections detail our methodology (Section 2), experimental results (Section 3), discussion of the mechanisms involved (Section 4), and concluding remarks (Section 5).

Experimental setup

The experimental setup is composed of a microfluidic device and an imaging system. As shown in Fig. 1(a), the microfluidic chip is placed on the stage of an inverted optical microscope (IX73, Olympus) attached to a high-speed camera (v2012, Phantom, or FASTCAM Mini UX50, Photron). To avoid motion blurring, the high-speed recordings are illuminated with a pulsed diode laser (CAVILUX HF, CAVITAR, wavelength 810 nm, pulse duration 20 ns). The details of the microfluidic device are shown in Fig. 1(b). A Y-shaped microfluidic channel is made from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) by soft photolithography and bonded onto a glass slide (75mm×51mm×1.2mm, Electron Microscopy Sciences), following the protocol in Ref. [20]. The height of the microchannel is about 20 μm, with two inlets and one outlet.

Schematics of the experimental setup (not to scale). (a) Optical observation and illumination. (b) Details of the sample shown in the dashed box in (a). (c) Generation and measurement of the driving voltage and the amplified voltage. (d) Setup of the piezoelectric transducers on the glass slide connected to the amplifier. The orientation of the Y-shaped microchannel is the same as shown in (b) and (d).

To generate surface-attached micro- and nanobubbles, we apply the standard ethanol–water exchange method [24]. Two 5 ml syringes (1005 TLL, Hamilton) filled with equal volumes of ultrapure water (Genie PURIST, RephiLe Bioscience, resistivity 18.2 MΩ·cm) and absolute ethanol (99.9%, Huaxia Reager) at room temperature are connected via metal needles and silicone tubing to the Y-shaped microchannel. A syringe pump (LSP02-2B, Longer Precision Pump) infuses both liquids at a constant flow rate of 0.025 ml/min, forming a water–ethanol interface before mixing within the microchannel. Micro- and nanobubbles nucleate on the side of the water phase, exhibiting a banded distribution along the water-ethanol–glass contact line. After the syringe pump stops, the surface-attached micro- and nanobubbles remain stable in the quiescent liquid and they are then observed with optical microscopy through a 20× objective (LUCPlanFL N, Olympus). The pixel size is calibrated using a net grid reticle (GCG-YP908, Daheng Optics) to be 1.27 μm/pixel.

To exert strong ultrasonic fields to the surface-attached micro- and nanobubbles, four piezoelectric transducers (P-5A20, Feitian Electronics) are glued next to the microfluidic chip, converting the driving voltage into mechanical vibrations of the substrate, which are transmitted through the glass plate into the microchannel. The piezoelectric transducers are discs of a diameter of 16 mm, with a thickness resonance frequency of 1 MHz and an electromechanical coupling factor of 0.53 in the thickness direction [48]. The driving signals are provided by a function generator (DG4202, Rigol) between 100 kHz and 2 MHz and amplified to a maximum amplitude of 100 V by a high-voltage amplifier (ATA-1220D/ATA-2021H, Aigtek), both being monitored with an oscilloscope (MSO5104, Rigol); see Fig. 1(c) and (d). To prevent overheating of the piezoelectric elements, the applied signals are configured as interval bursts consisting of 5×103 cycles of sinusoidal waves delivered every 100 ms. Further details regarding the signals can be found in Appendix A.

Direct measurement of the acoustic pressure within the sealed microchannels proves challenging. Therefore, following the approach of Tandiono et al. [49], we perform this measurement in a separate water droplet. The result suggests that a standing-wave field with a pressure amplitude reaching up to 1.5 MPa is established near the substrate under the settings employed in subsequent experiments. The details are presented and discussed in Appendix B.

Experimental observations

Before the ultrasound is switched on, the surface-attached micro- and nanobubbles generated with the ethanol–water exchange method are distributed along the centerline of the Y-shaped microchannel, with their contact radii ranging from 2 μm to 20 μm, as shown in the first frame of Fig. 2. At a driving frequency between 200 kHz and 2 MHz, the surface-attached micro- and nanobubbles remain unchanged in the channels. Yet, between 100 kHz and 200 kHz, the responses of the surface-attached micro- and nanobubbles to the ultrasound are found.

In this section, we provide the first observations of the removal of surface micro- and nanobubbles by cavitation in the microchannel. Finally, to investigate the mechanism of bubble removal, we show and analyze the interactions of surface micro- and nanobubbles with cavitation bubbles.

Removal of surface micro- and nanobubbles by cavitation in microchannel

Fig. 2 depicts an overview of the removal of surface micro- and nanobubbles by cavitation at an ultrasound frequency of 160 kHz. Once the ultrasound is switched on, cavitation bubbles emerge on the side walls of the Y-shaped microchannel, as marked by the red arrows at t=16ms. Here the time t denotes the duration of the ultrasound exposure. Then these cavitation bubble clusters expand to the centerline of the microchannel where the surface-attached micro- and nanobubbles reside. At t=24ms, the surface bubbles with large contact radii merge with the cavitation bubble clusters and then detach from the substrate, as marked by the red arrow in the enlarged view below. At t=32ms, more surface bubbles with smaller contact radii merge with the cavitation bubble clusters and detach from the substrate. Until t=40ms, almost no surface bubbles remain at their original positions. At this time, the cumulative cycles of the sinusoidal waves N reach 5×103. At t=896ms, i.e., after N=4.5×104, the surface micro- and nanobubbles in the observation area are removed from the substrate.

Fig. 2 reveals that surface-attached micro- and nanobubbles are unstable in the presence of acoustic-driven cavitation bubbles. Besides, this first observation suggests an attraction of mobile cavitation bubbles toward the surface-attached bubbles. Before we look at the latter observation, we study the origin of cavitation nucleation in the microchannel.

Cavitation nucleation in microchannel

Our experimental observations suggest that cavitation nucleation in the microchannel consists of three main stages. At first, daughter bubbles with a diameter of tens of microns are released from preexisting millimeter-sized gas bubbles attached to the channel wall due to the interfacial instabilities. Second, microbubbles are seeded by pinch-off from these daughter bubbles, creating a cluster of collective moving cavitation bubbles. Third, these microbubbles are transported between adjacent cavitation bubble clusters. Now we provide evidence and report representative observations of these three stages in detail.

Stage I: daughter bubble release

When the infused liquid wets the microchannel, air may be trapped at the side walls thereby forming gaseous bubbles. Because the height of the microchannel is only 20 μm, the upper and the lower surfaces of the large gas bubbles shown here contact the top and bottom walls of the microchannel, respectively. As shown in the first frame of Fig. 3, a gas bubble with a maximum lateral size of about 200 μm is initially attached to the side wall of the microchannel, while a second larger bubble is visible on the right. At about 27.93 ms after the ultrasound is switched on, the liquid interfaces show some undulations as a result of standing surface waves.

The amplitude of the capillary wave is large enough to shed water droplets toward the side wall into the gas bubble, as shown at 28.33 ms. Because the water droplets are attached to the side wall, they tend to contact the vibrating air–water interface and merge with the water in the microchannel, leading to the rupture of the interface, as shown by the arrow at 28.80 ms. A daughter bubble is thus released with a lateral size of about 20 μm, which migrates along the side wall toward the main channel, as marked by the arrow in the frame at 29.00 ms.

Stage II: cavitation seeding from daughter bubble

While the surface oscillation of the large bubble in Fig. 3 is rather moderate and results in the release of a smaller daughter bubble, the daughter bubble itself may be much closer to the (non-linear) resonance size of the ultrasound field and therefore have more pronounced instabilities. When this daughter bubble travels to the main channel and pins its contact line at the side wall, shown in Fig. 4 with an equilibrium radius of ≈19.2μm, it responds with the shedding of traveling microbubbles after the ultrasound is switched on; see the arrows at 1.90 ms and 8.80 ms. The maximum radius of the shed microbubbles is about 3 μm. The seeding of the multiple microbubbles from an oscillating surface-attached bubble is similar to the phenomena reported in Ref. [50]. The multiple microbubbles shed from the wall-attached bubble travel in curvilinear trajectories into the bulk liquid (arrows at 11.3 ms), thus serving as free cavitation nuclei. Consequently, the surface-attached microbubble evolves into a cavitation bubble cluster.

Stage III: microbubble transport between neighboring cavitation bubble clusters

After the ultrasound is switched on, multiple cavitation bubble clusters can develop along the side wall of the microchannel, as shown in the snapshot at 10.27 ms in Fig. 5. The cavitation bubble clusters distribute with equal distances between each other, of about 250 μm, which could be related to the standing waves traveling in the PDMS. This can be compared with the results in Ref. [51], where gaseous bubbles attached to a PDMS surface distribute with equal distances comparable to the wavelength of the surface wave during the ultrasound. The velocity of the surface wave traveling in the PDMS is about 33 m/s [51], the driving frequency of the ultrasound is 150 kHz, and thus the wavelength of the surface wave is about 220 μm, similar to the space observed in Fig. 5.

In summary, following the three main stages shown above, the cavitation nuclei are generated in the microchannel, thus allowing the subsequent interactions between cavitation bubbles and surface-attached micro- and nanobubbles. Increasing the driving pressure amplitude can reasonably enhance all three stages, thereby accelerating the removal of the surface-attached micro- and nanobubbles, as shown in Appendix C.

To analyze this force between a free cavitation bubble and surface micro- and nanobubbles, we present a theoretical model in the next section.

Acoustic radiation force analysis

Modeling assumptions

We here extend the concept of mutual acoustic radiation force between free bubbles [52], [53], [54], [55] to the case of a cavitating bubble interacting with a surface-attached micro- and nanobubble. The sketch for the model is shown in Fig. 8(a), where a surface micro- and nanobubble in a spherical-capped shape is attached to a glass slide, with a contact radius a and the center O of the circular contact line. A spherical cavitation bubble is assumed to be located at a distance l from point O, with a pulsating radius Rb(t). A one-dimensional coordinate is established with the origin O and the r-axis from point O toward the center of the cavitation bubble. The applied acoustic pressure is p1=pasin2πf1t, with the frequency f1 and the amplitude pa. The driving frequency is considered as 107 kHz and the sound speed in the water is about 1.5 × 103 m/s, leading to the wavelength of about 1.4 mm, much larger than the equilibrium radius of the cavitation bubble in our experiment, i.e., Rb,0=8.6 μm, as measured from the high-speed snapshot at 0 ms in Fig. 6(a). Therefore, we assume that the acoustic field is nearly uniform at the position of the cavitation bubble.

Schematic of the theoretical model for the acoustic radiation force between a cavitation bubble and a surface-attached micro- and nanobubble. (a) Coordinates and notations. (b) Transmission of the acoustic wave from the solid through the air to the liquid.

Since the distances between the centers of the cavitation bubble and the projected areas of the surface bubbles are less than 100 μm, the primary Bjerknes force on the cavitation bubble may not vary in its direction during the interactions of the free cavitation bubble with the surface bubbles. The secondary Bjerknes force between the cavitation bubble and the surface micro- and nanobubble is induced by the pressure gradient near the cavitation bubble, which is caused by the acoustic pressure radiated by the surface bubbles.

Next, we discuss the oscillation of the surface micro- and nanobubbles. A surface micro- and nanobubble exhibits oscillations in its gas–liquid interface while maintaining a constant contact radius. This leads to variations in the contact angle. Dockar et al. [46] reported that under an acoustic pressure with a 5 MPa amplitude and a frequency around 1 GHz, the contact angle of these bubbles varied by only within 5°. They further observed that the amplitude of this variation decreased as the driving frequency deviated from the bubble’s natural frequency (around 10 GHz). Therefore, at our lower driving frequency of 100 kHz, the variation of contact angles for the surface micro- and nanobubbles is expected to be significantly smaller than 5°. This allows us to simplify the model by treating these bubbles as thin, flat gaseous disks with radius a and neglecting their height during oscillation. Since the center-to-center distances between the surface micro- and nanobubbles and the cavitation bubble are significantly larger than the contact radii a, we can assume that the pressure wave propagates from the solid wall, through the gas in the bubble, and into the surrounding liquid. Consequently, every point on the gaseous disk oscillates at the same frequency and phase, emitting a spherical wave into the semi-infinite liquid like a point source located at the origin O.

Fig. 8(b) illustrates the acoustic wave transmission from the solid through the gas disk. The solid’s oscillation (denoted by “0”) transmits into the air (denoted by “1”) as a plane wave with a transmission coefficient of tp,01=2ρ1c1/(ρ0c0+ρ1c1). Here, ρ and c represent density and sound speed, respectively, with subscripts 0 and 1 indicating solid and air. Similarly, the air’s oscillation transmits into the liquid (denoted by “2”) with a transmission coefficient of tp,12=2ρ2c2/(ρ1c1+ρ2c2).

We then discuss the contribution of the pressure wave radiated from each surface bubble A–C to the dynamic behavior of the cavitation bubble by calculating it at a constant distance of l=50μm. We substitute the provided parameters into Eqs. (4) and (5) and employ the Runge–Kutta method with MATLAB (MathWorks) to solve these coupled differential equations. The acoustic amplitude is selected as pa=0.1MPa to determine the time evolution of the cavitation bubble radius, which best fits the range of the cavitation bubble radius during oscillation with the experimental measurements (3.3–16.6 μm), as shown in Fig. 9(b). For surface bubble A (red curve), during the driving cycle, the bubble radius expands from its equilibrium size (Rb,0=8.6 μm) to a maximum radius of about 16 μm as the driving pressure becomes negative (refer to Fig. 9(a) for pressure profile). Subsequently, the bubble contracts to a minimum radius of about 3 μm when the pressure becomes positive again, followed by two additional smaller rebounds in the bubble radius. Because the cavitation bubble is driven below resonance and at high driving amplitude, it shows an unsteady oscillatory behavior, which prevents its radius from converging within a single cycle [60]. For surface bubbles A–C, the three curves in Fig. 9(b) nearly coincide, indicating that the pressure waves radiated from the surface micro- and nanobubbles have a minimal influence on the overall pulsation of the cavitation bubble.

Characteristics of acoustic radiation forces

To characterize the acoustic radiation forces exerted on the cavitation bubble from the surface-attached micro- and nanobubbles, we calculate the mutual acoustic radiation force acting between the cavitation bubble and each surface bubble, assuming a constant distance between them. We substitute the calculated bubble radius changes shown in Fig. 9(b) into Eq. (3) to determine this force F1. The results for bubble 1 interacting with surface bubbles A–C at a distance of l=50 μm are shown in Fig. 9(c) for a single cycle. The acoustic radiation force pulses between positive (pushing away) and negative (pulling closer) values with a magnitude of about 0.1 nN. The force is strongest for surface bubble C, which has the largest contact radius. In our experiments, this phenomenon of pushing and pulling forces can persist for thousands of driving cycles. The specific effect depends on both the time elapsed and the distance between the cavitation bubble and each surface micro- and nanobubble, as illustrated further in Fig. 7. To quantify this long-term effect, we introduce the concept of the secondary Bjerknes force FB. This force is calculated by averaging the acoustic radiation force at a constant distance over a single driving cycle. This approach avoids the complexities of calculating unsteady bubble dynamics over a longer period, which is not representative of the actual conditions in our experiments. We then perform the calculation of FB by substituting different bubble distances within the range of 5–60 μm.

Fig. 10 shows how the secondary Bjerknes force changes depending on the distance between the cavitation bubble and each surface micro- and nanobubble, where the force is consistently attractive, and its strength rapidly increases as the decrease gets smaller. The inset in Fig. 10 illustrates this relationship, where the force is roughly proportional to the inverse square of the distance, i.e., |FB|∝l-2. However, for distances greater than 20 μm, the force weakens by a factor of 10 compared to the case when they are 5 μm apart. This finding helps explain our observations in Fig. 7(d)–(f), where the cavitation bubble tends to be attracted to nearby surface micro- and nanobubbles within a distance of about 20 μm.

Initially, when the ultrasound is turned on, the force pulling the cavitation bubble toward surface bubble A is the strongest compared to the forces from surface bubbles B and C, as shown in Fig. 11. This stronger attraction to bubble A is likely the reason why the cavitation bubble is first observed moving toward bubble A in Fig. 6.

As the cavitation bubble gets close to surface bubble A, the attractive force exerted by surface bubble B strengthens rapidly. This increase in force from bubble B raises the possibility that the bubble might be pulled toward it next. Once both bubbles A and B detach from the surface, the attractive force from bubble C becomes dominant and continues to pull the bubble. It’s important to note that the bubble’s motion is also influenced by the primary Bjerknes force, which arises from the complex distribution of the ultrasound field (see Appendix B). However, the primary force mainly causes random movements when the cavitation bubble is far from any surface micro- and nanobubble, and the secondary Bjerknes forces are weak, as observed in Fig. 7(b) and (c).

Conclusions

This work presents a novel mechanism for detaching surface micro- and nanobubbles with contact radii of 2 μm to 20 μm from a substrate using strong ultrasound fields. At a driving frequency of 100 kHz to 200 kHz, the ultrasound creates cavitation bubbles in the liquid. These cavitation bubbles are then attracted to the surface micro- and nanobubbles, eventually merging with them. The existence of cavitation nuclei is the necessity of cavitation in microchannels. From experimental observations, we summarize three crucial stages for cavitation nucleation, namely, daughter bubble release, cavitation seeding from the daughter bubble, and microbubble transport between neighboring cavitation bubble clusters.

To understand the underlying interaction of surface micro- and nanobubbles with cavitation bubbles, we develop a theoretical model based on acoustic radiation forces. While the model shows the minimal influence of surface bubbles on the cavitation bubble’s size evolution, it reveals a critical role for the secondary Bjerknes forces. These forces, generated by acoustic waves from oscillating surface bubbles, attract the cavitation bubbles toward the surface bubbles. The force magnitude increases significantly as the distance between the bubbles decreases. Our findings demonstrate how surface micro- and nanobubbles can manipulate the trajectory of cavitation bubbles in strong ultrasound fields. This mechanism offers a new pathway for detaching surface bubbles and generating free cavitation nuclei in the flow.

These results contribute to the advancement of cavitation nuclei theory and hold potential for various engineering applications, including drag reduction in microscale flows, mineral flotation, medical imaging, water treatment, surface cleaning, and electrochemical processes.

Measurement of the strong ultrasonic field

To understand the intensity and distribution of the acoustic pressure near the glass slide (without a microchannel bonded), we employ a method similar to Ref. [49]. As shown in Fig. 13(a), a small droplet of deionized water (about 500 μl) is placed on the glass slide surrounded by four piezoelectric transducers.

A sample measurement of the acoustic pressure field. (a) The xOy coordinate system on a glass slide without bonding the microchannel. (b) Driving voltage signal with a frequency of 670 kHz and (c) the corresponding acoustic pressure at (x,y)=(0,0). (d) Spatial distribution of the maximum value of the absolute acoustic pressure |p1|max.

We use a fiber optic hydrophone (HFO690, ONDA) to measure the acoustic pressure. The hydrophone tip is positioned using a precision triaxial translation stage with 0.01 mm control accuracy in each direction. The tip is carefully immersed in the water droplet and positioned approximately 40 μm from the glass slide using the combination of the translation stage and microscopic observation. We define the center of this area as the origin and establish the xOy coordinate system as shown in Fig. 13(a). By moving the hydrophone tip along the x and y axes, we measure the variations of the acoustic pressure at different locations over time. This allows us to map the spatiotemporal distribution of the acoustic pressure near the surface of the glass slide during the application of the driving voltage.

Fig. 13(b) shows a sample measurement with a continuous driving voltage of 670 kHz at an amplitude of around 0.75 V. All four piezoelectric transducers are activated simultaneously by an amplified voltage with an amplitude of about 12 V. The hydrophone signal measured at (x,y)=(0,0) is converted into acoustic pressure using a post-processing program. As shown in Fig. 13(c), the unfiltered acoustic pressure, with an amplitude of about 1.5 MPa, oscillates at the same frequency as the driving voltage but with a nearly half-wavelength phase difference.

We scan a 2mm×2mm square area by moving the hydrophone tip along the x and y axes. Due to the hydrophone’s diameter of 100 μm, the scan spacing is set to 200 μm. Fig. 13(d) shows the spatial distribution of the maximum absolute pressure |p1|max at each measurement point. The pattern resembles a standing wave, with the highest pressure occurring at the center point (0,0) and a roughly symmetrical distribution around it. The maximum pressure measured (around 1.5 MPa) aligns well with previous findings [61].

Influence of the driving pressure amplitude on the bubble removal

Fig. 14 demonstrates the effect of driving pressure amplitude on removing the surface-attached micro- and nanobubbles. We compare the high-speed snapshots of the removal process for two experiments with the same driving frequency of 160 kHz but different amplified voltage amplitudes (15.8 V and 24.9 V). Consistent with observations in Fig. 2, the surface-attached micro- and nanobubbles are initially generated along the centerline of the microchannel. At the lower amplified voltage (15.8 V, Fig. 14(a)), the surface micro- and nanobubbles remain unchanged between t=0ms and t=432ms. Conversely, the higher voltage (24.9 V, Fig. 14(b)) removes nearly all surface bubbles within the same timeframe. Complete removal of the surface bubbles in Fig. 14(a) begins at t=732ms. These observations suggest that increasing the amplified voltage amplitude enhances driving pressure amplitude. This likely promotes cavitation nuclei generation and intensifies cavitation bubble/cluster movement within the microchannel. Consequently, more opportunities arise for surface bubbles to interact with approaching cavitation bubbles, leading to faster removal.
