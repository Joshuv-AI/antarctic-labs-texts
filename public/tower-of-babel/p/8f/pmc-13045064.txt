Educational benefits of pre-class videos and viewing perspectives on ultrasound-guided central venous catheterization training: a prospective randomized controlled trial
Authors: Po-Hsiang Liao, Chung-Ting Chen, Yi-Tzu Lee, Cheng-Hao Lee, Chorng-Kuang How, Hsien-Hao Huang, Teh-Fu Hsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraining in ultrasound-guided central venous catheterization (CVC) is constrained by limited hands-on opportunities and instructor availability. Pre-class instructional videos may improve procedural learning; however, the optimal filming perspective—first-person point-of-view (POV) versus face-to-face (F-t-F) side-view—remains unascertained. We compared the effects of video perspectives on ultrasound-guided CVC training-related skill acquisition.MethodsThis prospective, randomized, single-blind study included 70 postgraduate physicians in pre-class POV video (n = 23), pre-class F-t-F side-view video (n = 23), or control (no pre-class video, n = 24) groups. Participants completed pre-training assessments, one-on-one hands-on training, and post-training evaluations, with outcomes including a 17-item checklist score (0–34, Competency-Based Assessment Tool for Ultrasound-Guided Central Venous Catheter Placement), global rating (1–10), completion time, and self-rated confidence analyzed with the Kruskal–Wallis and Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction.ResultsBaseline demographics were comparable. The POV group showed the highest pre-training median global rating score, which significantly exceeded that of other groups (p < 0.001). Both video groups had significantly higher pre-training self-rated confidence than controls (p = 0.01), without differences between POV and F-t-F (p = 0.553). Overall time to pre-training assessment completion did not differ significantly among groups in the primary analysis; post-hoc as-treated analysis showed significantly faster task completion in POV compared with the control group (p = 0.007).ConclusionPre-training videos significantly enhanced training efficiency. POV and F-t-F videos improved learners’ competency and confidence than in controls. Overall procedural efficiency did not differ significantly between POV and F-t-F; the POV group achieved higher global rating scores, suggesting a potential advantage of the first-person perspective.Trial registrationThe trial was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT06947733, registered on 23 April 2025).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-08870-z.Keywords: ultrasound-guided, central venous catheterization (CVC), first-person perspective, Point of View, side-view

Background

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) has been recently popularized in emergency medicine [1]. Although ultrasound training enhances clinical decision-making and diagnostic accuracy [2], challenges such as limited equipment, few experienced instructors, and high student-instructor ratios reduce hands-on opportunities and teaching quality [1, 2]. A prospective randomized controlled trial (RCT) explored instructional videos and peer-assisted learning in ultrasound education [3]. Traditionally, ultrasound training involves bedside or peer-to-peer instruction. The high student demand and faculty unavailability constrain individualized hands-on instruction for every learner. Thus, instructional videos constitute a viable solution. Peer-assisted learning with trained student tutors is feasible and effective. Although ultrasound is well studied diagnostically, its instructional role in invasive ultrasound-guided procedures remains unexplored.

Importance

The optimal approach for filming procedural training videos is academically debated. First-person point-of-view (POV) filming, with footage from the operator’s first-person perspective, potentially enhances realism and improves learning outcomes. In UK medical schools, GoPro cameras were used to record ward rounds, handovers, and case discussions from the clinician’s perspective, and thereby providing an immersive, reproducible educational experience [4]. Compared with small-group simulations, POV filming requires fewer resources and time while facilitating effective learning. In nursing education, the effectiveness of different video perspectives for technical training was evaluated. An RCT comparing three video conditions (face-to-face [F-t-F], over-the-shoulder, and mixed [alternating both views]) with a no-video-instruction control group [5] showed learners in the mixed group performed significantly better than those in other groups, without significant differences between the F-t-F and over-the-shoulder perspectives, which outperformed the control group.

Although immersive or virtual reality–based instructional videos have been investigated in CVC training [6, 7], prior studies have focused primarily on immersion or realism rather than perceptual–motor alignment, which may play an important role in procedural skill acquisition. Whether viewing the procedure from the operator’s first-person perspective improves CVC skill acquisition compared with traditional instructional viewing angles remains uncertain, and this potential advantage has not been clearly established in the existing literature. Therefore, this study aims to address this gap by examining whether first-person perspective instructional videos enhance CVC procedural skill acquisition.

Goals of this investigation

This study evaluated the training effectiveness of pre-class instructional videos in invasive ultrasound-guided procedures to determine which perspective more effectively enhances procedural competency and confidence among trainees. The primary objective was to assess the impact of pre-class video learning on the effectiveness of procedural skill acquisition by comparing trainees who did or did not watch instructional videos before training. The secondary objective was to investigate the influence of different video perspectives—first-person POV filming versus traditional F-t-F side-view recording.

Learners acquiring procedural skills must coordinate multiple interdependent steps, which imposes substantial intrinsic cognitive load. According to Cognitive Load Theory [8], novices benefit when instructional designs reduce extraneous load and support early schema formation. Pre-class instructional videos—particularly those offering first-person perspectives—may help learners construct initial cognitive frameworks before hands-on practice, thereby lowering cognitive burden during early attempts and facilitating more efficient skill acquisition.

Methods

Study design and setting

This single-center, prospective, randomized, parallel, single-blinded study was conducted at Taipei Veterans General Hospital, from July to November 2024. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Veterans General Hospital (No. 2024-06-006B) and adhered to CONSORT reporting guidelines (Supplement 1). All participants provided written informed consent on enrolment following research project promotional posters and email notifications.

No ultrasound-based studies have explicitly compared the effectiveness of first-person POV instructional videos, F-t-F side-view videos, and a control group in CVC placement. Therefore, we referred to nursing education research that utilized similar video perspectives to evaluate students’ performance in urinary catheter placement [5]. The sample size was estimated using checklist-based scoring (converted to a percentage scale) and one-way ANOVA power analysis; with a significance level of α = 0.05 and a power of 0.8, 24 participants per group were deemed necessary, with a total enrollment of 72 participants.

Participants

In Taiwan, licensed physicians complete a 2-year postgraduate general medicine training program (PGY) before specialized residency. Most PGY-1 physicians have never independently performed CVC insertion on actual patients, and approximately half of those with prior experience have never used real-time ultrasound guidance.

All PGY physicians at the institution were invited via email by the hospital’s Department of Medical Education. The invitation included information about the study purpose, training procedures, and a registration link. Participation was voluntary and interested PGY physicians enrolled after reviewing the study information.

We enrolled 70 PGY physicians interested in ultrasound-guided CVC training in emergency medicine. Two participants withdrew owing to clinical duties and did not complete all evaluations (final sample < 72). Table 1 presents the participants’ baseline characteristics. No participant experienced any physical or psychological harm during the study.

Video preparation

To record the POV perspective video, a GoPro camera (HERO12 Black) was mounted on the instructor’s head, to capture the procedure from the operator’s viewpoint. For the F-t-F side-view video, two additional cameras (DJI Osmo Pocket 3) were strategically positioned: one near the ultrasound machine on the operator’s right-hand side (side-view), and another directly facing both the ultrasound screen and the instructor (front-view); both these views were combined to create the F-t-F side-view instructional video, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Both video formats were matched for duration (25 min and 42 s), resolution, and frame rate. At the end of both instructional videos, a Pikachu and a SpongeBob SquarePants cartoon image were inserted into the respective group’s video. A pre-class question about the cartoon character verified full video viewing. The videos were uploaded to YouTube with restricted access; only participants whose emails were pre-registered could view them starting 3 days before the training session. After study completion, participants’ email access was deleted to prevent video sharing and unauthorized pre-exposure for future learners.

Interventions

Figure 2 presents the study flowchart. Participants were randomly assigned into three groups using a computer-generated randomization table with block randomization by a study assistant: (1) the first-person POV video, (2) the F-t-F side-view video, and (3) the control, without a pre-class video. Three days before the training session, all participants received a pre-class reminder email. The POV group received the CVC procedure checklist together with an instructional video recorded from the first-person perspective; the F-t-F side-view group received the checklist and an instructional video filmed from the side-view perspective; the control group received only the CVC procedure checklist.

Flowchart of the randomized study design. In total, 70 postgraduate-year (PGY) physicians were block-randomized into three groups: control (no pre-class video), F-t-F video, and POV video. The video groups received both the procedural checklist and their respective instructional video whereas the control group received only the checklist. On the training day, the participants completed a pre-session questionnaire, an independent pre-training CVC procedure (video-recorded for checklist and global rating evaluation), a one-on-one teaching session, a post-training assessment, and a post-session questionnaire

Training-day procedure

On the training day, participants completed a pre-session questionnaire, which included basic demographic information, self-assessment of procedural skills, and confirmation that they watched the pre-class instructional video. Next, participants underwent a pre-training assessment wherein they performed ultrasound-guided (GE Venue ultrasound) CVC on a CAE Blue Phantom™ Gen II Ultrasound Central Line Training Model; their performance was video-recorded, and instructors evaluated their technique using a competency checklist (Supplement 2, Competency-Based Assessment Tool for Ultrasound-Guided Central Venous Catheter Placement [UGCVCP-CAT]). The procedural steps for CVC followed internationally recognized guidelines for ultrasound-guided central venous access [9, 10]. The UGCVCP-CAT was adapted from a validated checklist for ultrasound-guided internal jugular CVC [11]. Given time constraints, the assessment focused on sonographic technique, needling, and catheter placement. Preliminary preparatory steps—such as obtaining informed consent, hand hygiene, and sterile field preparation before full draping—were excluded from scoring to focus on the critical technical domains of ultrasound operation and procedural needling skill performance, while building upon a validated assessment framework.

Next, a one-on-one instructional session was conducted wherein instructors described the procedural steps and technique refinement. Then, the participants completed a post-training assessment wherein they independently performed ultrasound-guided CVC and their performance was reevaluated using a competency checklist. Finally, participants completed a post-session questionnaire, which included satisfaction ratings and self-confidence regarding their ability to perform the procedure.

All assessments in this study were conducted by a single instructor (the first author, PHL) who was responsible for both teaching activities and performance evaluations. To maintain blinding, the instructor was unaware of the group allocation; thus, assessments were conducted without bias regarding study groups.

Outcome measurement

Before the one-on-one instructional session, each participant underwent pre-training assessment wherein they performed a complete ultrasound-guided CVC insertion, without any prior guidance or prompts. The instructor evaluated their performance using an objective checklist (total score 0–34 points; Supplement 2, UGCVCP-CAT), categorizing each procedural step into three levels: not achieved, partially achieved, or fully achieved. Additionally, the instructor provided a global rating score (range: 1–10 points) that reflected an overall impression of the participant’s procedural skill and completion quality.

Primary outcomes during pre-training included three metrics. First, the total checklist completion score, which represents the sum of all completed procedural steps. Second, the global rating score, indicating a holistic assessment of procedural fluidity and overall competency. Third, the time elapsed during the pre-training assessment (total duration taken to complete the procedure).

The post-training assessment, with independently performed ultrasound-guided CVC reevaluated using the competency checklist, generated the post-test score. The differences between the pre- and post-training scores (checklist and global rating) were recorded to objectively assess the post-training skill acquisition and procedural improvement.

Before and after the training, self-rated confidence scores (range: 1–10) for independently performing ultrasound-guided CVC were recorded. Changes in confidence scores pre- and post-training quantified confidence improvement.

Statistical analysis and data presentation

Continuous variables were reported as median (IQR), whereas categorical data were represented as counts (percentages). Given the small sample size and the results of normality tests which indicated that the data did not follow a normal distribution, non-parametric statistical methods were applied. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test when all expected cell counts were ≥ 5; otherwise, Fisher’s exact test was applied. For continuous variables, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparisons among the three groups whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for comparisons between two independent groups. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for paired sample pre- and post-training comparisons. A significance level of α = 0.05 was set for general hypothesis testing. The Bonferroni correction for multiple testing was applied, with an adjusted significance level of 0.0167. All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.13) and NumPy package (version 2.3.0).

Results

Comparable baseline characteristics

A total of 70 participants were randomly assigned to three groups: First-Person POV Video (N = 23), F-t-F Side-View Video (N = 23), and Control (N = 24). Baseline characteristics were balanced across groups (Table 1), without significant differences in sex (p = 0.91), age (p = 0.28), or PGY level (p = 0.62). Most participants had limited prior CVC experience: 60.0% had never performed one independently (p = 0.65), and 52.9% lacked ultrasound-guided CVC experience (p = 0.42). Video engagement verification showed that 86.96% correctly identified the cartoon character at the video’s end (p = 0.67). Pre-class video viewing time varied but did not differ significantly (p = 0.22), with most participants watching the video at least once. Overall, no significant baseline differences were found among the three groups, which ensured comparability for subsequent analysis.

POV video group performed better in pre-training independent ultrasound-guided CVC insertion assessment

All numerical values for pre- and post-training performance metrics, including global rating scores, checklist totals, confidence ratings, and assessment times, were summarized in Table 2. The pre-training global rating score significantly differed among the three groups (p < 0.001), with the highest median score (8.0 [6.5–8.0]) in the POV group, compared to the F-t-F side-view (6.0 [6.0–7.0]) and control (4.5 [3.0–6.0]) groups (Fig. 3A; Table 2). After training, all groups showed improvement; however, the POV group retained the highest median post-training score (9.0 [9.0–9.5]) compared to the F-t-F (8.0 [8.0–9.0]) and control (8.0 [6.75–8.0]) groups, with a significant difference (p < 0.001). However, the overall improvement in global ratings was smaller in the POV and F-t-F groups (2.0 points each) compared to the control group (2.5 points), suggesting that initial pre-training exposure to instructional videos contributed to higher baseline performance (p < 0.01; Fig. 3C; Table 2).

The pre-training total checklist score differed significantly across groups (p < 0.001, Fig. 3B; Table 2), with the POV group having the highest median score (31.0 [29.0–33.0]) followed by the F-t-F group (30.0 [28.0–31.5]) and the control group (25.0 [17.25–29.0]). After training, all groups achieved a near-maximal checklist score of 34.0 points, with a statistically significant improvement (p < 0.01). However, the degree of improvement was largest in the control group (7.5 points), followed by the F-t-F group (4.0 points) and the POV group (3.0 points) (p < 0.001; Fig. 3D; Table 2), which further indicated that those without initial video exposure benefited the most from the hands-on training session.

A strong positive correlation was observed between the global rating scores and the total checklist scores (r = 0.78, p < 0.001), which indicated good concordance between subjective expert ratings and objective step-based performance measures.

Trend of shorter pre-training assessment time in the POV group vs. control group

The total time spent completing the pre-training assessment did not differ significantly among the three groups (p = 0.24). The POV group had a shorter median completion time (18.33 [16.02–22.05] minutes) compared to the control group (21.62 [18.74–23.43] minutes; Fig. 4A; Table 2). This difference was statistically significant at the uncorrected threshold (p = 0.04, α = 0.05); however, after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (corrected α = 0.0167), it did not reach statistical significance.

In the video intervention groups, participants were asked how many times they watched the video, and a specific cartoon character was shown at the end of each video. Participants who had truly completed watching the video at least once were expected to correctly identify this character in the subsequent questionnaire as a validation of video completion. We conducted an additional as-treated analysis by reclassifying participants who either answered incorrectly or did not answer the “cartoon character” question into the control group. The time elapsed during the pre-training assessment (minutes) was then re-analyzed accordingly. The results showed median (IQR) times of 22.09 (18.74–24.79), 19.71 (17.42–23.50), and 18.22 (15.78–21.85) minutes for the Control, F-t-F video, and POV video groups, respectively, with a statistically significant reduction in the POV group compared to Control (p = 0.047). Notably, three participants in the POV group and one participant in the F-t-F group were reclassified owing to incomplete video viewing. After this adjustment, the POV group demonstrated a significantly shorter time to completion of the pre-training phase, indicating faster completion of all checklist requirements during the initial operation (Fig. 4B; Table 2).

Pre-training self-rated confidence differed significantly among groups (p = 0.01), with the POV and F-t-F groups reporting higher confidence levels (6.0 [4.5–7.0] and 6.0 [5.0–7.0], respectively) compared to the control group (4.0 [1.75–5.25]; Fig. 5A; Table 2). Post-training, all groups showed increased confidence, with the POV group achieving the highest median score (8.0 [7.0–9.0]), followed by the F-t-F group (8.0 [7.0–8.0]) and the control group (7.0 [6.75–8.0]) (p < 0.01; Fig. 5B; Table 2). Discussion

This randomized controlled study examined the effectiveness of pre-class instructional videos for ultrasound-guided central venous catheterization (CVC) training and compared two filming perspectives: first-person (POV) and face-to-face (F-t-F). Pre-class videos significantly improved learners’ baseline performance and confidence. Among the two perspectives, the POV group achieved the highest pretraining global rating and checklist scores, indicating a potential advantage in procedural fluency and visuospatial understanding.

Pre-class videos help learners visualize procedural sequences and probe–needle coordination. This aligns with previous studies suggesting that video-based pretraining accelerates early skill acquisition and allows instructors to focus on refining advanced techniques during one-on-one sessions. The superior baseline performance observed in the video groups supports the concept of “flipped procedural learning,” in which multimedia instruction precedes simulation-based practice to optimize teaching efficiency. In a survey-based study, students reported that pre-class ultrasound videos were highly beneficial for understanding procedural steps and probe handling before practical assessments [12]. A recent RCT comparing a video-based student-tutor model (ViST) with a faculty–led course found no difference in examination scores, suggesting that video instruction may serve as an effective backup when instructors are limited [3]. In our prospective study, we similarly observed that access to pre-course videos allowed learners to acquire the essential procedural sequence and key techniques before one-on-one instruction, resulting in faster pre-test completion times and higher pre-test scores. This, in turn, enabled instructors to focus feedback on correcting residual weaknesses, thereby helping students achieve higher checklist scores with reduced teaching time.

This study focused on a complex, skill-based teaching activity in which trainees are required to perform real-time ultrasound scanning while simultaneously manipulating a needle to accurately access the target central vein. This dual task imposes higher technical difficulty and cognitive load than scanning-only training. In the surgical field, a 2023 meta-analysis of 22 studies found that video-based surgical education significantly improved knowledge or skill scores in approximately two-thirds of the included studies, underscoring the instructional potential of procedural videos [13]. An RCT on skin suturing compared a first-person–view (FPV) live streaming method with a F-t-F teaching method and found no significant difference in Direct Observational Procedural Skills (DOPS) checklist scores between groups [14]. Similarly, in laparoscopic knot-tying training, adding a first-person perspective capturing the operator’s hand movements did not enhance the learning curve compared with showing only the endoscopic view, although trainees perceived the FPV as beneficial for learning new laparoscopic skills [15]. In the context of CVC training, an RCT demonstrated that video-based learning was more effective than lecture-based learning for teaching ultrasound-guided CVC placement to anesthesia and critical care residents [16]. More recently, a Canadian study compared traditional video modeling with immersive video modeling—featuring a 180-degree wide field of view—and found no significant difference in NASA Task Load Index scores, procedural times, or entrustment ratings between the study groups [6]. Our results extend this evidence to ultrasound-guided invasive procedures requiring simultaneous interpretation of sonographic images and precise needle manipulation. The POV group showed shorter completion times than controls, suggesting immersive perspectives may improve workflow efficiency. Although not significant after correction, the trend supports that first-person visualization reduces cognitive load and improves motor sequencing.

Pre-class videos elevated initial competence, but improvement after direct instruction was greater in the control group—likely a ceiling effect. All groups achieved similarly high post-training scores, underscoring that instructor-led practice remains critical for consolidating skill. Pre-class videos, however, can elevate readiness and reduce instructor time needed to bring novices to competence.

Instructors noted that control-group students required repeated demonstrations of fundamental steps such as probe positioning, needle alignment, and image interpretation. In contrast, those who viewed pre-class videos, particularly in the POV group, entered training with a clearer procedural framework, allowing focus on fine motor control, ergonomics, and complication prevention. Thus, pre-class videos reduced instructor workload while maintaining feedback quality.

Research in augmented-reality and smart-glass systems has corroborated the value of seeing the image in situ. A crossover trial that superimposed real-time sonographic images in an AR headset shortened key steps of central-line placement and reduced NASA-TLX cognitive-load scores, particularly mental demand and frustration [7]. Smart-glass live streaming of the tutor’s view during knot-tying yielded high student usefulness ratings and facilitated delayed self-reflection [17]. Even without image overlay, 3-D GoPro recordings have shown excellent stability and simplicity for recording complex flap harvests, which suggests a low-cost path to scalable first-person content creation [18]. Collectively, these innovations imply that wearable or immersive displays can reinforce the educational benefits of first-person positioning by minimizing gaze shifts and split attention.

This study has some limitations. First, cognitive load and long-term retention were not formally assessed; future studies should incorporate validated metrics to quantify mental effort. All pre- and post-test assessments during the hands-on sessions were conducted using phantoms and clinically realistic ultrasound simulators, without additional wearable devices; we therefore elected not to introduce further measurement tools, such as the NASA Task Load Index, to avoid disrupting procedural flow. This cross-sectional evaluation focused on checklist performance, time efficiency, satisfaction, and confidence. Future work could employ longitudinal designs to determine whether first-person pre-session video training induces measurable changes in direct patient outcomes, as suggested in systematic reviews of video-based surgical education [13]. Long-term follow-up could help clarify whether such training modalities influence skill retention or decay over time—an area highlighted as critical in simulation-based procedural training research [19]. The answers to these questions will provide stronger evidence for the sustained clinical impact of immersive video approaches in POCUS education.

Second, the sample size of this study was modest. Although the planned sample size was 72 participants based on a priori power analysis, only 70 completed the study due to two withdrawals (2.8%). This small reduction may have slightly decreased statistical power, although the impact was likely minimal. Participants were recruited on a voluntary basis from a non-mandatory training course. As a result, the cohort may have had higher intrinsic motivation and engagement than the general trainee population, introducing potential self-selection bias and limiting the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, although randomization minimized baseline differences, unmeasured factors such as individual learning experience, prior informal exposure, background knowledge or self-directed practice may have influenced performance. These factors should be considered when interpreting the findings and their generalizability.

Third, video exposure was verified using a pre-class confirmation question; however, this approach ensured access to the video but did not guarantee complete or attentive viewing. Detailed viewing analytics (e.g., watch-time data) were not available.

All assessments were conducted using ultrasound phantoms rather than live patients, which may limit the direct applicability of these findings to clinical performance. Although a single instructor ensured consistency of evaluation, this approach could still introduce observer bias, despite blinding to group assignment. In addition, the study primarily examined immediate post-training outcomes, without assessing skill retention or decay over time.

Finally, as a single-center study involving PGY trainees, institutional training culture and instructor experience may influence outcomes. The generalizability of the results to other learner groups and institutional settings may be limited.

In conclusion, pre-class instructional videos enhanced the effectiveness of ultrasound-guided CVC training. Both POV and F-t-F perspectives improved pre-training competency and confidence compared with no video, with comparable checklist scores and procedural efficiency. The POV group achieved higher global rating scores, suggesting a potential benefit of the immersive first-person perspective. Integrating pre-class videos into procedural training constitutes a practical, scalable strategy to strengthen skill acquisition, with POV recordings adding value.
