Beyond the floodwaters: women’s multidimensional vulnerability to flood disasters – a scoping review
Authors: Farah Khalida Abdul Rahman, Nor Aliya Ayub, Rozita Hod, Rosnah Sutan, Idayu Badilla Idris, Nurfashareena Muhamad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFlood disasters, intensified by climate change, are increasing in frequency and severity globally, disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations, particularly women. Women’s vulnerability during flood disasters extends beyond biological or physical differences and is shaped by broader social, economic, cultural, and institutional conditions. However, synthesised evidence on women’s vulnerabilities in flood contexts remains limited.MethodsA scoping review was conducted using the Arksey and O’Malley framework and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines. A systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science (WoS), and Scopus databases identified 19 studies published between 2020 and 2024 that met the predefined inclusion criteria. Studies were eligible if they examined women’s vulnerabilities in the context of flood disasters and were original peer-reviewed articles. Data were extracted and analysed thematically to identify patterns and relationships among factors contributing to women’s vulnerabilities.ResultsFive interacting vulnerability domains were identified: physical (including sexual and reproductive health), social, psychological, economic, and environmental. Evidence across studies indicated that these domains are influenced by broader structural conditions such as gender norms, governance exclusion, and socioeconomic inequality, which may interact across exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity to shape compounded gendered flood impacts. In addition, findings highlighted cross-domain patterns of adaptive capacity, where women employed context-specific strategies to respond to constrained conditions. Based on this synthesis, a conceptual framework was developed to illustrate how structural conditions relate to vulnerability processes, adaptive responses, and disaster outcomes.ConclusionWomen’s vulnerability to flood disasters reflects a complex interplay of multidimensional risks shaped by gendered roles, socioeconomic disadvantage, limited resource access, and gaps in disaster planning and response. While caregiving responsibilities and health risks increase direct exposure, structural constraints may limit coping and recovery capacity. At the same time, adaptive capacity is actively expressed but often constrained and underrecognised within formal disaster systems. The proposed framework positions gender as a cross-cutting determinant and offers a conceptual lens for identifying policy entry points for gender-responsive disaster risk reduction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-026-04683-w.

Background

Globally, floods are the most frequent type of disaster of natural origin, accounting for an estimated 42% of the 10,320 disasters recorded worldwide over the past 30 years [1, 2]. Climate change is intensifying the frequency and severity of flood events, leading to immediate damage to property and infrastructure as well as long-term consequences for public health, livelihoods, and social cohesion [3]. These impacts are expected to worsen with increasing heavy rainfall events and continued challenges in limiting global warming to below 1.5 °C [4]. Flood disasters have profound and far-reaching consequences for affected populations, particularly for vulnerable groups such as women [5]. Given their frequency, increasing severity under climate change, and far-reaching social impacts, floods provide a particularly relevant context for examining the multidimensional nature of women’s vulnerability.

Vulnerability to climate extremes, defined as a combination of exposure, susceptibility, and limited capacity to withstand climate stressors, varies across different socioeconomic groups [6]. Evidence from disaster research indicates that women are more vulnerable than men in terms of preparedness levels, morbidity, and mortality during disasters [7, 8]. Although disaster scholarship increasingly recognises vulnerability as socially and structurally produced, existing literature rarely synthesises how upstream determinants interact across domains to shape women’s vulnerability in flood contexts. Most existing studies focus on general disasters or entire communities, with women’s experiences often underrepresented or insufficiently analysed [9–11]. There is limited research specifically examining the unique impacts of floods on women, leaving a critical gap in understanding their distinct vulnerabilities. Accordingly, this scoping review synthesises evidence on multidimensional vulnerability processes affecting women during flood disasters and develops a conceptual framework to illustrate how structural conditions may relate to vulnerability pathways and disaster outcomes.

Methodology

The study was designed as a scoping review using peer-reviewed articles retrieved from international electronic databases. This review followed the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley [12] and was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines [13].

Research question and eligibility framework

The central research question was: “What are the vulnerabilities of women during flood disasters?” To structure the review process and define the eligibility criteria, the Population–Exposure–Outcome (PEO) framework was applied.

The population of interest comprised women affected by flood-related disasters, as identified or defined within each included study. Studies involving mixed populations were also eligible if findings relating specifically to women were reported or analysed separately, enabling the review to capture gender-specific vulnerability patterns across diverse study settings. No age-based exclusion criteria were applied during study selection.

The exposure of interest included flood-related events, such as flash floods, river floods, urban flooding, coastal flooding, and tsunamis. Tsunami events were operationally classified as water-related coastal inundation disasters due to their comparable mechanisms of large-scale water displacement, infrastructure disruption, and gendered vulnerability dynamics.

The outcomes of interest were women’s vulnerabilities associated with flood disasters, including physical, social, psychological, economic, and environmental dimensions. The PEO framework was used as it is well-suited for qualitative and mixed-methods research questions, allowing a structured approach to exploring population-specific vulnerabilities.

Accordingly, studies were eligible for inclusion if they were original peer-reviewed research articles published between January 2020 and November 2024. The publication timeframe was selected to capture recent evidence reflecting flood risk patterns and evolving vulnerability dynamics in the context of climate change. It also aligns with the implementation period of the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015–2030), enhancing the relevance of the findings to contemporary disaster risk reduction priorities.

Studies were excluded if they were reviews, editorials, bulletins, government reports, conference abstracts, or other forms of non-peer-reviewed literature; did not report women-specific findings; or were unrelated to flood-specific disaster contexts.

Information sources and search strategy

Three electronic databases (PubMed, Web of Science [WoS], and Scopus) were systematically searched. These databases were selected due to their extensive interdisciplinary coverage of public health, disaster research, environmental sciences, and social sciences literature. Together, they provide broad indexing of high-quality peer-reviewed journals relevant to gender and disaster vulnerability research. To enhance comprehensiveness, manual screening of reference lists of included studies was also conducted.

The search strategy was structured according to the PEO framework, prioritising terms relating directly to women and female populations, and included combinations of keywords such as “women”, “flood*”, “vulnerab*”, “socioeconom*”, “access”, and “psychological”. While the term “gender” may identify relevant studies, it was not used as a primary search term because the review specifically focused on women’s vulnerabilities during flood disasters. The complete search strategies are presented in Table 1. No language restrictions were applied. The final search was conducted on November 7, 2024.

Search Strategies for Related Articles

Study selection process

All retrieved records were exported to EndNote reference management software for duplicate removal and tracking. A total of 1,846 records were initially retrieved. After the removal of 463 duplicate records, 1,383 articles remained and underwent a three-stage screening process. First, titles were screened and articles that did not reference women, floods, or vulnerability were excluded. The remaining records then underwent abstract screening against the predefined inclusion criteria. Finally, full-text articles were assessed for eligibility based on relevance to the Population–Exposure–Outcome (PEO) framework and the established inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Two independent reviewers, FKAR and NAA, conducted the screening process. Any disagreements were resolved by consensus. From 1,383 records screened, 1,347 were excluded, leaving 36 reports sought for retrieval. Three reports were not retrieved, and 33 were assessed for eligibility. Fourteen studies were excluded at the full-text stage (6 due to unrelated populations and 8 lacking a vulnerability focus), resulting in 19 studies included in the final synthesis.

Data charting process

Data extraction was performed using a standardised Excel spreadsheet developed specifically for this review. The extraction framework was informed by the PEO model and captured information on study characteristics, including authorship, publication year, and country of origin, as well as study design and sample characteristics. Data were also extracted on the types and dimensions of vulnerabilities explored, together with key findings relating to women’s experiences and the conclusions reported by each study.

Two reviewers (FKAR and NAA) independently extracted data from all included studies. Following independent extraction, the reviewers compared entries to ensure consistency and completeness. Any discrepancies were discussed and resolved through consensus. Where necessary, a third reviewer, RH, was consulted to ensure methodological rigor and alignment with the research objectives. The charting process was iterative, allowing refinement of categories as familiarity with the literature increased. This approach ensured that emerging vulnerability patterns were systematically captured while maintaining consistency across studies.

Data synthesis and presentation

A thematic analysis approach was employed to synthesise findings across the included studies. The process was primarily inductive, while also being guided by established disaster vulnerability frameworks to support conceptual organisation.

First, extracted findings related to women’s flood experiences were reviewed repeatedly to achieve familiarization with the data. Initial codes were generated at the study level to capture recurrent patterns, such as disruptions in maternal healthcare, restricted evacuation decision-making, loss of informal income, and shelter-related insecurity. Coding was conducted manually using Microsoft Excel, enabling systematic categorisation and comparison of text segments across studies.

In the second stage, codes were examined for similarities and conceptual overlap. Related codes were clustered into broader subthemes (e.g., sexual and reproductive health service disruption, domestic role burden, financial exclusion, violence exposure, infrastructure limitations). These subthemes were subsequently consolidated into five overarching vulnerability domains: physical (including sexual and reproductive health), social, psychological, economic, and environmental.

These domains were informed by existing disaster vulnerability frameworks and operationalised based on measurable indicators reported in the included studies. Specifically, physical vulnerability refers to direct exposure to health and survival risks, including indicators such as health impacts, food insecurity, and access to essential resources. Social vulnerability reflects relational and societal constraints, encompassing participation in decision-making, gender roles, and cultural norms. Psychological vulnerability captures the mental and emotional impacts of disaster exposure, including emotional distress, fear, and exposure to violence. Economic vulnerability relates to financial stability and livelihood security, including income loss, financial access, and livelihood disruptions. Environmental vulnerability refers to the physical and built environment, including housing conditions and infrastructure-related risks. Full details of the operational indicators used to classify findings are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

To enhance credibility, theme development was reviewed collectively by the research team, including senior authors with expertise in public health and disaster risk reduction. This process ensured conceptual clarity, minimised individual bias, and strengthened analytical consistency. Synthesised findings were then presented narratively and in tabular form, integrating both descriptive patterns and contextualised insights.

Study Characteristics

Within Asia, the studies were conducted in Bangladesh (3 studies, 16%) [17, 18, 21], Pakistan (2 studies, 11%) [26, 30], India (2 studies, 11%) [19, 28], Indonesia (2 studies, 11%) [25, 31], Japan (1 study, 5%) [16], Nepal (1 study, 5%) [24], Vietnam (1 study, 5%) [15] and Iran (1 study, 5%) [27]. In Africa, the studies were conducted in South Africa (3 studies, 16%) [14, 23, 29], Kenya (1 study, 5%) [22], Zimbabwe (1 study, 5%) [5], and Malawi (1 study, 5%) [20].

The studies employed various methodological designs, including qualitative research (13 studies, 68%), mixed-methods approaches (4 studies, 21%), and quantitative studies (2 studies, 11%). The study populations predominantly focused on women in flood-affected communities, with specific attention to subgroups such as single mothers, widows, elderly women, and pregnant and lactating women, particularly those from low-income or flood-prone areas. Additionally, some studies included men to provide insights into gendered disparities and women’s experiences.

Frequency of identified vulnerability subthemes

Vulnerability patterns differed across population subgroups. Physical and health-related vulnerabilities were more frequently reported among pregnant and lactating women, whereas widows, single mothers, and elderly women, faced distinct economic, social, safety, and mobility-related challenges. Detailed mapping of vulnerability domains, subthemes, evidence indicators, and contributing studies across all included studies is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Physical vulnerability

Physical vulnerability comprised six interrelated subthemes: direct physical health risks, sexual and reproductive health (SRH) disruption, differential vulnerability and mortality, food insecurity, limited survival capacity and access constraints.

Direct physical health risk and sexual reproductive health disruption

Direct physical health and sexual and reproductive health (SRH) risks constituted a key dimension of physical vulnerability. Physical health risks were identified in four studies (21%), primarily from qualitative and mixed-methods research conducted in flood-affected rural and community settings in Bangladesh, Pakistan, Vietnam, and Kenya, where women were frequently exposed to contaminated water, poor sanitation, and unsafe environmental conditions. These exposures contributed to infections, diarrhoeal diseases, respiratory illnesses, and other waterborne conditions [15, 18, 22, 26].

Sexual and reproductive health (SRH) disruptions were particularly evident in displacement and resource-constrained contexts in Nepal, Iran, Bangladesh, and Pakistan, where flood-related interruptions to healthcare services limited access to antenatal care, skilled birth attendance, and contraceptive services, particularly among pregnant and lactating women [24, 27, 30, 32]. A mixed-methods study found that 79.4% of births occurred at home during flood periods, with 6.0% involving miscarriage or infant mortality, while 64% of women experienced antenatal or postnatal service disruption [24, 32]. Qualitative evidence further highlighted increased maternal health risks, unintended pregnancies, and broader reproductive health challenges including impact on maternal and child nutrition in resource-constrained settings [24, 27].

Differential vulnerability and mortality

Differential vulnerability and mortality were consistently observed across five studies (26%) conducted in flood-prone settings in Bangladesh, Indonesia, Malawi, and Japan. Evidence from quantitative and mixed-methods studies demonstrated that women experienced higher levels of vulnerability compared to men across multiple indicators [16, 18, 20, 21, 31].

In Malawi, quantitative findings showed that female-headed households had significantly higher odds of extreme vulnerability compared to male-headed households [20], while longitudinal evidence from Japan further reinforced these patterns, demonstrating a 1.5-fold higher fatality rate among women, particularly with increasing age [16]. These patterns were especially pronounced among elderly women and female-headed households, highlighting how age and household structure intersect with gender to shape vulnerability outcomes.

Food insecurity

Food insecurity was a recurring concern among women in rural and low-income flood-prone communities in Bangladesh, Kenya, and South Africa [18, 22, 23]. Across these settings, women often reduced their own food intake to prioritise other household members, particularly children [18]. Quantitative findings from rural Bangladesh indicated a higher prevalence of starvation among women compared to men (44% vs. 17%), while qualitative evidence from Kenya highlighted increased nutritional vulnerability among pregnant and lactating women [22]. Disruptions to food supply and reduced household purchasing capacity further exacerbated food insecurity during flood events [18, 22, 23].

Limited survival capacity

In flood-affected communities in Zimbabwe and Indonesia, two qualitative studies (11%) highlighted gendered differences in survival capacity, particularly during rapid-onset flood events [5, 25]. Limited swimming ability, lower physical strength, and restrictive cultural clothing reduced women’s capacity to respond effectively during floods. Elderly women and those with caregiving responsibilities were disproportionately affected by these mobility constraints.

Access constraints

Restricted access to resources essential for health, safety, and survival was consistently reported across Bangladesh, Kenya, Pakistan, Nepal, and South Africa in qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods studies [22, 26, 29, 30, 32, 33]. Women faced barriers in accessing flood shelters, healthcare services, clean water, sanitation facilities, and disaster preparedness resources. In several settings, inadequate water and sanitation infrastructure forced women to rely on unsafe practices, particularly in displacement and low-resource environments [22, 26]. Additional constraints, including limited mobility, restricted access to technology, and reduced access to early warning systems, further impeded timely evacuation and access to essential services [22]. Rural, low-income, and female-headed households faced the most severe access barriers.

Social vulnerability

Social vulnerability was characterised by five interrelated subthemes: disaster risk reduction (DRR) exclusion, restrictive cultural norms, disproportionate domestic roles, limited family support, as well as overcrowding and lack of privacy in shelters.

Exclusion from Disaster Risk Reduction (DRR) processes

Exclusion from disaster risk reduction (DRR) processes was reported across nine studies (47%), conducted in Bangladesh, Nepal, Indonesia, and African settings [5, 15, 21, 22, 24, 29, 31–33], particularly among women in rural and low-income communities, widows, and female-headed households.

Gender norms and institutional practices limited women’s participation in preparedness planning, access to early warning information, and influence over resource allocation [16, 21]. Quantitative studies reported low levels of involvement in disaster planning and relief distribution, with fewer than half of women participating in preparedness-related decisions in some settings [24]. Qualitative evidence further indicated limited representation in local governance structures and community meetings [22, 27], reducing women’s opportunities to shape disaster preparedness and response activities [5, 15, 29, 33].

Restrictive cultural norms

Restrictive cultural norms were reported across seven qualitative studies (37%), conducted in flood-affected communities and informal settlements in Pakistan, Indonesia, India, and African settings [5, 19, 25, 26, 29–31]. These studies highlighted the influence of gendered expectations on women’s disaster experiences.

In some settings, women were required to obtain permission from male household members before evacuating, resulting in delayed responses to flood emergencies [25, 30]. Cultural expectations limited women’s participation in preparedness activities and restricted their access to relief resources and economic opportunities [5, 29]. Stigma further discouraged young women from accessing menstrual hygiene products distributed by male workers, reducing their ability to maintain basic hygiene during disaster conditions [26]. Traditional caregiving and domestic responsibilities also constrained women’s capacity to respond during disasters, particularly in conservative, rural, and low-income settings [5, 22].

Disproportionate domestic roles

Disproportionate domestic and caregiving responsibilities were reported in seven studies (37%) involving women from rural communities, informal settlements, and tsunami-impacted households, in Vietnam, Malawi, Zimbabwe, Kenya, and Indonesia [5, 15, 16, 20, 22, 25, 31].

Traditional gender roles placed primary responsibility on women for protecting children, older adults and ill family members during floods [5]. In many settings, these responsibilities increased substantially during disasters, as women were required to manage both caregiving and recovery-related tasks simultaneously [15, 20, 22]. In some contexts, women prioritised the safety of dependents over their own, increasing their exposure to flood-related risks [25, 31]. Mothers and women with multiple dependents bore the greatest caregiving burden during flood events.

Limited family support

Limited family support was found among widows and single mothers in flood-affected rural and low-income communities in India, Iran, and South Africa, based on three (16%) qualitative studies [19, 27, 29]. Women in this group often experienced reduced support from spouses or extended family members, resulting in increased caregiving and economic responsibilities during and after flood events [19, 27]. The absence of family support further intensified social isolation and limited their ability to access resources, participate in recovery activities, and maintain household stability [29].

Overcrowding and lack of privacy in shelters

Qualitative studies from South Africa and Iran reported overcrowded living conditions and lack of privacy in displacement and temporary shelter settings [14, 27]. Women were often accommodated in shared spaces, compromising privacy and affecting safety, dignity, and interpersonal relationships, particularly in resource-constrained environments.

Psychological vulnerability

Psychological vulnerability emerged as a multidimensional domain encompassing four interrelated subthemes: violence exposure, dual burden and caregiving strain, resource scarcity stress, and loss of hope, with heightened vulnerability observed among widows and women in displacement or shelter settings.

Violence exposure

Exposure to gender-based violence (GBV) was reported across seven qualitative and mixed-methods studies (37%) in Nepal, Pakistan, Iran, and South Africa [14, 19, 24, 26–28, 30]. Women experienced increased risks of physical, sexual, and verbal violence during and after flood events, particularly in overcrowded shelters, displacement camps, and insecure community environments.

Quantitative evidence reported GBV prevalence of up to 21% in disaster-affected settings, while many mothers expressed concerns about their ability to protect their children [24]. Qualitative findings further highlighted fear of harassment in flooded areas and increased vulnerability among widows living alone [19, 28]. In displacement settings, inadequate shelter conditions, including lack of privacy, poor security, and insufficient sanitation facilities, further increased exposure to violence, including sexual harassment and exploitation [14, 27, 30]. Adolescent girls also reported shame related to menstrual hygiene management in public shelters [26].

Dual burden and caregiving strain

Balancing income generation with unpaid caregiving responsibilities contributed substantially to psychological vulnerability. Qualitative and mixed-methods studies from Bangladesh, South Africa, India, Zimbabwe, and Indonesia reported psychological distress associated with managing household responsibilities, caregiving, and disaster recovery simultaneously [5, 17, 23, 28, 29, 31]. Women experienced anxiety, stress, and physical exhaustion associated with balancing household responsibilities, income generation, and disaster recovery [23, 28, 31]. Emotional strain was further intensified by limited control over resources and pressure to meet family needs, particularly in low-resource settings [5, 29]. Psychological strain was most acute among mothers and female-headed households managing multiple dependents.

Resource scarcity stress

Resource scarcity stress was reported among women in low-income urban settlements in South Africa, rural households in Nepal, and Kenya [22–24]. Quantitative findings demonstrated that loss of essential services, including access to clean water and electricity, was significantly associated with emotional distress (β = 0.37, p = 0.040) and anxiety related to resource loss (β = 0.40, p = 0.039) [23].

Qualitative evidence highlighted stress associated with resource shortages and unsafe living conditions, particularly where access to basic needs was disrupted [24]. In addition, resource scarcity contributed to household tension, including marital strain and conflict linked to time pressure and limited resource control [22].

Fear and loss of hope

In low-income and resource-constrained settings in South Africa, Indonesia, and India, women reported persistent fear of future disasters and uncertainty regarding recovery as reported in qualitative and mixed-methods studies [19, 29, 31]. This fear was often compounded by chronic financial strain and instability, contributing to emotional distress.

Qualitative evidence highlighted the psychological impact of losing homes, livelihoods, and a sense of stability [29, 31]. Among widows and socially vulnerable groups, feelings of hopelessness and despair were particularly pronounced, reflecting concerns about family survival and limited access to support systems [19].

Economic vulnerability

Economic vulnerability comprised multiple interrelated subthemes, including post-disaster income loss, limited financial access and support systems, limited financial independence, limited financial security and economic hardship.

Post-disaster income loss

Flood-related disruptions to agricultural land, infrastructure, and local markets resulted in substantial income loss among women in Bangladesh, Pakistan, Kenya, Indonesia, and South Africa [15, 21–23, 30]. This was reported in five studies (26%) and was particularly evident among women engaged in agriculture, informal labour, and part-time employment.

These losses were driven by reduced agricultural productivity, disrupted markets, and unsafe working conditions, which limited women’s ability to sustain income-generating activities [15, 22, 23]. In some contexts, women experienced complete income loss due to flooded fields and restricted mobility, further exacerbating economic instability during recovery [23, 30].

Limited financial access and support systems

Limited access to financial resources constrained recovery among female-headed households, single mothers, widows, and women in low-income communities in Zimbabwe, Malawi, South Africa, and Indonesia [5, 20, 29, 31]. Women in these settings often had limited savings, restricted access to credit, and reduced opportunities for income generation, especially where livelihoods depended on informal work or small-scale farming.

Gaps in external support systems further compounded these challenges. Evidence from three studies (16%) showed that government compensation schemes, social grants, and relief support were often insufficient, delayed, or poorly managed, limiting their effectiveness in supporting sustained household recovery [14, 19, 24]. In some cases, reliance on unstable labour and self-reliance strategies among widows highlighted the inadequacy of formal support mechanisms [14, 19]. In contexts of male outmigration, women were required to assume expanded financial and caregiving responsibilities, including managing households, agriculture, and finances independently, which further intensified long-term economic precarity [24].

Limited financial independence

Limited financial independence constrained recovery among women in low-income households and informal settlements in Bangladesh, South Africa, and India [15, 17, 18, 28, 29]. Dependence on male earners was reported in three studies (16%) [15, 17, 29]. Women often had limited control over financial resources and decision-making, with some studies reporting low bank account ownership and minimal influence over household financial decisions despite contributing to income [18, 28]. Limited land and property ownership among women, attributed to patriarchal norms, further constrained financial autonomy and long-term recovery capacity [22].

Limited financial security and economic hardship

Limited financial security was reported among women in Bangladesh, South Africa, and Japan, particularly among older women and those in low-income households [16, 18, 29]. Quantitative evidence indicated lower income levels and reduced financial stability among women compared with men [16, 18]. Many women were employed in unstable informal sectors, limiting income security and increasing vulnerability to financial shocks during and after flood events [29].

Economic hardship was evident among women in flood-affected communities, particularly in low-income settings in Pakistan and Bangladesh, as reported in qualitative studies [15, 30]. Women faced increased expenditures related to healthcare, housing repairs, and temporary shelter [15]. In extreme cases, financial strain was associated with adverse outcomes such as child marriage and human trafficking [30].

Environmental vulnerability

Environmental vulnerability comprised two interrelated subthemes: infrastructural deficits and unsafe housing conditions.

Infrastructural deficits

Infrastructural deficits were reported across five studies (26%) conducted in Bangladesh, South Africa, and Pakistan, particularly in rural and low-resource settings [14, 15, 17, 18, 23, 26]. The studies highlighted multiple structural deficiencies, including poor drainage systems, limited flood protection measures, damaged road networks, and restricted access to safe water and sanitation [18, 27, 29, 30]. These conditions increased flood impacts, contributed to physical isolation, and disrupted access to healthcare, markets, and other essential services [17]. Prolonged disruptions to basic utilities further affected recovery, including lack of access to clean drinking water for up to seven days among 86.9% of participants and unreliable electricity supply [23]. These conditions disproportionately affected women, as they increased caregiving burdens, exposure to environmental hazards, and constraints on mobility [18].

Unsafe housing conditions

Housing instability represented another environmental vulnerability pathway in flood-affected communities in Vietnam, South Africa, and low-income urban settlements, particularly among residents of informal and poorly constructed housing [14, 15, 23]. Evidence from three studies (16%) highlighted unsafe residential environments, including structurally weak dwellings, flood-prone construction, and recurrent housing damage. These conditions increased vulnerability to injury, displacement, and compromised safety and privacy during flood events [14, 15, 23].

Adaptive capacities and coping strategies

Alongside these vulnerability domains, the included studies documented a range of strategies employed by women to manage flood-related constraints, forming part of their overall vulnerability profile across flood contexts. A detailed summary of women’s adaptive strategies and capacities identified across the included studies is presented in Supplementary Table S2. These strategies span multiple dimensions and can be synthesised into three broad forms of response. First, knowledge-based adaptation is reflected in women’s use of indigenous environmental indicators, such as observing hill flowers or ecological changes, to anticipate flood onset in the absence of formal early warning systems [5, 20]. Second, resource improvisation was evident in responses to infrastructural gaps, including the construction of temporary sanitation facilities in displacement settings in Pakistan [26]. Third, livelihood and social resilience strategies included asset management, income diversification, fishing, and gardening to support household recovery following flood events [15, 18]. These strategies are further reinforced by strong social capital, characterised by solidarity, trust, and mutual support within communities [31].

Discussion

This review indicates that women’s vulnerability to flood disasters arises from interacting physical, social, economic, environmental, and psychological pathways shaped by structural inequalities. Flood exposure amplifies pre-existing gender disparities, transforming chronic disadvantage into acute disaster risk. Vulnerability and adaptive capacity are not opposing conditions but are co-produced within the same structural constraints that shape women’s disaster experiences. Importantly, vulnerability is not experienced uniformly but varies according to intersecting factors such as socioeconomic status, household composition, and life stage [34, 35].

These findings align with established vulnerability frameworks that conceptualise disaster risk as arising from interactions between exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity [36]. In flood contexts, environmental exposure intersects with gendered sensitivities, such as reproductive health needs and caregiving responsibilities, while adaptive capacity remains constrained by structural inequalities. Similarly, the Social Vulnerability Index identifies socioeconomic conditions, infrastructure access, and demographic characteristics as key determinants of disaster impact [37].

Extending these approaches, this review conceptualises gender as a structural determinant shaping vulnerability processes rather than a demographic descriptor, consistent with intersectional feminist frameworks that emphasise power hierarchies and systemic inequities in health and policy analysis [38]. Gender inequality functioned as a cross-cutting driver of vulnerability across all domains and was closely intertwined with economic disadvantage. Flooding further exacerbates poverty among women [39], with evidence showing women in poorer neighbourhoods experience deeper economic setbacks and slower recovery trajectories than men or women of higher socioeconomic status [40]. While higher socioeconomic status is often associated with greater disaster preparedness and adaptive capacity [41], lower-income groups are more likely to suffer substantial material losses and prolonged disruption [42].

These five domains interact through cascading and reinforcing mechanisms consistent with disaster risk cascade models [35, 43]. Environmental precarity can trigger cascading risk pathways extending into physical illness, economic loss, and psychological distress, demonstrating interconnected hazard impacts. Economic insecurity further constrains adaptive capacity by limiting access to health services, safe relocation, and recovery resources, while restrictive gender norms simultaneously shape evacuation capacity, aid access, and governance participation [44]. Consistent with the IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report, poverty, governance challenges, and limited access to essential services constrain adaptive capacity and increase vulnerability to climate-related hazards [34].

Women’s economic vulnerability is shaped by entrenched gender norms within patriarchal economic systems [45], which restrict access to stable income, assets, and financial autonomy, thereby constraining recovery capacity before and after disasters [46]. This is consistent with broader evidence that social determinants fundamentally shape disaster outcomes [47, 48].

Exclusion from preparedness planning and risk communication further limits women’s ability to influence decisions and access recovery resources [44, 49]. This exclusion reduces decision-making power and obscures women’s capacities and contributions, and even where inclusion exists, participation is often symbolic rather than substantive [50].

At the same time, findings indicate that women are not passive recipients of risk but active agents in disaster preparedness and response. This interpretation is supported by broader disaster literature demonstrating women’s proactive strategies such as preparing emergency kits, elevating household structures, and participating in community-based disaster response groups [51–53]. Under constrained conditions, they also adopt adaptive behaviours such as adjusting livelihood activities during flood events [54, 55]. Recognising and strengthening these capacities may contribute to more effective and inclusive disaster risk reduction strategies [44, 48, 56, 57].

Beyond economic, gender, and governance pathways, health system disruption represents a critical mechanism linking environmental hazards to adverse outcomes. In low-resource settings, such disruptions can become life-threatening [58–61]. Evidence from flood-affected contexts documents increased population-level morbidity alongside elevated maternal and neonatal risks among displaced populations. These outcomes are further shaped by individual-level factors such as pre-existing health conditions and pregnancy, as well as contextual determinants including poverty, inadequate shelter, and weak healthcare systems [62].

Broader climate–health research shows that continuity of essential services, including sexual and reproductive health care, is critical for preventing avoidable morbidity during environmental shocks [63–65]. For example, implementation of the Minimum Initial Service Package (MISP) in disaster-affected Pacific settings demonstrated that maintaining reproductive health services through pre-positioned supplies, trained providers, and community engagement can substantially mitigate maternal and neonatal risks during emergencies [64, 65]. From a systems perspective, health system resilience encompasses the capacities to absorb shocks, adapt to changing conditions, and transform structures that generate vulnerability. These dimensions, originally derived from ecological resilience theory and increasingly applied to health systems, are considered fundamental for maintaining service continuity during disasters and climate-related disruptions [66].

In addition to these health system challenges, displacement, livelihood loss, and insecurity following floods are associated with elevated psychological distress and potential intergenerational effects [67, 68]. Prenatal disaster-related stress has been linked to altered early child development, supporting life-course models that identify gestational environmental shocks as determinants of long-term health trajectories [69–71]. Empirical evidence further indicates that psychological distress can compromise economic productivity, with affected individuals more likely to experience increased sickness absence, reduced work performance, and underemployment [72].

Beyond individual-level health pathways, structural environmental conditions further shape disaster risk. Unsafe housing, overcrowded shelters, and inadequate sanitation increase exposure to secondary hazards and disproportionately affect women [44]. Evidence also shows that disparities in infrastructure access are socially patterned and associated with unequal health outcomes, reflecting and reinforcing broader structural inequalities [34, 73]. In disaster contexts, infrastructural vulnerability interacts with gendered social norms that shape mobility, safety, and access to resources, producing compounded risk environments for women.

These findings indicate that gendered vulnerability is generated through interacting social, economic, and institutional processes rather than isolated risk factors [34, 36, 37]. Women should not be viewed as a homogenous group. Vulnerability varied across subpopulations, including young girls, older women, widows, single mothers, female-headed households, and women living in rural, low-income, and informal settlement settings. Pregnant and lactating women were particularly affected by disruptions to sexual and reproductive health services. These intersecting characteristics shape how vulnerability manifests across domains [34, 35].

Taken together, these findings highlight the need for an integrative explanatory framework to clarify how interacting determinants generate gendered flood vulnerability. Accordingly, a conceptual framework was developed to synthesise these mechanisms into a coherent explanatory structure. This model is intended as an interpretive synthesis rather than a predictive model.

Conceptual framework of women’s vulnerability to flood disasters

To synthesise these findings into a structured explanatory model, Fig. 2 presents a conceptual framework of structurally mediated vulnerability among women in flood disasters, developed through the integration of empirical findings and established vulnerability science theory [36, 37]. Building on these foundations, the framework conceptualises vulnerability as arising from the interaction of exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity. Unlike conventional approaches that treat gender as a demographic attribute, this framework positions gender as a cross-cutting structural determinant shaping all vulnerability processes. This perspective reflects the heterogeneous nature of women’s vulnerability identified in this review, where intersecting social, economic, and demographic factors produce differential risk across population groups.

Building on this perspective, the framework conceptualises interconnected pathways linking structural drivers to disaster outcomes to support applied planning and evaluation. Specifically, it identifies upstream structural determinants, including gender and cultural norms, governance structures and exclusion processes, socioeconomic inequality, institutional fragility, and infrastructural deficits that shape interacting vulnerability domains spanning physical, social, economic, environmental, and psychological dimensions.

The ordering of vulnerability domains is for visual presentation only and does not imply a hierarchy, as the domains are interconnected and interact dynamically across disaster contexts. Similarly, although the framework is presented in a directional format for clarity, the pathways should not be interpreted as linear causal relationships. Rather, structural determinants influence exposure, access to resources, decision-making power, and adaptive capacity through interacting mechanisms that evolve across disaster phases. Governance operates as a cross-cutting influence that shapes resource distribution, participation in decision-making, and the effectiveness of disaster risk reduction systems, thereby affecting multiple vulnerability domains simultaneously [34, 35].

The framework further extends existing approaches by explicitly integrating health-related mechanisms, including sexual and reproductive health disruptions within physical vulnerability and intergenerational and psychosocial impacts within psychological vulnerability. These dimensions are rarely incorporated into disaster vulnerability frameworks despite growing empirical evidence supporting their relevance [63, 66]. These five vulnerability domains do not operate independently; rather, they interact through cascading pathways in which disadvantage in one domain amplifies risks in others, producing compounded gendered impacts during flood events. This interpretation aligns with disaster scholarship recognising risk as socially produced rather than hazard-driven alone [34, 35]. Gender-sensitive DRR approaches further emphasise the importance of addressing women’s differential exposure, capacities, and constraints while also recognising and leveraging their existing strengths in disaster preparedness and response [74].

However, policy implementation often fails to translate these principles into practice, particularly in addressing women’s socioeconomic and psychological needs [75]. The present framework helps explain these gaps by illustrating how interventions frequently target downstream risks while overlooking upstream structural determinants. For example, inadequate implementation of disaster risk management legislation has been shown to heighten vulnerability during major flood events such as Cyclone Idai [76]. Structural inequalities, including disparities in access to resources, education, decision-making power, and opportunities, continue to shape differential disaster impacts and recovery trajectories [44, 77].

The framework also highlights pathways through which interventions can reduce vulnerability by translating theoretical constructs into operational entry points. Specifically, it maps structural drivers to modifiable policy domains including governance inclusion, infrastructure planning, health system resilience, and social protection [44, 78]. Policies that institutionalise women’s participation in disaster governance, strengthen gender-responsive service provision, and improve access to information and training can simultaneously influence exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity [56, 79]. To effectively reduce women’s vulnerability, it is essential to address pre-existing “vulnerability bundles” through risk mitigation strategies aligned with the four priority areas of the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (SFDRR) [80].

Finally, the framework underscores the importance of data systems capable of capturing gender-differentiated risk. The systematic collection of sex-disaggregated disaster data is essential for designing targeted interventions that respond to the specific needs of women and girls, strengthening early warning systems, and ensuring equitable policy responses [35, 44].

The framework therefore provides a practical tool for policymakers, disaster risk reduction practitioners, and public health planners to design gender-responsive preparedness and recovery strategies. By identifying how upstream structural determinants generate interacting vulnerability domains, it supports multisectoral planning and enables targeted interventions at points where structural inequities amplify disaster risk.

Implications for policy, practice, and research

The implications vary across resource settings, reflecting differences in institutional capacity, health system resilience, and social protection infrastructure, consistent with the IPCC synthesis report on climate vulnerability [34]. In low- and middle-income countries, structural constraints such as limited infrastructure, weaker social protection systems, and constrained health service capacity may intensify vulnerability pathways and restrict adaptive capacity [81, 82]. In higher-income contexts, although institutional capacity may be stronger, vulnerabilities may persist through social inequality gradients, exclusionary governance processes, and differential access to risk communication and preparedness resources [83, 84]. These differences indicate that gender-responsive disaster risk reduction strategies must be context-specific, with priorities aligned to structural conditions rather than applied uniformly across settings [35, 85].

Effective risk reduction requires coordinated action across governance, health, economic, and infrastructure systems [34, 86]. Strengthening inclusive governance, resilient health systems, women’s economic security, and gender-responsive infrastructure is likely to yield the greatest gains in reducing vulnerability [35, 44, 87]. Incorporating women’s perspectives across all levels of disaster governance is critical to ensuring that policies and action plans reflect lived experiences and local realities [88], as emphasised in gender-responsive mechanisms such as the Gender Action Plan under the SFDRR [35].

To ensure adaptation planning is both relevant and inclusive, policy recommendations must be deliberately tailored to the heterogeneous needs of different groups of women. For example, for elderly women and persons with disabilities, early warning systems should move beyond generic broadcasts to include targeted approaches such as door-to-door notifications, which better accommodate mobility and sensory limitations [16, 20]. For young girls, disaster relief kits should prioritise menstrual hygiene management (MHM) supplies to prevent reproductive health risks and reduce school disruption following floods [21, 26]. For widows and displaced single mothers, targeted financial support, such as microcredit for women-led informal businesses, can strengthen household recovery, given their role as primary income providers [21, 28, 30]. In parallel, policies should not only address structural vulnerabilities but also recognise and strengthen women’s existing adaptive strategies and community-based resilience practices, including their roles in household preparedness, livelihood adaptation, and community-level risk communication [35].

Beyond policy and practice, the findings also highlight priorities for future research. Future studies should move beyond single-axis analyses to incorporate intersectional perspectives, including ethnicity, disability, age, pregnancy status, and minority identity, to better capture the complexity of women’s vulnerability to flood disasters. Longitudinal research is also needed to examine long-term physical and psychological health outcomes, as well as to evaluate the effectiveness of gender-responsive disaster risk reduction interventions across different sociocultural contexts [38, 89].

Strengthening gender-sensitive DRR also requires a broader evidence base that integrates peer-reviewed research with grey literature, including government plans, policy briefs, and NGO programme reports. Established frameworks, such as the Sendai Framework’s Gender Action Plan, UN Women’s guidance on inclusive recovery, and regional climate adaptation instruments, provide actionable models that can be adapted to national and local contexts. Leveraging these tools can support more inclusive, evidence-informed policies and enhance resilience among women in flood-prone settings [35].

Limitations

This review has several methodological and evidentiary limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the geographic distribution of included studies was concentrated in Asia and Africa, which may limit the transferability of findings to regions with different sociocultural, policy, and infrastructural contexts. This geographic concentration may reflect a high burden of flood exposure and vulnerability experienced in many parts of Asia and Africa, where recurrent flood events frequently intersect with socioeconomic inequalities and infrastructure constraints [1, 90, 91].

Second, the search was limited to three major databases (PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus). Although these databases provide broad interdisciplinary coverage, relevant studies indexed elsewhere may not have been captured. In addition, the exclusion of grey literature may have resulted in the omission of practice-based and policy-oriented evidence.

Third, a substantial proportion of included studies employed qualitative methodologies. While these approaches provide rich contextual insights, they may limit the generalisability of findings.

Finally, although this review identified heterogeneity in women’s vulnerability across several subgroups, the depth of intersectional analysis remains limited. While some studies reported multiple social characteristics (e.g., age, marital status, ethnicity, and disability), these factors were typically analysed in isolation rather than through an intersectional lens, limiting understanding of compounded vulnerability.

Conclusion

This review demonstrates that women’s vulnerability during flood disasters arises from interacting physical, social, psychological, economic, and environmental dimensions shaped by gendered roles, socioeconomic disadvantage, limited resource access, and systemic gaps in disaster planning. While caregiving demands and health risks increase direct exposure, structural inequalities and infrastructural constraints limit coping and recovery capacity, with impacts varying across different subgroups of women.

By conceptualising gender as a cross-cutting structural determinant and synthesising how upstream conditions may shape interacting vulnerability pathways, the proposed framework offers a structured lens for understanding compounded disaster risk and identifying intervention entry points within disaster risk reduction (DRR) systems.

These findings underscore the need for gender-responsive disaster strategies that engage women across preparedness, response, and recovery while addressing underlying inequalities that shape risk and recognising women’s roles as active agents in resilience and adaptation. Achieving equitable and sustainable resilience requires grounding interventions in both robust evidence and women’s lived experiences.
