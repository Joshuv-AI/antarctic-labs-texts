Plasmatron-assisted reforming of CO2 and CH4 under catalyst combined with steam to generate hydrogen-rich fuel gas
Authors: Chiu-Hsuan Lee, Je-Lueng Shie, Chih-Hua Chang, Min-Hao Yuan, Yi-Hung Chen, Manh Van Do, Ching-Yuan Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Synthesis gas (CO + H2), or syngas, is an environmentally friendly fuel that can be produced from coal, oil, natural gas, biomass, and solid recovered fuels (SRF). Methane (CH4) and carbon dioxide (CO2) are major greenhouse gases, and their reforming into syngas offers a promising approach for greenhouse gas mitigation. This study investigates the steam plasma-catalytic reforming of CH4 and CO2 using a thermal plasmatron coupled with catalysts and steam as a dehydrogenating agent to produce hydrogen-rich syngas. The effects of CH4/CO2 ratios (3/7–7/3), total flow rates (0.5–2.5 slpm), temperatures (573–873 K), steam injection (0–3 mL min−1), catalysts (Al2O3, Ni/Al2O3, Ni-Ce/Al2O3), and space velocities (3072–9215 h−1) were examined. Performance indicators included CH4 and CO2 conversions, CO and H2 selectivities, H2/CO ratio, H2 yield, and energy conversion efficiency (ECE). The Ni-Ce/Al2O3 catalyst improved CH4 and CO2 conversions from 95.12% and 86.22% to 98.33% and 91.7%, respectively. With steam addition, conversions and CO yield slightly decreased, but the H2 yield and H2/CO ratio increased markedly to 192.5% and 3.39, indicating high-purity hydrogen formation. The plasma energy demand can be supplied by renewable energy sources, and CH4 and CO2 feedstocks can be derived from biomass or waste incineration, highlighting the potential of this process for sustainable fuel production.

Introduction

Among various carbon management strategies, carbon capture and utilization technology (CCU) has attracted considerable attention because it offers opportunities to both mitigate CO2 emissions and transform carbon resources into commercially valuable chemicals.1 CCU is regarded as one of the key technological pathways for achieving net-zero emissions, as it can simultaneously offset direct emissions and facilitate carbon recycling through the production of value-added products.2 The production of synthesis gas (syngas) from captured CO2 is considered a practical pathway for the early deployment of CCU technologies. As an intermediate platform chemical, syngas can be further converted into a variety of fuels and value-added products. In addition, the H2-to-CO ratio can be tailored according to the requirements of subsequent conversion processes, providing considerable flexibility for chemical manufacturing.3 The production of syngas (a mixture of carbon monoxide (CO) and hydrogen (H2)) through the electrochemical reduction of carbon dioxide and water has emerged as an attractive strategy for promoting the green transformation of a wide range of industrial sectors.4 In 2022, the worldwide syngas market, encompassing hydrogen and ammonia production pathways that utilize syngas as an intermediate feedstock, was estimated at roughly 218 million m3 h−1.5 Continued expansion of this sector is anticipated, and the market is forecast to grow at a CAGR of around 6% through 2028.5 According to the International Energy Agency (IEA) (2021), achieving current net-zero emission targets requires the accelerated implementation of effective CCU technologies to ensure that global carbon emissions remain on a trajectory consistent with the 2050 climate objectives. In this context, the production of low-carbon syngas derived from carbon dioxide, together with the expansion of electrified transportation systems and hydrogen-based mobility solutions, is expected to play a vital role in reducing emissions across industrial operations and the transportation sector, thereby advancing overall decarbonization efforts.6,7

Syngas is an essential platform intermediate for the manufacture of a wide range of chemicals and sustainable fuels. Typical downstream products include ammonia, methanol, acetic acid, methyl formate, dimethyl ether, and various synthetic liquid fuels such as gasoline and diesel, highlighting its strategic importance in modern chemical and energy industries.8 While coal remains an important raw material for synthesis gas production, natural gas has become the principal source of syngas worldwide.9 The increasing preference for natural gas is largely attributed to its cost-effective processing routes, high conversion efficiency, and comparatively favorable environmental performance, making it an attractive feedstock for large-scale syngas generation.10 Synthesis gas can also be produced from biomass and even from organic wastes.11,12 Gasification is the thermo-chemical conversion of solid biomass or residues into product gas. As the fourth most significant energy source worldwide, biomass accounts for nearly 14% of global primary energy supply.13 Gasification offers a versatile conversion route for transforming biomass and waste materials into valuable intermediates, facilitating the establishment of integrated biorefinery platforms and supporting the transition to a more sustainable energy economy.13 Due to the similar physicochemical properties shared by many biomass resources, they can be converted into energy carriers and biobased products of comparable quality. In addition, biomass represents a sustainable carbon feedstock with inherently low sulfur content.11 The substitution of conventional fossil fuels with biomass-derived resources can significantly decrease greenhouse gas emissions, while the formation of sulfur oxides remains minimal. These attributes make biomass an attractive option for addressing global warming concerns and mitigating the environmental consequences of acid rain.12 The utilization of carbon dioxide as a gasification agent has attracted growing research interest in recent years due to its potential role in carbon utilization and resource recovery. From a process perspective, CO2-assisted gasification is categorized as an allothermal gasification system, where external heat input is required to sustain the endothermic gasification reactions.12 CO2 plasma reforming is another promising method, allowing the conversion of CO2 to chemicals. From a broader perspective, plasma-based reforming represents a sustainable alternative for carbon monoxide generation. Beyond improving the environmental performance of the production process, its inherent flexibility supports decentralized operation, reducing dependence on large-scale centralized infrastructure and expanding its suitability for diverse industrial and energy-related applications.

The growing concerns over greenhouse gas emissions and the economic impacts of rising fossil fuel prices have accelerated interest in biomass as a sustainable substitute for conventional fossil resources. As a result, biomass utilization has gained increasing attention in both energy and chemical production sectors. Major renewable biomass feedstocks include wood-based materials, dedicated energy crops, as well as residues generated from agricultural and forestry activities, all of which offer significant potential for renewable energy generation and resource recovery.11,12 Thermochemical routes such as combustion, pyrolysis, partial oxidation, gasification, and synthesis provide effective pathways for converting waste streams into valuable energy products, enabling enhanced resource recovery and energy utilization. However, most of the thermal applications of biomass will also lead to high emissions of CO2 and CH4, such as combustion, partial oxidation and gasification.14

CH4 and CO2 can be converted into valuable intermediates or products, for example, hydrogen, syngas, C2H4 and CH3OH15 to improve the efficiency of energy transformation. The dry reforming of methane (CH4) with carbon dioxide to generate syngas (CO and H2) has recently gained increasing interest owing to its dual advantages in resource utilization and environmental mitigation. Nevertheless, this process is strongly endothermic and generally requires intensified reaction strategies to achieve industrially relevant conversion rates. Catalytic systems and non-thermal plasma technologies have therefore been widely explored as promising enabling approaches. While reactor configurations for CH4–CO2 reforming are often derived from those employed in methane steam reforming, the major barrier to the industrial implementation of catalytic dry reforming remains carbon formation on the catalyst surface, which progressively results in catalyst deactivation and loss of activity.16–29

According to previous studies,30,31 the catalytic activity of group VIII metals in the methane and carbon dioxide reforming reaction follows the order of Ru, Rh, Ir, Ni, Pd, and Pt, while the tendency for carbon deposition follows the sequence of Ni, Pd, Pt, Ir, Ru, and Rh. Although Ru and Rh exhibit high catalytic activity with minimal carbon deposition, their scarcity and high-cost limit practical applications. Nickel (Ni) is relatively inexpensive; however, it suffers from severe carbon deposition during the reaction.30 A commonly adopted strategy to address this issue is the use of bimetallic catalysts composed of nickel and cerium. Cerium oxide can provide active oxygen species that oxidize carbon deposits on the catalyst surface,31 thereby extending the catalyst's lifetime.

This study aimed to convert CH4 and CO2 into syngas using a two stage plasma-catalytic reforming system. The effects of CH4/CO2 ratio, steam addition, catalyst type, and operating conditions on CH4 and CO2 conversion, syngas production, H2/CO ratio, and energy conversion efficiency were systematically investigated to determine the optimal reforming conditions.

In this study, the novelty lies in the development of a two-stage plasma–catalytic reforming system that integrates CH4–CO2–H2O co-reforming within a single controllable platform for syngas tuning, together with the application of a carbon-resistant Ni-Ce catalyst operating under plasma conditions. This integrated approach enables enhanced interaction between plasma activation and catalytic conversion, improved control of syngas composition (particularly H2/CO ratio), and increased resistance to carbon deposition, thereby providing a more flexible and stable pathway for CCU-based syngas production compared with conventional single-stage or separately optimized reforming systems.

Materials and methods

Plasmatron operational procedure

Plasma reforming experiments were performed using a carrier gas (N2) injected with temperatures ranging from 573 to 773 K from 10 kW power capacity of plasmatron shown in previous study32 and water flow rates of 1, 2, 3 mL min−1, which were heated to produce steam. The present work aims to systematically assess the performance of plasma-assisted CH4–CO2 steam reforming at different water feed rates and to elucidate the effects of key operating parameters on process efficiency and product distribution. Non-condensable gaseous products generated during the experiments were collected and analyzed using a gas analyzer in conjunction with gas chromatography coupled to a thermal conductivity detector (GC-TCD). For continuing feeding, CH4 and CO2 were injected into the reactor under the ratios of 4 : 1, 4 : 2, 4 : 3, 4 : 4, 4 : 6 and 4 : 8 at the total flow rates of 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2 and 2.5 slpm (standard liter per minute). The flow rate of the nitrogen carrier gas (N2, 99.99% purity), denoted as (QN), was regulated to the desired level within the range of 10–12 L min−1 under standard conditions (101.3 kPa and 293 K) using a rotameter. The plasma reactor was powered by a chopper-type power control unit supplied by Taiwan Plasma Corp., with the input power (PL) adjusted between 1 and 3 kW. These operating powers corresponded to reactor temperatures ranging from 573 to 873 K. Gaseous products were analyzed using a Thermo Scientific FOCUS gas chromatograph coupled with a thermal conductivity detector (GC-TCD) and fitted with a Supelco packed column (60/80 Carbon Xen-1000, 15 ft × 2.1 mm i.d.). The injector and detector were operated at 453 and 513 K, respectively. After injection, the column temperature was maintained at 513 K for 10 min. Helium served as the carrier gas in both columns A and B at a constant flow rate of 30 mL min−1, while the injection volume was fixed at 2 mL. Repeated experimental runs were performed to confirm the reproducibility of the measured parameters.

Chemicals

High-purity standard gases were employed for analytical calibration, including N2 (99.9999%) and CO2, CO, and H2 (99.95%). Quantitative determination of the gaseous products was performed using linear calibration relationships established from the responses obtained with these reference gases.

Catalyst production method

Commercial aluminum oxide (Al2O3) particles with diameters ranging from approximately 3 to 5 mm were used as the catalyst support, shown in Fig. S1. The support was purchased from Alfa Aesar (Lot: K14X016, CAS: 1344-28-1).

Nickel(ii) nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, purity 98%) and cerium(iii) nitrate hexahydrate (Ce(NO3)3·6H2O, purity 99.5%) were employed as precursor solutions. Metal loading on the Al2O3 support was conducted via impregnation and co-impregnation methods. Prior to impregnation, the Al2O3 particles were pretreated by washing with deionized water and subsequently dried.

Preparation of Ni/Al2O3 catalyst

A 50 wt% aqueous solution of nickel nitrate hexahydrate was prepared. The Al2O3 support was immersed in 250 mL of this solution for 30 minutes. The impregnated sample was then dried at 110 °C for 60 minutes, followed by calcination in a high-temperature furnace. The temperature was raised to 550 °C over 2.2 hours and maintained at this temperature for an additional 6 hours. The Ni content in 50 wt% nickel nitrate hexahydrate aqueous solution is the highest, which is 11.58%.

Preparation of Ni-Ce/Al2O3 catalyst

A mixed precursor solution of nickel nitrate hexahydrate and cerium nitrate hexahydrate was prepared with a molar ratio of 50 : 10. The Al2O3 support was immersed in 250 mL of this mixed solution for 30 minutes. The sample was then dried at 110 °C for 60 minutes and subsequently calcined in a furnace by heating to 800 °C over 2.2 hours, followed by a 6 hour dwell at the final temperature. The ratios of Ni and Ce in Ni-Ce/Al2O3 are 12.58 and 4.03 wt%, respectively.

The above catalysts were all reduced with 10% hydrogen before the experiment. The element contents are presented in Table S1. Fig. S2(a–h) presents the EDS(LEO 1530) spectra of the catalysts, indicate that the incorporation of Ce affected the deposition behavior of Ni species on the Al2O3 support. In addition, XRD (MAC Sience, MXP18) patterns of the catalysts shown in Fig. S3. These results confirm that both Ni and Ce species were successfully deposited on the Al2O3 support and suggest the formation of Ni-containing active phases and CeO2 species within the catalyst structure.

Results and discussion

Effects of feed rates of CH4 and CO2 alone on the reforming reaction

Fig. 1(a) illustrates the effects of methane feed rate on the conversion and selectivity in the plasma reforming process. The methane flow rates investigated were 2.00, 1.50, 1.00, 0.75, 0.50, and 0.25 slpm. After plasma reforming, hydrogen was identified as the major gaseous product, while carbon deposition was observed inside the reactor, indicating that methane was dissociated into hydrogen and solid carbon. As the methane feed rate decreased from 2.00 to 0.25 slpm, the methane conversion increased from 66% to 87%, and hydrogen selectivity improved from 64% to 80%. These results demonstrate that a lower methane feed rate enhances both methane conversion and hydrogen selectivity.

Effects of CH4 and CO2 flow rates on CH4/CO2 conversion and H2/CO selectivity using plasma reforming. Conditions: temp. = 673 K, power = 1.83 kW, N2 = 7 L min−1, operation time = 30 min. (a) Effects of CH4 flow rates alone on CH4 conversion and H2 selectivity. (b) Effects of CO2 flow rates alone on CO2 conversion and CO selectivity.

From the gas composition analysis, it was observed that when the methane feed rate increased from 1.00 to 2.00 slpm, the unreacted methane concentration sharply increased from 21 000 ppmv to 90 000 ppmv. Therefore, to maintain a high methane conversion while allowing for sufficient throughput, a methane feed rate of 1.00 slpm was selected as the optimal condition.

In Fig. 1(b), the influence of CO2 feed rate was also investigated under plasma-only conditions, with flow rates set at 2.00, 1.50, 1.00, 0.75, 0.50, and 0.25 slpm. After the reaction, a small amount of carbon deposition was detected, suggesting that the reaction mechanism likely involves the decomposition of CO2 into CO and O, followed by secondary reactions leading to carbon formation via CO → C + O.33 When the CO2 feed rate was reduced from 2.00 to 0.25 slpm, the CO2 conversion increased from 16.0% to 19.2%. This indicates that decreasing the CO2 feed rate promotes a higher degree of CO2 conversion. However, the selectivities of CO from CO2 reforming alone are all near 0%. Consideration of the following CH4–CO2 reforming tests at various molar ratios, the optimum operation of CH4 flow rate is controlled at 1 slpm.

Effects of methane and carbon dioxide feed ratio on the reforming reaction

Fig. 2(a)–(c) present the results obtained from experiments using mixed CH4 and CO2 feeds. The methane flow rate was fixed at 1 slpm, while the CO2 flow rate was varied to achieve different CH4/CO2 feed ratios of 4 : 8, 4 : 6, 4 : 4, 4 : 3, 4 : 2, and 4 : 1. The influence of these ratios on the conversion of CH4 and CO2, the selectivity toward H2 and CO, and the variation of product gas concentrations was investigated. The overall reaction mechanism can be expressed as CH4 + CO2 → 2CO + 2H2. As the CH4/CO2 ratio increased from 4 : 8 to 4 : 1, the CH4 and CO2 conversions rose from 67 to 91% and from 49 to 88%, respectively. Meanwhile, the selectivity toward H2 increased from 32 to 99%, whereas CO selectivity decreased from 96 to 68%. These results indicate that higher CH4/CO2 ratios promote greater methane and carbon dioxide conversions and higher hydrogen selectivity, but lead to reduced CO selectivity. Moreover, the CH4 conversion was consistently higher than that of CO2, which can be attributed to the formation of CO2 from CO reduction reactions.34,35

Effects of CH4/CO2 molar ratio on CH4 and CO2 conversions, H2 and CO selectivity, H2/CO ratio, and energy conversion efficiency (ECE) using plasma reforming. Conditions: temp. = 673 K, power = 1.83 kW, N2 = 7 L min−1, CH4 flow rate = 1 slpm, operation time = 30 min. (a) CH4 and CO2 conversion. (b) H2 and CO selectivity. (c) H2/CO ratio and ECE.

Although a higher CH4/CO2 ratio enhances both CH4 and CO2 conversion, the absolute amount of CO2 treated decreases correspondingly. In terms of product gas concentrations, the maximum CO concentration was observed at a CH4/CO2 ratio of 4 : 6, while the highest H2 concentration occurred at a ratio of 4 : 1. Considering energy conversion efficiency, the H2/CO ratio reached its maximum value at a CH4/CO2 ratio of 4 : 6. Therefore, based on plasma reforming studies of CH4 and CO2, and taking into account the issues of carbon deposition,36,37 CO2 utilization, and overall energy efficiency, the optimal CH4/CO2 feed ratio was determined to be 4 : 6.

Effect of temperature on the reforming reaction

To investigate the effect of plasma reactor temperature on the conversion of methane and carbon dioxide, as well as on the selectivity of hydrogen and carbon monoxide and the variation of gas concentrations, four operating temperatures (573, 673, 773, and 873 K) were selected for comparative analysis, as shown in Fig. 3(a)–(c). As the plasma temperature increases, gas molecules are more likely to undergo fundamental plasma reactions such as ionization, excitation, and dissociation. When the temperature rises from 573 K to 673 K, the CH4 and CO2 conversion rates increase from 79.6 to 95.1% and from 74.2 to 86.2%, respectively. Meanwhile, the selectivity of H2 and CO increases from 56.5 to 86.7% and from 72.7 to 90.0%, respectively.

Effects of temperature on CH4 and CO2 conversion, H2 and CO selectivity, H2/CO ratio, and energy conversion efficiency (ECE) using plasma reforming. Conditions: CH4/CO2 molar ratio = 4/6, total flow rate = 0.5 slpm, operation time = 30 min. (a) CH4 and CO2 conversion. (b) H2 and CO selectivity. (c) H2/CO ratio and ECE.

At temperatures above 673 K, the CH4 and CO2 conversion rates remain relatively stable at approximately 93% and 80%, while the selectivity of H2 and CO remains above 86% and 90%, respectively. The variation in gas concentration indicates that the product gas concentration at 673 K is higher than that at 773 K and 873 K. This is mainly attributed to the increased carrier gas flow rate required to achieve higher reaction temperatures, which leads to dilution of the product gas. Consequently, the highest energy conversion efficiency is achieved at 673 K. Considering energy consumption, conversion efficiency, product selectivity, and gas concentration simultaneously, 673 K is identified as the optimal operating temperature for this plasma reaction system.

Effect of steam on plasma reforming of methane and carbon dioxide

Fig. 4(a)–(c) illustrate the effects of introducing different amounts of water vapor (0, 1, 2, and 3 mL min−1) into the plasma reactor on the conversion of methane and carbon dioxide, the selectivity of hydrogen and carbon monoxide, and the variations in gas concentration. When the water vapor flow rate was 1 mL min−1, the CH4 and CO2 conversion rates decreased from 95.1 to 90.3% and from 86.2 to 58.9%, respectively. In contrast, the selectivity of H2 increased from 86.7 to 108.5%, while that of CO decreased from 90.0 to 81.2%. The H2 selectivity exceeding 100% indicates that additional hydrogen was produced through the dissociation of water vapor, suggesting that steam participates actively in the plasma-induced reactions.

Conditions: temperature = 673 K, power = 1.83 kW, N2 = 7 L min−1, CH4/CO2 molar ratio = 4/6, total flow rate (CH4 + CO2) = 0.5 slpm, operation time = 30 min. (a) CH4 and CO2 conversions. (b) H2 and CO selectivity. (c) H2/CO ratio and ECE.

When the water vapor flow rate was further increased to 3 mL min−1, the CH4 and CO2 conversion rates decreased to 84.5% and 64.3%, respectively, whereas the selectivity of H2 and CO reached 169.7% and 83.1%, respectively. Although the addition of water vapor leads to a decline in methane and carbon dioxide conversion rates, it significantly enhances hydrogen production. These results demonstrate that introducing steam into the plasma system effectively promotes hydrogen generation despite the reduced carbon conversion efficiency. The H2 yield exceeding 100% was mainly attributed to the additional hydrogen source provided by steam addition. After introducing steam into the system, the CH4 and CO2 conversion efficiencies as well as the CO yield decreased, while H2O and CO may have been consumed through secondary reactions with other species, leading to the formation of CH4 and CO2.

The H2 yield (>100%) increase observed after steam addition was likely associated with the occurrence of the steam reforming reaction (CH4 + H2O → CO + 3H2) and coke gasification reaction (C + H2O → CO + H2). In addition, the reverse Boudouard reaction (2CO → C + CO2) may also have occurred,38 which could partially explain the decrease in CO2 conversion. Furthermore, steam addition slightly improved the system's energy conversion efficiency (ECE).39

Effect of adding catalyst on reforming reaction

Fig. 5(a)–(f) illustrate the influence of coupling a two-stage plasma reactor with a catalytic reactor on the overall conversion performance. After 30 minutes of operation, the effects of three catalysts—Al2O3, Ni/Al2O3, and Ni-Ce/Al2O3—were compared. The CH4 conversion rates were 95.12%, 95.48%, and 98.33%, respectively, while the CO2 conversion rates were 86.22%, 88.11%, and 91.70%. The selectivity of H2 was 86.68%, 87.91%, and 87.64%, whereas the CO selectivity was 90.00%, 88.24%, and 90.10%, respectively. The H2/CO ratios were 0.84, 0.88, and 0.85, and the corresponding energy conversion efficiencies were 0.198, 0.206, and 0.215. Therefore, the addition of a Ni-Ce/Al2O3 catalyst significantly enhanced the conversion rates of CH4 and CO2 from 95.12% and 86.22% to 98.33% and 91.7%, respectively. The selectivities of H2 and CO also increased from 86.68% and 90.0% to 87.64% and 90.10%, respectively. Effect of space velocity on the reforming of CH4 and CO2 in plasmatron-assisted catalysts are shown in Fig. S4.

These results indicate that the addition of Al2O3 alone had a negligible effect on improving the conversion rates and selectivity. The Ni/Al2O3 catalyst slightly enhanced the overall conversion but caused a minor decrease in CO selectivity. Although CO is readily formed on Ni-based catalysts, a high energy barrier is required for its desorption into free CO molecules. The accumulation of CO on the catalyst surface consequently reduces the overall yield. Therefore, facilitating CO desorption is essential for improving the catalytic performance of nickel-based systems. Furthermore, although the dry reforming of methane and carbon dioxide is an endothermic reaction, the formation of CO and H2 on the Ni catalyst surface is thermodynamically more favorable than the formation of methanol or formaldehyde.

The highest H2/CO ratio was obtained using the Ni/Al2O3 catalyst, which may be attributed to incomplete CO desorption. Overall, the Ni-Ce/Al2O3 catalyst exhibited the highest energy conversion efficiency, demonstrating its superior catalytic performance and energy utilization capability in the plasma-assisted reforming process. In the Ni-Ce/Al2O3 catalyst, the conversions and concentration variations of CH4 and CO2 were more pronounced than those observed for Ni/Al2O3. After incorporating Ce into the Ni catalyst, the specific surface area was found to increase from 133 m2 g−1 to 227 m2 g−1, approximately doubling its value. This enhancement is presumed to contribute to the superior catalytic performance, with the ECE value also reaching its maximum upon Ce addition. These results demonstrate the outstanding performance of the Ni-Ce/Al2O3 catalyst.

Effect of steam addition on catalytic plasma reforming of methane and carbon dioxide

Fig. 6(a)–(f) present the effects of combining a two-stage plasma reactor with a catalytic reactor, along with the introduction of 3 mL min−1 of steam, on the conversion behavior of CH4 and CO2. After 30 minutes of operation, the results were compared as follows. The CH4 conversion rates over Al2O3, Ni/Al2O3, and Ni-Ce/Al2O3 catalysts were 85.1, 70.2, and 75.4%, respectively, while the CO2 conversion rates were 65.2, 33.0, and 38.4%, respectively. The H2 yields were 162.3, 179.4, and 192.5%, and the CO yields were 79.7, 70.8, and 66.6%, respectively. The H2/CO ratios were 1.96, 3.08, and 3.39, and the corresponding ECE values were 0.228, 0.183, and 0.204, respectively. When steam was introduced into the plasma system containing the Ni-Ce/Al2O3 catalyst, the H2 yield and H2/CO ratio increased markedly to 192.5% and 3.39, respectively, indicating the production of high-purity hydrogen.

Conditions: temperature = 673 K, power = 1.83 kW, N2 = 7 L min−1, CH4/CO2 molar ratio = 4/6, total flow rate (CH4 + CO2) = 0.5 slpm, space velocity = 4607.3 h−1, operation time = 30 min. (a) CH4 conversions. (b) CO2 conversions. (c) H2 selectivity. (d) CO selectivity. (e) H2/CO ratio. (f) ECE. [△] Ni-Ce/Al2O3, [□] Ni/Al2O3, [◇] Al2O3.

Overall, both CH4 and CO2 conversion rates exhibited a decreasing trend with increasing reaction time when Ni/Al2O3 and Ni-Ce/Al2O3 catalysts were applied, gradually stabilizing after approximately 20 minutes. Compared with the reaction using only the Ni-Ce/Al2O3 catalyst, the decline in CO2 conversion was the most significant, decreasing from 91.7 to 38.4%. Similarly, compared with the reaction with steam addition alone, the CO2 conversion also decreased from 86.2 to 38.4%. These results suggest that the simultaneous presence of steam and catalyst may suppress the conversion of CH4 and CO2. In contrast, both the yield and concentration of H2 were markedly enhanced in the presence of the catalyst. This improvement is likely attributed to the reaction between oxygen-active sites on the Ni-Ce catalyst and H2O, which generates additional H2.40–42 The promotional effect of Ce in Ni-based catalysts has also been widely reported in comparison with other Ce-promoted systems supported on different oxides, such as SiO2 and ZrO2, where Ce incorporation improves Ni dispersion, enhances catalyst reducibility, promotes oxygen mobility, and suppresses carbon deposition; however, these effects are strongly dependent on the nature of the support.43,44

The decrease in CO yield can be attributed to the occurrence of the water–gas shift reaction (CO + H2O → H2 + CO2) and the Boudouard reaction (2CO → C + CO2), which reduce CO2 conversion while enhancing H2 production. As shown in the temporal evolution profiles, H2 yield and concentration increased significantly between 10 and 20 minutes, indicating that the steam–species interactions were still developing and reached a steady state after 20 minutes. The CO yield fluctuated substantially within the initial 0–2 minutes, as CH4 and CO2 had just been introduced and their consumption was limited, stabilizing after 2 minutes.

Furthermore, the H2/CO ratio increased remarkably upon catalyst addition. In addition to the consumption of CO via the water–gas shift reaction,45 the increase may also be associated with the reactions CeO2−x + xH2O → CeO2 + xH246,47 and the interaction between Ni active sites and CH intermediates derived from CH4 (ref. 48 and 49) decomposition, both of which promote H2 formation. Consequently, the H2/CO ratio reached as high as 3.39. These findings indicate that the synergistic effect of steam and catalyst can effectively shift the syngas composition toward hydrogen production, demonstrating the high potential of this process for efficient and large-scale generation of high-purity H2.

Reaction mechanism of the reforming of CH4/CO2

Most of the thermal applications of biomass will lead to high emissions of CO2 and CH4, such as combustion, partial oxidation and gasification. The major reactions are mentioned below:

Water-gas shift reaction:10CO + H2O → CO2 + H2 ΔH = +41.2 kJ mol−1

Methane production reaction:11C + 2H2 → CH4 ΔH = −75 kJ mol−1

The reaction scheme presented above indicates that higher water concentrations favor the production of H2 and CO2. Moreover, the dominant reaction pathways are predominantly endothermic, requiring continuous heat or energy input to proceed effectively. Relative to conventional thermochemical treatment processes, plasma-assisted reactors provide enhanced reaction intensity due to the generation of highly energetic ions, radicals, and excited species, together with the capability of operating at substantially elevated temperatures. The required energy is supplied in the form of electricity and converted into plasma energy within the reactor. Subsequently, this energy is transferred to the reactants, initiating concurrent thermal and plasma-induced chemical processes that proceed synergistically within the plasma reaction zone. The alternative pathway corresponds to vibrational excitation. In the case of most non-electronegative species (e.g., N2, H2, CO, CH4, and CO2), electron-impact vibrational excitation occurs via transient intermediate states. This mechanism is considered highly efficient, as it requires electron energies lower than those necessary for direct bond dissociation, thereby favoring energy-efficient excitation processes. Nevertheless, the relatively high energy consumption of the process highlights the need for the development of high-performance catalysts to support its industrial deployment. In plasma-assisted catalytic dry reforming of methane, catalysts play a critical role by enhancing local electric fields, altering discharge behavior, and reducing the effective activation energy, which collectively improve process energy efficiency. At the same time, plasma exposure can modify the physicochemical properties of catalysts, improving reactant adsorption, strengthening metal–support interactions, and thereby enhancing overall catalytic performance. Consequently, the combination of plasma and catalyst generates a synergistic effect, with reaction mechanisms far more complex than those observed in conventional catalytic reforming or pure plasma reforming processes.53

Reducible metal oxides, particularly cerium oxide (CeO2), have been widely utilized as catalyst supports or promoters to engineer catalysts with a high density of oxygen vacancies. These oxygen vacancies (Vo) are commonly recognized as key active sites for CO2 activation on metal oxide surfaces, since they promote charge transfer processes and consequently lower the activation barrier associated with CO2 dissociation. The enhanced mobility of lattice oxygen (Ox) in ceria, together with the strong Ni–ceria interfacial interaction, contributes to the effective oxidation and removal of deposited carbon.56–59

Conclusion

Thermal plasma-assisted activation of CH4–CO2 reforming for syngas production is considered an efficient and environmentally benign technology, offering a promising route for the large-scale valorization of hydrocarbon resources and the conversion of greenhouse gases. This study provides a systematic investigation of catalytic dry reforming and steam reforming within the CH4–CO2 system and further examines in detail the influence of critical reaction parameters—such as gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), reaction temperature, steam feed rate, and catalyst composition—on catalytic performance and product distribution. The feedstock of CH4–CO2 was simulated from the emissions of biomass combustion or gasification into high-value syngas. These results demonstrate the feasibility of this process to produce hydrogen-rich gas. The energy required for this system can be supplied through biomass gasification. Accordingly, this technology not only enables the energy utilization of greenhouse gases but also effectively reduces greenhouse gas emissions derived from the thermal treatment of waste or biomass. Therefore, it represents a promising technology with strong potential for commercialization in net zero emission (NZE). This study contributes to a deeper insight into plasma chemistry while also offering technical support for the effective utilization of biomass-derived methane resources in the context of carbon neutrality and sustainable energy transition.
