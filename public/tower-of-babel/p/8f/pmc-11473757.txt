3D alignment of distant patterns with deep-subwavelength precision using metasurfaces
Authors: Maryam Ghahremani, Andrew McClung, Babak Mirzapourbeinekalaye, Amir Arbabi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Measurement of the relative positions of two objects in three dimensions with sub-nanometer precision is essential to fundamental physics experiments and applications such as aligning multi-layer patterns of semiconductor chips. Existing methods, which rely on microscopic imaging and registration of distant patterns, lack the required accuracy and precision for the next generation of three-dimensional (3D) chips. Here we show that 3D misalignment between two distant objects can be measured using metasurface alignment marks, a laser, and a camera with sub-nanometer precision. Through simulations, we demonstrate that the shot noise-limited precisions of the lateral and axial misalignments between the marks are λ0/50, 000 and λ0/6, 300 (λ0: laser’s wavelength), respectively. With its high precision and simplicity, the technique enables the next generation of 3D-integrated optical and electronic chips and paves the way for developing cost-effective and compact sensors relying on sub-nanometer displacement measurements.

Introduction

Realizing the next generation of 3D and heterogeneous integrated optical and electronic chips requires precise lateral and vertical alignments of patterns on different workpieces1–3. The conventional alignment approaches involve lithographically patterning fiducial marks, such as crosshair reference features on the workpieces, and aligning them using microscopic imaging. While various advanced techniques have improved the achievable alignment precision by optimizing detection methodologies4 and mark patterns5–7, they primarily address the alignment of patterns separated by sub-micron distances. Many emerging applications require high-precision alignment of patterns separated by significantly larger spacings. For example, miniaturized compound flat optical systems require accurate 3D alignment of workpieces separated by several hundred microns8–21.

More recently, metasurfaces have found application in optical position and movement metrology systems. Consisting of arrays of scatterers, metasurface components can generate high-resolution amplitude, phase, and polarization modulation of optical fields. The high signal-to-noise ratio of these fields enables the precise localization of rapidly varying features with deep-subwavelength precision, facilitating high-precision position and movement metrology22–24. In addition, the lateral movement of a polarization-splitting metasurface along one axis has been measured by rotating the polarization of the two split beams and interfering with them in the far-field25. However, while these approaches excel in high-precision position and movement metrology, they are not suitable for measuring relative distances between two spatially separated workpieces. Far-field holograms generated by metasurfaces and other diffractive elements have been utilized for aligning them, but the alignment precision is shown to be low and on the order of microns26,27.

Here, we introduce and experimentally demonstrate a distance metrology technique that uses the interference of far-field holographic patterns generated by cascaded metasurfaces. The technique enables fast and simultaneous measurement of relative in-plane and axial distances between two workpieces with sub-nanometer precision. Sub-nanometer lateral misalignments are encoded in the asymmetry in the interference pattern of the holograms and can be detected using an automated estimator. The technique does not involve microscopic imaging and only requires a laser and a camera. From a single camera image, the technique provides a λ0/50, 000 lateral and λ0/6, 300 axial precision (λ0: incident wavelength) for the measurement of distances between workpieces separated by millimeters. In addition, the technique enables ultrasensitive displacement sensors for monitoring positional shifts, structural deformations, and thermal drifts in sensitive instruments and experimental systems.

Results

Consider two planar, optically transparent workpieces intended for alignment, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. The workpieces, which can be patterned glass or semiconductor wafers, are positioned parallel to each other, and metasurface alignment marks are patterned on them. To align the workpieces along the x, y, and z axes, a pair of alignment marks—one mark on each workpiece—is employed. The pair, depicted schematically in Fig. 1b, is illuminated by a normally incident beam. The far-field intensity pattern resulting from the light transmitted through both marks is analyzed to determine the misalignment between the marks and, consequently, the workpieces. Working together, the two optically cascaded alignment marks generate two holograms that interfere in the far field, and their interference pattern can be highly sensitive to the relative displacement of the alignment marks. Figure 2b shows a schematic of the holointerferometric alignment marks based on two parallel telescopes formed by four metalenses. Each of the alignment marks comprises two concentric metalenses with annular apertures that have different focal lengths (f1 and f2) but the same numerical apertures (NAs). When the marks are aligned, the inner metalens on one of the marks is confocal with the outer metalens on the other mark, forming two inverting telescopes that are denoted by blue and magenta colors in Fig. 2b. Illuminated by a normally incident beam, the telescopes generate two parallel annular beams when aligned and beams that are deflected to different angles when there is a lateral misalignment.

An illustration of the top view of one of the alignment marks with dimensions used in our demonstration is shown in Fig. 2c (see “Methods” for details). For the demonstration, we have assumed an operating wavelength of λ0 = 850 nm, f1 = 380 μm, f2 = 720 μm, and a refractive index of 1.45 for the material between the two alignment marks. Figure 2d shows simulated electric fields of the two holograms generated by the two telescopes used in this design. The metalens areas and their relative phases are selected such that the two holograms interfere destructively on the optical axis when the holograms are aligned, and an intensity null is generated on the axis as shown in Fig. 2e (see “Methods” for details). As shown in Fig. 2e, when the two marks are aligned, the interference pattern of the two holograms is circularly symmetric. A small lateral misalignment leads to the deflection of the two annular beams by different angles and shifts the hologram far-field patterns along the displacement direction by different amounts, thus making the interference pattern asymmetric along that direction. An example interference pattern when the marks are misaligned along the x-direction by 50 nm (λ0/17) is shown in Fig. 2e, illustrating the asymmetry. A misalignment along the z direction defocuses the holograms and thus their interference patterns, as shown in Fig. 2e.

As an experimental demonstration of the concept, we designed and fabricated the alignment marks shown in Fig. 2b, c. The metalenses were designed to have spherical aberration-free (hyperbolic) phase profiles and annular apertures. To prevent undesired interference between the unscattered light and the alignment marks’ holographic patterns, we deflected the holograms off-axis by adding a phase ramp of a 5∘ deflector to the phase profile of the second alignment mark. We designed and implemented the metasurfaces using square cross-section amorphous silicon (aSi) nano-posts on fused silica that are cladded with SU-8 epoxy (Fig. 3a inset). The design curve shown in Fig. 3a was obtained using the grating averaging technique28,29 and was used to map the desired phase to the nano-posts’ widths. We fabricated the alignment marks on fused silica substrates by depositing aSi, patterning it using electron beam lithography and dry etching, and cladding the nano-posts by a layer of SU-8 (see “Methods” for more details). Figure 4b shows simulated and experimentally measured far-field patterns for different lateral and axial misalignments of the two workpieces (see “Methods” for simulation details and Supplementary Fig. 2 for more results). When the samples are aligned, the two holograms interfere destructively on the optical axis, and the interference pattern is composed of multiple concentric rings. A lateral misalignment of the samples laterally moves the two holograms by a different amount, thus changing their interference pattern. For a small lateral misalignment, the relative hologram movement makes the intensity distribution asymmetric along the movement direction (x-direction in Fig. 4b), and for large misalignments, the two holographic spots are spatially separated. A misalignment along the axial direction defocuses both holograms and their interference patterns, resulting in the reduction of the peak intensity and the relative intensity of different rings. Figure 4c presents the simulated intensity results for the aligned sample and for the lateral misalignment of Δx = 10 nm. As the intensity cut along the θy = 0 shows, a 10 nm lateral misalignment can be readily visually detected. The accuracy and precision of the proposed alignment technique are dictated by systematic errors and the signal-to-noise ratio of the recorded images (see the Discussion section). The mechanical vibrations, most probably caused by the camera fan, were the dominant source of random errors in our measurements, and the sample movement accuracy was limited by the actuators to ~ 60 nm, thus we could reliably detect ~ 50 nm lateral misalignment from a single captured image as shown in Fig. 4d. We attribute slight differences between the measured and simulated results shown in Fig. 4b, d to mechanical vibration noise in our setup and to interference with the undesired light scattered from an aperture in the setup that was used to reduce the incident laser beam diameter.

Discussion

The holo interferometric alignment technique that we introduced and demonstrated here enables the 3D alignment of patterns that are separated by significant distances (1.1 mm in our demonstration) with deep subwavelength precision and a long alignment range (see Supplementary Fig. 3). The technique can also be used for smaller distances between the patterns. By proportionally scaling the markers’ distance, metalens’ dimensions and focal lengths, and the camera lens’ focal length, the captured image and its sensitivity to misalignments remain unchanged. Using this approach, the distance between the marks can be reduced to ~ 10 μm (see Supplementary Fig. 4). For smaller distances, wave optics effects such as edge diffractions from the first mark should also be considered in the design.

The high sensitivity of the interference pattern to the misalignment between the workpieces is achieved by using the on-axis destructive interference of the two holograms (see Supplementary Fig. 5 for the case that holograms interfere constructively). The accuracy of the alignment is affected by systematic errors in the mounting and measurement system, and its precision by stochastic errors such as mechanical vibrations and imaging noise.

Systematic errors that can affect the alignment accuracy include a non-zero angle between the laser beam’s optical axis and normal to the substrates, unparallel mounting of the two substrates, and an error in the laser’s wavelength or the refractive index of the materials between the marks. An error in the incident angle of the laser or an angle between the two samples causes a lateral misalignment error. For the proof-of-concept demonstration presented here, a 1 millidegree error in the laser incident angle or a 50 millidegree error in the parallel alignment of the samples, leads to a 20 nm lateral misalignment (see Supplementary Fig. 6). To ensure the laser is normally incident on the samples and to align the two samples parallel to each other, we measured the reflection of the laser beams from the sample (see “Methods” for details), and this simple approach provides an angular accuracy higher than one millidegree. In contrast to the angular errors, which cause lateral misalignments, an error in the laser wavelength results in an axial misalignment. For the example demonstration presented here, a 1 nm error in the laser wavelength causes a 1.7 μm axial misalignment (see Supplementary Fig. 7). There is no fundamental limit on reducing systematic errors, and they can be minimized to any desirable degree by the proper design and calibration of the measurement setup. For example, the angular errors in aligning the laser beam and the samples can be reduced by using a laser with a larger beam diameter, or a laser wavelength can be measured with an accuracy of a part per million or better using a wavemeter.

Stochastic errors such as the mechanical vibrations of the samples and the camera, and the imaging noise (e.g., shot noise) limit the alignment precision. By a proper design of the setup, the mechanical vibrations can be minimized and, in principle, reach their thermal limits (~ kBT energy in each vibrational degree of freedom, kB: Boltzmann constant, T: temperature) and their reductions down to 10−19 m level have been demonstrated30. For a low-noise image sensor, like the one used in our demonstration, shot noise is the dominant source of noise (see “Methods” for more details). The shot noise is a fundamental limit when using classical light sources such as lasers and limits the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the recorded images. Therefore, with a proper mechanical design of the setup, the alignment system can be shot noise limited.

As Fig. 4c shows, it is possible to visually detect a 10 nm lateral misalignment, however, this approach is a heuristic and unreliable way of determining the misalignment values and their precisions, especially when analyzing noisy measurement data. For inferring parameters from noisy measurement data and determining their precisions, the proper approach is defining estimators and finding their standard deviations31. An estimator is a function of the measured data and can be considered a random variable that returns different estimates of the parameter when operating on different repeated measurement data. The precision of the parameters depends on the estimator definition; however, there is a lower bound (Cramér-Rao bound) on the standard deviations of all unbiased estimators (i.e., estimators whose means are equal to the true values of the parameters)31.

Considering the misalignment values Δx, Δy, and Δz as the three unknown parameters to be estimated from a single captured noisy image, assuming a shot noise-limited measurement and the specifications of our camera, we computed the Cramér-Rao lower bound for the misalignment estimation errors for the marks (see Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Fig. 8 for details). For the aligned marks, we found a lower bound of ~ 16 pm for the standard deviation of the estimated lateral misalignment values, but the lower bound on the standard deviation of the estimated Δz diverges because the captured intensity image I is insensitive to axial misalignment to the first order in Δz (i.e., ∂I/∂Δz = 0) when the marks are aligned.

The Cramér-Rao bound provides a limit on the alignment precision, but it does not offer an approach for determining the misalignment values from the measured images. To demonstrate the achievable alignment precision of the marks, we employed the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) which is known to approximately reach the Cramér-Rao bound in many practical cases. MLE is a simple and intuitive estimator that returns parameter values that make the observed measurement results most probable. We used MLE to estimate misalignment values from experimentally captured images and have presented the results in Supplementary Fig. 9. Using MLE, we found the standard deviation values of ~ 16 pm and ~ 19 nm for the estimated lateral and axial misalignment values inferred from a single shot-noise limited image when the marks were aligned (see “Methods” and Supplementary Fig. 10). The shot-noise limited standard deviation values obtained using MLE for a few other misalignment values are presented in Supplementary Fig. 8 and are close to their Cramér-Rao bounds.

The lower precision for estimating Δz is because of the low sensitivity of the holograms to axial misalignment, which is due to the finite depth of focus of the metalenses that form the holograms. Thus, a simple approach to improve the axial alignment’s precision is to align the marks away from the confocal point. For example, by using MLE, we found that aligning the marks at (Δx, Δy, Δz) = (0, 0, 1 μm) results in lateral and axial standard deviations of 17 pm (~ λ0/50, 000) and 134 pm (~ λ0/6, 300), respectively (see “Methods” and Supplementary Figs. 8 and 10).

Deep subwavelength accuracy and precision in locating features within recorded intensity or complex field images have been previously reported32,33. Indeed, such a level of precision is the foundation for achieving nanometer resolution in localization microscopy33. In addition, the distance between two objects is routinely measured with deep subwavelength precision using interferometers, and extreme precisions have been achieved in such measurements34. Nevertheless, the proposed technique provides deep subwavelength accuracy and precision for simultaneously measuring relative lateral and axial distances and aligning two workpieces separated by a macroscopic distance. It uses asymmetry in the interferometric pattern of the holograms to detect lateral misalignment. Therefore, quadrant detectors, which have significantly larger saturation power than camera pixels and thus a larger shot-noise limited signal-to-noise ratio, along with heterodyne detection, can be used to improve the lateral precision beyond its current λ0/50, 000 value. Furthermore, the parallel telescope design used as a proof-of-concept is not necessarily the optimal selection for the phase profiles, and more optimal hologram designs might achieve higher precisions or have smaller footprints.

While we focused on 3D translational alignment using a single pair of alignment marks, the utilization of multiple spatially separated pairs of marks enables aligning other degrees of freedom. For instance, three pairs of alignment marks can be employed to fully align two rigid workpieces. Furthermore, the proposed holointerferometric technique’s high accuracy and precision, along with the simplicity of the inspection setup (a laser and an infinity-focused camera), and the elimination of the need for microscopic imaging, position it as an attractive alternative to current backside alignment methods. In addition to its applications in aligning meta-optics wafers, the use of a near-infrared laser enables the alignment of multiple stacked silicon wafers, a requirement for achieving 3D integrated circuits. The alignment technique’s high precision is more than adequate for both current and next-generation optical and electronic chip patterning and packaging.

The computational efficiency of the forward simulation of the marks interference pattern and MLE enables real-time estimation of the misalignment values and rapid alignment adjustments in practical applications. The MLE method provides optimal estimates close to the Cramér-Rao bound, ensuring efficient use of the information contained in the diffraction patterns. These combined benefits make our approach well-suited for high-precision alignment systems. In addition, the simplicity and miniature form factor of the alignment marks can pave the way for the development of cost-effective and compact instruments and sensors that rely on precise measurements of sub-nanometer displacements.

Methods

Alignment mark design

As shown in Fig. 2b, c, the alignment marks are composed of two concentric metalenses with annular apertures. The inner and outer metalens aperture regions are defined by r1 < r < R1 and r2 < r < R2, respectively, where r is the radial coordinate, and r1, R1, r1, and R2 are the radii that specify the annular regions. The inner and outer metalenses are assumed to have focal lengths of f1 and f2, respectively, and hyperbolic phase profiles (i.e., are spherical-aberration-free for normal incidence)35. Therefore, the design has 6 design parameters (the radii and the focal length). We determined these parameters by assuming an axial alignment distance of d = f1 + f2 = 1100 μm between the marks, the size of the marks 2R2 = 1 mm, a gap of 5 μm between the two annular apertures, a numerical aperture of 0.83 for the metalenses, and R1/R2 = r1/r2 = f1/f2, where the last assumption ensures that all the power incident on the first alignment mark passes through the second one. In addition, using power conservation, it can be shown that the condition for the holograms to have equal on-axis far-field intensities is given by A1,inA1,out = A2,inA2,out, where Ai,in and Ai,out are the areas of the input and output apertures of the ith hologram. Because of the symmetry of the two alignment marks, this condition is satisfied in the current design. Device fabrication

To fabricate the metasurface alignment marks, a 560-nm-thick layer of amorphous silicon was deposited on one side of a 0.5-mm-thick fused silica substrate using plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition. A ~ 200-nm-thick layer of positive electron beam resist (ZEP 520A-7, Zeon Chemicals) was spin-coated on top of the amorphous silicon layer, followed by spin-coating an 80-nm-thick layer of a charge dissipation layer (AR-PC 5090, Allresist GmbH). The metasurface nano-post patterns were written using a 50 keV electron-beam lithography system (HS50, Elionix Inc.). After the exposure, the charge dissipation layer was removed using water, and the resist was developed using a developer (ZED-N50, Zeon Chemicals). Next, a hard mask was created by evaporating ~ 80 nm of aluminum oxide on the sample and lifting off the resist in a solvent (Remover PG, Kayaku Advanced Materials Inc). The pattern was transferred from the hard mask to the underlying amorphous silicon layer using an inductively coupled plasma dry etch process using a mixture of C4F8 and SF6 gases. Following the etch, the hard mask was removed by immersing the sample in an 80 ∘C solution of ammonium hydroxide and hydrogen peroxide. Finally, to form a permanent protective layer over the nano-posts, a ~ 5-μm-thick layer of SU-8 2005 epoxy was spin-coated on the sample, reflowed by baking at 95 ∘C, UV exposed, and baked for an hour at 200 ∘C to cure the epoxy.

Measurement procedure

The experimental measurements were conducted using the measurement setup schematically shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, whose simplified version is presented in Fig. 4a. A free-space λ0 = 850 nm laser source (TA100, Toptica) was coupled to a single mode fiber (780-HP, Thorlabs) and passed through a 2 × 2, 90:10 fiber coupler (TW850R2A2, Thorlabs). The 90% arm of the coupler was connected to a fiber collimation package (F280APC-850, Thorlabs) which generated a collimated beam with a waist diameter of 4 mm. The beam angle was adjusted using mirrors mounted on kinematic mounts (KS1, Thorlabs) so that it impinged normally on the first workpiece. To ensure normal incidence, we monitored the specularly reflected beam’s power that was coupled back to the fiber by measuring the power in the 10% reflected arm of the coupler using a power meter (1918-C with a 918D head, Newport Corporation). We can achieve an incident angle smaller than 1 millidegree using this approach (see Supplementary Fig. 6). The first workpiece was mounted on a rotation stage to allow for the rotational alignment of the workpieces that will be needed for permanently bonding the samples in the future. The second workpiece was mounted on a kinematic mount (KM100B, Thorlabs) which was attached to a three-axis translation stage (M-462-XYZ-M, Newport Corp.) equipped with electrostrictive actuators (AD-100, Newport Corp.). The kinematic mount was used to adjust the angle of the second workpiece and maximize the power reflected back to the fiber, thus ensuring the two workpieces are parallel to each other with an angular accuracy on the order of one millidegree. The translation stage was used to move the second workpiece laterally and axially relative to the first one with ~ 60 nm resolution. The far fields of the holographic beams were imaged using a CCD camera (CoolSnap K4, Photometrics) with a 150 mm focal lens (AC254-150-AB-ML, Thorlabs).
