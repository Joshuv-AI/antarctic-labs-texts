Introduction

Pancreatic cystic lymphangiomas are infrequent benign tumors originating from lymphatic vessels, representing <0.2 % of all pancreatic cystic formations [17]. These lesions are generally asymptomatic and are often identified incidentally during imaging studies conducted for conditions unrelated to the pancreas. Lymphangioma involves lymphatic vessels in various regions of the body, such as the neck, axilla, mediastinum, visceral organs, skeletal structures, and mesentery. Lymphangiomas of the pancreas are extremely rare. Until 1994, only 29 cases had been reported in the literature [1]. The incidence rate of pancreatic lymphoma is <5 %, and the symptoms vary depending on the size and location of the tumor [2]. It is most rarely located in the retroperitoneum, making up <1 % of all lymphangioma cases. The exact prevalence of cystic lymphangioma is uncertain because there is limited information available in published studies [3]. These conditions typically manifest in infants and children, particularly those under the age of two, and are extremely rare in adult populations [4]. Pancreatic lymphangiomas can be symptomatic or asymptomatic. When symptomatic, affected individuals may experience acute abdominal pain and distention, palpable abdominal masses, loss of appetite, weight loss, nausea, and vomiting [5,6]. In the case of non-invasive tumors, pancreatic lymphangiomas are often found incidentally during routine check-ups or abdominal ultrasound sonography. However, a definitive diagnosis is confirmed by pathological examination after obtaining a biopsy [2,[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Here, we present a rare case of pancreatic lymphangioma in a 58-year-old Iranian woman who presented with chief complaints of acute abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting.

Methods

This case report adheres to the SCARE Checklist, ensuring comprehensive documentation of the patient's presentation, diagnostic assessment, surgical intervention, and follow-up outcomes to enhance the quality and clarity of our findings [27].

Case presentation

A 58-year-old Iranian woman with a medical history of lumbar vertebral pain and no other remarkable previous medical, surgical, drugs, psychosocial, or family history was admitted to our institution's surgical emergency ward with chief complaints of acute epigastric pain, nausea, and vomiting. Subsequent history-taking conducted upon admission indicated a negative smoking status. During the physical examination, the patient's abdomen revealed tenderness in the left upper quadrant and a significantly large palpable mass. Initially, the surgeons conducted primary investigations to rule out acute pancreatitis, myocardial infarction, and intestinal perforation or obstruction.

The results of the medical tests were unremarkable: amylase was 98 U/L (normal range: 30–110 U/L), lipase was 27 U/L (normal range: 0–160 U/L), white blood cell count (WBC) was 11.8 × 10^3/μL (normal range: 4.0–10.5 × 10^3/μL), hemoglobin was 10.6 g/dL (normal range: 12.0–15.5 g/dL for females), platelets were 212,000/μL (normal range: 150,000-450,000/μL), blood urea nitrogen (BUN) was 7 mg/dL (normal range: 7–20 mg/dL), creatinine was 0.76 mg/dL (normal range: 0.6–1.2 mg/dL), sodium was 135 mEq/L (normal range: 135–145 mEq/L), potassium was 3.8 mEq/L (normal range: 3.5–5.0 mEq/L), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was 20 U/L (normal range: 10–40 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 9 U/L (normal range: 7–56 U/L), alkaline phosphatase was 182 U/L (normal range: 44–147 U/L), calcium was 9.4 mg/dL (normal range: 8.5–10.2 mg/dL), phosphorus was 2.8 mg/dL (normal range: 2.5–4.5 mg/dL), total bilirubin was 0.60 mg/dL (normal range: 0.1–1.2 mg/dL), direct bilirubin was 0.3 mg/dL (normal range: <0.3 mg/dL), quantitative troponin was <1.5 ng/mL (normal range: <0.04 ng/mL), and qualitative troponin was negative. These results indicated no acute pancreatitis or myocardial infarction, aside from mild leukocytosis. Therefore, the laboratory data did not indicate acute pancreatitis or myocardial infarction. The patient underwent further investigations, including abdominopelvic ultrasound sonography and abdominopelvic computed tomography (CT) with intravenous (IV) contrast.

Ultrasonography results revealed a large lobulated and anechoic structure. Thus, an abdominopelvic CT scan with IV contrast was performed and revealed a cystic lobulated mass, measuring approximately 105 × 112 × 74 mm, located in the left upper quadrant just anterior to the kidney and medial to the bowel loops. The mass contained thin septation with no significant changes, suggesting a differential diagnosis of lymphangioma versus a mesenteric cyst (Fig. 1). At this juncture, in light of the patient's significant pain and lack of response to initial treatment with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and intravenous fluid therapy, as well as the inconclusive results from alternative imaging techniques, an exploratory laparotomy was conducted. After opening the abdomen through a midline incision and reaching the retroperitoneal cavity, we explored the patient's internal viscera. We discovered a cystic mass on the body and tail of the pancreas invading the small bowel mesentery, resembling a hydatid cyst. Subsequently, the cyst was meticulously dissected from the affected mesentery, and adhesion lysis was performed to resect the cyst from the pancreas (Fig. 2). Subsequent to the comprehensive resection of the cyst performed by an attending thoracic surgeon, with assistance from a vascular surgery fellow, a biopsy specimen was collected for histopathological analysis through microscopic evaluation. The analysis indicated the presence of lymphangioma, distinguished by the presence of dilated lymphatic channels and the absence of atypical cells exhibiting mitotic characteristics. This finding effectively excluded the possibility of malignant neoplasms, thereby reinforcing our initial diagnosis (Fig. 3). Furthermore, in light of the accessibility of histological examination and to mitigate the financial burden on our patient, immunohistochemical analysis for the identification of CD31 and D2–40 endothelial cell markers was not conducted. Upon completion of the post-surgery recovery period, the patient was discharged in good health and scheduled for an outpatient follow-up visit within the next 7 days. Upon follow-up after seven days, the patient's condition was good, and no sign of tumor relapse or surgical complications were noted.

Histopathological examination of the cystic Lymphangioma revealed dilated lymphatic spaces lined by endothelial cells, characteristic of this benign vascular malformation. Additionally, the presence of lymphoid aggregates within the cyst wall supported the diagnosis.

Discussion

Lymphangioma of the pancreas is a rare entity that affects <1 % of the general population, mostly children [11,12]. The etiology of cystic lymphangioma is infrequently documented in the existing literature. Nonetheless, one plausible explanation for this condition is the congenital obstruction of lymphatic ducts, which leads to the development of lymphangiectasia, a process that typically begins in childhood [25]. This uncommon condition predominantly impacts children, with a lesser degree of prevalence observed in females, who exhibit a higher susceptibility to the development of lymphangiomas [10]. The differential diagnosis encompasses pancreatic pseudocysts, serous and mucinous cystadenomas, intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms, congenital neoplasms, pancreatic ductal carcinoma exhibiting cystic degeneration, and echinococcal cysts [26].

Although the head and neck regions were previously the most common sites of lymphangioma, there is now a slightly increasing trend in reporting gastrointestinal (GI) tract structure involvement in the literature. A study conducted by Tan-Garcia et al. showed that out of 167 patients diagnosed with lymphangioma, 28.7 % had experienced GI tract involvement, and 28.1 % experienced head and neck involvement. This study addresses new trends in lymphangioma involvement, shifting from common sites in the head and neck to GI structures [13]. Limited attempts have been made to determine the exact cause of lymphangioma. However, some researchers suggest that this condition could stem from abnormalities in the lymphatic vessels during embryonic development, leading to obstruction of lymphatic flow or from inflammatory processes involving these anatomical structures [12,14]. Most lymphangiomas are asymptomatic and can be detected during routine check-ups. However, these benign vascular tumors may exert compressive pressure on adjacent structures, leading to abrupt complications such as acute abdominal pain, intestinal obstruction, and mesenteric ischemia [6,14,15]. Regarding the pre-operative diagnostic strategies, and consistent with what we did for our case, ultrasonography is widely regarded as the primary diagnostic tool for investigating suspected cystic lymphangiomas due to its non-invasive nature, cost-effectiveness, and avoidance of ionizing radiation. However, it is recommended to complement this technique with CT and MRI scans to overcome its limitations, such as providing a comprehensive view and gathering additional information on structural characteristics, internal and peripheral contrast enhancement, and regional lesion spread. Imaging plays a vital role in the evaluation of cystic lymphangiomas by ruling out malignancy and providing precise anatomical localization of the tumor prior to surgical intervention. Currently, CT scans are conducted using a multi-slice approach, enabling the acquisition of volumetric data of the targeted anatomical area through various methods such as multi-planar reconstruction (MPR), maximum intensity projection (MIP), surface shading display (SSD), and volume rendering (VR) [16].

According to other studies in the literature, Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) has become an increasingly important diagnostic tool for evaluating cystic pancreatic lesions, including lymphangiomas. MRCP allows for non-invasive visualization of the biliary and pancreatic ducts, providing valuable information about the anatomical location and extent of the lesion. In some cases, unlike our case where previous ultrasonography and blood sample results did not suggest malignancy, MRCP can help distinguish cystic lymphangiomas from other pancreatic cystic lesions, such as cystadenomas or cystadenocarcinomas. However, MRCP alone may not always provide a definitive diagnosis, and endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) may be necessary to obtain cytological and biochemical analysis of the cyst fluid [[17], [18], [19]].

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) has a more limited role in the diagnosis of cystic lymphangiomas, as it is an invasive procedure with potential complications. ERCP is primarily used when MRCP inadequately visualizes the terminal common bile duct or pancreaticobiliary junction, or when a therapeutic intervention is needed. In some cases, ERCP may be used to obtain a tissue sample for cytological analysis, but this is not a common practice. Overall, MRCP should be considered the primary diagnostic test for suspected cystic lymphangiomas, with ERCP reserved for specific situations where additional information or intervention is required [18,19]. Therefore, we chose not to impose a financial burden on the patient since MRCP and ERCP were not indicated.

Distal pancreatectomy is a surgical intervention utilized for the treatment of pancreatic lymphangioma. When the lymphangioma is situated in the tail of the pancreas, this procedure involves the excision of the affected pancreatic segment along with the lymphangioma [20,21]. Distal pancreatectomy is particularly beneficial in cases where the lymphangioma is sizable and infiltrative, as it enables a more comprehensive removal of the tumor and its adjacent tissues. Furthermore, this surgical approach aids in maintaining pancreatic function by selectively removing the impacted portion of the pancreas, as opposed to a more extensive pancreaticoduodenectomy [21]. However, due to the cystic structure being localized in the adjacent omentum in the pre-operative imaging modalities, we performed a laparotomy surgery to remove the cystic tumor entirely, preserving the pancreas.

The scarcity of this condition necessitates the development of a standardized medical protocol, be it medical or surgical, within the current body of literature. Many other cystic pathologies share a set of common physical features with the disease, including hydatid cysts, pancreatic serous cystadenoma, benign cystic lesions, and neoplastic cystic tumors. Therefore, surgical excision is required to establish a definitive diagnosis through microscopic examination [12]. Despite significant advancements in medical laboratory tests and technologies, there is still a lack of knowledge regarding the development of preoperative diagnostic tests for pancreatic lymphangiomas. Ultrasonography is widely regarded as the primary diagnostic tool for investigating suspected cystic lymphangiomas due to its non-invasive nature, cost-effectiveness, and avoidance of ionizing radiation. However, it is recommended to complement this technique with CT and MRI scans to overcome its limitations, such as providing a comprehensive view and gathering additional information on structural characteristics, internal and peripheral contrast enhancement, and regional lesion spread [[19], [20], [21], [22],24].

Study limitations

The findings presented in this study are derived from a single case, which inherently limits the generalizability of the results. Additionally, the short follow-up period of one-week post-surgery constrains the assessment of long-term outcomes. Although imaging techniques such as ultrasonography and computed tomography (CT) scans facilitated the diagnostic process, definitive confirmation necessitated histopathological examination, highlighting the need for improved preoperative methodologies. The scarcity of existing literature on pancreatic lymphangiomas further complicates the establishment of standardized management protocols. Furthermore, as this is a case report, there exists a potential risk of selection bias that may influence the interpretation of the findings. These limitations emphasize the necessity for further research involving larger sample sizes and extended follow-up periods to enhance the understanding and management of this rare condition.

Conclusion

Pancreatic lymphangioma is an extremely rare benign neoplastic proliferation that affects <5 % of the general population, with fewer than 100 documented cases worldwide. It can manifest with various symptoms ranging from being asymptomatic to causing acute abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting. The exact etiology of lymphangioma formation remains unclear; however, it is believed to be associated with abnormalities in lymphatic vessels during embryonic development or inflammatory processes. Ultrasonography, abdominopelvic CT scan, and MRI are reported to be effective imaging modalities for diagnostic challenges. Furthermore, surgical excision is considered the gold standard for both diagnosis and treatment because no definitive preoperative diagnostic test is currently available. Further research is warranted to establish a standardized medical approach for managing this rare condition.
