Nutritional deficiencies should be systematically assessed as potential adverse effects in incretin-based obesity therapies
Authors: Jean-Philippe Drouin-Chartier, Antoine Caron, Jacob Lessard-Lord
Published: 2026

Dear editor,

Nutritional risks induced by, or associated with, incretin-based medications for obesity management have been largely overlooked to date. We therefore read with great interest the study by Almandoz et al. on the impact of tirzepatide use on nutritional status, based on a post hoc analysis of data from the SURMOUNT program [1].

We commend the authors for leveraging the available data to address this important and timely question. This contribution is particularly valuable given that nutritional status data from large privately sponsored incretin-based obesity pharmacotherapy trials remain limited, and that any available information from the SURMOUNT program is therefore highly relevant to the scientific and medical communities.

As acknowledged by the authors, however, the substantial risk of ascertainment bias, a form of information bias, requires that the results be interpreted with great caution. In the current context, the very low reported prevalence of nutritional issues may reflect limited surveillance rather than truly low prevalence. For instance, the study reports a prevalence of vitamin D deficiency ranging from 0.3% to 1.6%, depending on the trial and treatment group. By contrast, National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data from the general U.S. adult population indicate prevalences of severe and moderate vitamin D deficiency of 3% and 22%, respectively, with vitamin D insufficiency and sufficiency observed in 41% and 35% of adults [2]. Data on vitamin D deficiency from Canada and the European Union show similar rates [3,4]. The markedly lower prevalence reported in the SURMOUNT program is therefore difficult to reconcile with population-level data, particularly because the trial population included individuals with obesity, whose risk of nutritional deficiencies is higher than that of the general population [5], as described in the rationale and discussion of the paper.

Closely related to nutritional status are dietary intakes themselves. The SURMOUNT program protocol states that participants were recommended to complete a 3-day diet and exercise diary before each counseling visit to support adherence [6]. During these visits, participants’ diets were reviewed and additional advice was provided when needed to maximize adherence. However, to our knowledge, none of these dietary intake data have been published to date. These data represent a critical missing piece for fully understanding the implications of the nutritional-status findings reported by Almandoz et al. Specifically, they would help determine whether the apparently very low prevalence of nutritional deficiencies reflects actual dietary adequacy, supplementation, clinical monitoring, or under-ascertainment.

This issue echoes findings from a recent U.S. claims-based study of more than 460,000 individuals using a GLP-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1RA), which reported nutritional deficiencies in approximately 13% and 22% of patients within 6 and 12 months respectively after GLP-1RA initiation. Vitamin D deficiency was the most prevalent nutritional deficiency associated with GLP-1RA therapy, reaching approximately 8% after 6 months and 14% after 12 months. Importantly, the recorded prevalence was higher among patients who had consulted a registered dietitian [7]. This suggests that the involvement of allied health professionals may be critical for investigating and detecting such deficiencies. However, only 8% of patients had seen a registered dietitian within the first 6 months after treatment initiation. The higher recorded prevalence of nutritional deficiencies among patients who had consulted a registered dietitian also suggests substantial ascertainment bias and a lack of systematic surveillance of nutritional status in real-world practice.

Overall, these data reflect a larger issue in incretin-based obesity therapy clinical research: beyond acute nutrition-related adverse effects, such as gastrointestinal symptoms, nutritional-status risks are not consistently acknowledged or assessed as potential adverse effects of these medications. This is concerning because incretin-based obesity therapies such as tirzepatide and semaglutide can reduce food intake by approximately one third [8,9]. In the context of generally low diet quality and a high prevalence of insufficient intakes of key nutrients in the general population [[10], [11], [12]], such reductions in food intake may further compromise nutritional adequacy and contribute to long-term adverse effects, such as osteoporosis. Adequate surveillance of nutritional status through the assessment of dietary and supplement intakes, as well as biomarkers, therefore, appears essential. These examples illustrate why nutritional deficiencies and dietary inadequacies should not be considered secondary or peripheral outcomes, but rather clinically meaningful safety endpoints to be systematically assessed in trials of incretin-based obesity therapies and included in clinical practice.
