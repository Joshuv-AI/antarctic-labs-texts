Emergency nurses’ perspectives on integrating a research innovation curriculum into clinical education: a qualitative study of attitudes, needs, and challenges
Authors: Shui-xiu Huang, Yu Zhao, Wei Zhang, Guo Tian, Li Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the recognized importance of research for enhancing nursing quality, emergency nurses (ENs) face significant barriers to research engagement. Insufficient structured training may reduce their interest and capacity. Understanding ENs’ perspectives on integrating a research innovation curriculum into clinical education is critical for developing effective, context-specific strategies, particularly given the underexplored nature of their views on curriculum integration in high-pressure settings.ObjectiveTo explore emergency nurses’ attitudes toward integrating a research innovation curriculum into clinical education and to identify their perceived needs and challenges.DesignA qualitative descriptive study.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted with 23 emergency nurses from a tertiary hospital in Shenzhen, China. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis.ResultsThree main themes emerged: (1) Dual Perspectives: Enthusiasm for Growth and Concerns about Workload; (2) Essential Support Systems: Beyond the Individual; and (3) Content and Pedagogy for Effective Learning. Participants strongly supported the curriculum’s potential to enhance professional development but expressed concerns about time constraints due to heavy clinical workloads and family responsibilities. They highlighted the need for institutional support, including dedicated time and space, managerial and peer encouragement, and family coordination. Additionally, they desired diverse, practical, and skill-based content to foster innovative thinking and a collaborative learning culture, with an emphasis on foundational research concepts (e.g., study design and basic statistical analysis) alongside emerging tools like artificial intelligence (AI) for data handling and innovation.ConclusionEmergency nurses are receptive to integrating research innovation training into clinical education but emphasize the need for robust institutional support to overcome structural barriers. Hospital administrators should implement supportive systems tailored to nurses’ needs to foster a sustainable culture of inquiry and innovation.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04485-3.

Introduction

As patient expectations for medical technology and nursing services continue to rise, nursing staff are required not only to enhance their professional clinical skills and knowledge but also to develop their research and innovation capabilities [1]. Strengthening evidence-based practice and research competence among nurses is widely recognized as an effective strategy for improving the overall quality of nursing care. Keeping up with the latest developments in research methodologies and emerging information technologies is a key factor in advancing clinical nursing practice [2]. Developing nurses’ research and innovation abilities can significantly improve healthcare quality, enhance work efficiency, optimize patient outcomes, reduce medical costs, increase the effectiveness of nursing services, and improve access to medical care [3]. According to the International Council of Nurses (ICN) competency framework adapted for China, core nursing competencies consist of seven components: critical thinking and research aptitude, clinical care, leadership, interpersonal communication, legal and ethical practice, professional development, and teaching–coaching skills [4]. However, multiple studies have shown that critical thinking and scientific innovation are the most underdeveloped abilities among nursing staff [5, 6]. Therefore, incorporating systematic nursing research and innovation training into routine continuing education programs is essential for enhancing nurses’ research capabilities and should become a priority for healthcare institutions in the future.

Background

Numerous studies have identified barriers to nurses’ engagement in scientific research and evidence-based practice, including insufficient time and limited access to targeted, systematic training. Specific challenges include difficulties in collecting clinical research data, analyzing findings accurately, and searching for scientific information effectively [7]. To address these, healthcare institutions have implemented strategies such as providing research methodology training, organizing team-based problem-solving sessions, offering access to scientific resources, and adjusting talent development models [8, 9]. While these interventions have improved nurses’ research capabilities to some extent, gaps persist due to mismatches between nurses’ specific knowledge needs and institutional support [3, 10]. Understanding these needs is critical for designing tailored research and innovation training programs integrated into continuing education.

Emergency departments, as high-pressure environments managing critically ill patients, place unique demands on nurses’ research and innovation capabilities [11]. However, heavy workloads, frequent shift rotations, and high stress levels pose significant barriers. Compared to ward nurses, emergency nurses receive less systematic research training and often rely on experience-based learning, limiting their research capacity and impacting care quality [12]. Moreover, emergency nurses’ specific perspectives on integrating research innovation curricula-encompassing foundational elements like study design, basic statistical analysis, and emerging tools such as AI for research processes-remain underexplored, despite their potential to bridge these gaps.

Methods

Aim

This study aimed to investigate emergency nurses’ attitudes toward integrating systematic research and innovation courses into routine education programs and to identify their content needs.

Design

A qualitative descriptive study was conducted to provide a comprehensive summary of participants’ experiences and perspectives, suitable for informing clinical education planning. The study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research Checklist [13].

Setting and recruitment

Emergency nurses were recruited from a tertiary hospital in Shenzhen, Guangdong, China, using purposive sampling in July 2024. The head nurse distributed study information and consent forms. Maximum variation sampling ensured diverse demographic characteristics (age, gender, education). Inclusion criteria were: (1) ≥ 6 months of emergency nursing experience; (2) effective communication ability; and (3) voluntary participation with informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) inability to attend interviews due to leave; and (2) participation in related intervention studies. Data collection ceased at information saturation (no new themes after the 20th interview), with three additional interviews (21–23) conducted to confirm stability of themes and test for any emergent variations in responses.

Interview guide

A preliminary interview guide was developed based on a systematic literature review and research team discussions, then pilot-tested with three emergency nurses. Minor adjustments improved question flow and clarity. The final guide included semi-structured, open-ended questions probing key domains: attitudes toward research integration (e.g., “What are your thoughts on adding research and innovation training to your regular clinical education?“); perceived barriers and needs (e.g., “What challenges do you foresee in participating in such training, and what support would help?“); and desired content and methods (e.g., “What topics, such as study design, basic statistics, or AI tools for research, would make this training most useful?“). (See Supplementary File 1 for the full guide).

Data collection

Individual video-conferencing interviews (via Tencent Meeting) accommodated participants’ schedules and ensured privacy. Interviews averaged 45 min (range: 38–55 min). After explaining the study’s purpose and procedures, written informed consent was obtained, emphasizing voluntariness and confidentiality. Interviews were audio-recorded, supplemented by notes on non-verbal cues. The interviewer encouraged open expression without influencing responses. Data were anonymized using codes (e.g., A1) to protect privacy.

Data analysis

Data collection and analysis occurred concurrently. Transcripts were created within 24 h and returned to participants for member checking. NVivo 20.0 facilitated thematic analysis, following six steps: (1) data familiarization; (2) initial code generation; (3) theme searching; (4) theme review; (5) theme definition and naming; and (6) report production. Two researchers (SXH, YZ) independently coded the first five transcripts, developing a coding tree through consensus. SXH coded remaining transcripts, with YZ reviewing 30% for consistency. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. To ensure analytic dependability, the 30% subset reviewed by the second coder was selected purposively to include transcripts representing diverse demographic characteristics (e.g., age, years of experience, educational level) and variation in expressed viewpoints. The initial coding tree was iteratively refined during analysis as new insights emerged; all updates were documented in version-controlled codebooks and applied retroactively to earlier transcripts to maintain consistency. An audit trail was maintained throughout the study, comprising dated analytical memos, meeting notes from coding discussions, and decision logs regarding theme definitions and code modifications. This process supported transparency and traceability of interpretive decisions.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Peking University Shenzhen Hospital (Approval No: [2024] No. 086) and adhered to the Helsinki Declaration.

Rigorousness and reflexivity

Trustworthiness followed Lincoln and Guba’ s criteria. Credibility was ensured through member checking, peer debriefing, and audit trails. Transferability was supported by thick descriptions. Dependability and confirmability were maintained through detailed audit trails and peer debriefing. The primary interviewer (SXH), a senior nurse and PhD in the department, entered the study with pre-existing assumptions that emergency nurses would value research training for career advancement but face workload barriers, informed by her clinical leadership role. Bracketing techniques, including journaling pre- and post-interview reflections, were used to minimize bias and encourage dissenting views.

Discussion

This study reveals emergency nurses’ multifaceted perspectives on integrating a research innovation curriculum into clinical education, highlighting enthusiasm tempered by structural and personal challenges. The discussion addresses the three themes: dual attitudes, support systems, and content preferences.

Navigating dual attitudes

Participants expressed enthusiasm for research as a pathway to professional growth, aligning with the ICN’s competency framework and global evidence-based practice trends [14, 15]. This enthusiasm stems from a desire for personal and professional growth, viewing research as a pathway to enhance their clinical expertise and contribute meaningfully to the advancement of the nursing profession [16]. On the other hand, this enthusiasm is met with a stark reality: the overwhelming burden of clinical work and personal responsibilities. The concern that “there is no time to learn new things” due to night shifts and family commitments is not merely a logistical issue but a fundamental barrier to engagement. This finding resonates with previous studies that have identified time as the most significant obstacle to nurses’ participation in research activities [17]. It underscores a critical gap between the aspirational goal of a research-literate nursing workforce and the practical constraints of the emergency department environment. Therefore, the “opposition” expressed by some nurses is less a rejection of research itself and more a pragmatic response to an already saturated workload. This duality suggests that any successful integration of a research curriculum must begin by acknowledging and directly addressing this tension, rather than assuming universal readiness [18].

The imperative for comprehensive support systems

Participants recognized the value of research innovation courses for promoting evidence-based practice and enhancing patient care quality [19], yet emphasized the need for collaborative support from hospital administrators, colleagues, and family to overcome implementation barriers. This aligns with prior qualitative evidence showing positive attitudes toward research but persistent gaps between knowledge acquisition and practical application [20]. Hospital leaders should prioritize curriculum development in innovative research skills-beyond routine clinical training-to address suboptimal research competencies [19, 21]. Notably, nurses reported challenges in translating research knowledge to clinical settings, highlighting deficiencies in current programs [22]. To bridge this, support systems must clarify foundational research concepts (e.g., defining research as systematic inquiry involving study design, valid variables, and basic statistical analysis) while incorporating modern tools like AI for efficient data processing and idea generation, adapting to evolving educational paradigms [23].

Designing for motivation: content and pedagogy for innovation

The third theme, motivation and content requirements, provides a clear blueprint for the design of future training programs. The nurses are not passive recipients of knowledge; they have specific, actionable ideas for what would make the curriculum effective. The paramount need is for the cultivation of innovative thinking. Participants felt constrained by conventional thinking and sought training that would teach them “how to build” this skill. This goes beyond simply presenting research methods; it requires fostering a mindset of inquiry and creativity [24]. To achieve this, diversity in content and form is essential. The rejection of traditional “storytelling” lectures in favor of “case sharing, group learning, and practical operations” reflects a demand for experiential and adult learning principles [25, 26]. Nurses want to learn by doing, discussing, and applying knowledge to real-world scenarios, including hands-on sessions on statistics basics and AI applications to modernize research engagement.

Finally, the repeated emphasis on a sharing culture is perhaps the most powerful insight. The desire to “share what we have learned with each other” is not just about social interaction; it is a strategy for knowledge co-creation, reinforcement, and application. This collaborative culture is the fertile ground in which innovation can truly take root and flourish [27]. Therefore, future research and innovation training programs should incorporate sufficient opportunities for learners to communicate and share experiences [28], in order to improve teaching effectiveness and promote the application of knowledge in clinical practice.

Limitations

This single-site study may limit transferability due to self-selection and social desirability biases, and the absence of administrators’ perspectives. Findings are also shaped by the specific context of tertiary public hospitals in mainland China-characterized by high patient volumes, fixed nurse-to-patient ratios, mandated but often unsupported continuing education, and limited institutional research infrastructure. An organizational culture that prioritizes clinical duties, centralized decision-making, and policy emphasis on service efficiency may further constrain the applicability of results to settings with stronger research support and protected scholarly time. Future multi-site studies across different hospital levels, incorporating administrative input, are recommended.

Conclusions

Emergency nurses support integrating research innovation training into clinical education but require robust institutional support to address barriers. Administrators should develop tailored systems to foster a sustainable culture of inquiry and innovation. These findings can inform policy reforms in nursing education across similar high-pressure settings.
