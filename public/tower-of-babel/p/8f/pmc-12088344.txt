Reducing Postoperative Opioids in Pediatric Orthopaedics: An Updated Tiered Prescription Guideline with a “Tier 0”
Authors: Mikaela H Sullivan, Courtney E Baker, Daniel S Ubl, Elizabeth B Habermann, A Noelle Larson, Todd A Milbrandt
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is growing data on strategies to reduce opioid prescriptions in pediatric orthopaedics. Our institution implemented tiered guidelines for opioid discharge prescriptions in 2018 to standardize prescribing patterns across our entire pediatric orthopaedics practice. In 2022, we updated the tiered system with a goal to further minimize the quantity of opioids prescribed and eliminate opioids altogether for certain procedures. The aim of this project was to evaluate the effects of the updated tiered guidelines for discharge opioid prescribing following common pediatric orthopaedic procedures.MethodsA quality improvement project was conducted at a single academic institution. Guidelines for discharge opioid prescriptions were updated in January 2022 and established 5 tiers of increasing invasiveness for 28 procedures. The updated guidelines included a “Tier 0” which provided no opioids, reorganized surgical tiers based on prior data, and decreased the doses recommended for tiers. Opioid prescriptions were reported as oral morphine equivalents (OMEs). Univariate tests were performed to assess statistically significant differences before and after implementation of updated guideline eras, comparing 2020–2021 and 2022.ResultsOverall, 747 patients (2020–2021, n ​= ​500; 2022, n ​= ​247) were included. The proportion of patients receiving discharge opioid prescriptions decreased from 89.8% to 80.6% (P ​< ​0.05), between 2020 and 2021 and 2022. This resulted in decreased overall OME prescribed (median 75 vs 45, P ​< ​0.05) and OME prescribed in those >10 years (135 vs 113, P ​< ​0.05) while 30-day refill rates (9.4% vs 6.1%), and OME per kilogram in those <11 years of age (median 0.9 vs 0.8) remained unchanged (both P ​> ​0.05). Discharge prescriptions falling within overall guidelines increased after guideline implementation (83.3% vs 85.4%), though this was not statistically significant and guideline adherence was low among Tier 0 ​at 31%.ConclusionsIncluding “Tier 0” of procedures receiving no opioid prescriptions, reorganizing procedures within tiers, and decreasing the recommended doses to an already strict tiered guideline for discharge opioid prescriptions significantly decreased the quantity of opioids prescribed. We noted continued excellent overall adherence and, importantly, no overall increase in refills, with areas for improvement in Tier 0. Quality improvement projects can continue to reduce the amount of opioids prescribed following pediatric orthopaedic procedures.Key Concepts(1)Updated tiered guidelines, including a “Tier 0” for discharge opioid prescriptions following pediatric orthopaedic procedures were effective in reducing the quantity of opioids provided at discharge.(2)A continued high adherence rate was observed with the updated guidelines, though areas for improvement include adherence to no opioids in Tier 0.(3)Refill rates remained similar after implementation of the updated guidelines.(4)The study results suggest opportunities for continued improvement projects to reduce the number of unnecessary opioids prescribed to pediatric orthopaedic patients.Level of EvidenceIII.Keywords: Pediatric orthopaedics, Opioid, Guidelines, Prescription, Tiered

Introduction

Given the ongoing opioid epidemic, opioid prescribing is a priority for quality improvement in orthopaedic surgery. Both adult and pediatric patients who receive legitimate opioid prescriptions are at increased risk of future opioid misuse [1,2]. The opioid prescription size and duration are associated with subsequent opioid misuse [3,4], which makes initial opioid prescribing an opportunity for intervention [[4], [5], [6]]. Orthopaedic surgeons are among the main prescribers of opioids and have the ability to make a strong impact [7]. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons publishes guidelines for safer pain management, including standardized opioid prescription protocols that utilize practice-based use consensus, enforcement of strict limits on quantities prescribed, and restricted use for nonsurgical patients [8].

In pediatric orthopaedic patients specifically, opioids are often overprescribed [9,10]. Studies have shown 56%–78% of prescriptions written for pediatric patients after orthopaedic procedures remain unused [9,11,12]. Several institutions implemented opioid prescribing guidelines for pediatric orthopaedic patients, resulting in an overall decrease in the quantity of opioids prescribed [[13], [14], [15]]. Despite this, standardized analgesia guidelines for pediatric orthopaedic procedures are historically lacking, allowing for variation between institutions and providers.

Our institution implemented tiered guidelines in 2018 to both standardize analgesia practices for orthopaedic pediatric procedures and decrease the amount of unnecessary opioids prescribed. Our guidelines separated common orthopaedic procedures into four tiers, in order of increasing invasiveness, and delegated the maximum number of discharge opioid doses allowed for each tier. Quality improvement (QI) analysis in 2019 revealed a 91% adherence rate to guidelines, a 62% reduction in overall oral morphine equivalents (OME) prescribed, and no difference in refill prescription rate [14]. In 2022, our institution sought to improve upon these results by updating our guidelines to further decrease the amount of unnecessary opioids prescribed based on our prior findings and experience. We hypothesized that discharge medications could be further decreased while maintaining good compliance and pain control.

The aim of this project was to evaluate the effects of the updated tiered guidelines for discharge opioid prescriptions following common pediatric orthopaedic procedures. We sought to determine if even more stringent opioid guidelines were effective and clinically practical. Primary outcomes included total opioid doses prescribed at discharge, compliance with guidelines recommendations, and opioid refills within 30 days at discharge.

Materials and methods

This was a QI project at a single academic center with 5 orthopaedic surgeons from January 2020 to December 2022, building off a QI project started in 2018 (Fig. 1), and our institutional review board deemed this study to be exempt from institutional review board approval. Methods were modeled after the original study performed at our institution [14].

Original discharge tiered opioid prescribing guidelines for oxycodone and hydrocodone/acetaminophen [14].

In 2018, 28 common pediatric orthopaedic procedures were organized into four groups with different levels of invasiveness by consensus decision-making by the practice partners, and tiered guidelines for opioid prescribing were matched with each level [14]. In January 2022, updated tiers (Table 1) and guidelines (Fig. 2) were implemented based on our initial QI analysis. The changes included: (1) creation of a “Tier 0” comprised of the least invasive surgeries that would receive no opioid prescriptions; (2) reorganization of Tiers 1–4 by anatomic location and acuity of surgery; and (3) decrease in the amount of opioid doses prescribed in Tier 1 (from 18 to 6 doses), Tier 2 (from 30 to 15 doses), and Tier 3 (from 42 to 30 doses). As is standard in our practice, opioid prescriptions are a tertiary pain control option following acetaminophen and nonsteroidal antiinflammatory medications. At the beginning of every quarter, when new residents rotate onto pediatric orthopaedics, the guidelines were reviewed. No additional education was provided, and there was no formal review process for implementation to check compliance with guidelines. Discharge prescriptions are written primarily by residents and advanced practice providers under the supervision of attending surgeons. Postdischarge prescriptions are requested through the patient portal and written by the resident physician or advanced practice provider or during postoperative visits with the surgeon. For the purposes of this study, tracked refill prescriptions are the number of filled refills (see Fig. 2).

Patients age 0 through 17 years who underwent any of the listed procedures during the study interval were included. Patients who underwent surgery from January 2020 to December 2021 comprised the original guideline cohort, and patients who underwent surgery from January to December 2022 comprised the updated guideline cohort. Patient demographics, surgical details, discharge opioid prescription amounts, and refills were collected. Refills were defined as any opioid prescription written from 1 to 30 days after discharge in our healthcare system. Opioid naivety was defined as no exposure to opioid pain medications 8–90 days before surgery.

To evaluate the effects of the updated opioid prescribing guidelines, opioid prescriptions, compliance with guidelines, and refill rates were compared between original guideline (2020–2021) and updated guideline (2022) cohorts. Patients in both cohorts were evaluated according to updated tiers to allow for direct comparison of opioid prescriptions (ie patients in the original guidelines were reorganized by the updated tiers when comparing opioids). All opioid prescriptions were compared in units of oral morphine equivalents (OMEs). Patients aged 11 or older were evaluated by OME and patients 10 or younger were analyzed by OME per kilogram (OME/kg). Statistical calculations include mean, median, standard deviation (SD), and interquartile range (IQR) with univariate comparison between original and updated guideline cohorts using Wilcoxon rank sum and t tests for continuous variables and categorical variables with χ2 and Fisher exact tests. All statistical analysis was performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Probability values ​< ​0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient and cohort demographics

Patients in the original guideline cohort and patients in the updated guideline cohort did not differ significantly in age, sex, or opioid naïve status when evaluated as an entire cohort or by tier (Table 2). There were 500 procedures in the original guideline cohort and 247 procedures in the updated guideline cohort. When compared within each tier, the distribution of procedures was not statistically significantly different between original and updated guideline cohorts (P ​> ​.05).

Original and updated guideline cohort comparison

Total OME prescribed at discharge decreased by 40% following implementation of updated guidelines from a median of 75 OME (IQR: 0 to 900) to 45 OME (IQR: 0 to 405), in original and updated guideline cohorts, respectively (P ​< ​.01) (Table 3). A similar statistically significant decrease in total OME was observed in the subgroup of patients older than 10 years (P ​= ​.02) but not in those 10 years and younger (P ​= ​.07). The number of patients’ discharge prescriptions that met OME guidelines was similar between the original and updated guidelines, with 83% and 85% meeting guidelines (P ​= ​.46). The opioid refill rate within 30 days of discharge was not significantly different in the original and updated guideline cohorts, 9% vs 6% (P ​= ​.12). After implementation of updated guidelines, the percentage of patients discharged without any opioids significantly increased from 10% to 19% (P ​< ​.01).

The number of prescriptions that met OME guidelines significantly increased for Tier 0 procedures, from 10% to 31% (P ​< ​.01), and this held for subgroup analysis of patients greater than 10 years old. Tier 1, 2, 3, and 4 procedures had similar rates of prescriptions that met OME guidelines, ranging from 90% or above in the updated guideline subcohorts. There was no difference in opioid refill rates within 30 days of discharge after guidelines were updated in all tiers (all P ​> ​.05). The number of patients that received no opioids at discharge significantly increased in Tier 0 and Tier 1, from 10% to 31% (P ​= ​.01) and from 21% to 40% (P ​< ​.01), respectively, but no significant differences in the other tiers (all P ​> ​.05).

Discussion

Reducing the amount of opioids prescribed and standardizing prescribing practices is a national priority, especially within orthopaedic surgery. Few evidence-based studies, however, have focused this priority within the pediatric orthopaedic population [16], leading to our institution's implementation of tiered guidelines in 2018 and updated guidelines in 2022. This study demonstrated that even more stringent guidelines can further decrease the amount of opioids prescribed and maintain adherence without a concomitant increase in refill rates, and argues for a continues push in pediatric orthopaedics to decrease opioid prescriptions in this population.

From 1999 to 2016, the mortality rate from opioid overdoses increased nearly three times in the pediatric and adolescent population [17]. Recent literature has indicated that prescription opioid use in adolescence is associated with future misuse [6]. Other pediatric surgical specialties have shown the incidence of persistent opioid use following surgery was 4.4% compared to 0.1% in nonsurgical control groups [1] and have created guidelines to decrease the amount of discharge opioid prescriptions after surgical procedures [18]. A 2017 systematic review on opioid prescriptions after orthopaedic surgeries, however, found no basis for prescription recommendations, calling for quantitative research focusing on pediatric prescribing and pain management [19]. Several studies since then have identified overprescribing following pediatric orthopaedic procedures, with up to 78% of prescriptions remaining unused [11,12] and other institutions have implemented guidelines that decreased the number of opioids prescribed [13,15].

In order to reduce the quantity of opioids prescribed at discharge and standardize opioid-prescribing practices, our institution implemented prescribing guidelines with a tier-based system in 2018 [14]. Implementation of these guidelines reduced the overall OME prescribed at discharge by 62%, while reaching guideline adherence of 91% and avoiding an increase in refill rates. With these promising results, we implemented more stringent, updated guidelines that (1) added a “Tier 0” that provides no opioid prescriptions; (2) reorganized Tiers 1–4 by anatomic location and acuity of surgery; and (3) decreased the doses of OME prescribed in Tiers 1, 2, and 3.

The first goal of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of the updated tiered discharge opioid prescribing guidelines to reduce the quantity of opioids prescribed. Our results showed the implementation of these updated guidelines reduced the overall OME prescribed by 40%. This reduction was largely the product of the significant reduction of opioids in two of the procedure tiers. Tier 0, with recommended guidelines of 0 doses, revealed a decreased median OME prescribed from 75 to 38. Tier 1, with updated opioid guidelines reduced from 18 to 6 doses, showed a decrease in median OME prescribed from 23 to 15. Further, the proportion of patients discharged without opioids significantly increased after the updated guidelines were implemented, from 10% to 19%. These results demonstrate that our more stringent prescription guidelines had the greatest effect on the least invasive surgical procedures. As the tier-based opioid quantities were compared via the updated procedure organization structure (as seen in Table 1), the effect of the reorganized tiers on opioid prescriptions cannot be causally determined.

The second goal of the study was to evaluate adherence to recommendations. In our updated analysis, original guidelines demonstrated adherence of 91%, and updated guidelines demonstrated a similar adherence of 85%. This indicates the updated 5-tiered prescribing guideline was widely followed by providers. Adherence to guidelines was only significantly different in Tier 0, with 10% of the original guideline cohort in concordance with the new guideline of 0 opioid doses for these procedures and 31% of the updated guideline cohort in compliance. The findings of this subset demonstrate improvement in discharging patients without opioids but also an area for improvement. There are multiple possible reasons compliance in the new Tier 0 was low: provider lack of familiarity with the new guidelines, provider hesitation with providing no opioids, among others. Review of the guideline results undertaken as part of the QI process and continued familiarity with the tiers may increase adherence moving forward.

The third goal of the study was to evaluate opioid refill rates within 30 days of discharge to ensure the decreased opioid discharge guidelines met the patient's postoperative pain control needs. There was no difference in refill rate between the original guideline and updated guideline cohorts. Tier-specific analysis also showed no difference in refill rates between cohorts. This demonstrates that the updated discharge opioid guidelines did not shift the opioid burden to increased opioid refills, presumably confirming that patients' pain was adequately controlled with the newest guidelines, however, direct assessment of patient pain control was not performed.

While the mean and median OME prescribed increased sequentially for Tiers 1–4 as expected, interestingly, Tier 0 had a higher observed mean and median OME than Tier 1, underlying the lower updated guideline compliance rates of the Tier 0 group compared to other tiers. This finding is also secondary to the higher percentage of patients prescribed no opioids at discharge in the Tier 1 group compared to the Tier 0 group (40% compared to 31%). This suggests further review of procedures in Tiers 0 and 1 is necessary, likely involving additional reorganization of procedures within these specific tiers. This may also be due to the shift in reassigning procedures to different tiers at the start of 2022. In the initial tiered guidelines, the original Tier 1 had the lowest rate of compliance (67%), at which time the authors suggested further review of the procedures assigned to Tiers 1 and 2 and the role of opioids therein. In this study, the updated Tier 1 had a high rate of compliance (91%) similar to Tiers 2–4, suggesting reorganization of the procedures between Tiers 1 and 2 had been appropriate.

Analysis of our updated tiered guidelines is comparable to other comprehensive guidelines. Jones et al. implemented a 4-tiered guideline for pediatric orthopaedic procedures with an electronic medical record order set and demonstrated a similar 90% or greater compliance and 54% reduction in opioids prescribed [13]. The authors evaluated initial data of overall opioid usage after certain procedures and arranged the procedures into minor, moderate, major, and spinal fusion tiers by amount of opioids used historically. They then made opioid-dosing recommendations based on the average doses required. This is similar to our current study in that the tiers are arranged in order of increasing invasiveness or perceived need for opioid pain medications. In contrast, however, our study sought to challenge the historical dosing for all tiers to further decrease the amounts of opioids prescribed rather than simply standardizing the previously accepted doses for procedures. Jones et al. did, however, include nonopioid pain medications to their guidelines, which optimized multimodal pain management and ensured that patients are discharged with these modalities. To our knowledge, these (theirs and ours) are the only available tiered prescribing guidelines in the pediatric orthopaedic literature. Strengths of our guidelines include the implementation of even more stringent dosing guidelines that are not overly granular and easily adopted, without adding another order set. Further, the framework can be easily adapted to unlisted procedures to fit surgeons’ practices.

This study is not without limitations. In order to provide direct comparison between the original and updated guideline cohorts, the tier comparisons were based on the new procedure organizational structure (ie, patients were grouped into tiers based on the updated procedure groups in Table 1 in both the original and updated guideline cohorts). Though this limits the ability to make conclusions on the impact of procedure reorganization, it does not impact the comparison of the overall original and updated guideline cohorts. Given multiple changes were made in the update guidelines and varied rates of change and adherence were observed in the tiers, our results do not demonstrate cause-and-effect relationships and could, rather, be attributed to a cultural shift expedited by the guidelines. Statistical results for patients less than or equal to 10 years old in Tiers 0, 2, 3, and 4 are at risk for small sample size bias. Also, guideline levels were established based on physician experience and evaluation of original guideline results. We did not capture the number of opioids used at home or pain control scores after discharge. Because of this, we are unable to make definitive conclusions on pain control with these implementations, though low refill rates are reassuring. Future studies may benefit from postoperative pain journals to further assess at-home pain control from implemented changes. Refill rates only track refilled prescriptions at the study institution and cannot account for prescriptions from outside providers or emergency departments. There were no other formal changes made to pediatric orthopaedic pain management protocols during this study, though this was not strictly controlled given the retrospective quality improvement study. Given that the updated guidelines made multiple improvements at the same time, we cannot be certain if the majority of the effect was made manifest by a subset or a single one of the changes. It is assumed that they all played a role in further decreasing total opioids prescribed. Further, these prescribing guidelines do not take into account age groups for each procedure, which may affect results. Lastly, as the cohort periods are consecutive, there is a limited washout period between the cohorts to allow a direct comparison between guidelines, though this would underestimate our findings.

Conclusions

Updated, stringent tiered guidelines for discharge opioid prescriptions following pediatric orthopaedic procedures were effective in further reducing the quantity of opioids provided at discharge, maintained high adherence, and did not result in increased refill rates. We recognize that opioid prescriptions remain an incompletely optimized aspect of current practice, with additional benefits achievable as we minimize the real risks of opioids to our patients. While these study findings are promising, our results suggest opportunities for improvement as well as an opportunity for guidelines to push the field even further toward decreased opioid prescriptions. Further study should include even more stringent opioid-dosing guidelines, reorganization of Tier 0 and 1 procedures to optimize the patients who get no opioids at discharge, and consideration of nonopioid pain medication guidelines to ensure patients are discharged with multimodal strategies for pain control. Quality improvement projects should continue to strive to reduce the number of unnecessary opioids prescribed to pediatric orthopaedic patients.
