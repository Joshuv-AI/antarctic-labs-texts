Multimodal Imaging Unmasks Occult Acute Anterior Myocardial Infarction After Blunt Chest Trauma in a Young Male Patient

History of Presentation

A 36-year-old man with no known past medical history was visiting from Italy when he experienced sudden, severe substernal chest pain while surfing. While in the water, he was struck by a large wave, causing blunt anterior chest trauma against his surfboard. He immediately developed 10/10 crushing pressure-like pain localized to the center of his chest that was constant, associated with shortness of breath, nausea, and diaphoresis. The pain worsened with movement and deep inspiration and did not improve with nitroglycerin administered in the field. He denied radiation to the jaw or arm and had never experienced similar symptoms. Emergency medical services were called, and the patient was transported to the emergency department, where he remained in severe distress with ongoing 8/10 chest pressure despite multiple doses of sublingual nitroglycerin. An initial electrocardiogram (ECG) in the hospital showed diffuse tall, narrow, tented T waves in V3-V5 along with low-amplitude P waves seen in Figure 1.

Electrocardiogram Showing Diffuse Tall T Waves in V3-V5 With Low-Amplitude P Waves

On arrival, the patient was alert but in moderate distress. Vital signs were stable (blood pressure 128/82 mm Hg, heart rate 88 beats/min, respiratory rate 18 breaths/min, oxygen saturation 98% on room air, and temperature 37.1 °C). He appeared clammy and diaphoretic. Cardiovascular examination revealed regular rate and rhythm without murmurs, rubs, or gallops. Jugular venous pressure was not elevated. Lungs were clear to auscultation bilaterally. The abdomen was soft and nontender. Extremities showed no edema or cyanosis. Neurologic examination was nonfocal.

Past Medical History

The patient had no reported past medical history, no cardiac risk factors, and no family history of premature coronary artery disease. Social history was significant for active tobacco use (approximately 5 cigarettes daily), daily electronic cigarette use (vaping), and occasional cannabis use. Patient denied alcohol, cocaine, and other illicit drugs. Patient lived in Italy and was visiting a friend in the United States at the time of the event.

Differential Diagnosis

The diagnostic quandary is centered on trauma-related causes, including musculoskeletal chest wall pain, rib fracture, pneumothorax, pulmonary contusion, and esophageal injury. Cardiac etiologies were considered lower on the list because of the patient's young age and lack of risk factors. Other cardiac possibilities included coronary artery spasm, coronary embolism, myocarditis, and Takotsubo cardiomyopathy triggered by acute stress. Pulmonary embolism and aortic dissection were also entertained given the trauma mechanism and acute dyspnea. The combination of trauma history with rising troponin levels necessitated a systematic approach: ruling out life-threatening noncardiac causes first, then confirming myocardial injury, and finally proceeding to definitive coronary angiography. This stepwise reasoning excluded trauma-only etiologies and nonischemic cardiac causes, leading directly to the diagnosis of acute coronary occlusion.1

Investigations

Initial laboratory results showed leukocytosis (white blood cell count 15.7K/μL), hypokalemia (potassium 3.2 mEq/L), high-sensitivity troponin I 197 ng/L, and B-type natriuretic peptide 17 pg/mL. A chest radiograph showed no acute cardiopulmonary pathology. Serial troponin I rose rapidly to 2,600 ng/L at 3 hours and 5,700 ng/L at 6 hours, with new Q waves developing in the anterior leads on repeat ECG seen in Figure 2.

Q Waves Developing and Most Prominent in Leads V2 and V3

Computed tomography angiography (CTA) performed to exclude pulmonary embolism and aortic dissection revealed mild central bronchial wall thickening without consolidation, effusion, or pneumothorax; no aortic dissection or aneurysm; but crucially demonstrated coronary vascular calcifications and clear hypoperfusion of the left ventricle as seen in Figure 3. Corresponding sagittal views of the chest CTA in Figure 4 further confirm significant left ventricular hypoperfusion, warranting to obtain an echocardiogram. CTA chest in Figure 5 demonstrates proximal left anterior descending (LAD) artery contrast filling defect.

A formal transthoracic echocardiogram performed before coronary angiography demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 40% to 45%, with hypokinesis involving the mid-to-distal anteroseptal, anterior, anteroapical, and inferoapical segments, as illustrated in Videos 1 and 2 (apical 2- and 4-chamber views, respectively). Serial ECGs demonstrated evolving anterior changes, including tall, narrow, symmetric “tented” T waves progressing to more localized anterior/precordial involvement with eventual Q-wave formation.

Management

The patient was given aspirin 325 mg, sublingual nitroglycerin, morphine, and hydromorphone in the emergency department. After CTA and echocardiography findings confirmed acute anterior ischemia, he was taken emergently to the cardiac catheterization laboratory via right radial access. Selective coronary angiography demonstrated 100% thrombotic occlusion of the proximal-to-mid left anterior descending (LAD) artery due to plaque rupture, with the distal LAD not visualized on initial injection (Video 3). Intravascular ultrasound confirmed thrombotic occlusion. Aspiration thrombectomy was performed first, with clot removal seen in Figure 6, followed by percutaneous coronary intervention with a drug-eluting stent postdilated to 4.0 mm, restoring TIMI flow grade 3 depicted in Video 4. The left ventricular end-diastolic pressure was 14 mm Hg. The procedure was uncomplicated, with hemostasis achieved using a TR BAND (Terumo Medical Corporation). Residual moderate-severe distal left circumflex disease was managed medically given the patient's young age. After the procedure, the patient remained hemodynamically stable. Troponin peaked and down-trended. Repeat echocardiography showed improving wall motion and ejection fraction recovery.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient remained hemodynamically stable after percutaneous coronary intervention. Serial high-sensitivity troponin levels peaked and then down-trended. Repeat transthoracic echocardiography before discharge showed improvement in left ventricular ejection fraction and resolution of wall motion abnormalities. He was discharged on hospital day 4 on dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin 81 mg and clopidogrel 75 mg daily), high-intensity statin, β-blocker, and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor,2 in accordance with current AHA/ACC guideline-directed medical therapy for ST-elevation myocardial infarction.3

At 30-day outpatient follow-up, the patient was asymptomatic with no recurrent chest pain. Repeat echocardiography demonstrated complete recovery of left ventricular ejection fraction to the normal range. He had successfully quit smoking and was participating in cardiac rehabilitation. The patient returned to Italy with instructions for continued cardiology follow-up and medical therapy.

Discussion

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) after blunt chest trauma is an uncommon but clinically significant entity, occurring in <1% of patients with blunt thoracic injury. Most cases of blunt cardiac injury involve myocardial contusion or arrhythmias, whereas coronary artery injury leading to AMI remains exceedingly rare and is largely described in isolated case reports and small series.4 When coronary involvement does occur, proposed mechanisms include plaque rupture, intimal dissection, thrombosis, or vasospasm, with the LAD artery most frequently affected due to its anterior anatomic position.4,5

In young adults, AMI accounts for only 2% to 6% of all myocardial infarctions, typically in the presence of traditional risk factors such as smoking, dyslipidemia, or substance use.6 The occurrence of trauma-induced plaque rupture resulting in complete coronary occlusion in a young patient without significant cardiovascular risk factors, as demonstrated in this case, represents a rare and diagnostically challenging clinical entity. This case is particularly instructive in highlighting how blunt chest trauma can precipitate acute plaque rupture and how multimodal imaging can unmask an otherwise occult myocardial infarction.

Serial high-sensitivity troponin measurement provided the first objective evidence of myocardial injury, rising dramatically from 197 to 5,700 ng/L within hours.7 The diagnostic turning point was CAT, initially obtained to exclude pulmonary embolism. Unexpected findings of left ventricular hypoperfusion and a proximal coronary filling defect prompted urgent escalation to invasive coronary angiography.8 Transthoracic echocardiography further corroborated the diagnosis by demonstrating regional wall motion abnormalities precisely localizing to the LAD territory.1 Together, this multimodal imaging approach (CTA + echocardiography + biomarkers) established a rapid and accurate diagnosis, enabling timely reperfusion. Without these complementary modalities, the infarction may have progressed, increasing the risk of adverse outcomes such as malignant ventricular arrhythmias, cardiogenic shock, or permanent myocardial damage.

Although plaque rupture is classically associated with established atherosclerotic disease, it may occur in younger individuals with minimal underlying plaque burden, potentially accelerated by smoking or systemic inflammation. In this context, blunt chest trauma may act as a trigger through mechanical shear stress, catecholamine surge, coronary vasospasm, or thrombogenic activation, ultimately precipitating acute coronary occlusion.9 This case challenges the conventional assumption that myocardial infarction occurs predominantly in older patients with traditional cardiovascular risk factors and underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in trauma-related chest pain, particularly when accompanied by rising cardiac biomarkers.

From a systems perspective, this case highlights the value of incorporating advanced imaging into the diagnostic pathway for undifferentiated chest pain in the emergency setting. Early use of CTA and point-of-care echocardiography may reduce missed diagnoses of acute coronary syndromes and improve door-to-balloon times in atypical presentations.8,10 Future studies are warranted to evaluate the diagnostic yield, cost-effectiveness, and optimal integration of multimodal imaging strategies in patients presenting with trauma-related chest pain and equivocal initial electrocardiographic findings.

Conclusions

Multimodal imaging with CTA and echocardiography rapidly identified acute anterior myocardial infarction in a young male patient after blunt chest trauma, enabling timely reperfusion and excellent outcome. This case underscores the importance of maintaining a broad differential and using advanced imaging when classic ECG changes are initially overlooked.

Take-Home Messages
