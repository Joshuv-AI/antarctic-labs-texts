Insomnia‐Driven Hippocampal Atrophy: Evidence From Bidirectional Mendelian Randomization
Authors: Dandan Han, Xiang Li, Shaohua Zhao, Jiayin Liu, Xiaojiang Wang, Yu Jiang, Junrong Ding, Baojing Wang, Erfeng Cui, Ding Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundObservational studies link insomnia to hippocampal atrophy, yet causal inference remains limited by confounding and reverse causality. While Mendelian randomization (MR) studies have explored sleep‐brain associations, bidirectional causality between insomnia and hippocampal subfield volumes remains unaddressed.MethodsWe conducted bidirectional two‐sample MR using genome‐wide association study (GWAS) summary data. Genetic instruments for insomnia (N = 462,341) and eight hippocampal volume imaging‐derived phenotypes (IDPs; N ≈ 31,968–33,219) were selected (p < 5 × 10⁻⁸, LD r² < 0.001), with F‐statistics > 10 ensuring robustness. Primary analyses employed inverse‐variance weighted regression, supplemented by sensitivity analyses (MR‐Egger, weighted median, MR‐PRESSO) to assess pleiotropy and heterogeneity. Multiple testing was controlled using Benjamini–Hochberg FDR.ResultsForward MR revealed inverse associations between genetically proxied insomnia and six of eight hippocampal phenotypes: T1 left hippocampus volume (OR = 0.62, 95% CI = 0.43–0.89; P FDR = 0.014), left hippocampus gray matter volume (OR = 0.63, 95% CI = 0.47–0.84; P FDR = 0.008), right hippocampus gray matter volume (OR = 0.69, 95% CI = 0.49–0.98; P FDR = 0.035), left and right hippocampal volumes from automated subcortical segmentation (ORs = 0.60, 95% CI = 0.42–0.86, P FDR = 0.010; and 0.71, 0.51–0.99, P FDR = 0.037), and right whole‐hippocampus volume from subfield segmentation (OR = 0.61, 95% CI = 0.43–0.86; P FDR = 0.010). Sensitivity analyses supported robustness; reverse MR detected no evidence of effects of hippocampal volumes on insomnia (P FDR = 1).ConclusionsGenetically proxied insomnia shows strong genetic evidence consistent with a causal reduction in hippocampal volumes. Clinically, early intervention in insomnia, particularly through cognitive‐behavioral therapy for insomnia, may help preserve hippocampal health. This study highlights the importance of addressing insomnia as a public health priority. Future research should investigate the underlying biological mechanisms and incorporate more diverse cohorts to enhance the generalizability of these findings.Keywords: brain imaging, genome‐wide association study, hippocampal volume, insomnia, Mendelian randomization

Introduction

Insomnia—characterized by persistent difficulties in initiating or maintaining sleep or early awakening—is common (≈30% of adults) and increasingly implicated in neuropsychiatric and neurodegenerative conditions [1, 2, 3]. Observational neuroimaging studies consistently associate chronic insomnia with hippocampal structural alterations, a region central to memory consolidation and emotion regulation. Reports of smaller hippocampal gray matter and subfield‐specific changes have been noted in individuals with insomnia [4, 5]. However, these associations remain vulnerable to residual confounding and reverse causation [6], limiting causal inference and translation.

Mendelian randomization (MR) strengthens causal inference by leveraging genetic instruments less susceptible to confounding and reverse causation [7, 8, 9]. Prior MR work has linked several sleep traits to global brain structure and has often emphasized cortical morphology rather than hippocampal segmentation–based outcomes. For example, daytime napping has been associated with total brain volume without hippocampus‐specific effects [10], and large‐scale genetic analyses have questioned a causal link between habitual sleep duration and brain structure [11]. However, few MR studies have directly targeted hippocampal volumes derived from automated subcortical segmentation (“aseg”) and hippocampal subfield segmentation (“sub‐seg”) as primary outcomes—a gap that is clinically meaningful given the hippocampus's central role in memory and emotional regulation and its potential sensitivity to sleep disturbance.

Building on this gap, the present study centers on eight hippocampal imaging‐derived phenotypes (IDPs) spanning T1‐based bilateral hippocampal volumes, gray‐matter‐constrained measures, aseg‐derived volumes, and whole‐hippocampus sub‐seg outputs, enabling an anatomically focused test of directionality between insomnia and hippocampal structure.

Clarifying directionality bears immediate clinical and public‐health relevance. If insomnia drives hippocampal atrophy, early treatment—particularly cognitive‐behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT‐I) and, where appropriate, pharmacologic options such as dual orexin receptor antagonists—may help preserve hippocampal integrity and cognitive function; conversely, the absence of reverse effects would argue against targeting hippocampal volume to treat insomnia itself. We hypothesized that genetically proxied insomnia leads to reduced hippocampal volumes, whereas hippocampal volumes do not causally influence insomnia. Accordingly, we conducted a bidirectional two‐sample MR across eight hippocampal IDPs with extensive robustness checks.

Methods

Study Design and Data Sources

A bidirectional two‐sample MR analysis was conducted to evaluate the causal relationship between insomnia and hippocampal volume IDPs (Figure 1). Genetic instruments for insomnia (exposure) and eight hippocampal IDPs (outcomes) were derived from separate genome‐wide association study (GWAS) datasets of European ancestry. Summary‐level data sources included the insomnia GWAS curated by the MRC Integrative Epidemiology Unit (IEU), comprising 462,341 individuals (9,851,867 single nucleotide polymorphisms [SNPs]), and hippocampal IDPs GWAS obtained from the UK Biobank BIG40 resource (Table S1, Figures S1 and S2).

Schematic workflow of the bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis. The framework outlines the causal inference process between insomnia and hippocampal imaging‐derived phenotypes (IDPs). Key steps include: (1) genetic instrument selection for insomnia (exposure) and eight hippocampal IDPs (outcomes) from GWAS summary data (p < 5 × 10⁻⁸, LD clumping: r² < 0.001); (2) forward and reverse MR analyses using inverse‐variance weighted (IVW) regression; (3) sensitivity analyses (MR‐Egger, weighted median, MR‐PRESSO) to assess pleiotropy and heterogeneity.

We focused on eight widely used hippocampal IDPs—bilateral T1‐FIRST volumes, gray‐matter–constrained volumes (FAST), automated subcortical segmentation (aseg), and whole‐hippocampus sub‐segmentation—because they capture complementary aspects of hippocampal macrostructure directly implicated in memory consolidation and neurodegenerative vulnerability. These IDPs are standardized within the UK Biobank processing pipelines and have established reliability; detailed definitions are provided in Tables S1–S3.

To evaluate potential overlap bias, we estimated a conservative upper bound on sample overlap between the exposure (insomnia) GWAS (N = 462,341) and each hippocampal IDP GWAS (N ≈ 31,968–33,219) as max‐overlap = min (N exposure, N outcome)/max (N exposure, N outcome), assuming in the worst case that all outcome participants are nested within the exposure sample. As a rule of thumb, values < 10% are generally acceptable in two‐sample MR.

Genetic Instrument Selection

SNPs significantly associated with insomnia were selected from the above GWAS data, with a screening criterion of p < 5 × 10−8. To ensure the independence of instrumental variables (IVs), linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping was performed (r² < 0.001, kb = 10,000), excluding interdependent SNPs. Instrument strength was ensured by retaining SNPs with F‐statistics > 10 (F = β 2/SE2) to minimize weak instrument bias. These IVs must meet the three core assumptions of MR: relevance, independence, and exclusion restriction [12, 13]. Considering factors like Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, depression, schizophrenia, and traumatic brain injury as risk factors for hippocampus [14, 15, 16, 17, 18], this study searched the LDtrait website (https://ldlink.nih.gov) and excluded SNPs directly associated with these risk factors and those significantly related to brain IDPs (p < 5 × 10−8). Additionally, SNPs with inconsistent alleles between exposure and outcome, and palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequencies, were excluded.

MR and Sensitivity Analysis

The primary analysis method was the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method, providing a foundational causal estimate by averaging the Wald ratios of multiple SNPs. The IVW method assumes all IVs are valid with no horizontal pleiotropy. To verify the robustness of the results, various sensitivity analysis methods were used, including MR‐Egger regression, the weighted median method, simple mode, and weighted mode analysis. MR‐Egger regression was used to assess pleiotropy between IVs and outcome variables, with a non‐significant intercept (p > 0.05) indicating no significant horizontal pleiotropy. The weighted median method provides consistent causal effect estimates even if up to 50% of the IVs are invalid. Simple mode and weighted mode analyses were further used to confirm the robustness of the results [13].

To evaluate heterogeneity and pleiotropy among IVs, Cochran's Q test, funnel plot analysis, and the MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR‐PRESSO) test were performed. Cochran's Q test assesses heterogeneity among IVs, with a significant Q test (p < 0.05) indicating significant heterogeneity. Funnel plot analysis visualizes heterogeneity in the data. The MR‐PRESSO test detects and corrects for bias due to pleiotropy among IVs, with a significant MR‐PRESSO test (p < 0.05) indicating outliers to be removed and MR analysis to be rerun [19]. Additionally, leave‐one‐out analysis assesses the influence of individual SNPs on MR results, and scatter plots further visualize the correlation between exposure and outcome.

Statistical Validation

To assess the causal relationship between hippocampal volume IDPs and insomnia, this study also conducted reverse MR analysis, using the same SNP screening criteria and analysis methods as in the forward MR.

All data processing and analysis were performed using the TwoSampleMR and MRPRESSO packages in R version 4.4.0. Data processing steps included extracting exposure and outcome data from GWAS databases, merging data from different sources to ensure consistency of IVs, and standardizing extracted data to ensure comparability of analysis results.

We prespecified IVW as the primary estimator. Benjamini–Hochberg FDR at 5% was applied separately within each direction across the eight hippocampal IDPs (forward: insomnia → IDPs; reverse: IDPs → insomnia); P FDR values are reported. Estimates from MR‐Egger, weighted median, simple/weighted mode, and MR‐PRESSO are presented unadjusted as robustness evidence. We additionally performed post hoc power analyses with the mRnd calculator using outcome‐specific sample sizes, βxy = ln(OR), unit variances, and R 2 appropriate to each direction (0.0027 for the insomnia instruments in forward MR; IDP‐specific R 2 ≈ 0.007–0.024 in reverse MR). Full inputs and powers are provided in Tables S4 and S5.

Ethical Considerations

All contributing GWAS obtained approval from their respective ethics committees, and UK Biobank studies follow institutional and national guidelines. The present analysis used de‐identified, summary‐level data only and did not involve any new data collection or re‐identification procedures. Accordingly, it qualified for exemption from additional institutional review.

Results

Genetic Instrument Selection for Forward MR Analysis

Based on the GWAS summary statistics for insomnia, we initially identified 42 genome‐wide significant (p < 5 × 10−8) and mutually independent SNPs (Table S6). During subsequent quality control, nine palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequency were excluded. In addition, three SNPs showing significant associations with potential confounders (e.g., psychiatric disorders) and two SNPs significantly associated with brain IDPs were removed from downstream analyses (Table S7). In total, 28 SNPs were retained for causal‐inference analyses (Table S8). All retained instruments demonstrated adequate strength, with F‐statistics ranging from 29.85 to 199.34—each exceeding the conventional threshold of 10—indicating a low risk of weak‐instrument bias.

Primary MR Findings

Maximum exposure–outcome GWAS overlap across hippocampal IDPs was 6.91%–7.18% (< 10%); given instrument strength (all F > 10), overlap bias is likely minimal.

Sensitivity Analyses and Robustness Checks

Sensitivity analyses corroborated the primary findings. MR‐Egger regression demonstrated no evidence of directional pleiotropy (intercept p > 0.05 for all outcomes) (Table S9). Weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode methods consistently supported the inverse associations observed in IVW analyses (Figure 2).

However, MR‐PRESSO identified significant horizontal pleiotropy (p < 0.05) for three outcomes: T1 image left hippocampus volume, left whole‐hippocampus volume from sub‐seg, and right whole‐hippocampus volume from sub‐seg (Tables S9 and S10). After excluding outlier SNP (rs17709610), pleiotropy was mitigated for T1 image left hippocampus volume (p = 0.054) and right whole‐hippocampus volume from sub‐seg (p = 0.120). In contrast, pleiotropy persisted for left whole‐hippocampus volume from sub‐seg (p < 0.05), precluding causal inference for this specific measure.

Heterogeneity tests revealed no significant variability across SNPs for most outcomes (Cochran's Q p > 0.05), except for left hippocampal volume from aseg (Q = 40.17, p = 0.049). The application of random‐effects IVW models accounted for this residual heterogeneity, ensuring robust effect estimates. Leave‐one‐out analyses confirmed that no individual SNP disproportionately influenced the results (Figure 3). Scatter and funnel plots further validated the consistency and symmetry of effect estimates across instruments (Figures 4 and 5).

Post hoc power was high for most outcomes: left hippocampal gray matter (power = 0.98), left/right aseg volumes (0.99/0.84), and right whole‐hippocampus (sub‐seg, 0.98). Moderate power was observed for the right hippocampus on T1 (0.58). Full inputs and powers are listed in Table S4.

Reverse MR Analysis

Reverse MR analyses found no evidence of causal effects of hippocampal IDPs on insomnia after FDR control across the eight outcomes (all P FDR = 1, Figure S3). Formal power calculations (mRnd) indicated limited power to detect the small observed effects (β ≈ −0.01 to −0.02; power 0.05–0.42 across IDPs), despite the large outcome sample (N = 462,341) and modest instrument R 2 for IDPs (≈0.007–0.024). We therefore interpret these results as no evidence of effect rather than a definitive absence of effect (Table S5).

Discussion

This bidirectional MR study supports a likely causal role of insomnia in reduced hippocampal volumes, based on convergent IVW estimates and high statistical power for most forward outcomes. In contrast, reverse‐direction analyses yielded no evidence of effect, but power was limited for the small observed effect sizes (power ≤ 0.42), so very small reverse effects cannot be excluded. Sensitivity analyses (MR‐Egger, weighted median/mode, MR‐PRESSO) and strong instruments (F > 10), together with low GWAS overlap reported in the Results, reduce concern about major bias. Our findings refine prior MR work on sleep–brain relationships by adding hippocampal subfield–level resolution. Earlier studies largely emphasized global or cortical phenotypes (e.g., whole‐brain IDPs, cortical thickness/surface area) and did not interrogate hippocampal segmentation [20, 21]. By targeting hippocampal IDPs, we isolate region‐specific effects that broader endpoints may have obscured.

Several convergent mechanisms may explain why chronic insomnia leads to hippocampal atrophy (Figure 6). (i) HPA‐axis dysregulation and glucocorticoid neurotoxicity: insomnia is linked to elevated nocturnal cortisol and altered circadian secretion [22, 23], and glucocorticoid receptor overactivation in hippocampal neurons promotes dendritic retraction, synaptic loss, and apoptosis, particularly in CA fields and dentate gyrus [24]. (ii) Neuroinflammation and glial activation: experimental sleep loss triggers astrocytic phagocytosis and microglial activation, accelerating synaptic pruning and inflammatory cascades [25, 26], with clinical and translational data likewise linking sleep disturbance to heightened inflammatory signaling relevant to hippocampal vulnerability [27]. (iii) Impaired adult hippocampal neurogenesis: sleep deprivation reduces proliferation and survival of newborn dentate gyrus neurons and disrupts plasticity pathways (e.g., cAMP–PKA–CREB and neurotrophin signaling) [28]. (iv) Impaired metabolite clearance during reduced slow‐wave sleep: slow‐wave sleep supports glymphatic clearance of amyloid‐β and tau [29, 30], and insomnia‐related deficits may compromise this process and promote downstream damage. Together, these pathways provide a coherent biological context for the forward MR signal linking insomnia to smaller hippocampal volumes.

Mechanistic pathways linking insomnia to hippocampal atrophy.

Several factors could underlie the lack of detectable reverse causality. Insomnia pathophysiology is distributed across thalamocortical and limbic circuits, so any hippocampal contribution may be indirect, subfield‐specific, or state‐dependent and therefore diluted in aggregate MRI phenotypes [31]. Phenotypic heterogeneity—across insomnia subtypes (sleep‐onset vs. maintenance) and in stress resilience or genetic susceptibility—can further attenuate population‐average signals [32, 33]. In addition, measurement error and between‐pipeline differences among hippocampal IDPs, combined with the modest first‐stage R² noted above, reduce effective power and bias estimates toward the null.

The genetic evidence supports a prevention‐oriented perspective: timely identification and treatment of clinically significant insomnia may help preserve hippocampal health. First‐line CBT‐I remains central; where appropriate, dual orexin receptor antagonists can be considered within guideline‐concordant care [34, 35]. Future interventional studies could incorporate hippocampal‐sensitive outcomes (e.g., volumetric or subfield measures alongside cognition) to evaluate whether effective insomnia treatment is associated with measurable structural preservation.

Our reliance on European‐ancestry GWAS limits generalizability. Outcome instruments explained modest variance (≈0.007–0.024), constraining reverse‐direction power and leaving very small effects undetectable. Residual pleiotropy persisted for the left whole‐hippocampus (sub‐seg), so that metric should be interpreted cautiously.

Priorities include replication in ancestrally diverse cohorts, randomized or longitudinal interventions (e.g., CBT‐I or orexin‐pathway agents) with hippocampal volume or subfield endpoints, integration of multi‐omics to refine mechanisms and heterogeneity, and methodological improvements such as harmonized imaging pipelines and multivariable MR to reduce residual bias.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that insomnia may be a causal risk factor for reduced hippocampal volumes, with high power in the forward direction. Reverse analyses provided no evidence of effect, yet limited power means very small reverse effects cannot be ruled out. These results support a prevention‐first approach: early identification and guideline‐based CBT‐I, with dual orexin receptor antagonists when appropriate, may help preserve hippocampal integrity and related functions. For personalized medicine, integrating genetic risk with hippocampal imaging could aid risk stratification and treatment monitoring. Future multi‐omics work in ancestrally diverse, longitudinal, or interventional cohorts is needed to refine mechanisms and assess generalizability.
