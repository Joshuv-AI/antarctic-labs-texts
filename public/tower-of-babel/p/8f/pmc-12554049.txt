Improving calcium bioavailability from fish bone waste: the role of sodium hydroxide in bio‑calcium extraction from tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) bones
Authors: Theeraphol Senphan, Natthapong Mungmueang, Vachira Choommongkol, Chodsana Sriket, Hideki Kishimura, Phanat Kittiphattanabawon, Soottawat Benjakul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Calcium deficiency is a global health issue motivating the search for sustainable calcium sources from aquaculture waste. This study investigated sodium hydroxide (NaOH) concentration effects on bio‑calcium quality and bioavailability extracted from tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) bones. Tilapia bones were treated with 0.5 M, 1 M, and 2 M NaOH solutions for 30 min compared to untreated controls. Increasing NaOH concentrations decreased yield while improving brightness, ash content, and calcium bioavailability. The 2 M NaOH treatment produced bio‑calcium with highest calcium bioavailability (8.57 %), surpassing both control (7.26 %) and commercial calcium carbonate (0.72 %) by 12-fold. Higher NaOH concentrations reduced moisture, protein, and fat contents while increasing hydroxyproline and decreasing lipid oxidation. SEM showed smoother surfaces with homogeneous pores in 2 M NaOH-treated samples. ATR-FTIR and EDS confirmed consistent hydroxyapatite structure. This optimization provides a sustainable approach for converting fish waste into high-bioavailability calcium supplements, supporting circular economy in aquaculture while addressing global calcium deficiency.

Introduction

Bones, muscles, and many biological functions require calcium (Raskh, 2020). Many people cannot get enough calcium from diet, increasing their risk of osteoporosis and other bone problems (Arnold et al., 2021). Thus, developing sustainable, effective, and alternative calcium sources is popular. According to Terzioğlu et al. (2018), bio‑calcium from fish bones can replace conventional calcium sources like calcium carbonate and calcium citrate. Fish bones provide an abundant source of calcium phosphate as hydroxyapatite, structurally analogous to the mineral constituents of human bones and teeth (Okpe et al., 2024; Sriket, Niwet, Kuimalee, et al. (2023)). The resemblance renders bio‑calcium derived from fish bones highly accessible and readily assimilated by the body. Besides its elevated calcium concentration, bio‑calcium derived from fish bones also encompasses other beneficial constituents, like collagen and trace minerals, which may enhance its overall health advantages (Likittrakulwong et al., 2021; Sriket, Niwet, Kuimalee, et al. (2023)). Quality and safety considerations are fundamental when utilizing fish bones for bio‑calcium production, as comprehensive assessment protocols ensure the resulting products meet food and pharmaceutical standards (Bondoc & Șindilar, 2002). Washing, deproteinizing, and demineralizing fish bones are common steps in bio‑calcium extraction (Nemati et al., 2017; Sriket, Niwet, Kuimalee, et al. (2023)). The extraction procedure depends on the deproteinization agent concentration, such as NaOH (Idowu et al., 2020). The concentration of NaOH significantly affects the yield, purity, and overall quality of the resulting bio‑calcium (Owuamanam & Cree, 2020). Although there is growing interest in bio‑calcium derived from fish bones, studies examining the effects of NaOH concentration on the characteristics and calcium bioavailability of bio‑calcium extracted from tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) bones are limited. Bio‑calcium can be extracted from tilapia bone waste, as tilapia (O. niloticus) is one of the most widely farmed and consumed fish species globally (Arumugam et al., 2023).

Bio‑calcium extraction from marine fish like cod, salmon, and tuna has received more attention than from freshwater fish like tilapia. While different NaOH concentrations have been studied for protein removal from fish bones, their effects on calcium bioavailability are unclear. Most research on bio‑calcium has focused on either the extraction technique or its physical properties, but few have studied how processing parameters affect bioavailability. Furthermore, the optimal NaOH concentration for maintaining both protein removal efficiency and calcium bioavailability has not been systematically determined for tilapia bones specifically. This study addresses these research gaps by providing the first systematic comparison of NaOH concentrations (0.5 M, 1 M, and 2 M) for tilapia bone bio‑calcium extraction with emphasis on bioavailability assessment. Unlike previous studies that focused primarily on extraction yield and chemical composition, from marine species such as salmon (Idowu et al., 2020), cod (Venkatesan et al., 2015), and tuna (Nemati et al., 2017), this research prioritizes calcium bioavailability through comprehensive physicochemical and structural characterization. Tilapia-specific optimization is essential as it represents the second most cultured fish globally yet receives limited research attention compared to marine fish species (Ahmad Fara et al., 2015; Hart et al., 2022). Previous bio‑calcium studies have predominantly examined single alkaline concentrations without systematic bioavailability assessment (Owuamanam & Cree, 2020; Zhang et al., 2024). This research is necessary to bridge the knowledge gap between extraction methodology and biological functionality, enabling development of commercially viable bio‑calcium supplements from aquaculture waste that can effectively address global calcium deficiency while supporting sustainable practices.

The present study was therefore conducted to systematically evaluate the effects of varying NaOH concentrations (0.5 M, 1 M, and 2 M) on the physicochemical properties, structural characteristics, and calcium bioavailability of bio‑calcium derived from tilapia fish bones. This research aims to determine optimal processing conditions that maximize both protein removal efficiency and calcium bioavailability for sustainable calcium source development.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Acrylamide, tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane, bis-acrylamide, glycerol, sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), sodium hydroxide and glycine were sourced from Merck in Darmstadt, Germany, while bromophenol blue, and 2-mercaptoethanol were acquired from Sigma Aldrich Chemical Co. in St. Louis, MO, USA. Ethanol, potassium hydroxide, and hydrogen peroxide were obtained from Lab-Scan in Bangkok, Thailand. All chemicals used were of analytical grade. The experimental approach systematically evaluated four treatment conditions: control and three NaOH concentrations (0.5 M, 1 M, and 2 M) applied for 30 min at 25 °C. All treatments maintained a consistent bone-to-solution ratio (1:10 w/v) and stirring conditions (500 rpm). Analytical evaluation followed a sequential approach: yield assessment, physical properties, chemical composition, structural characteristics, and bioavailability determination.

Extraction of bio‑calcium from tilapia fish bones

The process of extracting bio‑calcium from tilapia fish bones was adapted from the method described by Sriket, Niwet, Kuimalee, et al. (2023). The axial skeleton of Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus), cultured for eight months in a biofloc system at a commercial organic farm affiliated with the Faculty of Fisheries Technology and Aquatic Resources, Maejo University, Chiang Mai, Thailand, was collected after fillet processing. The bones were transported within 2 h to the Meat Science and Fishery Laboratory, Program in Food Science and Technology, Faculty of Engineering and Agro-Industry, Maejo University. Fresh tilapia fish bones were boiled at 95 °C for 10 min. The fish bones were then washed using a high-pressure water jet (Warrior, Polo, China) to remove any residual flesh. The cleaned bones were then soaked in sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solutions at different concentrations: 0 M (control), 0.5 M, 1 M, and 2 M (w/v) at a bone to solution ratio of 1:10 (w/v). The mixture was continuously stirred using a stirrer (IKA-Labortechnik, Germany) at 500 rpm and 25 °C for 30 min. The treated bones were then washed with clean water until the sample reached a neutral pH (7.0–7.5) and dried in a hot air oven at 50 °C for 1 h (moisture content 2 ± 1 %). The dried bones were then ground using a ball mill at 300 rpm for 2 min. The ground bones were then soaked in 95 % ethanol at a bone to solvent ratio of 1:2 (w/v) and continuously stirred using a stirrer at 500 rpm and 25 °C for 1 h. The mixture was then filtered through cheesecloth to separate the liquid ethanol from the treated fish bones. The treated bones were air-dried at room temperature for approximately 5 h to ensure complete ethanol removal before further processing. The ethanol-treated bones were then soaked in 1 % (v/v) hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) solution at a bone to solution ratio of 1:2 (w/v), washed with distilled water, and dried in a hot air oven at 50 °C for 2 h. The dried bones were then ground using a ball mill and sieved through a 100-mesh sieve to obtain bio‑calcium powder with a particle size less than 150 μm. The bio‑calcium powder was then packed in aluminum foil bags (100 g per bag) and stored in a freezer at −18 °C before further analysis.

Physical properties

Physical appearance

The physical appearance of the bio‑calcium from tilapia fish bones prepared using different concentrations of sodium hydroxide solution was captured using a digital camera (X-A3, Fujifilm, Tokyo).

Color values

The color values of the bio‑calcium from tilapia fish bones prepared using different concentrations of sodium hydroxide solution were analyzed using a colorimeter (Color Flex EZ, Hunter Lab, U.S.A) in the CIE system. The L* value (lightness), a* value (redness or greenness), and b* value (yellowness or blueness) was analyzed, and the ∆E* value (color difference) was calculated using eq. (2):(2)ΔE∗=ΔL∗2+Δa∗2+Δb∗2where ∆L*, ∆a*, and ∆b* are the differences between the sample's color parameters and the standard's color parameters for whiteness, with L* = 93.59, a* = −0.98, and b* = 0.35.

Water activity

The water activity (aw) of the bio‑calcium from tilapia fish bones prepared using different concentrations of sodium hydroxide solution was analyzed using a water activity meter (Series 3 TE, AquaLAB, U.S.A). A 1 g sample was placed in the sample cell of the water activity meter before analyzing the aw value at 25 °C.

Chemical properties

Chemical composition

The chemical composition (moisture, ash, protein, and fat contents) of the bio‑calcium from tilapia fish bones prepared using different concentrations of sodium hydroxide solution was analyzed according to AOAC methods 927.05, 942.05, 920.38, and 984.13, respectively (AOAC, 2000).

Hydroxyproline content

The hydroxyproline content of the bio‑calcium from tilapia fish bones prepared using different concentrations of sodium hydroxide solution was determined. A 2 g sample was placed in a screw-capped test tube, and 15 mL of 6 mM sulfuric acid (H2SO4) was added. The mixture was mixed until the foam disappeared, vented briefly, then sealed and hydrolyzed at 121 °C for 16 h. After cooling, the hydrolysate was neutralized and subjected to colorimetric detection using chloramine-T and p-dimethylaminobenzaldehyde reagents according to standard protocols. Hydroxyproline standards (10–60 ppm) prepared in the same acid matrix as samples were used for calibration. The hydroxyproline content was reported in units of mg hydroxyproline/g sample.

Lipid oxidation

Lipid oxidation was analyzed using the thiobarbituric acid-reactive substances (TBARS) method according to the method of Buege and Aust (1978). A 1 g sample of bio‑calcium was mixed with 5 mL of TBA solution containing 0.375 % (w/v) thiobarbituric acid, 15 % (w/v) trichloroacetic acid, and 0.25 M hydrochloric acid. The mixture was mixed using a vortex mixer (Vortex-Genie 2; Scientific Industries, Inc., Bohemia, NY, USA) for 2 min and then heated at 95 °C for 10 min. After cooling, the mixture was centrifuged using centrifuge (Eppendorf, Centrifuge 5910, Germany) at 5000 rpm at 25 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was collected, and the absorbance was measured at 532 nm using a spectrophotometer (VIS-732G, Rayleigh, China). Distilled water was used as the blank solution, and 1,1,3,3-tetramethoxypropane (MDA) at concentrations of 0–10 μg/mL was used as the standard solution. The lipid oxidation was reported in units of mg MDA/kg sample.

Protein patterns (SDS-PAGE analysis)

The protein patterns of the bio‑calcium from tilapia fish bones prepared using different concentrations of sodium hydroxide solution were analyzed under non-reducing and reducing conditions using SDS-PAGE according to the method of Laemmli (1970) with some modifications. A 1 g sample of bio‑calcium powder was mixed with 10 mL of 10 % (w/v) sodium lauryl sulfate (SDS) solution and heated at 95 °C for 1 h before centrifugation to obtain the supernatant. The supernatant was then mixed with sample buffer at a ratio of 1:1. The sample buffer contained 0.6 M Tris-HCl buffer solution (pH 6.8), 0.025 % (w/v) bromophenol blue, 10 % (w/v) beta-mercaptoethanol (BME), and 10 % (v/v) glycerol. The mixture was then heated at 95 °C for 5 min and cooled. A 4 % polyacrylamide stacking gel and a 15 % polyacrylamide separating gel were prepared and run at 220 V and 30 mA for 45 min using a Mini-PROTEAN Tetra Cell (BioRad Laboratories, Inc., Richmond, CA., U.S.A). The gel was then stained with 0.1 % (v/v) Coomassie blue R-250 solution containing 5 % (v/v) acetic acid overnight and then destained with 30 % (v/v) methanol solution containing 10 % (v/v) acetic acid until the gel was clear. The molecular weights of the proteins in the bio‑calcium were compared with a protein standard with molecular weights of 37–250 kDa. The distance traveled by the standard protein bands from the starting point and the distance traveled by the tracking dye from the starting point were measured, and the relative mobility (Rf) was calculated using eq. (3):(3)Rf=Distance traveledbythe protein from the starting pointmmDistance traveledbythe trackingdyefrom the starting pointmm×100

A standard curve was plotted showing the relationship between the Rf values and the log of the molecular weights of the standard proteins. The Rf values obtained were compared with the standard curve to determine the molecular weights of the proteins in the bio‑calcium from tilapia fish bones prepared using different concentrations of sodium hydroxide solution.

Structural characteristics

Attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopic analysis

The functional groups of the bio‑calcium from tilapia fish bones prepared using different concentrations of sodium hydroxide solution were analyzed using ATR FTIR spectroscopy. Bio‑calcium samples were prepared using the KBr pellet method and pressed into transparent pellets. An FTIR spectrometer (EQUINOX 55, Bruker, Ettlingen, Germany) equipped with a horizontal ATR Trough plate crystal cell (45° Zinc Selenide (ZnSe); 80 mm long, 10 mm wide and 4 mm thick) (PIKE Technology Inc., Madison, WI, USA) at 25 °C was used in the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1. Background correction was performed before each measurement. Peak identification and assignment were performed by comparing with reference spectra, with particular attention to characteristic bands for phosphate, carbonate, and hydroxyl functional groups.

Particle size distribution

The particle size distribution of the bio‑calcium was analyzed using a laser diffraction particle size analyzer. A HELOS (H4542) laser diffraction sensor was used in conjunction with a RODOS/L, R5 dry dispenser (Sympatec, Clausthal-Zellerfeld, Germany), and the data were processed using PAQXOS 6.0.2 software. The particle size distribution was described in terms of D10, D50 (mean diameter), and D90.

Microstructure and elemental distribution

The microstructure and elemental distribution of the bio‑calcium from tilapia fish bones prepared using different concentrations of sodium hydroxide solution were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) according to the method of Sriket, Niwet, Kuimalee, et al. (2023) with some modifications. The samples were observed using a scanning electron microscope (JSM-5410LV, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) after gold coating. The surface of the samples was detected in secondary electron mode at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV and a magnification of 300 × .

Bioavailability assessment

In vitro calcium bioavailability of bio‑calcium from tilapia fish bones

The in vitro calcium bioavailability of the bio‑calcium from tilapia fish bones prepared using different concentrations of sodium hydroxide solution was determined according to the method described by Sriket, Niwet, Kuimalee, et al. (2023). A sample of 150 mg of bio‑calcium was mixed with 100 mL of 5 mM HCl-KCl buffer solution (pH 1.5). Then, 100 mL of 5 mM pepsin solution (32 units/mL) in 1 M HCl-KCl buffer solution (pH 1.5) was added. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 60 min (stomach conditions) with continuous shaking (Innova 4340 Illuminated refrigerated incubator shaker, USA). The pH was then adjusted to 6.8 using 1 M NaHCO3. Next, 1 mL of a mixture of bile and pancreatic enzymes (containing pancreatin (10 mg/mL), trypsin (14,600 μg/mL), and bile extract (13.5 mg/mL) in 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.2)) was added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 3 h (small intestine conditions) with continuous shaking. The mixture was then heated at 95 °C for 10 min, and the final volume was adjusted to 150 mL before centrifugation at 7000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and analyzed for calcium and phosphorus content using an inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES).

Statistical analysis

The experiments were conducted with at least three replicates per sample. The experimental design was a completely randomized design (CRD). The data were analyzed for mean values, standard deviations, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) using Duncan's multiple range test (DMRT) at a 95 % confidence level (p < 0.05) using SPSS software version 28.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA).

Results and discussion

Production yield

Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution concentration significantly affected bio‑calcium yield from tilapia fish bones. As shown in Table 1, increasing NaOH from 0.5 M to 2 M lowered yield from 76.08 % to 73.75 %. The control sample showed the highest yield (85.22 %). All NaOH treatments significantly reduced these values (p < 0.05). According to Sriket, Niwet, Pui, et al. (2023), higher alkaline concentrations remove more organic components from fish bones, such as proteins and lipids. Increased NaOH concentration dissolves and removes organic matter from fish bones more efficiently. More inorganic mineral phase, mostly calcium phosphate in the form of hydroxyapatite and reducing yield was observed after this process (Toppe et al., 2007). This study found that selectively removing organic components purifies bio‑calcium, as seen by the increased ash content and whiteness (L* value). A higher NaOH content may provide a purer bio‑calcium product with better whiteness, but it may also lower yield. Physical properties

Physical appearance

The physical appearance of bio‑calcium samples is shown in Fig. 1. The samples were prepared with different NaOH concentrations (0.5 M, 1 M, and 2 M) for 30 min. The appearance was significantly affected by NaOH concentration. A light-yellow color was observed in the control sample. The whiteness increased with increasing NaOH concentration. The whitest color was achieved with 2 M NaOH treatment. The color changes were attributed to organic component removal. The proteins and lipids were effectively removed by higher NaOH concentrations (Idowu et al., 2020). However, mineral degradation could be caused by excessive NaOH concentration. Calcium and phosphorus could be lost from the inorganic structure (Owuamanam & Cree, 2020). The optimal conditions were determined at 2 M NaOH for 30 min. White powder with good purity was produced under these conditions.Fig. 1Appearance of tilapia bones after NaOH treatment at different concentrations for 30 min: (A) treated bones (B) bio-calcium powder. Color values

The color values of the bio‑calcium from tilapia fish bones prepared using different concentrations of sodium hydroxide solution (0.5 M, 1 M, and 2 M) for 30 min, compared with the control (Table 1), showed that the concentration of sodium hydroxide had a significant effect on the color values of the bio‑calcium (p < 0.05). The bio‑calcium L* value increased with higher sodium hydroxide concentration. The a* and b* values decreased simultaneously. The color of bio‑calcium became whiter at higher sodium hydroxide concentrations. These changes indicated improved whiteness. The white color development was attributed to organic component removal. Proteins and lipids were effectively removed by higher alkaline concentrations. Similar results were reported by Idowu et al. (2020). Their study showed enhanced organic matter removal at higher alkaline concentrations. This removal produced whiter and purer bio‑calcium.

Extended soaking times in sodium hydroxide solution enhance organic substance removal efficiency, resulting in whiter bio‑calcium products. Therefore, both the concentration of the alkaline solution and the soaking time should be considered to obtain bio‑calcium with high quality in terms of color and chemical composition. However, analyzing only the color values may not be sufficient to conclude whether the bio‑calcium is good quality, as color values are only a preliminary physical indicator. It is still necessary to analyze other properties, such as chemical composition, crystal structure, purity, and safety, to comprehensively confirm the quality of the bio‑calcium (Venkatesan et al., 2015), which will be useful for applications in the food and medical industries.

Water activity

The water activity of bio‑calcium was significantly affected by NaOH concentration. Water activity decreased from 0.31 to 0.29 as NaOH concentration increased from 0.5 M to 2 M (Table 1). The control sample showed the highest water activity (0.38). Similar trends have been reported in other bio-mineral studies, where alkaline treatment reduced water activity through organic matter removal (Venkatesan et al., 2015; Ahmad Fara et al., 2015). The elimination of water-binding organic components such proteins, which maintain moisture in the fish bone matrix, decreases bio‑calcium water activity with increasing NaOH concentration (Darko et al., 2024). As these organic compounds are eliminated after alkaline treatment, bio‑calcium has less water binding and retention, decreasing water activity. Reduced water activity inhibits microbial growth and deterioration, preserving bio‑calcium product stability and shelf life (Patil et al., 2024).

Chemical properties

Chemical composition

The chemical composition of the bio‑calcium from tilapia fish bones was analyzed (Table 1). The samples were prepared using different concentrations of sodium hydroxide solution (0.5 M, 1 M, and 2 M) and compared with the control. The moisture, protein, and fat contents in the bio‑calcium were decreased with increased sodium hydroxide concentration. These results are consistent with the findings of Hart et al. (2022). They found that soaking fish bones in stronger alkaline solutions enhanced organic substance removal. The removal of proteins and lipids from the bones resulted in bio‑calcium with higher ash content. The ash primarily consisted of minerals, especially calcium and phosphorus (Toppe et al., 2007). However, Owuamanam and Cree (2020) noted that an excessively high concentration of alkaline solution may cause mineral loss during production. Therefore, an appropriate concentration of alkaline solution should be considered.

The chemical composition was significantly altered by the NaOH treatment. The protein content was reduced from 30.23 % to 27.54 %. The fat content decreased from 2.24 % to 0.17 %. The ash content was increased from 65.76 % to 70.41 %. These changes indicated the effective removal of organic components. The presence of collagen in bio‑calcium higher ash content suggested an increased concentration of minerals. These results were supported by the calcium bioavailability data.

Hydroxyproline content

The hydroxyproline content of the bio‑calcium from tilapia fish bones is depicted in Fig. 2A. The samples were prepared using various concentrations of sodium hydroxide solution and compared with the control. The hydroxyproline content was increased with higher concentration of NaOH solution. This increase indicated a higher collagen content in the bio‑calcium. This finding is consistent with Sriket, Niwet, Pui, et al. (2023). They reported that bio‑calcium from hybrid catfish bones contained hydroxyproline at 27–30 mg/g. The amino acid analysis also showed high amounts of proline, glycine, aspartic acid, alanine, and glutamic acid in the bio‑calcium. NaOH treatment increased hydroxyproline content from 27.8 to 40.5 mg/g, confirming effective collagen preservation during extraction. Lipid oxidation

The lipid oxidation of bio‑calcium samples was measured by TBARS values (Fig. 2B). A significant decrease in TBARS values was observed with increasing NaOH concentration (p < 0.05). The control sample showed the highest TBARS value at 130 μg MDA/kg. The values were decreased to 80, 55, and 20 μg MDA/kg in 0.5 M, 1 M, and 2 M NaOH treatments, respectively. The reduction in lipid oxidation was correlated with the decreased fat content shown in the chemical composition analysis (Table 1). The fat content was reduced from 2.24 % to 0.17 % with increasing NaOH concentration.

The decreased TBARS values were attributed to two main factors. The lipids were effectively removed by the alkaline solution. This removal was supported by Idowu et al. (2020). They reported effective lipid removal from salmon bones using alkaline treatment. The pro-oxidants were also eliminated during alkaline treatment. These pro-oxidants included heme proteins and metal ions (Wu et al., 2024). The removal of both lipids and pro-oxidants improved the stability of bio‑calcium. The low TBARS values indicated minimal lipid oxidation. This reduction suggested improved oxidative stability. The TBARS values obtained in this study were lower than those reported for untreated fish bone powder in other studies. Similar trends were observed in alkaline-processed marine by-products by other researchers. The progressive decrease in TBARS values with increasing NaOH concentration confirms the effectiveness of this treatment method across different fish species. Enhancing stability is beneficial for long-term storage.

Protein pattern

The protein patterns of bio‑calcium samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE under non-reducing and reducing conditions (Fig. 3). The samples were prepared from control and NaOH-treated tilapia bones. Three prominent protein bands were observed in both conditions. Under reducing conditions, the molecular weights were determined at 250 kDa (β chain), 133 kDa (α1 chain), and 116 kDa (α2 chain). No significant differences were detected among the NaOH concentrations. These protein patterns were identified as type I collagen components. This identification was based on the molecular weights reported by Kittiphattanabawon et al. (2005). Their study of bigeye snapper bone collagen showed α1- and α2-chains at 120 and 110 kDa. Similar patterns were reported for Spanish mackerel bones by Li et al. (2013). The protein bands were preserved across all NaOH concentrations. This preservation of protein bands supported the hydroxyproline content results. Structural characteristics

Functional group analysis

The ATR-FTIR spectra of bio‑calcium samples were examined (Fig. 4). The samples were produced by treating tilapia bones with various concentrations of NaOH (0.5 M, 1 M, and 2 M) for 30 min each. All samples showed hydroxyapatite's distinctive absorption peaks. The phosphate (PO43−) groups were detected at 560–600 cm−1 and 1020–1120 cm−1. The carbonate (CO32−) groups were identified at 870 cm−1 and 1400–1550 cm−1. The 0.5 M NaOH treated sample (B) displayed increased surface porosity. The 1 M NaOH treatment (C) created larger pores on the surface. The 2 M NaOH treatment (D) resulted in the most extensive surface erosion. The pore size increased progressively from small isolated pores in control samples to interconnected porous networks in 2 M NaOH treatment. This structural transformation directly correlates with the improved calcium bioavailability shown in Table 2. The alkaline treatment progressively dissolves protein matrix and organic materials, creating pathways for digestive fluid penetration. The increased surface roughness and porosity provide multiple contact points for enzyme interaction during digestion. Similar microstructural changes have been reported in other calcium-rich materials where alkaline pretreatment enhanced dissolution rates. The porous structure formation explains the mechanism behind improved bioavailability, as the increased surface area facilitates faster calcium ion release in simulated gastrointestinal conditions. Elemental distribution

The elemental distribution of bio‑calcium samples was examined using EDS (Fig. 7). Ca, P, and O were identified as the main elements in all samples. The Ca/P molar ratios were 1.67, 1.65, 1.66, and 1.64 for control, 0.5 M, 1 M, and 2 M NaOH treatments, respectively. These ratios were close to the theoretical value of hydroxyapatite (1.67) (Toppe et al., 2007). The elemental mapping revealed significant variations among the treatments. The concentration of NaOH influenced the carbon distribution. Samples treated with 1 M and 2 M NaOH exhibited lower carbon content relative to the control and the 0.5 M NaOH treatment. The observed reduction was linked to improved removal of organic matter (Ahmad Fara et al., 2015). The sodium signal intensity increased with NaOH concentration, suggesting sodium incorporation during alkaline treatment. Magnesium was detected at low levels (0.5–0.8 %) in all samples. The diminished carbon content corresponded with the lowered protein and lipid levels. The uniform distribution of Ca and P aligned with the ATR-FTIR analysis of the hydroxyapatite structure. Calcium bioavailability

The calcium bioavailability (%) of the bio‑calcium from tilapia fish bones was determined in an in vitro simulated gastrointestinal tract system (Table 2). The samples were prepared using different concentrations of sodium hydroxide solution (0.5 M, 1 M, and 2 M) for 30 min. Control and calcium carbonate were used for comparison. The highest calcium bioavailability at 8.57 ± 0.05 % was obtained from the 2 M NaOH treatment. This value was significantly higher than the control (7.26 ± 0.05 %) and calcium carbonate (0.72 ± 0.01 %). The development of bio‑calcium from fish bones requires adherence to established quality and safety protocols to ensure the final products meet standards for human consumption and nutritional supplementation (Bondoc, 2014). The increased bioavailability was supported by Wawrzyniak and Suliburska (2021). The residual proteins and lipids were removed by an alkaline solution, increasing the specific surface area and improving calcium dissolution in the simulated gastrointestinal tract system. However, mineral loss could be caused by excessive NaOH concentration (Owuamanam & Cree, 2020). Three main factors were attributed to this improvement. The organic matrix was removed by the alkaline solution, exposing the mineral crystals. These changes were confirmed by chemical composition analysis. The protein content was reduced. The lipid content was decreased. The ash content was increased. The organic matrix removal was successful. Higher calcium bioavailability was observed in bio‑calcium compared to calcium carbonate. This finding was supported by Abdellah Ali et al. (2021). The bio‑calcium structure was similar to human bones. Better absorption was achieved due to this similarity. The enhanced calcium bioavailability observed with increasing NaOH concentration can be attributed to three main mechanisms. The removal of organic matrix components exposes calcium phosphate crystals directly to digestive enzymes, enhancing dissolution efficiency. The increased surface porosity observed in SEM analysis (Fig. 6) provides more contact areas for enzymatic interaction. Furthermore, the preservation of collagen structure supports calcium absorption through beneficial amino acid-mineral interactions that facilitate intestinal uptake.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that controlled alkaline treatment achieves selective organic matrix removal while preserving essential mineral structures in tilapia bone waste. The optimal 2 M NaOH concentration balances protein elimination (9 % reduction to 27.54 %) and lipid removal (92 % reduction to 0.17 %) with hydroxyapatite preservation, as confirmed by consistent Ca/P ratios (1.64) and maintained ATR-FTIR phosphate peaks. The mechanism involves progressive matrix dissolution creating enhanced surface porosity, directly correlating with improved dissolution in simulated gastrointestinal conditions. The resulting 18 % bioavailability improvement (8.57 % vs 7.26 % control) and 12-fold superiority over calcium carbonate (0.72 %) demonstrates functional advantages of bio-derived calcium. Preserved collagen structure (40.5 mg/g hydroxyproline) and reduced oxidation (130 to 20 μg MDA/kg) provide additional nutritional and stability benefits. This research establishes a scalable approach for converting aquaculture waste into high-value calcium supplements while addressing both sustainability and nutritional supplementation challenges.
