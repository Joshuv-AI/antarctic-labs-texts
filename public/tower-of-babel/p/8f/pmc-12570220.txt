Association between uric acid to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio and chronic kidney disease in middle-aged and older adults: results from two nationally representative population-based study
Authors: Zifang Fan, Chengyi Feng, Xin Li, Zihan Zhang, Hao Gong, Jia Di
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUric acid to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (UHR) is considered a novel marker of inflammation. The aim of this study was to investigate the association between the UHR and chronic kidney disease (CKD) in middle-aged and older adults. This study used data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). The association between UHR and CKD prevalence was analyzed using weighted multivariable logistic regression models. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used to analyze nonlinear relationships. Weighted subgroup analyses were performed to validate the robustness of the findings. The diagnostic performance of UHR for CKD was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. This study included 10,968 participants from NHANES and 9,012 participants from CHARLS. The mean age of NHANES participants was 59.60 years (standard deviation [SD] = 10.97), with 5,575 men (50.83%). CHARLS participants had a mean age of 59.58 years (SD = 9.40), and 4,203 (46.64%) were men. Weighted logistic regression analysis revealed a positive association between UHR and CKD prevalence (CHARLS: OR = 1.83, 95% CI = 1.44–2.32, p < 0.001; NHANES: OR = 1.81, 95% CI = 1.58–2.07, p < 0.001). The RCS regression models demonstrated a significant nonlinear relationship between UHR and CKD. ROC analysis indicated that UHR demonstrated moderate CKD discrimination (CHARLS: AUC = 0.707; NHANES: AUC = 0.645). UHR is positively associated with an increased prevalence of CKD in middle-aged and older adults, and this association exhibits cross-ethnic consistency.Keywords: UHR, CKD, NHANES, CHARLS

Background

The prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) has affected over 10% of the global population and emerging as one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide [1]. The etiology of CKD is diverse, with diabetes mellitus (DM), cardiovascular disease, hypertension, and obesity identified as significant risk factors [2]. Concurrently, chronic inflammation, as an independent pathological state, accelerates the progression of glomerulosclerosis and tubulointerstitial fibrosis through mechanisms such as pro-inflammatory cytokine storms, oxidative stress cascades, and immune microenvironment remodeling [3]. Clinical studies have confirmed that elevated levels of systemic inflammatory biomarkers, such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), are independently associated with all-cause mortality in CKD patients [4]. However, testing for these inflammatory biomarkers faces challenges of high cost and poor accessibility in clinical settings. Therefore, exploring novel inflammatory-metabolic composite biomarkers with high sensitivity and low cost holds significant clinical value for achieving early screening and risk stratification of CKD [5].

Uric acid to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (UHR), a novel biomarker, has gained increasing recognition. Previous studies have shown that elevated UHR is closely associated with diverse pathological processes, including thyroiditis [6], diabetic nephropathy [7], nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [8], hypertension [9], all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [10], and osteoporosis [11], offering new insights into chronic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation [12]. Biologically, UHR quantifies the dynamic equilibrium between the pro-inflammatory effects of serum uric acid and the anti-inflammatory capacity of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) [13,14]. Elevated UHR levels signify this imbalance, which may exacerbate renal injury by amplifying oxidative stress and low-grade inflammatory responses [15].

In Chinese individuals with dysglycemia or primary aldosteronism, poor control of UHR predicts an increased risk of future rapid decline in renal function and the development of CKD [16]. However, to our knowledge, few studies have investigated the precise association of UHR with CKD in middle-aged and older populations or its cross-ethnic generalizability. Therefore, our study aims to investigate the cross-ethnic consistency of the association between UHR and CKD in middle-aged and older adults, which may be helpful in identifying a novel biomarker for early prevention and control of CKD.

Methods

Data sources

This study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). CHARLS comprises over ten thousand households, randomly selected from 150 counties and districts nationwide, as well as 450 villages and communities, spanning families and individuals aged 45 and above in China. It encompasses a wide range of nationally representative variables, including demographics, family, health status, cognition, healthcare, work, retirement, finances, housing, and serves as a vital resource for understanding the socioeconomic landscape and health dynamics among older adults in China [17].

NHANES, conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) under the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), is designed to continuously assess the health and nutritional status of the non-institutionalized population in the United States. NHANES collects comprehensive data on various health-related subjects, encompassing demographics, socioeconomic factors, dietary patterns, and health-related information, which are obtained through household interviews. Subsequently, blood samples are acquired at a mobile examination center (MEC).

Study population

This study initially included 17,705 Chinese participants with blood samples from the CHARLS spanning from 2011 to 2012 and 62,160 U.S. participants of NHANES from 1999 to 2010. Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) age < 45 years; (2) missing data on HDL-C, serum uric acid (SUA), or serum creatinine (Scr) precluding calculation of the UHR and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR); (3) missing records of key covariates, including sex, education level, age, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, cardiovascular disease (CVD), DM, and hypertension. Finally, 10,968 NHANES participants and 9,012 CHARLS participants were eligible for this cross-sectional analysis. All participants provided written informed consent, and data from NHANES received approval from the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board. Data from the CHARLS received authorization from the Peking University Institutional Review Board (IRB00001052-11015).

Data assessment and definitions

Definitions of UHR and other inflammation biomarkers

In our study, UHR was considered the primary exposure variable. UHR was calculated by dividing SUA (mg/dL) by HDL-C (mg/dL). To further explore the connection between UHR and CKD, we also examined the link between other inflammatory biomarkers and renal function. Counts for lymphocytes, neutrophils, platelets, white blood cells, and monocytes were expressed in units of ×103 cells/μL. These additional inflammation biomarkers included systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), platelet/high-density lipoprotein ratio (PHR) and platelet to white blood cell ratio (PWR). SII = platelet counts × neutrophil counts/lymphocyte counts. SIRI = neutrophil counts × monocyte counts/lymphocyte counts. PHR = platelet counts/HDL-C. PWR = platelet counts/white blood cell counts.

Definitions of CKD

CKD was diagnosed by an eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 [18]. eGFR was calculated using the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaborative (2021 CKD-EPI) equation [19]. In women, eGFR = 142 × (Scr/0.7)−0.241 × 0.9938Age × 1.012 if Scr ≤ 0.7 mg/dL and 142 × (Scr/0.7)−1.200 × 0.9938Age × 1.012 if Scr is > 0.7 mg/dL. In men, eGFR =142 × (Scr/0.9)−0.302 × 0.9938Age if Scr is ≤ 0.9 mg/dL and 142 × (Scr/0.9)−1.200 × 0.9938Age if Scr is > 0.9 mg/dL.

Covariates

The covariates included in our study were demographic elements (sex, age, race, education level, marital status and BMI), health habits (smoking and drinking status), health situations (DM, hypertension and CVD), medical intervention (the use of lipid-lowering drugs) and laboratory measurements (total cholesterol, triglycerides and CRP).

Age was treated as a continuous variable in regression models and stratified into ≤ 60 and > 60 years for subgroup analyses. Education level was categorized as less than high school, high school or equivalent, and above high school; marital status was classified into married and others (unmarried, cohabiting, divorced, separated, or widowed); BMI was calculated as weight (kg)/height2 (m2). Smoking and alcohol consumption were defined as yes (current or former) or never. Hypertension was defined as an average systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg or an average diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg, self-reported history, or use of antihypertensive medication. DM was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%, self-reported diagnosis, or use of antidiabetic medication. CVD was defined as self-report or medication history. Lipid-lowering therapy was defined as taking prescriptions for hypercholesterolemia or hyperlipidemia.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentage) and compared using Rao-Scott adjusted chi-square test. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviations (SD) and compared using weighted Student’s t-test when normally distributed and shown as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum test when non-normally distributed. To ensure national representativeness of the analytical results for Chinese and U.S. populations, all analyses were performed using the proper sample weights.

To further explore the association between UHR and CKD, participants were stratified into quartiles based on UHR levels and categorized into CKD or non-CKD groups according to the presence or absence of CKD. Weighted multiple logistic regression models were used to measure odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). When UHR was treated as a continuous variable, it was standardized using z-score transformation (mean = 0, SD = 1). Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, race, education level, marital status, and BMI; Model 3 further incorporated total cholesterol, triglycerides, CVD, DM, hypertension, CRP, lipid-lowering drugs, smoking, and drinking status. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression evaluated potential nonlinear association of UHR and CKD. A P value for nonlinearity < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for the nonlinear association. Furthermore, subgroup analyses were conducted based on sex, age, education level, marital status, BMI, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, DM, CVD and lipid-lowering therapy.

Additionally, the predictive ability of UHR for CKD was assessed using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and the area under the curve (AUC). Sensitivity analyses were performed using unweighted model analyses to assess whether the results were influenced by the weighted data processing. All analyses were conducted in R software (version 4.2.2), and statistical significance was defined as P value < 0.05.

Association between UHR and CKD

As shown in Table 3, the weighted logistic regression analysis revealed a significant positive association between UHR and CKD. In the unadjusted model, elevated UHR levels were linked to an increased risk of CKD (CHARLS: OR = 1.52, 95% CI = 1.34–1.73; NHANES: OR = 1.52, 95% CI = 1.38–1.67). After adjusting for potential confounders in Model 3, this association remained significant (CHARLS: OR = 1.83, 95% CI = 1.44–2.32; NHANES: OR = 1.81, 95% CI = 1.58–2.07). Compared with Quartile 1, the risk of CKD was significantly higher in Quartile 4 (NHANES: OR = 5.47, 95% CI = 3.74–8.01; CHARLS: OR = 8.39, 95% CI = 4.61–15.28). The RCS regression model revealed significant nonlinear associations between UHR and CKD risk in both unadjusted and adjusted CHARLS models and adjusted NHANES model (Adjusted covariates in Model 3: NHANES: p < 0.001, P for nonlinearity < 0.001; CHARLS: p < 0.001, P for nonlinearity < 0.001), as shown in Figure 2.

Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses

As shown in Figure 3, the association between UHR and CKD risk was evaluated across subgroups stratified by different factors (sex, age, education level, marital status, BMI, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, DM, CVD and lipid-lowering therapy), respectively. Significant associations persisted across all stratified subgroups except for the other marital statuses participants in CHARLS. Interaction analyses revealed significant effect modification by sex, age, and BMI in NHANES, whereas significant effect modification was observed for alcohol consumption and CVD in CHARLS (P for interaction < 0.05). Unweighted model analysis was performed to assess whether the results were influenced by the weighted data processing, and it showed that the association between UHR and CKD remained significant (CHARLS: OR = 1.85, 95% CI = 1.60–2.13; NHANES: OR = 1.91, 95% CI = 1.77–2.07) (Table S2 and Table S3).

Subgroup analyses of the relationship between UHR and CKD were performed in NHANES (A) and CHARLS (B), adjusted for triglycerides, total cholesterol, and CRP.

Predictive performance

ROC analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive performance of UHR for CKD. In CHARLS, the AUC was 0.707 (95% CI = 0.672–0.742), corresponding to a sensitivity of 72.2% and specificity of 59.4%. In NHANES, the AUC was 0.645 (95% CI = 0.630–0.660), achieving a sensitivity of 70.3% and specificity of 50.2% at the optimal threshold (Figure 4). These results demonstrate that UHR has a moderate predictive ability in distinguishing between CKD patients and non-CKD individuals, with superior predictive accuracy in CHARLS. Furthermore, we compared the associations of several other markers with the incidence of CKD (Figure S7 and Figure S8).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the relationship between UHR and CKD in individuals over 45 years of age. Our findings indicated that elevated UHR levels were associated with a higher risk of developing CKD, and this correlation was consistent across different ethnic groups. Our results suggest that UHR could function as a straightforward, accessible, and cost-effective biomarker for the early detection and intervention of individuals at high risk for CKD, particularly in areas with constrained healthcare resources, potentially enhancing clinical utility.

In recent years, the association between UHR and CKD has been discussed across various population groups. For example, a study by Li et al. demonstrated that elevated UHR was significantly associated with an increased risk of renal function decline among hypertensive individuals (OR = 1.32, p = 0.02) [20]. Additionally, Liu et al. identified that high cumulative UHR (cumUHR) exposure was an independent risk factor for the incidence and progression of CKD [21]. To further validate the generalizability of the relationship between UHR and CKD, we analyzed an independent external validation dataset comprising 40,138 participants aged 45 years or older. The data were sourced from the Physical Examination Center of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, spanning from 2021 to 2024. Participants were stratified into quartiles based on UHR, revealing significant differences in age, sex, BMI, hypertension, DM, CVD, total cholesterol, triglycerides, Scr, HDL-C, uric acid, and eGFR across the quartile groups (p < 0.05) (Table S4). Baseline characteristics of participants stratified according to the presence or absence of CKD were presented in Table S5. Furthermore, UHR was positively associated with the incidence of CKD (OR = 4.65, 95% CI = 3.74–5.78) (Table S6). RCS analysis revealed a significant nonlinear association between UHR and CKD risk (p < 0.001) (Figure S9). Moreover, the ROC curve demonstrated moderate predictive performance of UHR for CKD (AUC = 0.650) (Figure S10). These results further confirm the robustness of our findings.

The association between UHR and CKD may be mediated through multiple pathophysiological mechanisms. Serum uric acid, the terminal product of purine metabolism, has been consistently identified as an independent risk factor for CKD development and progression, primarily through impaired renal excretion mechanisms [22–24]. The pathophysiological continuum encompasses several interrelated pathological processes, including endothelial dysfunction-induced microcirculatory disturbances, abnormal activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) promoting glomerular hypertension, and oxidative stress-mediated tubulointerstitial fibrosis and glomerulosclerosis [20,21,25]. Notably, cohort studies have demonstrated a non-linear J-shaped relationship between SUA levels and adverse renal outcomes, particularly in early-onset renal failure and all-cause mortality among established CKD populations [26]. Large-scale epidemiological data also support that elevated serum uric acid levels are significantly associated with both incident CKD and its progression [27]. Moreover, this association persists across metabolic disease states, as evidenced by Zhou et al.’s findings in diabetic cohorts, where hyperuricemia independently predicted CKD incidence with dose-response characteristics [24].

HDL-C not only exerts renoprotective effects through its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties [28], but also plays a pivotal role in maintaining glomerular lipid homeostasis via its reverse cholesterol transport (RCT) mechanism. Dysfunctional HDL-C particles markedly impair renal cholesterol efflux capacity, resulting in pathological lipid accumulation within glomerular mesangial cells and podocytes. These lipid deposits further activate inflammasome signaling pathways and oxidative stress responses, thereby accelerating the progression of glomerulosclerosis and tubulointerstitial fibrosis [29]. Clinical studies have demonstrated that low HDL-C is not only associated with elevated risks of systemic inflammatory diseases [30], but also shows significant correlation with renal impairment in diabetic kidney disease (DKD), as evidenced by a large-scale cohort study [31]. Furthermore, emerging evidence indicates that reduced HDL-C serves as an independent predictor of CKD progression [32], with its decline significantly elevating risks of both CKD incidence and disease advancement [33]. Notably, low HDL-C may increase the risk of hyperuricemia [34], thereby reinforcing the synergistic pathogenic interaction between lipid metabolism disorders and dysregulated uric acid metabolism. Specifically, the co-occurrence of HDL-C reduction and serum uric acid elevation confers a substantially amplified disease risk. In clinical surveillance for high-risk populations, particular attention should be directed toward individuals exhibiting this dual metabolic perturbation.

Our subgroup analyses demonstrated that overall trends were consistent across all subgroups in the two populations. Interaction tests revealed that sex, age, and BMI in NHANES, while alcohol consumption and CVD in CHARLS, were identified as being the interactive factors influencing correlation between UHR and CKD (p < 0.05). The association between UHR and CKD exhibited significant sex disparities, with a stronger correlation observed in the female individuals. Premenopausal women exhibit estrogen concentrations 2 to 3-fold higher than males by enhancing uric acid excretion efficiency and inhibiting urate crystal deposition. However, the abrupt decline in estrogen during postmenopause leads to attenuated uric acid clearance capacity, resulting in serum uric acid concentrations progressively approaching male physiological ranges alongside a marked increase in hyperuricemia incidence [35]. This metabolic transition is particularly prominent among elderly women, where elevated serum uric acid demonstrates a more pronounced association with CKD risk [30]. Therefore, the prevalence of CKD stages 3–5 in females exceeds that in males [36], suggesting that estrogen modulates renal injury progression, potentially through serum uric acid metabolic pathways. This mechanism likely underlies the sex-specific predictive efficacy of UHR. Moreover, our study also demonstrated the subgroup characterized by higher serum uric acid and non-alcohol consumption exhibited an increased risk of CKD. A cohort study of 9,116 middle-aged non-diabetic Japanese males revealed that elevated serum uric acid was positively associated with increased CKD risk, while moderate daily alcohol intake mitigated this risk [37], suggesting that alcohol consumption may confer renal protection through modulation of serum uric acid metabolic pathways.

A further analysis of risk stratification in the CKD subgroup was also conducted in our study. Previous studies have identified hyperuricemia as an independent risk factor for all-cause mortality and CVD mortality in patients with CKD stages 3–4 [38], and have demonstrated a significant positive correlation between hyperuricemia and CVD-related hospitalization risks [39]. Similarly, a stronger positive association between UHR and CKD was observed in our study. This may be because serum uric acid has a synergistic effect on the disease progression of CKD combined with CVD through pathophysiological mechanisms such as vascular endothelial dysfunction and systemic inflammatory activation.

The strengths of this study are reflected in three aspects: First, by integrating two nationally representative population-based cohorts CHARLS and NHANES, we systematically revealed a cross-ethnic consistency between UHR and CKD. Second, the study strictly adhered to complex sampling design protocols, employing weighted statistical analyses to correct for selection bias. Data reliability was ensured through standardized quality control procedures implemented in both CHARLS and NHANES. Third, external data validation confirmed the generalizability of this association. ROC curve analyses further substantiated UHR’s moderate predictive efficacy for CKD (CHARLS: AUC = 0.707; NHANES: AUC = 0.645), indicating its potential value as a clinical screening tool for CKD.

However, our study still has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design is unable to verify the causal relationship of UHR and CKD, so future investigations should validate this association through prospective cohort studies or Mendelian randomization analyses. Second, although creatinine-based eGFR was used for CKD diagnosis, the absence of urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) data may have underestimated CKD prevalence and compromised evaluation accuracy for proteinuria-related renal injury. Third, due to the limitations of public databases, this study did not exclude participants using medications affecting uric acid metabolism (e.g. thiazide diuretics, allopurinol), so this study’s conclusions may be constrained by the lack of data. But considering the fact that the number of patients taking the medication is very small, which has a relatively minor impact on the results, therefore, the situation of patients taking uric acid-lowering drugs has not yet been included at present. Despite these limitations, the transnational multi-center design and external validation in clinical populations provide robust evidence supporting UHR as a promising biomarker for CKD risk.

Conclusions

In this study, we found that a higher level of UHR was associated with an increased risk of incident CKD in individuals over the age of 45 years in both Chinese and U.S. populations. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the generalizability of these findings across different populations. UHR may serve as a novel biomarker for the early detection of CKD in middle-aged and elderly individuals. In regions with limited medical resources, monitoring UHR could aid in the early identification and intervention of high-risk individuals with CKD, demonstrating its potential clinical utility.
