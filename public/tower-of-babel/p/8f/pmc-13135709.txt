From Home to Hallways: How Adverse Childhood Experiences and School Context Shape Student Relationship Development
Authors: Dana M Sox, Katherine Ross, Nancy L Deutsch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSchools are critical settings for supporting student mental health; research is needed to identify key levers for addressing the impact of Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) on students' relationships.MethodsDrawing on survey data from rural high school students (N = 2902), we examine the associations between ACEs scores and multiple developmental relationships (parenting adults, teachers, peers, neighbors, program leaders). School‐level engagement and support, and teacher relationships were also examined as potential protective factors.ResultsHigher ACEs scores were associated with weaker developmental relationships across all groups. However, positive relationships with teachers and supportive school engagement and support slightly moderated the negative influence of ACEs on youths' relationships with parenting adults.Implications for School Health Policy, Practice, and EquityDistrict and school leaders should support educators in building strong relationships with students by protecting their capacity, providing trauma‐informed professional development, and ensuring access to mental health services for students and staff.ConclusionsBuilding strong relationships with teachers and fostering a supportive and engaging school environment can be key strategies for buffering the risks associated with adversity and ultimately supporting young people's development of strong, healthy relationships.Keywords: adolescents, adverse childhood experiences, developmental relationships, school environments, trauma‐informed schools

Introduction

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), approximately 64% of adults in the United States have encountered one or more Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) [1]. ACEs include exposure to instability, violence, neglect, and abuse [1]. These experiences can impact children socially, behaviorally, and academically [2, 3, 4]. ACEs can make it challenging for youth to experience positive relationships with adults and, therefore, the benefits that supportive adult relationships can afford [5, 6]. This may compound risk, given that supportive relationships can be particularly beneficial in promoting positive outcomes and mitigating the impact of adverse experiences for youth. For example, poor child–parent relationships can lead to both adverse experiences and negative outcomes for youth (e.g., increased drug use) [7]. Further, family protective factors (i.e., “having a close relationship with a competent, prosocial family member”, 1441) can moderate the relation between ACEs and social relationships (i.e., satisfaction with personal relationships) [8]. Therefore, relationships that support development (e.g., developmental relationships) by encouraging people to discover who they are, develop skills, expand social capital‐building opportunities, and engage with and contribute to the world around them could act as a protective factor against adversity experiences on youth outcomes [9].

School‐level factors could also play a potential protective role in mitigating the influence ACEs have on youth outcomes, including relational development. For example, a safe school environment [10] and school connectedness [11] have been shown to play a protective role in promoting child wellbeing (i.e., health, education, emotional well‐being, and social behavior) and reducing suicidal ideation, thereby, helping to mitigate the negative influence that ACEs can have on youth. This study, grounded in a risk and protective factor framework, examined the potential interplay between risk (i.e., ACEs) and potential buffering factors (i.e., school‐level factors, including developmental relationships with teachers) in relation to developmental relationships with parenting adults to inform targeted prevention and intervention efforts.

Theoretical Framework

This study is grounded in and integrates two theoretical frameworks: the Protective Factors Model and the Developmental Relationships Framework. The Protective Factors Model [12] acknowledges that all young people face risk and that positive outcomes can be actualized in the face of risk. In other words, there are factors that can support positive development through moderating the impact of risk factors. These are called protective factors (i.e., environmental, social, and individual factors that support resilience and help youth overcome adversity). This model reflects the underlying purpose of this study to determine protective factors that support youth with adverse experiences.

Li and Julian described “developmental relationships” as relationships that consistently support positive youth development across settings and developmental stages through emotional attachment, reciprocal interactions that increase in complexity over time, and power sharing that gradually favors the young person [13]. Li and Julian also identified developmental relationships as an “active ingredient” in promoting lasting change [13]. The Search Institute developed a measure to operationalize a framework to support the conceptualization of developmental relationships [14]. The Developmental Relationships Framework consists of five essential actions that help to facilitate the development of positive relationships for young people and subsequently promote positive youth development more broadly. These five actions include: expressing care, providing support, challenging youth to grow, sharing power with youth, and expanding youth's possibilities [14, 15]. Previous research has shown that young people with strong developmental relationships report a host of positive outcomes (e.g., social–emotional skills, GPA, school discipline, and communication skills) [14]. The Developmental Relationships Framework was used as a foundation for this study.

Adolescent Development and Developmental Relationships

Adolescent development is characterized by extensive biological, physical, and social changes [16]. Although adolescents often seek autonomy from their parents during this time [17], adolescent relationships with adults remain essential. In particular, advice and ongoing communication from parents and other trustworthy adults can support positive development in adolescents, specifically in relation to their psychosocial health [18]. This shift in peer importance creates circumstances where peers become a core influence on adolescents' identity development [19] and their perspectives on the value of school and academic achievement [20].

For high schoolers, peer and teacher support has been shown to be a protective factor, mitigating the socioemotional disruptions [21] and downward trajectory of academic performance [22] often experienced during the transition from middle school to high school. Moreover, positive teacher‐student relationships are significantly associated with increased educational investments (e.g., classroom preparation) and academic achievement [23]. Within the mentoring literature, strong mentoring relationships have also been linked to improvements in adolescents' relationships with their parents and more positive perceptions of those relationships [24, 25]. However, despite this evidence, the intersection of adversity, adolescents' relationships with caregiving adults, and school‐level factors (i.e., teacher and student relationships and school support and engagement) remains underexplored. This study aims to address this gap by examining the connection between youths' relationships with parenting adults and school‐level factors for young people who have experienced adversity.

Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs)

Adverse childhood experiences, or ACEs, were first defined by Felitti and colleagues and include measures of abuse and household dysfunction [26]. A higher cumulative ACEs score has been consistently associated with a higher risk of negative outcomes [27] in a variety of populations, including rural settings [28]. Research has shown ACEs to have a lasting influence on relationships, especially relationships with parents. Higher ACEs scores are associated with decreased closeness and communication satisfaction with parents [29] and lower peer relation quantity and status [30]. Moreover, adverse experiences perpetuated by the parent or caregiver may lead to insecure attachment and long‐term developmental problems (i.e., emotional, cognitive, and behavioral problems later in life) [31, 32].

However, supportive relationships have been shown to moderate or mitigate risks associated with ACEs [33]. For instance, strong social support from peers and teachers (as reported by students) has been found to reduce the association between family‐related adversities (i.e., parental substance abuse, verbal and physical abuse, intimate partner violence, sexual abuse within the family, and parental incarceration) and non‐suicidal self‐injury and suicidal behaviors among ninth‐grade students [34]. Additionally, school‐level factors (i.e., school connectedness, school safety) have been shown to play a protective role, reducing or buffering the negative relations between ACEs and youth outcomes (e.g., child well‐being, academics, and suicidality) [10, 11, 35]. Furthermore, high school‐level emotional competence has been shown to reduce the likelihood of involvement in negative relationships such as gang membership [36]. Another study, however, showed that school support (e.g., support from teachers and access to clubs and other out‐of‐school time activities) did not moderate the relation between ACEs and risky behaviors (i.e., alcohol or marijuana use) [37]. Therefore, it is still unclear whether and how school‐level factors are related to ACEs and youth outcomes. This study investigates the relation between ACEs and supportive youth relationships and examines whether school‐level supports can play a protective role.

Current Study

The two main aims of this study are to determine: (1) the association between ACEs and adolescents' different developmental relationships (different adults, friends), and (2) if school‐level factors, including developmental relationships with teachers, buffer the influence of ACEs on developmental relationships with parenting adults. We hypothesized that ACEs would be negatively associated with multiple developmental relationships (e.g., with parenting adults, teachers, friends) [5, 38]. However, given that school‐level factors and developmental relationships are known protective influences that promote positive youth outcomes [10, 11, 23], we hypothesized that these variables may also serve as buffers, supporting the developmental relationships of students who have experienced adversity.

Methods

Community Context

This study is a secondary data analysis of self‐reported, cross‐sectional data that was collected in one rural Midwest County to support county‐wide efforts to understand the resilience of resident youth in the face of the COVID‐19 pandemic. As of 2020, the county had 82,208 residents, a median household income of $65,739, and was predominantly White (86.6%) and male (50.5%) [39].

Procedures

The Indiana University of Pennsylvania Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved the procedures. District and school personnel helped recruit students, created a video that discussed the purpose of the survey, and provided class time for teachers to administer the survey. The research team provided considerations for implementing the survey and a script for teachers to use. Passive consent forms were sent to all parents prior to data collection, and students were able to assent to complete the survey at the beginning of the survey. Students completed the self‐administered anonymous questionnaire electronically on a school‐provided device (e.g., Chromebook) during the school day. No personally identifying information was collected (including student IDs or names), ensuring complete anonymity and confidentiality. It took participants, on average, 15 min to complete the survey, but this varied depending on the student's developmental stage (i.e., grade level and age). Participants received no compensation for completing the survey. The county was composed of two school districts. Participation rates were 78% (out of 7275 eligible students) in one district and 74% (out of 703 eligible students) in the second.

Participants

Data for the current study came from a subsample of 2900 high school students (ages 13–20, M = 15.6) from all four high schools in the county. Data were collected between September and October 2022. The county also operates one virtual school serving grades K‐12, which was excluded from this high school‐only sample. The high school sample was chosen because only high school students self‐reported on the adversity items, and the connection between ACEs and high schoolers' developmental relationships has been previously understudied. The sample was 50% female, 45% male, and 5% preferred to self‐describe. Thirty‐three percent of students self‐identified as non‐White, and 35% indicated some level of family financial strain (i.e., “we cannot buy the things we need sometimes” or “we have just enough money for the things we need”). Seventy‐three percent of students self‐identified as straight, followed by 3% as gay or lesbian and 9% as bisexual. Table 1 provides participants' descriptive characteristics. Compared to county demographics (86.6% White), the sample included higher racial diversity (33% non‐White students).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Students were included in this study if they were enrolled in one of the four traditional high schools (grades 9–12) during fall 2022, their parents did not opt out via the passive consent process, and they assented to participate. Students enrolled in the virtual K‐12 school were excluded, as this analysis focused on traditional high school settings.

Measures

Risk Factors

Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs)

Twelve items were adapted from Felitti et al. [26] and developed with county school personnel. Participant response options varied across items from frequency (i.e., often, occasionally, never, and prefer not to answer or more than once, once, never, don't know, and prefer not to answer) to endorsed (i.e., yes, no, don't know, and prefer not to answer). For consistency, all items were recoded to reflect whether or not the youth had experienced each adverse event. For example, if response options were often, occasionally, and never, both often and occasionally were recoded as indicating the presence of the adverse experience, while never was coded as the absence. Each item was thus dichotomized (1 = experienced, 0 = not experienced), and the 12 binary items were summed to create a total ACEs score, with higher scores indicating a greater number of adverse experiences. This total score was used in all subsequent analyses. Examples of items included: “Have you ever seen or heard any parents or adults in your home slap, hit, kick, punch, or beat each other up?”, “How often have you witnessed violence in your neighborhood?”, and “How often have you felt that you had no one to protect you?”

Protective Factors

School‐Level Contextual Factors

A school‐level holistic measure of school engagement and support was developed using the “school” subscale from the Developmental Assets Profile [40], a validated assessment of strengths and assets associated with positive youth development [41]. The subscale consists of 10 items and examines school engagement (e.g., “I do my homework.”), motivation (e.g., “I want to do well in school and my other activities”), boundaries and expectations (e.g., “I have a school that gives students clear rules”), and a caring school community (e.g., “I have teachers who help me do my best”). Items were rated on a 4‐point Likert scale ranging from 0 (Not at all or Rarely) to 3 (Extremely or Almost Always), and summed to create a composite score, with higher scores indicating greater perceived school engagement and support. The subscale demonstrated strong internal consistency in this sample (α = 0.88). While the subscale includes conceptually distinct constructs, it was treated as a unified composite to capture a broad and holistic perspective of school context.

Developmental Relationships 360

The developmental relationships 360 youth survey [15, 42] assessed five relational groups: parenting adults (including both parents and parenting caregivers), friends, teachers, neighbors, and program leaders (out‐of‐school time staff and leaders). Across these groups, the survey measured five elements of developmental relationships: express care, challenge growth, provide support, share power, and expand possibilities. Participants responded using a 4‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Rarely) to 4 (Almost Always). Example items include: “How often do the following people show you that you matter to them?” (i.e., express care) and “How often do the following people challenge you to be your best?” (i.e., challenge growth). For each relational group (e.g., parenting adults), the five items were averaged to create a composite score reflecting the overall quality of that developmental relationship, with higher scores indicating a strong perceived developmental relationship with those people.

Psychometric support has been established in a peer‐reviewed study [42] of middle and high school students (N = 1295), with CFA supporting the five‐element structure, strong internal consistency (a = 0.89–0.96), and measurement invariance across grade levels, though independent replication remains limited. In the present study, reliability was excellent across all relationship types (DRt, a = 0.89; DRpa, a = 0.89; DRn, a = 0.94; DRf, a = 0.85; DRpl, a = 0.93). Five types of relationships—parenting adults (DRpa), teachers (DRt), neighbors (DRn), friends (DRf), and program leaders (DRpl)—were included as dependent variables in the regression models, with DRt also examined as a moderator.

Covariates

Demographic covariates included gender (i.e., male, female, prefer to self‐describe), race (i.e., White, African American or Black, American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Hispanic of Latino/a/x, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, Other, Multiracial), financial strain (i.e., “can't buy the things we need sometimes,” “have just enough money for the things we need,” or “no problem buying the things we need”), LGBT (i.e., straight, gay or lesbian, bisexual, prefer to self‐describe, prefer not to answer), and age (continuous). We used Stata's factor variable syntax (i. variable) in our regression models to implement dummy coding automatically, ensuring that all categorical predictors were appropriately included and interpreted relative to their reference categories. The reference categories were: male (gender), White (race), no financial strain, and straight (LGBT identity).

Analytic Plan

Descriptive statistics and correlations were calculated for all variables of interest. Then, Kruskal‐Wallis tests, followed by Dunn's post hoc tests, were used to explore sociodemographic group differences in both predictor and outcome variables. Hierarchical regression models were used with covariates entered at the first step and predictors entered at the second step to explore the direct relationship between adversity and the different types of developmental relationships. Subsequently, interaction terms were entered at the third step to explore the association between developmental relationships with parenting adults and developmental relationships with teachers and school engagement and support. Due to normality concerns, the bootstrapping technique was used to examine all regression models. Additionally, because the predictor was continuous, it was mean‐centered for all interaction models. All analyses were conducted in Stata 18 [43].

Results

ACE scores were treated as continuous variables in all analyses. Scores ranged from 0 to 12, with a mean of 2.87 (SD = 2.83). The distribution was positively skewed (skewness = 1.03, kurtosis = 3.35), indicating that while most students reported few adversities, a subset experienced multiple ACEs. In particular, the plurality of the sample has 0 ACEs (25%), followed by 6 or more (18%), and 1 ACE (18%). The adversity reported by the most participants was “felt unloved, unimportant, or not special in family” (n = 1095; 49.57%), followed by “felt not close or not looked out for in family” (n = 899; 40.50%) and “parental [or guardian] divorce” (n = 847; 38.82%). The adversity experienced least frequently was “victim of neighborhood violence” (n = 235; 10.27%).

Interactions/Moderations

Based on regression results (ACEs were more strongly associated with developmental relationships with parents compared to others) and the well‐documented connection between adversity and youth/parental relationships [44, 45], DRpa was used as the only outcome of focus for the two moderation models examined. Figure 1 illustrates conceptual diagrams of the moderation regression models of interest. Linear regressions with interaction variables showed that ACEs on DRpa were moderated by DRt (R 2 = 0.36, W (3) = 1379.10, p < 0.001; 𝛽 = 0.014, SE = 0.006, p = 0.027; see Table 4). Additionally, ACEs on DRpa were moderated by school engagement and support (R 2 = 0.35, W (3) = 1307.72, p < 0.001; 𝛽 = 0.002, SE = 0.0008, p = 0.045; see Table 4). This means that although the relationship between ACEs and DRpa is consistently negative, this negative relationship decreases as DRt and school engagement and support increase. However, probing these interactions revealed modest buffering effects. For the ACEs × DRt interaction, simple slopes analysis showed that at low levels of DRt (1 SD below the mean), the effect of ACEs on DRpa was 𝛽 = −0.112 compared to 𝛽 = −0.101 at the mean and 𝛽 = −0.09 at high levels (1 SD above the mean). Additionally, for the ACEs × school engagement and support interactions, simple slopes analysis revealed that at lower levels of school engagement and support (1 SD below the mean), the effects of ACEs were 𝛽 = −0.101, compared to 𝛽 = −0.091 at mean levels, and 𝛽 = −0.082 at high levels (1 SD above the mean). Thus, while statistically significant, these interaction effects were small in magnitude, with the negative association between ACEs and parental relationships attenuating by only 0.02 units across the range of each moderator.

Discussion

This study sought to examine the relationship between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and adolescents' developmental relationships with five key groups of people in their lives (parents, teachers, friends, neighbors, program leaders), as well as school engagement and support. Overall, the findings show that higher ACE scores were associated with weaker developmental relationships across all relationship types. Moreover, developmental relationships with teachers and school context (i.e., school support and engagement) were found to modestly buffer the negative effects of ACEs on youths' relationships with parenting adults. These findings contribute to a growing body of research that emphasizes how cumulative childhood adversity is linked to interpersonal relationships and school connectedness during adolescence.

Descriptive analyses revealed variation in ACE exposure across racial/ethnic groups, with Asian students reporting lower average ACE scores compared to other groups. However, given the small proportion of Asian students in this sample (3.79%), future research with larger, more diverse samples is needed to examine whether the associations between ACEs and developmental relationships vary across racial and ethnic groups, and whether school‐based protective factors function similarly or differently across cultural contexts.

This study also showed that higher ACE scores were negatively associated with developmental relationships with parenting adults, teachers, friends, neighbors, and program leaders, which is consistent with prior research showing that youth with adversity often struggle to form and maintain supportive relationships [5, 38]. For example, children with a high cumulative ACE score are less likely to sustain mentoring relationships and more likely to have lower‐quality student‐teacher interactions [46, 47, 48]. Additionally, higher ACE scores are negatively correlated with family functioning (i.e., “ways in which family members interact together, maintain relationships, solve problems, communicate, and care for each other,” 293) [49] with a higher frequency of ACEs related to poorer family functioning. The negative associations we found with all relationships highlight the widespread influence that adverse experiences can have.

This study also investigated whether developmental relationships with teachers and school context (i.e., school engagement, motivation, boundaries and expectations, caring school community) were protective factors that could moderate the impact of ACEs on developmental relationships with parents. In particular, results showed that developmental relationships with teachers and a supportive school context moderated the association between ACEs and youth's relationships with their parents, although these buffering effects were modest in magnitude. These findings provide partial support for previous literature where school connectedness has been shown to play a protective role in buffering risks and adverse outcomes associated with ACEs [11, 35]. Additionally, early findings from mentoring research have demonstrated that mentoring relationships had a positive impact on a variety of youth outcomes, in part due to improving youth's relationships with their parents [50]. Results from this study indicate a similar mechanism whereby stronger teacher relationships and higher school engagement and support slightly reduced the negative impact of ACEs on parent–child relationships. However, the small effect size may suggest that school‐based factors alone may not buffer the negative effects of ACEs on parent–child relationships, and holistic interventions may be needed to meaningfully alter this association. Aligned with literature on webs of support [51], these results add additional evidence to the importance of developing a network of relationships that help support youth in myriad ways, including improving family relationships. Future studies could examine how the five actions within the Developmental Relationships framework (i.e., expressing care, providing support, challenging youth to grow, sharing power with youth, and expanding possibilities) [14, 15] relate to the development of positive relationships among youth with ACEs, as teachers and other adults may need additional support to effectively build connections with these youth.

Implications for School Health Policy, Practice, and Equity

This study provides empirical evidence supporting the importance of strong teacher‐student relationships and school environments that provide ample support and foster engagement. However, with the ever‐increasing demands on educators and schools, it can be challenging to prioritize these factors within the everyday school environment [52]. To address this, district and school leaders should consider policies that protect the time, capacity, and relational space educators need to foster meaningful connections with students. For example, schools could implement advisory periods so teachers can meet with the same small group of students every day, providing dedicated time to check in and build relationships with students outside of academic instruction.

Additionally, investing in professional development that leads educators and schools to become trauma‐informed can help bridge the gap between identifying what trauma is and how it can appear in school and classroom settings. While trauma‐informed approaches remain an emerging area of research, early evidence suggests that they can improve student behavior and disciplinary outcomes [53], increase staff awareness and understanding [54], and decrease student psychopathology symptoms [55]. Schools could implement trauma‐informed professional development that teaches educators to recognize signs of trauma and how to respond with intention. This training might also include scenarios that allow teachers to practice trauma‐informed responses in a safe, structured environment.

Finally, district leaders should evaluate and clarify the role of school‐based mental health professionals, as well as ensure there is adequate staff available to meet student needs. These steps can help to define what supports can be delivered within the classroom by educators and what should be referred to more specialized mental health personnel. This not only protects educators' capacity but also ensures that both students and staff receive effective and timely support. For example, schools could adopt a multi‐tiered system of support (MTSS) model [56] that defines roles for staff and teachers so that classroom teachers are trained to provide Tier 1 supports (e.g., basic emotion regulation strategies in the classroom setting), school counselors provide Tier 2 interventions (e.g., small group meditation and relaxation techniques), and school psychologists or social workers deliver Tier 3 services (e.g., trauma‐focused cognitive behavioral therapy). Together, these recommendations can promote more equitable access to mental health services, foster safer and more responsive school environments, and strengthen teacher‐student relationships.

Limitations and Future Directions

This study identifies potential protective factors that modestly buffer the impact of ACEs on developmental relationships, but it has several limitations. First, its cross‐sectional, single community design prevents causal inferences and limits generalizability. Additionally, because the data were self‐reported, some of the data may be subject to social desirability biases. Furthermore, while interaction terms were statistically significant, the coefficients remained relatively small across both models. This may be related to the way ACEs were measured (i.e., as a continuous sum score). However, ACEs can be operationalized in various ways (e.g., sum score and thresholds), which can influence findings and limit comparisons across studies. Moreover, the ACEs scale does not account for the severity or frequency of experiences, nor does it include all relevant experiences (e.g., death of a caregiver). Future research should consider using other measures of adversity that provide a more comprehensive measure of adversity, as well as investigating how different methods of measuring ACEs relate to youth's developmental trajectories and their relationships with others.

Conclusions

Findings from this study reveal that students' adversity exposures are negatively associated with all developmental relationships (i.e., parenting adults, teachers, friends, program leaders, neighbors); however, the impact is largest in developmental relationships with parenting adults. Additionally, while school protective factors (i.e., school engagement and support and developmental relationships with teachers) showed statistically significant buffering effects on the negative impact of ACEs on youth's developmental relationships with parental adults, these protective effects were modest in magnitude. Future research could explore whether an intervention focused on improving developmental relationships or enhancing school engagement and support increases the buffering results seen within this study. Additionally, research on interventions that integrate school and family efforts may be particularly valuable given the modest protective capacity of school factors alone.
