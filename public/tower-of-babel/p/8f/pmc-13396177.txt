Incidence and risk factors of malaria with hematological and immunological evaluation and integrable analysis of a fractional mathematical model
Authors: Manal Adil Murad, Shayma Adil Murad, Thabet Abdeljawad, Manar A Alqudah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria during pregnancy remains a major public health challenge, contributing to maternal anemia, low birth weight, stillbirth, and preterm delivery. This study investigated the prevalence of malaria among 400 pregnant women and evaluated its associated risk factors, hematological alterations, and inflammatory responses, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-) and interferon-gamma (IFN-). Anti-Plasmodium IgM and IgG antibodies, TNF-, and IFN- were measured using ELISA, while hematological indices were assessed using a Coulter counter. The overall malaria prevalence was 51.2%, with 68.3% IgG and 31.7% IgM seropositivity among infected cases. Infection was significantly associated with age, residence, education level, animal husbandry, and housing conditions. Infected women exhibited significant reductions in red blood cell count, hemoglobin, and hematocrit, alongside elevated white blood cell counts and pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF- and IFN-). Motivated by these clinical findings, we further analyzed an established mathematical model of malaria in pregnancy within the framework. We proved the existence of a -integrable solution using the Leray–Schauder nonlinear alternative and the Banach fixed-point theorem, and examined stability in the sense of Ulam–Hyers and Ulam–Hyers–Rassias. Numerical simulations generated using MATLAB illustrate and support the theoretical results. To the best of our knowledge, this study contributes to the analytical investigation of malaria-in-pregnancy models within the setting, highlighting its mathematical significance.Keywords: Blood parameters, Cytokines, Malaria(Pregnant women), Risk factors, Mathematical model(Fixed point theorems & Stability analysis)Subject terms: Diseases, Immunology, Medical research

Introduction

Malaria is a parasitic disease caused by protozoa of the genus Plasmodium, transmitted primarily through the bite of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes1. The four primary species of Plasmodium responsible for human malaria are P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale, and P. malariae2. These parasites are prevalent in areas with mosquito populations and favorable environmental conditions. Factors such as temperature, humidity, and rainfall influence mosquito breeding and survival3. In addition to mosquito bites, malaria can also be transmitted through blood transfusions, organ transplants, or the use of contaminated needles and syringes4.

Pregnant women are particularly vulnerable to malaria, which can lead to a range of adverse outcomes including maternal anemia, preterm delivery, miscarriage, stillbirth, and low birth weight5. It is estimated that infection in pregnancy leads to an additional 521,000 neonates born with low birth weight, considering current levels of intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy coverage6. In non-endemic areas, P. falciparum during pregnancy is more likely to cause symptomatic disease and severe complications such as cerebral malaria, compared to non-pregnant women7.

Malaria alters hematological factors, affecting red and white blood cells as well as platelets. These changes are caused by the parasite’s lifecycle within red blood cells and the host’s immune response. Common blood abnormalities include decreased hemoglobin due to destruction of both infected and uninfected red blood cells8. Hematocrit, mean corpuscular volume, and mean corpuscular hemoglobin are depleted, indicating microcytic, hypochromic anemia in chronic infections. Other notable effects include neutrophilia, eosinophilia, leukopenia, and thrombocytopenia9.

The defense mechanism against Plasmodium involves a complex interplay of cytokines. Cytokines such as interleukin-12 (IL-12), interleukin-18 (IL-18), interferon-gamma (IFN-), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-) are critical in enhancing the activity of immune cells and initiating antiparasitic responses. On the other hand, interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-) help regulate inflammation, thereby preventing excessive host-mediated tissue damage10.

In Iraq, various studies have examined aspects of malaria, including epidemiology, clinical manifestations, and treatment11,12. The goal of this study is to investigate the incidence, associated risk factors, and to screen blood indices, TNF-, and IFN- for the tested samples.

Recently, mathematical modeling has received increasing attention from researchers, especially in the context of fractional calculus. These models describe the spread of infectious diseases through mathematical equations, aiding in the prediction of outbreak dynamics and the evaluation of control measures to support public health decisions, see13–15.

Several studies have highlighted the effectiveness of fractional-order derivatives in modeling infectious diseases using mathematical frameworks, underlining the critical role of memory and nonlocal effects in disease persistence and transmission. Bhatia et al.16 investigated tuberculosis transmission with a Caputo fractional mathematical model, while Khan et al.17 conducted a theoretical study on the spread of SARS-CoV-2 using a fractional-order epidemiological model. A fractional-order model employing the Caputo derivative was also applied to analyze cancer cell dynamics18.

Numerous academics have conducted epidemiological studies on malaria using mathematical modeling. For instance, as presented in19, the work extends an existing malaria model into a nonlinear optimal control framework to assess the effectiveness of personal protection, treatment, and mosquito reduction strategies, supported by theoretical analysis and numerical results. Al Basir et al.20 developed a malaria model that incorporates awareness and control measures to evaluate the impact of interventions and optimize disease management costs. The authors in21–23 examined the impact of awareness, vector control, and environmental factors on malaria transmission using mathematical modeling approaches.

The existence and uniqueness of solutions are fundamental in validating mathematical models, ensuring that the system admits a well-defined solution under given conditions. In this context, Cui and Zhou24 and Li25 investigated fractional differential equations with nonlocal and functional boundary conditions, establishing the existence and uniqueness of solutions. Furthermore, Murad et al.26 and Dai et al.27 analyzed fractional differential equations using different forms of Ulam–Hyers stability, providing important results on the stability behavior of solutions. More generally, fractional-order models capture memory effects and ensure key solution properties, including boundedness, non-negativity, and existence28, which are fundamental for validating epidemiological models and ensuring biologically meaningful solutions. Similarly, Zafar et al.29 demonstrated the existence and uniqueness of solutions and analyzed the stability of a fractional-order SEIR model, providing a methodological framework for infectious disease modeling. In addition, Ulam–Hyers stability has been widely employed to assess the robustness and stability of solutions in fractional-order systems30.

The study of fractional differential equations in -spaces plays a crucial role in understanding the qualitative behavior of solutions, particularly regarding their existence, uniqueness, and stability under various boundary conditions. Such frameworks provide a broader and more flexible setting for analyzing real-world systems governed by memory and hereditary properties. The authors in31,32 established an existence theorem for fractional differential equations in -spaces, providing foundational results in this field. Agarwal et al.33 further advanced this framework by examining -solutions of fractional integral equations. More recently, Souid et al.34 investigated the stability of -integrable solutions using piecewise constant functions, thereby contributing additional insights to the theoretical development of fractional models in functional settings.

Malaria during pregnancy remains a significant public health concern, with serious implications for both maternal and fetal health. Despite numerous studies modeling malaria transmission in general populations, there is a lack of integrated approaches that combine clinical observations in pregnant women with tailored mathematical models.

Our study combines a clinical investigation of malaria in 400 pregnant women with a novel pregnancy-specific mathematical model. Clinically, we assessed prevalence, risk factors, hematological parameters, TNF-, and IFN-, finding a 51.2% infection rate, with higher risk among younger, rural, and multigravida women, and altered blood and inflammatory markers in infected individuals.

Mathematically, we developed a compartmental model including healthy (H), infected (I), and recovered (R) pregnant women, along with the mosquito vector (V), and rigorously analyzed its existence, uniqueness, and stability. This integrated approach provides original insights into malaria’s effects during pregnancy and establishes a solid quantitative and theoretical framework for evaluating interventions and understanding disease dynamics in this vulnerable population. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to develop and analyze a malaria model specifically for pregnant women within the framework.

Materials and methods

This section presents the methodological framework of the study, including the classification of pregnant participants, the laboratory techniques used to diagnose malaria in pregnant women, the serological assays conducted to detect malaria-specific antibodies, as well as the procedures for data collection and the statistical methods applied in the analysis.

Study Population: A total of 400 women in pregnancy within the age range 15–44 years old were recruited. They attended gynecology and obstetrics hospital and clinics in Duhok city. Informed written consent earned and data collection primarily conducted by administering a questionnaire to gather the necessary information. Participants were recruited using a consecutive sampling technique, where all eligible pregnant women who met the inclusion criteria and consented during the study period were enrolled until the desired sample size was achieved.

Sample Collection: From each participant about five milliliter of blood was obtained using a sterile syringe, one milliliter is placed into a tube with anticoagulants.While, other four milliliter put in the second tube without anticoagulant, left for 20 minutes to clot and then centrifuged for 10 minutes at 3000 rpm to get sera, the serum put in sterile Eppendorf tubes, labeled and stored at -C until being used35.

Measurement of Plasmodium IgM and IgG Antibodies using ELISA: The measurement of IgM and IgG antibodies using ELISA test (Nova Lisa, Germany), the procedure achieved according to manufacture company. The calibrators and serum samples incubated in microplate wells coated with inactivated antigens. After incubating and washing the samples, the wells treated with a conjugate labeled with horseradish peroxidase (HRP). After the second incubation and washing step, the wells then incubated with tetra methylbenzidine (TMB). Finally, an acidic stop solution added and the absorbance read at 450nm using an ELISA microplate reader.

Measurement of Blood Parameters: Complete blood count evaluated using a Coulter count machine (Swelab, Germany).

Measurement of (TNF-) and IL-18 using ELISA: The detection of the TNF- and IFN- by ELISA test (Elabscience, USA), the procedure done according to the manufacturing company.

Inclusion Criteria: Those who willed to participate in the work. Pregnant women aged 15–45 years with a confirmed pregnancy who provided informed consent were included.Participants were clinically stable and had not received antimalarial or immunosuppressive drugs before sample collection.

Exclusion Criteria: Those did not agree, with infectious, non-infectious, chronic and autoimmune diseases excluded. Women with chronic illnesses such as HIV infection, diabetes, renal or liver disease, or autoimmune disorders were excluded. Those with other acute infections, multiple pregnancies, recent blood transfusion, or prior antimalarial treatment within 48 hours were also excluded.

Statistical Analysis: The statistical program R and a chi-square test were used. The means, standard deviation, range, and data (n) with percentage (%). A P-value is a statistically significant.

Discussion

Malaria is a life-threatening condition that causes severe complications and disruption of body functions. The frequency of infection was 51.2%. The result is in line with Awosolu et al41. and Nyasa et al42., who discovered the rate of infection as 55.0% and 53.2%, respectively. Inversely, the authors of43,44 revealed 1.4% and 31.8% positive ratio. Generally, this may be related to the sample size, different methods of diagnosis, climate change, and socioeconomic conditions45.

Our results showed that malaria prevalence was highest among young pregnant women and lowest in older women. This increased susceptibility in younger women, particularly adolescents and young adults, is likely due to their limited acquired immunity, whereas older women develop partial immunity through repeated exposure. Reported prevalence varies across studies, with 3.5% in the 15–24 age group versus 1.1% in 36–45 years46, and contrastingly, 33.3% in 40–51 years versus 7.4% in 17–25 years47.

Furthermore, malaria was more common in countryside areas than in urban ones. This could probably be attributed to limited access to healthcare services, low socioeconomic status, and favorable environmental conditions for mosquito breeding in rustic regions48. A similar output recorded by Setegn et al49. found a percentage of 25.5% in rural and 5.9% in city residents, sequentially. However, Bag et al50. investigated a frequency in civic areas (18.8%) compared to rural (6.7%).

Multigravida women showed higher malaria positivity than primigravida, consistent with Diouf et al51., who attributed increased susceptibility in first pregnancies to limited pregnancy-specific immunity. Partial immunity develops over successive pregnancies, though findings contrast with52, highlighting the complexity of immune responses across populations. Hematological assessment revealed that malaria-positive individuals had significant reductions in RBC count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, MCV, and MCH, indicating hypochromic microcytic anemia, as supported by the authors of53. Changes in leukocyte profiles reflect immune activation and systemic inflammation, while decreased platelet counts indicate thrombocytopenia, a common complication of severe malaria, as noted by Garba et al54.. Monitoring blood parameters is essential for assessing disease severity and guiding early interventions to prevent anemia and related complications. In infected individuals, TNF- and IFN- levels increased via Th1/Th2 cell activation, stimulating immune responses, consistent with12,46. Elevated cytokines are attributed to parasite phagocytosis, toxin exposure, and oxidative stress involving reactive oxygen species, which further activate leukocytes and cytokine release55.

Biologically, the transmission parameter reflects higher malaria prevalence in younger and rural women, while the complication parameter corresponds to anemia and low hemoglobin. Constant recruitment rates and represent population dynamics, and the stability analysis shows convergence toward an endemic equilibrium, consistent with the observed prevalence (51.2%). Integrating mathematical results with clinical observations underscores the model’s epidemiological relevance and its role in guiding prevention and control strategies.

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations. First, it included 400 pregnant women from a single city, which provides valuable insights into malaria in this population; however, the findings may not be fully generalizable. Including a larger sample size from multiple regions or cities would improve the robustness and external validity of the results. Second, parasite density and malaria severity were not assessed, which could influence hematological and cytokine levels. Third, limited laboratory resources restricted the range of biochemical and immunological parameters analyzed. Fourth, the mathematical model relies on simplified assumptions and estimated parameters; only the existence, uniqueness, and local stability of the solution were analyzed, without considering global stability or external/random effects.

Conclusion

This study shows that malaria during pregnancy affects hematological and immunological parameters, including reduced hemoglobin, red blood cell counts, and platelets, along with elevated TNF- and IFN-, which may impact maternal health. The findings indicate that younger women, rural residents, those living in poorly constructed houses, and women raising domestic animals may be more vulnerable to infection. The fractional Caputo mathematical model provides a theoretical framework for understanding disease dynamics, with unique and locally stable solutions under the model assumptions. These results may support early diagnosis, monitoring, and targeted preventive strategies, while future studies with larger and more diverse populations and extended modeling approaches could further improve understanding of malaria dynamics in pregnant women.
