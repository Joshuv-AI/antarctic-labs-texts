Prevalence and characteristics of coronary anomalies in Bulgarian patients undergoing coronary angiography
Authors: Rozen Grigorov, Stefan Yambolov, Ivaylo Borisov, Nikolai Nikolov, Svetoslav Georgiev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary artery anomalies (CAAs) are rare congenital abnormalities with variable reported prevalence. Data from Eastern Europe remain limited. This study assessed the prevalence and characteristics of CAAs in a large Bulgarian angiographic cohort.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 9 567 consecutive patients who underwent diagnostic coronary angiography between January 2014 and July 2025 at a single tertiary center. CAAs were classified according to the Angelini system into anomalies of origin and course, intrinsic anomalies and anomalies of termination. Prevalence was calculated both including and excluding myocardial bridges and commonly considered benign anatomical variants.ResultsOverall, 530 anomalies were identified (5.5%), including 396 myocardial bridges (4.1%). After exclusion of myocardial bridges and anatomical variants, 83 patients had true CAAs (0.87%). Anomalies of origin and course predominated (63.9%), most commonly involving anomalous origin of the left circumflex artery from the right coronary sinus or right coronary artery (0.32% of the population). Coronary fistulae accounted for 36.1% of anomalies (0.31%). High-risk, Rigatelli class III anomalies were uncommon (9.6%).ConclusionThe prevalence and distribution of CAAs in this Bulgarian cohort are consistent with international data. Background

Coronary artery anomalies (CAAs) are congenital abnormalities involving the origin, course, termination or intrinsic anatomy of the epicardial coronary arteries [1]. Although their overall prevalence in the general population is low, typically below 1%, certain subtypes are clinically significant and have been associated with myocardial ischemia, arrhythmias, and sudden cardiac death [2–4].

The literature shows considerable variability in the definition, classification, and reported prevalence of CAAs. Some authors include myocardial bridges and anatomic variants, such as separate ostia of the left anterior descending artery (LAD) and the left circumflex artery (LCx), anterior origin of the right coronary artery (RCA) or high take-off of the coronary arteries, while others regard these as benign variants rather than true anomalies. As a result, the frequency of CAAs in angiographic and computed tomography series ranges widely from 0.43% to 5.79% [5–7].

Despite their rarity, coronary anomalies have not only epidemiological relevance but also important clinical implications. Accurate identification is essential for appropriate risk assessment, interventional planning, treatment, and understanding of regional population patterns.

The present study aims to describe the prevalence and spectrum of coronary artery anomalies in a large Bulgarian cohort.

Materials and methods

This retrospective, single-center study was conducted at the Interventional Cardiology Clinic of St. Marina University Hospital, Varna, Bulgaria. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee of the Medical University of Varna (№26/22.01.2026). The analysis included all patients who underwent diagnostic coronary angiography between January 2014 and July 2025. Data were extracted from the institutional catheterization laboratory registry and verified by reviewing the original angiographic recordings and procedural reports.

Each anomaly was classified according to the Angelini classification (anomalies of origin/course, anomalies of termination, intrinsic anomalies) and the Rigatelli functional classification (Classes I–III) [2, 8].

All angiographic protocols were screened for:

Anomalies of origin and course, including cases with separate ostia of the LAD and LCx, anterior origin and high take-off of the RCA or LCA;

Intrinsic anomalies, which included myocardial bridges but excluded patients with coronary aneurysms or ectasia, as the etiology of these findings (atherosclerosis, positive remodeling, connective tissue disorders, or vasculitis) could not be determined;

Anomalies of termination, encompassing arteriovenous fistulas between coronary arteries and the pulmonary artery (PA), cardiac chambers, or venous structures.

We did not screen for: separate ostium of the conus branch and the RCA, split RCA and dual LAD, as these variants are not systematically reported in our catheterization laboratory registry.

The coronary artery dominance was classified as right dominant when the posterior descending artery (PDA) originated from the RCA, left dominant when the PDA originated from the LCx, and balanced when the PDA originated from the RCA in combination with a large posterolateral branch originating from the LCx or both the RCA and LCx gave rise to a PDA [9].

For the purpose of this analysis, “true coronary artery anomalies” were defined as deviations from normal coronary anatomy, excluding myocardial bridges and commonly accepted benign anatomical variants, such as separate ostia of the LAD and LCx and high take-off or anterior origin of the coronary arteries. Myocardial bridges were analyzed separately due to their high prevalence and generally benign clinical course.

Descriptive statistics were reported both including and excluding these variants, facilitating comparison with previously published studies.

Statistical analysis was performed using Python with standard scientific libraries for statistical and graphical analysis. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as absolute numbers and percentages of the total cohort.

Results

A total of 11 827 angiographic protocols were screened. After exclusion of repeat procedures in the same patient and incomplete procedures, 9 567 unique coronary angiograms were included, corresponding to 9 567 individual patients.

Among them, 8 527 (89.2%) had right-dominant circulation, 693 (7.2%) had left dominance, and 347 (3.6%) exhibited balanced coronary circulation.

Prevalence of coronary anomalies in the studied population

Overall, 530 coronary anomalies were identified. These included 396 myocardial bridges and 134 anomalies of origin and course, intrinsic anomalies, or anomalies of termination, corresponding to a total prevalence of 5.5% in the studied population.

When myocardial bridges (4.1% of the population) and anatomic variants (separate ostia of LAD and LCx (n = 28, 0.29%), high take-off/anterior origin of the right (n = 21, 0.21%) or high take-off of the left coronary artery (n = 2, 0.02%) were excluded, 83 patients (0.87%) were found to have true coronary anomalies.

Demographic and angiographic characteristics

The mean age of patients with coronary anomalies was 62.6 ± 10.2 years, with 50 males (60.2%) and 33 females (39.8%).

The arteries most frequently involved were the LCx in 34 cases (41.0%), the RCA in 25 cases (30.1%), the LAD in 18 cases (21.7%), and the left main coronary artery (LM) in 3 cases (3.6%). Combined involvement of two vessels was observed in three cases, including LAD and RCA in two patients (2.4%) and LAD and LCx in one patient (1.2%).

According to the Angelini classification, 53 anomalies were categorized as anomalies of origin/course, and 30 anomalies as anomalies of termination.

Anomalies of origin

Anomalies of origin were observed in 53 patients (63.9% of all anomalies; 0.55% of the total population). The vast majority (52 out of 53) originated from the aortic sinuses, while 1 case represented an anomalous origin from the pulmonary artery.

The most frequent subtype was anomalous origin of the LCx from the right coronary sinus (Fig. 2), identified in 19 patients (22.9% of anomalies; 0.20% of population), while LCx origin from the proximal RCA was seen in 9 patients (10.8%; 0.09%), and LCx origin from the right coronary sinus with a common ostium with the RCA – in 2 patients (2.4%; 0.02%). The second most common anomaly of origin was RCA origin from the left coronary sinus (Fig. 3), seen in 12 patients (14.5%; 0.13%).

A single case of ALCAPA (LM arising from the pulmonary artery) was identified (1.2% of anomalies; 0.01% of the total population). The anomaly was corrected at the age of 48 with ligation of the LM and bypass surgery with an arterial graft to the LAD.

Anomalies of termination

Anomalies of termination were found in 30 patients (36.1%; 0.31%). The majority of these involved communications between a coronary artery and the pulmonary artery: LAD–PA fistulae in 16 patients (19.3%; 0.17%), RCA–PA in 6 (7.2%; 0.06%), LCx–PA in 2 (2.4%; 0.02%). In 3 patients there was a combined fistula between LAD/RCA–PA or LAD/LCx–PA. A RCA-PA fistula is shown in Fig. 5.

Rigatelli functional classification

According to the Rigatelli classification, most anomalies were categorized as Class I and II, representing benign anomalies or anomalies with potential to cause ischemia. Class III anomalies with high risk of myocardial ischemia and sudden cardiac death were rare (n = 8; 9.6%) and included cases of interarterial course of the LCx, ALCAPA, and several fistulas, specifically LAD–PA (n = 3), RCA–PA (n = 1), LAD/RCA–PA (n = 1), LAD/LCx–PA (n = 1), and LCx–coronary sinus fistula with hypoplastic RCA (n = 1).

Discussion

Prevalence and classification

Coronary artery anomalies are uncommon congenital abnormalities, with reported angiographic prevalence ranging between 0.3% and 1.3% in most large series [7, 10]. When myocardial bridges, separate ostia of the LAD and LCx, high take-off of the RCA and split RCA are included, the frequency may reach 5–6% [1, 11, 12]. In this study, representing one of the largest single-center angiographic series from Eastern Europe, the overall prevalence of anomalies was 5.5%. The prevalence of true coronary anomalies, excluding separate ostia, high take-off, and myocardial bridges, was 0.87%. These figures fall within the range reported in major angiographic studies such as those by Angelini et al. (5.64%) [1], Kardos et al. (1.34%) [12], and Sivri et al. (0.74%) [13].

The Angelini classification remains the most widely accepted system and was used in this study to group anomalies into those of origin/course, termination, and intrinsic coronary anatomy. Consistent with prior reports, anomalies of origin and course predominated (63.9%), followed by anomalies of termination (36.1%) [1, 12, 13].

The distinction between “true anomalies” and anatomical variants is not uniformly defined across studies, contributing to variability in reported prevalence. Reporting results both including and excluding anatomical variants facilitates comparison with previously published series. Anomalies of origin and course

Taken together, anomalous origin of the LCx from the right coronary sinus or from the RCA was the most frequent subtype, involving 31 patients and 37% of all anomalies, corresponding to 0.32% of the total angiographic population. This is similar to the incidence reported by Yamanaka and Hobbs (0.37%) [7], Sivri et al. (0.36%) [13], Erol et al. (0.43%) [16] and Nawale et al. (0.33%) [17]. These anomalies usually follow a retroaortic course and are considered benign in the absence of atherosclerotic stenosis.

Anomalous origin of the RCA from the left coronary sinus was the second most common anomaly (14.5% of all anomalies; 0.13% of total cases). This variant is clinically important, as an interarterial course between the aorta and pulmonary artery has been associated with myocardial ischemia and sudden cardiac death [22]. Other rare anomalies included single coronary artery (RCA from LM or LM from right sinus), double circumflex artery, and LAD origin from the right sinus. A single case of ALCAPA was also detected, representing 0.01% of all angiograms, in line with its reported rarity (1 in 300 000) [23]. However, it is worth mentioning that most angiographic series in adult populations do not report ALCAPA cases.

In contrast to our findings, Sidhu et al. reported dual LAD as the second most common anomaly in their angiographic series (0.68% of the angiographic cohort) [14]. We did not provide data on dual LAD in the present analysis, as this variant is not routinely or systematically documented in our laboratory reports. This limitation is consistent with many large angiographic series, in which dual LAD is either inconsistently defined or omitted altogether [7, 14].

Similarly, some authors have identified split RCA as the most frequent anomaly; however, this subtype was likewise not included in our study because of the lack of structured reporting for this variant [1].

These differences highlight a fundamental challenge in coronary anomaly research. The multitude of variants and the lack of uniform diagnostic and reporting standards lead to substantial heterogeneity in published incidence rates. Consequently, comparisons across studies should be made cautiously.

Anomalies of termination

Anomalous coronary fistulae were identified in 30 patients (0.31% of the cohort), comparable to large angiographic series (0.06–0.9%) [7, 11, 14, 24, 25]. The LAD–PA fistula was the most common subtype (19.3% of anomalies), followed by RCA–PA (7.2%) and LCx–PA (2.4%), consistent with previous studies where the pulmonary artery was the most frequent drainage site, however, in previous studies, the RCA is reported as the most frequent artery of origin [1, 14, 26]. Most fistulae in our study were small and hemodynamically insignificant, corresponding to Rigatelli class I or II. However, selected cases were categorized as class III based on features suggestive of hemodynamic significance, including larger fistula size, associated clinical presentation, echocardiographic findings, and evidence of inducible ischemia.

The distribution of CAAs in our Bulgarian cohort is similar to that observed in international series, suggesting no major geographic or ethnic differences in anomaly patterns. However, our registry adds valuable regional data from Eastern Europe, where large angiographic studies on CAAs are lacking.

Anatomical classification provides an essential framework; however, the clinical relevance of CAAs is strongly influenced by their hemodynamic and functional impact. Recent EURO-AAOCA registry data highlight heterogeneity in diagnostic and management strategies across European centres and support the integration of anatomical assessment with clinical presentation and functional testing in selected high-risk anomalies [27]. Invasive physiological assessment and stress imaging may reveal dynamic compression and inducible ischemia that are not apparent from anatomical findings alone [28, 29].

Limitations

The retrospective single-center design and inclusion of only patients undergoing angiography may have introduced selection bias toward individuals with suspected ischemic heart disease. The true prevalence in the general population may therefore be lower. Furthermore, functional assessment (e.g., ischemia testing or physiological measurements such as fractional flow reserve) was not systematically performed, which may limit the ability to fully assess the clinical significance of certain coronary anomalies. The anatomical course of anomalous coronary arteries arising from the opposite sinus was not systematically characterized in all cases and was therefore not included as a separate variable, which may limit detailed risk stratification of these anomalies. Finally, the absence of systematic reporting for certain anatomical variants (separate ostium of the conus branch, dual LAD, split RCA) limits their inclusion in the present analysis and may affect direct comparability with studies in which these variants are routinely reported.

Conclusion

Most coronary anomalies are benign and do not require specific treatment. However, recognition of potentially high-risk variants is essential, as they may require further functional assessment or, in selected cases, surgical or percutaneous intervention.

CAAs were identified in 5.5% of all angiographic examinations, and in 0.87% when applying stricter criteria that excluded myocardial bridges and anatomical variants. Anomalies of origin and course predominated, most commonly involving the circumflex artery. Recognition of these patterns is important for accurate diagnosis, risk stratification, and interventional planning in contemporary clinical practice.
