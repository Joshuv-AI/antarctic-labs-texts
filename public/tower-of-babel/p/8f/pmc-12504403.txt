A reappraisal of association between ventilator-associated events and mortality among critically ill patients using marginal structural model: multicenter observational study
Authors: Susumu Nakahashi, Kei Suzuki, Takaya Nakashima, Yoshiro Hayashi, Yuko Tanabe, Aiko Tanaka, Shinsuke Hashiuchi, Chizuru Yamashita, Yusuke Ito, Takeshi Wada, Ayahiro Yamashita, Masanori Shima, Tetsuya Hoshino, Kiyoshi Moriyama, Satoshi Kazuma, Hyun Ah Lee, Yoshikazu Yamaguchi, Yukari Nakamura, Yu Kawanobe, Toshiki Sofue, Yuki Nishimura, Tomohiro Shinozaki, Tadahiro Goto, Satoru Hashimoto, Yuji Fujino, Nobuaki Shime, the Japan VAE study Investigators Group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeVentilator-associated events (VAEs), developed by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), are considered a new medical quality indicator associated with poor outcomes. However, previous studies failed to adjust for changes in patient severity, leaving open the possibility that VAEs are just a surrogate for severity. This study aimed to reevaluate the association between VAEs and mortality, adjusting for changes in severity over time.MethodsThis multicenter observational study was conducted in 18 ICUs in Japan between May 2020 and December 2022. Patients aged ≥ 12 years who received mechanical ventilation for ≥ 3 consecutive days were eligible. VAE was diagnosed according to the CDC definition. The primary outcome was 30-day in-hospital mortality, whose association with VAE was estimated by the inverse probability weighted proportional hazards model, with temporal changes in patient severity treated as time-dependent confounders.ResultsAmong 1,094 subjects, 106 VAEs (9.7%) were identified, giving an incidence rate of 10.0 per 1,000 ventilation days. VAEs were significantly associated with increased 30-day hospital and ICU mortality (HR 2.00; 95% CI 1.23–3.26 and HR 1.92; 95% CI 1.03–3.57), and longer hospital and ICU length of stay (HR 0.72 95% CI 0.54–0.97 and HR 0.47; 95% CI 0.23–0.96). The population attributable risk fraction of VAE-related mortality was 8.8% for in-hospital deaths and 8.2% for ICU deaths.ConclusionsVAEs were associated with an increased risk of mortality after adjustment for severity, as a time-dependent confounder.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00134-025-08074-x.Keywords: Ventilator-associated pneumonia, Quality indicator, Surveillance, Mechanical ventilation, Intensive care unit

Take-home message

The association between VAEs and mortality has been reexamined with appropriate adjustment for temporal changes in severity. Using causal inference methods, we demonstrated a significant association between VAEs and poor prognosis, thus supporting its potential utility as a quality indicator.

Introduction

In 2013, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)/National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) launched a new medical quality surveillance system [1]. They released the definition of the ventilator-associated events (VAEs) as a quality indicator (QI) capable of comprehensive monitoring and automated diagnosis of ventilator-associated respiratory complications. VAEs refer to complications that worsen respiratory function and can be diagnosed easily and objectively. VAEs are defined based on mechanical ventilation settings that involve “an increase of 3 or more points in positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) or 0.2 (20%) or more points in fraction of inspired oxygen (FIO2), for more than two days after a stable period,” regardless of pathophysiology. However, VAE surveillance is only utilized by the NHSN and a limited number of surveillance providers (Supplemental Materials 1). The reason for the barriers to adopting VAE surveillance is that the relationship between VAEs and prognosis has been questioned among some stakeholders [2–6].

VAEs may be nothing more than a surrogate indicator of severity. Previous studies have reported worse prognosis for patients with VAEs than for those without VAEs. However, the ability to control confounders is a concern. In many previous observational studies, severity was assessed only at baseline point [7–15]. VAE is essentially an event of underlying critical illness [7, 10, 14, 16–18] and patients with and without VAEs are inherently different in severity of illness at the onset or before acquisition of VAEs, so that mortality differences between these groups cannot be fully attributed to VAEs [6, 8, 14, 15]. The difference in mortality may be due to the difference in the severity of the patient at that time. Therefore, VAEs could be a simply marker of illness level.

To solve the above issue, we expect to better control the occurrence of VAEs and the impact of severity changes on mortality by performing the analysis with treating the temporal changes in severity indicators as time-dependent confounders [19–21]. This study aimed to reevaluate the association between VAEs and mortality by appropriately adjusting for patient severity over time.

Methods

Study design and settings

This is a multicenter, prospective observational study, involving 18 intensive care units (ICUs) in Japan (Supplemental Fig. 1), from May 2020 to December 2022. The study protocol was approved by the Mie University Institutional Review Board (IRB: H2019047) and by the ethics committees of all the other participating hospitals, and it included an opt-out policy for the patient or proxy. The types of participating hospitals are provided in Supplemental Fig. 1. More detailed information about the institutions, such as the number of ICU beds and ICU physicians, is provided in Supplemental Table 1. The construction followed the STROBE statement.

Study participants

We enrolled patients aged ≥ 12 years who underwent invasive mechanical ventilation for ≥ 3 days in the ICU. For patients with multiple ICU admissions, only the first eligible admission was included; subsequent readmissions requiring mechanical ventilation were not enrolled.

Measurements

Data collection included demographics (age, sex, body mass index [BMI]), medical history, ICU admission diagnosis, and arterial blood gas analysis. ICU data were recorded daily for up to 30 days, including mechanical ventilation settings, treatments (fluid volume, prone positioning, sedatives, vasopressors), Richmond agitation-sedation scale (RASS) and sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) scores, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) use, antibiotic therapy, ventilator-free days, and extubation status. Procedural information (continuous renal replacement therapy [CRRT], transfusions) was obtained from the diagnosis procedure combination (DPC) system.

Exposure: definition of ventilator-associated events

The VAE was defined according to CDC guidelines [22], focusing on changes in oxygenation parameters (PEEP or FIO2) after a certain period of stability. The study classified events into VAEs, ventilator-associated condition (VAC) only, infection-related ventilator-associated complication (IVAC) only, and possible ventilator-associated pneumonia (PVAP) based on specific criteria. Detailed definitions and classification criteria are provided in the Supplemental Table 2.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality within 30 days of ICU admission. Secondary outcomes included ICU mortality, survival at discharge, and successful weaning from mechanical ventilation within 30 days.

Confounders

Confounders were selected based on previous research [11, 23–26]; baseline confounders recorded on ICU admission day 1 included age, sex, BMI, primary diagnosis, chronic diseases, coma status, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Time-varying confounders recorded daily included mechanical ventilation use, fluid volume, prone positioning, sedative use, vasopressor use, RASS score, SOFA score, antibacterial therapy, extracorporeal ECMO use, CRRT use, and tracheostomy. We included RASS scores as time-varying covariates specifically to account for sedation levels, with participating ICUs generally following the Japanese Society of Respiratory Care Medicine (JSRCM) guidelines for ventilation sedation [27]. Detailed operational definitions of time-dependent confounders (fluid volume, sedative use, and vasopressor use) are provided in Supplement Materials 2. For missing data, BMI values were imputed using admission means, and RASS scores using daily means. Sex was not imputed. Missing rates for confounders are shown in Supplemental Table 3.

Statistical methods

Baseline ICU admission characteristics were compared between patients with and without VAEs. Categorical variables were presented as n (%) and continuous variables as median interquartile range [IQR]. The incidence rates of VAEs and primary and secondary outcomes before and after VAEs were calculated using the person-time method. As the VAE exposure is time-varying, we used Cox models with VAEs as a time-varying variable to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). First, we adjusted for baseline confounders (model 1) and both baseline and time-varying confounders (model 2). As time-varying confounders may be affected by the previous exposure status, we applied inverse probability (IP)-weighted Cox models to account for time-varying confounding [28]. Detailed statistical methodology is provided in the Supplemental Materials 3. Population attributable risk fraction (PARP) was calculated as P*(RR − 1)/[P*(RR − 1) + 1], where P represents the exposure proportion and RR represents the risk ratio [29]. PARP estimates the theoretical reduction in mortality if VAEs were completely prevented. All statistical analyses were performed with R software version 4.3.2.

Subgroup analysis

We conducted subgroup analyses to evaluate the true clinical impact of preventable VAEs. First, medically futile cases were excluded, as withdrawal of life-sustaining treatments is often restricted in Japan, causing inevitable VAEs during end-of-life care. Patients receiving futile care (labeled by investigators) were excluded and outcomes reassessed. Second, to assess coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic influence, patients were stratified by COVID-19 diagnosis status. For both analyses, VAE incidence and association with in-hospital mortality were evaluated using IP-weighted Cox models adjusting for the same baseline and time-varying confounders as in the primary analysis.

Results

Description of the population

Between May 2020 and December 2022, the database recorded 1,099 ICU admissions. Of these, we excluded 5 admissions due to missing documentation of the VAE occurrence. The remaining 1,094 ICU admissions were eligible for the current analysis. There were 106 VAEs (9.7% of eligible admissions), including 41 cases of IVAC only (38.7% of VAEs) and 20 cases of PVAP (18.9% of VAEs). Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics for the overall cohort and for patients with and without VAEs. The median patient age was 69 years (IQR 56–77 years), and 715 patients (68%) were men. Emergency admission accounted for 55% of cases, and pneumonia was the most frequent primary diagnosis (21%). Postoperative patients comprised one-third of cases. Seventy percent of patients had at least one comorbidity. The median SOFA score on admission was 8 (IQR 6–11). The overall in-hospital mortality was 27%, 30-day in-hospital mortality was 18%, and ICU mortality was 12%. Comparing patients with VAEs and non-VAEs groups, the proportion of men was significantly higher in the VAEs group (77% vs. 67%). The prevalence of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) was also significantly higher in the VAEs group (29% vs 19%). Mortalities were significantly higher in the VAEs group (in-hospital mortality, 58% vs 23%; ICU mortality, 36% vs 9%). The characteristics of VAEs subgroups (VAC only, IVAC only, and PVAP) are presented in Supplemental Table 4. Detailed information on hospital characteristics and drug administration for VAEs versus non-VAEs groups and VAEs subgroups (VAC only, IVAC only, and PVAP) is presented in Supplemental Tables 5 and 6. The cumulative incidence of VAEs is illustrated using Kaplan–Meier curves in Fig. 1. The incidence rates per 1,000 ventilator days were as follows: VAEs, 10.0; VAC only, 6.1; IVAC only, 2.0; and PVAP, 1.9.

Association between VAEs and outcomes

The results of Model 1 (adjusted for baseline confounders only) and Model 2 (adjusted for both baseline and time-varying confounders) are shown in Supplemental Table 7. For in-hospital mortality, the hazard ratios in VAEs group as compared to non-VAEs group were 4.14 (95% CI 2.89–5.93) in Model 1 and 1.57 (95% CI 1.06–2.32) in Model 2. The IP-weighted Cox estimates shown in Fig. 2 indicate that VAEs group was associated with significantly higher 30-day in-hospital mortality (HR 2.00, 95% CI 1.23–3.26). For secondary outcomes, VAE incidence was associated with ICU mortality (HR 1.92, 95% CI 1.03–3.57). VAEs were also associated with longer hospital and ICU length of stay (HR 0.72, 95% CI 0.54–0.97, and HR 0.47, 95% CI 0.23–0.96). VAEs were not significantly associated with the likelihood of weaning from mechanical ventilation (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.66–1.05). The PARP was 8.83% (95% CI 2.18–17.96) and 8.18% (95% CI 0.29–19.94), for the 30-day in-hospital and ICU mortality, respectively.

Figure 3 illustrates the associations between VAEs subtypes (VAC only, IVAC only, and PVAP) and outcomes compared with the non-VAEs group. PVAP consistently showed the strongest association with adverse outcomes, particularly for 30-day in-hospital mortality (HR 3.62, 95% CI 1.82–7.20) and ICU mortality (HR 7.12, 95% CI 3.42–14.8). VAC only also demonstrated significant associations with these outcomes, while IVAC only showed less consistent results. All subtypes were associated with survival at hospital discharge, with IVAC only showing the strongest effect (HR 0.40, 95% CI 0.24–0.68). For survival at ICU discharge, all subtypes showed negative associations, although confidence intervals included the null value. Weaning from mechanical ventilation was most strongly impacted by IVAC only (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.42–0.85), while VAC only and PVAP showed non-significant associations. Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis of non-futile cases (Fig. 4 and Supplemental Table 8) showed that the association between VAEs and mortality was no longer significant after excluding futile cases. However, VAEs remained associated with lower survival at hospital discharge, lower survival at ICU discharge, and delayed weaning from mechanical ventilation. In COVID-19 patients (n = 205), VAE incidence was higher (13.2%) but was not associated with mortality risk (HR 1.81, 95% CI 0.63–5.21), while non-COVID-19 patients (n = 889) had lower VAE incidence (8.9%) but were associated with higher mortality risk (HR 2.60, 95% CI 1.41–4.61). Discussion

The diagnosis of VAE was associated with an increased risk of death in mechanically ventilated ICU patients, even after adjustment for time-dependent confounders. Associations between VAEs and increased risk of prolonged ICU and hospital stay were also confirmed.

The association between VAEs and 30-day mortality in our study strongly supports the findings of previous studies [7–15, 30]. However, previous studies on VAEs have varied in primary objective, study population (e.g., minimum duration of mechanical ventilation), number of study sites, and analytical methods. Similar to our study, two previous studies focused on patients with more than 3 days of mechanical ventilation, employed a multivariate regression analysis, and used the HR for death as a risk indicator [12, 14]. These studies demonstrated a clear correlation between VAEs and mortality, and the HRs were comparable to our results (Meagher’s in-hospital mortality HR: 2.83 [12]; Fang’s 90-day mortality HR: 2.45–2.64 [14]). Meanwhile, to our knowledge, our study represents the first report on VAEs that adjusts for longitudinal information about patient medical conditions by continuously monitoring disease severity and clinical practices during ICU stay. Critically ill patients may experience rapid deterioration or recovery of organ function, both of which can significantly affect the risk of developing VAEs and the prognosis over time. In fact, severely ill patients are at high risk of respiratory compromise owing to various factors in their clinical course [31]. Therefore, we considered it essential to incorporate the progression of disease severity before VAE onset into our analysis [32].

In contrast to some ICU complications (e.g., delirium), which have overturned the results of previous observational studies when results were reanalyzed with adjustment for time-dependent confounders [33], one potential reason for our robustness of the VAE–mortality relationship lies in the definition of VAE itself, which is designed to capture only severe impairment in oxygenation by incorporating certain thresholds [34]. Supporting this, the association between VAEs and mortality was not observed when thresholds lower than those defined by the CDC were applied [35]. Indeed, patients without gas exchange impairment have been found to follow a relatively benign clinical course [36], and sustained deterioration in oxygenation is an independent risk factor for mortality in mechanically ventilated patients [37].

The increased mortality associated with VAEs can be interpreted as evidence that the VAE criteria effectively identify severely ill patients with worsened oxygenation [34]. However, the rapid progression of the underlying pulmonary disease may be mistakenly diagnosed as a VAE because the VAE criteria rely solely on mechanical ventilator settings. This may strengthen the VAE-mortality relationship. For example, VAEs occurring in ARDS, which accounted for a significant proportion of the VAEs in this study, may reflect lung injury and deterioration in oxygenation caused by secondary insults, such as VAP or ventilator-induced lung injury [38]. Alternatively, these events may reflect the progression of ARDS per se. Although it remains difficult to clearly distinguish between these scenarios, VAEs are not merely markers of severe disease progression. This is because only patients whose condition worsens after a period of stability or improvement under mechanical ventilation are eligible for the VAE definition [39, 40]. Furthermore, the association between VAE and mortality observed in our study was identified after adjusting for a comprehensive set of time-varying markers, including severity of illness, age, sex, comorbidities, medications (e.g., vasopressors, sedatives, and antibiotics), fluid volume, CRRT, duration of mechanical ventilation prior to event onset, and other clinical practices and settings. Our findings demonstrate a robust association, but they do not definitively prove causality or quantify the proportion of VAEs that could be prevented. We recognize the importance of conducting prospective interventional studies, which are crucial for validating VAEs as a quality indicator and definitively assessing the preventability of VAEs.

It is also notable that the increased mortality risk associated with VAEs was confirmed in a country with different health service delivery models than those in the United States. VAEs are defined by specific mechanical ventilation parameters. Therefore, mechanical ventilation management can affect the diagnosis of VAE and its associations with outcomes. The results of this study, however, indicate that VAEs are associated with worse patient outcomes despite the differences in respiratory management resources (fewer intensivists, nurse practitioners, and no respiratory therapists in Japan) and practices [41–46] may indicate the robustness of the VAE-outcome relationship. Further research is needed to validate the association between the outcomes of VAEs across different respiratory care delivery models.

The PARP of VAEs in this study was 8.8% for in-hospital death. Previous studies reported lower VAP-PARP [29, 32]. In contrast, two studies with large cohorts estimated the PARP for ARDS alone to be 16–18% and 14–19%, respectively [47, 48]. Taken together, the attributable mortality of VAEs probably depends on the disease underlying their diagnosis.

In our subgroup analysis limited to non-futile cases (Fig. 4), the significant association between VAEs and mortality identified in the primary analysis was no longer observed. However, VAE occurrence remained significantly associated with lower ICU and hospital discharge survival rates and delayed ventilator weaning. These findings suggest that, in terminally ill patients, VAEs might reflect inevitable respiratory deterioration rather than preventable complications. Such “futile VAEs” may therefore obscure the true relationship between VAE incidence and mortality, challenging the use of overall VAE rates as a universal quality-of-care indicator, particularly in ICUs caring for many end-of-life patients. However, since the “futile care” subgroup does not represent a consistently defined population across the study sites, the findings from the “futile care” subgroup analysis should be interpreted with caution.

Limitations

The findings of this study need to be interpreted with caution. First, this was an observational study with moderate sample size, and we cannot rule out the possibility that unobserved confoundings were present (e.g., early mobilization, home ventilator use, lack of standardized protocol for ventilator management, daily staffing levels, patient goal of care), even after accounting for a relatively large number of covariables. Reliable estimation of attributable mortality is partially limited (if not impossible) for accurate assessment in observational studies [49]. Second, confounding factors associated with non-pulmonary complications have not been corrected. However, we adjusted for changes in SOFA score over time, including other organ functions. Third, as the study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, approximately 20% of the subjects were COVID-19-positive patients. The timing of the study and inclusion of COVID-19 patients with their higher VAE incidence [50, 51] may affect result generalizability. Our subgroup analyses, however, revealed different VAE-mortality associations between COVID-19 and non-COVID-19 patients, with significant association found only in the non-COVID-19 cohort. These findings suggest that the inclusion of COVID-19 patients did not artificially inflate the overall association between VAEs and mortality observed in our study population. Fourth, acceptance of medical futility is diverse among the countries [52]. As our subgroup analysis suggests, VAE rates in ICUs with many medically futile cases may poorly reflect care quality, as these events often represent inevitable end-of-life deterioration rather than preventable complications. Fifth, our data on dedicated ICU physicians and ICU beds-to-nurse ratios represented site-level characteristics of participating ICUs rather than patient-level, time-varying covariates. These time-fixed, site-level staffing characteristics could not be incorporated into our MSM analysis in the same way as patient-level time-varying confounders. Therefore, we could not adjust for potential confounding by daily staffing variations or fully account for baseline site-level staffing differences in our primary analysis. Finally, the database for the reimbursement policy may have generated biased data for the same patients included in clinical databases.

Conclusion

This study indicated the association between VAEs and mortality after adjustment for severity as a time-dependent confounder.
