Thermomechanical Behavior of Biobased Benzoxazine: Structure–Property Relationship and Role of Aromatic Side Groups
Authors: Mohammad Fahad Zaki Khan, Gideon Abels, Andreas Hartwig, Katharina Koschek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biobased benzoxazine monomers derived from sesamol and monofunctional amines with aromatic side groups were synthesized to study the influence of aromatic side groups on the thermomechanical properties. Furfurylamine is a typical component for biobased benzoxazines and is known to influence the thermomechanical properties of the benzoxazine polymers by increasing the cross-linking density. In this study, the effect of amines similar to furfurylamine on the thermomechanical properties has been investigated. Differential scanning calorimetry analysis was used to analyze the impact of the aromatic side groups on the melting temperature of the monomers, with the pyridine side group having the lowest melting point and onset temperature. All monomers showed cross-linking capabilities to various degrees. Thermogravimetric analysis revealed the thermal stability of the polymers up to 260 °C with less than 5% weight loss and a good char yield of at least 44%. Microcombustion calorimetry data revealed that the peak and total heat release rates decreased with different aromatic side groups, with sesamol furfurylamine-based benzoxazine exhibiting the lowest values. Dynamic mechanical analysis results showed that the furfurylamine and aniline-based polymers exhibited high glass transition temperatures of 220 and 180 °C, respectively. In contrast, the benzylamine-based polymer was mechanically weak and the pyridine-based polymer was extremely brittle, preventing mechanical measurements for these systems. All in all, this study presents various alternatives for furfurylamine, offering promising cross-linking capabilities and subsequent thermomechanical properties.

Introduction

Benzoxazine resins are high-performance thermosets exhibiting excellent thermomechanical properties. Molecular tunability enables structural modifications for property optimization. At the same time, this allows for the choice of renewable starting materials and efficient manufacturing, thereby improving sustainability.

However, biobased benzoxazine alternatives demonstrate lower thermomechanical properties compared to petroleum-derived counterparts. Bulky alkyl chains or labile substituents diminish cross-linking density, reducing glass transition temperature (T g), mechanical strength, thermal stability, and flame-retardant properties. Integration of aromatic structural elements elevates the benzoxazine resin performance. Comparative analyses in the research of Liu et al. and Haubold et al. confirm that aromatic structuresexemplified in bisphenol A/furfurylamine and linear diamine systemssignificantly enhance thermal stability, T g, and storage modulus. Given the stringent application requirements for superior thermomechanical behavior, strategic aromatic functionalization represents a vital pathway to optimize biobased benzoxazine performance while addressing current limitations.

While biobased alternatives are promising, there is a need to explore suitable amines to enhance their properties. Several aromatic amines, such as aminophenol, cyclohexylamine, and diaminophenylmethane, can modify the chemical structure of benzoxazine to improve its aromatic content and thermomechanical properties. However, these amines are not derived from biobased sources. Currently, only a few biobased amines have been identified for developing benzoxazines, with furfurylamine being the most common, synthesized from biobased sources via reductive amination of furfural. , Other amines, such as benzylamine, can be produced either through the reductive amination of benzaldehyde or extracted from the leaves and flowers of the plant Reseda media. Furthermore, amines traditionally derived from nonrenewable sources, such as aniline and 3-aminopyridine, can now be derived from renewable alternatives. A patent by Gernot and Jorgen claims that aniline can be produced from biobased o-aminobenzoate. , Additionally, Covestro has demonstrated a method for deriving aniline from sugar. Similarly, 3-aminopyridine can be synthesized from pyridine, which can also be obtained from biobased sources. , Despite the promising potential of these biobased amines with aromatic side groups, the impact of these aromatic side groups and cross-linking abilities on the benzoxazine properties has not been comprehensively studied.

In addition to amines, phenolic compounds also play a significant role in the synthesis of biobased benzoxazine monomers. Compounds such as cardanol, − phloretic acid, , guaiacol, , eugenol, , urushiol, thymol, coumarin, resveratrol, and sesamol have been utilized to synthesize biobased benzoxazine monomers. Recently, studies have shown that sesamol can be used to synthesize biobenzoxazine with excellent properties. Salum et al. synthesized benzoxazine from sesamol and furfurylamine under environmentally friendly conditions, adhering to the principles of green chemistry. I Necolau et al. synthesized a sesamol-polyethylenimine-based benzoxazine and compared its physicochemical and thermomechanical properties with various aliphatic diamine linkers. These studies highlight sesamol as a promising choice for benzoxazine development.

In the context of improving the performance of biobased benzoxazines, it is important to note that these materials generally exhibit lower thermomechanical properties. However, recent studies have shown that biobenzoxazines synthesized from furfurylamine demonstrated improved thermomechanical performance. The furan group can undergo cross-linking reactions during the polymerization, significantly improving the thermomechanical properties of the obtained polymer. Other biobased amines with aromatic side groups, such as benzylamine, aniline, and 3-aminopyridine, share a similar chemical structure and potential cross-linking ability with furfurylamine. Therefore, it is of scientific interest to investigate structurally related biobased amines to furfurylamine and study their cross-linking ability and the impact on the properties of the benzoxazine monomer and polymer. Benzoxazines derived from amines that not only possess electron-rich aromatic rings but can also actively participate in improving the cross-linking density during curing, similar to furfurylamine, will form polymers with enhanced thermomechanical properties.

This study investigates biobased benzoxazines synthesized from sesamol with various amines, considering furfurylamine, benzylamine, aniline, and 3-aminopyridine, to assess how different aromatic substituents affect benzoxazine monomer and polymer performance. Monofunctional benzoxazines were chosen to directly link thermomechanical properties to specific amine groups using furfurylamine-based benzoxazine as reference.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of Biobenzoxazine with Oxazine Ring Substitution

Four benzoxazine monomers were synthesized via a one-pot Mannich condensation using sesamol as the phenolic component and one of the selected primary amines: furfurylamine, aniline, benzylamine, or 3-aminopyridine (Scheme ). The resulting monomers are designated as S-fa (sesamol-furfurylamine-based benzoxazine), S-an (sesamol-aniline-based benzoxazine), S-ba (sesamol-benzylamine-based benzoxazine), and S-ap (sesamol-aminopyridine-based benzoxazine). The chemical structures were confirmed using 1H NMR, 13C NMR, FT-IR, and HR-MS.

The 1H NMR spectra of S-fa, S-ba, S-an, and S-ap are shown in Figure . For the S-fa monomer, the Ar–CH2–N and N–CH2–furan signals overlapped at 3.81 ppm, indicating an integral value of four and the doublet multiplicity of the signal. The S-ba monomer exhibited two close peaks, each integrating two protons for the Ar–CH2–N and N–CH2–phenyl groups. In the S-an and S-ap monomers, the Ar–CH2–N signal shifted downfield from 3.90 to 4.53 ppm, while the O–CH2–N methylene signal shifted downfield from 4.74 to 5.35 ppm. These downfield shifts were attributed to the decreased electron density of the nonmethylene-bridged aromatic side groups. The methylene group in the O–CH2–O of the sesamol ring appeared at 5.90 ppm for all of the monomers except S-ap, which shifted slightly downfield at 6.00 ppm. The peaks from 6.30 to 7.60 ppm corresponded to the aromatic ring for all the monomers. Additionally, the C=CH–N and N=CH–C groups in the pyridine ring of the S-ap monomer appeared at 8.30 and 8.60 ppm, respectively, with shifts attributed to the electronegativity of nitrogen.

The chemical structures of S-fa, S-ba, S-an, and S-ap monomers were further confirmed by 13C NMR spectra (see Figures S1, S2, S3, and S4 in the Supporting Information). The signals for the N–CH2–furan/phenyl group in S-fa and S-ba monomers appeared at 47 and 54 ppm, respectively, with the upfield shift attributed to the electronegative effect of the phenyl group, producing a deshielding effect. The Ar–CH2–N group of the benzoxazine ring appeared around 48 ppm for all monomers. The O–CH2–N group appeared at 78 ppm for the S-an and S-ap, while for the S-fa and S-ba, the signal was shifted to 82 ppm due to the deshielding effect. The O–CH2–O group of the sesamol ring was observed at around 100 ppm for all monomers. The remaining peaks, from 97 to 152 ppm, corresponded to the aromatic region of the monomers.

FT-IR spectroscopy confirmed the monomer structure (Figure S5, see the Supporting Information), showing signals between 2950 cm–1 and 3150 cm–1 corresponding to the aromatic side groups in all monomers. Moreover, the characteristic peak of C=N of the pyridine ring was observed at 1666 cm–1, whereas the absorption at 1475 cm–1 was attributed to the distinctive methylenedioxybenzene group of the sesamol. The monomers showed characteristic benzoxazine bands at 1442 cm–1, 1398 cm–1, and 1192 cm–1, corresponding to CH2 bending, C–N–C asymmetric stretching, and H–C–O-C torsional motion in the oxazine ring, respectively. Overlapping symmetric and asymmetric C–O stretching modes of the sesamol moiety and benzoxazine ring were observed between 1060 cm–1 and 1020 cm–1, as well as between 960 cm–1 and 890 cm–1. The absorption at 869 cm–1 was assigned to the out-of-plane bond for tetrasubstituted benzene, indicating benzoxazine formation.

Impact of Side Groups on the Monomer Properties

Polymerization Behavior of Benzoxazine Monomers

The effect of the aromatic side group on the melting temperature (T m), onset temperature (T onset), polymerization peak temperature (T p), and molar enthalpy of polymerization (ΔH M) was investigated by using DSC. The thermograms showed sharp and symmetric endothermic and exothermic peaks (Figure ). The endothermic peaks observed between 60 and 120 °C were assigned to the T m, which decreased for S-fa, S-ba, S-an, and S-ap, with S-fa exhibiting the highest and S-ap the lowest T m (Table ). The S-fa monomer contains a furan group attached to the methylene bridge, resulting in a flexible and semirigid structure that promotes efficient molecular packing. Its geometry facilitates stronger π-π stacking interactions, contributing to efficient molecular packing and high crystallinity. In contrast, S-ap monomer has a pyridine side group that constitutes nitrogen in the aromatic ring. The lone pair on the pyridine nitrogen can induce repulsive interactions, disrupting orderly packing in the solid state. As a result, the S-ap exhibits reduced crystallinity with a lower T m. This difference is also reflected in their physical states; S-fa is a highly crystalline solid at room temperature, whereas S-ap remains a liquid, solidifying gradually over time.

The polymerization onset temperature for all monomers was within the range of 200 to 250 °C. S-ba exhibited the highest T onset of 233 °C, while S-ap and S-fa had the lowest T p of 213 and 218 °C, respectively. This trend was also reflected in the exothermic peak maximum temperature T p, where S-ba had the highest T p of 241 °C, compared to S-ap and S-fa, which had T p values of 230 and 229 °C, respectively. However, the accuracy of the polymerization peaks may be affected by the overlap with degradation peaks, as indicated by the TGA data (Table ).

The sharpness of the T m and T p peaks indicates higher crystallinity. Generally, a sharp and narrow peak in a DSC thermogram reflects a highly ordered and uniform crystal size, resulting in melting over a narrow temperature range. Conversely, a less ordered crystalline structure with varying crystal size contains a certain amount of smaller crystals, which melt at comparatively lower temperatures. This leads to peak broadening. The order of crystallinity and uniformity of crystal sizes appear to gradually decrease in the order of S-fa, S-ba, S-an, and S-ap, as shown in Figure . DSC peak profiles further support this observation, indicating that the S-fa monomer exhibits the most homogeneous crystalline population, while the broad peak in S-ap suggests a more heterogeneous distribution of crystal sizes. Additionally, the extra methylene group in S-fa and S-ba enhances side group rotation, improving crystallization and resulting in higher melting points compared to S-an and S-ap.

The results of the modified Ozawa and Kissinger method showed that the side groups affected the E a of polymerization. All of the polymers were highly pure, suggesting that polymerization initiation resulted from the self-initiation of the benzoxazine ring rather than from impurities. The findings revealed that the S-ap and S-fa monomers underwent ring opening with a minimum energy. On the other hand, the S-ba and S-an monomers required the highest amount of energy for the ring-opening polymerization. The lower E a for S-ap can be attributed to the electron-withdrawing nitrogen in the pyridine ring, which enhances reactivity. The ring-opening process breaks the oxazine ring C–O bond, forming an oxyanion and a carbocation intermediate. The lone pair on the pyridine group stabilizes the carbocation, while the delocalization stabilizes the oxyanion. These effects lower the transition state energy, thereby reducing the activation energy for the S-ap monomer. In contrast, the oxygen atom in the furan ring donates electron density to the aromatic system via lone-pair delocalization. This electron-donating character diminishes stabilization of the cationic reactive intermediate, resulting in a slightly higher activation energy for polymerization relative to that of S-ap. Conversely, S-an and S-ba polymerizations exhibit higher activation energies due to reduced stabilization of the cationic intermediate. The activation energy trend closely parallels the T onset of the corresponding monomers.

TGA was used to assess the thermal stability of the monomers under a nitrogen atmosphere (Table and Figure S8). All studied monomers were stable up to 140 °C, with initial degradation temperatures at 5% weight loss (T 5%) occurring between 167 and 177 °C. S-fa and S-ba exhibited a T 5% around 168 °C, while S-an and S-ap were more stable up to T 5% of approximately 176 °C. The monomer char yields of S-fa, S-an, and S-ba were similar at 10.5% and 14%, respectively, but S-ap showed an exceptionally high char yield of 37%. This can be attributed to the two nitrogen atoms in the pyridine group of the S-ap monomer. These nitrogen atoms promote the formation of heteroaromatic networks during pyrolysis, reducing volatilization and favoring carbon residue, resulting in higher char yield.

Effect of the Side Group on the Thermomechanical Properties of the Polymer

Study of Gel Content

Based on the DSC and TGA data in Table , the monomers were cured at 160 and 170 °C for 2 h each, followed by a temperature increase to 180 °C for 4 h to prepare polymer samples for further analysis. The completion of polymerization was confirmed by DSC.

In order to determine and compare the cross-linking capabilities of the different aromatic side groups, the gel content of the polymerized monobenzoxazines was determined. For this, the Soxhlet extraction method was performed over 24 h with acetone as solvent to dissolve all non-cross-linked parts of the polymer, like monomers or linear chains, leaving behind only the insoluble 3D network. The gel content was calculated using eq , where the weight of the sample after extraction (m network) was divided by the sample’s initial weight (m 0). gelcontent(%)=mnetworkm0×100 3

Poly­(S-ba) exhibits the lowest gel content of 52%, whereas poly­(S-an), poly­(S-fa), and especially poly­(S-ap) show higher values of 85%, 89%, and 96%, respectively (Table ). The exceptional cross-linking of S-ap can most likely be attributed to the two heteroatomsone embedded within the ring and one directly attachedenhancing the ring carbon atom reactivity. In contrast, S-fa and S-an contain a single heteroatom within or directly attached to the aromatic ring, resulting in moderately lower cross-linking reactivity. Benzylamine, with its amine group separated from the ring by a methylene group, is not conjugated to the aromatic system and therefore cross-links much less than the other monomers.

In S-fa, the benzylic methylene linking the amine to the furan ring imparts additional conformational freedom, facilitating favorable orientations for cross-linking reactions and yielding a higher gel content compared to poly­(S-an). These findings are consistent with the data in Table and the overall trends. Gel content analysis indicates that the incorporation of a heteroatom within the aromatic ring or directly attached to the side group increases the cross-linking density, as observed for poly­(S-fa), poly­(S-an), and poly­(S-ap). In contrast, poly­(S-ba) contains an additional methylene group and lacks a heteroatom, diminishing ring activation and reactivity and resulting in a lower gel content. Effect of Side Groups on the Thermal Stability and Flammability of the Benzoxazine Polymer

TGA was performed to investigate the thermal stability of poly­(S-fa), poly­(S-ba), poly­(S-an), and poly­(S-ap) polymers in a nitrogen atmosphere. The thermal degradation of polybenzoxazine typically occurs in a three-step process. The first stage consists of the degradation or evaporation of the amine from the branches and chain ends. The second stage involves the evaporation of amine from the main chain, followed by the cleavage of the phenolic linkage and degradation of the Mannich base in the last step. The thermograms were segmented into three stages (Figure ). In the first region between room temperature and 250 °C, the polybenzoxazines exhibit ∼5% mass loss, which is consistent with volatilization of residual amines and labile polymer end groups. The main decomposition event happens in the second region between 250 and 600 °C. All polymers showed similar mass loss behavior except for poly­(S-ba), which was significantly less stable due to the evaporation of amines from the main chain of the polymer. The third >600 °C represents the high-temperature char formation with slower degradation involving cleavage of the phenolic linkage and degradation of the Mannich bases. Poly­(S-ba) exhibits the highest mass loss of all studied polymers in both the intermediate (250–600 °C) and high-temperature regions (>600 °C). This behavior is consistent with its low gel content (Table ), indicating a higher ratio of non-cross-linked, volatile species. In contrast, poly­(S-fa), poly­(S-an), and poly­(S-ap) show comparable and higher thermal stability, reflected in elevated T 5%, T 10%, and T 15% values and char yields of 60–70%. The TGA results correlate with the gel content data; a higher cross-linking density suppresses early degradation and increases the level of char formation. Consequently, poly­(S-ba) forms a less thermally stable network due to insufficient cross-linking (Figure ).

Combustion behavior was assessed by microcombustion calorimetry (MCC), which reports the peak heat release rate (P HRR), the temperature at P HRR (T PHRR), and the total heat released (THR). Char yield was determined gravimetrically from the sample masses before and after testing.

As summarized in Table , poly­(S-fa) exhibits a P HRR value approximately 8-fold lower than poly­(S-ba), and a T PHRR that is higher by 222 °C. Poly­(S-an) and poly­(S-ap) show P HRR at least 1.5 times higher than poly­(S-fa) and T PHRR at least 239 °C lower. Their THR values are nearly identical to each other and at least 1.5 times higher than those of poly­(S-fa), which itself has a THR value at least four times lower than poly­(S-ba). The char yields are broadly similar across the polymer series except for poly­(S-ba), which has the lowest char yield of 44%. Overall, the MCC data indicate superior fire performance for poly­(S-fa)lower P HRR and THR with delayed heat releasewhereas poly­(S-ba) is the least thermally robust, consistent with its lower char yield. Gel content analysis (Table ) ranks the networks as follows: poly­(S-ap) > poly­(S-fa) > poly­(S-an) > poly­(S-ba). Accordingly, the MCC response is strongly modulated by cross-link density, particularly for poly­(S-ap), poly­(S-an), and poly­(S-ba). Poly­(S-ba) undergoes more complete combustion with earlier heat release (lower T PHRR) and the lowest char yield, consistent with its low gel content due to reduced cross-linking and the presence of a benzylic methylene, which increases fuel content and promotes scission. Thus, the combustion behavior of the benzyl side group reflects the combined effects of benzylic CH2 and insufficient cross-link density. Poly­(S-ap) exhibits the highest gel content and correspondingly lower P HRR and THR values compared to poly­(S-an) and poly­(S-ba). Its reduced P HRR relative to poly­(S-an) was attributed to the pyridine ring nitrogen atom, which can enhance flame retardancy at elevated temperatures (e.g., via char promotion and radical quenching). Poly­(S-ba) shows the highest P HRR, consistent with its low gel content and the benzylic methylene group, which increases the fuel content and facilitates scission.

Study of Thermomechanical Properties and Cross-Linking Density

DMA was performed to investigate the thermomechanical properties of the polymers. Poly­(S-ba) and poly­(S-ap) could not yield intact DMA specimens. Poly­(S-ba), with the lowest gel content, formed an under-cross-linked network and consequently showed poor mechanical integrity and fractured on demolding. In contrast, poly­(S-ap) displayed the highest gel content and formed a highly rigid network. This excessive cross-linking led to pronounced brittleness and microcracking during the molding process, likely due to internal stresses generated upon curing. These contrasting behaviors highlight the strong influence of aromatic groups on network architecture and mechanical performance in sesamol based polybenzoxazines. Attempts to adjust the curing temperature and pressure did not resolve the brittleness or mechanical weakness.

The storage modulus (E′) and loss modulus (E″) of poly­(S-fa) and poly­(S-an) were determined by using DMA at room temperature (Figure ). The glass transition temperature (T g) was assessed from the peak of the tan δ curve. At 25 °C, E′ is 3873 MPa for poly­(S-fa) and 198 MPa for poly­(S-an), making poly­(S-fa) ∼19 times stiffer. This difference reflects two concurrent effects: the furan moiety in poly­(S-fa) promotes additional cross-linking and an increase in network density, whereas curing-induced degradation in poly­(S-an) introduces porosity that lowers the stiffness. The T g values are high for both systemswith poly­(S-fa) around 220 °C and poly­(S-an) at 180 °Cand correlate with their gel contents (>85%), consistent with restricted chain mobility in covalently cross-linked networks (Table ).

The cross-linking density of poly­(S-an) was found to be approximately 543 mol·m–3, whereas for poly­(S-fa), it was 36,805 mol·m–3. These values align with the higher E′ and T g values of poly­(S-fa) and confirm the strong contribution of the furan ring to network formation.

Aniline-derived polybenzoxazine showed a lower cross-linking density, indicating more linear segments and greater chain mobility. This is reflected in a pronounced drop in the storage modulus consistent with an increased viscoelastic response relative to furfuryl amine-based benzoxazine. In contrast, the furan-derived polybenzoxazine (poly­(S-fa)) maintains a higher E′ with temperature and exhibits a larger separation between storage and loss moduli (E′–E″), indicating a more solid-like behavior. These differences arise from the substantially higher cross-link density in poly­(S-fa), which restricts segmental motion and enhances thermomechanical stability.

Effect of the Amine-Derived Side Group on Network Formation

Polybenzoxazine network formation mainly proceeds via two curing pathways, as previously reported. , Scheme illustrates the network-formation mechanism in the context of sesamol-based benzoxazines derived from different amines with aromatic side groups. Initially, upon heating, the benzoxazine ring undergoes heterolytic cleavage of the O–CH2 –N, triggering ring opening and forming an ionic pair consisting of a phenolate anion and an iminium cation. From this point, two distinct reaction pathways are possible. In one pathway, the nucleophilic attack of the phenolate oxygen on the iminium species yields a phenoxy-type structure. Alternatively, the iminium ion may react via electrophilic aromatic substitution at the ortho position of the phenolic ring, resulting in a phenolic benzoxazine–amine structure.

(Top) Curing Mechanism of Sesamol-Amine-Based Benzoxazine Showing Phenolic and Phenoxy Bridge Formation. (Bottom) Proposed Cross-Linked Structure of Polybenzoxazine with Different Aromatic Side Groups

The nature of the aromatic side group plays a decisive role in directing the polymerization mechanism by modulating the electronic effects, reactivity, and network architecture. Furan substituents, being electron-rich and heteroaromatic, enhance ring-opening polymerization by stabilizing cationic intermediates such as iminium species and can additionally participate in secondary reactions (e.g., electrophilic substitution or thermally induced cross-linking), thereby increasing the cross-link density. Benzene rings provide a comparatively neutral aromatic environment; they primarily act as rigid structural units and favor classical benzoxazine curing through electrophilic aromatic substitution at the ortho position, contributing to thermally stable, highly cross-linked phenolic networks. In contrast, pyridine moieties introduce an electron-deficient heteroaromatic character that suppresses electrophilic substitution on the pyridine ring, resulting in N-centered cross-links (−CH2–N–CH2−), which may rearrange into phenolic bridges on the activated phenolic ring, thereby increasing 3D network formation. Finally, the benzyl side group has a flexible methylene spacer, which can increase segmental mobility and reduce steric constraints, resulting in enhanced toughness compared to directly attached aromatic substituents. These mechanistic distinctions are consistent with and supported by the experimental data presented in this work.

Conclusions

In this study, four monofunctional biobased benzoxazine monomers with different aromatic side groups were successfully synthesized. The investigation revealed that among them, furan-, benzene-, and pyridine-based polybenzoxazines showed high gel contents, good thermal stabilities, high char yields, and low P HRR and THR values, whereas the benzylamine based polymer showed low gel content and comparatively lower thermomechanical and fire performance than its counterparts. The furan-based polybenzoxazine showed postcuring effects, indicating an increase in cross-linking density upon thermal treatment, as evidenced by the two HRR peaks. In terms of mechanical performance, the furan-based polybenzoxazine had a 19 times higher storage modulus than the benzene-based polybenzoxazine. However, both exhibited a high glass transition temperature. DMA measurements could not be performed for the benzylamine- and pyridine-based polybenzoxazines due to their mechanical weakness and extreme brittleness, respectively. Overall, this study elucidates the structure–property relationships of the benzoxazine monomers and their resulting polymers, providing a framework for tailoring and improving their thermomechanical performance in future molecular designs.

General Procedure for the Polymerization of Benzoxazines

Benzoxazine monomers were melted and degassed under vacuum at 100 °C for 10 min. The resulting melt was transferred to a metal mold and cured with a hot press. The curing protocol consisted of heating the mold at 160 °C for 2 h, followed by incremental temperature increases to 170 and 180 °C, each maintained for 2 and 4 h, respectively. A constant pressure of 30 bar was applied to ensure the formation of pore-free samples. The completion of polymerization was confirmed using DSC. The resulting polymers were brittle and black in color.

Characterization Methods

1H NMR spectra were acquired on an AVANCE NEO NMR-spectrometer (Bruker, Massachusetts) at a proton resonance frequency of 600 MHz at 25 °C. 13C NMR spectra were acquired at a carbon resonance frequency of 150 MHz by using the same spectrometer. Experiments were performed in DMSO-d 6 using the protonated solvent as a reference signal, and the chemical shifts were reported in parts per million (ppm).

FT-IR spectra were recorded on an Equinox 55 ATR spectrometer (Bruker, Bremen, Germany) in attenuated total reflection (ATR) mode using a diamond crystal with 32 scans from 650 cm–1 to 4000 cm–1 and a resolution of 4 cm–1.

High-resolution mass spectrometry (HR-MS) analysis was conducted using a Finnigan MAT 95XL mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany) with electron impact (EI) as the ionization source and an electron energy of 70 eV.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurements were performed using a Discovery DSC (TA Instruments, Hüllhorst, Germany) with 10 K/min in a range from 0 to 300 °C on a specimen scale of 1–2 mg under a N2 atmosphere. All samples were measured in 3-fold determination, and the average values were used for the evaluation.

TGA measurements were carried out with a Q5000 (TA Instruments, Hüllhorst, Germany) in a temperature range from 25 to 800 °C and a heating rate of 10 K/min under both ambient and nitrogen atmospheres with a sample size of 1–2 mg for monomer and polymer samples.

MCC measurements were performed on an FAA microcombustion calorimeter (Fire Testing Technology, East Grinstead, UK) according to ASTM D7309 in a temperature range from 75 to 1000 °C with a heating rate of 1 K/s. The resulting data was fitted using software from the same manufacturer to determine the peak heat release rate (P HRR), the temperature at P HRR (T PHRR), and the total heat release (THR). In addition, the onset of the first HRR maximum was defined as T i. Char yields were determined by weighing and comparing the sample masses before and after the measurement.

DMA experiments were performed using a Dynamic Mechanical Analyzer Q800 (TA Instruments, Hüllhorst, Germany). Thermomechanical properties were determined in a temperature range from 0 to 250 °C with a heating rate of 2 K/min and a frequency of 1 Hz in a single cantilever bending mode using a test specimen of 35 × 10 × 4 mm. The maximum loss modulus determines the glass transition temperature (T g).
