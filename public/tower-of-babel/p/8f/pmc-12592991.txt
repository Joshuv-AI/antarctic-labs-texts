Due to Limitations in the Feasibility of the Perme Score, it should not be Used to Classify Muscle Weakness in Intensive Care Unit Patients
Authors: Sinda Zarrouk, Josef Finsterer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
How to cite this article: Zarrouk S, Finsterer J. Due to Limitations in the Feasibility of the Perme Score, it should not be Used to Classify Muscle Weakness in Intensive Care Unit Patients. Indian J Crit Care Med 2025;29(10):882–883.

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the article by Delazari et al. on a prospective observational study at a single center that evaluated the association between the Perme score and intensive care unit-acquired weakness (ICUAW) in 97 patients undergoing prolonged mechanical ventilation (MV) using the handgrip strength test (HGST) as a diagnostic reference.1 Intensive care unit-acquired weakness was detected in 78.4% of patients, and low Perme scores were associated with a higher probability of ICUAW.1 In the multivariate analysis, the Perme score remained an independent predictor of ICUAW.1 The study is noteworthy, but some points should be discussed.

The first point is that the Perme score includes several exercises that cannot be performed on intubated and ventilated patients.2 These include item 9 (from lying to sitting), item 10 (static balance while sitting on the edge of the bed once the position has been assumed), item 11 (from sitting to standing), item 12 (static balance while standing once the standing position has been assumed), item 13 (transfer from bed to chair), item 14 (walking), and item 15 (endurance).2 Therefore, the Perme test is not an optimal tool for predicting muscle weakness in intensive care patients.

The second point is that it is not clear why muscle weakness was not assessed by clinical neurological examination. Neurologists have the most experience in assessing muscle weakness, atrophy, or fasciculations. They can also assess whether the muscle weakness has a cause in the central nervous system (CNS) or the peripheral nervous system (PNS). The neurologist can assess whether the patient has acquired critical neuropathy or myopathy, or whether the muscle weakness and wasting are due to a side effect of current medication or to immunological myositis, myasthenia, a paraneoplastic syndrome, sepsis, an endocrine disorder, a metabolic disorder, an infectious disease, or vasculitis.

The third point is that premortem muscle strength was not assessed.1 The only exclusion criteria were pre-existing neuromuscular diseases, limb amputation, GCS ≤8 or motor score <6, or inability to understand the HGST instructions.1 According to the “Methodology” section, patients with a history of stroke, hemorrhage, venous sinus thrombosis, encephalitis, meningitis, acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, or other CNS disorders associated with muscle weakness were not excluded. Causes of muscle weakness in the CNS can be easily overlooked in ventilated and sedated patients. Unless causes of muscle weakness in the CNS have been thoroughly ruled out, these patients should not be included in the study cohort.

The fourth point is that depression was not included in the assessment. Since grip strength depends not only on cognitive performance but also heavily on the patient's willingness to cooperate and mood, it would have been important to test all included patients using a widely used depression scale.3

In summary, the Perme score cannot be recommended as a means of predicting ICUAW, as it only detects manifest muscle weakness, does not distinguish between causes of muscle weakness in the CNS and PNS, and only a limited number of exercises required for the complete implementation of the score to be performed in ventilated patients.
