The association of depressive symptoms and dietary inflammatory index with frailty in older adults with diabetes
Authors: Xixi Li, Huanrui Zhang, Guoxian Qi, Baosen Zhou, Wen Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOlder adults with diabetes exhibit heightened risks of depression and frailty, with chronic inflammation proposed as a potential mechanism. This study investigates the combined association of depressive symptoms and pro-inflammatory diets with frailty.MethodsThis study utilized data from older adults with diabetes collected during the 2007–2018 cycles of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Dietary inflammatory potential was evaluated using the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII). Depressive symptoms were evaluated using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9. Participants were classified into six mutually exclusive groups: No depressive symptoms & Low-DII, No depressive symptoms & High-DII, Mild depressive symptoms & Low-DII, Mild depressive symptoms & High-DII, Moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms & Low-DII, and Moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms & High-DII. Frailty was defined by a modified 35-item Frailty Index. Weighted multivariable logistic regression evaluated individual/joint associations of DII and depressive symptoms with frailty. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses assessed robustness.ResultsA total of 2667 older adults with diabetes (weighted mean age 69.7 years, 53% male) were recruited. Elevated depressive symptoms score and higher DII independently increased frailty risk (adjusted OR per unit increase: depression OR = 1.16, 95% CI = 1.12–1.21; DII OR = 1.12, 95% CI = 1.02–1.23). Compared with reference group (No depressive symptoms & Low-DII), participants with Moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms & high DII exhibited the highest frailty risk (adjusted OR = 13.82, 95% CI = 7.67–24.92). The combined depressive symptoms-DII association with frailty was stronger in subgroups of males, older adults < 70, and non-Hispanic Whites.ConclusionAnti-inflammatory nutritional patterns and psychological well-being may jointly associate with lower frailty risk, supporting multidimensional care models to alleviate health burdens.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-07897-2.Keywords: Frailty, Diabetes, Depression, Dietary inflammatory index, Older adults

Introduction

With the rapid growth of population aging, the prevalence of diabetes among older adults is close to 20%. Advanced-age diabetes is characteristically associated with compromised quality of life, functional limitations, and heightened susceptibility to disability and premature death [1]. Frailty often co-occurs with diabetes. Previous research has indicated that diabetes is linked to a higher risk of frailty [2, 3]. Frailty represents a highly vulnerable state, characterized as a multidimensional syndrome featuring diminished physiological reserves and increased vulnerability to stressors, thereby raising the risk of adverse health events [4, 5]. The coexistence of frailty and diabetes contributes to accelerated functional decline, diminished quality of life, and elevated risks of disability and mortality in the older adults.

Depression is a prevalent condition in primary care settings, and is recognized as a chronic disorder. Clinical presentations often include physical complaints (e.g., fatigue, pain, insomnia), while core affective symptoms may involve depressed mood or marked loss of interest or pleasure in most activities [6]. The incidence of depression is significantly higher in patients with diabetes: approximately one in six individuals with diabetes develops depression; the incidence is twice that observed in individuals without diabetes; and clinical depression is common in this population [7]. Meanwhile, increasing evidence suggests that depression is associated with frailty. A meta-analysis—including 84,351 community-dwelling older adults—confirmed that older adults with depression are at increased risk of frailty. Specifically, older adults with depression are more likely to become frail than those without depression [8]. A study on the predictive role of depressive symptoms for frailty and its components in Chinese middle-aged and older adults found that depressive symptoms predicted frailty during both the 2-year and 4-year follow-up periods [9]. Moreover, studies have demonstrated that the co-occurrence of geriatric frailty and depression constitutes a high-risk comorbidity [9]. Moreover, studies have demonstrated that geriatric frailty combined with depression constitutes a high-risk comorbidity [10–12]. Compared with younger individuals, older adults with frailty and depression exhibit unique clinical manifestations, show reduced response to antidepressants, and face increased risks of chronic diseases, treatment resistance, and mortality [13]. Therefore, a better understanding of the association between depressive symptoms and frailty in older adults with diabetes, as well as their shared risk factors, is of critical importance.

Depression is known to co-occur with systemic low-grade inflammation (e.g., IL-6 dysregulation), which mechanistically intersects with frailty development [14, 15]. Chronic systemic inflammation is one of the candidate pathogenic factors contributing to both frailty and depression. Dietary inflammation has received increasing attention. As a modifiable factor, dietary patterns are integral to diabetes management. Long-term unhealthy diet can lead to a decline in dietary quality, which may create an inflammatory environment in the body, thereby providing conditions for the occurrence and development of various diseases, such as cardiovascular disorders and metabolic syndrome [16, 17], as well as a higher likelihood of various types of cancer [18, 19]. Currently, a validated index has been established to evaluate the dietary inflammatory potential, known as the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII). This index has been associated with inflammatory biomarkers and age-related conditions, including atherosclerosis, cardiovascular diseases, and cognitive impairment [20–23].

Previous studies have observed a significant association between DII and depression. Older adults with depression show a higher risk of dietary inflammation. The DII score of patients with depression is higher than that of participants without depression [24]. In another study, a strong correlation between the DII and depressive disorders persisted among patients with chronic diseases such as diabetes, hypertension, and coronary heart disease. Compared with participants in the lowest quintile of the DII, those in the highest quintile had approximately 2.11 times greater odds of depression among individuals with chronic disease [25]. Pro-inflammatory diets may contribute to depression pathogenesis by chronically activating the immune system, leading to low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress. These effects can disrupt tryptophan–kynurenine metabolism, dysregulate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, and potentially involve the gut–brain–microbiota axis [26]. Meanwhile, studies have reported that depression is often associated with unhealthy dietary patterns. For instance, individuals with depression exhibit higher average intakes of fat—particularly saturated fat—compared with healthy controls, which may elevate the risk of dietary inflammation [27]. Advancing age is associated with less healthy and more unbalanced dietary patterns, which may lead to higher DII scores in older adults. From these findings, it can be inferred that older adults are prone to consuming pro-inflammatory diets, which may contribute to depressive symptoms. Concurrently, individuals with depressive symptoms may further increase their risk of dietary inflammation, creating a vicious cycle. Thus, the interaction between pro-inflammatory diets and depressive symptoms could exacerbate frailty risk in older adults. However, data on the potential role of the DII in the depression–frailty relationship among older adults with diabetes remain scarce. Furthermore, whether the combined effect of pro-inflammatory diets and mild-to-severe depressive symptoms further amplifies frailty risk in this population warrants further investigation.

Therefore, our study aims to investigate the association between depressive symptom severity and frailty in older adults with diabetes and to examine the potential role of the DII in this relationship using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Furthermore, increasing awareness of pro-inflammatory diets and depressive symptoms among older adults with diabetes may help reduce dietary inflammatory load, alleviate depressive symptoms, and ultimately decrease the risk of future frailty.

Methods

Study population

This analysis utilized data from older diabetic adults aged ≥60 years, obtained from seven consecutive cycles (2007–2018) of the NHANES study. NHANES is a cross-sectional health survey conducted every two years, designed to represent health status of the non-institutionalized civilian population in the United States. A total of 2721 older adults with diabetes who completed dietary recalls, PHQ-9 assessment, and had available frailty index data were included in this study. Diabetes mellitus was defined as a self-reported diagnosis, hemoglobin A1c levels of 6.5% or higher, fasting plasma glucose levels of 126 mg/dL or above, or the use of insulin or oral hypoglycemic agent. A total of 2667 samples were retained for the final analysis after excluding 54 participants due to missing covariate information (Fig. 1). The NHANES survey protocol received approval from the Ethics Review Board of the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. According to relevant regulations, the secondary study of publicly accessible datasets did not require approval from the local ethics committee.

Flowchart of study sample selection process

Assessment of dietary inflammatory index

Dietary data were obtained through two 24-hour dietary recalls and calculated in accordance with the guidelines of the USDA Dietary Research Food and Nutrient Database. The two dietary recalls were conducted through an interview at the Mobile Examination Center (MEC) and a subsequent telephone follow-up. The Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) was calculated using a standardized algorithm derived from previously validated methods and tools [28]. The DII incorporates 45 dietary components known to influence inflammation [21], with detailed inflammatory effect scores and calculation procedures provided in Supplementary Table 1. In summary, the total DII score for each participant was calculated by summing the individual dietary parameter scores, with higher values indicating a stronger potential for dietary inflammation. In the absence of an established cutoff value for the DII, individuals with a DII score above the median of the study population were classified as having a high-DII diet.

Assessment of depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were evaluated with the validated Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a nine-item self-report screening instrument designed to assess the severity of depressive symptoms rather than to establish a clinical diagnosis of major depressive disorder. Participants were required to recall the frequency of each symptom experienced over the previous two weeks (each item rated 0–3; total score range 0–27). The PHQ-9 has demonstrated strong clinical validity [29] and is widely used in population-based studies to identify individuals with elevated depressive symptom burden, although a high score does not necessarily indicate a confirmed clinical diagnosis [30]. We defined depressive symptoms severity as none ( < 5), mild (5–9), and moderate-to-severe (≥10), following prior research [31].

Definition of frailty

To support research in diet and nutrition, the Rockwood team developed a 36-item version of the Frailty Index (FI) based on an original 46-item scale, with exclusion of items related to dietary consumption and nutritional condition [32, 33]. The index incorporates domains such as self-reported health status, vital signs, and laboratory parameters. Given the diabetic population in this study, a modified 35-item FI was adopted by excluding items specifically related to diabetes. The FI score was computed as the ratio of present health deficits to the total items, with resultant values bounded between 0 and 1. An FI score above 0.21 was defined as frailty, otherwise considered non-frailty. Supplementary Table 2 contains comprehensive details on the FI items.

Covariates

At the MEC, trained interviewers collected demographic and health-related information from study participants using standardized structured questionnaires. The collected information included age, sex, education level, ethnicity, marital status, smoking status, physical activity, dietary energy intake, body mass index, and self-reported hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and cancer. Physical activity was derived from self-reported work-related, transportation, and recreational activities. Weekly equivalent moderate-to-vigorous physical activity minutes were calculated by weighting vigorous-intensity activities as twice the duration of moderate-intensity activities, while walking or bicycling for transportation was considered moderate intensity. Participants achieving ≥350 equivalent moderate-to-vigorous physical activity minutes per week were categorized as physically active.

Statistical analysis

To account for NHANES’s complex sampling design, we included sampling weights (WTDR2D) and addressed clustering (SDMVPSU) and stratification (SDMVSTRA) per NHANES guidelines. Continuous variables were reported as weighted means with standard error, while categorical variables were reported as frequency counts and weighted proportions. Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics across groups were compared using analysis of variance, t tests, and Rao-Scott chi-square tests. Initially, multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to assess the individual associations of depressive symptoms severity and dietary inflammatory potential with frailty risk, while restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were applied to explore possible non-linear patterns in these relationships. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Subsequently, to explore the joint association of depressive symptoms severity and dietary inflammatory potential with frailty risk, participants were classified into six mutually exclusive groups combining depressive symptoms severity (None, Mild, Moderate/Severe) and DII status (Low/High by cohort specific median): No depressive symptoms & Low-DII, No depressive symptoms & High-DII, Mild depressive symptoms & Low-DII, Mild depressive symptoms & High-DII, Moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms & Low-DII, and Moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms & High-DII. Subsequently, multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the joint association of depressive symptoms severity and DII status with the frailty risk. The unadjusted model did not control for any confounding variables. Model 1 included adjustments for sex and age. Model 2 incorporated additional adjustments for ethnicity, marital status, education level, BMI, smoking status, physical activity, and total energy intake based on Model 1. To enhance the methodological rigor and confirm the robustness of our findings, we performed both subgroup and sensitivity analyses. Subgroup analyses were performed across stratifications of age ( < 70 vs. ≥70 years), sex, and ethnicity to assess potential effect modification. For sensitivity analyses, we further adjusted for hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and cancer to evaluate their specific contributions to frailty risk despite the comorbidity components already included in the frailty index. Second, considering the possible non-linear relationship between age and frailty risk, a quadratic term for age was introduced into the model to capture possible non-linear patterns. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value < 0.05.

Results

This study included 2667 older adults with diabetes, representing a weighted sample of 14,242,255 individuals, with a weighted mean age of 69.72 years and 53% male participants. Table 1 showed sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the study sample stratified by combined depressive symptom severity and DII groups. Individuals with concurrent moderate-to-severe depression and high DII exhibited distinct profiles, characterized by a higher proportion of females, socioeconomic disadvantages (lower education and being widowed/divorced/separated), increased prevalence of obesity, and reduced caloric intake compared to those with no/mild depressive symptoms and low DII (p < 0.05). Furthermore, individuals with coexisting moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms and high DII had higher frailty index scores and a greater prevalence of frailty (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2). Supplementary Table 3 summarized sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the study sample stratified by frailty status. Significant differences were found between the frailty and non-frailty groups across all variables, with the exception of ethnicity and smoking status.

Table 2 presents the associations between depressive symptom scores and DII with frailty risk, respectively. After adjusting for covariates, elevated depressive symptom scores demonstrated a significant positive association with increased frailty risk (OR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.12–1.21). Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed no evidence of nonlinearity in this association (P for nonlinearity = 0.117) (Fig. 3). After classifying depressive symptoms into three categories—none, mild, and moderate-to-severe—the mild (OR = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.45–2.93) and moderate-to-severe (OR = 6.54, 95% CI: 4.02–10.66) groups were both associated with significantly elevated odds of frailty relative to the reference group. Analysis of DII and frailty risk revealed similar patterns, with higher DII levels showing a positive association with frailty risk (OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.02–1.23) in fully adjusted models. The dose-response relationship remained linear across the association (P for nonlinearity = 0.103) (Fig. 3). Comparative analysis of dietary patterns demonstrated that participants in the higher DII group had 62% greater frailty risk compared to those in the lower (OR = 1.62, 95% CI: 1.19–2.22).

The results showed that the joint associations of depressive symptoms severity and DII status with frailty were consistent with the main analysis. Discussion

This study found that depressive symptoms are associated with frailty in adults with diabetes, and the risk of frailty increases significantly as depressive symptom severity increases. A similar pattern was observed between the DII and frailty risk: higher DII scores were positively associated with an increased likelihood of frailty. Furthermore, as both depressive symptom severity and DII levels increased, participants demonstrated a higher risk of frailty. The group with moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms and a high DII had the highest risk of frailty—a finding more prominent in males, adults aged under 70 years, and non-Hispanic Whites.

Diabetes has a high prevalence among older adults, and frailty often coexists with diabetes. The co-occurrence of frailty and diabetes is significantly associated with an elevated risk of both disability and mortality [2], highlighting the necessity to investigate the influencing factors of frailty in older diabetic patients. Frailty is a complex multi-factor syndrome with numerous influencing factors. Evidence from epidemiological research suggests a robust interrelationship between depression and frailty [34]. A study in patients with atrial fibrillation revealed that those with anxiety or depression and sleep disorders had a nearly 500-fold higher risk of developing frailty compared to individuals without these conditions, while patients with sleep disorders alone had a 13-fold increased risk of frailty relative to those without anxiety/depression or sleep disorders [35]. Another prospective cohort study found that non-frail participants with late-life depression had a 2 to 4-fold higher risk of developing frailty within one year compared with non-depressed patients [36].

However, there are relatively few studies on the association of depression with frailty in older adults with diabetes. A study reported that when comparing diabetes subjects with depression to those without depression, the likelihood of frailty was significantly increased by 1.72 times [37], indicating that depression is an important factor related to the risk of frailty in older diabetes patients. Consistent with this, our study confirms the association between depression and frailty in older adults with diabetes. Moreover, we demonstrate a significant positive correlation between higher depressive symptom scores and increased frailty risk, with a clear dose-response relationship indicating progressively greater frailty risk with worsening depressive symptoms severity.

Why were depressive symptoms associated with the frailty of older adults with diabetes? The explanations for the relationship between depressive symptoms and frailty include unhealthy lifestyles and pathophysiological mechanisms. Depression may increase the risk of frailty because older people with depression become less active, lack energy, and experience social isolation, leading to the emergence of physical symptoms of frailty [38]. Further research on the potential pathophysiological mechanisms and causal relationship between depression and frailty has found an association between frailty and inflammation in late-life depression, and it was discovered that frail older adults typically show increased levels of inflammatory biomarkers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and fibrinogen [38, 39]. Compared with non-depressed individuals, those with major depressive disorder (MDD) show elevated levels of various peripheral inflammatory biomarkers. The literature suggests that psychological stress triggers the activation of both the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and the sympathetic nervous system, resulting in the secretion of stress-associated hormones, including cortisol and catecholamines. Stress-induced cytokine release triggers an acute phase response and inflammation [40, 41]. At the same time, diabetes is an inflammatory disease, and chronic inflammation serves as a key mediator of diabetes-associated complications [42]. Therefore, it is hypothesized that diabetes combined with depression, especially severe depression, may lead to a more significant increase in inflammatory factors. Consistent with this mechanism, our data demonstrate a graded increase in frailty risk corresponding to escalating depressive symptoms severity among older diabetes patients. Inflammatory factors, such as IL-6, may directly cause frailty or reduce its core components such as muscle mass, strength, and power, as well as slow down exercise performance [43].

Recognizing that inflammatory processes contribute substantially to frailty pathogenesis through multiple pathways, and considering the established significance of lifestyle factors in diabetes management, our study further explored the impact of a pro-inflammatory diet on frailty in older diabetes patients. Our analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between higher DII levels and frailty risk. The dose response relationship remained linear throughout the association, with participants in the high DII group having a 62% higher risk of frailty than those in the low DII group. Notably, our observations corroborate the evidence synthesized in a systematic review and meta-analysis, reinforcing the robustness of this relationship. This meta-analysis was designed to comprehensively examine the association between DII scores and frailty risk. Five studies involving 13,908 older people provided data on frailty risk, and the results showed that older people with higher DII scores had an increased risk of frailty [44]. Higher DII scores may put the older adults in a pro-inflammatory state, which may accelerate muscle atrophy and increase the risk of frailty [44–47].

Our study also conducted a joint analysis of the depressive symptom severity and DII status with frailty risk. It was found that the group with moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms and high DII had the highest risk of frailty, and elevated DII would enhance the association of depressive symptoms with frailty. Our subgroup analysis revealed that in all subgroups, as the severity of depressive symptoms and DII levels increased, the population showed a higher risk of frailty. However, when stratified by sex, no significant trend was observed between high-DII diets and increased frailty risk compared to low-DII diets among women with mild depressive symptoms. This discrepancy may be attributed to men’s relatively fixed unhealthy lifestyle patterns. A study examining the influence of lifestyle patterns on depression among adults with diabetes found that men were overrepresented in the unhealthy lifestyle group (50.09%) than women [48]. In contrast, women with mild depressive symptoms exhibit minimal dietary changes, potentially maintaining physiological stability through alternative mechanisms such as stronger social support networks. It is only when depressive symptoms progress to moderate or severe levels that unhealthy dietary patterns may translate into observable frailty risk. We also observed no significant association between high-DII diets and increased frailty risk compared to low-DII diets among individuals aged over 70 with mild depressive symptoms. This may be attributed to frailty being a multifactorial outcome resulting from the interplay between aging and chronic diseases [49]. With advancing age, the accumulation of chronic conditions and progressive physical decline become more strongly correlated with frailty, potentially attenuating the impact of DII and depressive symptoms. Only when emotional health deteriorates severely do the adverse effects of poor lifestyle factors become pronounced in frailty manifestation. Additionally, it should be noted that stratified analyses inevitably result in smaller subgroup sample sizes, which may lead to unstable effect estimates. Given that subgroup analyses are exploratory in nature, these findings should be interpreted with caution and warrant confirmation in studies with larger sample sizes and adequate statistical power.

Pro-inflammatory dietary habits combined with depressive symptoms were associated with elevated risk of frailty in older diabetes patients, which may be attributable to the shared inflammatory pathways activated by pro-inflammatory dietary patterns, depressive disorders, and diabetes. Therefore, it is recommended that older diabetes patients adopt a diet associated with low DII scores or an anti-inflammatory diet [50]. The Mediterranean diet (MD) is consistently associated with lower DII scores. MD has been proven to be related to reducing cardiovascular risk [51], preventing metabolic syndrome [52], and lowering the risk of cancer [53], and it also helps improve muscle mass and function [54]. In addition, various nutrients, including vitamins C, D, A, and E, as well as β-carotene, anthocyanins, zinc, and magnesium, certain spices such as rosemary and ginger, and dietary components such as fiber, monounsaturated fatty acids, garlic, and onions, all have anti-inflammatory effects [55, 56]. Depressive mood can also be addressed through interventions such as lifestyle modifications, antidepressant medications, and psychotherapy. Elucidating the dual influence of mood disorders and dietary-induced inflammation offers critical insights for developing targeted frailty prevention strategies in older adults with diabetes.

Study Limitations: First, the cross-sectional design of this study limits its ability to establish a clear causal relationship between DII, depressive symptoms, and frailty risk in diabetes patients. Second, DII scores are calculated based on food component intake data, and individuals with missing data are excluded, which may lead to selection bias. At the same time, self-reported dietary information increases the possibility of recall bias. Third, since significant associations were mainly observed in the U.S. population, the generalizability of our results to older populations in other global regions may be limited. Finally, in the moderate/severe depression stratum, the result showed a wider confidence interval, likely reflecting the relatively smaller sample size. Although the association remained statistically significant, the precision of the estimate may be limited, and larger studies are needed to confirm these findings. In future, prospective longitudinal studies are needed to elucidate the temporal relationships and potential causal mechanisms linking DII, depressive symptoms, and frailty progression in individuals with diabetes.

Our findings identified combined of nutritional profiles with pro-inflammatory potential and depressive symptoms may be associated with higher frailty risk in older adults with diabetes. Targeted strategies integrating dietary inflammation modulation and mood evaluation may inform clinical approaches to addressing the multifactorial burden of frailty, potentially aligning with improved health outcomes and reduced healthcare system strain.
