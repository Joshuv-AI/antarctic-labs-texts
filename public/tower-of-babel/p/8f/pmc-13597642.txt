Why Sexual Choking Is Not as Safe as You Think: A Call to Action from Sex Researchers and Kink Educators
Authors: Stephen E. Ratcliff, Susan Wright, Debby Herbenick, Aidan A. Sunassee, Kiana Cummings, Tess Zachary, Anna Randall, Russell Stambaugh, Gael Strack, Doug D. Brown, K. Tony Korol-Evans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This is a synthesis of the research area and issues involving sexual choking, followed by calls to action based on the synthesis. We describe the health risks associated with sexual choking, which is often performed as a form of strangulation, along with its growing popularity in at least the Australia, Iceland, Italy, New Zealand, United Kingdom, and United States. Beginning with a clarification of the often-obfuscated terminology, this paper describes various ways that people are exposed to the practice of sexual choking. Our team identified social media, online pornography, and other popular media as potential drivers of the higher numbers of people reporting engaging in sexual choking compared to older studies. However, there are significant problems with consent and legal issues, including cases where consensual adult choking involving injury or death have resulted in criminal or civil charges in the U.S. This group of researchers, educators, and health experts offers calls to action in specific steps: (1) Widespread education about sexual choking and its risks; (2) Empowering critical evaluation through media and pornography literacy; (3) Organizational engagement in increasing risk awareness; (4) Health education curriculum development; (5) Healthcare provider continuing education; (6) Funding research that addresses sexual choking topics. The goal of this paper is to synthesize the research on this concerning trend in a manner accessible to a diverse array of audiences, and to offer calls to action to address this public health issue for researchers, educators, clinicians, students, kink/BDSM community members, and mainstream persons alike.

Introduction

In this paper, we identify the emerging evidence of the potential harms of sexual choking/strangulation, describe how mainstream versions of sexual choking differ from many kink/BDSM practices, and present calls to action related to this sexual practice. Specifically, we identify a need for education, research, and policies to address sexual choking/strangulation, a practice that has become increasingly popular among adults in at least the Australia, Germany, Iceland, Italy, New Zealand, UK, and USA. It was the aim of this team to attempt a synthesis of the literature in layman’s terms with actionable steps that allied organizations, community members, and citizens can implement.

Sexual choking usually involves the use of one or both hands or, much less often, an arm, leg, or ligature (e.g., rope, collar) to press against or squeeze the neck, leading to at least some restriction of blood flow and/or air flow (Herbenick et al., 2022b, c). Thus, sexual choking is usually a type of neck compression or strangulation—even though “choking” is the term widely in use by people who engage in it as well as in pornography, social media, television shows, and online articles (e.g., Cardoso, 2022; Conte et al., 2025; David, 2024; Herbenick et al., 2022d). Indeed, a challenge in identifying literature related to this sexual practice, as well as in educating about it, is that a number of different words (i.e., choking, strangulation, breath play, breath control, scarfing) have been used to describe it.

Recent research finds higher numbers of people reporting engaging in sexual choking compared to older studies (Daminato et al., 2024; Döring et al., 2024; Herbenick et al., 2021, 2025a, b, c, d, e; Sharman et al., 2025b; Vilhjálmsdóttir & Forberg, 2023). In 2011, in an online convenience sample of young, heterosexual adults, researchers found that 13% of women and 9% of men reported that they had ever been choked with hands during sex (Bridges et al., 2016). Nearly a decade later, a US study of 4989 randomly sampled college students found that two-thirds of women, 29% of men, and 56% of transgender and nonbinary students had ever been choked during sexual activities (Herbenick et al., 2021). Further, a convenience survey conducted in Iceland found that 70% of 18–24-year-old adults report having engaged in choking in their lifetimes (Vilhjálmsdóttir & Forberg, 2023), while an Australian study found that 57% of adults aged 18–35 years old had ever been choked and 51% had ever choked a partner (Sharman et al., 2025b). In a 2022 US nationally representative survey, 41% of women and 18% of men aged 25–29 had ever been choked during sexual activities as compared with < 8% among those ages 49 and older (Herbenick et al., 2025b).

As a general pattern, these and other studies have found that having ever choked or been choked is more prevalent among people younger than 40 as compared to older than 40 and is rare among people ages 60 and above, suggesting an age cohort effect (Herbenick et al., 2025b; Vilhjálmsdóttir & Forberg, 2023); however, there are some outliers. For example, in a recent qualitative study of Australian men who have sex with men and who had engaged in sexual choking, several participants were over 60 and the oldest participant was 81 years old (Mullin et al., 2026). Also in a US nationally representative survey, 1% of women aged 60 and < 2% of men ages 60 + report that a partner had ever choked them with consent (Herbenick et al., 2025b). In terms of gender, more men and gender diverse people, as compared to women, reporting choking their partners, and more women and gender diverse individuals, as compared to men, report being choked (Daminato et al., 2024; Döring et al., 2024; Herbenick et al., 2023a, 2025a, b, c, d, e; Sharman et al., 2025b; Vilhjálmsdóttir & Forberg, 2023).

Although some historical accounts have documented accidental deaths from autoerotic asphyxiation (Tarr, 2016), there are limited historical research data about people’s experiences with partnered sexual choking. Adults who engage in sexual choking describe doing so for reasons related to perceived sexual norms, to please their partner, to enhance arousal or orgasm, or because it feels kinky or adventurous (Herbenick et al., 2022b, c). There is also an important distinction to be made between people who engage in sexual choking without being aware of the health risks and may benefit more from education about harm reduction alternatives, and those people who engage in sexual choking in part because of the risks involved. For example, research has found that one common motivation for engaging in adolescent autoerotic asphyxiation appears to be thrill seeking, wherein the risk of death increases sexual pleasure (Jenkins, 2000). However, while death may be a risk many people are aware of, fewer people seem to be aware of the short term (e.g., Hou et al., 2023a; Schori et al., 2022) and the potential for longer-term health risks of sexual choking beyond death, including cumulative neurological damage (e.g., Herbenick et al., 2023a). Therefore, this paper concludes with a series of calls to action for educators, researchers, and policymakers to address sexual choking and work toward prevention in order to mitigate potential harms associated with this sexual practice.

Health Risks of Being Choked

People who have been choked during sex have reported a range of choking-related symptoms including neck pain, bruising, and swelling, difficulty breathing or speaking, changes to their voice, and alterations in consciousness, which may include vision changes, dizziness, disorientation, and loss of consciousness (Herbenick et al., 2022a). Further, in a case report of a woman who had experienced both consensual and non-consensual sexual choking (i.e., strangulation), hyoid bone fracture was also observed (Marr & Bugeja, 2024), which has also been documented as part of strangulation in other violent contexts, albeit rarely. However, research from strangulation in the context of partner violence shows that many health problems from being strangled go unnoticed, often for days or weeks (de Boos, 2019; Di Paolo et al., 2009). Several emerging studies suggest that being choked during sexual activities may be associated with harms beyond these acute and delayed symptoms. One study of 41 women explored differences between women who have never been choked during sexual activities and women who have been choked 4 + times in the last 30 days and found differences in cortical thickening, gyrification, connectivity, and brain activation patterns during working memory (e.g., verbal and visual) tasks between the groups (Hou et al., 2023a, b; Huibregtse et al., 2022, 2025). That said, this was a cross-sectional study, and thus, the findings are correlational; subsequent longitudinal research is needed to examine the temporality of these findings.

Although research on the health effects of being choked during consensual sex is still emerging, there is sufficient evidence in the larger strangulation literature to indicate the following adverse health impacts: (1) pressure to the carotid arteries in the neck preventing blood flow to the brain and causing elevated blood pressures in the brain and decreased oxygen access risks affecting neurological health, potentially even at light pressures (Bichard et al., 2022; de Boos, 2019; Dunn et al., 2023), (2) an abnormal heartbeat which may then lead to cardiac arrest due to constriction of these veins (Dunn et al., 2023), (3) after being restricted due to artery constriction, when blood flow is stopped and then re-established, the brain tissue suffers from damaging proteins and lipids, and pro-inflammatory mediators are produced, triggering cell death (Busl & Greer, 2010), and (4) pressure to the front of the throat may cause collapse of the larynx and inability to breathe (Dunn et al., 2023).

Other studies have examined individuals’ mental health in relation to their experiences with being choked during sexual activities. One study of 4352 randomly sampled undergraduate students found that lifetime sexual choking history was associated with their reports of feeling depressed, sad, lonely, and for women (but not men) feeling anxious in the prior 30 days (Herbenick et al., 2022b). Yet, in another random sample study at the same university, the UCLA Loneliness Scale was used to assess feelings of loneliness, and there was no association between a history of having been choked and feeling lonely (Herbenick et al., 2025a), underscoring the need for subsequent research in this area.

There are important limitations to each of these studies: First, being cross-sectional data, these survey findings are correlational, and thus, directionality is not known. As the authors note in their work, it may be that feeling sad or alone makes people more likely to engage in choking, but it also may be that being choked/strangled contributes to poorer mental health; subsequent research is needed. Also, these are college student samples and findings cannot be generalized to the broader public, especially as many variables may uniquely impact college students’ mental health (Blanco et al., 2008; Perdrelli et al., 2015).

Sexual choking also has been associated with reports involving accidental death during this activity (Bauer et al., 2021). There have been a number of media reports about deaths that have been described as involving sexual choking as well as other forms of rough sex (We Can’t Consent to This, n.d.; Wright, 2025). Unfortunately, there are no systematic reporting mechanisms for these deaths, and there needs to be. Another data point is held by the National Coalition for Sexual Freedom (NCSF), who among their services includes taking reports of non-consensual incidents that occur during kink/BDSM (e.g., erotic force or restraint) activities for the subset of people who wish to report them. As of 2025, only 54 (7%) of 789 incident reports have included choking, 50 of which occurred since 2017 (National Coalition for Sexual Freedom, 2025). Eight of these choking incidents resulted in the death of the person who was choked, while six resulted in unconsciousness (Zachary & Wright, 2026). This further suggests that sexual choking is becoming more of a concern in recent years, though additional research is needed.

How People Learn About Choking

As noted in a US nationally representative sample of adolescents from 2011 to 2019, only half of surveyed individuals received sexual education in school (Lindberg & Kantor, 2022). As a result, many people learn about sexuality topics through social media such as TikTok (Fowler et al., 2022), FetLife (Fay et al., 2016), Only Fans (Lippman et al., 2023), and Facebook (Olamijuwon and Odimegwu, 2021) as well as through pornography, friends, sexual partners, mainstream media, and/or family members (Rothman et al., 2021). In terms of how people report having learned about sexual choking specifically, in a study of randomly sampled university students, the most cited sources of learning were sexual partners, friends, and pornography, while social media, memes, and erotica (including erotic books and fanfiction) were also commonly cited (Herbenick et al., 2025c).

Generally, some young adults appear to be unaware of the risks of choking and too often believe it to be safe, as was found in a recent qualitative study in Australia of 1528 adults, aged 18–35 (Conte et al., 2025). These authors note that participants may be conflating discussions about consent, pressure, and intensity with safety (Conte et al., 2025). This misperception that choking is safe appears common in analyses of online media articles about choking (Herbenick et al., 2022d), social media memes about choking (Herbenick et al., 2023b), and TikTok videos related to choking (Balle et al., 2026). Though sexual choking was not specifically mentioned, another study of 425 OnlyFans users illustrates new sexual practices being learned online, which was reported by the majority of study participants. The study that examined media articles found that they generally described choking in positive terms and indicated that choking could be done safely (Herbenick et al., 2022d). Likewise, social media memes often portrayed choking in positive ways, giving the sense that choking is normative, erotic, and even part of romantic interactions (Herbenick et al., 2023b). Finally, TikTok videos collected over 2 days in 2023–2024 included misinformation statements in all educational videos (n = 6) and the remainder (n = 90) contained content regarding desiring choking such as “being choked increases feminine attractiveness” and “ideal masculine partners choke” (Balle et al., 2026). With a lack of evidence-based sexual education provided in many schools, many people rely on informal methods of guidance from their sexual partners, friends, social media and pornography, which may perpetuate the circulation of misinformation about the risks involved in choking.

Pornography and Choking

In an analysis of magazines, videos, and Internet Usenet pornography available in a New York township, in addition to stories from a Usenet board, Barron and Kimmel (2000) found that choking was depicted in less than 1% of these media. Then, using a selection of pornographic videos that were popular from December 2004 to mid-2005, another study found that 7% of these videos depicted choking (Bridges et al., 2010). Similar results were found in a random sample of 400 videos identified in 2011, with aggregate violence described as occurring in less than 4% of videos (Klassen & Peter, 2015). In 2020, it was reported that 3% of PornHub scenes included choking and 4% of Xvideos included scenes of choking in an analysis of 4009 heterosexual videos (Fritz et al., 2020); however, these were short scenes (in contrast to Bridges’ longer videos) and are thus not directly comparable. A more recent study compared the most watched 15 videos on Pornhub, a popular adult website, from 2000 to 2024 (n = 255) and found that choking was “the most consistent increase we detected throughout the period of the study” (Shor & Liu, 2026). Shor and Liu also noted that “while not appearing in any of the videos in our sample uploaded to Pornhub in the 2000s, it [choking] was featured in 15% of the most viewed videos uploaded in the 2020s. Another study found that people who had more often viewed pornography were more likely to engage in choking (Wright et al., 2021). Finally, among other methods of learning about sexual choking, pornography was a notable source in a recently published qualitative study of 23 Australian men who have sex with men (Mullin et al., 2026). Similar results were found in a 2021 US campus study of 4254 students who noted pornography, friends, and sexual partners among the top three sources for learning about sexual choking (Herbenick et al., 2025e).

While the UK criminalization of strangulation depictions in pornography in the pending Crime and Policing Bill (Ministry of Justice & Davies-Jones, 2025) appear to have resulted in some pornography sites automatically restricting searches for “strangle” and “suffocation,” cursory searches by some members of this team found that, in the USA, searches are still permitted for related terms like “choking” and “breath play.” When one author (SR) conducted these searches in early 2025, these terms yielded videos that include apparent role play simulations of strangulation and suffocation. More recently, in early 2026, a second author (SW) conducted these searches on six of the largest pornography websites (Kink.com, Pornhub, Stripchat, xHamster, XNXX, and Xvideo) and found that searching for “choking” brought up videos on four of the websites, two of which also allowed searches for “strangulation,” while the other two websites only allowed users to search for “strangle” rather than “choking” or “strangulation.” Because many adults report getting sexuality information from pornography (Rothman et al., 2021; Westlake et al., 2023), adult entertainment producers are in a unique position to strongly influence viewers by mentioning that pornography is fantasy and fictional in nature.

Choking Game as a Possible Precursor

It is possible that, for some subset of people, the current adult sexual choking trends noted in these studies are an extension of the choking game played by children, adolescents, and young adults in which individuals strangle themselves or one another to experience euphoric sensations through brief hypoxia and/or lose consciousness from doing so (Mammoser, 2024). The choking game recently became a TikTok trend, but it had previously been noted as a trend in the mid-1990s (Katz & Toblin, 2009) prior to the Internet driving information sharing among youth about such risky behaviors. At that time, information about this game was shared even prior to, and apart from the emerging Internet, and many learned from friends. Eighty-two deaths were identified as being associated with the choking game from 1995 to 2007, mostly among male adolescents (CDC, 2010). At that time, adolescents appeared highly aware of this game (e.g., ranging from 36 to 91% in a systematic review of 10 countries) with varying awareness and engagement by region (Busse et al., 2015). For example, a study of 5348 eighth graders in the state of Oregon found that 64% of these adolescents had engaged in this activity at least once, and 40% believed there was no risk from this game (Ramoski et al., 2012).

In People v. Ford (2015), it was found that a teenager could not consent to the “choking game” due to the inherent risk of serious bodily injury and/or risk of death. While some platforms had removed thousands of choking game videos as of 2017, others have not, and new videos have been posted and shared since (Samarasekera, 2022). This provides one possible early template that may play out in some of the adults engaging in sexual choking today. While this is a possible connection, no studies have examined this connection and further research is warranted. However, peer transmission of erroneous information about sexual strangulation does occur in many forms, with evidence of this transmission found in a recently published study of Australian men who have sex with men, in which partner and peer learning were two major sources of learning about sexual choking for this study’s 23 participants (Mullin et al., 2026).

Choking in Kink Communities

While some people include choking under the larger umbrella of “breath play” (Begum, 2024), breath play (i.e., breath control) typically refers to behaviors that control breathing. Such practices tend to include placing a hand over the mouth and nose, ordering somebody to hold their breath, breath-swapping, wearing a hood, face-sitting, or other adjacent behaviors (Ando, 2009). Thus, we will next address choking in kink communities.

Kink refers to a variety of different erotic and sexually diverse communities that are characterized by fantasies and behaviors outside the mainstream such as consensual adult activities with bondage, power exchange, or intense stimulations (Sprott & Williams, 2019). Within kink and BDSM communities, it is generally recommended that people use a safety signal to communicate when they wish to change the intensity or stop this activity (Khan, 2016). While a recent scoping review found that 20% of adults engaged in kink behaviors in their lifetime (Brown et al., 2020), a 2015 US nationally representative survey found that less than 4% of adults in America have attended a BDSM party (Herbenick et al., 2017), which is a space in which they could potentially learn about community norms such as safety issues and methods of communicating consent (Dunkley & Brotto, 2020). This may be due to the introduction of the internet and the ability for people to engage in kink but not with communities (Wignall, 2022), which has changed the way education about kink is obtained.

Historically, the practice of sexual choking was not taught in the foundational kink books due to there not being a safe way of engaging in choking. As one example, Townsend’s (1972) famous “Leather List,” which was circulated in the late 1960s in Los Angeles, listed dozens of kink activities. Yet, neither choking nor strangulation nor breath play was included when the list was published in the Leatherman’s Handbook. Townsend (1972) goes on to advise that during the published kink activities, the bottom (e.g., receiver of the kink behavior) should have a “keyword” (p. 345) to use as a warning in case they have problems with circulation or breathing during any encounter, which assumes the ability to speak during kink activities. As a second example, since the mid-twentieth century sexually diverse communities have used different colors of handkerchiefs worn in select back pockets to advertise their sexualities for prospective partners, with colors representing specific kinks (Cornier, 2018). It is thus notable that there is no handkerchief color for breath play or choking in What Color is Your Handkerchief? A Lesbian S/M Sexuality Reader (Samois, 1979). This absence persists in contemporary handkerchief color coding, which has grown considerably to include additional activities (Guest User, 2019).

Third, several historical BDSM sources have cautioned readers against engaging in choking/neck compression, identifying it as an unsafe practice. The Samois’s Coming to Power (1981) states, “Bondage which can tighten around the neck is unsafe (p. 74). Additionally, Wiseman’s (1991) SM 101: A Realistic Introduction included an oft-quoted statement against this sexual practice, stating, “As a person with years of medical education and experience, I know of no way whatsoever that either suffocation or strangulation can be done in a way that does not intrinsically put the recipient at risk of cardiac arrest” (p. 387).

Within the kink communities, there have been different descriptions of what people now call “choking.” As one example, one of the earliest leather manuals describes the use of light pressure on the neck as well as breath restriction but also states that fantasy is a fundamental part of the practice, noting “He touches your throat, maybe even applies a bit of pressure, and you rage. You thrash and get high on the threat as much as on the reduced breathing, but mostly you fly on the fantasy of great danger” (Bean, 1994, p. 135). Further, in a contemporary essay McDermott (2016, para 9) states, “It’s within this context of safety, respect, and consent that submission becomes ‘submission’ and choking becomes ‘choking.’ It’s play-acting”. Another kink educator wrote, “One way to create the now-I’m-forced-and-helpless feeling of choking without choking is to wrap your hand around your partner’s throat without any pressure while you’re fucking” (Shays, 2012, p. 219). However, this suggestion was immediately followed by advice for how to choke someone in what they stated was “a safer way,” describing the compression of the carotid arteries “to reduce blood flow to the brain” (Shays, 2012, p. 219) in lieu of compressing the trachea. That said, none of us condone this practice based upon the previously reviewed literature that suggests these techniques are associated with causing harm. Even Shays (2012) included the disclaimer: “But I’m going to tell you right now, there is no way to choke someone without a huge risk of injury or possible death. There is abundant documentation of heart attacks, brain damage, seizures, and crushed larynxes—all these effects and more have been clearly linked to choking, strangulation, and other forms of controlling your own or another’s breathing… Passing out is enormously dangerous: there’s no way to recover those brain cells that were killed, and he could have a heart attack or a stroke.”

Some kink social and educational groups do allow play and educational workshops of methods of breath play that do not involve strangulation; however, further research is needed to determine the extent to which breath play and/or choking specifically are rare or common among various kink communities. One kink practitioner who sought out fear, risk and danger with their sexual activities specifically noted that “strangling” is a practice beyond the “safety net” (Newmahr, 2011). Wiseman found that “because of its severe and unpredictable risks, more and more SM (i.e., Sadomasochism, which is an archaic word for kink) party givers are banning any form of breath control play at their events” (Wiseman, 1996, p. 391). Weiss (2011) noted that breath play was still “widely prohibited” (p. 88) by San Francisco kink groups. Retroteks House of Gaspers claimed to be the first modern online asphyxiaphilia (e.g., arousal at intentionally restricting oxygen to the brain) community dating to a 1992 dialup bulletin board system; however, even this small group stated, “PEOPLE HAVE DIED playing at choking or hanging” (Ando, 2009). It was not until the mid-to-late 2000s that a few kink groups started holding breath play workshops that included how-to information about choking (Ando, 2009). However, Lehmiller (2018) found in his 2014 survey of over 4000 U.S. adults that safety guidelines are important when enacting fantasies and that acts “like strangulation” are considered to “go well past the point of being safe and sane,” in reference to the kink communities’ motto established in 1981 that activities must be “Safe, Sane and Consensual.” Choking also violates another kink community consent model created in 1999 called Risk-Aware Consensual Kink (RACK) (Williams et al., 2014) because of the participants’ inability to obtain comprehensive risk awareness due to the lack of information on the long-term health effects caused by sexual choking.

Since the 2011 publication of Fifty Shades of Grey, popular culture articles have appeared with how-to advice on engaging in a wide range of kinky and rough sex practices, including choking someone, with sex therapists and mainstream sex educators mistakenly referring people to the kink community to learn how to practice this behavior safely (Herbenick et al., 2022d; Triffin, 2016). Unfortunately, these online articles about choking often provide misinformation about choking, typically describing supposedly “safe” ways to engage in this behavior and understating the health risks (Herbenick et al., 2022d; Wright, 2025). Responding to this apparent increase in interest in sexual choking, the NCSF conducted an informal email poll in 2024 of 57 Coalition Partner groups that serve kinky people (Wright, 2025). They found that only seven (12.3%) groups allowed choking at their events, with five of those groups (8.7%) allowing it only for certain members who requested permission from the monitors or owners. It is notable that none of the 57 kink organizations provided how-to education on choking.

Education alone may not be sufficient to deter people from engaging in sexual choking despite the risk of injury and even death, perhaps because people who are involved in kink have been found to exhibit higher levels of “sensation seeking” which has been defined as a willingness to take risks for the sake of gaining sensations (Ronowska, 2025). For example, Sheff (2021) noted “perhaps the most common form of accidental death by kinky sex is via restricting oxygen and or blood flow to the brain during sexual interactions” (p. 261). Indeed, a literature review of accidental deaths during BDSM-related activities between 1986 and 2020 found 17 such cases (Schori, 2022). Relevant to the present paper, 88% of these accidental deaths involved manual strangulation. In half of these cases, the level of BDSM experience of the partners involved was described and, in all cases, both partners reportedly had prior BDSM experience, with three cases involving practitioners who provided instructions and CPR training beforehand (Schori, 2022).

Choking Is Not Legally Permitted in the USA

While a full review of the legality of choking internationally is beyond the scope of this paper, an overview in one country will provide some noteworthy discussion. In the USA, Affirmative Defense of Explicit Prior Permission (EPP) is a legal framework for consent to kink that was created by the American Law Institute in the revised Model Penal Code on Sexual Assault (Model Penal Code Section §213.10). EPP is currently in draft iteration with a full publication expected in late 2026 (Schulhofer, 2023). Under EPP, serious bodily injury is not allowed and according to established law, is defined as permanent serious disfigurement, or dysfunction of a limb or organ, or risk of death, all of which can be caused by choking (Bauer et al., 2021; Bichard et al., 2022; de Boos, 2019; Dunn et al., 2023). Thus, even under future expanded legal definitions that legalize some kink and rough sex behaviors, sexual choking is not legally permitted.

Today, all 50 states, the District of Columbia, the Uniform Code of Military Justice, the Federal Code, three US territories, and at least 20 Native American Tribal Codes have enacted laws criminalizing strangulation and/or suffocation (The Training Institute on Strangulation Prevention, 2025). The Federal Code is the most comprehensive and criminalizes any act of intentionally, knowingly, or recklessly impeding breathing or blood circulation by applying pressure to the neck or throat, regardless of visible injury or intent to kill. Violations are felonies punishable by up to 10 years under Title 18 USC § 113 (2021). These laws allow the crime to be prosecuted as a felony in most jurisdictions (Gwinn et al., 2024; Strack & Gwinn, 2011). Variations between states include how strangulation is legally categorized from a general intent crime to a domestic violence crime, and what proof or evidence is required for its prosecution. A detailed map of state variations is available (Training Institute on Strangulation Prevention, 2025). Across the USA, the passage of strangulation and suffocation laws has significantly increased the investigation and prosecution of felony cases (Brady et al., 2022; Gwin et al., 2024; Pritchard et al., 2017; Strack & Gwin, 2011).

US case law has consistently held that consent is not a valid defense to acts likely to cause grievous bodily harm or death: for example, in People v. Cline (2024), strangulation was classified as aggravated battery. Courts have similarly rejected the consent defense in BDSM contexts, as seen in John Doe v. George Mason University (2016), affirming that states can outlaw strangulation for public policy reasons, and in State v. Stone (2023) which affirmed prioritization of public safety over individual consent.

By way of illustration, in this last case State v. Stone (2023), the defendant argued that the victim consented to sexual choking during BDSM. At trial, Stone admitted to strangling the victim with a cord. The victim testified it was not consensual. Stone testified the victim consented to the choking and that they were practicing consensual BDSM. He further testified that he was careful to watch if she tapped out or struggled and was convinced that the victim did not lose consciousness. He believed she was enjoying it. He also testified that his intent was to deprive the victim of air only to the point that it was pleasurable and not to the point of unconsciousness or injury. On cross-examination Stone admitted he knew strangulation is dangerous and could cause death. The court ruled, and the Court of Appeal agreed, that consent is not a defense to the crime of strangulation.

Finally, sexual choking carries significant financial liability risks regardless of intent. Other parties can be held civilly liable in strangulation cases (Estate of Cayley Mandadi v. Trinity University, 202). Most insurance policies exclude coverage for illegal acts or intentional acts, meaning damages or legal defenses may not be covered for seemingly consensual sexual choking. Similar to driving under the influence, sexual choking is an ultrahazardous activity that could be considered a strict liability activity due to the potential for death or severe brain injury, which cannot be mitigated through reasonable care (Goodwin v. Reilley, 1985). As such, strict liability applies to any resulting harm (Restatement (Second) of Torts § 520, 1977).

Consent Challenges with Choking

Several unique challenges to consent are posed by sexual choking. First, recent research on college students has found that over half of those who have been choked during sex have only sometimes or never been asked for consent (Herbenick et al., 2021). In two studies, when asked by the researchers if the choking was non-consensual, very few said that it was non-consensual; however, some participants noted that there was no verbal discussion about the choking prior to it occurring (Herbenick et al., 2022a; Sharman et al., 2025b). Similarly, among kink communities, only 2% and 3% of the respondents reported having experienced non-consensual choking in two NCSF Consent Surveys (Wright et al., 2015, 2022). While there are multiple models of consent within kink communities, “non-consensual” is generally understood to mean that the activity was not discussed prior to doing it, including the potential health risks involved and how to stop at any time (Wright et al., 2022). Many people, especially men, regularly rely on nonverbal communication for consent to sexual activities (Newstrom et al., 2021); however according to kink ethics and norms, you cannot nonverbally obtain consent for the use of erotic force or restraint without explicit agreement and discussion of intensity and the type of behavior that will be done (Dunkley & Brotto, 2020; Schulhofer, 2023). Therefore, gestures such as placing a partner’s hand on one’s neck or one’s own hands to a partner’s neck (nonverbal signs that people frequently use to initiate choking as per Herbenick et al., 2025d) raise critical concerns regarding whether they constitute free, informed, and meaningful consent.

Secondly, about 40% of young adults who have ever been choked during sexual activities report that they have been unable to breathe or speak at times, which means that even if they and their partner(s) have agreed upon safe words, they may be unable to say them (Herbenick et al., 2025d). As the brain becomes deprived of oxygen, people may experience lightheadedness or feel dizzy or disoriented and may be unable to move their hands to use a signal to stop even if they want to (Rossen et al., 1943; Smith et al., 2011); they may even lose consciousness (Stellpflug, 2020). This severely undermines the reliability of such measures in ensuring consent, even though some sex educators encourage people to use a safe signal like raising a hand or tapping (WebMD, 2024), presumably so that the choker would know to stop. This advice also fails to account for the fact that consent negotiations are less likely to occur in sexual choking situations as compared to kink encounters which are typically discussed and planned out in advance.

Consent to choking and/or being choked also cannot be considered meaningfully informed without an understanding of the risks and harms associated with it, including both health and legal consequences. Many people engaging in choking are not aware of the harms associated with it (Conte et al., 2025; Sharman et al., 2025a), and without a thorough awareness of these risks, a participant’s “yes” lacks the necessary foundation of informed consent.

Harm Reduction Alternatives to Choking

The team that has put together this paper has observed that some sexuality educators and kink educators teach alternative ways to gain similar sensations, such as: (1) rebreathing in each other’s mouth in order to get a feeling of euphoria, (2) “neck holding” where a hand is placed on someone’s neck without any pressure from pressing or squeezing in order to create a sensation of fear or dominance, (3) pressing on the jaw bone or collar bone to simulate the pressure of choking without blocking the blood vessels or airway, (4) use of gags, hoods, and/or collars that are not intended to impede the normal breathing or circulation of the blood, and (5) corseting that restricts breathing without compressing the neck. We are not aware of any research that has examined the extent to which engaging in these acts during sexual activities may reduce potential risks or harms, but it is possible that at least some of these may offer interested individuals potentially safer alternatives to choking. Given how prevalent choking has become, more research is warranted that addresses possible harm reduction strategies and alternatives to choking.

These harm reduction techniques conform to explicit prior permission (EPP), which requires adherence to several important guidelines in order to qualify for the potential legal protections it offers: first, individuals must agree to the specific acts in advance and fully understand the associated risks. This includes evaluating whether any health issues could make breath play particularly dangerous for themselves, their partner, or in cases of pregnancy, a developing fetus. Second, participants must establish a reliable way to stop the activity at any time, such as a safe word or safe signal. Third, clear agreements should also be made on whether roleplay resistance will occur during the act. Finally, in order to attain EPP level consent, all participants must be legal adults, free from the influence of drugs or alcohol, and capable of giving consent willingly and without coercion. While not every person who engages in harm reduction strategies will opt to follow the requirements to get the protections EPP may provide, they are common norms in kink communities that are strongly encouraged. A recent study of 202 BDSM practitioners’ consent practices evidences these common norms, also noting that sobriety is highly encouraged (Tarleton et al., 2025). Because many of these techniques require a certain degree of skill to implement, interested parties are encouraged to take informed course instruction on relevant methods before implementing these harm reduction practices. Harm reduction strategies requiring less skill to begin employing include fantasy methods such as verbal dirty talk without bodily activity.

Call to Action in Six Parts

Based upon the reviewed evidence that sexual choking appears to be increasing among adults and based upon the health, consent, and legal risks reviewed, this multidisciplinary team of scholars, advocates, and legal experts has crafted the following call to action in six parts. Each part of the call to action is starting points that will need to be tailored to individual communities and contexts. This paper primarily contributes to these calls to action by serving as a brief synopsis of the relevant research that can be easily disseminated to classes and in educational and evidentiary support of the calls to action. We build on parallel media campaigns that have been done to curb sexual choking practices in Australia such as The Breathless Campaign (2025), It Left No Marks (Women’s Health NSW, 2024) and that warn about the choking game like Erik’s Cause (2012). We also heed the advice of Coverdale et al. (2025) and have aimed for actionable calls to action while maintaining that these are suggestions as starting points that will need to be tailored to be effective. Moreover, we cannot do this in isolation and call on you and your community to join in these efforts for these efforts to be successful.

Some individuals may realize the risks associated with sexual choking and cease these practices; however, others may hear about the risks associated with sexual choking and find motivation to pursue these practices regardless of, or even because of, these risks. Prior research has found that some individuals pursue sexual risks because of the thrill, and this has been associated with sensation seeking and impulsive adults (Charnigo et al., 2013) as well as insecurely attached adults (Kim & Miller, 2020) among others. To this group, campaigns like Erik’s Cause (2012) and this manuscript jointly warn that harm can occur even when practicing alleged “safe choking” or in sixth-grader Erik’s case, when taking precautions, death can still occur (Erik’s Cause, 2012). We believe that some sizable but unknown percentage of adults will shift their behaviors (in this case, decide not to engage in choking) when risk aware; however, some may not change their behavior. Also, there is some possibility that, in disseminating prevention messages and education to the public, some people will be motivated to experiment with sexual choking.

Because these are calls to action, we will furnish evidence and examples supporting our recommendations where we can while recognizing these limitations of the existing literature. The six calls to action for which this team advocates are: (1) widespread education about choking and its risks, (2) empowering critical evaluation through media and pornography literacy, (3) organizational engagement in increasing risk awareness, (4) health education curriculum development, (5) healthcare provider continuing education, and (6) funding research that addresses sexual choking topics.

Call to Action # 1: Widespread Education About Choking and Its Risks

Given the serious health consequences associated with being choked during sex, alongside the limited sexuality education available to young people and the online misinformation about this sexual practice being described as “safe,” we believe that education about sexual choking and its risks is needed. In some cases, this will require the more arduous task of re-educating those who already perceive some forms of choking as safe. There are multiple ways in which such education could be delivered, including public health guidance for healthcare providers, a public health campaign including public service announcements, fact-based information for adolescents and college students, and fact-based information provided for parents who could then lead conversations with their adolescent children.

People need to know that sexual choking carries a number of health risks, including the risk of death, and that there are no circumstances in which it is risk-free, contrary to messages people may have received from friends, partners, pornography, or online. Further, educational messages about sexual choking should note that many jurisdictions are clear that people cannot legally consent to something that causes a risk of death, such as choking/strangulation. In a recent study, Herbenick et al. (2025d) developed educational messages about sexual choking that addressed health-related risks, the need for consent, as well as potential legal consequences in the event of accidental injury or death. They found support for the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary efficacy of these messages, with men being significantly responsive to the messages about potential legal consequences.

Because some populations may react to information about choking with feeling that their freedom is being curtailed (e.g., based on reactance theory; Brehm, 1966), educational messaging should be framed as educational and not forceful. In addition, educators should be aware that for people who enjoy sexual choking because of the danger, there is a risk of “miseducation” by simply denouncing this behavior. For example, renowned autoerotic asphyxiation researcher Robert Hazelwood discouraged the use of television as a medium for preventative education because it alerted people who were previously unaware of the existence of this behavior (Hazelwood, 1989).

Another challenge in educating populations will be presenting the information in the most integrative manner to improve population implementation in preventing sexual choking. For example, messages should be politically ambivalent (i.e., without political skew or stance) as this improves scientific communication impact (Celniker & Ditto, 2024). Additionally, messages should consider utilizing two-side messages (i.e., pro and con) from a neutral perspective rather than a message that only presents the harms of sexual choking as this may further improve message integration (e.g., Wallace et al., 2024). Misinformation will also need to be responded to, such as articles that contend there is a safer way of engaging in sexual choking (e.g., Srinivasan, 2025, for an example). In this instance as well, messaging should utilize the growing body of literature to effectively challenge misinformation such as interventions to increase cognitive engagement with messaging rather than just debunking misinformation (e.g., Ecker et al., 2022). This could be accomplished through multiple approaches such as utilizing trusted authority sources and avoiding stigmatization in messaging (e.g., Bode & Vraga, 2021), utilizing social media to communicate key talking points in a carefully tailored manner (e.g., Ku et al., 2025), using narratives in messaging (Lillie et al., 2024), and including questions in health messaging to increase population cognitive engagement with messaging (Ecker et al., 2022). All of these efforts seek to increase the digital sexual literacy (Power et al., 2024) of targeted populations.

Therefore, there may be opportunities to address one or more of the harm reduction alternatives furnished in this article as well as to provide consent education, as well as the levels of risk involved, for other forms of breath play that do not involve sexual choking. It is possible that peer education may be effective in educating about choking and its risks, as one meta-analysis of peer education about HIV found a significant association with increased HIV knowledge and increased condom use (Medley et al., 2009); however, in a recent study related to educational messages about choking, researchers found that college students largely trusted experts (e.g., medical providers, college professors who teach sexuality and health courses) to share information about choking and its risks more so than they trusted peers or celebrities (Herbenick et al., 2025d). More research is needed to understand which types of influential sources might be well-received by different subsets of the population.

We encourage people who produce media, whether in mainstream media or pornography, to include messages indicating that choking is not safe and should not be tried at home. This could be encouraged by utilizing existing social media misinformation strategies (e.g., Kaur & Gupta, 2023) and possibly utilizing AI large language models (Chen & Shu, 2024). These strategies should both challenge misinformation and increase engagement with accurate information (Ecker et al., 2022). Further research is needed on the effects of education about partnered sexual choking and potential avenues for successfully reducing willingness to engage in life-threatening behavior.

Call to Action #2: Empowering Critical Evaluation Through Media and Pornography Literacy

We encourage mainstream educators in general and sexuality educators in particular to think critically about checking the accuracy of their information sources before disseminating it and provide education on vetting media sources for accuracy by using credible sources and fact checkers. We also suggest that educators teach individuals about the fantasy nature of adult erotic content and suggest existing pornography literacy research to assist them (Byron et al., 2021; Dawson et al., 2020; Zen et al., 2025). Such efforts could be integrated into existing health curriculum for adolescents, which researchers who conducted a pilot study found to be helpful in stemming pornography-based misperceptions (Rothman et al., 2018); however, efforts would need to be multi-systemic including recommendations disseminated to pornography producers and distributors, legislation to encourage warning labels for videos featuring choking content, and integration into health curriculum for college students. Efforts should also include public health information about pornography literacy disseminated via social media as research suggests that most young adults access health information from social media platforms (Lim et al., 2022). Campaigns are encouraged to utilize the social media platforms and techniques most apt to speak to younger audiences such as short TikTok style videos or memes.

Call to Action # 3: Organizational Engagement in Increasing Risk Awareness

While these calls to action can provide suggestions for what we believe needs to be done next, we cannot accomplish this work without a community effort of vested stakeholders with diverse specializations that can bring capabilities beyond the present team’s expertise. We encourage the use of interdisciplinary strategies in spreading accurate information, such as the resources in this article. We also encourage additional research on the effectiveness of engagement strategies by community stakeholders. This can include engaging key allies such as public health officials, medical and mental health professionals, domestic violence prevention professionals, sexual assault prevention and legal professionals, sexuality educators, community educators and organizers, media, and social media content creators. It is only by partnering together that we can tailor these messages to the needs of each unique community, prevent choking, and reduce harm.

Call to Action # 4: Health Education Curriculum Development

Many of the recommendations for call to action #1 can be tailored for call to action #4, including awareness of combating misinformation and effectiveness in preventative education. We believe that health education about sexual choking can be furthered by developing curriculum modules on choking behaviors for educational programs that target adults and high-risk groups (e.g., young adults, college students). These modules would go beyond speaking to the pornography literacy discussed in call to action #2 to include general instruction about clear consent communication, including navigating challenging situations due to the fact that choking often involves the inability to breathe or speak (and thus the inability to withdraw consent). Curricula should address the potential health consequences of being choked during sex as well as the legal implications, such as financial and criminal liability. For example, targeted health education for kink communities might include the legal risks of permitting sexual choking at events, and how choking may harm their group members both physically and psychologically. An example of disseminating this information would be if a person wishes to provide this information to kink communities, they could work with kink-specific organizations such as The Alternative Sexual Health Research Alliance, The Community-Academic Consortium for Research on Alternative Sexualities and/or the National Coalition for Sexual Freedom (NCSF).

Call to Action # 5: Healthcare Provider Continuing Education

We suggest that key professional educational stakeholders be engaged in offering Continuing Education modules on sexual choking that are designed to equip professionals to further disseminate information on the medical and psychological harms, consent limitations, legal challenges, and possible harm reduction alternatives to choking. Examples of these stakeholders include the American Psychological Association, American Medical Association, American Association of Sexuality Educators, Counselors and Therapists, the International Society for the Study of Women’s Sexual Health, and the World Association for Sexual Health. As part of this process, employ peer review and collegial response strategies when providers offer inaccurate or harmful advice, and ensure ethical practice by utilizing professional organizations when peer review or collegial response strategies do not work in order to ensure that all discussions of risky sexual behaviors are grounded in the latest research. Turkson and Knapp (2026) recently argued for the importance of psychotherapists becoming educated about sexual choking. Similarly, there have been calls for clinicians to address sexual choking through in-takes and for clinicians to become more educated in how to navigate assessment and conversations with patients about these high-risk sexual practices (Victoire et al., 2026). We join these calls for greater clinical engagement and education.

Call to Action # 6: Funding Research that Addresses Sexual Choking

Research on sexual choking/strangulation is needed to clarify, especially for certain subgroups of the population (e.g., by age, race, gender, sexual orientation, kink identification), how people come to learn about choking, as well as the roles of pornography, social media, mainstream media, and peer groups in potentially influencing this practice. We also need research on the potential physical and/or mental health sequelae; while several cross-sectional studies have been published, longitudinal research is needed that situates sexual choking in broader contexts of health, relationships, and adolescent and adult development. One area to consider is research on the similarities and differences between choking as conducted in sports like MMA compared to sexual or erotic circumstances. Researchers who are developing studies and surveys related to sexuality and sexual activities might also include questions about choking/strangulation, given its prevalence.

One catalyst to facilitating this research would be to increase funding available to study sexual choking/strangulation, its relationship to human health, as well as for the development and evaluation of educational messages and curricula aimed at prevention and/or harm reduction. It would be useful for public health organizations, as well as sexuality health and sexuality education organizations, to identify sexual choking as a public health concern, given the potential for adverse health consequences (including death).

Sexual choking represents a risk to overall sexual health, which the World Health Organization (n.d.) defines as “Sexual health, when viewed affirmatively, requires a positive and respectful approach to sexuality and sexual relationships, as well as the possibility of having pleasurable and safe sexual experiences, free of coercion, discrimination and violence” (para 1). The WHO continues by noting that sexual health requires sexuality free from harm, which based upon the reviewed literature, sexual choking is associated with serious harm, including the potential for death. Mitchell et al. (2021) build on the WHO definition to offer further clarification and elaboration that would be of further utility for officials seeking to implement this recommendation. As consensual sexual choking has increased in various countries around the world, so too have reports of non-fatal strangulation as part of sexual assault and rape (Cannon et al., 2019; Larsson et al., 2025), underscoring the need for funding and other resources to study these shifts in consensual and non-consensual sex and also the need for funding to develop and test interventions. For all these reasons, we encourage public health officials to address sexual choking as a major public health concern, which might support the allocation of foundation, state, and federal funding for research.

Conclusion

This review has detailed emerging evidence of the harms from all forms of sexual choking (i.e., strangulation), differentiation of this activity from kink/BDSM, and calls to action to disseminate evidence-based information about these high-risk sexual practices. Given the recent increases in sexual choking and its potential for harm, we believe that widespread education and a commitment to fund research and prevention addressing this topic are necessary to help mitigate the harms of being choked. Our team aimed to develop a readable synthesis of the literature with actionable steps that allied organizations, community members, and citizens can implement. Whichever variables are one day empirically evidenced as driving the growth in popularity of sexual choking, the dissemination of this manuscript and these calls to action in response to the harms from sexual and erotic choking are clearly warranted.
