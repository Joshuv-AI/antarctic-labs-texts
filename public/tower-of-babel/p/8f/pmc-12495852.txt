Evaluation of potential drug-drug interactions in the neonatal intensive care unit with two different decision support software: a retrospective study
Authors: Yunus Emre AYHAN, Ercan TUTAK, Burak ÖZDEMİR
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDrug-drug interactions (DDIs) are a critical concern in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) due to polypharmacy and the physiological vulnerability of neonates. This study aims to evaluate potential DDIs (pDDIs) using two different decision support software (DSS), Lexidrug™ and Micromedex®.MethodsThis retrospective study included neonates admitted to a tertiary NICU between January 1, 2022, and January 1, 2023, who received at least two systemically administered medications and stayed for ≥ 24 h. Patients with missing prescriptions or readmissions were excluded. pDDIs were assessed using two different DSS: Lexidrug™ and Micromedex®. pDDIs were categorized by severity, and D/X (major/contraindicated) or equivalent levels were considered clinically significant. A neonatologist and clinical pharmacist independently evaluated all identified pDDIs for clinical relevance. The consistency between DSS outputs was analyzed using Kendall’s W and Cohen’s kappa statistics. Chi-square and Mann–Whitney U tests were used to evaluate risk factors. A network map of drug interactions was also created using Gephi software.ResultsThe study included 280 patients. The gestational age median (interquartile range [IQR]) was 36 (34–38) weeks, and birth weight median (IQR) was 2745 (2181.2–3250) g. According to Lexidrug™ and Micromedex® databases, any level pDDIs were found in 151 (53.9%) and 127 (45.4%) patients, respectively. No patient had X/contraindicated level pDDIs. D/major level pDDIs were found in 95 (33.9%) and 71 (25.4%) patients, respectively. Vancomycin, gentamicin, and fentanyl were the most commonly involved agents in pDDIs. There was a very weak concordance between the two DSS for pDDIs at the X/contraindicated and D/major levels and a weak concordance for total pDDIs (Kendall’s W values; 0.004; 0.005; 0.249, respectively). According to the consensus, 7 (22.5%) and 9 (14.7%) of the different pDDI pairs were clinically significant according to Lexidrug™ and Micromedex®, respectively.ConclusionpDDIs were detected in a significant proportion of patients in the NICU. This study revealed weak agreement between different DSS used to assess pDDIs in the NICU. The findings underscore the importance of collaboration between clinical pharmacists and neonatologists when interpreting DSS results. Further prospective, multicenter cohort studies are warranted to validate these findings and improve DDI management strategies in neonatal populations.Trial registrationNot applicable.

Background

Drug-drug interactions (DDIs) refer to the alteration of a drug’s effect by the concomitant administration of another drug, which may lead to increased toxicity or reduced therapeutic efficacy. These interactions are generally classified as pharmacokinetic—affecting absorption, distribution, metabolism, or excretion—or pharmacodynamic—affecting receptor binding, additive/synergistic or antagonistic effects [1, 2]. Both types of interactions are clinically relevant in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), where neonates are exposed to multiple medications and physiological processes differ significantly from those in older children and adults.

In the NICU setting, managing DDIs presents particular challenges. On one hand, clinicians must rely on a relatively limited selection of medications approved for neonates; on the other hand, the immature organ systems of preterm infants and the lack of robust, evidence-based pharmacological data increase the likelihood of unpredictable and potentially serious outcomes [2–5].

Neonates, especially preterm and very preterm neonates, are often admitted to the NICU due to organ immaturity, congenital disease, or birth-related complications [4]. The extensive exposure to drugs in the NICU, the lack of evidence for personalized interventions in neonates, and the lack of neonate-specific formulations make neonates highly vulnerable to drug-related problems such as medication errors, adverse drug reactions, and drug-drug interactions [3, 5]. Potential drug-drug interactions (pDDIs) are clinically important situations defined as toxicity resulting from increased drug efficacy or treatment failure due to decreased drug efficacy caused by another drug administered concomitantly or within a short period of time. While many pDDIs may be preventable, some may involve necessary drug combinations that require careful monitoring rather than avoidance [1, 6, 7]. Side effects related to pDDIs can cause increased morbidity and mortality in patients, reduce the success rate of treatments, prolong the length of hospital stay, and increase treatment costs [8].

Implementing pDDI control systems can guide clinicians in making decisions to avoid severe and preventable events. In the few studies conducted on patients hospitalized in the NICU, the frequency of pDDI varies widely between 0 and 70% [9–12]. In DDI studies conducted on NICU patients, it was reported that the majority of pDDIs (61.4–90.6%) were at moderate risk levels, and major pDDIs were less frequent (29%) [10, 11]. Yalçın et al. detected clinically significant pDDIs causing adverse drug reactions in 38 (30.4%) of 125 patients with pDDIs in their study [12]. In pDDI studies conducted in NICUs, anti-infectives, furosemide, fentanyl, and fluconazole were reported as the most commonly involved agents in pDDIs. Lower gestational age, cesarean delivery, longer hospital stay, and a higher number of concomitant medications were identified as risk factors for exposure to pDDIs [10, 11].

There are few studies in the literature evaluating pDDIs in the NICU. Studies evaluating pDDIs with different decision support software (DSS) in various populations are available in the literature [13–15]. However, no study comparing pDDIs with different DSS in the NICU has been found. Neonatal period presents unique pharmacological challenges due to the immaturity of metabolic and excretory systems, underdeveloped enzyme pathways, and dynamic physiological changes. In preterm neonates, hepatic cytochrome P450 activity, renal clearance, and protein binding capacity are significantly reduced, leading to altered pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of many drugs [3, 5]. These developmental factors contribute to an increased susceptibility to drug toxicity or subtherapeutic effects, especially when multiple drugs are used simultaneously. As a result, identifying and managing pDDIs in this population is inherently more complex and clinically significant. This study primarily aimed to evaluate pDDIs in patients hospitalized in the NICU using two commonly utilized DSS— Lexidrug™ and Micromedex®—and to assess the level of agreement between them. These tools were selected based on their widespread use in neonatal and pediatric clinical settings and their comprehensive interaction databases. The study also aimed to characterize the prevalence, interaction profiles, and risk factors of pDDIs, and to incorporate clinical judgment by a neonatologist and a clinical pharmacist to determine the real-world clinical significance of the interactions identified by these systems.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective, descriptive study was conducted in the NICU of a tertiary-level teaching and research hospital located in an urban area. The NICU is a predominantly medical unit, consisting of 28 beds (12 Level III And 16 Level II beds), and admits an average of 600 neonates annually. Approximately 90% of admissions are inborn (delivered within the same hospital). The unit primarily serves preterm and term neonates with medical conditions; surgical cases are referred to other specialized centers. The mean length of NICU stay is approximately 7 days.

The study population included neonates hospitalized in the NICU between January 1, 2022, and January 1, 2023, who were prescribed at least two different systemically administered drugs, and who had a length of stay of at least 24 h and up to 28 days. Patients with missing prescription data or those readmitted during the study period were excluded. Eligible patients were retrospectively assessed for pDDIs.

Data collection

The patients’ sociodemographic information, disease and drug use information, birth weight, gestational age, delivery method, and discharge status were recorded through the hospital information management system with utmost respect for personal privacy. All prescription drugs were registered by their generic name and classified by the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification system [16].

Assessment of potential drug-drug interactions

The drugs included in each patient’s treatment were assessed for pDDIs by a clinical pharmacist and a neonatologist using Lexidrug™ Drug Interactions (Wolters Kluwer Health Inc.) and Micromedex® (Merative™) decision support software. pDDIs were categorized as X, D, C, and B levels in Lexidrug™ and as contraindicated, major, moderate, and minor levels in Micromedex®. pDDIs were categorized as B (minor), C (moderate), D (major), and X (contraindicated) according to the Lexidrug™ and Micromedex® DSS. Category B interactions are considered to have minimal clinical relevance and usually do not require any change in therapy. Category C interactions may require monitoring or dose adjustment but can generally be managed safely. Category D interactions are more serious and typically necessitate specific clinical interventions, such as close monitoring, dose modification, or considering alternative therapies. Category X interactions are contraindicated, as the risks of combined use clearly outweigh the potential benefits and such combinations should be avoided. X (contraindicated) and D (majör) level pDDIs were considered clinically significant pDDIs. Locally administered preparations, electrolyte solutions, and nutritional products for the patients’ treatments were not included in the pDDI assessment. Only systemically administered drugs were analyzed in terms of pDDI.

All pDDI pairs identified in the study were independently evaluated by a clinical pharmacist and a neonatologist to assess their clinical relevance in the NICU setting. In cases where initial evaluations differed, consensus was reached through discussion, with the neonatologist making the final decision when necessary, reflecting real-life clinical practice. The clinical relevance assessment was conducted in a single day after all pDDIs were identified. The number of disagreements and duration of discussions were not systematically recorded. Although there is no clinical pharmacist formally assigned to the NICU team, the pDDIs were retrospectively evaluated by a clinical pharmacist who was one of the study authors, in collaboration with the neonatologists working in the unit.

Sample size

The required sample size was calculated using G*Power software, based on the findings of Nasrollahi and Meera, who reported a 66.2% prevalence of pDDIs in neonates hospitalized in the NICU [10]. Assuming a null hypothesis prevalence of 50%, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a statistical power of 95%, the minimum sample size required to detect a statistically significant difference was calculated as 239 patients. The final sample of 280 neonates included in this study exceeded this requirement, thereby strengthening the validity of the results.

Statistical analysis

The data was statistically evaluated using the IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0 (Armonk, New York: IBM Corp.) program. Chi-square analysis was applied to compare categorical data and risk analysis. Since the data distribution was not normal, the two-group non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was used for continuous variables. Risk factors affecting pDDIs were evaluated, and probability ratios (95% confidence interval [CI], p) were provided. The consistency of the pDDI numbers of DSS was analyzed using Kendall’s W test. In Kendall W coefficient values, 0.00–0.10; very weak, 0.11–0.30: weak, 0.31–0.50: moderate, 0.51–0.70: strong, and 0.71–1.00: very strong relationships were categorized. The consistency between the pDDI entities of DSS was analyzed with Cohen’s kappa values [17]. All data with p < 0.05 at a 95% CI were considered statistically significant.

Gephi version 0.10.0 (Paris, France: Gephi Consortium) was used to better visualize the relationship between pDDIs in the form of a network map. In these network graphs, each node represents a drug, and the circle size is adjusted according to a ratio indicating a higher frequency of that drug. Edges indicate the interaction between two drugs. Percentage values ​​on the line indicate the percentage of the relevant pDDIs in the total interactions in the study. Since the line thickness is also adjusted according to the interaction frequency, thicker lines indicate interactions seen with higher frequency. In a pDDI network analysis, the more a node (drug) is among the network, the more likely it is to participate in forming the pDDI. Simplified versions of the networks were obtained by filtering.

Discussion

In this study, drug treatments used in patients hospitalized in the NICU were evaluated in terms of pDDIs by different DSS. When clinically significant D/major level pDDIs were considered, pDDIs were detected in approximately one-third of the patients. When total pDDIs were considered, pDDIs were detected in more than half of the patients. A weak correlation was detected between D/major level pDDI and total pDDI numbers among different DSS.

Prevalence and profile of potential drug-drug interactions

The current literature shows that studies aimed at defining and evaluating pDDIs in the NICU are limited compared to other populations. Different studies have reported that the prevalence of pDDIs in NICU patients ranges from 0 to 70% [11, 12, 18–21]. In addition, studies have significant differences regarding the frequency and severity of pDDIs [11, 12, 18–21]. In the studies mentioned, the mean pDDI numbers were reported as 3.5 ± 1.6 per patient, and the median was between 0 and 2 [18, 20, 22]. Yalçın et al. and Rosen et al. reported X/contraindicated and D/major level pDDIs as 12.5–37.5% in their studies [12, 21]. However, it can be concluded that X/contraindicated level interactions are generally observed at very low rates in the NICU [11, 12, 21]. In some studies, the number of patients with pDDI and the major level pDDI rates per patient were higher compared to this study. This difference may be related to the lower gestational age, lower birth weight, and higher number of prescribed medications in the patients included in the study [11, 20]. No pDDI at the contraindicated level according to both DSS was encountered in this study. However, approximately one-third of patients had D/major level pDDIs according to both DSS.

According to both DSS, this study found the gentamicin-vancomycin combination to be the most common pDDI pair at a major level. In addition, vancomycin, gentamicin, fentanyl, and fluconazole were the most common drugs causing pDDIs. Although there are differences in the drugs and interaction pairs causing pDDIs depending on the drugs used in the studies, the general profile is similar [11, 12]. Aminoglycosides and antiepileptic drugs are frequently used drug groups in treatment protocols in NICU [12, 23–29]. In addition, fentanyl is generally the preferred opioid agent in this patient group and is frequently administered with midazolam and phenobarbital [23]. In this study, pDDI pairs containing aminoglycosides/antibacterials, opioids, and antiepileptic drugs were frequently detected. The profile of agents causing pDDIs in the NICU is generally similar due to the limited drug spectrum. However, differences in the DSS used to assess drug interactions lead to differences in the reported pDDI pairs. For example, in this study, major pDDIs were detected between hepatitis B vaccine-acetaminophen and hepatitis B vaccine-dexamethasone, according to the Lexidrug database. However, these pDDIs were not included in the Micromedex database. This difference highlights that DSS may have different information sources, evaluation processes, and grouping approaches.

Risk factors for potential drug-drug interactions

The diversity of databases used to assess pDDIs in the NICU may be one of the main reasons for the differences in reported pDDI rates. DSS such as Lexidrug and Micromedex are frequently used in these assessments [7, 18, 20–22]. In addition, differences in medication use, number of medications prescribed, and updates to protocols and treatment guidelines across centers are likely to contribute to this variability [11, 18, 22, 30].

Analyses of pDDI risk factors in the literature have highlighted several contributors, including polypharmacy, low birth weight, lower gestational age, number of medications used, length of hospital stay, and the type of DSS employed in pDDI assessments [11, 12, 18–22, 24–26]. In the NICU, polypharmacy typically arises in the context of multiple or complex disease states and is known to increase the risk of pDDIs associated with adverse drug reactions [22, 27]. In detail, the total number of anti-infective drugs, the overall drug count, use of opioids, and exposure to central nervous system (CNS) agents—including sedatives, analgesics, and antiepileptics—have been identified as risk factors for pDDIs in NICU patients [6, 11, 12, 22, 28, 29].

In this study, in parallel with the literature, polypharmacy, and situations requiring more drug use, such as early gestational age before 34 weeks, were determined as factors that increase the risk of pDDI [18, 30, 31]. The increase in the risk of pDDIs due to polypharmacy can be considered an inherent consequence. On the other hand, the simultaneous use of anti-infectives with other agents used in the NICU potentiates the risk of pDDIs due to their narrow therapeutic index, complex pharmacokinetics, and frequent involvement in enzyme inhibition or renal elimination pathways [28]. pDDIs regarding respiratory and central nervous system suppression potential, which are commonly encountered among antiepileptics, sedatives, and opioids, are expected pDDIs [30, 32].

Evaluation of potential drug-drug interactions with different decision support systems

No study comparing NICU patients concerning pDDIs via different DSS was found in the literature reviews. However, there are studies evaluating Micromedex, Lexidrug, Medscape, and Drugs.com databases, which are widely used in the evaluation of pDDIs in terms of compliance in different populations [13–15]. Studies in which different compliance degrees were expressed as weak-strong between different DSS emphasized to healthcare professionals that pDDIs should be checked in multiple databases [33–36]. Differences in the working principles of different DSS and the characteristics of patient populations lead to variability in the evaluation of pDDIs. Therefore, instead of relying solely on DSSs with limited reliability and concordance, it is recommended that multidisciplinary healthcare teams—particularly those including clinical pharmacists with expertise in neonatal or pediatric pharmacotherapy—take an active role in the evaluation and management of pDDIs in NICUs.

It is among the recommendations to cross-reference more than one database before making decisions regarding patient care due to the existence of various databases in which pDDIs are defined inconsistently [37]. Furthermore, due to the inconsistency of DSS, developing a knowledge base for pDDIs requires proprietary database information in conjunction with clinical opinion [36]. The inconsistency of DSS suggests that relying on a single database to identify pDDIs may be misleading [38]. This study found no significant concordance between the D/major level pDDIs considered clinically significant for both DSS. As a natural consequence, the number of common pDDI pairs between both DSS was less than the total number of pDDI pairs. Similarly, when the pDDI pairs of the two DSS were evaluated by the clinical pharmacist and the neonatologist in this study, most pDDIs were not considered clinically significant. The pDDIs that were not considered significant among the pDDI pairs had a theoretical basis for predicting the possibility of an adverse event [22]. Similarly, in the study by Yalçın et al., although pDDIs were identified in approximately one-third of the patients, only 9.2% were considered to involve clinically relevant drug-drug interactions [12].

It is not easy to classify pDDIs according to the various levels. Furthermore, interpreting the results of pDDIs according to the severity level can be misleading. Even drug combinations labeled as contraindicated may not be absolute contraindications. Various reviews have reported that the possible outcomes of the same pDDI pairs can be significantly different and may contribute to clinician alert fatigue. Furthermore, similar to this study, it has been reported that concordance between different DDSs commonly used to control pDDIs is weak [39–41]. Therefore, a concerted effort is needed to identify the pDDIs with the highest clinical significance to design effective strategies to manage pDDIs [39]. Clinicians may be at risk of unnecessarily avoiding certain drugs due to alerts from clinically insignificant pDDIs, which can potentially compromise the effectiveness of treatment. On the other hand, there may be inconsistencies between clinical practice-based information and DSS-based pDDI information in terms of severity rankings and recommended actions to manage pDDIs [38]. For these reasons, efforts should be made to focus on pDDIs of more clinical importance [42, 43]. Collaboration between clinicians and clinical pharmacists can be recommended in managing pDDIs and preventing their harmful consequences [2, 7].

In this study, when the pDDI numbers of DSS were compared at all levels, a very weak level of consistency was found. According to the Micromedex database, more pDDIs were reported in major-level interactions than in Lexidrug. Regarding total pDDIs, the Lexidrug database reported more pDDIs than Micromedex. This is an essential finding in explaining the inconsistency between DSS. In Cohen’s kappa analysis, a high level of agreement was found between DSS, while in Kendall’s W analysis, the agreement rates were observed to be low due to the use of data with a broader range of numbers. This situation reflects the differences in the approaches of DSS in defining and reporting pDDIs.

Clinical relevance and future directions

One of the major challenges in evaluating pDDIs in neonates is the extrapolation of interaction data from adult populations. Many DDIs—such as nephrotoxicity from NSAIDs or cytochrome P450-mediated interactions—are based on pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties that do not necessarily apply to neonates, especially due to immature organ systems and low enzymatic activity (e.g., CYP2C19) [1, 2]. This may result in the overestimation or underestimation of clinical risks when using standard DSS tools in this age group.

In our study, inconsistencies were observed not only between DSS outputs but also between these outputs and expert consensus. For example, pDDIs such as those between dexamethasone and hepatitis B vaccine were classified as major by one software but not listed in the other, and were not considered clinically significant by our clinical team. This highlights the urgent need for more reliable, evidence-based tools.

Given these limitations, we suggest that future work should aim to develop and validate a neonatal-specific DDI decision support system, ideally informed by neonatal pharmacology, expert consensus, and real-world outcome data. Until then, it is crucial to involve both clinical pharmacists and neonatologists in DDI assessment to ensure that decision-making is both evidence-based and contextually appropriate for this high-risk population. Moreover, future multicenter, prospective, and blinded clinical trials comparing various DSS tools in NICU settings are essential to establish their reliability, concordance, and clinical applicability in real-world neonatal practice.

Limitations and strengths

One of the main limitations of this study is its single-center design and relatively small sample size. Furthermore, due to the retrospective nature of the study, the clinical consequences of the identified pDDIs could not be directly evaluated.

To mitigate potential bias in data collection, patients with missing or incomplete prescription records were excluded from the analysis. Nevertheless, as with all retrospective studies, the possibility of unrecorded interventions or undocumented clinical factors cannot be entirely ruled out.

Risk factor analysis was conducted using univariate Chi-square tests with odds ratio estimation. Therefore, potential confounding variables were not adjusted for, and multivariate modeling was not performed. Future studies with larger sample sizes should consider multivariate analysis to better control for confounding.

A notable strength of this study is its focus on comparing different DSS within a specialized NICU population. Despite being conducted at a single center, the findings may be considered generalizable to similar clinical settings nationwide.

Conclusion

In conclusion, pDDIs were observed in a significant proportion of neonates in the NICU. This study demonstrated weak concordance between the two DSSs used, highlighting notable differences in how pDDIs are defined and classified. These findings emphasize that reliance on a single DSS may lead to inconsistent and potentially misleading assessments.

The results underscore the importance of a multidisciplinary approach, particularly the involvement of clinical pharmacists and neonatologists, in interpreting DSS alerts. It was also observed that many pDDIs flagged by the software were not considered clinically relevant, reinforcing the need to focus on interactions with true clinical significance and to minimize unnecessary alerts that contribute to alert fatigue.

To improve pDDI management in neonatology, future efforts should aim to develop neonatal-specific DSS tools that incorporate age-appropriate pharmacological parameters, allow severity-based filtering of alerts, and integrate clinician feedback. Until such tools are available, combining DSS outputs with expert clinical judgment remains the most prudent strategy for safe and effective medication use in the NICU.
