More is more? The role of surgeon in the volume-outcome relationship: an Italian population-based cohort study
Authors: Salvatore Soldati, Paola Colais, Marina Davoli, Paola Michelozzi, Luigi Pinnarelli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionThe relationship between surgical volume and clinical outcomes is complex and varies considerably across surgical specialties. While the role of hospital volume is well-established, the specific contribution of surgeon-level volume and the interaction between these two factors are less clear.ObjectivesThis study investigates the association between surgeon volume and clinical outcomes for surgeons performing breast-conserving surgery for malignant breast cancer, laparoscopic cholecystectomy and surgery for malignant colon cancer.MethodsData from the Lazio Region’s health information systems (2020–2023) were analysed. Primary outcomes included 120-day reinterventions after breast-conserving surgery for breast cancer, 30-day complications after laparoscopic cholecystectomy and 30-day mortality after colon cancer surgery. The association between surgeon volume and outcomes was examined for all surgeons performing at least five procedures annually in Lazio hospitals. A stratified analysis was conducted to assess the impact of hospital volume on the surgeon-volume-outcome relationship, comparing outcomes between surgeons in low-volume and high-volume hospitals. Fractional polynomial and segmented regression models were employed to identify non-linear relationships and potential breakpoints in the surgeon volume-outcome association.ResultsHigher-volume surgeons demonstrated significantly improved outcomes across all three procedures, including lower reintervention rates, fewer complications and reduced mortality. This positive association was further amplified for surgeons practising in high-volume hospitals. Graphical analysis, which involved stratifying the volume-outcome relationship for individual surgeons by hospital volume (high vs low), strongly suggested a synergistic effect between surgeon and hospital volume, clearly illustrating how the benefits of higher surgeon volume were further amplified in high-volume settings.ConclusionsThese findings underscore the critical role of both surgeon-level and hospital-level factors in determining surgical outcomes. Optimising patient care requires a comprehensive approach that considers both individual surgeon performance and the overall quality of the healthcare system. Future research should focus on elucidating the underlying mechanisms driving these associations to inform the development of strategies for improving surgical care delivery.Keywords: SURGERY, Hospitals, EPIDEMIOLOGY, Adult oncology, Breast surgery

Introduction

The study of the relationship between the surgical volumes and clinical outcomes is a complex topic and requires an accurate evaluation of all the factors that can have an impact on outcomes. The volume of surgical procedures can be measured both at the hospital level, as the number of procedures performed within the hospital, and at the surgeon level, measured as the number of procedures performed by a specific surgeon.1

There is strong evidence of the relationship between high volumes of activity and clinical outcomes, and this relationship is more evident in highest volume hospitals.2 In fact, the hospitals with the highest surgical volume could have more experienced staff, better resources and more defined clinical pathways.3 However, in some fields of surgery, the relationship between surgical procedure volumes and treatment outcomes is still a matter of debate.4 In addition, this relationship must be analysed in different clinical contexts, and it is crucial to test the potential interaction between the hospital volume and surgeon volume.

In fact, it cannot be ruled out that even in hospitals with a low volume of activity, there are experienced staff or high-quality resources.5 Furthermore, it is often difficult to consider the effect of hospital or surgeon volume on the clinical outcomes with respect to other factors, such as clinical characteristics of the patients. Finally, it could be difficult to measure the volume of procedures performed by a specific surgeon, because this information could not be correctly or fully documented.6

In Italy, the relationship between the volumes of surgical procedures performed in hospitals and outcomes has been studied, and there is strong evidence of a positive association.7 The relationship with the volumes of procedures performed by the surgeon and the possible interaction with hospital volumes remains to be further investigated.

Consequently, insights into the relationship between hospital or surgeon volumes and health outcomes are needed and may be crucial to improving the quality of care delivered to patients and the quality of healthcare services.

This study aims to evaluate the relationship between surgeon volumes and health outcomes and the potential interaction with the volume class of the hospital in which the surgical intervention is performed. We focused on abdominal and oncological surgery, referring to conditions for which the association of clinical outcomes and hospital volumes has strong evidence in literature and has been tested in the Italian context.7

Material and methods

This study was based on data from the Lazio Region Health Information Systems (a central Italian region whose capital city is Rome), which cover over 5 million inhabitants in Italy. Specifically, we collected data from the Hospital Information System (HIS), the Healthcare Emergency Information System (HEIS) and the Mortality Information System (MIS).

Discharge abstracts, from both public and private hospitals, were routinely collected by the HIS and contained patient demographic characteristics (gender, age, place of birth, residence, etc.), admission and discharge dates (date of admission, code of admitting hospital, admitting ward/specialty division, origin of patient, etc.), in-hospital transfers (dates and wards/divisions involved), discharge (date, discharging ward, type of discharge, etc.), clinical characteristics of the patient at discharge, and procedures performed during the hospital stay (International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM)).

The HEIS database included all visits occurring in Emergency Departments of the Lazio region and collected: patient demographics, admission information, visit and discharge dates and hours, ICD-9-CM diagnosis at discharge, reported symptoms on arrival, status at discharge (eg, dead, hospitalised or discharged at home) and triage score.

The MIS database collected data on the death of all residents of the Lazio region from all regional Health Information Systems and from Regional Tax Register. The data extracted from the information system were anonymised, so the approval of an ethics committee was not required.

These administrative datasets are highly representative of the Lazio region’s population and healthcare events, thanks to their mandatory and exhaustive nature. They also provide substantially complete data for key variables essential to our analysis like age, sex, event date, primary diagnosis, surgical procedures, performing surgeon identifier and outcomes. This inherent completeness is because these variables are crucial for the dataset’s primary administrative and billing functions. As a result, our study can accurately reflect real-world epidemiological and healthcare utilisation dynamics at a population level.

Data from different information systems were integrated using a deterministic record linkage procedure based on a unique and anonymous subject identifier. This process allowed us to create a chronological, demographic, residential, clinical and healthcare-related patient profile.

The study protocols from the Regional Outcome Evaluation Programme (P.Re.Val.E.)8 were used to define these three clinical outcomes.

Moreover, for these conditions, adjusted risks of complications, mortality and reintervention per surgeon were estimated. Previous research suggested that a widely accepted threshold for defining surgical expertise was an annual volume of at least 50 procedures.9

All surgeons who performed less than five interventions per year were excluded from the analyses, because it was considered a very low volume of activity per year. Statistical analyses were performed per surgeon.

Risk adjustment methods were applied to take into account heterogeneously distributed across the surgeon of patient characteristics.10

For each study outcome, in collaboration with participating clinicians, we identified a set of risk factors for use in risk adjustment models. These conditions were assessed during the index hospitalisation and in hospitalisations within the 2 years preceding it. The full list of the definition criteria for each comorbidity or disease severity is published as supplementary material (online supplemental table 1). We adjusted the risks for the potential confounders listed in online supplemental tables 2–4, according to the specific outcome of interest.

Potential confounders were selected from the full list of comorbidities together with age and sex, using a bootstrap stepwise procedure, a fully data-driven approach, to determine which factors were associated with the outcomes of interest. Using this approach, 1000 replicated bootstrap samples were selected from the original dataset. A bootstrap sample is a sample of the same size as the original dataset chosen with replacement. Thus, a given subject in the original dataset may be selected multiple times, only once or not at all in a specific bootstrap sample. A stepwise procedure, using thresholds of p=0.05 for variable selection and elimination, was applied to each replicated sample. To avoid overparameterisation and improve estimator efficiency,11 12 only the factors selected in at least 50% of the procedures were included in the final ‘predictive’ model. These risk adjustment models were specific to the study population and allowed for adjusted outcome measures for comparison between surgeons.

For each outcome under study, multivariate logistic regression models without intercepts and centred covariates were applied to estimate adjusted surgeons’ log odds of outcome.

This approach enabled us to compare the outcome of a given surgeon with that of all the other surgeons and with the entire population under study.13,15

The relationship between surgeon activity volume and adjusted risk of outcome for each condition was obtained using fractional polynomials which constitute a flexible parametric approach for identifying the nonlinear functions that best fit the data under study.16

Each function was estimated using a system of weights defined on the basis of the number of cases treated by each surgeon. This methodology allows for the reduction of the potential distortion of the functional form for the high volumes of activity that are calculated on the basis of a small number of surgeons. The existence of breakpoints in the non-linear relationship between activity volumes and outcome was assessed with segmented models.17 These models are used when a relationship can be thought of as representable by two or more lines connected at unknown points, reducing the non-linear context to a linear context. The breakpoint is searched for through an iterative algorithm which, starting from an initial point, tests the possible changepoints working on the difference between the angular coefficients (inclination of the broken lines). The breakpoint can be interpreted as the value below or above which there is a statistically significant change in the slope of the curve, that is, the threshold value where the effect of the covariate changes.

Finally, the relationship between surgeon volume and outcome stratified by the volume of activity of the hospital where the surgery was performed was calculated to evaluate whether facility volumes influence surgeons’ outcomes.

High-volume hospitals were categorised by applying specific annual intervention thresholds tailored to each surgical outcome. For the proportion of resections within 120 days after breast-conserving surgery for breast cancer, hospitals with a volume of 135 or more interventions per year were classified as high-volume. In the context of 30-day complications after laparoscopic cholecystectomy, high-volume hospitals were those performing at least 100 interventions annually. Lastly, for 30-day mortality after surgery for colon cancer, hospitals conducting 50 interventions per year were considered high-volume.

These thresholds used to define high-volume hospitals in our study were based on national standards established by the Italian Ministry of Health, as outlined in Decree No 70 of 2 April 2015. This regulation defined minimum activity volumes for specific surgical procedures to ensure quality and safety in hospital care. Specifically, the decree identified minimum annual procedure volumes for various surgeries and treatments, based on evidence linking higher volumes to better clinical outcomes.

Statistical analyses were carried out using Stata software, version 15 (StataCorp 2015. Stata Statistical Software: Release 15. College Station, TX: Stata Corp LP) and R statistical software.

More specifically, 5772 interventions were performed by 235 surgeons in 2020, 6345 by 236 surgeons in 2021, 6627 by 222 surgeons in 2022 and 6891 by 198 surgeons in 2023. In particular, the incidence of reintervention at 120 days was high among surgeons who performed fewer than 25 procedures annually, with this threshold volume identified as a breakpoint in this research. We repeated the analysis on the relationship between volume and outcome, examining surgeons separately based on the volume of their respective hospitals. Specifically, we differentiated between surgeons working in high-volume hospitals (at least 135 interventions annually) and those operating in low-volume facilities (fewer than 135 surgeries).

From 2020 to 2023, we observed 19 high-volume hospitals and 67 low-volume hospitals (in at least 1 year of the study). The former accounted for 88% (n=22 638) of the overall surgical interventions for breast cancer performed in the Lazio region. Mainly for this reason, in figure 1A, we noted that the relationship between volume of activity and 120-day reintervention for surgeons operating in high-volume facilities has remained nearly consistent compared with what was observed in figure 1T. Conversely, such association seems to be lacking for clinicians operating in low-volume hospitals. As illustrated in figure 1B, a notable decrease in the adjusted rate was observed up to 50/70 interventions. However, beyond this threshold, there was no additional reduction with the increasing surgeon volume. On the contrary, it appeared to suggest an increase of 120-days reintervention rate.

Surgery for laparoscopic cholecystectomy

Between 2020 and 2022, a total of 25 449 laparoscopic cholecystectomy procedures were performed by 2228 surgeons in Lazio region hospitals. In 2023, 9950 procedures were carried out by 777 surgeons, bringing the total number of procedures to 35 399. Notably, a mere 5% of surgeons, on average, executed a minimum of 45 interventions annually, accounting for 25% of all surgical procedures.

An association between surgeon volume and 30-day complications was identified (figure 2T). Specifically, we observed a significant increase in complications at 30 days post-surgery for clinicians who conducted a lower number of than 20 procedures per year. This volume cut-off has been identified as the breakpoint. Moreover, the adjusted proportion of complications significantly decreased up to 45/50 procedures (complications decreased from 22% to 5%) and continued to show a slight decrease with increasing surgeon volume until stabilisation. Highlighting as depicted in figure 2A, an almost identical association was obtained when analysing the relationship only for surgeons who operated in high-volume facilities (more than 100 interventions annually). As seen in figure 2B, we found a slightly different relationship between the clinician’s volume and complications within 30 days for surgeons who operated in hospitals with fewer than 100 surgeries per year. Specifically, the proportion of complications went down until about 45 procedures a year, continued to decrease until around 50–65 interventions and then started to go up as the surgeon volume increased.

Surgery for colon cancer

Between 2020 and 2023, a total of 10 257 surgical interventions for colon cancer were performed in Lazio hospitals. More specifically, we observed 2310 surgeries conducted by 422 surgeons in 2020. This number increased to 2505, 2.693 and 2749 procedures in 2021, 2022 and 2023, respectively, performed by 410, 415 and 436 surgeons. While the number of surgeons remained relatively stable, a small subset of high-volume surgeons, representing only 7% of the total, performed more than 20 procedures annually, contributing to 40% of all surgical interventions. In 2020, only three surgeons achieved the benchmark of performing at least 45 procedures. This number doubled in subsequent years, with six surgeons achieving this target in 2021, 2022 and 2023.

An association between surgeon volume and 30-day mortality was discernible in our study (figure 3T). Significantly, the risk of mortality decreased with increasing surgical volume, reaching a breakpoint for this relationship at approximately 15 procedures per year. Beyond this point, the risk continued to decline, stabilising at around 60 annual procedures per surgeon. The same type of association was evident from figure 3A, where we stratified only the surgeons linked to high-volume hospitals (at least 50 interventions annually). Interestingly, even among surgeons in low-volume facilities, the stratified analysis demonstrated a volume-outcome relationship comparable to the overall trend analysis (figure 3B), deviating from the pattern observed in the two previous procedures. These findings suggest a potentially reduced impact of facility volume on the individual surgeon’s volume-outcome relationship in this specific context.

Discussion

This study explored the relationship between surgical volume, at both the surgeon and institutional levels, and patient outcomes for three specific procedures: colon cancer surgery, breast cancer surgery and laparoscopic cholecystectomy. The findings demonstrated a significant association between higher surgical volumes and improved clinical outcomes across all three conditions and established an association between surgeon expertise and improved health outcomes. Surgeons with greater experience showed lower complication rates and better prognoses for their patients. These results align with a growing body of evidence underscoring the ‘volume-outcome relationship’ in surgery.18

Surgeon volume and outcomes

High surgeon volumes were consistently associated with better outcomes. For colon cancer surgery, increased surgical experience correlated with reduced postoperative complications, lower mortality rates and improved long-term survival.19 20 These findings are supported by existing literature that attributes better performance to refined technical skills, improved intraoperative decision-making and familiarity with complex cases. Similarly, in breast cancer surgery, higher-volume surgeons were more likely to achieve complete resections with negative margins, reducing the need for additional procedures.18 For laparoscopic cholecystectomy, higher surgeon volumes correlated with fewer conversions to open surgery, shorter operative times and lower complication rates, particularly in complex cases such as acute cholecystitis.21

Institutional volume and outcomes

The study also revealed that the institutional volume plays a critical role in determining patient outcomes. High-volume centres demonstrated superior results, even when accounting for individual surgeon volume. This was particularly evident for colon and breast cancer surgeries, where comprehensive care often involves multidisciplinary teams, advanced diagnostic tools and specialised postoperative care.22 Institutional volume was a key factor in fostering robust perioperative protocols, efficient coordination among healthcare providers and access to advanced technologies. For laparoscopic cholecystectomy, high-volume hospitals were better equipped to manage complications and provide timely interventions.19 A high-performing hospital setting, with adequate resources and a high volume of procedures, can contribute to improved patient outcomes.

Combined effects of surgeon and institutional volumes

Interestingly, the interaction between surgeon and institutional volumes amplified the benefits. High-volume surgeons operating in high-volume centres achieved the best outcomes, suggesting a synergistic effect.18 Conversely, low-volume surgeons in high-volume institutions often performed better than high-volume surgeons in low-volume centres, highlighting the importance of institutional resources and organisational support. These findings support the hypothesis that both individual and institutional factors play an important role in determining surgical outcomes and emphasise the need for policies that centralise complex surgical procedures in high-volume settings while ensuring adequate training and credentialing for surgeons.

Limitations and future research

The potential impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on our study, in terms of both surgical volume and postoperative outcomes, warranted careful consideration. The initial phase of the pandemic in 2020 significantly disrupted healthcare systems globally, leading to a notable reduction in surgical volumes across various specialties. Our study, focusing on surgical interventions within the Lazio region between 2020 and 2023, corroborated this trend. We observed a consistent pattern of decreased surgical volumes in 2020 for breast cancer surgery, laparoscopic cholecystectomy and colon cancer surgery, followed by a gradual recovery in the subsequent years (as detailed in table 1). Regarding postoperative outcomes, a slightly higher early reintervention rate after conservative breast cancer surgery was observed in 2020 (5.8%) compared with subsequent stable years (4.6%). Conversely, 30-day complication rates after cholecystectomy (2020: 2.2% to 2023: 1.6%) and 30-day mortality after colon cancer surgery (range 4.0%–5.0%) remained relatively stable across the study period despite volume changes. In conclusion, while the pandemic reduced surgical volumes in 2020, clinical outcomes for these procedures remained relatively consistent throughout the 2020–2023 period. These data indicate that the integrity of the procedure’s safety profile was generally preserved despite the changes in surgical volume, although the slightly elevated re-resection rate in breast cancer in 2020 warrants further investigation to fully elucidate the potential underlying factors.

While this study underscores the importance of volume, it also raises questions about other factors influencing outcomes, such as hospital setting and available resources. Moreover, it is important to acknowledge that the use of repeated measures for individual surgeons over the 4year study period could introduce autocorrelation within the datasets, potentially affecting the analysis of trends. However, the larger dataset helped mitigate this issue by providing more data points for the regression models, thus improving the accuracy and reliability of our predictions. Future research should focus on resource-limited settings to determine how these findings translate across diverse healthcare systems.

Implications for healthcare policy and practice

These results provide valuable insights for policymakers seeking to establish minimum volume thresholds for individual surgeons. When combined with existing hospital volume thresholds, these guidelines can help to improve the quality and safety of care. Our data suggest that below certain volume thresholds, the number of procedures performed has a negative impact on health outcomes. Policymakers should consider strategies to regionalise complex surgeries to high-volume centres while promoting continuous professional development for surgeons. Additionally, patients could benefit from transparent reporting of surgical outcomes by volume, enabling informed decision-making. In clinical practice, these findings support the establishment of minimum volume thresholds for surgeons and institutions to enhance patient safety and outcomes.20 22 Moreover, reorganising healthcare services based on volume could help to address the fragmentation of surgical care and reduce disparities in access to effective treatments.
