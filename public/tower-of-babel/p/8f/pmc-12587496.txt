Immediate versus delayed weight bearing for fractures of the pelvis, acetabulum, distal femur, and proximal and distal tibia: a feasibility randomized controlled trial
Authors: Peter N Mittwede, Vivian Li, David O Okhuereigbe, Alice C Bell, Casey Loudermilk, Haley K Demyanovich, Joshua E Lawrence, Kristin E Turner, Murali Kovvur, Jordan P Brand, Daniel J Cunningham, Daniel J Johnson, Aresh Sepehri, Marcus F Sciadini, Jason W Nascone, Mark J Gage, Eric C Hempen, Nathan N O’Hara, Gerard P Slobogean, Robert V O’Toole
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly weight bearing is frequently allowed and encouraged following fixation of hip and femoral and tibial shaft fractures, while pelvis, acetabulum, and lower extremity periarticular fractures are often prescribed 6–12 weeks of restricted weight bearing. Our objective was to perform a feasibility randomized trial of immediate versus delayed weight bearing as tolerated (WBAT) for fractures of the pelvis, acetabulum, distal femur, and proximal and distal tibia.MethodsA parallel two-arm pilot feasibility trial was performed at one academic level I trauma center in the United States. Patients with a fracture of the pelvis, acetabulum, distal femur, proximal tibia, or distal tibia were eligible. Patients were randomized to either immediate WBAT or delayed (≥ 6 weeks) weight bearing after surgery. Our feasibility criteria included the following: (1) 25% overall enrollment (surgeon and patient agreement to participate in 50% eligible cases), (2) > 90% follow-up at 3 months), (3) 100% correct documentation of weight bearing status, and (4) 100% correct documentation of secondary outcome (reoperation).ResultsSurgeons allowed inclusion of 64/133 (48.1%) of eligible patients (by fracture type, 76% of pelvis; 43% of acetabulum; 38% of distal femur; 45% of proximal tibia (39% of tibial plateau); and 56% of distal tibia fractures). 50/64 (78%) of these patients agreed to participate in the study, for an overall enrollment of 38% (50/133). The follow-up rate was 46/50 (92%) at the 3-month follow-up. Correct documentation of postoperative weight bearing status and the secondary outcome of reoperation was achieved in all cases 50/50 (100%). There were two reoperations (one hardware failure and one wound infection) in the immediate WBAT group, and none in the delayed weight bearing group.ConclusionAll four of the feasibility criteria were met. While we did not meet our target of 50% of patients whom surgeons were willing to randomize, we did exceed the target of 25% overall patient enrollment. This feasibility trial demonstrates that a randomized controlled trial of early versus delayed weight bearing would be feasible for certain fracture types, and these findings will be used to help design future studies in this realm.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05595148.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40814-025-01708-3.Keywords: Early weight bearing, Fractures, Feasibility, Randomized controlled trial

Background

Weight bearing status is an important component of the postoperative care of orthopaedic trauma patients. Early weight bearing is associated with a number of benefits following fixation of hip and long bone shaft fractures [1–3], and patients are often allowed to be weight bearing as tolerated (WBAT) immediately following these procedures. The benefits of early weight bearing include shorter length of stay, more rapid fracture healing, and lower mortality rates [1–3]. However, weight bearing restrictions are typically employed for patients in the early postoperative period following fixation of many pelvis, acetabulum, distal femur, and proximal and distal tibia fractures, particularly for intra-articular fractures. This is due in large part to the potential risks of early hardware failure or loss of fixation prior to bone healing. However, delayed weight bearing after fracture fixation has been shown to be associated with an increased rate of various complications, delayed improvement in functional outcomes, and delayed radiographic bone healing [4–6].

The risks associated with delayed weight bearing have encouraged clinicians to consider the safety of early weight bearing after fixation of certain lower extremity fractures. Retrospective studies have shown low rates of hardware failure with early weight bearing following fixation of fractures of the pelvis [7, 8], acetabulum [9], distal femur [10–13], proximal tibia [14–16], and distal tibia [17]. Randomized controlled trials with ankle [18] and distal femur [19] fractures have shown no difference in rates of early hardware failure between early and delayed weight bearing. In addition to evaluating whether existing fixation constructs are safe for early weight bearing following surgery, various investigators have also focused on increasing the stability of fixation constructs so patients may safely bear weight after surgery. Examples include the trend to fix distal femur fractures with dual-plate or nail-plate constructs or to provide pelvis fractures with more fixation than has been utilized in the past.

We designed a feasibility trial to address whether a larger trial of immediate versus delayed weight bearing following fixation of pelvis, acetabulum, and periarticular and short-segment distal femur, proximal tibia, and distal tibia fractures may be possible. In particular with this feasibility study, we focused on surgeon and patient willingness to participate in such a trial, given that orthopaedic surgeons have historically been unwilling to allow immediate weight bearing after surgically stabilizing these injuries. Another goal was to focus on if assigned weight bearing status would be accurate in all postoperative clinical documentation given the traditional restrictions in postoperative weight bearing for these injury types. For future studies, we are ultimately interested in whether the benefits of earlier mobilization and weight bearing for patients outweight the potential risks, particularly given the high amount of variability that exists with these fractures with regard to the severity of bony and soft tissue injury.

Methods

Study design and setting

A study timeline is shown in the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) diagram in Fig. 1, and a CONSORT checklist is attached. This parallel two-arm feasibility trial was conducted at one level I academic trauma center in the Department of Orthopaedic Surgery at R Adams Cowley Shock Trauma Center at the University of Maryland in the United States. The study was approved by the center’s Institutional Review Board (#HP-00103274). The trial was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov prior to the start of patient enrollment.

CONSORT diagram showing timeline for the study

Participants

Patients above the age of 18 with a fracture of the pelvis, acetabulum, distal femur, proximal tibia (including tibial plateau), or distal tibia (excluding pilon) treated operatively were eligible for inclusion. Intra-articular fractures were eligible, and fractures of the distal femur, proximal tibia, and distal tibia were eligible if they extended into the proximal and distal segments of these bones based on the AO definition (a square with sides the length of the widest part of the epiphysis) [20]. We did not include tibial plafond fractures, as it was determined that surgeons would not be willing to randomize these patients prior to the beginning of the trial.

Patients were excluded from the study if language barriers made it unable for us to obtain consent, if their follow-up was going to be at another institution, if they would not be able to comply with weight bearing restrictions (e.g., due to intellectual disability), or if they could not provide consent. Those who had a fracture of the ipsilateral extremity that the surgeon did not want to weight bear (e.g., a Lisfranc fracture-dislocation on the same extremity as an otherwise eligible posterior wall acetabulum fracture) were also excluded. Patients could only have one eligible fracture to be included in the study.

Screening

If a patient with an eligible fracture received operative fixation, at the end of the case, the surgeon was asked by a member of the clinical or research teams if he/she would allow inclusion into the study. The screening form was built into the electronic brief operative note so all operative orthopaedic trauma patients were screened. If the surgeon refused inclusion, the reason(s) was documented. If the surgeon agreed to include the patient in the study, the patient was then approached by a member of the research team within 72 h of surgery. The patient was left non-weight bearing (NWB) or foot flat weight bearing (FFWB) until study inclusion was determined (based on surgeon discretion, as NWB for the short-segment and periarticular femur and tibia fractures and FFWB for pelvis/acetabulum fractures are the standard postoperative weight bearing statuses at our institution for these injuries).

Group allocation

Patients with an eligible fracture who consented to participate in the study were randomized after surgery per the protocol below to either immediate or delayed weight bearing after surgery. In the immediate weight bearing group, patients were made weight bearing as tolerated (WBAT) within 72 h after definitive fixation, or as soon as enrolled and randomized, while in the delayed weight bearing group, patients were made either NWB or FFWB for 6 or more weeks postoperatively based on surgeon discretion and fracture type. At the 6-week follow-up visit, we allowed surgeon discretion on when to advance weight bearing in the delayed WBAT group, as the study only dictated the weight bearing status during the first 6 weeks after surgery.

By default, patients in the delayed weight bearing group with femur or tibia fractures were assigned to be NWB, and patients with pelvis or acetabulum fractures were assigned to be FFWB (the standard postoperative weight bearing status at our institution for these injuries). NWB at our institution refers to no weight being allowed on the affected extremity during ambulation. FFWB refers to when the patient uses crutches or a walker and places minimal weight on the extremity and uses the extremity only for balance while ambulating. WBAT refers to when therapists allow the patient to bear as much weight as they are able to, either with or without an assistive device, during ambulation and other mobility exercises. In this study we did not assess patient compliance with assigned weight bearing status, but will plan to do so in a future definitive trial. Members of the research team did confirm that the orders for weight bearing status in the orthopaedic surgery and physical therapy notes reflected the assigned group.

Randomization/blinding

If the surgeon allowed inclusion into the study, and the patient agreed to participate, randomization was performed using REDCap software by members of the research team, and the clinical team was informed so that orders for the appropriate weight bearing status could be placed in the chart. Randomization was performed after fixation and patient consent. Randomization was performed by research staff using block randomization based on fracture type and by age (greater than 60 versus 60 years old and younger), with a block size of 4. We chose to block randomize by age because patients who are older and those who are younger often have different mechanisms of injury and often have different potential risks/benefits associated with early and delayed weight bearing. We chose to stratify by fracture type to ensure we had similar numbers of patients randomized to each weight bearing group for each of the five different fracture types enrolled in this study.

Due to the nature of the groups to which the patients were assigned in this study, it was not possible to blind either the patients or surgeons in this study. Also, because a major feasibility criterion involved the assessment of correct documentation of weight bearing status by the research personnel, they were not able to be blinded. In this feasibility trial we were not assessing outcomes other than reoperation, so the potential bias introduced by lack of blinding was felt to be minimal. In a future trial when we are collecting patient reported outcomes, we do plan to blind research personnel collecting this information to minimize potential bias.

Secondary outcome

The secondary outcome of interest for the feasibility trial was reoperation (for any reason). This was monitored by the clinical and research teams, and by an independent medical monitor for up to 12 months. If a reoperation occurred, the reason was documented, and determinations were made whether weight bearing status may have contributed to the reoperation. In a future definitive trial we will also assess mortality, fracture-specific patient-reported outcomes, ambulatory status, and days spent at home.

Sample size justification

Using the approach described by Lewis et al. [21], the feasibility study’s statistical power was based on the primary feasibility of enrollment. Identifying over 120 eligible patients provided over 90% power to ensure that > 50% of patients willing to randomize was statistically greater than our 25% feasibility threshold with a one-sided alpha of 5%. In addition, 50 willing patients provided over 90% power to ensure the > 50% surgeon’s willingness to randomize was statistically significantly greater than our 25% feasibility threshold with a one-sided alpha of 5%. The power calculations were performed using a one-sample test of proportions with the uncertainty calculated using the Wilson score method.

Statistical analyses

The study results were summarized as frequencies and percentages. The confidence intervals were calculated using the Wilson interval method. Given the feasibility nature of the trial, inferential statistics were not used. The above descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data with regard to the feasibility outcomes for enrollment, follow-up, documentation of weight bearing, and documentation of. All calculations were performed using R Version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Feasibility criterion 1—enrollment

During the 11-month enrollment phase, there were 133 eligible patients. A total of 6 orthopaedic trauma surgeons participated in the study. In 64/133 (48.1%) cases, the surgeon allowed inclusion into the study. By individual fracture type, inclusions were as follows: 13 of 17 (76%) pelvis, 13 of 30 (43%) acetabulum, 14 of 37 (38%) distal femur, 15 of 33 (45%) proximal tibia (including 11 of 28 (39%) tibial plateau), and 9 of 16 (56%) distal tibia fractures. The numbers were just below the overall 50% feasibility target for surgeon willingness to randomize and were not met for individual fractures such as those of the acetabulum, distal femur, proximal tibia, and tibial plateau. Feasibility criterion 2—follow-up

Early follow-up rates have been excellent, with 88% (44/50; 95% CI, 76%–95%) at the 6-week follow-up and 92% (46/50; 95% CI, 81%–98%) at the 3-month follow-up. The target of 90% follow-up was met for the 3-month follow-up. Telephone contact was achieved with the 4 patients who were unable to make it in person to their 3-months follow-up due to transportation issues.

Feasibility criterion 3—documentation of weight bearing

All patients 50/50 (100%; 95% CI, 93%–100%) had correct postoperative documentation of weight bearing status while inpatient following randomization to a group, and all patients who presented in person had correct documentation of assigned weight bearing status at their first clinic follow-up.

Feasibility criterion 4—documentation of secondary outcomes

There was 100% correct documentation of the secondary outcome of reoperation for 50/50 of the enrolled patients. We were able to achieve telephone contact with the 4 patients who were unable to make it to their 3-month visit in person to confirm that they did not require a reoperation and were not otherwise having any major issues. There were two reoperations in the immediate WBAT group, and none in the delayed weight bearing group. Both reoperations had surgical site infections and one patient also had early loss of fixation. Our composite outcome of unplanned reoperation occurred in 2 of 50 patients (4%; 95% CI, 0.5%–14%). In the immediate WBAT group, unplanned reoperation occurred in 2/27 patients (7%; 95% CI, 0.9%–24%) and in the delayed weight bearing group, unplanned reoperation occurred in 0/23 (0%; 95% CI, 0%–15%).

The first patient had a severe, contaminated type IIIA open intra-articular distal femur fracture, was randomized to the immediate WBAT group, and had an early reoperation due to infection and loss of reduction. The second patient had a closed left bicondylar tibial plateau fracture and was randomized to the immediate WBAT group, and had an early reoperation due to infection.

Discussion

We designed a pilot trial to evaluate the feasibility of immediate versus delayed WBAT for fractures that surgeons have typically not allowed early weight bearing on, including those of the pelvis, acetabulum, distal femur, proximal tibia (including tibial plateau), and distal tibia (excluding pilon) (Table 3). Inclusion criteria for the above eligible fracture types were purposefully kept broad to evaluate why surgeons may not be willing to enroll their patients into the study. The feasibility criteria focused on enrollment, follow-up, and correct documentation of weight bearing status and the secondary outcome of reoperation. The four feasibility criteria were met, including the overall target enrollment rate of 25%, though surgeons were willing to enroll just under 50% of patients with eligible fractures. Interestingly, findings also highlighted certain fracture types and characteristics that contributed to surgeons being unwilling to randomize to immediate versus delayed WBAT, including excessive joint impaction, excessive intra-articular comminution, and poor bone quality. Notably, surgeons were willing to enroll a moderate percentage of tibial plateau fractures (39%). There were no extra-articular short-segment femur or tibia fractures that surgeons were unwilling to randomize.

Regarding the remaining feasibility criteria, early findings suggested excellent follow-up rates and accurate documentation of the weight bearing status of patients in both the inpatient setting and during clinic follow-ups. Documentation of the secondary outcome (all-cause reoperation) has also been accurate. In total, two reoperations took place. One reoperation occurred for a patient who had a grossly contaminated, comminuted, intra-articular type IIIA open distal femur fracture and underwent external fixation and several irrigation and debridement procedures prior to closure and fixation of his fracture with lateral and medial plates. The patient was randomized to immediate WBAT and was readmitted 3 weeks after surgery following the first clinic visit when it was found that there was drainage from the surgical incision and loss of fracture fixation with displacement. The patient underwent a single irrigation and debridement and revision open reduction internal fixation. While early weight bearing may have contributed to the loss of fixation, this patient was likely not a great candidate for this study given the extremely severe nature of the initial injury. The second patient had a closed bicondylar left tibial plateau fracture, and was found to have wound drainage at his first post-up follow up visit. This patient was scheduled for surgery 3.5 weeks after his initial definitive fixation. At this reoperation, the patient had a procedure for incision and drainage with removal of hardware.

Prospective studies on early weight bearing following pelvis, acetabulum, and lower extremity periarticular fractures have been limited. The most commonly studied fracture type recently has been ankle fractures, where randomized controlled trials and prospective cohort studies have generally shown no difference in complication rates between early and delayed weight bearing, and either no difference or improved outcomes with earlier weight bearing [18, 22–25]. Limited numbers of prospective studies on distal femur and distal tibia have had similar findings of no difference in complication rates [19, 26]. Thus, additional high-quality studies are needed to highlight which lower extremity fractures are safe to allow early weight bearing for following fracture fixation, and we believe our feasibility study provides important information that will guide future prospective studies on this topic.

Our study had a number of strengths. We believe this feasibility trial helped identify fractures and fracture characteristics among the included injuries that surgeons were willing to allow early weight bearing for following fixation. Much interest exists in the orthopaedic community in understanding the optimal timing of postoperative weight bearing to optimize recovery of function and bone and soft tissue healing and to minimize orthopaedic and non-orthopaedic complications; studies such as this are required to understand the feasibility of weight bearing trials for injuries where early weight bearing has not traditionally been allowed. This study also provided a complication profile for allowing patients to be WBAT after these injuries where questions may exist regarding the optimal timing of postoperative weight bearing.

The feasibility trial also had a number of limitations. First, we did not focus on a particular injury type, which would have allowed for a more focused approach. However, we wanted to include a number of injury types and patterns where decisional uncertainty exists with regard to the risks and benefits of early weight bearing, so that future trials can focus on the injury (or injuries) of greatest interest and feasibility. Second, the amount of actual weight bearing was not assessed in patients, so the true weight bearing status after hospital discharge is unknown. To measure this in the definitive trial, we will use a questionnaire at the follow-up visit to ask patients about their compliance with their assigned weight bearing status. Finally, there is a possibility that surgeons may have altered fixation strategies (e.g., added additional fixation to increase construct stability) to allow patient inclusion into the study, knowing that the possibility of immediate WBAT was an option. However, our evidence shows that surgeons were comfortable refusing the inclusion of patients into the study, suggesting that fractures were fixed as the surgeon deemed appropriate, and inclusion into the study was allowed only if the treating surgeon felt it was safe for the patient to bear weight early. Third, we did not assess outcomes that may be important to patients in this feasibility study. In the definitive trial, we will plan to assess various patient reported outcomes, mortality, days spent at home postoperatively, and ambulatory status at various time points. Finally, this was a single-center study and was thus limited in its size and external generalizability. Based on the results of this feasibility study, we are currently in the process of planning a similar trial that is multicenter in nature that will focus only on pelvis and acetabulum fractures.

Conclusions

Our feasibility trial results for immediate versus delayed weight bearing for fractures of the pelvis, acetabulum, distal femur, and proximal and distal tibia suggested that while patients are generally willing to participate in such a trial, there are fracture types and characteristics that surgeons may be less willing to randomize. Future studies will provide important information regarding the safety and risks and benefits of allowing patients to be immediately WBAT after fixation of the aforementioned fractures.
