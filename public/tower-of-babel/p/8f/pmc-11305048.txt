Socio-ecological influences on access to abortion care in Costa Rica: a qualitative analysis of key perspectives from clinical and policy stakeholders
Authors: Emma Halper, Blake Erhardt-Ohren, Melissa Cobb, Oscar Hidalgo-Mora, Sebastián Ospina-Henao, Amari O’Bannon, Roger Rochat, Subasri Narasimhan, Anna Newton-Levinson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCosta Rica prohibits abortion except under narrow circumstances to save the pregnant person’s life. The country boasts historically strong support for social policy and human rights, while also presenting a complex and restrictive abortion access landscape. From September 2021 to March 2022, we conducted 23 interviews with obstetrician-gynecologist (OB/GYN) physicians, OB/GYN medical residents, and policy stakeholders to explore the socio-ecological influences on abortion access in Costa Rica. We sampled clinicians and policy stakeholders from the Universidad de Ciencias Médicas listserv through snowball sampling and conducted semi-structured in-depth interviews in Spanish. We identified limited access to comprehensive sexual health education, lack of support from interpersonal networks, inadequate provider knowledge and training, financial and migratory status, and both provider and community stigma as substantial barriers to abortion access. This study addresses a gap in published research around the social determinants of abortion in Costa Rica and sheds light on the attitudes and opinions of the medical and policy stakeholder communities about abortion access. The results highlight the need for expanded access to comprehensive sexual health education, abortion-related training for healthcare providers, and increased programming efforts, such as funding, outreach, and implementation, to ensure comprehensive reproductive health services are available and accessible, especially for vulnerable populations in Costa Rica.

Introduction

Access to comprehensive abortion services is a human right and the lack of access to safe abortion care leads to physical, mental, and social consequences for people with pregnancy capacity globally.1 The Latin America and Caribbean (LAC) region has an array of abortion landscapes across countries, and in the past decade has experienced notable shifts both towards and away from abortion restriction.2 Some of the most significant transformations are a result of substantial feminist activism and grassroots mobilisation activities through the Green Wave, which have resulted in more progressive national abortion legislation in countries like Argentina, Mexico, and Uruguay.3,4 However, there still exist many legal restrictions to abortion access, such as gestational age requirements or abortion permitted only in cases of violence, incest, or fetal impairment. These restrictions impact more than 97% of women of reproductive age in the LAC region.5 Central America, in particular, has some of the world’s most restrictive abortion policies, and abortion is often illegal even in cases of rape, incest, and when the life of the pregnant individual is at risk.6,7

Costa Rica, located in Central America, is in a unique situation: the country is acclaimed for having some of the most progressive social policies in the LAC region, and is considered an “exemplary democracy” and a “distinguished champion of inter-American human rights law”.8 Costa Rica also has highly restricted access to abortion, and has been the focus of several highly visible abortion cases that captured the attention of the international human rights community.8 In recent years, there has been increasing debate around whether to decriminalise abortion in Costa Rica, but it has been met with fierce opposition from conservative and religious groups.

In 1970, Costa Rica’s Penal Code criminalised abortion except “to save the life or health of the woman”.9 In 2019, the Norma Técnica, or “Technical Standard”, was introduced by the Costa Rican government to provide clarity to clinicians about when and how to provide abortion services in diagnosed situations of medical necessity, referred to as “therapeutic abortion”.10,11 The Norma Técnica states that therapeutic abortions are permissible if four requirements are met: the pregnant individual provides consent; the abortion is provided by a medical provider; the service is provided to avoid “endangering” a woman’s life or health; and the danger to the life or health of the woman could not have been avoided by other means.12

Despite the increased attention on abortion access and policy in the LAC region, there are major gaps in the literature related to the abortion policy landscape in Costa Rica. While we do not have data on the safety of abortions in Costa Rica, the Guttmacher Institute estimates that approximately 41% of unwanted pregnancies end in induced abortion in the country.13,14 Due to severely restricted access to legal abortion, this means that many of these are likely to be extra-legal abortions. There is minimal research on abortion access, beliefs, and attitudes in Costa Rica, especially pertaining to vulnerable populations, who may face additional burdens to accessing abortion services. We did not find published literature about the abortion perceptions and service provision experiences of Costa Rica’s medical community or key non-medical policy stakeholders, both of whom are critical to service delivery and who influence national abortion policies. Additionally, the United Nations has identified that abortion data are especially lacking for populations living in situations of vulnerability in Costa Rica, i.e. refugees, migrants, ethnic minorities, and low-income individuals, who face compounded barriers to abortion.15 These populations make up a significant proportion of Costa Rica’s population, with an estimated 356,000 asylum seekers in the country and migrants comprising approximately 15% of total residents.16,17

This analysis is part of a larger study that sought to identify the factors, perspectives and attitudes influencing abortion access in Costa Rica, focusing on two key stakeholder groups: clinicians and policy stakeholders. For this analysis, we sought to describe clinician and policy stakeholder perceptions of influences on access to abortion services in Costa Rica using the social ecological model (SEM), and to identify additional considerations for vulnerable populations. Findings from this study may provide policymakers and activists with more information to inform future abortion policy initiatives in Costa Rica and other countries in the Latin American region with similar abortion contexts.

Methods

We conducted this qualitative study between September 2021 and March 2022 as part of a partnership between Emory University, the Universidad de Ciencias Médicas (UCIMED), and the University of California, Berkeley. The multidisciplinary US and Costa Rican team consisted of US-based sexual and reproductive health services researchers with expertise in abortion access and qualitative research methods, graduate and undergraduate research assistants with fluency in Spanish, Costa Rican clinician researchers and medical students, and a Costa Rican legal expert with experience in women’s health policy. The student researchers functioned as the primary data collection team and were diverse in terms of gender, race/ethnicity, and age. All received training in qualitative research methods, cross-cultural conduct of research, and discussing sensitive topics. The larger study aimed to understand the perceptions of obstetrician-gynecologist (OB/GYN) clinicians and medical residents, and policy stakeholders about abortion in Costa Rica. We selected these two groups as they both have insights into and influence over access to abortion care in Costa Rica, making their perspectives crucial for understanding the complexities of the topic. The study protocol and all materials were approved by the Emory University Institutional Review Board (#STUDY00002394) and the UCIMED Ethics Committee (#586-06-2021) in August 2021. We present this manuscript in accordance with the COREQ checklist.18

We utilised semi-structured in-depth interviews, as opposed to focus group discussions, given the sensitivity of the research topic. We created separate interview guides for the two participant groups, which UCIMED collaborators reviewed for cultural acceptability and relevance. We originally authored the guides in English, translated them into Spanish, and then back-translated into English to ensure no differences in meaning between the source and translated documents. The clinician/resident guide asked about participants’ views of people seeking abortion care, training they had received on abortion service provision and/or reproductive health ethics, and the impact of national abortion policy on patients’ health and well-being. The stakeholder guide explored the impact of current abortion laws on the accessibility of abortion services, and the relationship between health policy and individuals’ sexual and reproductive rights.

We initially recruited clinicians and medical residents through an email announcement distributed through UCIMED listservs, followed by snowball sampling within the UCIMED network for additional recruitment. Practising OB/GYNs and residents pursuing the OB/GYN specialty were eligible for participation. For policy stakeholders, a key informant versed in Costa Rica’s legal and policy environment provided an initial list of connections to the research team, and then we used snowball sampling to recruit additional participants. Policy stakeholders were eligible if they had worked in the past five years in a role or organisation addressing reproductive health laws, policies, or access to health services. To assess a full range of perspectives, we purposively recruited both pro-abortion and anti-abortion stakeholders, such as activists, researchers, consultants, and politicians. We concluded recruitment when we reached concept and meaning saturation for clinicians and stakeholders for the primary analyses, and when we reached out to everyone on the contact list provided by UCIMED.

Prior to interviews, we collected verbal and written informed consent from all participants as well as demographical data. We conducted all interviews in Spanish using online conferencing software and recorded the audio. The interviews were conducted by pairs of researchers, with one person serving as interviewer and the other as note-taker. The interviews were auto-transcribed and translated. The research team reviewed transcripts for quality assurance and modified the English translations when necessary. We analysed interviews using content analysis. We first developed a codebook incorporating inductive codes (grounded in the data) and deductive codes (derived from the interview guide), and refined code definitions through inter-coder assessment and research team discussions. Two groups within the research team coded the de-identified transcripts in pairs using MAXQDA 2022.19 We met weekly as a research team to review coding and discuss emerging themes as well as encourage reflexivity and prevent biases in our analysis. The research team together discussed positionality and reviewed patterns, concepts and themes in the data to reach consensus on the interpretation of the data and on significant contextual details. Initial themes were developed through deep reading and annotation of codes, use of memos, and discussion amongst the full research team to identify patterns within the data. The lead author further refined themes and categorised them according to levels of the SEM,20 which is a framework used to understand overlapping non-medical factors that influence health outcomes. The SEM is often used in sexual and reproductive health (SRH) given the complexity of overlapping, contextual factors that influence SRH behaviours, decision-making, and access to services.21–23

In this manuscript, both OB/GYN physicians and medical residents are referred to as “clinicians” and key policy stakeholders are referred to as “stakeholders”. Vulnerable populations in this manuscript are defined as historically marginalised groups experiencing compounded barriers to healthcare access, such as populations with migratory status, low-income status, or ethnic minorities.

Results

In total, we conducted 23 interviews with 15 clinicians and eight stakeholders (see Table 1). The participants included 16 people who identified as female, and seven people who identified as male. The average age of clinicians was 40 years, and the average age of stakeholders was 48 years. Among stakeholders, participants both supported and opposed derestricting abortion access in Costa Rica. No clinicians reported providing abortion care, legal or clandestine. Participants described influences on abortion access in Costa Rica influenced at all levels of the SEM, as depicted in Figure 1. In total, we identified nine themes of influence across the SEM. Figure 1.Social determinants of access to safe abortion in Costa Rica

Social determinants of access to safe abortion in Costa Rica

Individual level

Access to sexual health education influenced sexual and reproductive health knowledge, agency and abortion decision-making

Participants highlighted critical gaps in knowledge of sexual and reproductive health and national abortion policies among individuals in Costa Rica. Most clinicians and stakeholders viewed insufficient general education and a lack of sexual health education as key contributors to experiencing barriers to preventing unintended pregnancies, communicating, and advocating for themselves with healthcare providers, and making informed decisions about the management of unintended pregnancies.

Additionally, several respondents from both groups discussed that there is a very poor understanding of Costa Rica’s abortion policy among the general population, leading to misinformation. For example, one participant said: “[The Norma Tecnica] mostly generates a certain uncertainty; not everyone is familiar on the topic, not everyone knows the laws on the matter … since [abortion] is still not legalized here, in Costa Rica … so the majority of people are afraid, of course.” (Female clinician)The majority of participants from both stakeholder and clinician groups stated that the general population does not receive any sex education in school and that they did not receive sexual education themselves when they were younger. Most participants explicitly stated a need for enhanced sexual education for adolescents and young girls in Costa Rica so that they could better prevent and manage unintended pregnancies, as well as make informed decisions about their bodies. For example, one clinician stated the following: “The young population should be more educated on [sexual health] because they are the most prone to try to interrupt a pregnancy. I think there should be more access to birth control and family planning in school … and a more effective sexual education for them.” (Female clinician)Approximately half of each participant group recognised the unique barriers experienced by vulnerable populations in accessing quality general and sexual health education. Several clinicians stated that most unintended pregnancies they saw were among individuals with lower levels of general education and little to no sexual health education. One clinician shared the following about how people with lesser access to education and financial resources have more difficulty in their communication with medical professionals about sexual and reproductive health options: “In areas … a little more marginal, so to speak, access [to sexual health resources and information] is a little more difficult … I would say that [limited] education makes your consults a little more complicated.” (Male clinician)They revealed that issues of literacy and language barriers (e.g. native languages of Indigenous populations) impacted their ability to counsel these individuals effectively about their pregnancies. Some stakeholders spoke specifically about how certain populations with migratory status have interrupted access to education, and even less access to sexual health education relative to the general population. They emphasised the impact of insufficient sexual health education and information on vulnerable populations’ ability to exercise their rights to seek services and to advocate for themselves with health care professionals. For example, a stakeholder shared this about their view of vulnerable populations’ access to abortion information: “If you are a refugee, you probably don’t even know what a therapeutic abortion is … even if you need it, you probably won’t know how to ask for it or you won’t know that you need it.” (Female stakeholder)One clinician discussed how people with migratory status or who have lower income have less access to information about sexual health and about the national abortion policy: “They don’t have the [abortion policy] information and a doctor isn’t going to give it to them. And third, they don’t have the resources because it’s expensive. To get [an abortion] here clandestinely, it is expensive to get the medicine … So, most are probably going to end up having the baby.” (Male clinician)One stakeholder noted how, conversely, people with greater access to information and resources can hold clinicians accountable to provide abortion services when indicated by the law: “Women with better socioeconomic status and from metropolitan areas definitely at least have access to more information to hold the health professionals who are treating them accountable and [ensure] that this element, abortion, is considered among the decisions they can make.” (Female stakeholder)

Interpersonal level

Interpersonal networks impact pregnant individuals’ decisions to terminate pregnancies and who they turn to for support

Both clinicians and stakeholders spoke about the influence of interpersonal networks, such as family and friends, on individuals’ decisions to terminate their pregnancies. A few clinicians and stakeholders spoke about how individuals with supportive families may accept their pregnancies and may be less inclined to look for options to terminate. Alternatively, some stakeholders argued the opposite and said that some people may be more inclined to get abortion due to the influence of their family. For example, one stakeholder shared this about how a family’s reputation may influence some people to terminate their pregnancies: “I would say reasons [to get an abortion] that have to do with this concept of honor, which is not only a woman’s, but the family’s, too.” (Female stakeholder)Some stakeholders also mentioned that individuals seeking termination may be more likely to discuss their situation with their close friends, and through these friendships, may be better connected to abortion networks. A stakeholder with knowledge of abortion networks emphasised the influence of being connected to abortion networks: “There are also networks of women who are very hidden who help other women to have abortions, with the same thing, perhaps have access to pills or have brought them from abroad. But that’s a little less common and it’s harder to get because you have to be in the right circles.” (Female stakeholder)In contrast, both clinicians and stakeholders spoke about how vulnerable populations and those with migratory status, such as migrants and refugees, often do not have access to social networks of support in Costa Rica. They believed that this lack of connection impacted these populations’ decision-making process and ability to seek services. For example, a stakeholder spoke about how vulnerable populations have less access to abortion networks and familial support: “It is much more likely that if you are a refugee, you have even less money to be able to do it, even less support networks, because here you are not living in your territory. Maybe you don’t have your family … you don’t have friends. So, all of that of course is going to make it even more difficult for you.” (Female stakeholder)

Community level

Abortion stigma shapes the way that abortion access is discussed in society and restricts people’s ability to seek and use abortion services

A consensus among clinicians and stakeholders was that the topic of abortion is highly stigmatised in Costa Rica and not discussed openly. For example, a clinician shared the following about societal stigma towards abortion in Costa Rican society: “I think that pregnancy termination is a subject that just mentioning it makes some people’s hair stand up, but I also think this is due to misinformation.” (Female clinician)One stakeholder emphasised how rare it is for people to publicly discuss abortion in Costa Rican society, which has increased its stigma: “It is something that exists on the margins, not visible and not accepted, not discussed” (Female stakeholder).

“I think that pregnancy termination is a subject that just mentioning it makes some people’s hair stand up, but I also think this is due to misinformation.” (Female clinician)

Other participants described how the stigma around abortion restricts people’s ability to seek and utilise abortion services. Approximately half of both clinicians and stakeholders discussed how they viewed abortion stigma as leading people to terminate their pregnancies in secret, resorting to potentially dangerous methods, in order to minimise the social ostracisation associated with the procedure. For example, one clinician shared this about how individuals have extra-legal abortions and do not discuss it with others: “The process is ‘get pregnant, get scared, I don’t want to have this pregnancy, I Google it, I look for misoprostol, I put it in myself, without knowing the dose, without knowing anything; I have the abortion, I get scared a lot, where I see the bleeding, and I come for emergencies; I don’t tell anyone.’” (Female clinician)A few study participants also discussed how the stigma around abortion was associated with other stigma related to sexuality: “Here, we are barely getting rid of taboos in this country … homosexuality is still an issue. Family planning is still an issue in 2020. I do believe we are still in diapers about sexuality rights.” (Female clinician)Some participants spoke about how societal stigma surrounding abortion and the fear stemming from restrictive abortion policies may deter individuals living in rural or underserved areas who lack access to more progressive social support systems from seeking pregnancy termination. For example, individuals living in more rural areas may be subject to more intense community oversight and religious stigma. One stakeholder responded to the question of why individuals often do not terminate their pregnancies with the following: “Fear. Fear of ending up in prison or dying … I think fear is one of the biggest factors. And religious fear too. Or social punishment. I also think that this is going to vary a lot, depending on the area or the region in which you are asking. Because I live in the capital. Maybe in some rural or remote areas, the punishment or the spiritual and family repercussions could be greater than what I perceive here. It’s like in the villages everything is known, everybody knows everything about everybody, it’s very small.” (Female stakeholder)One physician also spoke about how people in rural areas of Costa Rica may be more impacted by stigmatising attitudes when it comes to reproductive health and abortion access, thus limiting the likelihood for people to request a legal abortion: “There is also the issue of Latin American machismo, which is a reality … access to health [in rural hospitals] is totally different from San José … There is a lack of self-empowerment just through being a woman. So a patient coming to the decision on their own to have an abortion or a salpingectomy probably won’t happen.” (Male clinician)

Institutional/health system level

Providers’ lack of abortion training and policy knowledge curtails access to abortion services

Participants identified several institutional barriers to abortion access at the health system level, with the most prominent being a lack of understanding of abortion policy among health care providers. All clinicians discussed this theme, in addition to one stakeholder. When probed about their level of training on induced abortion, almost all clinicians shared that they learned very little in their medical training about abortion. One clinician shared the following about how abortion provision was not a topic covered in their medical education: “We learned very little about unwanted pregnancies and we learned very little about abortion techniques.” (Female clinician)Additionally, some clinicians offered anecdotes about how they navigated the need to make difficult medical decisions in the wake of insufficient training on abortion. One clinician shared how they instead relied on the tenets of basic clinical care when they received no training during their residency: “There were no regulations to discuss anything [related to abortion care]. I didn’t follow any rules, you practically followed your intuition and your basic training that you had to be able to deal with that type of situation.” (Female clinician)Most clinicians also reported little to no training on the national abortion policy or therapeutic abortion provision in their health centres. A few clinicians shared that while they received information on uterine evacuation after miscarriage, they were never trained on how to terminate a pregnancy. One clinician emphasised their lack of training on safe abortion provision despite training on uterine evacuation: “We were always taught to do abortions of dead fetuses, malformed fetuses or septic processes, but we were never taught to do termination of pregnancy of unwanted abortions. There is no training on that subject.” (Female clinician)In addition to insufficient training on abortion provision, several clinicians also mentioned a lack of familiarity with the Norma Técnica. Clinician participants shared that they had never read the policy and were therefore unclear in which situations abortion was legally protected: “I think it’s [abortion] allowed before 12 weeks and when the mother’s health or life is in danger, but that’s all I could tell you. Honestly, I’ve never read it [the Norma Técnica]”. (Male clinician)A few participants highlighted that rural facility health providers more frequently lacked awareness of the current national abortion policy but were more likely to serve marginalised groups. For example, one stakeholder shared the following about how providers’ understanding of abortion policy may be constrained in these settings: “Particularly in indigenous areas, coastal and rural communities that do not have enough information and there are physicians who are not as up to date, shall we say, on what they should be doing.” (Female stakeholder)

Fear, personal beliefs and stigma lead to provider hesitancy to engage with abortion care

Many clinicians acknowledged the lack of clarity of the national abortion policy in facilities and as the main driver of service provision hesitancy for clinicians, who feared criminalisation and losing their medical licences, despite feeling that they wanted to provide services when medically necessary.

Clinicians stated that there was very limited or no discussion on the topic among their peers given the stigma surrounding abortion. This also meant that there was not much space for sharing knowledge on the topic. Many clinicians shared that, given limited clinical training and lack of standardised implementation of national abortion policy across facilities, a woman’s access to legal abortion is thus often determined by the individual belief system of their doctor: “It [abortion] is a topic that is not normally taught … not even in the Medical Ethics course, are we taught the ethical and moral elements of the subject. It has always been thought of in a very individual way, following the opinion of each person.” (Female clinician)More than half of the clinicians and one stakeholder also cited conscientious objection (a doctor’s ability to refuse to perform a therapeutic abortion if it is against their belief system) as a barrier to accessing legal abortion, and a contributing factor to abortion stigma. One clinician shared this about how a woman’s abortion access is influenced by stigma and the ethical stance of the treating clinician: “I feel that the first level where they face obstacles is in the access to health services; and after the woman gains access to the system, she is left up to the will of the treating doctor, because there is no uniformity in approach for these women. Then, if she is lucky enough, it will be someone who validates her felt need, then she will do well. If it was someone very conservative or someone who is not open minded, then it will not go well.” (Female clinician)Additionally, almost all clinician participants who spoke about conscientious objection supported the law and agreed with providers’ rights to protect their personal beliefs. No clinicians shared direct experiences of using conscientious objection to refrain from providing a legally induced abortion, but several indicated that they would conscientiously object if they were asked to perform an abortion. For instance, one clinician described their likely thought process if a person came in seeking an induced abortion: “If someone wants to have an abortion, it’s their decision, but I wouldn’t do it. It’s a personal issue for me as well … If someone wants to abort, let them abort. I’m not going to judge her, but I’m not going to do it … That problem is hers.” (Male clinician)Similarly, several clinicians spoke about how many of their peers would also not feel ethically comfortable in performing an abortion. For example, one clinician shared: “It’s not that simple … among the people I know, most are super Catholic … With therapeutic abortions it’s always hard to know who wants to participate. We’ve had to do hysterectomies with the fetus in situ, and there’s pretty much only one or two who do it. The rest of the team conscientiously objects”. (Female clinician)In every instance where a clinician expressed desire to refrain from providing induced abortions due to moral objections, they reported feeling comfortable with providing post-abortion care, which involves counselling, treatment for incomplete abortion and related complications, and other related reproductive health services.24

No study participants spoke explicitly about why they might not want to provide abortion services to vulnerable populations. However, one clinician shared that in rural areas where there are fewer OB/GYNs available, clinicians are not legally allowed to exercise conscientious objection if they are the only specialist available.

Public and private facilities present vastly different options for abortion access and care quality

A few clinicians and stakeholders discussed the differences in abortion access between public and private institutions. Several participants from both groups stated their awareness of extra-legal abortion service availability at private facilities for those with financial access. One stakeholder highlighted the long-standing availability of abortion in private facilities: “It has always been done here in private places … Here, in Costa Rica, there have always been private clinics that have performed abortions on demand and with pay … Without even resorting to [the Norma Técnica].” (Female stakeholder)

Structural level

Financial status is considered the primary determinant of access to abortion services

Clinicians and stakeholders identified financial status as the leading determinant of abortion access. Both groups reported that one’s socioeconomic status determines not only if they are able to access abortion, but how. One stakeholder spoke about the impact of financial resources on abortion procedure type: “Well, like so many other things, I would say that this has a lot to do with access to economic resources and that then there will be different ways of doing it for different people, depending on their economic possibilities.” (Female stakeholder)Nearly all clinicians and stakeholders emphasised that individuals who have financial resources have the option to leave Costa Rica and to seek abortion services in other countries where it is accessible, and that individuals are often counselled by clinicians to do so. Several clinicians confirmed that when individuals come to their facility with an unwanted pregnancy, they share information with the individuals about other countries where they can seek abortion services, including Mexico and the United States. One stakeholder shared this anecdote highlighting the compounding resources, including financial capacity and family support, necessary to leave the country to seek abortion services: “She had the means to be able to travel … which implies not only having money, but also having a United States visa, in her case … it’s not just any person … being ready at the moment needed, with a current visa, with the money to be able to go. And with a support network. So, she travels with her stepmother and her mother-in-law and goes to Miami … she has a spectacular treatment, they treat her very well. She completes her abortion, it’s a matter of two, three days and they come back here.” (Female stakeholder)Other clandestine abortion options available for individuals with resources that were mentioned by participants included accessing private facilities for abortion services or self-managed medical abortion through pills provided by extra-legal retailers. Respondents from both groups acknowledged these options as costly and inaccessible for the majority of the population, who do not have the financial means to access private health care where abortion medication is available. One stakeholder stated this about a woman’s experience discreetly accessing abortion medication from a private provider: “Her private gynecologist recommended that she get pills … pretending he didn’t say anything … and told her he will wait for her in his office after she takes them. In other words, the private gynecologist was okay with her having an abortion, but he said, ‘I can’t do it by law. I’m too afraid of losing my license’ … And he sent her to buy her pills clandestinely.” (Female stakeholder)Many clinicians and stakeholders acknowledged that financial resources directly impact the ways in which individuals access information and services for abortion care. Study participants believed that the common options for extra-legal abortion in Costa Rica mentioned above, including leaving the country, receiving abortion care in private facilities, or accessing abortion pills extra-legally, are not financially possible for the majority of the population, which leaves individuals from vulnerable populations or low-resource settings with a harsh reality: they either need to carry their unwanted pregnancy to term or terminate the pregnancy under potentially unsafe conditions which may lead to poor health outcomes. One clinician spoke about the lack of agency women with limited resources face in accessing abortion services: “Patients who cannot leave the country have to live with their cruel and sad reality – which is to continue with pregnancy – that they don’t have a way to terminate, that there is no other way … ” (Female clinician)A few clinicians and stakeholders spoke specifically about the impact of limited resources on an individual’s ability to access medical abortion. First, that they have access to information … and secondly, that they can afford it because both the pills or the drugs and the medical procedure are expensive. So, definitely women in a disadvantaged socioeconomic situation … I don’t think they have access to these practices.” (Female stakeholder)

Migratory status impacts people’s ability to access reproductive healthcare services

Participants frequently discussed the influence of migratory status on access to sexual and reproductive health (SRH) care. Clinician and stakeholder groups agreed that socioeconomic status impacts abortion options, but clinicians and stakeholders differed on opinions about access to services specifically for individuals with migratory status. When probed about differences in abortion access, the majority of clinicians responded about SRH services more broadly. They stated that refugee, migrant, and immigrant populations have the same access to reproductive healthcare (including access to contraception and post-abortion care) as the rest of the population. These clinicians referred to Costa Rica’s Universal Healthcare System to support this claim. For example, a clinician shared: “There are laws that protect the woman who does not have public healthcare, the state protects her … Women are protected during pregnancy and childbirth without the need for them to be insured and without difference in treatment due to ethnicity, nationality or immigration status.” (Male clinician)However, almost all stakeholders and a few clinicians acknowledged substantial differences in access to reproductive health care services to prevent pregnancy or manage unintended pregnancies among populations with migratory status. Specifically, they cited discordance between the national healthcare policy and the ability to access reproductive healthcare. These participants emphasised that while all populations have rights “on paper,” in reality, certain populations are not able to access public or private services due to financial barriers or lack of documentation. Several respondents stated that if individuals seek reproductive health services but lack citizenship papers or a permanent residence and cannot pay the fee to access the most basic level of health insurance, they are unable to receive care. One clinician emphasised the barriers for low-income and migratory populations in Costa Rica in seeking reproductive health services: “We say solidarity, well, but when there is no support for this type of insurance, then you have to pay a fee that some consider to be very high … If you have no papers. If no one knows what your name is except you, you don’t have access to the services.” (Female clinician)A stakeholder discussed the nuances of immigration and financial status on access to legal and extra-legal abortion services in Costa Rica: “So, there is a class gap in terms of access to abortion in Costa Rica, which in itself is not possible [given the illegality of the procedure], but when you do it clandestinely there is another gap, because it is not universal, because it also depends on how much money you have, on your immigration status … if you are a refugee or not.” (Female stakeholder)

Religion influences reproductive health care policy in Costa Rica

Participants frequently cited the influence of religion on SRH services and abortion policy in Costa Rica as particularly influential on service access. Several clinicians and stakeholders acknowledged that the political climate in Costa Rica is quite conservative, which impacts open discussion on SRH services, and particularly abortion. Study participants cited the impact of Catholicism on Costa Rican politics and attributed the religious beliefs of policymakers to limited conversations around abortion. One stakeholder shared this about the conservatism of the Costa Rican Congress and how they avoid political discourse around abortion: “We are in a country that is still very conservative in many things, you know that here we have an official religion, the state has a Catholic religion … . Certain issues that have to do with women’s health are permeated by this cultural reality.” (Female clinician)

Discussion

This study presents new evidence to address a research gap on access to abortion in Costa Rica. We found that key stakeholders perceive a range of factors across the SEM as influencing access to abortion care for individuals capable of pregnancy in Costa Rica and that these are primarily concentrated at the health systems and structural levels. Access to comprehensive sexual health education empowers individuals to exercise their reproductive rights and addresses structural barriers affecting healthcare accessibility. Interpersonal networks and family support can act as facilitators or barriers to abortion services, while community-level stigma shapes the way that care is or is not accessed. Additionally, provider training, policy knowledge, and personal beliefs affect where and how individuals seeking abortion services can receive care, as does the type of facility accessed. Structural factors such as financial status, and the influence of religion on abortion policy in Costa Rica, further affect service availability and accessibility. Our study points to the additional barriers that these additive factors have for vulnerable populations. Our study findings complement those of other studies on abortion access in low- and middle-income countries and highlight several opportunities to address barriers to access across the SEM.

At the individual level, we found that stakeholders perceived access to sexual health education as influencing sexual and reproductive health knowledge and thus individuals’ agency over their health, and their abortion decision-making. Though research from the LAC region is limited, studies in low-and-middle-income countries find similar results about the association between SRH knowledge and abortion access. One such study in rural Tanzania concluded that improved health education and services are urgently needed to dispel misconceptions about abortion and unsafe abortion practices.25 A multi-country study in Africa found that access to adequate education serves as a primary prevention for unsafe abortion.26 The overwhelming sentiment from both clinicians and stakeholders about inadequate sexual health education in Costa Rica in our study highlights the opportunity for increased engagement of local and regional organisations in Latin America to promote access to comprehensive SRH education. In an abortion-restrictive setting such as Costa Rica, abortion hotlines may also be appropriate for providing reliable information in a discreet manner.27

We found that in the Costa Rican context, support from family and friends is instrumental for connecting pregnant individuals to abortion networks and services. A similar study in Mozambique reported that support from a pregnant individual’s partner or parents is key to the decision on whether and how to terminate a pregnancy.28 A LAC-focused study also found social support was instrumental in finding access to safe abortion networks and services.2 These results again point to the need for sexual health education programmes, not only for individuals capable of pregnancy, but their partners and support networks, and to the need for programmes that provide support to individuals with unwanted pregnancies and facilitate their access to safe abortion. Informal networks to provide abortions, such as those used at the Thailand-Myanmar border or the Socarristas en Red activism group in Argentina, have also been successful in increasing access to safe services in restricted settings.29–31 These groups provide information, and support to individuals seeking abortion care, filling gaps left by restrictive legal environments or limited formal healthcare options. Through grassroots efforts and community solidarity, they empower individuals to make informed choices about their reproductive health and access the care they need.29,30,32 Feminist and activist organisations in other LAC countries and the US have also used innovative methods to create linkages to care such as through virtual outreach or mobile applications.33,34 The creation of informal networks may be especially impactful for people from vulnerable or marginalised populations, who may have limited access to support networks and information.

Participants in our study reported that at the community level, stigma strongly influences access to abortion care. This type of stigma is not limited to Costa Rica: a 2021 World Health Organization study found that abortion stigma leads to an avoidance of the health system in many cases, despite the fact that unsafe abortion-related complications are a significant cause of morbidity and mortality among individuals in Latin America.35 Globally, abortion stigma silences people from exercising their reproductive choices, and serves as a barrier to safety for abortion seekers, as it leads to a lesser quality of abortion care and more people seeking unsafe abortions.36,37 Abortion stigma has also been found to impact attitudes towards abortion care policies, including in favourable legal contexts, and leads to further marginalisation of abortion care.37 Interventions that address abortion stigma have been tested at individual and structural levels across the Latin America region. These include counselling and peer support to those who experience stigma, protest and social movements to call attention to stigmatising attitudes and promote social change, and increased community organising and advocacy from local feminist and human rights organisations.23 Specifically, protest and social movements have proven effective in other contexts, such as in Argentina, Mexico and Uruguay, where popular movements contributed to legalised abortion. These movements have supported governments expanding their definitions of the human right to health, even in countries like Colombia that still maintain abortion restrictions.38 These interventions would potentially have the same effect on both normalising abortion, expanding legal allowances on therapeutic abortion, and minimising stigma in Costa Rican society.34,39,40

Several themes emerged related to barriers and facilitators at the health system level. While Costa Rica’s 2019 Norma Técnica was introduced to bring clarity to policy on induced abortion provision and establish clear, mandatory protocols for physicians to evaluate pregnancy risk, it has been criticised by SRH advocates for insufficient roll-out and poor standardisation in health facilities.12,41 Correspondingly, we found that clinicians felt unfamiliar with the policy and were unlikely to receive any training on induced abortion provision, even years after the Norma Técnica was introduced. In addition to receiving little to no training on abortion provision, clinicians in our study sample had limited discussions with their peers about the topic, preventing those with training from sharing knowledge and learning from one another. These study findings indicate an urgent need for abortion-related training, specifically to educate providers on current policies as well as to prepare them to provide care for those who do qualify for abortions under the law, both at the workplace and national level. Specifically, there should be training integrated into the curricula on the national abortion policy and its implementation in both residency programmes and medical schools in Costa Rica, which will contribute to more widespread clarification on the policy in medical spaces and could lead to clinicians becoming advocates for their patients when abortions are medically necessary. Appointing someone at each facility to make sure this policy is adhered to, and to help evaluate informal policies applied by providers and institutions, will serve as an accountability measure and will be integral to its successful implementation.37 For clinicians who are interested in further engaging on the topic of abortion, a Values Clarification and Attitude Transformation (VCAT) workshop would be helpful for them to think about their professional roles related to abortion provision beyond their personal beliefs. VCAT workshops are endorsed by the World Health Organization as an effective way to address the role of abortion stigma in preventing women from getting necessary care, and this could be an impactful exercise in the Costa Rican context, given how little clinicians learn and engage on the topic.37,42 Additionally, hospitals in Costa Rica could implement a programme that has been proven successful in Uruguay, a country now with a progressive abortion policy. Before the country legalised abortion, a local advocacy organisation partnered with hospitals in the capital city for harm reduction related to abortion. Specifically, the organisation provided steps to doctors that they should follow for postabortion care when patients have decided to have an abortion or had already had one.34

At the structural level, financial status, coupled with the influence of religion on abortion policy in Costa Rica, complicates pregnant individuals’ ability to access timely and safe abortion services. This study’s findings on financial barriers to abortion are clear: safe abortion services are only accessible to individuals who have adequate resources and can afford to either clandestinely visit private facilities, pay online for abortion pills to be mailed to them, or who can leave the country and seek services where abortion policy is less restrictive. This result complements existing global and regional literature around financial status as a leading determinant of abortion access.2,43,44 Across the LAC region, wealthy people are able to leave their countries of residence for abortions or can pay to access private facilities.2 Through our study findings on abortion stigma in political spaces, our study upheld findings from other studies which have found that across the Latin American region, conservative political governments and societal norms often impose structural barriers that significantly restrict and stigmatise abortion access.2,45–47 Addressing structural barriers such as disparate financial resources and the influence of religion on politics is important for minimising abortion-related taboos and increasing the ability for individuals to access services. The institution of a public policy whereby the government offers free or discounted educational resources, family planning services, and maintains stock at health facilities, could reduce disparities.

Across the SEM, vulnerable populations are disproportionately affected by barriers to abortion services and experience disparate access to sexual health education and information, support networks, reproductive health infrastructure, and financial resources. The study findings corroborate existing global and LAC-focused literature on barriers to general health services for vulnerable populations globally, as well as in Costa Rica specifically.2,48–51 While most clinicians stated that migrants and refugees have access to the same healthcare as Costa Ricans under the law, some clinicians and all stakeholders reported vast difference in general SRH service access between Costa Rican citizens and others. This finding complements findings from another study in Costa Rica that reported substantial difficulties for migrants living in Costa Rica in accessing healthcare and staggeringly low insurance coverage rates for this population.49,50 Our study corroborates evidence from the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) and the Costa Rican Demographic Association reports that found that vulnerable populations have worse access to SRH services, and that lower-income individuals are more likely to access unsafe abortion than people with adequate financial resources.15,52 These findings support the need for interventions that not only address availability of abortion services, but even more the accessibility of SRH services broadly, both geographically and financially, especially for vulnerable populations.

Strengths and limitations

Findings from this study should include several important considerations. This study is limited by its relatively small sample size and lack of participant demographic diversity: most participants worked in urban areas so the results should be interpreted within that context. However, this is one of the first qualitative studies on population level access to abortion services in Costa Rica and benefits from the inclusion of expert testimony of both clinicians and important policy stakeholders. Though this manuscript includes information about the needs of general and vulnerable populations, it was collected through clinician and stakeholder interviews, and thus does not include valuable information from vulnerable populations themselves. This is one of few studies to analyse the social determinants of abortion access in Costa Rica, shedding light on an under-researched topic in a country with restrictive abortion policies. We look forward to research that continues to explore how to mitigate barriers to services for these populations.

Conclusion

This study found that the social determinants of abortion access in Costa Rica included barriers and facilitators across all levels of the social ecological model for both general and vulnerable populations. Study findings highlight the need for enhanced sexual health education provision for individuals capable of pregnancy and their communities as well as abortion service provision training for clinicians and programmes and interventions that support education and facilitate knowledge sharing. Improving access to safe and legal abortion services in Costa Rica is an urgent public health issue, and this research reveals opportunities to enhance the bodily autonomy of people who can become pregnant in Costa Rica. Ultimately, this research reveals the need to address multilevel barriers to access to safe and legal abortion services in Costa Rica, so everyone can realise their human right to health.
