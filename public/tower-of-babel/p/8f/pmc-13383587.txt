Common carotid artery occlusion (CCAO) is a rare form of carotid occlusive disease,1–3 and bilateral involvement is even more uncommon, with only a limited number of cases reported.4–20 Etiologies and clinical presentations are heterogeneous, and management remains challenging because established evidence-based recommendations are lacking.1 Acute bilateral CCAO is particularly rare and, to our knowledge, no cases of sequential bilateral CCAO have been previously reported. We report a case of sequential bilateral CCAO in a patient with protein C deficiency and a patent foramen ovale (PFO), in whom recurrent paradoxical embolism was suspected. Protein C deficiency is a rare inherited thrombophilia primarily associated with venous thromboembolism; however, its contribution to arterial thrombosis remains controversial.21–23 In the presence of a PFO, however, paradoxical embolism may represent a potential mechanism of arterial ischemic events.24,25 This case highlights the importance of considering paradoxical embolism in the differential diagnosis of bilateral CCAO when thrombophilia and PFO coexist.

Illustrative Case

A 56-year-old man presented with sudden-onset motor aphasia and right hemiparesis. His medical history included hypertension and distal gastrectomy for peptic ulcer disease. His family history was notable for cerebral infarction, myocardial infarction, and deep vein thrombosis in first-degree relatives.

Initial MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging showed no obvious acute ischemic findings (Fig. 1A), whereas MR angiography (MRA) demonstrated the absence of flow-related signal in the left internal carotid artery (ICA) and middle cerebral artery (MCA) (Fig. 1B). Initial MRA showed no apparent abnormality in the contralateral anterior circulation or posterior circulation. The preserved signal in the left anterior cerebral artery, despite nonvisualization of the left MCA, suggested an additional occlusive lesion at the left carotid terminus, rather than an isolated proximal carotid occlusion. Digital subtraction angiography revealed occlusion of the left common carotid artery (CCA) proximal to the bifurcation (Fig. 1C). Emergency endovascular aspiration thrombectomy was performed. Following aspiration, a fixed stenosis at the ICA origin was revealed (Fig. 1D), prompting carotid artery stenting (CAS) (Fig. 1E). Although the CCA and ICA were successfully recanalized, angiography initially demonstrated contrast opacification only up to the carotid terminus (Fig. 1F). More distal angiography revealed persistent occlusion of the left MCA (Fig. 1G). Additional aspiration thrombectomy of the left MCA was therefore performed. Taken together, these findings did not support retrograde collateral flow from the ECA to the ICA, and the occlusion pattern was considered to be Riles type 2.26 Final angiography confirmed recanalization of the left CCA, ICA, and MCA (Fig. 1H). Postoperative CT showed a limited infarction in the MCA territory, without extensive damage from persistent large-vessel occlusion (Fig. 1I). Systemic heparin was administered intraoperatively, and dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and clopidogrel was loaded at the time of CAS and maintained postoperatively.

On postoperative day 7, mild left hemiparesis appeared for the first time. Follow-up brain MRI showed a new infarction in the right basal ganglia (Fig. 2A), and MRA and CT angiography revealed occlusion of the brachiocephalic artery and right CCA (Fig. 2B and C). The right anterior circulation appeared to be maintained through collateral flow via the anterior and posterior communicating arteries. Given the opacification of the distal right ICA beyond the occluded CCA and the partial preservation of the distal carotid vessels, the right-sided lesion was considered compatible with a Riles type 1A pattern. Extensive blood testing showed no evidence of autoimmune disease, inflammatory disease, or other coagulopathies; however, protein C activity and antigen levels were reduced to 21% and 36%, respectively. Direct sequencing of the PROC gene, performed as previously described,27 identified no pathogenic variants. Transesophageal echocardiography subsequently confirmed the presence of a PFO.

Given the patient’s stable hemodynamic status and preserved collateral circulation, the right-sided lesion was managed conservatively. Warfarin anticoagulation was initiated in addition to ongoing dual antiplatelet therapy, with a target prothrombin time–international normalized ratio of 2.0–3.0. The patient was subsequently transferred to a rehabilitation facility with mild residual right hemiparesis and motor aphasia.

Discussion

Observations

The present case highlights three clinically important points. First, it underscores the rarity and management challenges of bilateral CCAO, particularly when presenting sequentially. As summarized in Table 1, reported cases of bilateral CCAO are heterogeneous in their etiologies and clinical presentations.4–20 Most are chronic lesions associated with underlying atherosclerosis, inflammatory vasculopathy, or radiation-induced vasculopathy, often with relatively mild symptoms and, in some cases, managed conservatively. In contrast, acute bilateral CCAO is rare and has been reported mainly in association with traumatic injury,8 acute type A aortic dissection,16 or embolic mechanisms.19 Notably, these acute cases have occurred simultaneously rather than sequentially; to our knowledge, no cases of sequential bilateral CCAO have been previously reported. Given the limited evidence and variability in collateral circulation, optimal management remains undefined. Prior reports have suggested that successful revascularization and treatment selection in CCAO depend heavily on distal vessel patency and the adequacy of the collateral circulation.1,26 In the present case, emergency endovascular aspiration thrombectomy with adjunctive CAS achieved successful recanalization from the left CCA to the MCA and likely prevented a major infarction. However, subsequent contralateral occlusion, despite a technically successful initial revascularization, demonstrates that treating the index lesion alone may be insufficient when the underlying embolic mechanism remains active. Second, although protein C deficiency is classically associated with venous thromboembolism, its association with arterial ischemic stroke remains controversial.22,23 Nevertheless, several cases of arterial ischemic stroke associated with protein C deficiency have been reported.28–31 In the present case, the coexistence of protein C deficiency and a PFO provides a plausible mechanism for recurrent paradoxical embolism. Patients with PFO-associated cryptogenic embolism and thrombophilia have been reported to have a higher risk of recurrent stroke or transient ischemic attack than those without thrombophilia, and selected patients may benefit from PFO closure.24,25 The markedly reduced protein C activity and antigen levels, together with a family history of thrombotic disease and recurrent large-vessel occlusions, support an underlying prothrombotic state. Although venous thrombosis was not documented, the combination of thrombophilia and a right-to-left shunt suggests that paradoxical embolization is the likely mechanism. Although presumptive, this interpretation is consistent with existing literature.24,25 Antithrombotic management was challenging, as antiplatelet therapy was required to prevent CAS-related stent thrombosis, whereas anticoagulation was considered appropriate for protein C deficiency and suspected paradoxical embolism. Acute carotid stent thrombosis after CAS is rare but potentially catastrophic. Moreover, evidence comparing direct endovascular treatment with bridging therapy for acute large-vessel occlusion remains inconclusive. Therefore, in complex cases such as the present one, antithrombotic treatment should be individualized based on stent-related thrombotic risk, recurrent embolic risk, infarct size, and hemorrhagic risk.32,33

Third, the genetic findings in this patient were notable. Despite reduced protein C activity and antigen levels, direct sequencing of the protein C gene, including the promoter, coding exons, and flanking regions, identified no pathogenic variants. This does not exclude hereditary protein C deficiency, as causative abnormalities may reside outside the sequenced regions or involve mechanisms not detectable by direct sequencing.34,35 Accordingly, the diagnosis was based primarily on biochemical and familial evidence rather than molecular confirmation. An acquired reduction in protein C activity related to prior gastrectomy and postoperative nutritional disturbance cannot be fully excluded, although its contribution in the present case remains uncertain.21

From a practical perspective, this case suggests that broader etiological evaluation is warranted in patients with recurrent or atypical large-vessel occlusions. When carotid occlusion occurs in a sequential bilateral pattern, particularly in the presence of a family history suggestive of thrombophilia, assessment for inherited thrombophilia and right-to-left shunt may be diagnostically and clinically valuable.

Lessons

Sequential bilateral CCAO is exceptionally rare and may reflect an ongoing embolic or systemic thrombotic mechanism rather than a single hemodynamic event. In patients with recurrent cryptogenic large-vessel occlusion, particularly with bilateral involvement or atypical vascular patterns, evaluation for thrombophilia and right-to-left shunt should be considered.
