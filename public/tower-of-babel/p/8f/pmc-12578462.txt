Given the public’s trust in Canada’s health care system, governments have important responsibilities when partnering with private companies to provide care — they should be transparent about contracts, commission third-party evaluations, and safeguard patients’ health data privacy, while also ensuring that quality standards are met and patients are not paying for medically necessary care.

More than 6.5 million people in Canada do not have access to a regular primary care clinician.1 Health workforce challenges across the country have adversely affected primary care access, especially in rural and remote areas. To address the primary care access crisis, some provincial governments have partnered with virtual care corporations to provide formalized pathways to phone-, video-, and text messaging–based medical care. However, there are risks associated with direct-to-consumer virtual “walk-in” care, related to access, quality of care, and data privacy.2 These risks require careful consideration, particularly as formal partnerships could further entrench corporate virtual care within Canadian health care systems.

The characteristics of corporate virtual care partnerships vary by province. In some provinces, corporate virtual care is offered to those waiting for attachment to a primary care provider. In others, it is available to all residents. The physician services offered through these partnered programs are publicly funded. However, anecdotal evidence suggests patients are sometimes also asked to pay subscription fees or out of pocket for certain services, and a lack of clarity may exist over which care options are or are not publicly funded.3 No formal evaluations of corporate virtual partnerships in Canada have been published, but some are presently under way, with results expected in 2026.4 Published studies of publicly funded, privately delivered virtual care from other countries support inferences on the potential impacts for primary care access and overall health care utilization.5,6 Here we describe the heterogeneity of Canada’s corporate-provided virtual care programs, consider their benefits and risks, and propose a way forward.

How are provinces partnering with virtual care corporations to deliver primary care?

To better understand how corporate virtual care platforms have been integrated into primary health care across Canada, we identified current partnerships between corporate platforms and provincial or territorial governments using a structured Web search (November 2024). Although many provinces allow physicians to bill provincial insurance for virtual visits conducted through these companies’ platforms, we sought to identify evidence of explicit partnership between health ministries and corporate care providers. We defined a corporate virtual care partnership with a provincial government as cases that met the following criteria: virtual health care services were provided by a shareholder-owned corporation (e.g., Telus, Maple, Teladoc Health Canada), not a physician medical professional corporation; evidence existed of a formal contract as referenced in news media releases (Table 1); and these services were endorsed on the government’s official health services website. We used the search terms “virtual health care” and “telemedicine” and combined these with each province and territory’s name. We also contacted several content experts in provinces and territories with which the authors were less familiar, to confirm whether any formal partnerships existed. We identified no additional partnerships.

We found ongoing corporate virtual care partnerships in 4 provinces: New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, Nova Scotia, and Prince Edward Island (Table 1). In these provinces, virtual care services were provided free of charge to patients who were not registered on a primary care clinician’s roster (i.e., unattached patients). New Brunswick offered 2 corporate-provided virtual care programs: eVisitNB (using Maple platform) and NB Health Link (Medavie Health Services). eVisitNB provided on-demand virtual appointments with a nurse practitioner, to both unattached and attached patients. NB Health Link offered both virtual and in-person appointments to patients on the primary care wait-list. Virtual Care NL (Teladoc Health Canada) in Newfoundland and Labrador and Prince Edward Island’s Unaffiliated Virtual Care Program (Maple) provided on-demand virtual visits to patients on the primary care wait-list. Nova Scotia’s VirtualCareNS (Maple) program had 2 tiers of care: Full Care and Basic Care. Full Care was available to unattached patients for on-demand virtual visits. Clinicians could prescribe medications, order tests, and refer patients to specialists or in-person care. Basic Care, available for all residents, provided 2 free visits per year, with private payment required for additional visits. Basic Care clinicians could not order tests or refer patients to specialists or in-person care. Little information on the costs or funding models of these partnerships was available.

Several other provinces had previously intended to partner with corporate virtual care companies. In March 2020, the Alberta government announced a partnership with Telus Health (previously Babylon) to introduce a virtual health care app. However, physicians raised many concerns,15 and at the time of our search this service was no longer listed on government websites. Additionally, the government of British Columbia announced a contract for virtual care in late 2023, but the successful company had not been announced at the time of this article’s publication.16 The northern territories had pre-existing, noncorporate telehealth and virtual care services, offered through the territorial governments, to meet the needs of people in remote and sparsely populated areas. What are the benefits of these models and partnerships?

Supporting primary care access is the stated reason for establishing corporate virtual care partnerships. These virtual platforms provide services to those without access to primary care, particularly bridging gaps in rural services.2 Virtual visits provide increased convenience and facilitate access for patients who cannot travel to their nearest primary care clinic or hospital, such as those with mobility issues, those who cannot take time off work, or those who need to arrange child care. In a 2017 survey, patients reported high satisfaction with virtual visits, and about 10% reported that they would have gone to an emergency department if the virtual visit had not been available.17 Virtual visits can also be cost-saving, particularly if they can offer a timely assessment or avert an emergency department visit, while also integrating with appropriate longitudinal management of chronic conditions.18 By endorsing and paying for corporate virtual visits, provincial governments can provide more primary care options to residents who struggle to access primary care, including the many on primary care attachment wait-lists.

For governments choosing to implement them, corporate partnerships offered a quick response in the context of increasing attention to primary care access challenges. Developing the necessary technological infrastructure publicly may have been viewed as too time-consuming or inefficient, particularly in smaller provinces. In addition, where services are delivered by clinicians located out of province (as can be the case for eVisitNB, VirtualCareNS, and Virtual Care NL), this averted the need for direct recruitment or registration activities on the part of the province.

What are the risks of these models and partnerships?

Access to and equity of care

Video- and text-based visits benefit those who are digitally confident, have high Internet bandwidth, and own a device that can connect to the Internet.6 In England, the publicly funded National Health Service partnered with privately owned Babylon Health to create the GP (general practitioner) at Hand virtual care program in 2017. Independent evaluation of the service found that its patients were younger, healthier, and more affluent than the general population, and that the service was not being used by large numbers of older adults or those with complex health conditions.19 Virtual care aligns with the inverse care law, as it expands access for already advantaged groups, while remaining inaccessible to many with higher needs, such as people with lower incomes or who have less formal education.20

Although it is unknown whether this occurs within the context of government partnerships, corporate virtual care providers are known to promote private-pay options, raising another equity concern. Government-contracted services are by definition publicly paid, yet they are delivered through the same corporate platforms that promote private-pay options, which could lead to confusion, as evidenced by this being addressed on the Frequently Asked Questions page for VirtualCareNS. After their 2 government-funded visits, individuals in Nova Scotia may opt for ongoing care through Maple, at which point private payment would be required. Although the Canada Health Act prohibits direct-billing patients for medically necessary services, many corporate virtual clinics provide text-based medical assessments and other services that are not subject to the act, as they are not covered by most provincial physician service agreements.21

Finally, corporate virtual providers draw from a finite pool of primary care clinicians, potentially pulling them away from longitudinal family practice; clinicians are also drawn to such models, for their greater flexibility and convenience.22 Through the diversion of health care dollars and health human resources, corporate virtual care could worsen overall access to the comprehensive primary care that is foundational to public health.2,3

Health care system use and costs

Some evidence suggests that the existence of virtual-only walk-in care creates demand that might not otherwise have existed (i.e., supply-induced demand), increasing overall health care system use and costs.23 Users of the London-based GP at Hand program had greater health care utilization than similar-risk peers, and almost half used the program more regularly than they used their regular clinicians’ practice.24 When the supply of convenient virtual visits is high, demand for visits for low-acuity conditions — such as upper respiratory viral illness — can also increase, resulting in increased total costs without improving overall health outcomes. Supply-induced demand can exist in all types of health care, but the frictionless and immediate nature of corporate virtual care makes it especially susceptible to this phenomenon.

Although it would be helpful to compare outcomes with and without a virtual visit in patients who were unattached to a regular primary care clinician, previous research has instead compared virtual with in-person visits or compared virtual visits with different levels of care continuity. In the United States, virtual walk-in clinic visits for acute respiratory infections were more likely than initial in-person visits to lead to repeat office and urgent care visits.25 Similar findings were observed in Sweden, where users of direct-to-consumer telemedicine increased their short-term health care consumption (i.e., in-person and subsequent virtual visits) more than those who had an in-person physician visit.26 In Ontario, those who had a virtual visit with an outside physician were 66% more likely to visit an emergency department in the following 7 days than those whose virtual visits were with their own family physician, providing evidence of the link between relational continuity and health outcomes after virtual care.5

This evidence raises the concern that corporate-provided virtual care may have unintended consequences that increase system use and costs in a way that is not observed when some level of continuity of care exists between physician and patient. It is not yet known how effective and efficient corporate virtual care is as primary care of last resort, and how this compares with other last-resort options such as walk-in clinics or emergency departments. Moreover, comparisons to longitudinal primary care remain relevant, as health care dollars invested in corporate virtual care could be used to expand and support access to full-service, longitudinal primary care.

Safety and quality of care

Virtual visits present challenges for maintaining care quality, particularly where access to medical records is limited and both a physical examination and an existing relationship between the patient and physician are absent. In addition, longitudinal family physicians face the challenge of trying to manage their patients’ subsequent return visits with little information on the care provided. Safety incidents associated with virtual visits have been attributed to insufficient clinical information-gathering, limited ability for physical examination, and inattention to social circumstances.27 The hazards of virtual care were especially pronounced for patients with limited fluency in the clinician’s language, with disabilities, or with complex pre-existing conditions.27 An often-cited measure of acute care quality is antibiotic prescribing for viral upper respiratory tract infections; this may be higher in virtual care.28 Virtual walk-in clinics were found to prescribe more broad-spectrum antibiotics, perhaps related to the inability to conduct a physical examination and establish a more certain diagnosis.29

High continuity of care is broadly associated with improved health outcomes, including lower mortality.30 Consistent with this, virtual visits within an existing clinician–patient relationship have similar utilization outcomes to in-person visits.31 Meanwhile, outside of such a relationship, virtual visits are linked to higher downstream health care utilization.25,26 Corporate virtual care may fragment a patient’s care by introducing informational and relational discontinuity. However, continuity within a virtual care platform could mitigate these risks, as would bidirectional integration of medical records and connection to in-person care.

When partnering with corporate care providers, governments are responsible for ensuring care provided meets the quality standards of Canada’s health care system. This requires transparent reporting of quality of care using publicly accessible data, similar to quality monitoring done in the public system.

Privacy and transparency

Virtual care corporations are private, investor-owned third-party companies that are incentivized toward profit. The profit models and legal agreements of these companies are not publicly available. The Canadian for-profit virtual care industry values health care data and uses data to generate revenue through marketing of products and services.32 Some of the tools used to generate revenue may compromise the objectivity of physician care or the privacy of patient data.32 Patient trust in government-contracted services may cause them to consent to sharing data in ways beyond their usual comfort level.32 Indeed, when respondents in the OurCare survey were asked how willing they would be to use new virtual services if the company that ran the service sold their health data to pharmaceutical or insurance companies, 84% were not willing.33 When provincial governments contract with and endorse private companies, this could lead patients to assume that their private data will not be used for profit-generating interests — a potentially incorrect assumption.

What are governments’ responsibilities?

When provincial governments partner with corporate care providers, endorsement of the quality of services they provide is implied. Corporate virtual partnerships may also further entrench private companies within the broader health care system, becoming a permanent fixture even if provinces later improve access to full-service longitudinal primary care. Ongoing checks and balances are necessary to ensure standards are met and rules are not breached. Where these partnerships are introduced, governments must negotiate and enforce contractual obligations related to data privacy, security, interoperability requirements, and transparency (suggested actions for key partners are provided in Table 2).3

Conclusion

If federal and provincial governments choose to partner with private virtual care corporations, they will need to safeguard data privacy and security by creating regulations to govern patient data, clarify acceptable uses, and specify under which conditions and to whom data can be disclosed.34 Interoperability legislation would ensure that medical data can be shared within the patient’s circle of care, preserving informational continuity between clinicians. Greater transparency regarding corporate virtual care funding models and profits, including the details of government contracts and payments, would support public trust. As policy-makers appropriately seek to modernize the health care system, a risk exists that corporations’ focus on profits could detract from achieving larger health-system objectives. Additional regulation is needed in this space. Alternatively, provincial and territorial governments may consider expanding virtual care offered through the public system, prioritizing funding of longitudinal, integrated primary care.

Independent evaluation of corporate virtual care partnerships can confirm that equity, safety, privacy, and transparency criteria are met and maintained. Evaluation plans should be sensitive to patient-level impacts, such as convenience and satisfaction; however, these factors should not come at the expense of patient privacy and quality of care.

Corporate virtual care partnerships are presently used by some governments to address primary care access challenges. However, caution is needed before greenlighting corporations in the public health care sector, as once these programs are introduced, it may be difficult to modify what has been established. By leaving companies to self-regulate, change is unlikely to occur.32
