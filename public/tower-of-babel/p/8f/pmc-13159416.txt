Self‐Incandescent Heating‐Driven Microstructural Consolidation of Biscrolled Cu/CNT Yarns for Reduced Frequency‐Dependent AC Resistance
Authors: Feng Wang, Yoojoo Yun, Jung Hyun Ryu, Kyonghwa Song, Gwansik Kim, Seokmin Lee, Seong Chu Lim, Dongseok Suh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDeveloping flexible conductors that combine high conductivity, stable MHz‐range electrical behavior, and mechanical durability remains a challenge. This is primarily because conventional bulk‐type metals suffer from frequency‐dependent AC‐resistance increases, while standard composites often exhibit poor interfacial integrity. Here, we address these limitations through the synergistic integration of a biscrolling architecture with a rapid and efficient self‐incandescent heating (SIH) post‐treatment. The biscrolled structure promotes a uniform 3D distribution of copper, while SIH is associated with local Cu reorganization/reflow‐like restructuring, grain growth, and interfacial consolidation within the carbon nanotube (CNT) framework. These microstructural changes are consistent with the formation of a more densified conductive network, leading to a 68.8% enhancement in electrical conductivity (up to 3.63 × 104 S/cm) and a metallic temperature coefficient of resistance (TCR = 3.32 × 10− 3 °C− 1) approaching that of bulk copper. Notably, the yarns exhibit weak frequency dependence of resistance within the measured range (up to 10 MHz), indicating a reduced frequency‐dependent increase in resistance compared with solid copper wire. By combining exceptional mechanical resilience under extreme deformation with stable high‐frequency performance, SIH‐treated biscrolled Cu/CNT yarns emerge as a robust material platform for next‐generation flexible conductors and interconnects.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of electric vehicles (EVs), wearable electronics, and deformable energy systems has created an urgent need for lightweight and flexible conductors that maintain stable performance under electrical, thermal, and mechanical stress. While conventional metallic wires such as copper exhibit excellent electrical conductivity, their inherent rigidity and susceptibility to fatigue under repeated deformation limit their use in high‐frequency or flexible applications. To overcome these limitations, researchers have turned to hybrid conductors that incorporate nanoscale materials [1, 2, 3], such as carbon nanotubes (CNTs), which exhibit outstanding intrinsic conductivity [4, 5], mechanical strength [6, 7, 8], and interfacial compatibility with metallic matrices [5, 9, 10]. In particular, the integration of CNTs with cost‐effective and highly conductive copper has attracted significant attention, with early studies demonstrating enhanced current‐carrying capacity in Cu/CNT composites [2, 11, 12, 13], typically realized through sheath‐core architectures or embedded metallic nanofibrils [1, 2, 3, 14, 15, 16, 17].

However, such conventional sheath–core structures often suffer from poor copper distribution, weak interfacial bonding, and limited cyclic durability, resulting in localized Joule heating and reduced reliability. Biscrolling method has emerged as an alternative strategy, wherein CNT sheets are co‐twisted with conductive guest materials to achieve uniform embedding both internally and externally [18, 19, 20, 21]. The biscrolled configuration improves mechanical load sharing and electrical continuity compared to conventional sheath‐core designs by providing a more homogeneous 3D distribution of copper within the CNT framework. Nevertheless, even in biscrolled architectures, inherent microstructural defects within the metal phase, such as voids and grain boundaries, continue to impede optimal electrical and thermal transport. Conventional post‐treatments, including thermal annealing [22], laser sintering [23, 24, 25], and mechanical densification [13, 15, 26], can partially mitigate these issues but often require extreme conditions or specialized equipment that may damage nanoscale structures or impair flexibility. Furthermore, existing composite strategies have largely failed to address the frequency‐dependent resistance increase associated with skin‐effect‐related current crowding in high‐frequency operation.

In this work, we propose a dual strategy that synergistically combines structural design and microstructural engineering to overcome these challenges, as summarized in Figure 1. First, we employ biscrolling to construct a yarn architecture with a uniform, 3D distribution of copper within a continuous CNT network. This architecture may help distribute conductive pathways more broadly across the cross‐section and thereby reduce the tendency toward current crowding. Second, we introduce self‐incandescent heating (SIH) as a rapid, scalable, and non‐destructive post‐treatment to improve the internal microstructure of the composite [27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. Unlike conventional external heating, SIH provides rapid and highly localized Joule heating within the conductive yarn [32, 33], which may promote local Cu reorganization/coalescence under a transient thermal profile that is difficult to reproduce by bulk furnace treatment. The SIH process is driven by controlled Joule heating and monitored in real time (Figure S1), and is associated with local Cu reorganization/reflow‐like restructuring, grain growth, and interfacial consolidation between Cu and CNTs. As conceptually illustrated in Figure 1c, these changes may help reduce the tendency for surface cracking, improve interfacial cohesion, and reduce structural inhomogeneity, thereby mitigating the factors that contribute to localized heating and mechanical degradation.

As a result, the SIH‐treated biscrolled Cu/CNT yarns exhibit enhanced DC conductivity, a metallic temperature coefficient of resistance (TCR) approaching that of bulk copper, and weak frequency dependence of AC resistance up to 10 MHz. Combined with excellent mechanical resilience under bending and knotting, these results support the potential of SIH‐treated Cu/CNT yarns as a flexible conductor platform and suggest that SIH can serve as a useful microstructural engineering approach for advanced interconnect‐related and flexible electronic applications.

Results and Discussion

Fabrication of Copper/CNT Yarns

To systematically evaluate the influence of structural design and copper incorporation strategy, three types of Cu/CNT yarns were fabricated: a conventional sheath–core structure (R1) and two biscrolled configurations—one via pre‐electroplating (R2) and another via sputtering (R3). All yarns were constructed from three‐layer CNT sheets drawn from a vertically‐aligned CNT forest. The fabrication routes are summarized in Figure 2a, and the sample process information is summarized in Table 1.

Fabrication routes of Cu/CNT yarns with sheath–core and biscrolling structures.

Prior to electroplating (routes R1 and R2), the CNT sheets were rendered hydrophilic by immersion in 70% nitric acid for 15 min. This process introduces oxygen‐containing functional groups, enhancing wettability and nucleation site density for more uniform copper deposition. The electroplating was performed in an electrolyte consisting of a mixture of sulfuric acid and copper sulfate. The fabrication of the three yarn architectures proceeded as follows: the sheath–core yarn (R1) was prepared by first twisting the pretreated CNT sheet into a yarn, followed by electroplating at 30 mA for 10 min to form an outer copper layer; the biscrolled yarn via pre‐electroplating (R2) was obtained by first electroplating the pretreated CNT sheet (30 mA, 10 min), then twisting the metalized sheet into a yarn via biscrolling, with a secondary electroplating applied to enhance copper loading; whereas the biscrolled yarn via sputtering (R3) was fabricated by depositing a thin, uniform copper layer (≈150 nm) on each side of the as‐drawn CNT sheet via sputtering, followed by biscrolling and additional electroplating to increase copper content. All as‐fabricated yarn variants underwent self‐incandescent heating (SIH) treatment under vacuum. The onset of incandescence (see Figure 1b) was used as an operational indicator that the intended SIH condition had been reached.

Microstructural Consolidation and Homogenization by Self‐Incandescent Heating (SIH)

The electrical and mechanical performance of Cu/CNT yarns is critically governed by the quality of the Cu–CNT interface, which in turn depends on the uniformity and adhesion of the initially deposited copper coating. To address the inherent hydrophobicity of pristine CNTs, a nitric acid pretreatment was applied to introduce oxygen‐containing functional groups, significantly enhancing surface wettability and nucleation site density for subsequent copper deposition. As shown in Figure 2b, this pre‐treatment played a crucial role in determining the copper deposition behavior. Without it, electroplated copper formed discrete, poorly adhered granules, whereas the pretreated surface enabled a continuous and conformal copper layer.

We further employed self‐incandescent heating (SIH) to achieve comprehensive microstructural consolidation. Before SIH, the yarn surfaces exhibited distinct morphologies depending on the fabrication route. The electroplated biscrolled yarn (Figure 2c, top) displayed coarse, agglomerated copper nodules, while the sputtered biscrolled yarn (Figure 2d, top) featured a finer and more uniform coating. Under vacuum SIH treatment, intense Joule heating—visually confirmed by incandescent emission (Figure 1b)—promoted localized Cu reorganization and coalescence. Direct in situ temperature measurement was not available in our vacuum setup. Therefore, we use the more cautious description “reflow‐like restructuring” to describe the Cu consolidation behavior after SIH.

Cross‐sectional analysis further revealed that SIH‐driven structural consolidation extended uniformly into the yarn interior. Prior to SIH, both electroplated and sputtered biscrolled yarns exhibited porous internal architectures with insufficient Cu–CNT interfacial contact (Figure 3a,b, left panels). After SIH, the Cu phase became denser and more continuous, reducing internal voids and improving interfacial connectivity (Figure 3a,b, right panels). The sheath‐core yarn (R1) also showed pronounced compositional heterogeneity, with a Cu‐rich outer region and a carbon‐rich inner region (Figure S2); this trend is reflected in the marked difference between the local C/Cu ratios measured in the external and internal regions (Figure 3c). In contrast, the biscrolled architecture provided a more homogeneous scaffold that enabled more effective densification, resulting in a more uniform Cu distribution and improved interfacial integration.

Electrical Performance Enhancement and Metallic Transition

Capitalizing on the optimized microstructure, we systematically evaluated the electrical properties of the yarns. Self‐incandescent heating (SIH) treatment led to substantial improvements in electrical conductivity across all sample types (Figure 4a,b). The sputtered biscrolled yarns exhibited the most significant enhancement, with room‐temperature conductivity increasing by 68.8% from 2.15 × 104 S/cm to 3.63 × 104 S/cm. This enhancement is consistent with a reduction in electron scattering centers, likely associated with microstructural densification that reduced pore‐induced scattering and crystallographic evolution that reduced grain‐boundary‐related scattering.

(a, b) Temperature‐dependent electrical conductivity of sheath‐core, electroplated biscrolled, and sputtered biscrolled yarns (a) before and (b) after SIH. (c, d) Normalized resistance (R/R40°C) versus temperature (c) before and (d) after SIH; the nearly linear variation corresponds to a temperature coefficient of resistance (TCR) of ∼3.32 × 10− 3 °C− 1. (e) Current–voltage (I‐V) characteristics measured by two‐probe and four‐probe methods before and after SIH, confirming ohmic behavior and a reduction in resistance after SIH (inset: enlarged four‐probe curves). (f) Comparison of two‐probe resistance (R2p), four‐probe resistance (R4p), and contact resistance (Rc) before and after SIH.

Temperature‐dependent resistance measurements revealed a fundamental transition in the conduction mechanism following SIH treatment. Before SIH, all yarn configurations exhibited a weaker and less ideal metallic resistance–temperature (R–T) response, with a smaller positive temperature dependence. This behavior suggests that charge transport was limited by discontinuous metallic domains and by inter‐domain tunneling/contact resistance (Figure 4c). After SIH, all samples displayed linear, metallic R–T characteristics with a positive temperature coefficient of resistance (TCR). The optimized sputtered biscrolled yarn achieved a TCR of 3.32 × 10− 3 °C− 1, closely approaching that of pure copper (3.9 × 10− 3 °C− 1), consistent with the formation of a more continuous and better‐connected copper network (Figure 4d).

Current‐voltage (I‐V) characteristic measurements further verified the transition to ohmic behavior and reduction in overall resistance after SIH (Figure 4e). Comparative analysis of two‐probe and four‐probe resistance measurements (Figure 4f) allowed for the decoupling of bulk resistance from contact resistance. Both parameters showed significant reduction after SIH, indicating that the treatment not only optimized the bulk conductive pathways within the yarn but also effectively improved the electrode contact interfaces. These comprehensive electrical improvements motivated further investigation into their nanoscale structural origins.

Nanoscale Crystallographic Evolution Consistent With Reduced Electron Scattering

To elucidate the structural origin of the enhanced electrical performance, we conducted detailed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of the sputtered biscrolled yarns. Prior to SIH, representative wider‐area TEM images show that Cu existed mainly as fine, dispersed domains distributed along the CNT framework (Figure 5a, top). High‐resolution TEM (HRTEM) imaging and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) further indicate a nanocrystalline Cu structure, evidenced by diffuse diffraction features associated with numerous grain boundaries (Figure 5b,d, top).

TEM characterization of sputtered biscrolled Cu/CNT yarns before and after SIH. (a) Representative wider‐area TEM images showing multiple Cu domains before (top) and after (bottom) SIH; the insets highlight local morphology at higher magnification. (b) High‐resolution TEM images. (c) Fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns. (d) Selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns.

After SIH, the wider‐area TEM images reveal clear coarsening and coalescence of the Cu domains, accompanied by a reduced number density of fine particles and the formation of larger, more continuous Cu features (Figure 5a, bottom). The HRTEM images show more extended lattice fringes, and the SAED patterns evolve from diffuse rings to sharper diffraction features, indicating the formation of a more highly ordered and larger‐grained Cu microstructure (Figure 5b,d: bottom). Such crystallographic evolution is consistent with reduced grain‐boundary‐related electron scattering and therefore likely contributes to the improved macroscopic conductivity after SIH. Additional representative TEM/EDS evidence is provided in Figure S3.

Reduced Frequency‐Dependent AC Resistance Increase Within 10 MHz

To evaluate the frequency‐dependent electrical behavior of our conductors, we measured their resistance over the 1–10 MHz range. Although this frequency window is below the gigahertz regime typical of many modern wireless systems, it provides an experimentally accessible range for examining the evolution of AC resistance in these microscale yarn conductors. For reference, the classical skin depth of Cu at 10 MHz is approximately 21 µm. We note, however, that the classical skin‐depth expression is used here only as a first‐order qualitative reference, since the Cu/CNT yarns are heterogeneous, porous, and anisotropic rather than homogeneous metallic conductors. Within this experimentally accessible range, the measurements therefore serve to compare the evolution of AC resistance and its frequency dependence before and after SIH, rather than to establish complete high‐frequency behavior or strict skin‐effect mitigation.

As shown in Figure 6a, the normalized resistance (R/R1 Hz) of a standard copper wire increases markedly with frequency, whereas both the pristine and SIH‐treated Cu/CNT yarns exhibit much weaker frequency dependence up to 10 MHz. This trend is also evident in the plot of absolute resistance (Figure 6b), where the Cu wire shows a clear increase with frequency while the yarn conductors maintain comparatively stable values. The Cu wire was selected because its diameter is comparable to the overall diameter of the Cu/CNT yarns, enabling a geometry‐matched comparison of frequency‐dependent resistance behavior.

The observed difference in frequency‐dependent resistance can be discussed using the skin depth as a qualitative benchmark. Figure 6c presents the classical skin‐depth calculation across the measured frequency range, while Figure 6d compares the skin‐depth‐to‐radius ratio for the control wire and the yarn conductors. Because the present Cu/CNT yarns are porous, heterogeneous, and anisotropic, these calculations do not provide a rigorous quantitative description of the actual current distribution. Instead, they provide a first‐order framework for understanding why frequency‐dependent resistance changes may become relevant within the measured range. Accordingly, our main conclusion is drawn from the measured resistance–frequency responses (Figures 6a,b), while the skin‐depth plots are provided only as qualitative context rather than as the basis for quantitative claims. Within this context, the Cu/CNT yarns exhibit a weaker frequency‐dependent increase in AC resistance than the solid Cu wire, consistent with a reduced influence of skin‐effect‐related current crowding in the measured regime.

The conceptual cross‐sections in Figure 6e,f visually summarize this contrast. In the solid copper wire, increasing frequency drives current toward the outer region. In the biscrolled Cu/CNT yarn, the helical, interpenetrating network of copper and CNTs creates a multitude of conductive pathways throughout the cross‐section. This schematic is intended to illustrate a plausible transport picture consistent with the resistance data, rather than to claim direct proof of a fully uniform current distribution. While the enhanced microstructural uniformity and densification observed after SIH suggest that these biscrolled structures are promising candidates for frequency‐sensitive interconnect or shielding‐related applications within the measured regime, a detailed evaluation of their device‐relevant high‐frequency electrical performance, including insertion loss, characteristic impedance, and signal integrity, is still required.

Multifunctional Durability Under Electrical and Mechanical Stress

The integration of high electrical, thermal, and mechanical performance is crucial for the practical deployment of flexible conductors. The SIH treatment endows the biscrolled Cu/CNT yarns with precisely this combination of superior robustness. Under a constant applied current, the SIH‐treated yarn reaches a significantly lower steady‐state Joule heating temperature compared to its pre‐SIH state, while infrared thermal imaging confirms a more uniform heat distribution across its structure (Figure 7a). This is consistent with improved heat dissipation, likely associated with the more consolidated and continuous conductive network, which may help reduce localized hot spots.

Electromechanical stability was assessed through cyclic bending tests. The pristine yarn exhibited large, unstable fluctuations in normalized resistance during repeated deformation. In contrast, the SIH‐treated yarn showed much more stable electrical performance, with only minimal variation in resistance over numerous bending cycles, indicating improved interfacial integrity and structural cohesion (Figure 7b; Figure S4). The yarn's mechanical flexibility is visually documented in Figure 7c, showing its ability to be bent to angles exceeding 120° without fracture. This enhanced durability is consistent with the overall structural consolidation induced by SIH. SEM comparisons before and after SIH (Figure 7d,e) show distinct surface damage responses under bending and knotting: prior to SIH, the yarn exhibits more apparent cracking and structural disruption, whereas after SIH it maintains a more intact morphology under the same deformation conditions. These images are presented to illustrate improved resistance to deformation‐induced damage after SIH, whereas the primary evidence for SIH‐induced microstructural evolution is discussed in Figure 3. Together, these observations support more reliable performance under recurrent mechanical stress, further supporting the use of these yarns as promising conductors for flexible and wearable electronics.

Conclusion

In this work, we show that self‐incandescent heating (SIH) is a highly efficient and scalable post‐treatment that substantially enhances the performance of biscrolled Cu/CNT yarns. The SIH process is associated with local Cu reorganization/reflow‐like restructuring, grain growth, and interfacial consolidation, which reduce structural discontinuities and strengthen the Cu‐CNT interface. These microstructural transformations yield a remarkable 68.8% enhancement in electrical conductivity, a metallic temperature coefficient of resistance approaching that of pure copper, and superior thermal management. Within the measured frequency range (up to 10 MHz), the SIH‐treated yarns exhibit weak frequency dependence of AC resistance compared with a geometry‐matched Cu‐wire control. Coupled with outstanding mechanical flexibility and cyclic durability, these co‐optimized properties support the potential of these SIH‐optimized biscrolled yarns as promising conductors for flexible electronics and related interconnect‐oriented applications. More broadly, this study suggests that SIH can serve as a useful microstructural engineering platform for advanced metallic‐nanocarbon hybrid materials.

Experimental Section/Methods

Materials

Vertically‐aligned Carbon Nanotube (CNT) forests were supplied by A‐Tech System, Korea. Sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 98 wt.%), copper sulfate pentahydrate (CuSO4·5H2O), and nitric acid (HNO3, 70 wt.%) were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich.

Twisting Procedure

Twisting was conducted in a single‐end‐fixed configuration at 60 rpm, with a twist density of 5 turns cm− 1 for Cu‐coated sheets and 15 turns cm− 1 for bare CNT sheets.

Self‐Incandescent Heating (SIH)

A controlled DC current was applied to induce self‐incandescent heating (SIH). The yarn was maintained under the target incandescent condition for 5 min. The detailed current/resistance evolution during SIH is provided in Figure S1.

Characterization

Electroplating was performed using a current source (LakeShore 120). The surface and cross‐sectional morphology of the yarns were characterized by Scanning Electron Microscopes (SEM, JSM‐7000F, JEOL). Cross‐sectional SEM samples were prepared using an ion‐beam cross‐section polisher to minimize mechanical deformation during sectioning. High‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns were acquired using a Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM, JEM‐2100F, JEOL). Electrical properties were measured with a current source (Keithley 6220, Tektronix) and nano‐voltmeter (Keithley 2182A, Tektronix). The SIH process was implemented using a precision source/measurement unit (Keithley 2450, Tektronix), with current applied incrementally to achieve the desired thermal treatment. Frequency resistance variation was measured using Moku: Pro (Liquid Instruments). For comparison, a commercial enameled Cu wire (diameter: 250 µm; conductivity about 5.8 ( 107 S m−1) was used as the reference conductor in the frequency‐dependent measurements. Cyclic bending tests were performed using an instrumented stage with a bending angle of 120° per cycle. Additional setup details and resistance traces are provided in Figure S4.
