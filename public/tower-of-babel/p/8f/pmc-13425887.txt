Comprehensive analysis of taste attributes and metabolite profiles reveals taste diversity among flowering stalk varieties
Authors: Xinyuan Li, Chao Zheng, Manli Luo, Zifan Zhao, Haonan Li, Shuo Fu, Chong Tan, Zhiyong Liu, Feng Hui, Yun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFlowering stalk is a specialty vegetable that is highly valued for its unique taste; however, the taste metabolic diversity of different flowering stalk varieties has rarely been investigated. This study aimed to improve the understanding of taste quality in different varieties of flowering stalk by investigating the correlation between taste attributes and metabolite profiles through electronic tongue, sensory evaluation and widely targeted metabolomics techniques. GD exhibited the most favorable taste profile, characterized by high umami intensity and low levels of bitterness and astringency among five varieties. A total of 2747 metabolites were identified by widely targeted metabolomics, and 55 metabolites were down-regulated and 109 metabolites were up-regulated in GD compared with the other four varieties, including umami-related amino acid compounds, bitter-related alkaloid, terpenoid and glucosinolate compounds, and astringency-related polyphenol and flavonoid compounds. These findings enrich the metabolic information of flowering stalk and provide a theoretical basis for the taste optimization.Keywords: Flowering stalk, Electronic tongue technology, Sensory evaluation, Taste attributes, Widely targeted metabolomics, Metabolite profiles

Introduction

Flowering stalk (Brassica campestris L. subsp. chinensis) is a distinct variant derived from Brassica chinensis. Due to its wide adaptability to climate, it is very popular throughout China. Overseas Chinese played a key role in introducing flowering stalk to other countries, contributing to its global recognition. This vegetable was first exported from China to Japan in the 1920s, and subsequently spread to North America and Australia (Liu et al., 2021). At present, the main varieties of flowering stalk are ‘Gong Dong’ (GD) flowering stalk, ‘Lian Zhou’ (LZ) flowering stalk, ‘Zeng Cheng’ (ZC) flowering stalk, ‘Bai Cai Tai’ (BCT) flowering stalk and ‘Hong Cai Tai’ (HC) flowering stalk. Among them, GD is a major variety of leafy vegetables grown in Guangdong and is a primary source of vegetables for Hong Kong (Wu et al., 2026), LZ and ZC are local specialty varieties in Guangdong province (Niu et al., 2019; Sajjad et al., 2024), BCT and HC are the main cultivated varieties in Hunan and Hubei, respectively (Zhou et al., 2024). These cultivars are widely planted across the core production zones of South and Central China, covering both mainstream commercial types and regional endemic cultivars. Each variety has significant differences in taste, texture, nutrition, and market acceptance, and they are all favored by the market. Significant differences in taste quality among various flowering stalk varieties contribute to divergent levels of market recognition and consumer preference.

Good taste is one of the prerequisites for vegetable crops to be favored by consumers, and flavor constitutes the primary determinant of the overall sensory experience (Yue et al., 2024). The taste quality of vegetables is influenced by the complex interactions among various taste-related compounds. For example, naturally occurring sweet compounds such as glucose and fructose interact antagonistically with bitter components derived from glucosinolate degradation, establishing a fundamental balance in taste perception (Wei et al., 2021). Soluble sugar content varies among different fruits and vegetables, and sugar accumulation patterns can be classified according to the predominant types of soluble sugars, such as fructose, sucrose, and glucose. Song et al. (2023) reported a significant positive correlation between soluble sugar content and sensory quality in Chinese cabbage, suggesting its potential as a stress resistance indicator. Fructose is the primary sugar accumulated in Chinese cabbage leaf bulbs, followed by glucose (Liu et al., 2021). In addition to sweetness, umami is another favored taste attribute that contributes to the high consumer preference for flowering stalks. Umami taste arises from the synergistic interactions of various compounds, such as free amino acids (FAAs), 5′-ribonucleotides, and umami-enhancing substances (Yuan et al., 2024). Glutamate (Glu), L-aspartic acid (Asp), and inosine 5′-monophosphate (IMP) are widely acknowledged as the primary compounds responsible for umami taste, while other nucleotides such as 5′-guanosine monophosphate (GMP) and adenosine monophosphate (AMP), as well as sugars and organic acids, serve to enhance this taste sensation (Hajeb & Jinap, 2015; Wang et al., 2020).

In contrast to sweetness and umami, bitterness and astringency are generally perceived as undesirable by consumers. Plant-derived natural bitter compounds are primarily composed of alkaloids, terpenoids, and glucosinolates (GSLs) (Guo et al., 2023). Alkaloids constitute one of the major classes of naturally occurring bitter compounds in plants (Ashihara and Crozier, 1999). The bitter taste of alkaloids serves as a protective mechanism, as most toxic substances exhibit bitter properties. Bitter taste receptors enable organisms to detect and avoid harmful compounds, thus reducing the risk of potential poisoning (Nissim et al., 2017). Terpenoids represent another major class of common bitter compounds that play significant roles in various natural foods. For example, lettuce contains sesquiterpene lactones, which may adversely affect consumer acceptance and consumption behavior (Chadwick et al., 2016). Glucosinolates are unique bioactive compounds predominantly found in plants of the cruciferous family. These compounds and their hydrolysis products exhibit a diverse array of biological functions that are contribute to human health and nutrition. Additionally, they play important roles in plant growth and development, taste and nutrient composition, and plant defense system (Dong et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2023). However, previous studies have investigated consumers' sensory perception and acceptance of several Brassica crops, as well as potential taste-related barriers to consumer acceptance. These unpleasant tastes are primarily attributed to the presence of glucosinolates and their hydrolytic metabolites, particularly isothiocyanates (ITCs) (Bell et al., 2018). Astringency is characterized by a dry, rough, puckering sensation, primarily perceived at the tip of the tongue and the region anterior to the upper teeth, which is often associated with organic acids, phenolic compounds and flavonoids (Gonzalodiago et al., 2013).

Taste is a multifaceted characteristic that is influenced by various compounds that impart a specific taste to vegetables when bitterness and astringency are combined with sweetness, umami or other tastes (Zhang et al., 2025). Sensory attributes are inherently subjective, as individual consumers exhibit varying taste preferences. To achieve objective evaluation of food, the electronic tongue based on a biological sensory system is used to quickly identify changes in taste. In recent years, the electronic tongue has emerged as a highly sophisticated tool for taste analysis, significantly advancing the comprehension of taste profiles in various substances (Sheng et al., 2024; Yue et al., 2024). This cutting-edge device, designed to emulate the human taste system, integrates an array of sensors capable of detecting the flavor characteristics of various foods. Each sample produces a unique electronic fingerprint, facilitating accurate identification and quantification of taste attributes, including sweetness, bitterness, aftertaste B (bitterness aftertaste), aftertaste A (astringency aftertaste), astringency, umami, richness, and saltness (Wu et al., 2020). A comprehensive understanding of taste-related metabolites can facilitate taste enhancement and better meet consumer demand for palatable plant-based foods. However, no study has systematically characterized the taste metabolites present in flowering stalks to date. Wide-targeted metabolomics is a high-throughput profiling technique that combines the broad coverage of untargeted metabolomics and the high detection precision of targeted metabolomics, allowing simultaneous qualitative and quantitative detection of thousands of metabolites (Sawada et al., 2009).

To investigate the differences in taste profiles and identify key metabolites among five varieties of flowering stalk, an integrated analysis was performed using electronic tongue technology, Sensory evaluation and widely targeted metabolomics. This study not only clarified the significant differences in taste characteristics among different varieties, but also identified key metabolites that are associated with taste profiles, thereby laying a scientific foundation for quality evaluation and the breeding of flowering stalk varieties.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and sampling

The plant materials used in this study included five flowering stalk varieties: ‘Bai Cai Tai’ (BCT), ‘Lian Zhou’ (LZ), ‘Zeng Cheng’ (ZC), ‘Hong Cai Tai’ (HC) and ‘Gong Dong’ (GD) (Fig. 1). Laboratory of Vegetable Genetics, Breeding and Biotechnology of Shenyang Agricultural University collected seeds of five varieties and sowed them in a 50-hole plug tray in the greenhouse of Shenyang Agricultural University (41.8°N, 123.4°E), in mid-August with a day/night temperature of approximately 31/22 °C, natural light intensity, and the relative humidity ranging from 38% to 60%. After 20 d of seedling cultivation, the flowering stalk seedlings were transplanted to greenhouse experimental plots with row spacing of 15 cm × 15 cm under day/night temperature of approximately 29/18 °C. When the flower buds at the apex of the bolting stalk initiate sprouting and petal exposure and the corolla base being at the same horizontal plane as the leaf tips of the alternately arranged lateral leaves, the flowering stalk enters the harvest stage. At the harvest stage, the stalk tissues were sampled from the position 5 cm below the main inflorescence of each variety for glucosinolates and soluble sugar content determination, electronic tongue analysis and widely targeted metabolomics analysis. Three separate flowering stalks were collected, combined into a single sample, and then divided into three biological replicates for a single experiment.

The phenotypes of five varieties of flowering stalks.

Electronic tongue analysis

The electronic tongue system (TS-5000Z, Japan) was used for taste analysis of flowering stalk. The test sensors can detect multiple basic tastes (sourness, bitterness, astringency, umami, saltiness, and sweetness) and three aftertastes (aftertaste-A for astringency, aftertaste-B for bitterness and richness for umami).

Initially, the 800 g of stalks was homogenized using a juicer, followed by centrifugation at 4 °C for 15 min, and the supernatant was then filtered. The filtrate was transferred to a specialized beaker for electronic tongue analysis and placed in the machine's automatic sampling device. Subsequently, the potential difference between the sample and the reference solution was calibrated to zero to ensure accurate measurement of the basic taste profile. The sensor was gently rinsed with the reference solution, and the aftertaste was measured as the potential difference compared with the reference solution. The reference solution was consisted of 3 × 10−2 mol·L−1 potassium chloride solution and 3 × 10−4 mol·L−1 tartaric acid solution.

For each measurement, we placed 35 mL of sample in a dedicated beaker and continuously monitored the sample for 30 s at 25 °C. According to the preset instructions, each sample group was measured three times, and the data from the three measurements were used for further analysis.

Sensory evaluation

The stalks of each flowering stalk variety were washed and cut into cube-shaped pieces with a thickness of 2 cm for sensory evaluation. Following the method of Yan et al. (2025) with minor modifications, sensory evaluation was conducted by a trained professional panel of 15 members (8 females and 7 males, aged 23 to 30) from Shenyang Agricultural University, Shenyang, China. All.

assessors voluntarily provided written informed consent prior to their involvement. For each taste attribute, four aqueous reference solutions were prepared using a graded concentration system: sodium glutamate (0–1 g/L) as the umami standard, sucrose (0–1 g/L) as the sweet standard, quinine sulfate (0–1 mg/L) as the bitter standard, and tannic acid (0–1 mg/L) as the astringent standard. All samples were blinded and labeled using letter codes, and all evaluations were conducted in a blinded and random order for sweetness, umami, bitterness and astringency. The scoring criteria are as follows: 0 (no attribute detected), 1 (trace only), 2 (low intensity), 3–5 (clearly characterized), 6–8 (dominant in the sample), 9–10 (high intensity).

Determination of glucosinolates by palladium chloride method

The sample was dried, ground into a fine powder, and sieved. Exactly 0.1 g of the samples was weighed and transferred to a centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of 1 mL of distilled water. The mixture was homogenized at room temperature and immediately subjected to extraction in a water bath at 95 °C for 30 min. After cooling to room temperature, the sample was centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 min, and the supernatant liquid was collected for analysis.

Determination of soluble sugar content by High performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

Soluble sugar content was determined using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) following the method described by Hoehnel et al. (2022), with minor modifications. Samples from each group were ground into fine powder under liquid nitrogen, and 1 g of the resulting powder was transferred to a 50 mL centrifuge tube. 3 g of the powder was mixed with 10 mL of 80% (v/v) ethanol, and the mixture was vortexed and incubated in an 80 °C water bath for 30 min. After the water bath was completed, the samples were centrifuged at 10000 g for 5 min. The supernatant was carefully collected into a new 50 mL centrifuge tube, and the pellet was re-extracted twice using the same procedure. The combined supernatants were evaporated to dryness in a boiling water bath. The residue containing sugars was dissolved in 1 mL of ultrapure water, thoroughly mixed, and filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter prior to HPLC analysis. HPLC analysis was conducted using an Agilent 1260 system with the following specifications: a 7.8 × 300 mm Carbomix Ca-NP column (Sepax), ultrapure water as the mobile phase, a flow rate of 1.0 mL·min−1, a column temperature of 80 °C, a refractive index detector temperature of 35 °C, and an injection volume of 10 μL. Each group of samples was set as one replicate, with a total of three replicates.

Widely-targeted metabolomic analyses

Metabolites extraction

The sample extracts were analyzed using a UPLC-ESI-MS/MS system (UPLC, Waters Acquity I-Class PLUS; MS, Applied Biosystems QTRAP 6500+). The metabolite extraction involved adding an appropriate volume of extraction solution and magnetic beads, followed by grinding and ultrasonic treatment. Subsequently, the mixture underwent standing centrifugation, after which the supernatant was collected for instrumental analysis. The procedure was as follows: after vacuum freeze-drying, 50 mg of the sample was accurately weighed and transferred to a centrifuge tube. Next, 1000 μL of extraction solvent (methanol-acetonitrile-water in a volume ratio of 1:2:1) was added, followed by vortex mixing for 30 s. A steel bead was then added to the tube, which was subsequently placed in a grinding machine operating at 45 Hz for 10 min. The mixture was further subjected to ultrasonication for 10 min in an ice water bath. The samples were then incubated at −20 °C for one hour before being centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Finally, 300 μL of the supernatant was carefully collected through a 0.22 μm organic filter into a 2 mL vial. For quality control (QC) purposes, 10 μL from each sample was pooled and mixed.

LC-MS/MS Analysis

The temperature of electrospray ionization (ESI) was set to 550 °C, and the ion spray voltage (IS) was configured at 5500 V in positive ion mode and − 4500 V in negative ion mode. The gas settings for ion source I (GSI), gas II (GSII), and curtain gas (CUR) were 50 psi, 55 psi, and 35 psi, respectively. Collision-induced dissociation parameters were set to moderate levels. Instrument tuning and mass calibration were conducted using 10 μmol/L and 100 μmol/L polypropylene glycol solutions in QQQ and LIT modes, respectively. QQQ scans were performed in MRM mode with the collision gas (nitrogen) set to medium. Further optimization of declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE) was carried out, resulting in the completion of DP and CE settings for each MRM ion pair.

Qualitative and quantitative analysis of metabolites

Using the LC-QTRAP platform according to the method of Wang et al. (2025), metabolite identification and quantification were performed against the Biomarker Technologies proprietary database GB-PLANT which was constructed using standard substance information provided by standard product manufacturers and data from natural product websites. The substance was characterized using secondary spectrum information, and isotopic signals, repeated signals from K+, Na+, and NH4+ ions, as well as fragment ions of higher molecular weight compounds were removed.

Metabolite quantification was performed using triple quadrupole mass spectrometry in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. In the MRM mode, the first quadrupole performed selective screening of the precursor ions (parent ions) of the target analytes, while excluding ions derived from other substances with distinct molecular weights, thus achieving preliminary elimination of matrix interference. The precursor ions were then introduced into the collision cell, where they underwent induced ionization and fragmentation to generate a series of product ions (daughter ions). Thereafter, the third quadrupole conducted further selective filtering of these product ions to identify and collect the required characteristic fragment ions, which effectively eliminated the interference from non-target ions. The characteristic ions of each substance were screened out through triple quadrupole, and the signal intensity of the characteristic ions was obtained in the detector. After obtaining the metabolite mass spectrometry analysis data of different samples, the peak area of all substance mass spectrometry peaks was integrated, and the mass spectrometry peaks of the same metabolite in different samples were corrected.

Data analysis

Based on the BMKCloud (https://www.biocloud.net), the metabolites of the samples were qualitatively and quantitatively analyzed by mass spectrometry. PCA (principal component analysis), orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), heatmap analysis, cluster analysis, volcano plot and metabolite pathway enrichment analysis were conducted using the R software. Pearson correlation coefficient was used for correlation analysis, and the identified metabolites were annotated using KEGG compounds database.

The determination of sugar content and glucosinolate content were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Duncan's test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used in SPSS (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA), and a p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussions

Analysis of taste characteristics

The taste characteristics of five varieties of flowering stalks were evaluated using an electronic tongue. The taste values of the samples measured by the electronic tongue were summarized in Table S1, and a taste radar map was constructed in Fig. 2A. Of all the taste indicators, only the sourness fell below the non-taste point, rendering it invalid. The other taste indices, including sweetness, bitterness, aftertaste B (bitterness aftertaste), aftertaste A (astringency aftertaste), astringency, umami, richness, and saltiness were valid.

The taste of flowering stalk samples was analyzed by electronic tongue. (A) A radar chart illustrating the taste attributes of five sample groups. (B) Correlation analysis of taste attributes. The size and color of the circles represented the intensity and direction of the correlations, respectively. (C) Principal component analysis of sensory attributes. (D) The sensory evaluation of five flowering stalk varieties.

Umami and sweetness are the predominant taste attributes preferred by consumers, whereas bitterness and astringency exert negative effects on the taste profile of vegetables. All taste values except sourness were utilized for correlation analysis (Fig. 2B), which revealed that umami was negatively correlated with astringency, and positively correlated with saltiness. The unpleasant taste attributes of bitterness, astringency, bitter aftertaste, astringency aftertaste were positively correlated. Richness was a complex taste attribute that exhibited positive a correlation with both bitterness and sweetness. The PCA score plot based on the electronic tongue results of five flowering stalk varieties was presented in Fig. 2C. The contribution degree of the first two components was 77.2%, which can effectively distinguish the taste of five flowering stalk varieties. ZC and HC were positioned in the first quadrant, although they exhibit high levels of sweetness, they also display elevated bitterness and astringency, which may negatively affect the overall taste. LZ was positioned in the fourth quadrant, showing inverse correlations with sweetness and umami, and positive associations with bitterness and astringency. BCT was positioned in the third quadrant and showed a negative correlation with sweetness, along with positive correlations with umami and saltiness. In comparison, GD was located in the second quadrant, showing positive associations with saltiness, umami, and sweetness, while exhibiting negative correlations with bitterness and astringency. GD was characterized by high umami intensity, low bitterness and astringency, and moderate sweetness, resulting in a refreshing and sweet taste profile. Based on these characteristics, it was determined to be the most palatable variety in this study. Meanwhile, GD was also the most widely cultivated and planted variety of flowering stalk in southern China.

The sensory evaluation was conducted to assess the five varieties of flowering stalk for sweetness, umami, bitterness, and astringency (Fig. 2D). The sensory assessment of sweetness was consistent with the electronic tongue results: ZC exhibited the highest sweetness among the five varieties, followed by HC and then GD. Similarly, the perceived umami intensity correlated well with the electronic tongue results with GD showing the highest umami value. Notably, the bitterness and astringency values measured by the electronic tongue were not fully consistent with those from sensory evaluation. Although ZC exhibited relatively high bitterness and astringency values in electronic tongue measurements, these attributes were not perceived as intense in sensory evaluation, highlighting the complexity of human taste perception. Research indicates that when different taste compounds co-exist, they may interact with each other, with high concentrations of sweetness components inhibiting the perception of bitterness, and high concentrations of umami components suppressing astringency (Zhu et al., 2024). Overall, the general trends of sensory evaluation and electronic tongue analysis were consistent, but there were some differences, and this discrepancy underscores the complementary nature between instrumental assessment and human perception.

Determination of glucosinolates and soluble sugar in five flowering stalks

Glucosinolates and soluble sugars are essential components of flowering stalk taste quality. Glucosinolates are a unique class of secondary metabolites found in cruciferous vegetables, which have a significant and complex impact on the taste of these vegetables. These compounds themselves have no distinct taste, but when the vegetable tissues are mechanically damaged (such as by cutting or chewing), glucosinolates can be hydrolyzed under the catalysis of myrosinase to form volatile substances such as isothiocyanates and thiocyanates, thereby directly creating the characteristic taste of cruciferous vegetables (Zhu et al., 2023). The determination of glucosinolates by palladium chloride method indicated that the fresh weight of glucosinolates in all the flowering stalk varieties except for HC was between 0.300 mg/g and 0.500 mg/g, and the fresh weight of glucosinolates in HC was 2.136 mg/g, significantly higher than that of the other four varieties, which may partially explain the highest bitterness observed in HC (Fig. 3A).

To explore the variations in the characteristics of five flowering stalk varieties, the soluble sugar content of each variety was determined using High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) (Fig. 3B). The determination results of soluble sugar showed that the content of glucose was the highest in the flowering stalk, followed by fructose, and the content of sucrose was the lowest. The total sugar content in ZC was the highest, and the order was ZC > HC > GD > LZ > BCT, which was consistent with the sweetness value of electronic tongue, indicating that soluble sugars play a decisive role in the sweet taste of flowering stalk. The determination of soluble sugar indicates that the sweetness of flowering stalk is primarily attributed to its accumulation with glucose, fructose, and sucrose serving as the primary contributors to this sensory characteristic. The types and contents of soluble sugars in cruciferous plants are one of the key factors influencing their edible quality. These sugars exhibit significant variation in their distribution across edible organs, such as leaves, flower heads, and roots, thereby contributing to the distinct taste profiles of each. For instance, in cruciferous vegetables with flower heads such as broccoli and cauliflower, the relatively high accumulation of sucrose imparts a fundamental taste profile characterized by sweetness and juiciness, while the synergistic interaction between glucose and fructose further enriches the complexity of taste (Zaghdoud et al., 2012). In contrast, leafy cruciferous vegetable such as Chinese cabbage exhibited a higher proportion of fructose, which contributes to a refreshing and sweet taste upon chewing, thereby effectively mitigating the astringency potentially induced by oxalic acid and other compounds (Liu et al., 2021). As an edible organ, the flowering stalk has been found to contain glucose and fructose as the primary components of soluble sugars, whereas sucrose was present in the lowest amount.

Overview of the metabolites in five flowering stalks

A widely targeted metabolomics analysis was used to investigate the metabolic profiles of different flowering stalk varieties and to identify the key characteristic metabolites related to taste. The total ion current (TIC) chromatograms of quality control (QC) samples are shown in Fig. S1 A-H. The TIC chromatograms of the four groups of QC samples were highly consistent in retention time and response intensity, with no significant abnormal fluctuations, which indicated that the chromatographic separation and signal detection stability of the detection method are excellent. In addition, the correlation coefficient of QC samples was above 0.8 (Fig. S2) and the proportion of RSD of QC samples≤30% was no less than 0.6, which indicated that the repeatability of QC samples has good repeatability and the experimental process is relatively stable.

A total of 2747 metabolites were identified in five flowering stalk varieties, and these metabolites were divided into 18 metabolite groups, namely lipids (13.69%, 376), ketones, aldehydes, esters (13.4%, 368), others (10.3%, 283), terpenoids (10.16%, 279), sugars (8.3%, 228), flavonoids (8.19%, 225), organic acid (8.08%, 222), amino acids (6.48%, 178), alkaloids (4.51%, 124), polyphenols (3.57%, 98), nucleotides and derivatives (2.8%, 77), alcohols (2.51%, 69), coumarins (2.29%, 63), steroids (1.53%, 42), phenylpropanoids (1.46%, 40), quinones (1.2%, 33), lignans (1.09%, 30) and vitamins (0.44%,12) (Fig. 4A).

There were 2513, 2537, 2510, 2500 and 2499 metabolites in BCT, GD, HC, LZ and ZC, respectively (Fig. 4B). Among the identified metabolites, a total of 2294 unique metabolites were detected across all flowering stalk samples (Fig. 4C). DEMs profile

To elucidate the differences in taste among the five flowering stalk varieties, differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) were identified and subsequently utilized to evaluate their varieties. A total of 1714 DEMs were identified in the five varieties, accounting for 62.40% of the total metabolites. We compared and analyzed the DEMs affecting the taste, including sugars, amino acids, alkaloids, terpenoids, polyphenols, organic acids, flavonoids and glucosinolates.

The electronic tongue analysis revealed that GD exhibited the most favorable taste profile, characterized by low bitterness and astringency alongside high umami intensity. To elucidate the taste characteristics of GD, OPLS-DA models were used to maximize group differentiation and identify DEMs in the comparisons of GD vs BCT, GD vs HC, GD vs LZ and GD vs ZC. The established model exhibited high R2Y values (> 0.99) and Q2 values (> 0.76), which indicate excellent predictive performance while effectively avoiding overfitting (Fig. S3 A-D). The numbers of DEMs identified in GD vs BCT, GD vs HC, GD vs LZ and GD vs ZC were 730 (392 up-regulated and 338 down-regulated) (Fig. 5A), 728 (337 up-regulated and 391 down-regulated) (Fig. 5B), 730 (356 up-regulated and 374 down-regulated) (Fig. 5C), 730 (353 up-regulated and 377 down-regulated) (Fig. 5D), all of which are detailed in Table S2, S3, S4 and S5, respectively. In contrast to GD, all taste-related metabolites were screened out in the comparison group, and the numbers of up-regulated and down-regulated DEMs were presented in Fig. 5E, F, G and H, respectively.

Furthermore, K-means cluster analysis was used to demonstrate the relative expression trends of all DEMs in different flowering stalk varieties (Fig. 6E, Table S6). All metabolites were classified into 8 subclasses, with subclass 2 comprising 252 differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs), making it the most abundant subclass. Underlying metabolic pathways related to taste

KEGG is a public database for the study of metabolite accumulation and regulation (Kanehisa & Goto, 2000). In this study, we systematically annotated and enriched the differential metabolites for each control group, categorizing them into distinct KEGG pathways. The DEMs mapped to the KEGG database in GD vs BCT, GD vs HC, GD vs LZ and GD vs ZC were involved in 68, 66, 64 and 69 pathways, respectively (Table S7), and the main metabolic pathways were illustrated in the bubble diagram (Fig. 7A-D).

The top 20 pathways with the smallest p-values in the KEGG analysis were further examined, and it was found that the tryptophan metabolism (ko00380) pathway was significantly enriched across all four comparison groups and was the only common metabolic pathway. The pathways significantly enriched in GD vs HC, GD vs LZ and GD vs ZC were similar, containing biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (ko01110), biosynthesis of various plant secondary metabolites (ko00999) and phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis (ko00400), and the secondary metabolic biosynthetic was the pathway contained the largest number of metabolites in the three comparison groups. In addition, the significantly enriched pathways included numerous metabolic pathways associated with amino acids and terpenoids, such as arginine and proline metabolism (ko00330), glycine, serine and threonine metabolism (ko00260), monoterpenoid biosynthesis (ko00902), diterpenoid biosynthesis (ko00904), sesquiterpenoid and triterpenoid biosynthesis (ko00909). Metabolic pathways enriched in BCT vs GD were also associated with amino acids, including arginine and proline metabolism and glycine, serine and threonine metabolism.

The interplay among different metabolic pathways, mediated by shared metabolites, underscores the importance of investigating specific metabolic networks to comprehensively understand the biochemical differences among flowering stalk varieties. A network diagram was constructed based on the metabolome data to systematically display the main metabolic pathways involved, including TCA cycle, amino acid metabolism and terpenoid metabolism (Fig. 7E). In terms of central carbon metabolism, the TCA cycle, as the core hub of energy metabolism, provided a guarantee for the basic energy supply and carbon skeleton synthesis of different flowering stalk varieties. The abundance differences of metabolites such as glycine, serine, glutamic acid and proline in the amino acid metabolic pathway not only reflect the regulatory differences in nitrogen assimilation and protein synthesis among different flowering stalk varieties, but also imply the differentiation of their metabolic strategies in response to stress and during growth and development. At the secondary metabolic level, the biosynthetic pathways of monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, triterpenes and diterpenes (such as metabolites of forskolin, trienone and β-geraniol) and the quinoline alkaloid biosynthesis pathway mediated by tryptophan metabolism, there were significant differences in expression among varieties, which led to their unique accumulation of flavor compounds, stress resistance and medicinal active components.

Taste-related key metabolites.

Key metabolites related to the sweetness in flowering stalks

Sweetness constitutes a critical element of flowering stalk taste profile, contributing significantly to its unique taste. This study conducted a relatively quantitative analysis of sugar compounds associated with sweetness, including monosaccharides, disaccharides, oligosaccharides, and sugar alcohols. As the fundamental source of the sweet taste of flowering stalks, the composition of sugar metabolites directly determines the difference in sweetness. ZC is highly expressed in four types of sweet substances, including monosaccharides (D-(+)-Trehalose), disaccharides (Maltose), oligosaccharides (Manninotriose and 4,3′,4′-trimethoxylavandulifolioside A) and sugar alcohols (D-Sorbitol, L-Arabinitol and Perseitol). The complex accumulation of multiple types of sugar compounds produces a significant taste synergistic effect, which may be the reason that ZC has a higher sweetness than other varieties. The low sweetness of GD is related to its single dependence on disaccharides, including sucrose, 6-α-D-Galactopyranos and Melezitose.

The differential distribution of sweet amino acids contributes to the enhanced taste profile of the flowering stalks. The modifying effect of amino acids on plant sweetness can enhance sweetness perception by forming hydrogen bonds with sweetness receptors through α-H and carbonyl-O, similar to the mechanism of glucose and amino acids synergistically enhancing sweetness (Wada et al., 2001). In contrast, no sweet-tasting amino acids were detected in ZC, suggesting that amino acid compounds may not be key metabolites influencing sweetness.

Key metabolites related to the bitterness in flowering stalks

Bitterness is an extremely undesirable trait that can significantly reduce the taste quality of flowering stalk. Therefore, it is important to clearly identify the compounds that play a key role in taste improvement. Terpenoids and alkaloids are primary contributors to bitter off-flavors; these natural metabolites are major products of plant secondary metabolism (Bai et al., 2025). The bitter taste of terpenoids and alkaloids is not formed by chance, it is a defense mechanism that plants have gradually developed over a long period of evolution, and this bitterness can effectively resist the gnawing of insects and the invasion of microorganisms, thereby helping plants survive more effectively in the natural environment (Deng et al., 2022; Tholl, 2015). In our study, a comprehensive analysis of metabolites associated with bitter taste in five flowering stalk varieties was conducted.

Terpenoids represent one of the most diverse classes of plant secondary metabolites, predominantly occurring in vegetable as monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, and their oxygenated derivatives. Terpenoids were the most abundant bitter taste compounds identified in GD, including Forskolin, 1-O-Deacetylkhayanolide E, Spicachlorantin C, (4Ar,4bS,8R,8aS,10aS)-1,1,8a-trimethyl-7-methylidene-8-(3-oxobutyl)-2,3,4,4b,5,6,8,9,10,10a-decahydrophenanthrene-4a-carbaldehyde, Trichilenone, 3beta-O-cis-p-coumaroyl-20beta-hydroxy-12-ursen-28-oic acid, α-L-Arabinopyranoside, (3-(7-((2-Hydroxy-3-methoxybenzoyl)amino)heptyl)-6-methyl-4,9-dioxo-8-(phenylmethyl)-1,5-dioxanon-7-yl) 2-methylpropanoate, Octadecanoic acid, Neocurdione and Buxhyrcamine. Although terpenoids are closely related to the bitter taste of plant-based foods due to their special chemical structure, most of the above-mentioned terpenoid metabolites are beneficial to the human body. For instance, Forskolin can lower blood pressure (Ju et al., 2021), and Trichilenone has hypoglycemic and antioxidant effects (Song et al., 2018).

Alkaloids are a class of nitrogen-containing alkaline organic compounds found in plants, most of which have complex cyclic structures with nitrogen often incorporated within the rings. They are typically bitter but exhibited significant biological activity (Behl et al., 2022). In our study, alkaloid metabolites (Jatrorrhizine Hydrochloride, 1H-indole and Pheophorbide A) exhibit down-regulated expression in GD. Jatrorrhizine Hydrochloride, a bitter-tasting compound, is one of the core alkaloid constituents in Coptis and has been demonstrated to possess multiple pharmacological activities, such as anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects (Jiang et al., 2018). The 1H-indole, which also shares the structural characteristics of indole alkaloids, shares a common skeleton with known bitter alkaloids (such as berberine), and this type of skeleton has been confirmed to generate bitter taste signals by binding to oral bitter taste receptors (such as TAS2Rs).

Glucosinolates, as sulfur-containing secondary metabolites unique to cruciferous plants, have been closely associated with bitter taste perception, as confirmed by numerous studies, and have become one of the research hotspots in plant taste chemistry and sensory science. Glucosinolates themselves and their related metabolites are the core substances that cause the bitter taste in vegetables such as mustard, broccoli, and kale (Xia et al., 2024). Even when the total glucosinolate content decreases during seed germination, they remain the main source of the bitter taste in broccoli sprouts. In our study, a total of four glucosinolate metabolites were identified among the DEMs. However, four glucosinolate metabolites were not down-regulated in GD, and 1-Ethyl-2-hydroxyethyl glucosinolate was up-regulated, indicating that glucosinolate compounds may not play a significant role in bitter taste formation in flowering stalks.

Key metabolites related to the astringency in flowering stalks

Bitterness and astringency are unpleasant taste that can significantly reduce the quality of flowering stalk. The results of electronic tongue analysis indicate a positive correlation between bitterness and astringency (Fig. 2C). Astringency not only significantly compromises the edible quality of plants, but also undermines their desirable traits of sweetness, crispness, and tenderness, ultimately leading to reduced consumer acceptance (Gonzalodiago et al., 2013). Astringency is often associated with organic acids, phenolic acids and flavonoids (Song et al., 2023).

Astringency is not a perception of taste but an oral tactile experience, characterized by a dry and rough sensation on the oral mucosa. The core formation mechanism has been confirmed by numerous studies to be the interaction between polyphenolic compounds and oral salivary proteins. The intensity of astringency perception varies significantly among individuals and has been shown to influence the acceptability of polyphenol-rich foods (Dinnella et al., 2011). In our study, the polyphenolic compounds Caffeic acid phenethyl ester and 4-ethylsyringol were down-regulated in GD, potentially serving as key metabolites contributing to the reduced astringency observed in GD. Caffeic Acid Phenethyl Ester has also been identified in coffee and is considered to be related to its astringency (Linne et al., 2025). 4-Ethylsyringol, as a hydroxybenzene derivative, exhibits structural homology with phenolic compounds associated with astringency in wine. Methylation modifications of phenolic compounds can modulate their interaction strength with salivary proteins, thereby influencing the perceived intensity of astringency (El Rayess et al., 2024).

Flavonoids, as an important class of secondary metabolites in vegetables, not only endow them with nutritional functions such as antioxidation and antibacterial properties, but also have a direct and crucial connection with the formation of astringency. The flavonoid compound (such as kaempferol 3-O-(2‴-O-sinapoyl-β-D-sophoroside)) in rapeseed protein not only has a bitter taste, but also cause a distinct astringency when accumulated in excess. The astringency threshold of this compound is only 0.3 μmol/L, which is much lower than that of other polyphenolic components (Spaccasassi et al., 2025). In our study, a total of six flavonoid metabolites (Baohuoside V, Amaronol B, Semiglabrinol, [1,1’-Biphenyl]-3-carboxylicacid,5′-(3,4-dihydro-3,5,7-trihydroxy-4-oxo-2H-1-benzopyran-2-yl)-2′,6-dihydroxy-(9CI), Lupinalbin C and Cyclomammeisin) were found to be significantly down-regulated in GD. These compounds are rich in polar functional groups, particularly hydroxyl groups, which can engage in hydrogen bonding or hydrophobic interactions with salivary proteins in the oral mucosa, leading to conformational changes and precipitation of the proteins, which in turn trigger the perception of astringency (Wang et al., 2023).

Organic acids can also produce astringency (Song et al., 2023). The core of this lies in the fact that organic acids cause a dry and rough sensation in the mouth through chemical reactions. Organic acids can combine with proteins such as mucoproteins in saliva to form precipitates, reducing the lubricating function of saliva and thereby triggering astringency (Lawless et al., 1996). However, none of the organic acids were found to be down-regulated in GD, thus we speculate that organic acids are not the main metabolites responsible for the astringency of flowering stalk.

Key metabolites related to the umami in flowering stalks

Umami is the core plant-based taste characteristic that distinguishes cruciferous vegetables from sweetness, and its metabolites are of great significance for variety improvement and taste optimization (Xia et al., 2024). The analysis combines various umami-related amino acids with the electronic tongue data. Although no compounds directly associated with umami taste were identified among those significantly upregulated in GD, the levels of two key amino acid metabolites, (−)-aspartic acid and L-glutamic acid, widely recognized as primary contributors to umami taste were higher in GD compared to the other four varieties. This result is highly consistent with the conclusions of numerous published studies, confirming the core role of these two amino acids in the formation of umami taste in plants. Aspartic acid and glutamic acid are two major umami amino acids in plant-based ingredients; their concentrations directly determine the intensity of umami taste, and they can synergistically enhance overall umami perception through gustatory synergy (Liu et al., 2022). During the germination process of broccoli, the total content of free amino acids can increase by approximately four times. Among them, the taste active value (TAV) of umami amino acids such as glutamic acid and aspartic acid significantly rises, directly enhancing the umami perception of broccoli sprouts (Xia et al., 2024).

5’-Nucleotide compounds are the core source of umami taste, among which 5′-inosine monophosphate (IMP) and 5′-guanosine monophosphate (GMP) are the most crucial. They are the main contributors to umami taste in natural foods and are widely used as umami enhancers in the food industry (Kurihara, 2015). In the nucleotide and derivative compounds with upregulated expression in GD, adenosine 5′-diphosphoribose was identified. There are no reports directly linking this compound to flavor, but we speculate that it may indirectly affect the umami quality of the flower stalks by regulating the purine metabolic pathway. Adenosine 5′-diphosphoribose is a key member of the purine metabolic pathway, and its metabolic transformation can directly affect the synthesis efficiency of downstream purine nucleotides (Huang et al., 2018).

The relationship between taste-related metabolites and electronic tongue sensor

To further clarify the intrinsic connection between taste-related metabolites and sensory taste value, a correlation analysis was performed (Fig. 8A). As illustrated in Fig. 8A, sugars including D-(+)-trehalose, maltose, and manninotriose, exhibited a significant positive correlation with sweetness intensity, which is consistent with the well-established role of soluble sugars as primary sweet taste contributors in flowering stalk. In contrast, alkaloid compounds generally showed a negative correlation with sweetness, presumably due to their inherent bitter or astringent properties that may antagonize the perception of sweet taste. For bitterness, the sensory attribute was mainly positively correlated with alkaloids and polyphenols, which aligns with previous studies demonstrating that these two classes of metabolites are the major determinants of bitter taste in plants (Ashihara and Crozier, 1999). Umami, a key savory taste attribute, was strongly and positively correlated with amino acids, nucleotides, and their derivatives; this observation is consistent with the fundamental taste characteristics of umami, as amino acids (glutamate, aspartate) and nucleotides (inosine monophosphate, guanosine monophosphate) are well-recognized umami enhancers in food matrices (Kurihara, 2015). Astringency, on the other hand, was closely associated with polyphenols and flavonoids, which is attributed to the ability of these compounds to bind to salivary proteins, inducing a dry, puckering sensation in the oral cavity (Gonzalodiago et al., 2013).

Notably, a striking feature of the correlation analysis was the presence of cross-modal associations of several metabolites across multiple taste attributes, which may collectively modulate the comprehensive taste profile of flowering stalks. Specifically, multiple flavonoids, terpenoids, alkaloids, and polyphenols were positively correlated with both bitterness and astringency, suggesting that these metabolites may be core contributors to the ‘astringent-bitter’ taste axis and may act synergistically to enhance the perception of these two negative taste attributes. Additionally, many terpenoids that were positively associated with bitterness and astringency showed a negative correlation with umami, indicating a potential antagonistic relationship between these metabolites and umami-related compounds. Interestingly, some sugars that positively contributed to sweetness also exhibited a positive correlation with umami; this cross-modal synergy may help improve the overall taste palatability of flowering stalks by enhancing both sweetness and umami. Collectively, these results clearly visualized the quantitative relationship between chemical components and sensory attributes through a correlation matrix, providing direct empirical evidence for the regulation of flowering stalk taste, the screening of functional metabolites, and the practical application of metabolomics in sensory taste research.

To further dissect the metabolic basis of taste differences among flowering stalk varieties, hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA) was performed on taste-related differential metabolites, which further revealed distinct metabolic phenotypic patterns across the tested varieties (Fig. 8B). The HCA heatmap clearly resolved five non-overlapping clusters, with each cluster strictly corresponding to one of the five tested flowering stalk varieties. This distinct clustering pattern indicates strong metabolic divergence among the varieties, which ultimately underlies their significant sensory differentiation in taste attributes. The clear separation of clusters also suggests that taste-related metabolites can serve as reliable chemical markers for distinguishing different flowering stalk varieties, providing a theoretical basis for variety identification and quality evaluation.

Taste Activity Value (TAV) is a core indicator for quantifying the contribution of individual metabolites to the taste of a sample, and its calculation formula is the ratio of metabolite concentration to taste threshold. To adapt to the rapid taste assessment of a large number of unknown threshold metabolites in non-targeted metabolomics, this study uses Relative Taste Activity Value (RTAV) as an equivalent alternative parameter to TAV. In this study, we conducted RTAV calculations on taste-related metabolites for which taste threshold values could be found (Table 2). Among the sweet-related metabolites, maltose had the highest contribution to sweet taste among all flowering stalk varieties. Sucrose exhibited a greater contribution to the sweetness of GD, while D-sorbitol was the major contributor to sweetness in the other four varieties. Among the compounds associated with bitterness and astringency, 1H-indole appeared to contribute relatively prominently to the bitterness and astringency of cultivar GD, whereas 4-ethylsyringol was involved in the bitterness of the other four varieties. For umami taste, L-glutamic acid was one of the important sources of umami in cultivar GD, whereas (−)-aspartic acid contributed more prominently to the umami taste in the other four varieties. These findings indicate that there were significant differences in the sweet, bitter, astringent and umami characteristics of RTAV between GD and the other four varieties, which were mainly regulated by the differential accumulation and synergistic contributions of various metabolites related to taste.

Overall, this study integrated sensory evaluation, electronic tongue analysis and widely targeted metabolomics technology to identify taste-related metabolites in five varieties of flowering stalk, providing a theoretical foundation for selecting high-quality, flavorful varieties. However, considering the actual consumption scenarios of flowering stalk, this vegetable is often consumed after simple heat processing such as blanching and scalding, which is also a key focus for subsequent research. Studies have shown that cooking methods such as boiling and steaming can influence the taste of vegetables, which promotes the leaching of water-soluble organic acids, sugars, and polyphenols into the cooking liquid, directly altering basic taste attributes, such as sourness, sweetness, and saltiness detected by electronic tongue (Buratti et al., 2020). The composition of glucosinolates and their hydrolysates in cruciferous vegetables was also significantly affected. The heat processing can inactivate myrosinase, inhibit the formation of spicy isothiocyanate, and reduce the content of nitriles and other undesirable flavor substances, thereby diminishing the inherent pungency of pak choi and significantly altering its overall flavor profile (Chen et al., 2019). Subsequent studies will concentrate on the effects of cooking methods on taste and associated metabolites in different flowering stalk varieties, fitting the daily dietary habits of the consumers.

Conclusion

In conclusion, five varieties of flowering stalk as experimental materials and combined electronic tongue analysis, sensory evaluation and the widely targeted metabolomics to systematically analyze the correlation between taste characteristics and metabolite taste. The electronic tongue analysis reveals significant differences among varieties in key taste attributes, including umami, sweetness, bitterness, and astringency. The Widely targeted metabolomics further analyzed the diversity of taste in five varieties of flowering stalk. Compared with the other four varieties, ZC has the highest content of soluble sugar, the greatest sweetness value, and the largest number of up-regulated expressed sugar compounds, including monosaccharides, oligosaccharides, and sugar alcohols. In the GD variety with the highest umami value and the lowest bitter and astringent values, two up-regulated umami amino acid compounds, three down-regulated alkaloid compounds, eleven terpenoid compounds, six flavonoid compounds and three polyphenol compounds were identified. Overall, the integration of electronic tongue analysis, sensory evaluation, and widely targeted metabolomics not only enables precise discrimination of taste profiles among flowering stalk varieties but also provides a scientific foundation for selecting high-quality cultivars and identifying key taste-associated metabolites.
