Foundational Image Analyses for Ultrasonographic Screening of Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm
Authors: Kazumasa Orihashi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Objectives: With improved surgical outcomes for non-ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA), the primary objective has shifted toward the detection of asymptomatic AAA. Since ultrasonographic visualization from the anterior abdominal wall is often obstructed by intestinal gas, utilizing additional bilateral posterior approaches via the retroperitoneal tissue may be beneficial. This study investigates the feasibility of assessment using three approaches through computed tomography (CT) data analyses.Methods: The study included 27 surgical patients with AAA (AAA group) and 37 patients with other atherosclerotic diseases (non-AAA group). CT data were analyzed to locate the infrarenal aorta relative to the navel, availability of acoustic window, depth of the aorta, and assessment of aneurysmal aorta using three approaches.Results: The “AAA area” for ultrasonographic screening may be set at 0–4 cm above the navel. An acoustic window was unavailable in 8.1% of AAA cases and 7.4% of non-AAA cases in the anterior approach; however, it was available in the posterior approach. Although the depth of the aorta was greater in obese patients, it remained within 20 cm.Conclusion: Ultrasonographic screening is feasible by incorporating posterior approaches in cases where anterior visualization is difficult, enhancing the detection of asymptomatic AAA.

Introduction

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) presents a significant health risk, often progressing asymptomatically until it suddenly and potentially fatally ruptures upon reaching a diameter exceeding 60 mm. While pre-rupture detection and treatment yield a low operative mortality rate of approximately 1%, historical data prior to 2000 indicate a post-rupture mortality rate ranging from 40% to 70%, which remains high at 17% even with endovascular aortic repair.1–3) Notably, these statistics do not encompass patients who succumb to rupture before reaching the operating room. As surgical outcomes for AAA have seemingly plateaued, the primary objectives moving forward are twofold: to prevent the development of aneurysms and to identify them before they rupture. While the 2020 Guideline addresses aneurysm prevention,1) the detection before rupture remains inadequate. Active screening has demonstrated efficacy in reducing rupture-related mortality by half,4,5) yet challenges persist in ultrasonography-based detection, with 3%–8% of patients proving difficult to assess and ultrasonographic sensitivity being inferior to computed tomography (CT).6–9) The primary issues with ultrasonography-based detection stem from intestinal gas interference and the depth from the body surface in obese patients, prompting exploration into alternative imaging approaches. The lateral approach via the intercostal space, reported by Studer et al.,10) has shown promise in mitigating these challenges, yet its applicability to the infrarenal region, where AAA commonly occurs, is limited. Drawing from insights gained in retroperitoneal AAA surgery, it was observed that the abdominal aorta can be distinctly visualized via the kidney and retroperitoneal tissue behind the colon, with acceptable image quality at a depth of 20 cm even using a portable ultrasonographic system (Fig. 1).1) Specifically, visualization sequences from the right side, progressing to the inferior vena cava (IVC) and then the abdominal aorta, and vice versa from the left side, were found to be effective. While potentially beneficial in cases where the anterior approach is difficult, this visualization method is still in its nascent stages. Although a feasibility study in a clinical setting is mandatory, this study aimed to examine the theoretical feasibility, excluding technical factors, by analyzing CT data to facilitate subsequent clinical feasibility studies.

The ABA and IVC are scanned from the dorsal side of the colon via the retroperitoneal tissue. The order of ABA and IVC depends on the approach side. The IVC to ABA from the right side (B), and vice versa from the left side (C). (D) The color signal is clearly depicted with acceptable image quality within 20 cm. ABA: abdominal aorta; IVC: inferior vena cava; CT: computed tomography

Materials and Methods

Subjects and data analyses

This study included 27 consecutive patients with AAA group and 37 patients with atherosclerotic diseases such as aortic stenosis, thoracic aortic aneurysm, and coronary artery diseases (non-AAA group) who underwent surgical treatment at Kochi Medical School Hospital from January to December 2023. Cases involving reoperation and emergency surgery were excluded. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethical Review Board of Kochi Medical School (ERB-110151). Basic demographic data, including age, sex, body weight (BW), and body mass index (BMI), as well as pre-surgery CT data (plain or late phase: 5 mm slice), were collected and analyzed offline using graphic software, CANVAS X (Nihon Poladigital Co., Tokyo, Japan), calibrated with the scale on each image.

Location of the infrarenal aorta

The navel served as a body surface landmark for scanning to visualize the infrarenal aorta (Fig. 2A). Level values were recorded at three points: (1) the navel level with the widest skin depression; (2) the proximal end adjacent to the takeoff of both renal arteries; and (3) the distal end just before aortic bifurcation. The levels of the latter two relative to the navel level (mm) were calculated, with a cephalad direction indicated as a positive value.

Fig. 2 Measurements in the computed tomograms. (A) Level values in the slices of the proximal and distal ends of the infrarenal area, navel, and inferior poles of the kidney were recorded. (B) While no acoustic window is available from the anterior approach, the window is available via both posterior approaches. (C) Good acoustic window and a narrow window. (D): Anterior approach to the aneurysm. (E) Posterior approaches to the aneurysm.

Availability of acoustic window

In the anterior approach, visualization of the aorta is often obstructed by gas in the transverse colon and small intestines (Fig. 2B), while these act as an acoustic window in the absence of gas (Fig. 2C). However, a narrow window poses challenges in visualization. In this study, an “acoustic window” was defined as a window larger than 2 cm, equivalent to the diameter of a normal abdominal aorta. The ratio of slices with an effective acoustic window to the total number of slices of the infrarenal aorta was calculated. For the posterior approach, where the kidney served as an acoustic window (Fig. 2B), the level value of the inferior pole of the kidneys (Fig. 2A) and the ratio of slices with the kidney were calculated.

Depth of aorta and accuracy of diameter measurement

In obese patients, the aorta appeared distant from the body surface, resulting in unclear ultrasonograms. For the anterior and left/right posterior approaches, the shortest distance from the body surface to the aorta was measured in the slice with an available acoustic window (Fig. 2C) and correlated with BW and BMI. Since the aortic size may have been underestimated when measured in limited slices, the anterior–posterior dimension of the aorta was measured in slices with an effective acoustic window for cases with an available acoustic window and compared with the largest size of the aorta in all slices (true size of the aorta) (Fig. 2D). In cases without an effective acoustic window from the anterior approach, depth and aortic dimension were measured in the left and right posterior approaches (Fig. 2E). The arterial diameter was measured using CANVAS X by determining the distance across the outer surface of the aorta on the CT image, with calibration based on the scale present in each image. Similarly, the depth of the aorta was measured using the same method.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using EZR (eazy R; Jichi Medical University Saitama Medical Center, Saitama, Japan) software.11) Given the sample size and unclear normality, data were expressed as the minimum to maximum value (median). Differences between the two groups were assessed using the Mann–Whitney test, and paired data were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank sum test. Categorical data were analyzed using the chi-squared test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Physical data

The AAA group comprised 19 male and 8 female patients, with ages ranging from 69 to 93 years (median: 79 years). The non-AAA group included 29 male and 8 female patients, aged 49–86 years (median: 76 years). Surgical treatments in the non-AAA group included coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) in 14 cases, aortic valve replacement in 10 cases (with CABG in two), and thoracic aortic aneurysm repair in 13 cases (with CABG in four). Although there were more male patients in both groups, no significant difference was observed between them (p = 0.465). However, ages were significantly higher in the AAA group (p = 0.009). The BW ranged from 42.6 to 110 kg (median: 60.0 kg) in the non-AAA group and 40.0 to 82.0 kg (median: 60.1 kg) in the AAA group, with no significant difference between the groups. Similarly, BMI ranged from 18.0 to 38.1 (median: 22.6) in the non-AAA group and 17.7 to 31.9 (median: 23.9) in the AAA group, also with no significant difference between the two groups.

Location of the infrarenal aorta

The distal end of the infrarenal aorta was located at –5.0 to +5.0 cm (median: –0.5 cm) in the non-AAA group and –3.0 to +5.0 cm (median: –1.1 cm) in the AAA group, with no significant difference observed between the groups (Fig. 2A). The proximal end was situated at +4.0 to +14.5 cm (median: +7.5 cm) and +5.5 to +11.0 cm (median: +8.5 cm), respectively, with the latter group tending to have a higher proximal end level, although this was not statistically significant (p = 0.080). The length of the infrarenal aorta was 6.0 to 11.4 cm (median 9.0 cm) in the non-AAA group and 6.0 to 13.0 cm (median 9.5 cm) in the AAA group, with a longer length observed in the latter group, albeit not statistically significant (p = 0.079).

Availability of acoustic window, depth, and accuracy of aortic diameter

In the anterior approach, the effective acoustic window was available in 56.3% (median) of slices of the infrarenal aorta in the non-AAA group and 72.2% (median) in the AAA group, with the latter showing a significantly higher percentage (p = 0.004). However, this availability varied widely, ranging from 0% to 100% in both groups. The absence of an acoustic window was noted in three cases (8.1%) of the non-AAA group and two cases (7.4%) of the AAA group. In all these cases, an acoustic window was available in both the right and left posterior approaches.

Among cases with an available acoustic window, the depth of the aorta ranged from 2.5 to 13.9 cm (median: 7.7 cm) in the non-AAA group and from 1.2 to 10.9 cm (median: 5.2 cm) in the AAA group, with the latter exhibiting a significantly shorter depth (p = 0.009). In the left posterior approach, an acoustic window was available in every case, with the depth of the aorta ranging from 9.5 to 17.9 cm (median: 13.9 cm), significantly longer than in the anterior approach (p <0.0001). However, in the right posterior approach, an acoustic window was unavailable in two cases due to the dorsal location of the ascending colon, resulting in the aorta being located behind the vertebral body (Fig. 3). In the remaining cases, the depth of the aorta ranged from 12.1 to 18.6 cm (median: 15.2 cm), significantly longer than in the anterior and left posterior approaches (p <0.0001), but still within 20 cm from the body surface.

Figure 4 illustrates the correlation between the actual maximal diameter of the aorta and the maximal diameter measured only in slices with an available acoustic window. The marker is on the red dotted line if both values are identical. Although the acoustic window was available in only two-thirds of the entire slices in 8 out of 25 cases (32.0%) in the AAA group, the underestimated size was at most 8 mm (median 1.6 mm), allowing for the correct assessment of abnormal aortic size. In five cases (13.5%) of the non-AAA group, where the aorta was larger than 3 cm (indicative of aneurysm) and already diagnosed and regularly followed up, the size exceeding the limit could be identified in the limited slices with an available acoustic window.

Fig. 4 Underestimation of aorta size in a limited acoustic window. Despite some underestimation, aneurysmal change (>3 cm) was correctly estimated.

Figure 5 demonstrates the influence of obesity on the depth of the aorta in the anterior approach. The depth showed a positive correlation with BW and BMI in both the non-AAA group (r = 0.761 for BW, r = 0.695 for BMI) and the AAA group (r = 0.710 for BW, r = 0.760 for BMI). In the posterior approach, the depth exhibited a strong correlation with both parameters, although the difference was less pronounced compared to the anterior approach.

Kidney as an acoustic window

In the posterior approaches, the presence of the kidney ranged from 10.5% to 100% (median: 66.7%) of slices on the right side and from 21.1% to 100% (median: 66.7%) on the left side in the non-AAA group. In the AAA group, it ranged from 18.2% to 100% (median: 68.8%) on the right side and from 33.3% to 100% (median: 57.1%) on the left side. Statistical analysis revealed no significant difference between groups or between the left and right sides. Renal cysts were frequently observed, especially on the inferior pole, and appeared to serve as an acoustic window as well.

Discussion

While both acoustic and technical factors contribute to difficult visualization, this study primarily focused on the former, investigating the impact of a limited acoustic window on the underestimation of aortic size, the depth of the aorta due to obesity, and the utility of the posterior approach.

A limited acoustic window was encountered in 8.1% and 7.4% of cases in the non-AAA and AAA groups, respectively, aligning with the reported incidence range of 3%–8%. Although this study defined an acoustic window as “a window larger than 2 cm,” larger windows may be achieved using a sector probe or by pressing the probe against the abdominal wall to displace intestinal gas. Despite concerns that a limited acoustic window might lead to underestimation of aortic size, aortas larger than 30 mm could be accurately assessed (Fig. 4). Although the depth of the aorta increased in patients with higher BW and BMI, it remained within the imaging range of portable ultrasonography.

Nevertheless, there are cases where the aorta cannot be visualized at all in the anterior approach. Bennett et al.6) reported a low incidence of abnormal aortas found during the second screening in subjects with non-visualized aortas, suggesting that a non-visualized aorta may be small enough to evade detection. However, this study showed that posterior approaches could compensate for visualization deficits in the anterior approach. While the depth increases in the posterior approach, it remains within 20 cm, and pressing the probe against the abdominal wall would reduce the subcutaneous fat thickness (approximately 40%: unpublished data). In cases where the ascending colon is dorsally located, the left posterior approach is advised.

In subsequent feasibility studies using three approaches in the clinical setting, it is important to consider who performs ultrasonographic screening. Conventional large hospital-based screening conducted by trained professionals may not reach all candidates, particularly in aging and depopulated societies. The need to screen 530 individuals to detect a single aneurysm poses a problem of efficiency.12) To address these limitations, a larger number of individuals in wider fields need to be involved with minimal burden. Family doctors, leveraging existing patient data and proficiency with abdominal ultrasonography, are potential candidates. Studies have shown comparable diagnostic accuracy between family doctors and radiologists.13,14) Recent studies also highlight the benefits of including AAA assessment during routine echocardiography examinations.15–17) Thus, screening might focus on patients with certain risk factors. With the widespread adoption of home medical care, portable ultrasonographic devices, feasible for abdominal aorta visualization,18,19) offer a convenient solution in home medical care settings.

For an effective introduction of such a screening system, a protocol for visualization would be helpful, regardless of who is assigned the task. Importantly, the aim of screening is not precise measurement of aortic size but rather accurate detection of aneurysmal aorta larger than 3 cm, similar to detecting asymptomatic valvular disease through auscultation. Analogous to valve-specific areas like “aortic valve area” used in auscultation, defining an “AAA area” could aid in ultrasonographic assessment of the aorta. In this study, the infrarenal aorta was typically situated between 0.5 cm below and 7.5 cm above the navel in median values. As aneurysms requiring surgical treatment are predominantly located in the lower two-thirds of the infrarenal aorta, the AAA area may be within 4 cm above the navel. Scanning at the midline is appropriate, as the normal-sized aorta typically remains undeviated laterally. Although aneurysms with a tortuous course need a wider area approach, the aneurysmal aorta is closer to the surface, especially in the AAA area, due to the anterior curvature of the lumbar spine. Key findings to confirm the aorta’s identity include (1) adjacency to the IVC, (2) pulsatile movement, and (3) a pulsatile color signal, all observable with portable ultrasonography. Color Doppler mode can be particularly useful when an image resembling the aorta is visualized without apparent pulsatile movement, which may be due to the rigidity of the aortic wall from calcification. In such cases, a pulsatile color image can aid in confirming the identification of the structure as the aorta. Video data stored in the portable ultrasonographic system enable sharing the movement and blood flow information with other examiners, facilitating validation of examination as well as contributing to improved visualization techniques. Moreover, video data enable telemedicine applications.20)

Figure 6 presents a proposed algorithm for ultrasonographic screening of AAA based on the results of this study. The “AAA area” is initially scanned transversely using the anterior approach, targeting a depth of 5–10 cm. If an aorta-like image is visualized, it is identified as the aorta by observing pulsatile movement or flow signal, achieved by either tilting the transversely placed probe or positioning it longitudinally. The aorta is then assessed to determine if it exceeds 30 mm. If the aorta is not identified or visualized, the scan shifts to the left or right posterior approach, targeting a depth of 10–20 cm, utilizing the kidney as an acoustic window if necessary. The aorta is identified in the same manner as described above.

Despite the limited evidence due to the small sample size from a single institution, this study provides foundational data for establishing future screening systems. The author anticipates subsequent studies to investigate further and reduce the incidence of fatal ruptures.

Conclusion

Analyses of CT data have demonstrated that ultrasonographic visualization of the abdominal aorta is feasible by incorporating the posterior approach alongside the conventional anterior approach. This combined approach provides acceptable accuracy for detecting abnormal aortic sizes within the scanning range of a portable ultrasonographic system. In addition, the “AAA area” for scanning the infrarenal region has been proposed, which will be the focus of subsequent feasibility studies in clinical settings.
