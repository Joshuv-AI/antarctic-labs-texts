Immigrant perspectives on navigating the US healthcare system in the Post-COVID-19 landscape: a photo-elicitation project
Authors: Colleen Laurence, Mari-Lynn Drainoni, Sarah L Kimball, Michael K Paasche-Orlow, Olanike Asupoto, Sarah Barnes, Annette Nansubuga, Benjamin P Linas, Lucy B Schulson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Social isolation of immigrants can be exacerbated during pandemics.•Free protective resources and translated public health information create trust.•Trauma and immigration-informed care is important for clinical, non-clinical staff.•Photo-elicitation can mediate barriers to participation but must be done carefully.Keywords: Immigrant health, Refugee health, Covid-19, Photo-elicitation

Introduction

Over 13% of the United States (US) population is foreign-born (Ward and Batalova, 2024). From 2021 to 2022, the US foreign-born population grew by 912,000 people, which is the largest annual increase since 2014 (Ward and Batalova, 2024). These new immigrants to the US must navigate a complex healthcare system, and their healthcare needs may be influenced by migration history, immigration and citizenship status, comorbidities, race, ethnicity, and income level, among other factors (Derose et al., 2007; Misra et al., 2021; Pillai et al., 2023). In studies conducted prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, new immigrants cited multiple barriers to care, including inconsistent interpreter use in healthcare settings, experiences with stigma, fear of deportation, and bureaucratic obstacles (Derose et al., 2007; Hacker et al., 2015; Vernice et al., 2020).

The US healthcare system has emerged from the COVID-19 pandemic greatly changed, impacting healthcare access for all patients, but even more so for recent immigrants. For example, telemedicine has become a more significant medium for outpatient visits (Cutler, 2023). While telemedicine may reduce barriers to care, such as transportation and scheduling difficulties, it has had lower uptake among patients with limited English proficiency due to variable access to technology and comfort with telephonic or video interpretation (Blackstone and Hauck, 2022; Rodriguez et al., 2021). Physicians, nurses, and other frontline workers have also left healthcare in historic numbers due to COVID-related stress and risk, and those who continue to work remain at risk for work-related burnout, anxiety, and depression; these mental states for clinicians have been shown to correlate with lower quality care (Cutler, 2023; Pathman et al., 2022). As a consequence, patients may have experienced delayed or interrupted access to healthcare, especially mental healthcare, and patients served in safety-net settings in particular may have experienced higher rates of clinician turnover (Pathman et al., 2022; Mengesha et al., 2022).

Despite the marked increase in immigration to the US, less is known about recent immigrants’ experiences navigating the healthcare system overall and especially after the pandemic. Understanding these experiences is particularly important in Massachusetts (MA) as it has experienced the largest growth in its immigrant population of any other state: there has been a 28-percent increase in the proportion of the state population who are immigrants since 2010 (Larson and Fujiwara, 2024). In this study, we used photo-elicitation, which uses photographs during the interview process, to gain insight into the experiences of recently arrived immigrants from Haiti and Uganda who sought care at a safety-net hospital in Boston, MA in navigating the US healthcare system during and in the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

Setting

This single-center study was conducted in a large, safety-net academic health system. Within the health system is a multidisciplinary Immigrant and Refugee Health Center that provides primary and mental healthcare, case management, and legal navigation. The patient population of this multidisciplinary center includes immigrants, refugees, and asylum seekers from over 80 countries, with the highest percentage coming from Haiti, Uganda, Somalia, Ethiopia, and Nigeria.

Sample and recruitment

This study focused on English-speaking Ugandan and English- or Haitian Creole-speaking Haitian patients who had arrived within two years of recruitment. Most Ugandan participants moved to the US to escape political persecution (Bureau of Democracy, Human Rights, and Labor 2023), and most Haitian participants moved due to escalating insecurity, political instability, and poverty in Haiti in the wake of natural disasters and the assassination of President Jovenal Moïse in 2021 (Douyon, 2023). Eligible participants were ≥18 years old, engaged in primary care at the Immigrant and Refugee Health Center, and possessed a phone with which to take photos. Recruitment occurred during a primary care clinic visit between November 2022-August 2023. Research Assistants (RAs) informed potential participants of the study details, and if interested, the RAs consented them in either English or Haitian Creole, respectively. The primary risk outlined during the informed consent related to patient confidentiality, which was felt to be minimal with appropriate measures taken to safeguard information. We used purposive sampling to ensure distribution of perspectives between Haitian and Ugandan participants and across genders. The study received ethical approval by the Boston University Institutional Review Board.

Data collection

In the photo-elicitation method, participants share representative images for concepts that might otherwise be challenging to describe, especially in a non-primary language (Collier and Collier, 1986). We selected photo-elicitation because it creates a bridge between the lived experiences of the interviewee and interviewer and is recognized as participatory, which may mediate some barriers to participation for immigrants (Harper, 2002; MacFarlane et al., 2021), a historically underrepresented group in research (Aldridge, 2014; Etti et al., 2021).

We asked participants to take 5–10 photos that represented their experiences navigating the US healthcare system and their perspectives of the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on these experiences. They were given a photo-taking tip sheet with information on how to take representative photos, photos as metaphors, and photos to avoid (i.e., any photos in which people are identifiable) (Suppl. File 1). All materials were translated into Haitian Creole by a certified translator.

The RAs who were trained in qualitative methods interviewed participants over zoom or in-person four weeks after recruitment. Interviews were 30–60 min in length and, for Haitian-speaking participants, were with a bilingual RA. Prior to the interviews, the participants emailed their photos to the RA. The photos served as the interview guide, and the RAs used additional scripted probes to encourage participants to reflect on the context of the photos, how they related to their everyday life as well as experiences navigating the US healthcare system, and about their experiences participating in the study. For example, the RAs asked questions like “What was it like for you to go around and take these photos?” and “what it is like for someone who was not born in the United States to manage their health and get by in this healthcare system [and if there are] photos that you think best help tell the story about what your experience has been like managing your health?” Those who completed the study received a $75 “clincard” and either a parking voucher or public transportation pass.

Data analysis

We recorded the interviews and then transcribed, deidentified, translated as needed, and uploaded them to Nvivo, a qualitative software program. Two researchers (CL and LBS) with experience in photo-elicitation, qualitative methods, and immigrant health coded and analyzed the transcripts. We reviewed 6 interviews and developed a preliminary codebook (CL, LBS, MLD). The codebook was modified as needed as we reviewed additional transcripts. All interviews were double-coded and subsequent themes identified and discussed by two researchers (CL and LBS). Disagreements were resolved through consensus. A thematic analysis was conducted using an inductive, grounded approach.

Results

Our sample included 19 participants: eight from Uganda and 11 from Haiti. Ten participants identified as female. The average age of participants was 37 (Range 26–60 years-old) (Table 1). We identified five central themes: (1) US response to COVID-19 compared to country of origin; (2) the US healthcare system as a welcoming, supportive space; (3) influence of isolation and longing for home on immigrant experience and social determinants of health (SDOH); (4) difficulties navigating a complex, foreign healthcare system; and (5) experiences participating in photo-elicitation. These themes and illustrative quotes from the interviews are described below. Table 2 presents additional examples of quotes by theme, and additional appendices present representative pictures.

US response to COVID compared to country of origin

Impressions of the US response

Participants were overall impressed by the US COVID-19 response - particularly, the ubiquity of free masks, hand sanitizers, and tests (Fig. 1). They felt that the US population seemed to take COVID “more seriously” than those in their countries of origin based on the number of people wearing masks and observed measures for distancing. They also felt that US healthcare institutions were committed to protecting everyone from COVID-19 as evidenced by media about COVID-19 in multiple languages (Fig. 2). A Ugandan participant (20) shared, “the US government is trying to sensitize the masses to protect themselves and to ensure that they are safe from COVID…they're going a long way to make sure that no one misses this kind of information.” Communication in multiple languages signaled that the US cared about sharing information equitably whereas, in Uganda, information was often only available in one language and thus inaccessible to segments of the population.

Abundant free masks – Ugandan participant (15).

Information about COVID-19 in different languages – Ugandan participant (15).

The US in contrast to their country of origin

Conversely, participants reflected on the relative lack of resources and lower quality of healthcare in their countries of origin, and Ugandan participants referenced corruption in Uganda, which engendered distrust in the government and affected how they perceived COVID-19. One participant remarked,

“Many people in Uganda think COVID is a scam… Because of the leadership … for example, IMF give[s] Uganda $2 billion in 2020… all that money was either stolen or misused or mishandled. So, people think government just uses this pandemic… to get money from them and then they never [get] the services.” – Ugandan participant (15)

Restrictive quarantine and mandatory masking measures and pre-migration trauma in Uganda occasionally engendered persistent distrust of COVID-related communications and public health efforts in the US. One participant suggested that this skepticism often manifested through vaccination hesitancy.

“In Africa, not only in Uganda where I come from, but in entire Africa, many people have very many thoughts and … myths about such things [vaccinations]. They will say, this [vaccination] is meant to … make us barren…This is meant to make us people who will die earlier… People will always have many myths about such vaccinations.” – Ugandan participant (15)

However, the same Ugandan participant (15) also applauded the impact of US public health communication and vaccination campaigns in quelling these concerns, noting “They [vaccination photo campaigns] are telling you that the vaccination won't cause any harm to you…the entire family can really have it and… all the colors, all the races…It's for all of us.”

Finally, several Haitian participants described Haiti’s healthcare system as under-resourced. One Haitian participant (1) remarked, “Haiti's health care system is far behind the US. If I had to give a score, the United States would be 10, Haiti would be 2. Haiti has qualified doctors, but they do not have the equipment.” This reinforces that Haiti’s healthcare system suffers from systematic deficits and lack of infrastructure to support good practice rather than impugning the care provided by clinicians.

The US healthcare system as a welcoming, supportive space

Participants were largely positive about their experiences with the US healthcare system. They perceived medical services as reliable, high-quality, and broadly accessible. The healthcare system also supported their social needs such as housing, food, and legal support. Participants appreciated the thoroughness and inclusivity of their clinicians and how clinicians explained care processes and engaged them in decision-making. One participant (21) remarked, “[the US healthcare system] has offered excellent services to me that I did not experience back home in Uganda. I have seen everything being professional. I was oriented on what the doctors intended to do and the purpose of the blood collection.” The routine use of interpreters and provision of translated materials further reinforced a commitment to providing culturally responsive care (Fig. 3).

Welcoming language and environment – Haitian participant (31).

Influence of isolation and longing for home on immigrant experience and social determinants of health (SDOH)

Participants shared experiences of social isolation. Participants longed for certain familiar aspects of daily life and social connections that were difficult to come by (Fig. 4). Without work permits and with transportation and financial constraints, participants often felt confined to their residences or shelters, limiting their ability to establish social connections. The importance of social support was often referenced in relation to participants’ health and well-being. One participant lamented,

“Sometimes there are days when everything goes well and days when everything goes badly, you have pains everywhere, headaches thinking about how you live here, sometimes you stress, you grieve because your family, your child and your husband are far away from you, you live alone with a baby, you are unemployed.” - Haitian participant (33)

Planting tomatoes, reminders of home country – Haitian participant (29).

This lack of social support, compounded by other stressors (e.g., housing, lack of childcare support, and food insecurity), negatively affected their well-being (Fig. 5). These circumstances led to a sense of resignation for some participants, whereas others expressed hope that things would improve despite these challenges.

Taking care of a baby alone in the US – Haitian participant (31).

Difficulties navigating a complex, foreign healthcare system

Participants also shared their difficulties with the US healthcare system: reconciling differences between the healthcare systems in the US and their countries of origin, navigating US insurance complexities, tackling administrative bureaucracy, and understanding medication availability.

Urgent care availability

Participants were grateful to have primary care clinicians, but expressed frustration, surprise, and helplessness at how long they had to wait to see them, especially when acutely ill. “It's not been easy because navigating the healthcare system here is not something you just bump into like you want to go in,” said a Ugandan participant (24); “you have to schedule an appointment, you have to make these calls, make sure they fit you in…And when it comes to health, some things cannot wait.” This was often an adjustment from the model of care in their countries of origin where clinics offered primarily urgent, walk-in care.

Insurance limitations

One of the most significant challenges participants experienced was navigating US health insurance. Many participants expressed gratitude for statewide safety-net health insurance programs, which includes emergency health services, inpatient care, and certain outpatient and pharmacy services regardless of immigration status as well as additional tiers of services for established residents. However, participants still found the application process for these programs to be intimidating and the limited coverage to be frustrating, especially for medications. One Ugandan participant (23) had a negative interaction with an employee assisting them with safety-net insurance, which influenced their subsequent hesitancy to return stating, “I don’t feel like it’s safe for me to go there [patient financial services]. They didn’t treat me well…it’s according to the way someone treated me the first time that makes you feel like going back or not going back, and the way they [patient financial services] treated me made me feel like I shouldn’t go back.” Singular positive and negative experiences with clinical and non-clinical staff in the healthcare system left indelible impressions on participants, which could influence subsequent healthcare engagement.

One Ugandan participant (24) shared an encounter with a pharmacist in which other patients overheard that their insurance would not cover a medication, “They [other people] look at you as if you're a fraud or something. It's quite uncomfortable” (Fig. 6). Participants shared frustration, confusion, and shame as they reconciled mixed messages from clinicians who seemed unaware of the cost of medications and medication coverage; participants reported being forced to cobble together money for copays and negotiate administrative confusion with insurance companies.

Experiences at pharmacies in US – Ugandan participant (25).

Over-the-counter (OTC) medications and knowledge to self-manage

Participants expressed both enthusiasm and caution regarding the availability of OTC medications. In their home countries, common US OTC medications (e.g., acetaminophen) were accessed through a clinician or pharmacist. While participants appreciated easy access to these medicines, they also expressed concerns. A Ugandan participant (25) stated, “This kind of self-medication [with OTCs], you need to have the ability to read and understand. And then, the challenge with it is that…you'll find different kinds of medicine but with similar or same name and…it becomes very difficult to tell which ones you need specifically and what is the difference between.” This tension highlights the level of health literacy needed to navigate the formal and informal aspects of the US healthcare system (Fig. 7).

Availability of OTC medications, need for health literacy to navigate – Haitian participant (30).

Experiences participating in photo-elicitation

Positive experiences

Participants enjoyed taking part in research and felt that their contributions were important. One Ugandan participant (23) stated, “This is the platform that will help me share what I went through so that they can do something about it to help another person who is planning to come here.” In addition to “paying it forward,” others noted that taking pictures made them pay attention to their surroundings and learn more about COVID and the US healthcare system.

Negative experiences and challenges encountered

Conversely, other participants shared concerns about consent, confidentiality, and perceived transgressions with the photo-elicitation project. Specifically, some participants noted that as recent immigrants, they felt conscious of their race and immigration status while taking pictures. One shared,

“Yes, I was scared… I'm a guy who is here on asylum. I'm an immigrant…I'm an African …so here I'm taking pictures of these people’s businesses. You see when you stand outside sometimes and you take the pictures, they may not exactly know what your… [intention] was.” – Ugandan participant (14)

Other participants found it challenging to capture appropriate healthcare-related settings or objects while also preserving confidentiality. Finally, some participants questioned the methodology itself, wondering whether a photo could capture their thoughts around a disease as complex as COVID or their experiences with healthcare. Despite these challenges, participants found creative ways to adapt if they did not feel comfortable taking photos, such as finding pictures on Google that exemplified their experiences and using pictures taken by friends or family.

Discussion

In this study of the experiences of recently arrived Haitian and Ugandan individuals, we found that participants were satisfied with the US public health response to COVID-19, and despite feelings of isolation and the challenges of navigating their healthcare, participants felt that they received comprehensive healthcare. In reflecting on healthcare experiences in the US compared to their countries of origin, Ugandan participants frequently commented on corruption in Uganda whereas Haitian participants characterized the healthcare system in Haiti as under-resourced. Although participants felt that photo-elicitation allowed them to convey their perspective, they occasionally felt uncomfortable taking pictures.

The resources available (i.e., masks) and widespread translation of COVID public health information in the US compared to their countries of origin conveyed to participants the US’s ongoing concern for the COVID-19 pandemic and its commitment to protecting diverse populations from the virus. For some, these actions engendered trust and an increased likelihood of getting the COVID-19 vaccine, potentially contributing to equivalent rates of COVID-19 vaccination among US immigrants and non-immigrants in one study (Nolan et al., 2024). This is in contrast to prior research suggesting that inadequate public health COVID-19 messaging for immigrant populations in the US led to high rates of transmission and vaccine hesitancy in this population (Singer et al., 2023; Hayward et al., 2021; Brickhill-Atkinson and Hauck, 2021). Our findings may differ from prior research for several reasons. First, many of the participants in this study arrived in the US after the early waves of the pandemic when public health messaging may have improved. Second, some of the messaging participants noted may have been from this academic health system specifically. Finally, our participants represent two recently arrived immigrant groups compared to larger immigrant pools in other studies, and they were recruited from an engaged patient population within the health system’s Immigrant and Refugee Health Center, which intentionally provides and advocates for language support services. Regardless, we found that translated public health information is meaningful to immigrant populations and is a potential means to address distrust and fear contributing to vaccine hesitancy.

Participants remarked on how this health care organization made them feel safe, welcomed, and included in decision-making. For many, this was in contrast to their healthcare experiences in their countries of origin (Norman, 2015). Participants described how clinical staff provided care that was culturally-responsive and sensitive to their past trauma, embodying many characteristics of immigration-informed care (Saadi et al., 2019) and trauma-informed care. However, participants also noted that some interactions with non-clinical staff were difficult, as with one participant who felt inappropriately treated by financial services staff. These difficult interactions and any subsequent sense of isolation could have been exacerbated after COVID, though literature predating COVID suggests that lack of cultural responsiveness and linguistic competence by non-clinical personal negatively impacted the healthcare experiences of immigrant patients at that time as well (Calo et al., 2015).

Moving forward, health care organizations may need to provide additional cross-cultural and sensitivity training for non-clinical staff, acknowledging that immigrants’ impressions of healthcare systems and their vulnerabilities within it are based on more than clinical encounters alone (Saadi et al., 2019). In a health system, even small touchpoints with patient registration or financial services are opportunities to embrace strategies that are effective for individuals who may be unfamiliar with the American healthcare system, may have a trauma history, or may have concerns about disclosure of their citizenship status or identity. Additionally, such health care organizations can be more patient-centered in meeting the needs of recent immigrants by creating urgent care appointments or walk-in hours, similar to healthcare models in their countries of origin. These organizational and scheduling adjustments may also provide a foundation for a stronger alliance, potentially allowing patients to attend more appointments and demonstrating that health care organizations are responsive to the needs of all patients, including under-resourced populations who may have to overcome multiple barriers to attendance (Falicov et al., 2020).

This study reinforced that immigration status is a social determinant of health and influences the social support systems (i.e., family, friends, and community) available to immigrants. Other literature has suggested that isolation related to recent immigration was exacerbated during COVID-19 for asylum seekers and undocumented immigrants in particular as they experienced pandemic-fueled xenophobia and more isolation without established support or familiarity in the US (Mengesha et al., 2022). Isolation and loneliness can profoundly affect immigrant well-being and mental health (Mengesha et al., 2022; Hayward et al., 2021), worsening an already higher burden of PTSD as well as anxiety and depression (Singer et al., 2023). While the issues of immigrant isolation and mental health are complex phenomena with no unifying solution, health care organizations should consider how they can create inclusive, welcoming public spaces for all individuals and advocate for legislation that allows immigrants to work and live without fear such as expediting work authorizations (Wang et al., 2022).

Finally, there are both strengths and weaknesses in using photo-elicitation for research with immigrant populations. The photo-elicitation method can empower marginalized populations and support co-production of knowledge (Ortega-Alcázar and Dyck, 2012). Similar to prior research, photo-elicitation brought the researchers into the participants’ worlds (Kyololo et al., 2023; Dwyer and Buckle, 2009). However, we also identified some unintended consequences of this approach; the intersection of race and tenuous immigration status made some participants feel uncomfortable taking pictures. Future studies should explore the best approaches for conducting photo-elicitation research. Actions might include noting this risk during the consent process, providing participants with an explanatory research identification card should they be questioned, and facilitating regular debriefs during and after the research process during which time participants could share any discomfort experienced and potential remedies for it.

Limitations

Our study may not be generalizable to the experiences of all immigrants or other health care organizations given its focus on two of the largest immigrant populations served by this institution. Furthermore, our participants already had established relationships with clinicians and support staff in the Immigrant and Refugee Health Center specializing in the care of these populations, so they may not be representative of immigrants who are not yet in care or do not receive specialty support. While our study found some shared perspectives among Haitian and Ugandan participants, respectively, in their impressions of healthcare in their countries of origin, we did not feel that we had adequate data to perform an in-depth analysis and compare experiences of these two immigrant communities on other topics. We did distinguish between the experiences of these groups by specifying country of origin with each participant quoted. Although participants attributed their experiences - both good and bad - to the U.S. government and healthcare system, some of those experiences may be more reflective of the context in Massachusetts or this specific institution, and the themes may not be generalizable. Our participants experienced variable levels of comfort with photo-elicitation, and the method itself relies on taking pictures of “stimuli”, so our project’s data, while rich, may not be comprehensive depending on participant exposure and comfort level.

Conclusion

In this single-center qualitative study of Haitian and Ugandan immigrants using photo-elicitation, most participants felt that public health communications during the pandemic were effective, and availability of personal protective equipment was appropriate. We also found that this institution and its multidisciplinary Immigrant and Refugee Health Center played a critical role in supporting new immigrants with their medical and social needs. Institutions can become more patient-centered by advocating for greater support for immigrants through clinician and non-clinician staff training in immigration-informed care and trauma-informed principles and by considering alternative clinic visit models. We also found that immigration status is an important social determinant of health with many participants experiencing heightened social isolation, which was exacerbated by COVID-19 and impacted their physical and mental health. Health systems and policy efforts may consider ways to reduce isolation for these populations. Finally, photo-elicitation offers a unique opportunity for historically marginalized immigrant populations to co-produce and direct research discourse; however, researchers must be conscious of the ethical challenges implicit in the method.
