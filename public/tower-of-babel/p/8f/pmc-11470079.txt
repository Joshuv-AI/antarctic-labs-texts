The PANoptosis-related hippocampal molecular subtypes and key biomarkers in Alzheimer’s disease patients
Authors: Chen-Long Li, Qi Wang, Li Wu, Jing-Yi Hu, Qi-Chao Gao, Xin-Long Jiao, Yu-Xiang Zhang, Shan Tang, Qi Yu, Pei-Feng He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s Disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disorder, and various molecules associated with PANoptosis are involved in neuroinflammation and neurodegenerative diseases. This work aims to identify key genes, and characterize PANoptosis-related molecular subtypes in AD. Moreover, we establish a scoring system for distinguishing PANoptosis molecular subtypes and constructing diagnostic models for AD differentiation. A total of 5 hippocampal datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. In total, 1324 protein-encoding genes associated with PANoptosis (1313 apoptosis genes, 11 necroptosis genes, and 31 pyroptosis genes) were extracted from the GeneCards database. The Limma package was used to identify differentially expressed genes. Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was conducted to identify gene modules significantly associated with AD. The ConsensusClusterPlus algorithm was used to identify AD subtypes. Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) was used to assess functional and pathway differences among the subtypes. The Boruta, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), Random Forest (RF), and Support Vector Machine Recursive Feature Elimination (SVM-RFE) algorithms were used to select the three PANoptosis-related Key AD genes (PKADg). A scoring model was constructed based on the Boruta algorithm. PANoptosis diagnostic models were developed using the RF, SVM-RFE, and Logistic Regression (LR) algorithms. The ROC curves were used to assess the model performance. A total of 48 important genes were identified by intersecting 725 differentially expressed genes and 2127 highly correlated module genes from WGCNA with 1324 protein-encoding genes related to PANoptosis. Machine learning algorithms identified 3 key AD genes related to PANoptosis, including ANGPT1, STEAP3, and TNFRSF11B. These genes had strong discriminatory capacities among samples, with Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC) analysis indicating Area Under the Curve (AUC) values of 0.839, 0.8, and 0.868, respectively. Using the 48 important genes, the ConsensusClusterPlus algorithm identified 2 PANoptosis subtypes among AD patients, i.e., apoptosis subtype and mild subtype. Apoptosis subtype patients displayed evident cellular apoptosis and severe functionality damage in the hippocampal tissue. Meanwhile, mild subtype patients showed milder functionality damage. These two subtypes had significant differences in apoptosis and necroptosis; however, there was no apparent variation in pyroptosis functionality. The scoring model achieved an AUC of 100% for sample differentiation. The RF PANoptosis diagnostic model demonstrated an AUC of 100% in the training set and 85.85% in the validation set for distinguishing AD. This study identified two PANoptosis-related hippocampal molecular subtypes of AD, identified key genes, and established machine learning models for subtype differentiation and discrimination of AD. Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disorder and the most common cause of dementia, characterized by a gradual loss of memory, cognitive function, and daily living abilities in patients. AD poses a significant health burden across the globe1,2. The pathogenesis of AD is influenced by complex interactions between genetic and environmental factors3,4. Notably, the primary pathological features of AD include the presence of amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles5. β-amyloid (Aβ) plaques are the initial manifestation during the progression of AD, followed by the development of tau neurofibrillary tangles, eventually resulting in neuronal damage and cognitive impairment. Nonetheless, the precise mechanisms underlying the onset of the disease are scarcely understood6–8.

PANoptosis is an inflammatory programmed cell death pathway characterized by pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis features9. Pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis pathways were conventionally believed to operate independently without significant overlap. However, scientists have found extensive crosstalk and regulatory interactions among them, resulting in the establishment of the concept of PANoptosis. Existing evidence suggests that PANoptosis is driven by caspase and RIPK and regulated by a multiprotein PANoptosome complex. It is usually linked to biological processes including inflammation, infection, immune responses, and tissue recovery, involving multiple molecules as well as signaling pathways10,11. Recent studies have noted the important role of PANoptosis in various cancers12.

The neuronal damage in AD involves various cell death processes, and recent studies have also shown the involvement of necroptosis and pyroptosis in various pathological processes of AD, including Aβ amyloid aggregation, Tau accumulation, neuronal death, and blood-brain barrier damage13–16. Importantly, several molecules participating in PANoptosis (AIM2, Caspase-8, Caspase-1, RIPK3, and MLKL) are associated with neuroinflammation and neurodegenerative diseases17–21. Furthermore, in the 5xFAD mouse model, the lack of AIM2 reduces Aβ deposition and activation of microglial cells22. This suggests that PANoptosis regulates the progression of AD. However, the relevant biomarkers of PANoptosis in AD are significantly lacking, and their specific roles remain unclear, necessitating more extensive research to advance the understanding of PANoptosis in AD.

Machine learning algorithms, a type of artificial intelligence technology, are highly valued for their high fault tolerance and accurate results, making them particularly effective in complex scenarios. They can be used to eliminate redundant data and identify key factors, and are increasingly favored in precision medicine23. Additionally, they have shown great applicability in the field of AD24.

This study aims to identify AD-related biomarkers of PANoptosis through transcriptomic analysis and to explore the molecular subtypes of AD from the perspective of PANoptosis, with the goal of enhancing the understanding of PANoptosis in AD. AD samples were collected from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Based on PANoptosis-related genes, 48 important genes were identified using weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and differential analysis. Additionally, consensus clustering was adopted to identify two PANoptosis-related molecular subtypes in AD. Subsequently, machine learning algorithms were used to identify three PANoptosis-related Key AD genes (PKADg). A scoring system was further established to investigate the effect of the 48 important genes on AD patients and constructed a diagnostic model. Our findings provide a novel direction for further research into the pathological characteristics of AD.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

A total of 1324 protein-coding genes related to PANoptosis were obtained from the GeneCards database, including 1313 apoptosis-related genes, 11 necroptosis-related genes, and 31 pyroptosis-related genes, with a selection criterion of a relevance score greater than 3. The datasets GSE1297, GSE28146, GSE36980, GSE48350, and GSE5281 from the GEO database contain bulk RNA sequencing data from different brain regions of AD patients and healthy controls. We excluded all non-hippocampal samples and technical replicates, only one was selected. The GSE63061 dataset includes Blood Bulk RNA sequencing data from AD patients, patients with mild cognitive impairment, and healthy controls, from which we selected the AD and healthy control samples. The GSE175814 dataset contains single-cell sequencing data from the brain tissue of AD patients and healthy controls, and we used the AD samples from this dataset. Table 1 shows comprehensive sample information. Raw files from the bulk RNA sequencing datasets were acquired, and the R oligo package was used to read the data from the raw CEL files. The data was then standardized and expression levels were computed using the RMA method. Subsequently, the data was stored as a gene expression matrix for further analysis. Additionally, the combat function from the Sva package was adopted to remove batch effects between datasets. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to observe sample distribution before and after batch effect removal. The single-cell dataset GSE175814 was first analyzed using the Seurat package in R. Genes expressed in at least 5 cells and cells with at least 300 detected genes were retained. Additionally, to ensure the selection of high-quality cells, we filtered out cells with fewer than 2,500 detected genes and those with less than 5% mitochondrial gene expression for further analysis. After selecting an appropriate clustering resolution, all cells were clustered. Then, suitable cell markers were chosen using the CellMarker 2.0 database to annotate cell types before proceeding with further analysis.

Identification of differentially expressed genes

Differentially expressed genes between AD patients and normal individuals were identified using the “limma” package in the R programming language. The expression levels among different samples were standardized using the “quantile” method. Linear modeling was performed with the “lmFit” function, and a Bayesian approach, implemented with the “eBayes” function, was used to adjust the coefficients of the linear model, reducing estimated variance to enhance the accuracy and stability of the estimates. Final selection criteria were set as |logFC| greater than or equal to 0.58, and an adjusted p-value (adj.P) less than or equal to 0.05, to establish differential gene selection.

Functional enrichment analysis

We conducted GO and KEGG enrichment analysis on the obtained differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using the online biomedical research tool Metascape25, . The parameters used for analysis included a minimum overlap of 3, a p-value cutoff of 0.05, and a minimum enrichment of 1.5.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

WGCNA can reveal co-expression patterns among genes and organize related genes into modules for further exploration of their relationship with biological characteristics. We initially excluded ineligible samples using the “WGCNA” package in the R programming language. We calculated and selected the optimal soft threshold using the “pickSoftThreshold” function. Subsequently, we constructed an adjacency matrix for the gene co-expression network using the “adjacency” function, which was then transformed into a Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM). After calculating the similarity between genes (1-TOM), we grouped genes with similar expression patterns into the same modules, setting the minimum module size to 80. Modules with a similarity exceeding 75% were merged. Eventually, the “cor” function and “corPvalueStudent” were used to compute the correlation and p-values between each module and the phenotypes.

Machine learning algorithms were employed to filter key AD genes related to PANoptosis

Four algorithms—the Boruta algorithm, the LASSO algorithm, the RF algorithm, and the SVM-RFE algorithm—were employed to identify 48 significant AD genes related to PANoptosis, derived from the intersection of PANoptosis-related genes, differentially expressed genes, and WGCNA module genes. Ultimately, three PKADgs were obtained. Boruta can select all features relevant to the response variable rather than minimizing the cost function of the model. LASSO is a linear model regularization method used for feature selection and regression analysis. RF is a powerful and extensively applied ensemble learning algorithm in machine learning and data science. SVM-RFE identifies an optimal hyperplane to maximize the margin between different classes, enabling effective classification and regression. The implementation of these algorithms was separately conducted using the “Boruta,” “glmnet,” “randomForest,” and “caret” packages in the R programming language. Additionally, the GTEx portal was used to display the expression of important genes across various brain tissues in healthy individuals.

The ConsensusClusterPlus method was employed to identify PANoptosis-related subtypes in AD patients

This study conducted a Consistency Cluster Analysis using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” package in R to identify potential AD PANoptosis subtypes26. In this process, a hierarchical clustering algorithm was selected, and both inner and final clustering linkage methods used the Ward distance. A total of 1000 iterations were performed to establish the optimal number of clusters and reveal potential cluster structures in the data. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and heatmap visualization were employed to demonstrate sample distributions and inter-group differences among different clusters.

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA)

To further investigate differences in functionality and pathways between different subtypes, gene set variation analysis was performed using the R package “GEVA.” The gene set information related to GO and KEGG pathways and functions was sourced from enrichment analysis results and literature research. The corresponding gene sets were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB), version 2023.2.

Constructing the AD PANoptosis subtype scoring model

We initially reduced the dimensionality of the intersection genes using the Boruta algorithm to determine the AD PANoptosis subtype scoring system. Subsequently, based on previous studies27, we computed the Pearson correlation between the expression of each gene and the phenotype data. Further, we conducted PCA analysis using the prcomp function on positively and negatively correlated genes separately, resulting in two principal components, i.e., PCA_A and PCA_B. Constructing the PANoptosis diagnostic model for AD

The samples were randomly divided into training and validation sets at a 1:1 ratio to establish a tool for AD identification. We used the RF algorithm, SVM-RFE algorithm, and logistic regression to develop an AD diagnostic model. 10-fold cross-validation was used during model training. ROC analysis demonstrated the model’s performance. The model was constructed using the caret package in R, with the following detailed parameters:: method = “repeatedcv”, number = 10, repeats = 10, classProbs = TRUE, summaryFunction = twoClassSummary, allowParallel = TRUE, metric = “ROC”.

Identifying gene modules significantly associated with AD

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis was conducted on GSE48350 using the “WGCNA” package. First, we identified and removed three outlier samples from the dataset using hierarchical clustering. We computed the soft-threshold value using the pickSoftThreshold function. A soft threshold (β) of 8 (R2 = 0.867) was selected to meet the requirement for a scale-free network topology. Subsequently, we classified genes with similar expressions into the same modules and merged modules with a similarity above 75% (Fig. 3A–D). Ultimately, eight modules were obtained, with the black module showing the highest positive correlation with AD (Cor = 0.46, P = 3 × 10−4), containing 538 genes, whereas the brown module had the highest negative correlation with AD (Cor = −0.53, P = 1 × 10−5), including 1589 genes (Fig. 3E).

Identifying PKADgs using machine learning algorithms

A total of 48 important genes were obtained by intersecting 1324 protein-coding genes related to PANoptosis, 725 differentially expressed genes, and 2127 highly correlated module genes identified through WGCNA (Fig. 5A). Supplementary Table 1 shows the differential fold changes of the 48 important genes between AD and healthy controls. Furthermore, we conducted an enrichment analysis on these 48 genes. GO enrichment analysis revealed their enrichment in processes related to positive regulation of cell apoptotic process, positive regulation of phosphorus metabolic process, enzyme-linked receptor protein signaling pathway, negative regulation of the immune system process, non-developmental growth, positive regulation of cell migration, negative regulation of cell differentiation, positive regulation of peptidyl-serine phosphorylation, gliogenesis, cellular response to growth factor stimulus, regulation of plasma membrane bounded cell projection organization, focal adhesion, and more (Fig. 5B). KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that these genes were enriched in pathways including the MAPK signaling pathway, proteoglycans in cancer, PD-L1 expression and PD-1 checkpoint pathway in cancer, pathways of neurodegeneration - multiple diseases, and more (Fig. 5C). We also assessed the expression of the 48 important genes across various cell types in the brains of AD patients using single-cell data (Supplementary Fig. 1). The markers used for cell type annotation are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. Additionally, using the GTEx portal, we evaluated the expression of these 48 important genes in different regions of healthy brain tissue (Supplementary Fig. 3).

48 important genes. (A) The intersection of genes related to PANoptosis differentially expressed genes and highly correlated module genes. GO enrichment (B) and KEGG enrichment (C) of the 48 important genes.

To further pinpoint key genes, four machine learning algorithms were used, including, Boruta, LASSO, RF, and SVM-RFE, to reduce the dimensionality of the 48 important genes. Ultimately, we identified 3 PKADgs, i.e., ANGPT1, STEAP3, and TNFRSF11B (Fig. 6A–F). All three PKADgs had significantly higher expression in AD samples. ROC analysis demonstrated their excellent capacity to distinguish between AD and control group samples, with AUC values of 0.839, 0.8, and 0.868 for ANGPT1, STEAP3, and TNFRSF11B, respectively (Fig. 7A,B). Additionally, we validated these findings in another dataset (GSE36980), which showed that both ANGPT1 and STEAP3 were significantly upregulated in AD samples with good sample discrimination capabilities, with AUC values of 0.825 and 0.812, respectively (Fig. 7C,D). Additionally, based on single-cell data, we found that ANGPT1 is expressed in various cell types, with a relatively higher concentration in astrocytes and spiral ganglion neurons; STEAP3 is more concentrated in microglia; and TNFRSD11B is expressed more diffusely across different cell types (Supplementary Fig. 1).

AD patients’ PANoptosis subtypes

To identify PANoptosis subtypes in AD patients, we merged hippocampal samples from AD patients in five datasets, GSE1297, GSE28146, GSE36980, GSE48350, and GSE5281, resulting in a total of 81 AD samples. The combat function was used to remove batch effects between datasets. PCA analysis showed the sample distribution before and after batch effect removal (Fig. 8A,B). Subsequently, based on 81 AD samples, hierarchical clustering analysis was performed using the ConsensusClusterPlus package with 1000 iterations to divide the samples into two subtypes, i.e.,cluster1 and cluster2, based on considerations of CDF values and the incremental area under the CDF curve (Fig. 8C–E). PCA analysis showed the sample distribution of the two subtypes (Fig. 8F). A heatmap showed significant differences in the expression of the 48 important genes between the two subtypes (Fig. 9A).

Further, we conducted differential expression analysis between the two subtypes, resulting in 342 differentially expressed genes, including 119 downregulated and 223 upregulated genes. Subsequently, enrichment analysis was performed on these 342 differentially expressed genes. GO enrichment revealed that these genes were majorly enriched in functions related to the presynapse, axon, behavior response to stimulus, synaptic signaling, synaptic membrane, neuron projection development, cell body, inorganic molecular entity transmembrane transporter activity, sarcolemma, regulation of monoatomic ion transport, calmodulin binding, regulation of neuron projection development, modulation of chemical synaptic transmission, and synaptic organization (Fig. 9B). KEGG enrichment showed that these genes were majorly enriched in pathways including oxytocin signaling, retrograde endocannabinoid signaling, calcium signaling, rheumatoid arthritis, gap junction, and GABAergic synapse (Fig. 9C).

Furthermore, we computed the differences in PKADg expression between the two subtypes. The expression of all three PKADgs was significantly higher in cluster1 compared to cluster2 (Fig. 9D).

GSVA analysis was conducted to further elucidate the functional and pathway differences between the two subtypes (Fig. 10). The results revealed that cluster1 had higher positive regulation of apoptosis and apoptosis pathway scores, suggesting a higher level of apoptosis. Moreover, cluster1 had higher scores in the necroptosis signaling pathway. On the other hand, cluster2 obtained higher scores in the autophagy regulation pathway, while having lower scores in the negative regulation of autophagy function. Noteworthy, impaired autophagy contributes to AD pathogenesis28. Furthermore, cluster2 exhibited higher scores in functions related to synapses, as well as in behavioral, cognitive, cellular, and neuroactive receptor-ligand interaction functions, and calcium signaling pathways. This suggests that patients in cluster2 have a healthier hippocampal synaptic profile, with overall functionality less compromised unlike that in cluster1. Notably, the functional scores related to pyroptosis were similar between the two subtypes. Summarily, cluster1 is the apoptotic subtype, characterized by pronounced cell apoptosis and severe synaptic functionality damage, whereas cluster2 is the mild subtype, with higher autophagy levels and milder functional impairment.

Constructing an AD PANoptosis score model

A total of 30 scoring genes were retained after dimensionality reduction using the Boruta algorithm (Fig. 11A). Supplementary Table 2 lists the gene symbols for 30 genes. PCA analysis was separately performed for genes positively and negatively correlated with AD within the scoring genes, causing two principal components: PCA_A and PCA_B. The PANoptosis scoring model was established using the formula: PANoptosis_score = PCA_A - PCA_B. Using ROC analysis, the scoring system achieved an accuracy of 100% in distinguishing between cluster1 and cluster2 samples (Fig. 11B). Moreover, the PANoptosis scores of samples in cluster1 and cluster2 were significantly different, with cluster1 having significantly higher PANoptosis scores than cluster2 (Fig. 11C). A heatmap depicted the expression of the 30 scoring genes in AD samples (Fig. 11D). Additionally, we assessed the correlation between the three PKADgs and the scoring system. As a result, ANGPT1, STEAP3, and TNFRSF11B were significantly and positively correlated with the score (Fig. 11E–G).

Establishment of a PANoptosis diagnostic model for AD

After dividing the 164 samples into training and validation sets in a 1:1 ratio, based on 48 important genes, an AD PANoptosis diagnostic model was established using RF, SVM-RFE, and logistic regression algorithms. ROC analysis demonstrated the model’s performance, with AUC values of 100% for all three diagnostic models in the training set. In the validation set, the RF model achieved an AUC of 85.85%, whereas the SVM-RFE and logistic regression models had AUC values of 65.12% and 61.77%, respectively (Fig. 11H,I). This shows that the RF model can be effectively used to distinguish between AD patients and healthy individuals. Additionally, to enhance the applicability of this study, we split the peripheral blood dataset GSE63061 into a training set and a validation set in a 1:1 ratio. Using the 48 important genes, diagnostic models were established again using machine learning algorithms. In the training set, the AUC values for the RF, SVM-RFE, and logistic regression algorithms were 100%, 89.87%, and 91.34%, respectively. In the validation set, the AUC values were 69.66%, 71.06%, and 71.14%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4). This demonstrates that the diagnostic model based on these genes also performs well in peripheral blood.

Discussion

AD is a neurodegenerative disease that affects 500,000 patients globally with an indirect impact on the lives of tens of millions29. The majority of AD cases are sporadic and appear later in life2. However, recent evidence suggests that 60–80% of AD risk is attributed to genetic factors, with over 40 genetic risk genes associated with AD having been identified30. Despite the extensive research on AD, the formation of amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles are still regarded as the primary pathological hallmarks of the disease, and its molecular pathogenesis remains elusive31. The extensive crosstalk between cell pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis has been revealed with the establishment of the concept of PANoptosis9. Importantly, the role of PANoptosis has been extensively investigated in other diseases32. This work used various reliable bioinformatics analyses to explore PANoptosis biomarkers associated with AD, resulting in the identification of two PANoptosis subtypes. We further developed scoring and diagnostic models.

Through differential analysis of GSE48350, 725 differentially expressed genes were identified between the hippocampal tissues of AD patients and healthy hippocampal samples. These genes were majorly enriched in functions including chemical synaptic transmission, axon, presynapse, behavioral response to stimuli, regulation of chemical synaptic transmission, and cell body. They were also enriched in GABAergic synapse, neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, synaptic vesicle cycle, and autophagy pathways. This is consistent with previous research indicating that AD begins with subtle alterations in synaptic efficacy in the hippocampal region before apparent neuronal degeneration. Dysfunction of neuronal synaptic function is induced by Aβ through different cell surface receptors, with increased synaptic Aβ levels causing defects in crucial presynaptic functions, including axonal transport, synaptic vesicle cycling, and neurotransmitter release. Furthermore, Aβ oligomers with high affinity target the postsynaptic compartment of excitatory synapses, altering the synaptic structure, composition, and function33–35. Tau, a microtubule-associated protein, is predominantly enriched in axons under physiological conditions. Studies have shown that pathological tau with N-terminal sequences can bind to synaptic vesicles via synaptophysin-3, disrupting presynaptic function and causing synaptic suppression36,37.

Using the WGCNA package, 2127 module genes were highly correlated with AD in the GSE48350 dataset. Enrichment analysis revealed that these genes were associated with synaptic-related functions and pathways. Notably, these genes were enriched in functions including regulation of monovalent inorganic cation transport, regulation of plasma membrane projection organization, response to metal ions, calcium-regulated protein binding, and pathways including mineral absorption. Previous studies have shown that tau protein accumulation in the presynaptic region of neurons causes the release of intracellular calcium from the endoplasmic reticulum. This triggers a transient increase in spontaneous neurotransmitter release, followed by sustained synaptic suppression due to insufficient exocytosis38,39.

In total, we identified 48 important genes after taking the intersection of 1324 genes encoding PANoptosis-related proteins, 725 differential genes, and 2127 highly correlated module genes. Using algorithms including Boruta, LASSO, RF, and SVM-RFE on these 48 genes, we further narrowed down to three PKADgs, i.e., ANGPT1, STEAP3, and TNFRSF11B. ANGPT1 encodes a secreted glycoprotein belonging to the angiopoietin family, playing a crucial role in regulating angiogenesis, endothelial cell survival, proliferation, migration, adhesion, cell spreading, remodeling of the actin cytoskeleton in smooth muscle cells, and maintaining vascular quiescence. Neurons are the primary source of ANGPT1 in the brain40. ANGPT1 can increase Aβ secretion through the FOXA2/PEN1/APP pathway, hence accelerating AD41. STEAP3, also known as TSAP6, encodes a multi-pass membrane protein that acts as an iron transport protein. Studies suggest that STEAP3 is involved in the iron death-related pathway in alcohol-dependent AD patients42. This protein can also mediate downstream reactions to p53, including promoting apoptosis. Furthermore, the antisense gene of TSAP6 can reduce 50–55 kDa protein expression, significantly attenuating P53-induced apoptosis. TSAP6 enhances cellular sensitivity to apoptosis, and its synergistic action with Nix (a pro-apoptotic Bcl2-related protein) strengthens this effect. In addition, TSAP6 small interfering RNA (siRNA) can inhibit apoptosis in TSAP6-overexpressing cells43. The downstream factors of STEAP3 are key elements mediating necrotic apoptosis and iron death in ischemic retinal ganglion cells44. TNFRSF11B, also known as OPG, can suppress the activation of osteoclasts and induce apoptosis in osteoclasts as well as osteoclast precursor cells through the Fas/Fas ligand pathway45. The role of TNFRSF11B in other diseases is explored subject of existing studies. For instance, the OPG protein, expressed by tumor cells, can interact with TRAIL, thereby preventing the induction of apoptosis in tumor cells46–48. In addition, in Complete Freund’s adjuvant-induced rheumatoid arthritis, rosiglitazone and cabazitaxel demonstrate anti-rheumatic effects by modulating expression levels of RANKL/OPG49. Importantly, research suggests that TNFRSF11B can act as an early pathological marker for AD50.

Two PANoptosis subtypes in AD patients using the consensus clustering algorithm. Through Gene Set Variation Analysis, the two subtypes were characterized as apoptotic subtype and mild subtype. The apoptotic subtype had intense cell apoptosis and severe synaptic functionality damage in the hippocampal tissue, with higher scores in the necrotic apoptosis pathway. In contrast, the mild subtype demonstrated increased levels of autophagy, with milder functional damage. Noteworthy, no significant difference was noted in the scores of the two subtypes in the pyroptosis function. Studies on apoptosis and necrotic apoptosis in the nervous system have reached maturity. For instance, C1q, an activator of the classical complement pathway activated during AD development and progression, induces neuroinflammation by promoting the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, partly mediated by inflammasome activation. The activation of inflammasomes may mediate the role of C1q in inducing synaptic apoptosis51. Apoptosis of retinal ganglion cells occurs in the retina ganglion cell layer and other neurons of the laminar layers. Axonal injury and synaptic loss coincide with somatic cell loss in acute eye hypertension in mice52. In AD mouse models, TREM2 inhibits Aβ-induced cell apoptosis by activating the Mcl-1/Bim complex53. Zileuton, a selective 5-lipoxygenase (5LO) inhibitor, may alleviate depressive-like behavior induced by chronic mild stress (CMS), as well as abnormal neuroinflammation and apoptotic responses, synaptic and neurogenic abnormalities in the hippocampal region, exerting a beneficial impact on depression54. Liraglutide has neuroprotective effects against diabetes-induced cognitive impairment, associated with improvement in hippocampal synapses and inhibition of neuronal apoptosis55. Regulation and activation of Caspases 3 and 9 initially cause apoptosis at the presynaptic level56. Experiments have shown that injection of amyloid-β oligomers (AβO) significantly accelerates the accumulation of AβO, increases phosphorylated tau expression levels, and induces necrotic apoptosis. Moreover, AβO-induced necrotic apoptosis predominantly occurs in the Microglia cells of the AD brain. Through activation of Microglia cells, AβO increases necrotic apoptosis, resulting in cognitive impairment57. Necrostatin-1 mitigates hippocampal synaptic damage induced by subarachnoid hemorrhage by inhibiting necrotic apoptosis associated with the CREB-BDNF pathway58. Necroptotic bodies in granulovacuolar degeneration (GVD) may play a role in AD, representing a specific form of necrosis-related neuronal death associated with AD59. Studies indicate that Microglia cells assemble the multiprotein complex, the NOD-, LRR-, and pyrin-domain containing 3 (Nlrp3) inflammasome, causing the formation of apoptotic spec-like protein containing a CARD and subsequently promoting the nucleation of new Aβ plaques60. These studies highlight the potential of adopting mechanisms related to apoptosis and necroptosis in the field of neurological diseases. Thus, investigating the mechanisms of apoptosis and necroptosis is significant in the context of AD and PANoptosis.

Machine learning algorithms have achieved substantial success in medicine61. Herein, two models were constructed based on key genes: (1) the AD PANoptosis scoring model based on the Boruta algorithm; (2) the AD PANoptosis diagnostic model based on the RF algorithm, SVM-RFE algorithm, and LR. Consequently, the AD PANoptosis scoring model could effectively distinguish between apoptotic and mild patients, with an AUC value of 100%. Additionally, in the training set, the AUC values of the three AD PANoptosis subtype diagnostic models reached 100%, whereas the AUC value of the RF AD PANoptosis diagnostic model in the test set was 85.85%. This shows that our models can effectively distinguish between AD patients and healthy individuals, hence indirectly verifying the accuracy of our previous analyses. Additionally, using the 48 important genes, we established diagnostic models in peripheral blood data, which also yielded excellent results. This suggests that these important genes have a certain level of applicability in peripheral blood.

Conclusion

Through comprehensive bioinformatic analysis, this work identified 48 important genes and three key AD genes related to PANoptosis, i.e., ANGPT1, STEAP3, and TNFRSF11B, and investigated their expression across various brain tissues and cell types. We further identified two PANoptosis subtypes in AD patients, i.e., apoptotic subtype and mild subtype. Apoptotic patients displayed intense cellular apoptosis and severe synaptic functionality damage in the hippocampal tissue, whereas mild patients showed milder functional damage in the hippocampus. We eventually established an AD PANoptosis scoring system and an AD PANoptosis diagnostic model for differentiation of apoptotic patients, mild patients, and healthy individuals, demonstrating excellent performance. In conclusion, our findings indicate that, in the context of AD, PANoptosis may influence disease progression via both apoptosis and necrotic apoptosis.
