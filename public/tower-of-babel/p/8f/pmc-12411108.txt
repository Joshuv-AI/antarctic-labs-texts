Does the Single Nucleotide Polymorphism rs2228145 in IL6R Truly Reflect IL-6 Signaling in Mendelian Randomization Studies?
Authors: Mengyi Zhu, Minghui Cao, Liling Lin
Published: 2025

The study by Hamilton et al. (2025) (1), titled “Altered IL-6 signalling and risk of tuberculosis: a multi-ancestry mendelian randomisation study,” provides significant insights into the potential causal role of IL-6 signaling in tuberculosis (TB) risk. The authors suggest that reduced IL-6 activity might lower the odds of developing TB, based on Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis. While this approach appropriately emphasizes the complex role of IL-6 in TB pathogenesis, there are notable concerns regarding the use of rs2228145 (Asp358Ala) as a proxy for IL-6 activity.

The genetic variant rs2228145 has become a common instrument for MR studies investigating IL-6 activity and is often used as a proxy for IL-6 antagonism, including treatments such as tocilizumab (1,2,3). However, this variant does not precisely replicate the biological effects of IL-6 antagonism. Specifically, rs2228145 results in a substitution of aspartic acid by alanine at position 358 of the IL-6R, which leads to increased cleavage of membrane-bound IL-6R (mIL-6R) and higher circulating levels of soluble IL-6R (sIL-6R), thus enhancing the IL-6 trans-signaling (4). Unlike tocilizumab, which inhibits both classical and trans-signaling, rs2228145 primarily impacts classical signaling and may not fully reflect the broad immunomodulatory effects of IL-6 antagonism (Fig. 1A).

Impact of rs2228145-C variant and tocilizumab on IL-6 signaling pathways and disease associations. (A) Schematic illustration of IL-6 signaling via classical (mIL-6R) and trans-signaling (sIL-6R) pathways through gp130. The rs2228145-C allele is associated with decreased expression of mIL-6R and increased levels of sIL-6R, leading to weakened classical signaling and enhanced trans-signaling. Tocilizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting IL-6R, inhibits both classical and trans-signaling by blocking IL-6/IL-6R complex binding to gp130. (B) Association of the rs2228145-C allele with rheumatoid arthritis and asthma in large-scale case-control datasets. The variant is associated with a reduced risk of rheumatoid arthritis (OR, 0.96; 95% CI, 0.93–0.98; p=4.82×10−4), but an increased risk of asthma (OR, 1.04; 95% CI, 1.02–1.05; p= 2.65×10−7).

IL-6 exerts its biological effects through two primary signaling pathways: classical and trans-signaling (Fig. 1A). Classical signaling occurs when IL-6 binds to the mIL-6R, activating the JAK-STAT pathway to mediate various effects, including tissue repair and inflammation. In contrast, trans-signaling involves the IL-6/sIL-6R complex, which activates cells lacking mIL-6R, such as endothelial cells. This pathway is typically associated with pro-inflammatory effects but can also play anti-inflammatory roles under specific conditions (5,6).

The dichotomy between classical and trans-signaling is crucial for understanding the role of IL-6 in disease. For example, in rheumatoid arthritis, reduced classical signaling (as seen with rs2228145) may be protective by dampening inflammatory responses. However, in diseases like asthma, where IL-6 trans-signaling exacerbates inflammation, rs2228145 may not capture the full complexity of IL-6 signaling. Studies have shown that IL-6 trans-signaling plays a central role in driving airway inflammation in asthma, where sIL-6R levels in the lungs are elevated (7).

In our investigation using the FinnGen datasets (8), the rs2228145-C allele was found to be associated with a reduced risk of rheumatoid arthritis (odds ratio [OR], 0.96; p=4.82×10−4) but an increased risk of asthma (OR, 1.04; p=2.65×10−7) (Fig. 1B), supporting the notion that rs2228145-C exerts divergent effects in these 2 disease contexts. Meanwhile, tocilizumab, which blocks both signaling pathways, reduces IL-6-mediated inflammation and improves both rheumatoid arthritis and asthma outcomes (9,10). This example demonstrates why rs2228145-C cannot fully mimic the effects of tocilizumab, particularly in diseases where trans-signaling is prominent.

Given the complex and context-dependent nature of IL-6 signaling, using rs2228145 as a proxy for IL-6 activity in MR studies requires caution. Future MR studies should consider strategies to address these limitations. For instance, researchers might create genetic instruments that specifically target either classical or trans-IL-6 signaling, providing clearer insights into their distinct roles in disease outcomes. Additionally, combining MR analyses with experimental methods (e.g., gene editing or pharmacological interventions) would allow researchers to validate the causal effects of IL-6 signaling in relevant models. Furthermore, stratified analyses across populations and diseases, along with sensitivity analyses, could enhance the robustness of findings and account for potential pleiotropic effects. Additionally, given the well-documented variability in adverse immune responses to perioperative drugs, the immunophenotype elicited by pharmacological antagonism may differ markedly from genetic modulation (11). Genetic variants may mimic only a subset of drug-induced immunological patterns, underscoring the importance of integrating clinical immunotoxicity data when interpreting MR-based inferences in complex immune-mediated diseases.

In conclusion, while rs2228145 has contributed to our understanding of IL-6 signaling, it is crucial to recognize its limitations when used as a proxy for IL-6 activity. By adopting a more nuanced approach in MR studies, researchers can better elucidate the causal effects of IL-6 signaling and avoid oversimplifications that may undermine the validity of their conclusions.
