Risk factors for progression to albuminuria in individuals with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes: a 5-year cohort study
Authors: Lingli Zhou, Junxia Yu, Xiaoling Cai, Yu Zhu, Meng Li, Xueyao Han, Linong Ji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to determine the 5-year incidence of albuminuria in Chinese individuals with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and identify baseline risk factors for progression to albuminuria.MethodsAn observational cohort study was conducted with 604 individuals aged ≥ 18 years diagnosed with T2DM between January 2014 and December 2017 at a tertiary hospital in China, followed through November 2022. The cumulative incidence of progression from normoalbuminuria to albuminuria was assessed. Risk factors for progression were evaluated using multiple logistic regression.ResultsOf the 368 individuals with normoalbuminuria at diagnosis, the mean (SD) age was 54 (11) years. The 5-year cumulative incidence of progression to albuminuria was 7.9%. Higher urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) (OR 1.18; 95% CI, 1.10–1.26) and higher homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) (OR 1.18; 95% CI, 1.15–1.32) at diagnosis were independently associated with progression. After adjusting for baseline UACR, higher HOMA-IR at diagnosis was linked to a more rapid increase in UACR over time.ConclusionEarly screening for UACR and management of insulin resistance in individuals with T2DM may help delay the onset of microalbuminuria.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes, Insulin resistance, Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, Microalbuminuria

Background

Diabetic nephropathy (DN) is the most common complication of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and remains the leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) worldwide, contributing to increased morbidity and mortality [1]. The early detection of DN is crucial, with microalbuminuria serving as a primary marker. Microalbuminuria is defined as a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) of 30 mg/g or higher and develops in approximately 40% of patients within 10 years of being diagnosed with T2DM [2]. Current clinical guidelines recommend annual screening for microalbuminuria, starting at the time of T2DM diagnosis, as a routine measure for detecting DN. Previous studies, including our own, have demonstrated that UACR at the time of diagnosis reflects early glomerular injury and can predict the deterioration of kidney function [3–5]. Furthermore, microalbuminuria has been identified as an independent risk factor for coronary heart disease and mortality.

Given the substantial healthcare burden associated with diabetic kidney disease, it is essential to identify the progression rates and risk factors for microalbuminuria early in the disease course, in order to implement appropriate interventions that may delay the onset of DN. Previous studies conducted in Denmark, Israel, and the United Kingdom have shown that the annual incidence of microalbuminuria in individuals with newly diagnosed T2DM is approximately 2% [6]. In Asia, a retrospective study in Singapore reported a 5-year cumulative incidence of microalbuminuria of 17.3% from the time of T2DM diagnosis [5]. Clinical practice guidelines advocate for multifactorial interventions to prevent or slow the progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD) in individuals with T2DM. Risk factors associated with microalbuminuria include advanced age, elevated glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), obesity, hypertension, and dyslipidemia [7]. However, to date, no similar cohort studies have assessed the cumulative incidence and risk factors for microalbuminuria in patients with newly diagnosed T2DM in China.

Insulin resistance is a hallmark of T2DM and plays a critical role in the pathophysiology of the disease [8]. It occurs when the body’s cells, particularly in the liver, muscle, and adipose tissue, become less responsive to insulin, leading to impaired glucose uptake and elevated blood glucose levels. In response, the pancreas compensates by producing more insulin, a state referred to as hyperinsulinemia [9]. Over time, this compensatory mechanism becomes insufficient, resulting in the onset of T2DM. Insulin resistance also contributes to the development of other metabolic disturbances, such as dyslipidemia and hypertension, which are common comorbidities in T2DM and further exacerbate the risk of microvascular complications, including DN [10]. The homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) is a widely used tool to estimate insulin resistance based on fasting blood glucose and insulin levels. It provides a reliable measure of insulin sensitivity and has been shown to be a strong predictor of long-term outcomes in T2DM [11].

Accordingly, in this study we aimed to determine the 5-year incidence of albuminuria in Chinese patients with newly diagnosed T2DM and to identify baseline risk factors for progression to albuminuria.

Materials and methods

Subjects/Follow-up

This was a real-world observational and longitudinal cohort study that followed patients with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) for a period of 5 years, as previously described [10]. Briefly, we enrolled adult patients who had received a first-time diagnosis of T2DM at our hospital between January 2014 and December 2017 and provided written informed consent. All patients were treatment-naïve at enrollment. The mean duration of diabetes at baseline was 0.2 ± 0.5 years, confirming that participants were at an early stage of the disease.Participants were followed from the time of diagnosis (baseline visit) every 3–6 months until the development of microalbuminuria or until 5 years of follow-up was completed (censoring at November 2022 for those who did not develop microalbuminuria). The median follow-up time was 5.0 years for those who remained normoalbuminuric. Cohort entry was defned as the date of diagnosis, and all patients had at least 5 years of potential follow-up unless the endpoint occurred. Of these, 86 patients lacked baseline urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) data, and 150 patients had abnormal UACR at baseline, leading to their exclusion. For the remaining 368 patients with normal UACR (defined as UACR < 30 mg/g) at baseline, the mean UACR levels during the first year were used as the representative UACR values for each subsequent year: 1 st year (from the 3rd to the 12th month), 2nd year (13th to 24th month), 3rd year (25th to 36th month), 4th year (37th to 48th month), and 5th year (49th to 60th month). Of these 368 patients, 29 progressed to microalbuminuria (UACR > 30 mg/g, referred to as “progressors”), while 339 patients maintained normal albuminuria status throughout the 5-year follow-up (Fig. 1).

Medical histories, physical examination, and laboratory measurements

Demographic information and medical histories, including comorbidities, were collected at the patients’ initial outpatient visit at a tertiary hospital in China. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure > 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure > 90 mmHg, or the use of antihypertensive medications. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m). All blood samples were obtained after an overnight fast in the morning. HbA1c was measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (Premier Hb 9210, Trinity Biotech, USA). Serum total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), uric acid (UA), and serum creatinine (CRE) were measured using enzymatic methods on an automatic biochemical analyzer (Hitachi 7170 A, Tokyo, Japan). Retinopathy was assessed using fundus photography (TRC.NW400, Topcon Inc., Japan). Urine samples were routinely collected at baseline and every 3–6 months thereafter, with the UACR measured using a COBAS Integra 400 Plus System (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). Both the increase in UACR levels and the incidence of microalbuminuria, considered as quantitative and qualitative indicators of kidney disease progression, were analyzed. Insulin resistance was assessed using the Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR), calculated by the formula: fasting insulin (mU/L) × fasting plasma glucose (mmol/L)/22.5, with higher values indicating greater insulin resistance [12]. Estimated glomerular fltration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) creatinine-based equation [13]. Metabolic syndrome (MS) was diagnosed using the International Diabetes Federation consensus definition. In Chinese individuals, metabolic syndrome was defined by the presence of central obesity (waist circumference ≥ 90 cm for males and ≥ 80 cm for females) plus any two of the following four criteria: elevated triglycerides or lipid-lowering treatment, low HDL-C or treatment for this lipid abnormality, elevated blood pressure or antihypertensive treatment, and elevated fasting blood glucose or a prior diagnosis of T2DM (available at https://idf.org/about-diabetes/resources/idf-consensus-worldwide-definition-of-the-metabolic-syndrome/).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (SPSS, Chicago, Illinois). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Variables such as UACR, HOMA-IR index, and TG, which did not follow a normal distribution, were log-transformed and presented as medians with interquartile ranges. Between-group differences in normally distributed continuous variables were analyzed using independent t-tests, while categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all comparisons.

To assess the predictive value of the HOMA-IR index for progression to albuminuria, Cox regression analysis was conducted with progressively increasing adjustments for potential confounders. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and HbA1c. Model 2 was further adjusted for metabolic syndrome (MS), baseline UACR, and eGFR, while excluding BMI to avoid collinearity with MS and HOMA-IR (given that BMI contributes to the definition of MS). In Model 3, adjustment was made for ACEI/ARB treatment and the average HbA1c level over the 5-year follow-up (with baseline HbA1c excluded). For each patient, this average HbA1c was calculated as the mean of all HbA1c measurements recorded during the 5-year period. All results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), given that a logistic regression model was used.

Changes in UACR (log-transformed) from baseline were compared between groups using repeated-measures mixed-effect models. This model included time (years), log-transformed UACR values, and group as fixed factors, with baseline UACR levels as a covariate. Patients were categorized into three groups based on tertiles of the HOMA-IR index, and baseline characteristics were compared using analysis of variance (ANOVA) or chi-square tests. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to examine the cumulative incidence of microalbuminuria across the tertile subgroups.Due to the relatively low event count and fxed follow-up duration, we used logistic regression for the 5-year outcome as our primary analysis rather than Cox proportional hazards.

Results

Study population and progression to microalbuminuria

A total of 368 individuals with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and normal albuminuria at baseline were included in the study. Over the course of the 5-year follow-up, 29 individuals progressed to microalbuminuria, while 339 remained non-progressors. The incidence of microalbuminuria increased progressively, with the highest incidence observed in the first year after diagnosis (5.5%). The rate of progression slowed thereafter, as shown in Fig. 2. By the end of the 5-year follow-up, the cumulative prevalence of microalbuminuria was 7.9%, corresponding to an annual incidence rate of 1.6%.

Baseline characteristics of progressors and non-progressors

The baseline characteristics of progressors and non-progressors are presented in Table 1. Compared to the non-progressors, those who developed microalbuminuria had significantly higher fasting insulin levels [16.4 (8.5, 30.2) vs. 12.1 (7.8, 17.3), p = 0.024], higher HOMA-IR index (8.2 ± 6.6 vs. 5.6 ± 3.7, p = 0.001), and a higher prevalence of metabolic syndrome (86.2% vs. 65.8%, p = 0.029). Aside from these factors, the two groups did not differ significantly in BMI, HbA1c, eGFR, lipid profiles, or hypertension status. However, progressors had a higher prevalence of ACEI/ARB therapy at baseline (55.2% vs. 28.6%, p = 0.012). This indicates that some patients without hypertension were receiving ACEI/ARB, likely for early renoprotective benefits in diabetes.

Risk factors for progression to microalbuminuria

Table 2 presents the results of the binary logistic regression analyses for progression to microalbuminuria. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), which included all key covariates, higher baseline UACR and higher HOMA-IR were independently associated with greater odds of progression. Notably, simpler models (Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and HbA1c; Model 2 adding metabolic syndrome, baseline UACR, and eGFR) yielded similar trends – in each model, elevated HOMA-IR and UACR at diagnosis were significant risk factors for progression. Specifcally, for each 1 mg/g increase in baseline UACR, the odds of progressing to microalbuminuria increased by approximately 18% (OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.10–1.26). Similarly, for each 1-unit increase in HOMA-IR, the odds of progression rose by about 18% (OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.06–1.30). Notably, neither baseline HbA1c nor the average HbA1c levels over the 5-year period were found to be significantly associated with progression.

Impact of insulin resistance on uacr progression

To explore the effect of insulin resistance on the increase in UACR levels, patients were divided into two groups based on the median value of the baseline HOMA-IR index: 184 patients with higher insulin resistance (HOMA-IR ≥ 4.6) and 184 patients with lower insulin resistance (HOMA-IR < 4.6). Repeated-measures mixed-effect models, adjusting for baseline UACR levels, demonstrated that patients with higher insulin resistance at baseline experienced a more rapid increase in UACR levels, which remained higher over time compared to those with lower insulin resistance (p = 0.02), as shown in Fig. 3.

Kaplan-meier analysis of microalbuminuria progression by HOMA-IR tertiles

To further investigate the relationship between insulin resistance and the progression of microalbuminuria, Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed. Patients were stratified into three groups based on HOMA-IR index tertiles: Tertile 1 (HOMA-IR < 3.6), Tertile 2 (3.6 ≤ HOMA-IR < 6.5), and Tertile 3 (HOMA-IR ≥ 6.5). As shown in Table 3, patients in the highest HOMA-IR tertile (Tertile 3) were younger, had higher BMI, HbA1c levels, UACR, lipid levels, and a higher prevalence of central obesity and metabolic syndrome at baseline compared to those in the lower tertiles. Additionally, a higher incidence of microalbuminuria was observed in Tertile 3. Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrated that the probability of developing microalbuminuria was significantly higher in patients in the highest HOMA-IR tertile compared to those in the lowest (Tertile 3 vs. Tertile 1, p = 0.012) and middle (Tertile 3 vs. Tertile 2, p = 0.035) tertiles, as shown in Fig. 4.

Discussion

In this prospective study, we assessed patients with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and normal albuminuria at diagnosis. Over a 5-year period, the cumulative incidence of microalbuminuria was 7.9%, or 1.6% per year. Our results demonstrated that individuals with higher baseline insulin resistance, as measured by the HOMA-IR index, experienced a more rapid increase in UACR levels, which remained elevated over time. Furthermore, baseline insulin resistance was identified as a predictor for the development of microalbuminuria within 5 years. Notably, patients in the highest tertile of HOMA-IR had significantly higher incidences of microalbuminuria compared to those in the lowest tertile. Overall, these findings underscore the importance of early intervention for insulin resistance in newly diagnosed T2DM patients to mitigate the risk of diabetic nephropathy progression.

The 5-year cumulative prevalence of microalbuminuria observed in our study (7.9%) is lower than the incidence rates reported in previous studies, which ranged from 10.5 to 23%. For instance, the United Kingdom Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS) found an annual incidence rate of 2.0% for the development of microalbuminuria [6]. Similarly, a retrospective study conducted by Tan et al. in an Asian population (including India, Malaysia, and China) reported a 5-year cumulative incidence of 17.3% [5]. The lower incidence in our cohort (7.9% over 5 years) compared to some previous reports (e.g., 17.3% in a Singapore cohort) could be due to differences in population characteristics and risk profles. Our study population, drawn from an urban Chinese hospital, may benefit from better baseline health status or management. For instance, variations in diet and lifestyle, genetic factors, or aggressive multifactorial management of diabetes and blood pressure from the time of diagnosis could contribute to a lower observed incidence of microalbuminuria. It is also possible that detection timing differed – earlier identification and treatment of risk factors in our cohort may have delayed the onset of microalbuminuria.

Notably, in our cohort a slightly higher proportion of patients were on ACEI/ARB therapy than the proportion diagnosed with hypertension (especially among progressors). This likely refects the clinical practice of prescribing ACEI/ARBs for renoprotective benefts in diabetes. In other words, some normotensive patients were started on ACEI/ARBs as a preventive measure against diabetic nephropathy or due to other indications (e.g., cardiovascular protection).

Our study further demonstrated that higher baseline HOMA-IR was associated with elevated UACR levels and an increased incidence of microalbuminuria over 5 years. This association remained significant after adjusting for baseline UACR, highlighting the independent role of insulin resistance in the progression to microalbuminuria. Numerous studies have reported similar findings linking insulin resistance to kidney dysfunction in both diabetic and non-diabetic populations [14–17]. Specifically, the relationship between insulin resistance and microalbuminuria in type 2 diabetes has been documented in several cross-sectional studies. One prospective cohort study also confirmed that insulin resistance could predict the development of microalbuminuria in type 2 diabetic patients within a follow-up period of approximately 5 years [17]. However, most of these studies involved patients undergoing hypoglycemic treatments (such as metformin, sulfonylureas, and insulin), which may alter glucose and insulin levels, potentially influencing the assessment of insulin resistance through the HOMA-IR index. Given the confounding factors related to treatment effects, our study, which focused on treatment-naive patients with normoalbuminuria at baseline, provides strong evidence of an association between baseline insulin resistance and the development of microalbuminuria.

The underlying mechanisms linking insulin resistance to the development of microalbuminuria remain incompletely understood. Insulin resistance is often associated with hyperinsulinemia, which is commonly used as a surrogate marker of insulin resistance in clinical practice [18]. Hyperinsulinemia has been shown to exacerbate renal hemodynamics by inducing glomerular hyperfiltration, increasing renal vascular permeability, and promoting renal sodium retention [19–22]. Moreover, insulin receptors are expressed on renal podocytes, which play a crucial role in the early stages of diabetic kidney damage. Insulin signaling is essential for podocyte viability and tubular function. Recent studies in mouse models have demonstrated that podocytes lacking the insulin receptor exhibit apoptosis, effacement of foot processes, thickening of the glomerular basement membrane, increased glomerulosclerosis, and albuminuria [20]. Restoring insulin sensitivity at the level of the insulin receptor has been shown to protect podocytes from endoplasmic reticulum stress [23]. Therefore, interventions aimed at improving insulin sensitivity may have potential therapeutic implications in preventing or delaying the progression of diabetic nephropathy.

It is worth noting that recent data-driven cluster analyses of T2DM have reported differing risks of kidney complications among sub-types. For example, Xing et al. identified a Severe Insulin-Defcient Diabetes (SIDD) cluster that paradoxically had the highest prevalence of albuminuria, while the Severe Insulin-ResistantDiabetes (SIRD) cluster had the highest risk of reduced eGFR (CKD) [24]. Our finding that insulin resistance is associated with new-onset microalbuminuria may seem at odds with the higher albuminuria observed in SIDD patients. This discrepancy can be explained by differences in patient populations and disease stage. Our cohort consisted of newly diagnosed patients (few of whom would fall into a severe insulin defciency category at baseline), and we examined early nephropathy (microalbuminuria onset). In early T2DM, hyperinsulinemia and insulin resistance exert strong hemodynamic and inflammatory effects on the kidneys, promoting microalbuminuria. In contrast, the SIDD cluster in Xing’s study likely represents patients with longer disease duration and poor glycemic control, for whom chronic hyperglycemia and possibly co-occurring hypertension led to higher albuminuria rates. Thus, both insulin resistance and insulin deficiency can contribute to kidney complications, but at different stages and through different pathways. Our results highlight the SIRD-like mechanism (insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia) as a key driver of incipient nephropathy in early diabetes, complementing the cluster analysis fndings that SIDD contributes to diabetic kidney disease risk in later stages.

This study is one of the few to investigate risk factors for the development of incipient nephropathy in newly diagnosed T2DM patients over a 5-year period. To our knowledge, it is the first such cohort study conducted in China. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as an observational study, we were unable to collect complete data on medications used for diabetes and comorbidities over the 5-year follow-up. Nevertheless, treatment strategies largely adhered to clinical guidelines for T2DM, with ACE inhibitors or ARBs preferentially prescribed to patients with hypertension or proteinuria. Recent renal-protective drugs, such as sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2is); glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, may play a role in the progression of microalbuminuria. However, these drugs were not included in the study due to their exclusion from national medical insurance coverage until 2020, which likely limited their use among patients in our cohort. Second, although this is a prospective cohort study, our primary analysis evaluated the 5-year outcome in a cross-sectional manner (using logistic regression). A time-to-event analysis (e.g., Cox proportional hazards model) could provide additional insight into the timing of progression; however, given that nearly all patients were observed for the full 5-year period and only 29 events occurred, we chose a simpler approach. Future studies or meta-analyses with more events should utilize survival analysis techniques to confrm our fndings. Additionally, while we collected annual UACR measurements, more sophisticated longitudinal modeling (such as trajectory analysis or mixed-efects modeling) might reveal nuances in UACR progression related to insulin resistance. We have provided a preliminary observation that higher baseline HOMA-IR was associated with a steeper rise in UACR over time, but we acknowledge that the full longitudinal potential of the data could not be fully realized in this analysis. Third, we did not collect detailed data on the specific anti-diabetic medications each patient was taking at each follow-up, which is a limitation. Generally, patients were managed according to standard guidelines: most were initiated on metformin at diagnosis, with sulfonylureas or insulin added if needed for glycemic control. The use of pioglitazone (a thiazolidinedione insulin sensitizer) in our cohort was uncommon (< 5% of patients) and was not systematically tracked, so we are unable to assess its impact on microalbuminuria progression. Importantly, newer anti-hyperglycemic drugs with renal benefits (SGLT2is, GLP-1 RAs) were not available to our patients during most of the study period. Fourth, we did not perform stratified analyses (e.g., by sex or other subgroups) in this study. Given the relatively small number of progressor events, any subgroup analysis would have limited power; future larger studies could consider stratified or interaction analyses to further explore these associations.Future studies with a larger and more diverse cohort, including multi-center data, are needed to confirm our findings. Additionally, long-term follow-up beyond 5 years and investigation of the effects of newer renal-protective treatments are warranted.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study highlights the significant role of baseline insulin resistance in the progression of microalbuminuria in patients with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes. Early identification and management of insulin resistance may help delay the onset of diabetic nephropathy. Further research is needed to explore the underlying mechanisms and evaluate the long-term impact of insulin-sensitizing interventions on the prevention of kidney disease in this high-risk population.
