Glucocorticoid in giant cell arteritis: real-world treatment patterns over time and associated burden in a nationwide hospital-based study within the French health insurance database
Authors: Maxime Beydon, Diane Lacaille, Bruno Fautrel, Raphaèle Seror, David Hajage, Florence Tubach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveRecent advances in giant cell arteritis (GCA) management aim to reduce glucocorticoid (GC). We described changes in treatment patterns over time in real life and assessed associations between cumulative GC use and adverse outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective population-based study used the French national health insurance database (Système National des Données de Santé) and included 18 301 incident GCA cases between 2010 and 2022. Latent class growth modelling identified GC use trajectories. We described trends in cumulative dose and sparing agent use. Multivariable survival models assessed factors associated with sustained GC-free remission and association between GC cumulative exposure and mortality, serious infections, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and osteoporotic fractures.ResultsFour GC use trajectories were identified. Two high-dose trajectories declined over time. Yet, mean 2-year cumulative GC dose remained high, reaching 7.6 g among patients diagnosed in 2022. Methotrexate use increased until 2020 and plateaued thereafter while tocilizumab prescriptions rose, reaching over 25% in 2022. Male sex and baseline methotrexate or tocilizumab use were associated with faster GC tapering, while age >75 years, hypertension, kidney and neurodegenerative diseases were associated with slower tapering. Each gram of GC was associated with higher mortality (HR per gram 1.024, 95% CI 1.021 to 1.027). Yearly cumulative exposure to doses ≤5 mg/day was associated with increased infection (HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.29) and MACE (HR 1.04, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.08).ConclusionDespite reductions in high-dose use, cumulative GC exposure in GCA remains substantial and associated with adverse outcomes, even at the lowest doses.Keywords: Giant Cell Arteritis, Glucocorticoids, Mortality

Introduction

Glucocorticoids (GC) are still to date the cornerstone of giant cell arteritis (GCA) treatment, due to their rapid onset of action and remarkable efficacy, particularly in preventing ischaemic complications. However, disease relapses are common during tapering.1 2 As a result, patients with GCA have substantial cumulative GC exposure, both in terms of dose and treatment duration. In a monocentric descriptive study conducted in France, Castan et al estimated a total median cumulative GC dose exceeding 9 g during follow-up,3 similar to a large British population-based study spanning from 2000 to 2011.4 These studies were conducted before the increasing usage of GC-sparing agents aimed at reducing GC-related morbidity.2 5 A more recent retrospective study published in 2022 including 77 patients found a median cumulative dose of 4.8 g in relapse-free patients, although half of the cohort experienced a subsequent relapse.6 Likewise, a retrospective study of 47 patients with GCA diagnosed between 2020 and 2022 found that cumulative GC dose at 1 year ranged from 3.9 g in patients without relapse to 4.6 g in patients with at least one relapse.7

With the growing availability of disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), there is renewed hope that GC exposure in the treatment of GCA could be reduced or even avoided in the foreseeable future.8 In France, their use is recommended in patients at high risk of GC-related toxicity in order to accelerate GC tapering. However, there is to date little real-world evidence describing shifts in practice patterns since the availability of new agents. In addition, while some studies have identified factors associated with clinical relapse such as sex and large vessel involvement,9 robust investigations with large sample sizes investigating factors associated with prolonged GC exposure are lacking.

Although the adverse effects associated with cumulative GC exposure are well recognised and have been described across multiple inflammatory diseases, including GCA,10,16 studies quantifying the relationship between duration of exposure at a given daily dose and adverse outcomes remain scarce. It therefore remains unclear how high-dose regimens or prolonged exposure to lower doses are associated with adverse outcomes such as mortality, infections, cardiovascular events or osteoporotic fractures. Quantifying these associations may help inform therapeutic decisions, including the early introduction of GC-sparing agents in patients at highest risk. Moreover, current GCA guidelines recommend tapering to ≤5 mg/day by 1 year and discontinuation by 2 years.17 However, there is no evidence that the objective of 5 mg/day is safe.

Therefore, the objectives of this nationwide retrospective cohort study were first to characterise trajectories of oral GC use in the 2 years following GCA diagnosis and how these trajectories evolved over time. Second, to assess factors associated with time to sustained GC-free remission. Third, to evaluate the association between cumulative GC dose and cumulative duration of exposure time according to predefined dose categories (>30 mg/day, 15–30 mg/day, 5–15 mg/day, 1–5 mg/day), with risk of mortality, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), serious infections and major osteoporotic fractures.

Patients and methods

Setting and design

We designed a retrospective, population-based cohort study within the French national health data (Système National des Données de Santé (SNDS)), which links the health insurance claims database, the hospital discharge database and the death certificates database. It provides real-world, quasi-exhaustive nationwide data, as in France all individuals are enrolled in the same social security system and any reimbursed service, at least partially, is captured in the database. It notably includes all in-hospital diagnostic codes on discharge summaries using International Classification of Diseases 10 (ICD-10) codes, drug deliveries including Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification and dosage, costly in-hospital treatments, long-term disability status with ICD-10 codes, date and cause of death from death certificates. It covers almost the entirety of the French population (67 million individuals) and is now routinely used for research purposes.18 19 We were granted full institutional access to all variables available for research purposes. SNDS is a pseudonymised database and no individual patient consent was required.

Participants

We included patients with incident GCA diagnosed between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2022. Incident GCA was defined as the first hospitalisation with GCA recorded as the principal diagnosis. During the study period in France, hospitalisation was standard practice for almost all patients with new-onset GCA. Only one principal diagnostic code (ie, reason for hospitalisation) was required. The positive predictive value of GCA diagnostic codes recorded as the principal diagnosis (ICD-10 codes M31.5 and M31.6) in the hospital discharge database has been reported to be 97%.20 The minimum look-back period was 3 years. To enhance specificity and ensure inclusion of incident GCA, we excluded patients with more than two GC reimbursements in the year preceding the first GCA code, as well as patients with any dispensing of tocilizumab or methotrexate prior to first GCA code (online supplemental table S1). As the study focused on GC exposure, we further required at least one GC prescription dispensed in the month following the index hospitalisation discharge. This first postdischarge dispensation defined the index date.

Exposure

Oral GC exposure was assessed using data from the national health insurance claims database. Daily oral GC use during follow-up was estimated from the total amount of GCs dispensed per month. In case of hospitalisation, we assumed that the outpatient daily dose at the time of admission was maintained (conservative hypothesis) and provided by the hospital, thus any unconsumed medication dispensed prior to hospitalisation was carried over and included in the calculation of the first postdischarge period.

Tocilizumab exposure was captured both through in-hospital intravenous infusions and subcutaneous injections dispensed in local pharmacies whereas methotrexate was only captured in local pharmacy dispensing (online supplemental table S1).

Outcomes

Adverse outcomes of interest were all-cause mortality, MACE defined as a composite outcome of myocardial infarction, stroke or death from cardiovascular cause, serious infection defined as an infection requiring hospitalisation and major osteoporotic fracture (hip, pelvic, vertebral or humeral fracture). All ICD-10 codes and specific procedural codes (‘CCAM’) are presented in online supplemental table S1. We also evaluated time to sustained GC-free remission, which was defined as the first occurrence of a continuous 6-month period without any GC use.

Statistical analysis

Proportions are expressed as percentages, and continuous variables as mean (SD) or median (Q1–Q3), as appropriate.

First, we modelled daily GC dose trajectories over the 2 years following diagnosis using latent class growth mixture (LCGM) models, with daily dose modelled as a function of time treated as a continuous variable. Full details of model selection are provided in online supplemental material, page 2. In the selected model, time was expressed as a natural spline with 4 df, and a patient-level random slope without random intercept was specified as a linear function of time. Patients’ characteristics within each trajectory were described without pairwise or global statistical comparisons to avoid highlighting statistically significant but clinically irrelevant differences given the large sample size.

Second, to describe trends in GC use over time, we first calculated the cumulative GC dose within 2 years of diagnosis, overall and according to year of GCA diagnosis. We then estimated Kaplan-Meier survival curves for time to sustained GC-free remission by calendar year of diagnosis as well as cumulative incidence of tocilizumab or methotrexate prescription over time.

Finally, to capture the burden associated with GC exposure in GCA, we used multivariable Fine-Gray models to estimate subdistribution HRs. For time to sustained GC-free remission, we used a time-fixed Fine-Gray model. One model included baseline variables only, and two additional models with time origins set at 3 and 6 months were fit to evaluate the effect of GC dose during the first 3 and 6 months, respectively, and included patients who were alive and still receiving GC at the corresponding time zero. Then, we used a multivariable Cox model to estimate HRs for all-cause mortality, and cause-specific Cox models for infection, MACE and major osteoporotic fractures, with two time-varying exposures of interest: (i) cumulative dose (in grams) and (ii) cumulative duration of exposure to GCs (in years), within each of the four clinically relevant daily dose categories (1–5 mg/day, 5–15 mg/day, 15–30 mg/day and over 30 mg/day). Person-time was allocated to each category according to the patient’s average daily GC dose each month. One model per outcome and cumulative exposure variable was performed. Models were adjusted for baseline variables including age, sex, year of diagnosis, polymyalgia rheumatica, French Deprivation Index,21 dementia, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, stroke, myocardial infarction, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, cancer, proxies for tobacco use and obesity, anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy and osteoporosis. Number of hospitalisation days for GCA, number of C reactive protein measurements per month, cumulative number of measurements since GCA diagnosis and prescriptions of tocilizumab and methotrexate were included as time-varying covariates. In sensitivity analyses, all analyses were repeated without excluding patients with more than two steroid prescription dispensations in the year before GCA diagnosis. We also repeated the multivariable Fine-Gray models to look for factors associated with early sustained GC-free and DMARD-free remission. Covariate definitions are detailed in online supplemental table S1. All analyses were conducted in R V.4.3.3. LCGMs were fitted using the lcmm package.22

Results

We included 18 301 patients, of which 63% were female, mean age was 75.2 years (SD 8.7) and mean follow-up duration was 6.7 years (SD 3.7). A flow chart is available in online supplemental figure S1 and characteristics of patients included are shown in table 1. The mean 2-year cumulative oral GC dose following GCA diagnosis in the overall cohort was 8.9 g (SD 4.7). Sustained GC-free remission, defined as a continuous period of 6 months without any GC use, occurred in 7.7%, 35.6% and 80.6% of patients at 1, 2 and 5 years, respectively, with a median time to sustained GC-free remission from Kaplan-Meier estimator of 2.40 years (Q1–Q3: 1.74–4.07 years). Baseline use of methotrexate and tocilizumab was uncommon.

Glucocorticoid daily dose trajectories and trends over time

Figure 1A presents the four identified trajectories of GC use. The different trajectories identified include: medium-dose quick taper (MDQT) (n=9397, 51.3%), low-dose quick taper (LDQT) (n=6143, 33.6%), high-dose quick taper (HDQT) (n=1880, 10.3%), high-dose slow taper (HDST) (n=881, 4.8%). The first three trajectories showed a rapid taper over about 6 months, followed by an increase in daily GC dose after 1 year of follow-up, and all trajectories converged at 2 years of follow-up (figure 1A an 1B).

The proportion of patients in the HDQT and HDST decreased from 9.8% and 8.1% in 2010 to 7.3% and 1.6%, respectively, while the MDQT trajectory increased from 46.8% to 53.4% and the LDQT from 35.3% to 37.4% (figure 1C). The cumulative GC dose in the 3 and 6 months following GCA diagnosis was relatively stable across the study period with increasing calendar year, whereas the cumulative dose at 1 and 2 years of follow-up showed a modest decreasing trend (figure 1D). Patients diagnosed in 2022 had an average 2-year GC cumulative dose of 7.6 g (SD 3.1) vs 9.4 g (SD 4.8) in 2010.

Patients’ characteristics across trajectories are summarised in table 1. Patients in the LDQT trajectory were older at diagnosis, were more frequently female and had a greater frequency of polymyalgia rheumatica at GCA diagnosis. In contrast, the HDQT trajectory was characterised by the highest proportion of males and patients with obesity, the highest proportion of anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy (AION) at diagnosis, while showing the lowest mean age at diagnosis. Patients in the HDST trajectory were least likely to be diagnosed in teaching hospitals (20%), and had the highest rate of deaths, the greatest GC cumulative dose and the longest GC treatment duration. GC-sparing agents were prescribed more frequently in the high-dose trajectories. The trajectories were not modified when including patients with prevalent GC use (online supplemental figure S2).

Glucocorticoid daily dose during follow-up and sparing agents use over time

The proportion of living patients receiving GCs over time, stratified by daily dose, is shown in figure 2A. At 2 years mark, 55% of alive patients did not have GC dispensation at this timepoint; however, at 5 years, this proportion was only 72.1%, with 16.7% being on daily doses ≤5 mg/day and 11% on doses >5 mg/day. The median time to sustained GC-free remission decreased from 2.61 years (95% CI 2.55 to 2.65) in patients with GCA onset in the 2010–2015 period to 2.17 years (95% CI 2.13 to 2.21) among those with GCA onset in the 2020–2022 period (figure 2B). At 1 year, we observed 2388 patients in GC-free and DMARD-free remission out of 17 105 live patients (14%).

While cumulative incidence of methotrexate usage over time seems to be stable since 2020, prescription of tocilizumab has sharply increased in recent years, with >25% of patients diagnosed in 2022 receiving tocilizumab by the end of follow-up (figure 2C,D). Mean age at GCA diagnosis was 71.9 (SD 8.0) and 71.6 years (SD 8.0) for tocilizumab and methotrexate users, respectively, compared with 75.9 years (SD 8.7) among patients who did not receive either.

Factors associated with time to sustained GC-free remission

The main results from multivariable models of variables associated with time to sustained GC-free remission are presented in table 2. Factors which were associated with faster time to sustained GC-free remission were male sex, use of tocilizumab or methotrexate at baseline. Conversely, age ≥75 years, hypertension, neurocognitive disorder, chronic kidney disease, anterior optic ischaemic neuropathy and higher GC daily dose at index date were associated with slower time to GC-free remission.

Higher cumulative GC dose in the first 3 and 6 months was also associated with longer time to GC-free remission as were daily GC dose above 15 mg/day at 3 months and any dose higher than 7.5 mg/day at 6 months. Results of Fine-Gray models were similar without excluding patients with prevalent GC (online supplemental table S4) and when considering sustained GC-free and DMARD-free remission (online supplemental table S5).

Adverse outcomes associated with GCs according to dose and cumulative duration of exposure

Cumulative dose of GC was significantly associated with all outcomes. The HR for all-cause mortality was 1.024 (95% CI 1.021 to 1.027) per gram of GC (table 3). HR for serious infection was 1.050 (95% CI 1.038 to 1.062), for MACE 1.015 (95% CI 1.010 to 1.020) and for major osteoporotic fractures 1.013 (95% CI 1.006 to 1.019) per gram of GC. In table 3, we present HRs associated with different cumulative dose scenarios. For example, corresponding with a mean 2-year cumulative GC dose of 8.9 g, as observed in the whole cohort, the associated HR for all-cause mortality was 1.23 (95% CI 1.20 to 1.27).

Discussion

In this study, we identified four distinct GC usage trajectories. Over the past decade, the proportion of patients in trajectories characterised by high GC dose use has declined. Concurrently, the use of GC-sparing agents has steadily increased, especially tocilizumab, which is prescribed in more than a quarter of patients diagnosed in recent years. We identified that female sex, older age as well as some comorbidities such as chronic kidney disease, neurodegenerative disorders and hypertension were associated with slower GC tapering. Cumulative duration of exposure to high doses of GC was more strongly associated with serious infections and all-cause mortality than cumulative duration of exposure to lower doses; however, exposure to low doses (≤5 mg/day) was not safe either.

Although treatment strategies in GCA have been progressively shifting towards less GC use, our study shows that patients diagnosed in 2022 were still exposed to an average cumulative GC dose of 7.6 g in the 2 years following diagnosis. However, the latent class models clearly demonstrate that trajectories involving high doses, particularly those maintained over extended periods, have sharply decreased in recent years. Our findings suggest that this trend may be partly driven by the growing use of GC-sparing agents, and possibly in conjunction with recent guidelines that set explicit tapering goals.17

Studies specifically describing factors associated with duration of GC treatment in GCA are scarce and predominantly non-population-based, making them prone to selection bias.23 Interestingly, male sex was associated with faster sustained GC-free remission. Moreover, consistent with recent results from Narvaez et al,24 we observed that older patients experienced a longer time to sustained GC-free remission. The same pattern was observed among patients with comorbidities such as neurocognitive disorders, chronic kidney disease or hypertension. This could reflect clinician caution driven by comorbidity burden or concern for adverse effects, rather than more severe underlying disease or true biological resistance. This is particularly concerning, since these vulnerable populations are at higher risk of GC-related adverse effects and should be prioritised for timely GC withdrawal.

Furthermore, we sought to address a complex question regarding the association of cumulative duration and cumulative dose with GC-induced morbidities. Most previous studies have focused on current daily dose and risk of adverse events, which does not account for prior and future cumulative dose or duration of exposure.10 25

Adverse events related to GC in GCA are frequent, as recently reported by Curtis et al in a retrospective cohort in the MarketScan database. They reported that 83% of the GCA population experienced at least one GC-related adverse event within the first year of diagnosis.26 Gale et al, using MarketScan and the UK Clinical Practice Research Datalink in patients diagnosed between 2000 and 2015, reported an increased risk of osteoporosis (HR 1.03, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.04) and serious infection (HR 1.03, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.04) per gram of GC cumulative exposure, with no increased risk of myocardial infarction. However, the requirement for at least 1 year of follow-up may have introduced selection bias by excluding patients who died early from cardiovascular events, potentially underestimating cardiovascular disease risk.27 In another case-control study in the UK Clinical Practice Research Datalink, Wilson et al found that being currently exposed to doses >30 mg/day was associated with higher risk of death (OR 2.1, 95% CI 1.3 to 3.5), infection (OR 3.3, 95% CI 2.2 to 5.2) and fractures (OR 2.6, 95% CI 1.6 to 4.3), although this did not take into account the duration of exposure to such dose.16

Despite methodological differences, our results are in line with these findings. We found that exposure to doses >5 mg/day is associated with mortality, with a dose-dependent trend. Furthermore, the HR for mortality increased by 2.4% per gram of cumulative GC exposure. Based on our findings, HR for all-cause mortality associated with the mean cumulative dose observed in our cohort was thus 1.23, which is considerable. These results are particularly relevant given that recent randomised trials of tocilizumab and upadacitinib have demonstrated reductions of 1–2 g in cumulative GC exposure compared with placebo, yet lacked the statistical power and follow-up duration to detect the clinical benefits of such reductions.2 5

We also found that even at the lowest dose categories (≤5 mg/day), cumulative exposure was still associated with increased serious infection risk, suggesting even such low doses are not safe. Regarding cardiovascular events, our findings align with those of Pujades-Rodriguez et al, who concluded in a study including patients with multiple inflammatory conditions, including GCA, that no GC dose is entirely safe.10 Interestingly, we did not observe a dose-dependent effect regarding risk of MACE. This may reflect limited statistical power, but alternatively, GCs may act as proatherogenic agents,28 in which case cumulative exposure duration rather than dose intensity could be the more relevant risk factor. Likewise, as other studies reported in other diseases, we showed that in GCA, there is an association between GCs and increased risk of osteoporotic fractures even at doses inferior to 7.5 mg/day.29 30 Overall, although cumulative exposure to doses below 5 mg/day was not associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality, it was associated with major GC-related morbidities. This finding is particularly significant as we show that a non-negligible proportion of patients are exposed to GCs for longer than 2 years, even in recent years.

Despite the strengths of this population-based methodology, which limits selection bias and allows for a large sample size, and the complete capture of patients’ pharmacy medications dispensed, we acknowledge several limitations. As with most studies using claims databases, we did not have access to medical records and were therefore unable to assess clinical or biological variables. Consequently, disease activity could not be fully adjusted for and was instead approximated using proxies, including initiation of steroid-sparing agents, C reactive protein measurements and hospitalisation for GCA. Moreover, in-hospital prescriptions were unavailable, including methylprednisolone pulses, which may have led to underestimation of cumulative GC exposure and precluded evaluation of its association with sustained GC-free remission. Residual confounding remains possible. Regarding the diagnostic accuracy of ICD-10 codes for GCA, previous studies have shown excellent performance.20 To ensure we included incident patients with GCA, we excluded patients with more than two GC prescription dispensation in the year before the GCA code and included only those with a GC dispensation within 1 month after the GCA code. Although it was common clinical practice during the study period to hospitalise all patients with new-onset GCA, it remains possible that we missed some patients with mild disease activity who were not hospitalised. Finally, while outcomes such as all-cause mortality and hip fractures are reliably recorded in the database, other events such as vertebral fractures are known to be under-reported. As a result, GC-related osteoporotic events are likely underestimated.

We believe our work has several important implications. First, it shows that despite efforts over the past decade, average GC use in GCA remains high and sustained. Second, this study should serve as a reference point for evaluating GC use in the coming years, especially with the increasing adoption of GC-sparing agents. Third, although randomised trials have aimed to reduce GC use in GCA, the morbidity associated with reduced GC exposure had not been clearly established. Our findings highlight that the burden associated with cumulative GC exposure remains important, and that even low GC doses do not appear to be safe. Overall, our work underscores the need for continuous and accelerated efforts to reduce GC use in GCA.

Conclusion

In the first 2 years following GCA diagnosis, four distinct oral GC dose trajectories were identified: MDQT, LDQT, HDQT and HDST. Although tapering has become faster and the use of GC-sparing agents has increased in recent years, cumulative GC exposure remains substantial, both in terms of dose and duration. This is particularly relevant since each additional cumulative gram of GC is associated with increased risk of mortality, serious infection, MACE and major osteoporotic fractures. A dose-dependent relationship was also observed between daily dose and the risks of mortality and serious infection. These findings support continued efforts to limit GC use in the management of GCA.
