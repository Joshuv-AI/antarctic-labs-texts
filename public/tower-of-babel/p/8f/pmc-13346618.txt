Reply to: Lack of evidence for Initial Upper Palaeolithic attribution at Cueva Millán in Iberia’s Hinterland
Authors: Policarpo Sánchez Yustos
Published: 2026

replying to: T. Tsanova et al.; Scientific Reports 10.1038/s41598-026-55585-8 (2026).

In their Matters Arising, Tsanova et al. interpret the lithic assemblage from Cueva Millán Layer 1 (Millán 1) as Middle Palaeolithic (MP)1. They contrast this with what they incorrectly present as our attribution of Millán 1 to the Initial Upper Palaeolithic (IUP) and, by extension, to an early expansion of Homo sapiens into Iberia. However, our work neither classified Millán 1 as an IUP-type assemblage nor associated it with a specific species2. Here, we clarify our conceptual framework and classification proposal and demonstrate that their arguments for a MP attribution are not empirically supported.

Conceptual framework and IUP definition

Tsanova et al. define the IUP on the basis of a volumetric bidirectional core-reduction model and link it to the expansion of Homo sapiens across Eurasia and, often, to symbolic artefacts1. This biocultural approach departs from the foundational IUP formulations3–5.

The term IUP was first applied to Boker Tachtit (Israel). Levels 1–3, with bidirectional laminar production based on surface exploitation (Levallois-like), were assigned to the Emiran and interpreted as a transitional industry; whereas Level 4, characterized by unidirectional volumetric reduction, was considered fully UP and the original referent of the IUP3. Subsequent works broadened the IUP to include Emiran assemblages, including Boker Tachtipt Levels 1–34,6.

This broader definitional framework has two implications. First, the IUP is understood as a gradual technological continuum in core reduction terms, evolving from surface-oriented exploitation toward a fully volumetric conception5,7. Within this technological gradient, the IUP reduction method (IUP-RM) is defined by the implementation of the crested blade technique as its primary technological departure. This reduction system is oriented toward the production of blades and points, while retaining basal traits such as platform faceting and hard-hammer percussion. Second, the IUP becomes a widely distributed Eurasian phenomenon marked by substantial regional variability5,7. Accordingly, Tsanova et al.’s technological definition of the IUP is incompatible with this framework: it is neither restricted to fully volumetric technology nor to exclusively bidirectional removals.

We reported the presence of the IUP-RM at Millán 1 (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Text 1), alongside flake-based and bladelet-based reduction methods and the co-occurrence of MP and UP tool types2. From a classificatory standpoint, we defined Millán 1 as Arlanzian, a liminal entity associated with the formative phase of the UP2. More broadly, we grouped all liminal assemblages of this period under the term “initial Upper Palaeolithic” (initial UP), encompassing homologous “in transition” industries² (Supplementary Text 2). Our position is that there are assemblages with IUP core technology (IUP-RM), but not IUP assemblages. In sum, Tsanova et al.’s critique addresses a claim we did not make, namely that Millán 1 represents an IUP lithic assemblage and early evidence for Homo sapiens expansion into Iberia1. Nonetheless, we acknowledge that the subtle terminological distinction between initial UP and IUP, together with capitalization conventions, may have made the paper’s title open to an unintended reading at first glance (for other examples, see Supplementary Table 1).

Middle Palaeolithic misattribution

Tsanova et al. argue for a MP attribution of Millán 1 on four main grounds: the prevalence of flakes —some unequivocally attributable to the MP (Supplementary Text 3), the low proportion of retouched blades, the interpretation of the schéma croisé as a MP flake-based technology opposed to UP blade production, and an alleged lack of “true blades”1.

First, the prevalence of flakes is best explained by techno-economic grounds rather than taxonomic status. Millán 1 is a high-density, reduction-rich assemblage (n = 3,161), dominated by very small debitage (< 15 mm; n = 1,950), consistent with intensive on-site knapping2. In high waste-accumulation contexts with co-occurring reduction systems, high flake frequencies are expected. A comparable context of intensive on-site knapping is documented at Boker Tachtit, where flakes also outnumber blades, including among retouched tools3,8. By contrast, in the so-called IUP-type assemblage at Bacho Kiro (Bulgaria), blade cores are absent, laminar blanks entered the cave curated from off-site production, and the few reported cores are mainly geared toward flake production following conventional MP technologies9. This contrast shows that assemblage composition, such as flake/blade ratios and the presence of MP flake-based technologies, may reflect techno-economic strategies rather than rigid cultural norms. Second, the low proportion of retouched blades cannot determine classification, because retouch frequencies are sensitive to economic factors, including site function. Moreover, “well-defined” 1 IUP assemblages include both MP and UP tool types5,7, and the numerical predominance of the latter is not taxonomically meaningful10,11. In Millán 1, retouched artefacts are dominated by flake blanks, but UP tool types are present, often on laminar blanks2. Regardless, our aim is not to classify Millán 1 as an IUP-type assemblage. Third, Tsanova et al. treat the schéma croisé as a MP flake-based technology, in contrast to UP blade production1. This conflates a specific technical procedure, namely the exploitation of the flake ventral surface, with a distinct reduction method. Such procedure is not taxonomically diagnostic per se, as it has been implemented throughout the Palaeolithic within diverse reduction modalities and production objectives. Fourth, Tsanova et al. allege a lack of “true blades” based on a misinterpretation of our classification criteria. They assume that blanks with elongation index (EI) values between 0.75 and 0.50 were classified as blades (Supplementary 8 in ref.1), which is incorrect. In our system, EI 0.75–0.50 corresponds to elongated flakes (n = 153), whereas the metrical laminar range corresponds to EI < 0.50 (Supplementary Text 8 in ref.2 ) Blanks within this range were not automatically classified as blades: those that did not meet standard morpho-technological criteria were classified as laminar flakes (n = 21)2. Accordingly, artefacts classified as blades or bladelets meet standard metric and morpho-technical criteria (blades, n = 73; bladelets, n = 101, Supplementary Text 8 in ref.2). Tsanova et al.’s claim that “blades are rare or entirely absent” at Millán 1 is not empirically supported1. As shown in our experimental replication of the IUP-RM, blank elongation is strongly conditioned by the small size of the flint nodules exploited and by a range of technical decisions (Supplementary Text 10 in ref.2). These constraints affect blank proportions, producing not only blades and points but also elongated flakes and other by-products.

Mosaic pathways into the initial Upper Palaeolithic

Tsanova et al.’s MP attribution rests on a binary biocultural framework that links the IUP to Homo sapiens and the MP to Neanderthals1. The liminal nature of Millán 1 challenges this approach. The question is neither how closely it approximates a “canonical” assemblage profile nor whether it can be assigned to a particular human species. The key issue is whether it marks the end of a techno-cultural trajectory or the onset of a new one. The derived traits documented in Millán 1 align more closely with the initial stage of a new trajectory. Their joint occurrence within a single assemblage indicates a degree of innovation not previously documented in the Iberian MP record2.

Several other sites likewise challenge simplified and linear models of cultural change, favouring instead more complex and dynamic interpretations. Among these, Grotte Mandrin (France)12, Riparo l’Oscurusciuto (Italy)13, and Obi-Rakhmat (Uzbekistan)14 exhibit technological convergences with Millán 1. Despite their spatial and temporal differences, all four display shared traits, including IUP-RM, micropoints, prismatic bladelet production, and flake-based reduction methods. All combine innovative features with an intrusive presence within their respective regional cultural sequences. Rather than supporting an expansion or redefinition of the IUP as a taxonomic unit, this pattern points to recurrent technological configurations embedded within diverse regional pathways of cultural change.

Across Eurasia, initial UP assemblages display substantial variability, incompatible with rigid biocultural taxonomies. The relationships among these assemblages remain unresolved, but collectively they define a mosaic of pathways marking the formative phase of the UP, during a period of Neanderthal–Homo sapiens coexistence and interaction15.
