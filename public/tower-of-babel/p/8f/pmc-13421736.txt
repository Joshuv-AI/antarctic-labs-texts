Pertussis toxin-induced lymphocytosis shifts the tumor microenvironment toward immune activation via CXCL9 signaling
Authors: Jae-Keun Cho, Kyungmin Lee, Da-Yong Shin, Truc Do Thanh Nguyen, Woongyang Park, Se Jin Im, Han-Sin Jeong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA decrease in peripheral lymphocyte count in cancer patients predicts poorer prognosis and can be a useful discriminator in response to anti-tumor immunotherapy. However, therapeutic interventions to induce lymphocytosis and improve the efficacy of anti-tumor immunotherapy have not been investigated.MethodsIn this study, we used pertussis toxin (PTX) to induce lymphocytosis; toxicity was minimal at controlled doses. Using an in vivo squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) model, the effects of PTX on anti-PD1 treatment efficacy were evaluated. To characterize the underlying changes associated with PTX pretreatment, we conducted immunohistochemistry, transcriptomic analysis, flow cytometry, and chemokine array analyses.ResultsPTX increased lymphocyte counts in SCC-tumor-bearing mice and shifted tumors from low to high immune scores. PTX-induced elevation of CXCL9 led to lymphocytosis and increased immune cell infiltration in tumor tissues. Combining PTX with an anti-PD1 antibody significantly suppressed in vivo SCC tumor growth compared with anti-PD1 treatment alone. The combination treatment induced a greater proportion of T cells in the tumor microenvironment, and these findings were associated with increased terminally differentiated CD8+ T cells and higher Ki-67 expression. In addition, tumor-specific IFN-γ production was augmented in animals treated with anti-PD1 antibody and combination treatments, but not with PTX alone.ConclusionsPTX can induce high lymphocyte infiltration into the tumor microenvironment, creating a favorable immune environment for immune checkpoint inhibitors. These PTX actions could enhance the efficacy of anti-PD1 treatment in an in vivo SCC model.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-026-04436-6.

Background

Anti-tumor immunotherapy using immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) has become one axis of cancer treatments, alongside conventional cancer therapies [1, 2]. However, the response or benefit of ICI is limited to a portion of cancer patients [3]. As predictors of anti-tumor response to ICI, tumor mutational burden (TMB), tumor PD-L1 expression (tumor proportion score, TPS), and immune cells (combined positive score, CPS) are among the most commonly used markers in the clinic [4–9]. More practically, the blood cell counts in patients’ blood samples are a simple, readily accessible, and repeatable prognostic marker for many types of cancer. A decrease of peripheral lymphocyte count (lymphopenia) or an elevation of the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) predicts a worse prognosis in the pretreatment or salvage setting [10–15]. Peripheral lymphocyte count, along with NLR, can be a valid differentiating factor between good and poor responders to ICI [16–19]. However, the underlying mechanisms and therapeutic interventions that normalize lymphocyte counts or induce lymphocytosis in relation to anti-tumor immunotherapy responses remain incompletely understood.

A literature search showed that pertussis toxin (PTX) is among the most common stimulants of peripheral lymphocytosis [20, 21]. PTX is a secreted exotoxin produced by Bordetella pertussis, and it induces most of the systemic symptoms of pertussis disease, including profound leukocytosis as well as lymphocytosis [20, 21]. PTX is a key component of acellular pertussis vaccines and can be used as a single-component toxoid vaccine to induce anti-pertussis immunity [22, 23]. Thus, PTX is relatively safe in humans at controlled doses and could be readily applicable to cancer patients as a drug repositioning if proven effective in cancer treatment.

Most head and neck cancers have a squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) pathology, which is poorly immunogenic [24–26]. Indeed, the response rate to approved anti-tumor immune checkpoint inhibitors in head and neck SCC was limited to 30–40% of patients with metastatic disease [25, 27]. With this background, we focused on whether pretreatment with PTX to induce lymphocytosis can convert immune-cold tumors into immune-hot tumors, and whether this new approach can enhance the effectiveness of anti-tumor immunotherapy in an in vivo SCC model.

Materials and methods

Study protocol

The present study followed international, national, and/or institutional guidelines for humane animal treatment and complied with relevant legislation, as approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Samsung Biomedical Research Center, Seoul, Korea (Approval Nos. 20201210001, 20211118001, and 20221208001).

Tumor cell and animal models

The tumor cell line SCCVII (H-2 k) is a highly aggressive, spontaneously arising SCC cell syngeneic to C3H/HeJ mice. Four- to 6-week-old female C3H/HeJ mice were purchased from OrientBio (Seongnam, Gyung-gi, Korea). For an in vivo SCC model, 5 × 105 SCCVII tumor cells were injected subcutaneously into the flank of each mouse. Tumor volume was measured twice a week, and any cases with tumor necrosis were excluded from the study. Intervention (PTX or anti-PD1 treatment) started when the tumor volume reached 100–150 mm3 in each animal.

In vivo treatments of PTX and anti-PD1 antibody

PTX islet-activating protein was bought from List Biological Labs Inc. (Campbell, CA, USA) (Product No. 180), which has both enzymatic activity (through S1 subunit) and immunogenic activity. In-vivo MAb anti-mouse PD-1(CD279) antibody (clone RMP1-14) and in-vivo MAb Rat IgG2 Isotype control antibody (clone 2A3) were purchased from BioXcell (Lebanon, NH, USA). In a dose calibration study, we used 0.25, 0.5, and 1.0 μg of PTX in 100 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). For in vivo experiments, mice were injected with PTX (0.5 μg/100 μL PBS) (at day 0) and 150 μg anti-PD1 antibody or 150 μg IgG2a isotype control antibody at 1, 5, and 9 days after PTX injection. PTX toxin was administered intraperitoneally, and a mouse anti-PD1 antibody was subcutaneously injected in an area close to the tumor.

Characterization of tumor-infiltrating immune cells

Mice were sacrificed at the designated time and tumor tissues were collected. The tumors were transferred to 10 mL of R2 buffer (RPMI + 2% FBS + 2 mmol/L L-glutamine + 100 IU/mL penicillin/ 100 μg/mL streptomycin) in a 50 mL tube. The tumors were mechanically dissociated, and then 15 mL of supplement (RPMI + 5% FBS + 150 U/mL collagenase + CaCl2/MgCl2) was added before they were passed through a 70 μm cell strainer. After washing and centrifuging, the supernatant was discarded and the cells were resuspended in 6–8 mL of 40% Percoll in RPMI. Then, 63% Percoll solution (5 ml) was gently under-laid beneath the cell suspension. The interface layer was collected with three cycles of centrifuge and resuspension (ACK lysis buffer and R2 buffer). Then, the enriched cells were counted, and phenotyping was performed.

After cell counting, the cells were transferred into a 96-well round bottom plate. After centrifugation and washing with 220 μL of FE buffer (PBS + 2% FBS + 2 mM EDTA), 50 μL of antibody mixtures were incubated for 30 min at 4 °C. After further washing, the cells were fixed with 100 μL of 2% PFA for 10 min at 4 °C and resuspended in 220 μL of FE buffer. The stained cells were applied to a flow cytometer (BD FACSVerse™, BD Bioscience, MA, USA) and data were analyzed with the BD FACSuite™ software.

The mice's spleens were also collected and placed in 5 mL of R2 buffer. The spleen tissues were mechanically dissociated with 50 μm nylon mesh and the erythrocytes were lysed with ACK lysis buffer. After multiple washes, the cells were collected and resuspended in a 5 mL of buffer. The enriched cells were counted and phenotyped.

Immunohistochemistry

Sequential 4 µm-thick tumor sections were obtained from a representative formalin-fixed, paraffin embedded tumor block and used for immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis. Immune scores were calculated semi-quantitatively based on the median expression threshold. Each tumor section was assigned a binary score (0 for low, 1 for high) for each immune cell type (CD3+, CD8+, and CD45RO+) in both the tumor and the stroma [28]. The immune score was obtained by summing the six binary scores, yielding a range of 0 to 6.

The tumor proportional score (TPS) was defined as the number of PD-L1-positive tumor cells divided by the total number of viable tumor cells multiplied by 100%, and the combined positive score (CPS) was the number of PD-L1-stained cells (tumor cells, lymphocytes, and macrophages) divided by the total number of viable tumor cells, multiplied by 100% [7]. Single-cell transcriptome analysis

Tumor tissues and the draining lymph nodes were dissected from the mice and dissociated into single-cell suspensions using Liberase (cat no. 5401119001, Roche, San Francisco, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The tumors were removed at the predetermined volume (1000–1500 mm3), cut into 2 to 4 mm pieces, and put in GentleMACS C Tubes with Liberase [1 mL of 1X PBS (Cat. no. 10010023, Gibco, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), Liberase 5 mg/ml]. Tissue dissociation was performed using the program, 37C_m_TDK_1, with the GentleMACS Octo Dissociator (Cat. no. 130–093-235, Miltenyi Biotec, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) and incubated in a water bath at 37 °C. After dissociation, samples were filtered and centrifuged, the supernatant was discarded, and the sediment was resuspended in PBS.

The cell concentration of the single-cell suspension was maintained at 500 cells/μL (total 1 × 105 cells) and loaded onto Chromium Next GEM Chip G Single Cell Kit (Cat. no.1000120, 10X Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA). Single-cell RNA-seq libraries were constructed using Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3' Kit v3.1 (Cat. no.1000268, 10X Genomics). Individual libraries were diluted to 250–500 pM and sequencing was performed on an Illumina Novaseq6000 sequencing platform (San Diego, CA, USA), using an S2 Reagent Kit (200 cycles) in paired-end mode.

Raw sequencing data were processed with Cell Ranger (v5.0.0, 10X Genomics) software for barcode gene matrix generation. Single-cell RNA-seq data were analyzed using Seurat (v5.3.0). Doublets were identified and removed using scDblFinder, with sample identity used as a batch variable. Cells classified as doublets were excluded from downstream analysis. Quality control filtering retained cells with > 100 and < 7000 detected genes, < 25% mitochondrial gene content, and < 30,000 total UMIs. Data were log-normalized (scale factor = 10,000), and 2000 highly variable genes were identified using the “vst” method. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed, and the top 10 principal components were selected based on elbow plot inspection. Batch correction across samples was performed using the Harmony R package. UMAP was used for dimensionality reduction, and clustering was performed using the Louvain algorithm.

Cell types were annotated based on the expression of canonical marker genes. T/NK cells were subsetted and further divided into CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells based on relative CD4 and CD8a expression. CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ subsets were reprocessed independently (variable feature selection, scaling, PCA, Harmony integration, clustering) and annotated according to established subtype markers. For CD8 Tpex, CD8 Ttex, and CD8 Tprol subsets in tumor tissue, differential gene expression between the PTX and control groups was performed using FindMarkers without fold-change thresholding. Genes were ranked by average log2 fold change and subjected to Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using the Hallmark gene sets (MSigDB, Mus musculus) via clusterProfiler. Pathways with adjusted p-value < 0.1 were considered significant. Normalized Enrichment Scores (NES) were used to indicate directionality of enrichment (positive NES: enriched in PTX; negative NES: enriched in the control group).

Flow cytometry and antibodies

Surface staining was performed on ice for 30 min in PBS containing 2% FBS and 2 mM EDTA with the antibodies, listed in Table 1. Intracellular staining was performed with the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Cat. no. 00-5523-00, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and the following antibodies: FITC anti-Ki67 antibody, PE anti-TCF1 antibody, and APC anti-mouse Foxp3 antibody (Table 1). Flow cytometric analysis was performed on a Cytoflex flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) or a FACS Canto II of the BIORP (BD Bioscience, San Jose, CA, USA) and analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10, Tree Star, Inc., Ashland, OR, USA).

Chemokine array

Splenocytes were isolated from animals, and those cells (1 × 106 cells) were stimulated with treatment-naïve tumor-conditioned media (control group) and PTX-treated tumor-conditioned media for 4 days at 37 °C. A Proteome Profiler Mouse Cytokine Array Kit (Mouse chemokine array kit, ARY020, R&D System, MN, USA) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions.

ELISpot assay and evaluation of tumor-specific IFN-γ production

IFN-γ secretion in response to PTX treatment (at day 5 after PTX) was measured in mouse splenocytes and tumor lymphocytes using the Mouse IFN-γ ELISpot Kit (EL485, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). Cells were seeded into wells coated with immobilized antibody to capture secreted IFN-γ. The wells were incubated with biotinylated anti-IFN-γ antibody, followed by alkaline phosphatase-conjugated streptavidin. Subsequently, BCIP/NBT substrate was added to visualize spots. Visualized spots were imaged and counted using AID Classic ELR07 Elispot Reader (Advanced Imaging Devices GmbH, Strasberg, Germany).

To prepare apoptotic tumor cells, SCCVII and B16F10 cells were irradiated with ultraviolet (UV)-B for 10 min at 1.5 mW/cm2. The percentage of apoptotic cells was determined by flow cytometry. Splenocytes (1 × 107 cells/3 mL/well) from each group of mice were stimulated with the relevant antigen (the apoptotic SCC VII cells) or with the irrelevant antigen (the apoptotic B16F10 cells) for 4 days at 37 °C. The amount of IFN-γ in the culture supernatants was measured using an IFN-γ ELISA (PharMingen, San Diego, CA, USA). In this experiment, the time point for splenocyte harvest was 10 day after PTX (day 0) and/or anti-PD1 treatments (day 1,5, and 9).

CXCR3 inhibition assay

CXCR3 is a G protein-coupled receptor that binds interferon-inducible chemokines CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11 [29, 30]. To confirm the functional roles of chemokines induced by PTX treatment, we conducted an inhibition assay of CXCL9/10/11-CXCR3 axis using CXCR3 antagonist SCH546738 (Cat. No. HY-10017, MedChemExpress, NJ, USA). SCCVII cells (2 × 106 cells) were injected to induce in vivo tumors (day 0). In the inhibition group (n = 5), SCH546738 (600 μg/day) was administered orally on days 6, 8, 10, and 12 (four times), and the mice were injected with PTX (0.5 μg/mouse) on day 8 (one time). For comparison, only PTX was treated in the control group (n = 5) without SCH546738. On day 13, the animals were sacrificed, and the blood and tumor lymphocytes (and neutrophils) were analyzed, as well as chemokine arrays.

Statistical analysis

The Mann–Whitney U test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare continuous variable groups. Post-hoc analyses were conducted using the Dunnett T3 test. A two-way ANOVA and a linear mixed-effects model were used to assess differences in time-dependent tumor growth across groups. A two-tailed p-value less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant. The Prism 7 program for Windows (version 7.00, GraphPad Software Inc. SD, CA) was used for statistical analyses and graph plotting.

Results

PTX decreased NLR in tumor-bearing mice through lymphocytosis

First, different doses of PTX (0.25, 0.5, and 1 μg) were injected into healthy mice (non-tumor-bearing), and changes in lymphocyte and neutrophil counts were monitored. All doses of PTX induced some extent of lymphocytosis and neutrophilia in the peripheral blood of mice, but a single dose of 0.5 μg PTX led to greater lymphocytosis and lesser neutrophil elevation than other doses (Supplementary Fig. 1a-b). The NLR was slightly elevated (median 1.68, interquartile range, IQR 1.35–1.80 in a dose of 0.5 μg PTX) on day 5, then returned to the baseline level (Supplementary Fig. 1c). The change in blood cell counts was most noticeable on post-injection day 5. Therefore, we selected 0.5 μg of PTX and evaluated the response on day 5 in the subsequent experiments. None of the PTX doses affected the body weight of the mice (Supplementary Fig. 1d). At these controlled doses of PTX, we did not observe any clinical signs and symptoms of PTX toxicity, including cough, fever, and behavioral changes, and the sacrificed animals did not show any pathological changes in major organs (Supplementary Fig. 1e).

Next, we investigated the effect of PTX on tumor-bearing mice on lymphocyte numbers and NLR. To adjust for the potential effect of tumor burden, we separately evaluated peripheral lymphocyte count and NLR by tumor volume. PTX (0.5 μg) significantly increased lymphocyte counts but decreased the NLR in tumor-bearing mice, irrespective of tumor volume (Fig. 1a-c). Peripheral blood smear (Giemsa staining) also confirmed lymphocytosis in the blood of tumor-bearing animals (Fig. 1d-e). As a result, PTX evoked greater infiltration of immune cells (CD8+, CD45RO+ T lymphocytes) in tumor tissues, resulting in a high immune score. In the tumor cells, PD-L1 expression was similar between the control group and PTX-treated group, but the number of PD-L1+ (or PD1+) cells appeared to increase in tumor tissues (Fig. 2f) [31]. PTX enhances the effect of anti-PD1 treatment for in vivo SCC tumors

A single cycle (one dose of PTX followed by three successive anti-PD1 antibody treatments) and multiple cycles of combination treatment produced a notable difference in the tumor growth curve, compared to other groups, indicating significant inhibition of in vivo tumor growth (Fig. 3a-b). As the days after the treatments passed, the synergistic effects between PTX and anti-PD1 treatments became more pronounced, both with single and multiple treatments. However, statistical significance was not achieved due to the small sample sizes (n = 5–9 per group) with the Bliss independence test (Supplementary Fig. 2). Discussion

A decrease in lymphocytes in the peripheral blood of cancer patients, or an elevated NLR has been established as a reliable and straightforward indicator of poor prognosis across many cancer types [10–15]. It is also an index of the anti-tumor immune response to immune checkpoint inhibitors [16–19]. Beyond its prognostic role, we examined whether an interventional increase in lymphocyte counts may have therapeutic implications for cancer patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibition.

To this end, we conducted a literature search to identify candidate drugs that induce reactive lymphocytosis. As a potential immune modifier in this study, we selected PTX, which has long been used in clinical practice as a component of the pertussis vaccine [22, 23]. PTX is a protein-based AB5-type exotoxin of Bordetella pertussis with ADP-ribosylate GTP-binding mechanisms and is a causative agent of lymphocytosis [20, 21]. PTX is a major protective antigen, and detoxified PTX (chemically or genetically inactivated) has long been used as a vaccine for pertussis disease, including the filamentous hemagglutinin component [22, 23]. In vivo, PTX has a wide range of biological effects, including alterations in immune cells, leukocytosis, hyperinsulinemia, histamine sensitization, and changes in chemokines [20, 22]. The target cells of PTX include respiratory epithelial cells, endothelial cells, and immune cells (neutrophils, lymphocytes, and macrophages) [32–34]. Intriguingly, PTX inhibits neutrophil recruitment and directly impairs several neutrophil functions [35], while inducing a dramatic increase in blood lymphocytes (lymphocytosis) [33, 34]. PTX-induced lymphocytosis arises from inhibition of lymphocyte extravasation rather than from enhanced lymphopoiesis, with important mechanistic implications [36]. The observed increase in intratumoral or peripheral lymphocyte counts may reflect altered trafficking dynamics rather than a net increase in lymphocyte production.

Recently, research has shown that PTX can inhibit cancer cell proliferation, migration, and metastasis, and enhance HER2-targeted therapy in HER2-driven breast cancer [37]. Therefore, many unexplored roles of PTX in cancer treatment warrant investigation for drug repositioning. We also evaluated the potential effects of PTX on SCCVII cells and found that it did not affect tumor cell proliferation but did reduce cell migration. Moreover, CXCR3 expression levels did not change in tumor cells following PTX administration (Supplementary Fig. 6a-c), suggesting that the primary effect of PTX was mediated by immune cell changes.

The PTX used in this study is native PTX (not detoxified), which has both enzymatic activity (through the S1 subunit) and immunogenic activity. In contrast, the S1 subunit is inactivated (chemically or genetically) in the human pertussis vaccine, resulting in minimal enzymatic activity. Further experiments examining whether detoxified PTX can elicit an immunologic response sufficient to protect against the tumor should be conducted after our study (although detoxified PTX has been shown to induce protective immunity against pertussis infection) [22, 23]. The dose of PTX toxoids (a detoxified form of PTX) for human vaccination is typically < 25 µg/0.5 mL for infants. Adjusted doses of PTX toxoids are 0.125–0.48 µg per animal (body weight of 25–40 g), which is similar to the PTX dose used in our experiments.

As expected, we confirmed that PTX administration induced CD8+ T cell lymphocytosis in tumor-bearing and healthy (non-tumor-bearing) mice. Interestingly, immune scores increased significantly with PTX treatment, suggesting that PTX could shift immune-cold tumors to immune-hot tumors. However, PTX treatment did not significantly alter TPS or CPS. Therefore, the action of PTX may be mediated by lymphocyte infiltration (mainly CD8+ T cells), rather than by changes in PD-L1 expression in tumors or immune cells.

These findings led us to investigate the potential synergistic role of PTX with anti-PD1 antibody treatment. Compared with anti-PD1 treatment alone, PTX pretreatment could mobilize T cells from the tumor-draining lymph nodes to the tumor site, potentially priming an effector T cell response in the tumor microenvironment and enhancing the anti-tumor immune response when combined with an immune checkpoint inhibitor (anti-PD1 antibody). In detail, these enhanced anti-tumor effects with PTX and an anti-PD1 antibody could primarily result from the expansion of terminally differentiated CD8+ effector T cells. Based on our data, PTX alone appeared to have minimal effect on tumor growth, and the combination of PTX with immune checkpoint inhibitors (or tumor antigen-specific anti-tumor immunity) is a reasonable approach to overcome the limitations of single-agent therapy.

It was also essential to determine the key PTX signals that evoke lymphocyte mobilization in tumor-bearing mice. The chemokine array provided an outline of the underlying mechanism of PTX in the tumor microenvironment, revealing CXCL9 and other chemokines as the main signaling pathway. In lymphocytes, the CXCL9 pathway primarily operates through a paracrine feedback loop: IFN-γ from activated lymphocytes induces nearby cells, such as macrophages, dendritic cells, and endothelial cells, to secrete CXCL9, which then attracts and activates more lymphocytes [29, 30, 38, 39]. The secreted CXCL9 binds to its receptor, CXCR3, on activated T lymphocytes [29, 30, 38]. This binding induces lymphocytes to migrate toward CXCL9, effectively recruiting them to inflamed tissue or tumors [29, 30]. Our experiments analyzing chemokines in tumor tissue and splenocytes also demonstrated that PTX activated a variety of immune cells systemically, induced chemokine release, leading to increased T-cell infiltration into the tumor (Supplementary Fig. 7). One mechanism of resistance to checkpoint inhibitors may involve chemokine signaling, which suppresses anti-tumor immunity [40–43]. In this regard, administration of PTX before anti-PD1 treatment could be an attractive tool to reverse anti-tumor immune suppression by activating the CXCL9/10/11/CXCR3 axis and promoting subsequent lymphocyte recruitment.

In anti-PD1 antibody treatment groups (alone or in combination with PTX), splenocyte IFN-γ responses were tumor-specific and significantly higher than those in other groups. However, the amount of secreted IFN-γ did not differ between the anti-PD1 treatment alone and the combination treatment. Moreover, PTX alone did not significantly enhance tumor-specific IFN-γ production compared to the control group (at day 14). Therefore, PTX likely mediates chemokine-driven infiltration of CD8 + T cells but does not restore their function.

PTX and PD-1 blockade may exert complementary but mechanistically distinct effects. By analogy to the sequential mechanistic model proposed for IL-2 plus PD-1 combination therapy [44], PTX appears to act primarily on the quantitative arm of the antitumor response, whereas PD-1 blockade acts mainly on the qualitative arm. In our model, PTX induced only a partial and transient increase in IFN-γ, which was detectable at an early time point (< 10 days) (Figs. 5j, 6jk, and Supplementary Fig. 1a-c) but no longer apparent later (Fig. 3c). However, even this transient IFN-γ pulse may have been sufficient to initiate downstream signaling events that resulted in sustained CXCL9 upregulation. Thus, the major consequence of PTX may be the establishment of a CXCL9-CXCR3-dependent recruitment program, which promotes CD8+ T-cell trafficking into the tumor microenvironment and increases the pool of effector cells available for subsequent checkpoint blockade. In contrast, increased infiltration alone is unlikely to be sufficient for optimal tumor control, and the functional reinvigoration of these recruited T cells appears to depend largely on PD-1 blockade. In this framework, PTX establishes the cellular substrate necessary for response, whereas anti-PD1 provides the functional signal required for effective antitumor activity. This model may explain the enhanced tumor suppression observed with combination treatment and is also consistent with the concept of “immune-cold” tumors, in which insufficient T-cell recruitment is a major barrier to immunotherapeutic efficacy.

Although we observed the beneficial effect of PTX in combination with an anti-PD1 antibody, several questions remained unanswered in our study. First, PTX also affects myeloid cells, including macrophages, dendritic cells, bone marrow-derived suppressor cells, and neutrophils, which were largely unexplored in this study. In addition, we tested only the combined effects in a single in vivo SCC tumor model, limiting the generalizability of our findings to other tumor models. Regarding the signaling pathways, we did not conduct confirmatory experiments to assess potential mediators (other chemokines) involved in the action of PTX and combination treatments. In addition, we did not investigate potential interactions among neutrophils in the tumor microenvironment, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, or the anti-PD1 antibody in the current study. Therefore, further investigation is needed to confirm our findings and draw more robust conclusions.

Conclusions

In summary, PTX significantly increased lymphocyte count in tumor-bearing mice and shifted the tumors from low to high immune scores (Fig. 7). Chemokine array analysis revealed that PTX-induced elevation of CXCL9 could be responsible for lymphocytosis and high immune cell infiltration in tumor tissues. The combination of PTX and anti-PD1 treatments significantly suppressed in vivo SCC tumor growth, compared with anti-PD1 treatment alone. These findings were associated with an increase in activated terminally differentiated CD8 T cells in tumor tissue. Tumor-specific immunity was confirmed in animals receiving anti-PD1 treatment and in combination treatment. Therefore, we conclude that PTX can induce systemic lymphocytosis, greater lymphocyte infiltration into tumor tissue, and a favorable immune environment for immune checkpoint inhibitors, thereby enhancing the efficacy of anti-PD1 treatment in an in vivo SCC tumor model.Fig. 7Graphical summary of this study.
