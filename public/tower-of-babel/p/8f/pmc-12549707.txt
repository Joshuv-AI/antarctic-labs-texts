Locally advanced primary urethral carcinoma in an HIV-positive female patient: report of a case successfully managed with definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy at a referral cancer center in Abidjan
Authors: Evrard Narcisse Séka, Corneille Téa Saki, Amara Gozana Tuo, Paul Hyacinthe Foua Bi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFemale Primary urethral carcinoma is a rare malignancy frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage. Surgery, the most common treatment modality, is associated with substantial functional impairment. Here, we present the case of a 45-year-old HIV-positive woman with locally advanced squamous cell carcinoma infiltrating the bladder, vagina, and left obturator muscle, treated with definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy combining fractionated external beam radiotherapy and weekly cisplatin. The treatment resulted in complete remission, which was sustained after 25 months of follow-up. This case supports the effectiveness of definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy as an organ-preserving approach for advanced female squamous cell carcinoma of urethra.Keywords: Squamous cell carcinoma of female Urethra, HIV-Positive, Concurrent chemoradiotherapy, Organ preservation

Introduction

Primary urethral carcinoma (PUC) is an extremely rare malignancy, accounting for only 0.02 % of all female cancers and less than 1 % of all malignancies of the female genitourinary tract.1,2 Risk factors remain unclear though high-risk Human papilloma virus (HPV) infection were mostly documented.3,4 The initial signs are not pathognomonic, which often results in a late diagnosis. Consequently, many patients present with large, advanced lesions.5 Retrospective studies have shown that a combination of surgery and radiotherapy seems to improve local control and survival rates.4 In women, the urethra's anatomical characteristics allow for rapid involvement of adjacent organs, such as the bladder and vagina. Therefore, anterior pelvectomy is often the only feasible surgical option for locally advanced tumors. This inevitably results in significant morbidity and poor quality of life outcomes. Chemoradiotherapy ± brachytherapy is an attractive therapeutic strategy that has demonstrated encouraging tumor control and survival rates while preserving organ function.6,7 In HIV(Human Immunodeficiency Virus)-positive patients, concurrent chemoradiotherapy can be challenging as drug-drug interaction and immunodeficiency may increase the rate of treatment-related toxicities.8 In this report, we present a rare case of a locally advanced primary urethral carcinoma in an HIV-positive female patient successfully managed with definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCR).

Case presentation

She is a 45-year-old with HIV infection positivity on antiretroviral treatment for eight years. Her history reported pelvic pains that lasted for three years and worsened with hematuria and vaginal bleeding. As a result, she consulted a gynecologist. The clinical examination found an irregular, fixed, hard, painless mass at the anterior vaginal wall, that bled on contact and partially reduced the vaginal lumen. A trans-vaginal biopsy was performed and the pathological examination concluded to a moderately differentiated squamous cell carcinoma. Pelvic MRI showed a tumor of the urethra measuring 72x56 × 45 mm (craniocaudally, transversely, and anteroposteriorly), that infiltrated the entire length of the anterior vaginal wall, the lower part of the bladder, and the internal obturator muscle (Fig. 1 a, b, c and d). There was no pelvic lymph node metastasis. Thoracic and abdominal CT scan did not show any distant metastasis.

We concluded to was a moderately differentiated squamous cell carcinoma of the urethra, classified as T4N0M0 (according to the AJCC/UICC 8th edition, 2017). A Neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by anterior pelvectomy or definitive CCR was proposed. As she refused surgery, CCR was chosen. Patient was simulated in supine position, full bladder and empty rectum. An iodine radio-opaque solution was injected in the vagina to help tumor delineation. We used MRI and CT-scan images fusion to delineate the gross tumor volume (GTV). A 10 mm geographical margin was added to the GTV to obtain the tumor clinical target volume (CTVt). Nodal clinical target volume (CTVn) included common iliac, internal iliac, external iliac, presacral, and inguino-femoral lymph node areas. A 7mm geographical margin was added to the CTV to obtain the planning target volume (PTV) (Fig. 2 a). The doses were 46Gy to the lymph nodes areas and 66Gy to the tumor in conventional fractionation (Fig. 2 b). Radiotherapy was given concurrently with weekly intravenous cisplatin at a dose of 40 mg/m2. she received 5 cycles of this regimen. The treatment spread over 53 days. The patient experienced grade 2 radiodermatitis and cystitis during treatment. Three months after completing treatment, the mass had completely disappeared. No urinary or digestive disorders were noted. Six months later, a pelvic MRI confirmed a complete response (Fig. 3 a and b). During her most recent visit, 25 months after completing radiotherapy, she was still in complete remission, though she reported occasional involuntary urine leakage.

Discussion

Urethral carcinoma is a very rare cancer for which the risk factors are not clearly elucidated. HPV infection has been found to be more often associated with it3,4. HIV is an indirect carcinogen that promotes the development of HPV-related cancers9. Some authors have already reported on CCR in the treatment of locally advanced forms10, 11, 12. However, report on, and management of an HIV-positive patient is rarely described. This case was probably the first. External beam radiotherapy with concurrent cisplatin-based chemotherapy has been effective, with minor to moderate treatment-related toxicities. However, long term follow-up is necessary.

In previous studies, surgery, with or without adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy, has been the most commonly used therapeutic strategy in locally advanced carcinoma of female urethra.5,13 However, radiotherapy has been used for some patients and has led to good local control and organ preservation. Brachytherapy is the radiotherapy modality that has been often used. Sharma et al. reported on five cases of SCC treated with a combination of external beam radiotherapy (EBRT) and brachytherapy. At 25 month of follow-up, 2 patients (40 %) recurred, one locally and one in regional lymph node.14 Magnuson et al. reported on two cases of female urethral carcinoma. One patient received EBRT at a dose of 45 Gy combined with concurrent chemotherapy (5fluoro-uracile 750mg/m2 over 3 days J1 to J3 and J29 to J31, and Cisplatin 60mg/m2 J1 and J29), followed by brachytherapy at a dose of 25 Gy two weeks later. They obtained a long term local control of 90 months, but the patient experienced severe toxicities, such as necrosis and stenosis.15 In the report of Andraud et al., at 21month of follow-up, the use of brachytherapy led to a local control rate of 82.5 %, though 9 % of patients developed focal necrosis.16 Merten et al. reported on three patients treated with brachytherapy. At 103 months of follow-up, all patients had good tumor control. However, the female patients developed grade 3 vaginal synechiae.7 Brachytherapy is the most precise technique for delivering high doses to tumors while sparing surrounding tissue. However, it requires technically qualified personnel, and it can lead to severe, late adverse effects that can be difficult to manage.

In this report, we used external beam radiotherapy only with concurrent chemotherapy as brachytherapy was not feasible in our institution. In HIV-positive patient, concurrent chemoradiotherapy is challenging. Interaction between anti-retroviral therapy and chemotherapy drugs can be responsible of high-grade hematological toxicities17. However, a study of the AIDS malignancy consortium found that the majority of HIV-infected women can complete CCR with the same dose of cisplatin used in HIV-uninfected women18. Moreover, the NCCN recommends the use of the same treatment regimens as for HIV-negative patients (NCCN guidelines in oncology, cancer in people with HIV, version 1.2026 – September 5, 2025). Our patient was treated with concurrent cisplatin with acceptable hematological tolerance. We achieved local control for more than two years. Itoh et al. described two cases of unresectable advanced PUC treated with CCR. In contrast to us, the chemotherapy regimen included a combination of 3 drugs (Docetaxel 75mg/m2, 5Fluorouracil 1000mg/m2 and Cisplatine100mg/m2, every 28 days) leading to Grade 3 dermatopathy, anorexia and myelosuppression. They achieved a complete response, but survival data were unavailable.10 Coop et al. reported two cases of SCC of the urethra treated with external radiation therapy combined with concurrent chemotherapy using the same regimen as ours.11 They achieved good local control 18 months after treatment ended. Recently, Al Barajraji et al. reported another case of SCC of the urethra that was treated with definitive CCR. They also used weekly Cisplatin at a dose of 40 mg/m2 as concurrent chemoratherapy regimen. A good local control 24 months after treatment was obtained12. As in our patient, radiotherapy was delivered in conventional fractionation in these studies. However, the total doses administered (50.4 Gy, 64 Gy, and 59.4 Gy) were lower than those in our patient (66 Gy). Unlike their patients, who did not experience any notable side effects, our patient experienced late toxicity in the form of involuntary and inconsistent urine leakage. These results allow us to conclude that squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) of the female urethra can be controlled with relatively low doses of radiation, making external beam radiotherapy alone sufficient without the need for brachytherapy.

Anterior exenteration, the surgical technique suitable for locally advanced carcinomas of urethra, is associated with severe impairment of quality of life. Brachytherapy was effective. Long term disease control data were reported. However, it was source of considerable late toxicities in certain reports (necrosis, stenosis and synechiae).7,15,16 Concurrent chemoradiotherapy using external radiation therapy combined with concurrent weekly cisplatin chemotherapy was effective and less toxic. Therefore, it could be regarded as a good trade-off for the treatment of locally advanced SCC of the female urethra, even in HIV-positive patients. It may become the standard treatment for locally advanced SCC of the female urethra. Surgery and brachytherapy could be proposed as salvage therapies in the event of recurrence.

Conclusion

Concurrent chemoradiotherapy combining external radiotherapy and concomitant cisplatin-based chemotherapy is a conservative treatment modality that provides good control with an acceptable quality of life in female locally advanced squamous cell carcinoma of the urethra.
