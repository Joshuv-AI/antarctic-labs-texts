A Generalized Lichen Planus Following COVID-19 Vaccination: A Case Report
Authors: Jee Yun Doh, Ji Hyun Lee
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
As part of the efforts to overcome the ongoing coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, mass vaccination programs against the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) have been initiated. Since then, an increasing number of cutaneous adverse events associated with the COVID-19 vaccines have been reported. Lichen planus (LP) is a rare inflammatory mucocutaneous disease with various clinical presentations, although uncommon, occurring following vaccination. There have been several cases of LP reported following COVID-19 vaccination. However, there has been no report of generalized LP following the COVID-19 vaccine to our knowledge. Herein, we report a case of generalized LP following the Oxford/AstraZeneca (AZD1222) vaccine. A 68-year-old female presented with widespread, erythematous pruritic papules and plaques on the trunk and both limbs, which developed 2 to 3 days after administration of the AZD1222 vaccine. Histopathological examinations revealed cellular interface dermatitis. The patient was diagnosed with generalized LP and was successfully treated with systemic corticosteroid and cyclosporine. As the vaccination campaign against COVID-19 is ongoing and early recognition and treatment are essential to reduce the morbidity of this condition, clinicians should be aware that LP could follow COVID-19 vaccination regardless of the type of vaccine administered.

INTRODUCTION

Lichen planus (LP) is a chronic T-cell mediated inflammatory disease involving skin and mucous membranes. Although the etiology of LP remains unknown, vaccination is thought to be causative of LP12. Hepatitis B vaccine is the most commonly attributed vaccine associated with LP, followed by the influenza virus, herpes zoster vaccines, and Diphtheria, Tenatus, Pertussis (DtaP)3. Lately, several cases of LP that occur after COVID-19 infection and vaccination have been reported456. However, there have been no reports of generalized lichen planus following COVID-19 vaccination in the literature. Herein, we report a case of generalized LP following Oxford/AstraZeneca (AZD1222) COVID-19 vaccination.

CASE REPORT

A 68-year-old female presented to our clinic with a skin rash. The patient had a history of systemic contact dermatitis from poison ivy. These lesions were well-controlled through topical corticosteroids and narrowband ultraviolet B phototherapy. The patient reported a highly pruritic rash, distinctive from the contact lesions, on the trunk and limbs days after the first dose of the AZD1222 vaccine. No other contact history was suspected. Also, she had no other underlying medical conditions and had not introduced any new medications in the previous months.

Physical examination revealed multiple, variable-sized, erythematous papules and plaques on the trunk and limbs, showing Wickham striae and koebnerization (Fig. 1). We received the patient’s consent form about publishing all photographic materials. There were no mucosal or nail involvement and no significant laboratory findings, including complete blood count and routine biochemical parameters. Serologies for hepatitis C virus, hepatitis B virus, and human immunodeficiency virus were all negative. Histopathological findings showed hyperkeratosis, acanthosis, exocytosis with prominent vacuolar degeneration of basal layer and civatte body in the epidermis. A dense band-like infiltrate consisted of lymphohistiocytic cells obscuring the dermo-epidermal junction was identified (Fig. 2).

Based on clinicopathological findings and temporal association, the patient was diagnosed with generalized LP secondary to COVID-19 vaccination. The patient was treated with oral triamcinolone and desonide 0.05% lotion. Oral cyclosporine 100 mg was also added to treat persistent pruritus. Two months after initiating therapy, the LP lesions showed gradual improvements with residual post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation. The patient opted not to receive the second vaccine dose.

DISCUSSION

LP is a rare but well-known adverse event associated with vaccinations. Hepatitis B, influenza, shingles, and Diphtheria, Tenatus, Pertussis (DtaP) vaccines are all known to be linked to LP3. Although the exact pathogenesis is unknown, cross-reactivity of T cells is thought to be causative. Once exposed to these triggering factors, T cells against exogenous antigens showing cross-reactivity to epithelial basal cells could be developed. Along with up-regulation of Th1 response and inflammatory cascade initiated by pro-apoptotic cytokines including interferon (IFN)-γ, interleukin (IL)-2, and IL-4, T cells cause apoptosis of basal keratinocyte, leading to the development of LP2.

As the COVID-19 vaccination rate rose worldwide, reports of adverse events also increased. Delayed and acute local reactions in injection sites are the most common cutaneous adverse events, followed by urticarial, morbilliform eruptions, chilblains7. However, as shown in Table 1, only ten reported cases relate to LP67891011121314. Including our case, nine patients were over 50 years old, and five had a history of inflammatory dermatoses. This is consistent with previous findings that LP following vaccination is more common in the elderly and those with a history of inflammatory dermatoses3. While the presence of the LP with other inflammatory dermatoses could be due to coincidence, the concept of the immunocompromised district suggests otherwise. The concept suggests that local immunological imbalance makes diseased or injured skin areas more prone to developing novel diseases6. Also, eight presented with cutaneous LP, two with mucosal LP, and one with lichen planopilaris. However, only our case presented with a rare variant of generalized LP characterized by rapidly spreading, disseminated, erythematous, flat-topped, polygonal papules and macules15.

Pfizer-BioNTech (BNT162b2; New York, NY, USA) vaccine is an mRNA vaccine, while Oxford-AstraZeneca (AZD1222; London, UK) and Janssen (Ad26.COV2.S; Beerse, Belgium) vaccines are non-replicating viral vector vaccines. Although the genomic sequences are delivered differently, both vaccines administer the host with a sequence encoding viral spike (S) protein resulting in a predominant Th1 response showing from increased IL-2, TNF-α, and IFN-γ serum levels3161718. These cytokines play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of LP responsible for basal keratinocyte apoptosis2. Inactivated vaccines such as Sinovac (CoronaVac; Beijing, China) and Sinopharm (BBIBP-CorV; Beijing, China) are also thought to stimulate and induce a T-cell-driven response with upregulation of those cytokines711. Since the immunological changes from the COVID-19 vaccination are like those seen in the pathogenesis of LP, it is plausible that LP may occur due to the COVID-19 vaccine, although we cannot entirely rule out coincidence.

To our knowledge, there have not been any reports of generalized LP following the AZD1222 vaccine. Our case highlights that, although rare, LP should be considered as a possible adverse event from all types of COVID-19 vaccines.
