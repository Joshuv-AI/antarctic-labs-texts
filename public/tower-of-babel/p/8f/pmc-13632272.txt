Genome-wide detection and genomic selection based on genotype-by-environment interaction-associated signals for yak coat color
Authors: Yujiao Fu, Jiahong Zhao, Yuanyuan Yu, Binglin Yue, Hui Wang, Ming Zhang, Wei Peng, Shi Shu, Guowen Wang, Jincheng Zhong, Daoliang Lan, Jiabo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThrough long-term natural and artificial selection, yaks (Bos grunniens) have evolved diverse coat colors with important aesthetic and economic value. Previous studies indicate that these traits may also serve as important indicators of adaptation to cold, high-altitude environments. However, the genetic mechanisms underlying these traits remain unclear.ResultsHere, we systematically analyzed the genetic architecture of coat color in 511 yaks by combining red-green-blue (RGB)-based quantitative phenotyping with the QingXin 1st 30 K single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) genotyping chip. RGB components were highly correlated within body regions, but moderately correlated between the head and rump, indicating region-specific pigmentation patterns. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of head and rump coat color using five models (general linear model (GLM), mixed linear model (MLM), multiple loci mixed model (MLMM), fixed and random model circulating probability unification (FarmCPU), and Bayesian information and linkage disequilibrium (LD) iteratively nested keyway (BLINK)), together with genotype-by-environment interaction (GbyE) analyses revealed region-specific genetic regulation. Candidate genes associated with yak coat color included KIT and DISP3. KIT was detected in both GWAS and GbyE analyses, underscoring its potential key role in coat color. Heritability (h2) was higher for the head coat color (42.8%) than for the rump (18.0%). In addition, a high-LD block was detected near KIT, further supporting its role as a major candidate gene. Genomic selection (GS) using gBLUP and Bayesian generalized linear regression (BGLR) showed moderate-to-high accuracy, with higher accuracy under random than settings than aligned settings. Genomic-estimated breeding values (gEBVs) were strongly correlated between the head and rump regions, indicating consistent genetic regulation across body regions.ConclusionsOur study demonstrated that RGB-based phenotyping combined with GWAS and GbyE analyses could identify loci associated with coat color and potential GbyE interactions. GS results indicate that coat color traits are heritable and may inform precise breeding strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13160-5.

Background

Yaks (Bos grunniens) are cold-adapted domesticated bovids endemic to the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau [1]. These animals are distributed mainly across high-mountain regions at elevations of 3 000–5 000 m and are among the world’s highest-altitude domesticated species [2, 3]. They present different coat colors, which is a key phenotype than influences adaptation, ecology, social behavior, breed identification, market preference, and genetic improvement. Therefore, knowledge of coat coloration is vital for understanding population adaptations, ecological functions, and genetics.

Coat color is mainly determined by the amount and ratio of eumelanin to pheomelanin, which are regulated by key genes [4, 5]. Hair is formed by keratinized follicle cells, which provide nutrients, pigments, structural support, and growth signals [6, 7]. Consequently, hair follicles function as spatially structured regulatory units that integrate genetic, cellular, and developmental inputs to produce visible coat color phenotypes. Yak coat color varies widely and is typically black, white, or brown, with coloration often differing across body regions [8]. Among these regions, the head often displays a more pronounced color variation, which may be associated with region-specific functional or behavioral roles, such as individual recognition or social signaling [9, 10]. These variations may be driven by genetic differences and modulated by age, sex, and environmental factorts [11, 12]. Beyond appearance, coat color in yaks may contribute to thermal regulation and visual signaling, and play an important role in ultraviolet (UV) protection, making it a relevant trait for understanding yak adaptation and genetic improvement [13–16].

Yak coat color research has progressed from phenotypic observations to molecular genetics, and more recently, to whole-genome analyses. Early studies based on visual assessments were subjective, environmentally influenced, and provided limited insights into genetic mechanisms. Candidate gene approaches subsequently identified key pigmentation genes, such as MC1R, ASIP, TYRP1, KIT, PMEL, and MITF [17–21]. MC1R is linked to eumelanin synthesis, PMEL to brown coloration, and KIT to white spotting or depigmentation, with some KIT alleles in white yaks potentially introgressed during ancient yak–cattle hybridization. Recent whole-genome sequencing and selective sweep analyses have revealed structural variant selection signals underlying coat-color diversity [22]. For example, the pure white coat of the Tianzhu White Yak is associated with structural variation and translocation involving the KIT region between chromosomes 6 and 29, whereas candidate regions have been identified in black and brown populations [23]. Transcriptomic studies of skin tissues further revealed differences in melanocyte number, maturation, and gene expression among yaks with different colors, indicating that pigmentation is influenced not only by genotype but also by developmental and environmental factors.

Overall, current research has identified several key genes and variants associated with yak coat color. However, current studies on yak coat-color genes mainly rely on extreme phenotypes (e.g., pure black vs. pure white) for gene identification [24]. Therefore, it remains unclear whether these classical pigmentation genes regulate continuous color gradients and whether additional genes contribute to subtle color variation as different body regions of the same yak (e.g., head vs. rump) exhibit distinct coat colors. However, it remains unclear whether these differences result from differential expression of the same genes across regions or from independent regulation by different loci. Together, these limitations highlight the need for quantitative approaches capable of capturing continuous and region-specific variation in yak coat color. Therefore, given this gap in research, this study aims to use red, green, and blue (RGB)-based image phenotyping to quantify coat color traits in the head and rump regions of yaks and to convert traditional qualitative traits into continuous numerical measurements, thereby improving accuracy and repeatability. Three yak populations will be examined in this study, Maiwa yaks from Hongyuan (average altitude 3 600 m), Yushu yaks from Qumalai (average altitude 4 191 m), and Huanhu yaks from Gangcha (average altitude 3 309 m). These animals inhabit geographically distinct environments characterized by marked differences in altitude-related hypoxia, temperature regimes, and pasture composition. Yushu represents a high-altitude, colder, and more hypoxic environment, whereas Gangcha is relatively lower in altitude, with milder climatic conditions and more diverse alpine meadow resources, while Hongyuan shows intermediate environmental characteristics. These ecological contrasts provide a suitable framework for detecting genotype-by-environmen interactions (GbyE) [25, 26]. Therefore, the objectives of this study are to: (i) elucidate the genetic basis of continuous coat color variation in yaks, (ii) identify body-region-specific regulatory mechanisms, (iii) assess environment-dependent genetic effects, and (iv) evaluate the genomic predictability of coat color traits. This will be achieved by integrating phenotypic and genotypic data, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) using multiple models, and GbyE analyses to identify the loci associated with environment-dependent effects. Together, these efforts will potentially provide a theoretical foundation for understanding yak coat color genetics and advancing molecular breeding.

Materials and methods

Animal material

Data were collected between 2021 and 2023 from three yak populations: Maiwa yaks from the Longri Livestock Farm, Sichuan Province and the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau Base of Southwest Minzu University; Yushu yaks from the Yellow River Source Yak Breeding Farm, Qinghai Province; and Huanhu yaks from the Gonghe County Yak Cooperative, Qinghai Province. The three populations originated from geographically isolated farms across the northeastern Qinghai–Tibetan Plateau, separated by approximately 426–518 km. Phenotypic data on coat color were collected from 678 yaks (131 Maiwa, 118 Yushu and 262 Huanhu yaks), with genotypes available for 706 individuals (276 Maiwa, 277 Yushu and 153 Huanhu yaks). Phenotypic and genotypic data were matched by individuals, and animals missing either type of data were excluded. After matching with sex and age records, 511 yaks (180 males and 331 females, aged 2–9 years) were retained for subsequent association analyses (https://github.com/fu-yujiao/yak-coat-color). All populations were maintained under traditional grazing systems with natural mating and no formal breeding program or coat color-based selection was implemented. Owing to the nature of traditional pastoral systems, complete pedigree records were unavailable; however, this limitation does not affect GWAS analyses based on genomic relationship matrices. For each individual, blood and ear tissue samples were collected, photographs were taken, and sex, age, and geographic origin were recorded.

Phenotype data collection

High-resolution digital images were captured under natural light conditions using computer-assisted imaging, including both frontal and lateral views to clearly visualize the head and rump. Coat color differed among populations: Maiwa yaks were predominantly solid black, Yushu yaks were mainly black with a small proportion of white individuals, and Huanhu yaks frequently exhibited spotted patterns on the body or head (Additional file 1: Fig. S1).Images were processed using the Fiji ImageJ software (v2) [27] to extract RGB values from the head and rump. The head region was defined as the area between the eyes, with particular attention to the white spots, whereas the rump encompassed the dorsal posterior area extending to the base of the tail. Specifically, each image was opened in ImageJ, and the color picker tool was used to sample RGB values from the target region. For head measurements, three closely spaced sampling points were selected between the eyes, and the region defined by these points was used as a standardized region of interest (ROI, 5 cm2). When white patches were present, the ROI was adjusted to ensure their inclusion. For rump measurements, three sampling points were selected within the dorsal posterior region extending to the base of the tail, and the corresponding region defined by these points was used as the ROI (5 cm2). RGB values (integer range 0–255) were extracted from each pixel within each ROI. Three replicate measurements were collected per region by repeated sampling at adjacent points within each ROI [28]. The mean RGB values within each ROI were computed to characterize coat color. Each measurement was repeated three times, outliers were removed, and the mean value was used for subsequent analyses. Missing phenotypic values were coded as NA.

Genotyping and quality control

Blood, ear tissue, and hair samples were collected from the three yak populations using standard, minimally invasive, non-lethal procedures to ensure animal welfare. Yaks were restrained using conventional livestock-handling methods without anesthesia. Blood was collected via jugular venipuncture using sterile equipment, whereas ear tissue and hair samples were obtained by ear notching and hair plucking, respectively. All procedures were conducted by trained personnel under veterinary supervision and no complications were observed. No animals were euthanized, and all yaks were returned to their herds after sampling. Genomic DNA was extracted using a TIANamp Blood DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech (BEIJING) Co.,Ltd, Beijing, China) [29]. Yak genotyping was performed using the “Qingxin 1st ” targeted capture chip, developed in 2023 by Beijing Compass Agricultural Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) in collaboration with the Qinghai Academy of Animal Science and Veterinary Medicine (China) and Southwest Minzu University (China). The chip, which was based on resequencing data from 80 yaks across three breeds, included 29 223 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), of which 1 145 were functionally annotated and associated with key traits. In the present study, the chip achieved an average call rate of > 99.93% and a genotype concordance rate of 99.88% [30]. This chip represents a relatively low-density SNP platform. Quality control was performed using PLINK (v1.90), and SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.05, or missing rate > 5% were filtered out. Remaining missing genotypes were treated as missing data and handled automatically by PLINK during association analyses. SNPs with call rates > 0.99 were retained For subsequent analyses. Most SNPs exhibited heterozygosity values that were approximately 0.5, whereas R² values (squared correlation between adjacent markers) were concentrated near zero, indicating high genotype quality and minimal linkage disequilibrium (LD). Because yaks are domesticated animals subjected to long-term artificial selection, inbreeding, and non-random mating, the population is expected to deviate from the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE). Therefore, HWE-based filtering was not applied during quality control to avoid the potential removal of loci under selection. The final set of retained SNPs was distributed across 29 autosomes and two sex chromosomes (X and Y). After filtering, 29 223 SNPs were retained for GWAS analyses. The genotype data (GD and GM) and phenotypic (myY) file were used for subsequent analyses. Genotype data are available in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under BioProject accession number PRJNA1421058 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/PRJNA1421058) [30].

Population structure and heritability estimation

Principal component analysis (PCA), neighbor-joining (NJ) tree construction, and kinship heatmap visualization were performed using genome association and prediction integrated tool (GAPIT; v3.0) implemented in R (v4.3.3). These analyses supported the genetic analysis of coat color and related complex traits by providing insights into the population structure and genetic relationships among the sample population [30]. Heritability (h2) was estimated using mixed linear models implemented in GAPIT and Bayesian generalized linear regression (BGLR). GAPIT uses a single kinship matrix within a one-random-effect framework, whereas BGLR allows for the use of multiple kinship matrices. Association study

GAPIT, an R package for GWAS, integrates various statistical models for efficient and flexible genotype-phenotype analysis and was used both GWAS and GbyE analyses [31]. To comprehensively assess genotype-phenotype associations, five GWAS models implemented in GAPIT were used: the general linear model (GLM), mixed linear model (MLM), multiple loci mixed model (MLMM), fixed and random model circulating probability unification (FarmCPU), and Bayesian information and LD iteratively nested keyway (BLINK). GLM is computationally efficient but does not account for population structure or relatedness, which may increase false- positive associations [32]. MLM controls both population structure and kinship by including fixed and random effects, thereby reducing confounding bias [33]. MLMM extends MLM by iteratively adding significant SNPs as covariates (pseudo-QTNs), thereby improving the detection power for polygenic traits [34]. FarmCPU further separates fixed and random effects through iterative updating of pseudo-QTNs, thereby improving statistical power and reducing confounding effects between markers and kinship [35]. BLINK enhances computational efficiency and detection power by using an LD-based iterative fixed-effect framework combined with Bayesian information criteria, reducing false positives and improving true signal detection [36, 37]. An empirical significance threshold was estimated using phenotype permutations to control false positives. Phenotypic values and taxa labels were randomly shuffled among individuals to disrupt true genotype-phenotype associations while preserving the original phenotype distribution. Each permuted phenotype dataset was used to detect associated signals with the same GWAS model that was used in the original analysis. This procedure was repeated 50 times, and the genome-wide minimum P-value was recorded for each repeat. The mean of these values (4.58 × 10− 5) was obtained as the empirical significance threshold. A more stringent cutoff of 1 × 10− 5 was applied for downstream significance testing. The top three principal components (PCs), age and sex were included as covariates to control for population structure.

Multi-locus models enhance detection power and explanatory ability by iteratively incorporating multiple significant markers ( pseudoQTN) as covariates, thereby accounting for both LD and polygenic effects. GbyE interaction model

To detect potential GbyE interactions associated with yak coat color traits, a multi-environment genome-wide association study (ME-GWAS) was conducted using the GbyE software. The head and rump regions were defined as the two environments, and the mean RGB value of each region was used as the phenotype for GbyE analysis. Genotype (SNP matrix), phenotype files, covariates (sex, age, and population structure PCs), and marker information files were used as inputs. Idividual orders were kept consistent across files, and missing values were coded as NaN. In the GbyE genotype-coding framework, the mateix was generated using the Kronecker product of the original additive genotype matrix (G)⊗ and environmental design matrix (E). Specifically, each element of G was multiplied by the entire matrix E, resulting in a block-structured matrix encoding all genotype–environment combinations. The Kronecker product (⊗) is defined for any two matrices, regardless of whether they have the same dimensions. For a matrix G of size n × m and a matrix E of size p × q, the Kronecker product G ⊗ E produces a matrix of size (np) × (mq ) [38]. The G matrix contained genotype of 0, 1, and 2 representing two homozygote genotypes and one heterozygote genotype. The diagonal elements of E were set to 1 to represent each environment. To avoid the linear independence issues and account for the additive genotype matrix, the first column of the E matrix was set to one. LD block

LD analysis of surrounding significant SNPs was performed using LDBlockShow (v1.40) [41] on a Linux system. Genotype data from HapMap format files were converted to VCF format using TASSEL 5. Markers within 1 Mb of significant SNP were extracted, and pairwise LD coefficients (D′ and r²) were calculated. LD matrices were generated and visualized as heatmaps to illustrate linkage strength and haplotype block structure. The results were used to define haplotype block boundaries within candidate regions for subsequent gene identification.

Genome annotation

Significant loci identified through GWAS and GbyE analyses were annotated to identify associated functional genes. For this purpose, the BosGru_v3.0 genome annotation file (GTF format) was downloaded from Ensembl (http://ftp.ensembl.org/pub/release-99/fasta/bos_grunniens/). The GTF file provided the genomic coordinates of genes and transcripts used for gene annotation and candidate gene localization. For GWAS loci, candidate genes were defined as those located within or intersecting a 1 kb region upstream or downstream of each significant SNP, corresponding to the average LD span observed in the additive genomic background. For GbyE loci, candidate genes were defined based on local LD surrounding for each significant SNP. On chromosomes 1, 6, and 20, the LD block sizes were 275.16 kb, 190.14 kb, and 195.26 kb, respectively, with high-LD regions primarily confined within these intervals; therefore, annotation windows were set as ± 138 kb, ± 95 kb, and ± 98 kb to capture the corresponding LD regions. In contrast, for the locus on chromosome 14, in which the LD block spanned 313.95 kb, a window of ± 157 kb was used to capture the full extent of the associated region. These candidate genes functionally annotated in domestic yaks, providing a valuable basis for investigating the regulatory mechanisms underlying coat color variation.

Genome selection with different kinship

Conventional breeding for complex traits such as coat color often suffers from low accuracy and long generation intervals. Genomic selection (GS) overcomes these limitations by fully utilizing genomic information to improve prediction accuracy and accelerate breeding [42]. To comprehensively account for additive and interactive effects, three kinship matrices (KA, KI, and KAI) were constructed based on the entire GbyE genotype matrix and used as random effects for genomic prediction [39, 43, 44].

In the GbyE genotype-coding rule, G was expanded using the Kronecker product with the E, yielding additive and interactive genotype components. Based on these two genotype matrices, separate genomic relationship matrices were constructed using the VanRaden method implemented in GAPIT. The additive matrix captured the baseline genomic relatedness, whereas the interaction matrix represented the variation attributable to GbyE responses. KA was constructed using the additive genotype matrix, KI was was constructed using the interactive genotype matrix, and KAI was constructed using a combination of additive and interactive genotypes. In GS analysis, the gBLUP model (using both GAPIT and RKHS) were implemented in the BGLR package. Two sampling methods were used: random sampling, in which training and test sets were randomly partitioned for prediction of head and rump phenotypic traits, and paired sampling in which individuals were grouped to align the training and testing sets by simultaneously sampling head phenotypes and corresponding rump phenotypes from the same individuals. GAPIT–efficient mixed model association (EMMA) employs a linear mixed model (LMM) to estimate variance components and calculate individual genetic values. In contrast, BGLR applies Bayesian methods to sample the posterior distributions of marker effects, which are then combined to calculate genomic estimated breeding values (gEBVs) for each individual. The results were expressed as the correlation between the predicted and observed phenotypic values. The predictive accuracy of different kinship matrix models and methods (BGLR–RKHS and GAPIT–EMMA) was evaluated under two validation types to verify the reliability of GWAS results. GAPIT-predicted gEBVs were used to analyze the correlations between head and rump traits and the distribution of BVs across different yak breeds.

Results

Phenotypic extraction and distribution

To explore the relationships between coat color phenotypes across different body regions, RGB values from the head (R_H, G_H, and B_H) and rump (R_R, G_R, and B_R) of the yaks were subjected to correlation analysis.

The heatmap showed strong positive correlations among the RGB components within the same region (r = 0.98–1.00), indicating stable and coordinated color composition patterns. In contrast, lower correlations were observed between the head and rump regions (r = 0.51–0.59), suggesting that pigmentation differed in intensity and hue distribution across body regions (Fig. 1). Phenotypic extraction of the head and rump coat colors was conducted. Scatter plots, boxplots, frequency histograms, density distribution plots, and cumulative distribution plots were generated based on individual observations (Additional file 1: Fig. S2 and S3). The RGB values, ranging from 0 to 255, represent the light intensity of each channel and directly correspond to yak coat color. This variation likely reflects local regulation of melanogenesis regulation or pigment-related gene expression, which is consistent with high-altitude adaptation and regional coat color specificity in yaks.

Significant signals detected from additive and interactive models

GWAS of head coat color consistently identified a significant SNP (S6_52175265) on chromosome 6 across all five models. The following P-values were identified across the models (GLM (1.28 × 10− 29), MLM (2.43 × 10− 25), and MLMM (1.15 × 10− 41)), with the lowest P-value observed in the FarmCPU model (P = 1.15 × 10⁻40). However, the SNP remained highly significant in the more stringent BLINK model (P = 2.24 × 10⁻²⁷), with a low (MAF = 0.045), a pronounced negative effect (− 93.18), and an explained phenotypic variance of 82.97%, highlighting the robustness and biological relevance of this locus in head pigmentation (Fig. 2). GWAS of the rump region also detected significant signals, although they were less pronounced than those observed from the head (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). Compared with GLM, MLM, MLMM, and FarmCPU, the BLINK model employs multistep iterative regression combined with a fixed-random effects framework and stringent multiple-testing correction, thereby providing more reliable and significant SNP detection. Therefore, SNPs identified by BLINK were selected as candidate loci for further analysis using the GbyE model. Because coat color phenotypes are influenced not only by genetic factors but also by regional or environmental regulation, head and rump coat color phenotypes were treated as two distinct traits in the GbyE analysis. In the additive effects model, three significant SNPs were detected on chromosomes 1, 6, and 20 (S1_134093853, S6_52175265, and S20_28960131, respectively). A significant locus (S14_39078499) associated with interaction effects was detected on chromosome 14 (Fig. 3).

LD blocks and SNP effects

LD block analysis was performed for the four significant loci identified in the GbyE analysis. Within 1 Mb upstream and downstream of each locus, SNPs exhibiting strong LD were assessed for gene annotation to improve candidate gene reliability and reduce false-positives associations. For head coat color, multiple significant SNPs clustered within a high-LD block of near 52,175,265 bp on chromosome 6 (Fig. 4A). The scatter plot showed a clear aggregation SNPs based on –log10(P) and D′ values, and the red regions in the heatmap indicated strong LD among SNPs. For rump coat color, a similar high LD structure was observed near 39,078,499 bp on chromosome 14 (Fig. 4B), as well as on chromosomes 1 and 20 (Additional file 1: Fig. S6 and S7). Following LD analysis, we further evaluated the association between significant SNPs and phenotypes. For SNP S6_52175265 on chromosome 6, head RGB values differed significantly among genotypes in the additive genotype matrix, with GG and AA genotype showing the lowest and highest values, respectively. For SNP S14_39078499 on chromosome 14, RGB distributions differed significantly between the head and rump in the interactive genotype matrix, with head phenotypes concentrated in the GG genotype and rump phenotypes more broadly distributed (Fig. 4C and D). Furthermore, gEBVs calculated using GAPIT were used to assess correlations between head and rump traits. Scatterplot analysis of BVs for the head (BV.head) and rump (BV.rump) revealed strong positive correlations within each yak breed, indicating consistent genetic regulation of coat color across body regions. Strong positive correlations were observed within each breed: Maiwa (r = 0.82***), Yushu(r = 0.90***), and Huanhu (r = 0.90***), indicating high genetic consistency in coat color formation across body regions (Fig. 5).

Heritability estimats of coat color

The h2 estimates based on different genetic effect models indicated that yak coat color was influenced by both additive and non-additive genetic components (Additional file 1: Fig. S5). When only the additive effect (KA) was considered, h2 was 42.8% (genetic variance = 881.9, residual variance = 1 180). It increased slightly to 45.1% (genetic variance = 867.6, residual variance = 1 057.5) when the interactive effect (KI) was included and further increased to 48.5% (genetic variance = 1 095.7, residual variance = 1 165.4) in the combined model (KAI). These results suggest that coat color formation is not solely determined by the additive effects of individual genes but is also modulated by epistatic interactions. The h2 estimates varied across body regions and models, reflecting differences in genetic contributions. The additive h2 of head coat color was the highest (42.8%), indicating a strong genetic influence, whereas that of the rump was much lower (18%: genetic variance = 180.6, residual variance = 820.4), suggesting greater environmental or interactive effect. The jackknife analysis provided the mean h2, SD, and SE estimates for each genetic effect model (Additional file 1: Table S1). The combined additive and interactive model (KAI) further confirmed the contribution of interaction effects, whereas the model incorporating significant SNP effects (KA + KI) showed an h2 of 45.1%, indicating that genetic factors remained the primary determinant of coat color variations in yaks (Fig. 6).

Associated functional genes

Gene annotation was performed for significant loci identified through from GWAS and GbyE analyses to explore their potential functions. For GWAS, loci within ± 1 kb were annotated to KIT (chr6) NMU (chr6), PDGFRA (chr6), CHIC2 (chr6), and ADAMTS17 (chr17). For GbyE analysis, annotation windows were defined according to local LD structure. The loci on chromosomes 1, 6, and 20 were annotated using ± 138 kb, ± 95 kb, and ± 98 kb windows, respectively. In contrast, the locus on chromosome 14 exhibited an extended LD block spanning 313.95 kb and was therefore annotated using a ± 157 kb window upstream and downstream window of the loci. Based on these LD patterns, candidate genes were identified within the corresponding genomic intervals, including two unknown genes on chromosomes 1 and 20 (chr1 and chr20), KIT (chr6), and DISP3 (chr14) (Table 1). Previous studies have indicated that KIT is involved in melanocyte development and pigmentation, DISP3 regulates Hedgehog signaling, affecting hair follicles and color, NMU is involved in neuropeptide signaling and may influence melanocyte activity, PDGFRA regulates cell proliferation and development, and ADAMTS17 participates in extracellular matrix remodeling and tissue development. KIT was identified in both GWAS and GbyE analyses, indicating its potential key regulatory role in coat color traits.

GS prediction for yak coat color traits was evaluated using three kinship matrices derived from the GbyE model. Discussion

This study successfully delineated the genetic architecture of yak coat color by integrating high-resolution RGB phenotyping with multi-model GWAS and GbyE analyses. By quantifying color as a continuous traits, we enabled a more refined dissection of pigmentation patterns. This methodological framework provides a powerful approach for unraveling complex traits in livestock. The diversity of coat color patterns in yaks reflects polygenic control and complex regulatory interactions influenced by both selection history and environmental adaptation. The distinct phenotypic variation among the three yak breeds provides valuable contrasts for experimental design and helps elucidate breed-specific differences in coat color.

Coat color is also considered an adaptive trait. In Tibetan Plateau cattle, a light grey coat may be linked to UV exposure. A 1–2 Mb heterozygous inversion and two translocations on chromosome 6 may affect grey pigmentation by regulating KIT, thereby suggesting a role in evolutionary adaptation [47]. In the present study, strong phenotypic correlations within body regions, together with lower correlations between the head and rump, indicated region-specific pigmentation regulation. The GWAS consistently identified significant signals on chromosome 6, particularly near KIT. The repeated detection of KIT across multiple models underscores its pivotal role in determining yak coat pigmentation. Interestingly, KIT was also identified under in the GbyE model, highlighting its sensitivity to environmental and anatomical variations. The identification of DISP3 on chromosome 14 extends our understanding of pigmentation control beyond canonical pathways. Given its role in Hedgehog signaling, DISP3 may contribute to regional pigment patterning [48, 49]. These results indicate the identified genes from a multilayered regulatory network in which both genetic and environmental factors modulate coat color intensity and hue. GWAS can identify core coat color genes, whereas GbyE analysis can reveals environment-dependent interactions, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of complex trait genetics.

KIT was significantly associated with coat color variation in yaks. Consistent with its known role in pigmentation [50], KIT likely regulates melanocyte migration and melanin synthesis via the MAPK-MITF pathway [51, 52]. While KIT and MC1R influence coat color in other ruminants, our findings highlight a yak-specific genetic architecture potentially shaped by high-altitude adaptation. Chromosomal rearrangements near KIT in Tibetan cattle further suggest that structural variants may contribute to coat color diversity under strong UV exposure. Tibetan cattle belong to the Qinghai–Tibetan Plateau yellow cattle lineage, as described in previous genomic studies [47]. These results confirm the conserved pigmentation mechanisms in Bovidae and reveal yak-specific adaptations to plateau environments.

LD analyses revealed strong genomic clustering near significant loci, particularly around KIT and DISP3 on chromosomes 6 and 14, respectively, thereby enhancing confidence in these candidate genes. The presence of dense LD blocks around these loci suggests the existence of haplotype structures or potential regulatory elements that affect pigmentation-related gene expression. When yak coat color was represented using RGB values, individuals with lighter coat color had values closer to 255, whereas darker individuals had values closer to 0. Examination of the distribution of RGB values by genotype (GG, AG, and AA), indicated that SNP (S6_52175265) had an additive effect, individuals carrying the GG genotype tended to have lighter head coat colors, whereas most individuals carried the AA genotype. In contrast, SNP (S14_39078499) exhibited a significant genotype-by-location interaction. When comparing the head and rump RGB values, most data points clusted between 0 and 100, with a few exceeding 200, indicating that the effect of this SNP on coat color varied between body regions. These results highlight both the direct influence of genotype on coat color and the role of GbyE in shaping phenotypic variation in yaks.

Estimates of h2 showed significant differences in the contributions of genetic and residual variance to coat color traits across models. The KAI model, which considers both additive effects and gene-environment interaction effects, more closely reflects the underlying genetic mechanisms of yak coat color than the purely additive model (KA) [53]. In the Head-KA model, additive effects explained 42.8% of the variance (residual variance of 57.2%), while in the Rump-KA model, additive effects accounted for only 18% (residual variance of 82%), indicating lower h2 for rump coat color. The KAI model, incorporating both additive and GbyE effects, explained 48.5% of the variance (residual variance of 51.5%) and captured the genetic architecture more comprehensively. Similarly, the KA+KI model showed a 45.1% additive variance (residual variance of 54.9%), highlighting the contribution of interaction effects. These results highlight the need to include interaction terms in genetic models, because ignoring GbyE effects can underestimate heritable variance in pigmentation traits. Overall, models incorporating GbyE and genetic interactions provided more accurate estimates of coat color h2, supporting GS and marker-assisted breeding.

Combining GWAS and GS provides an additional approach for validating associated markers and improving prediction accuracy [54]. The prediction accuracies of BGLR and GAPIT in GS validation were slightly different under both random and aligned validation types, with random sampling generally having a higher prediction accuracy than aligned sampling. BGLR–RKHS was able to capture both additive and non-additive effects, including GbyE interactions and potential nonlinear relationships, whereas GAPIT–EMMA, which relies on a linear mixed model, primarily captured linear additive effects [55]. This contrast underscores the greater flexibility of BGLR–RKHS for modeling complex genetic architectures and suggests that models incorporating non-additive and interaction effects may improve the accuracy of genomic prediction for complex traits, such as coat color. The GS analyses demonstrated the practical value of these findings in breeding. Both the RKHS and gBLUP models achieved moderate to high prediction accuracies, with the random setting consistently outperforming the aligned model. Models incorporating both additive and interactive matrices (KAI and KA+KI) yielded higher accuracy, confirming that GbyE integration improved the genomic prediction of complex traits. BV analysis was conducted to identify individuals with high BV in both the head and rump regions. BV analysis showed distinct head and rump coat color distributions among the Maiwa, Yushu, and Huanhu breeds, with higher correlations in Yushu and Huanhu (r = 0.90) than in Maiwa (r = 0.82), suggesting that these traits could be used as combined selection indices. The discrepancy between the RGB-based phenotypic and gEBVs correlations in the head and rump regions reflects their distinct biological underpinnings. RGB values are image-derived phenotypes that are susceptible to local pigmentation, illumination conditions, and camera angles, which introduce region-specific noise that attenuates cross-regional correlations. In contrast, gEBVs estimate additive genetic effects from genome-wide markers, and because these effects are shared across body regions, gEBVs exhibit stronger correlations that reflect the underlying pleiotropic architecture rather than local environmental variation [56–58]. High-BV individuals may carry candidate coat color genes and serve as potential parents for hybridization or breed improvement, facilitating more efficient genetic enhancement and future validation.

This study demonstrated that integrating RGB phenotyping with GWAS and GbyE models can effectively dissect the genetic basis of complex traits in livestock. Despite these advances, this study had several limitations. The current sample size and environmental descriptors may have restricted the detection of low-effect loci. Moreover, functional validation of the candidate genes is required to confirm their mechanistic roles. Future studies should integrate transcriptomic and epigenomic data to further elucidate the regulatory networks governing regional pigmentation. Expanding phenotypic diversity and incorporating structural variant analysis will further enhance our understanding of coat color evolution and facilitate precise breeding in yaks.

Conclusions

This study combined RGB-based phenotyping with GWAS to reveal the genetic basis of yak coat color. Coat color was coordinated within regions but varied between body regions. Multi-model GWAS and GbyE analyses identified several candidate loci, with KIT being consistently associated with coat color variation, whereas DISP3 may contribute through signaling and developmental regulation. Both additive and interactive effects contributed to h2, and the genomic prediction achieved moderate accuracy. These findings improve our understanding of coat color genetics and provide a foundation for yak breeding and high-altitude adaptation studies. Future research should include broader populations and more detailed phenotyping, particularly for white or mottled coat types, to further elucidate the genetic architecture of coat color.
