Using Complex Interventions to Develop Programme Theory for a Family‐Focused Model of Telepalliative Care
Authors: Eithne Hayes Bauer, Anders Nikolai Ørsted Schultz, Anthony C. Smith, Frans Brandt, Georg Bollig, Karin Brochstedt Dieperink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPatients and families find telepalliative care to be a valuable and convenient supplement to conventional palliative care services. However, challenges persist in sustaining meaningful telepalliative care beyond the initial implementation. Developing structured models that are culturally and contextually aligned may be the key to sustainability.AimTo develop programme theory for a family‐focused model of telepalliative care for adult patients and their families.MethodsGuided by the British Medical Research Council's framework for developing and evaluating complex interventions, data were collected through workshops with key stakeholders, participant observations and ethnographic interviews. Reflexive thematic and secondary data analyses were performed to develop programme theory and identify key uncertainties.ResultsSix workshops were held at two Danish locations with 49 participants. Three themes were generated. (1) Virtual care in palliative settings: Adapting to new norms and evolving practices in telepalliative care. This theme explores how professionals, patients and families adapted to new norms in virtual interactions without structured guidance. (2) Balancing trust and technology: The role of face‐to‐face consultations in telepalliative care. This theme examines the balance between face‐to‐face consultations and telepalliative care. (3) Governance and equity in telepalliative care: Ensuring access, sustainability and quality. This theme explores factors affecting the implementation, sustainability and equitable access to telepalliative care services.ConclusionThe programme theory highlighted the link between clinicians' level of digital competency and benefits to patients and families in telepalliative care, emphasising the need to enhance clinicians' skills. Further exploration is needed to identify the specific digital skills required for delivering effective telepalliative care. Key considerations include the importance of face‐to‐face first visits to establish relationships and the concern that technology may overshadow palliative care.

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines palliative care as ‘an approach that improves the quality of life for patients and their families who are facing problems associated with life‐threatening illnesses by preventing and relieving suffering through early identification, correct assessment and treatment of pain and other problems, whether physical, psychosocial or spiritual’ [1]. Enormous challenges exist in access to palliative care, as currently only 14% of those in need receive palliative care, and the demand for palliative care is expected to increase due to the rise in ageing populations and the burden associated with non‐communicable diseases [2]. Alternative methods of delivering palliative care are necessary to face these challenges. Over the past few decades, telepalliative care, the ability to provide palliative care virtually [3], has emerged as one such alternative, spurred by the aforementioned challenges and advancement in widespread digitalisation [1]. Telepalliative care consists of synchronous (video, telephone) or asynchronous (email, messaging) methods [4]. In this study, our focus is on telepalliative care provided by specialised palliative care teams (SPCTs) to adult palliative care patients at home and their families via video consultations. Patients and families have reported that telepalliative care is a valuable and convenient supplement to conventional services [5]. It enhances family engagement and access to care while minimising burdens associated with travel and waiting times [6, 7]. Moreover, access to telepalliative care may increase the likelihood of patients dying in their preferred place of care, often at home [8]. The rapid shift towards telehealth during the COVID‐19 pandemic highlighted the challenges of sustaining meaningful telepalliative care practices that resonate with both providers and recipients [9]. Challenges include a lack of integration with existing services, insufficient tailoring to meet patient and family needs, perceived difficulties in rapport‐building and inadequacies in technology and workflows [4, 10]. These barriers, identified as critical factors, hinder the use and effectiveness of telepalliative care [6]. There is an increasing awareness that the transition from traditional face‐to‐face consultations to telepalliative care necessitates re‐evaluating models of care to ensure they are adequately adapted to the unique requirements of patients and families [11, 12]. In light of the rising demand for palliative care services and persistent access challenges, developing models to enhance telepalliative care has become an urgent priority. Telepalliative care interventions that proved feasible and acceptable in the literature [13] appear to have been developed as structured models. For example, Project ENABLE (educate, nurture, advise before life ends) from the United States, which aims to improve quality of life by remotely teaching basic skills and symptom management to patients and family caregivers [14], and other models that emerged in response to the COVID‐19 pandemic, illustrate the potential for tailored and integrated telepalliative care solutions to improve patient and family satisfaction in palliative care [15, 16]. A model from Taiwan developed by Wu et al. [17], during the pandemic, for conducting family video conferences in palliative care that integrates Shared Decision Making and the VALUE approach (value, acknowledge, listen, understand and elicit) is another example of a structured model for delivering telepalliative care. TapCloud, a model from the United States, combines synchronous and asynchronous communication methods to facilitate home‐based palliative care [15, 18]. These models are culturally and contextually adapted to their settings, in line with recommendations for developing complex interventions [19]. Although this may reduce their transferability to other settings, it underscores the need for applying specific frameworks to the development of telepalliative care interventions to enhance their effectiveness and acceptance [5]. One such framework is the British Medical Research Council's (MRC) guidance for the development and evaluation of complex interventions [20]. The MRC aims to improve the design and conduct of research to increase the utility, efficiency and impact of an intervention, with an emphasis on stakeholder involvement to increase contextual alignment and ease implementation [19]. In the Nordic countries, stakeholder‐driven research in healthcare is widespread and well described in telemedicine research [21].

Several studies have addressed the topic of telepalliative care for adult patients and families in Scandinavia [22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29]. Apart from Sigaard et al. [26], who are developing a digital platform for communication between providers and recipients across sectors, evidence is lacking on contextually adapted and theoretically informed telepalliative care interventions, indicating a knowledge gap in models for telepalliative care within the Scandinavian context.

An important aspect of telepalliative care is fostering a family‐focused approach, recognising that families play an important role in delivering informal care and are also, themselves, beneficiaries of palliative care [30]. Our understanding of family aligns with that of Wright and Leahy, who define family as those whom patients consider family [31]. Thus, families are not restricted to relatives only, but can also consist of good friends or trusted neighbours [31]. We are currently developing a contextually aligned, family‐focused model for telepalliative care for patients and families, guided by the MRC framework for developing and evaluating complex interventions [19]. This paper addresses a step in the development phase of the overall model, namely the development of a knowledge base referred to as programme theory to inform the intervention through early and ongoing stakeholder involvement. Programme theory describes how and why an intervention may be effective, key components, mechanisms, contextual features, uncertainties and how these may interact [19].

Aim

The aim of this study is to develop programme theory that will contribute to a family‐focused model of telepalliative care for adult patients and their families. To achieve this, we explored various aspects of telepalliative care from multiple stakeholder perspectives, identifying key uncertainties in the process.

Design and Methods

The epistemological foundation of this study is interpretive description (ID), a non‐categorical qualitative research approach that allows flexibility in data collection and analysis methods tailored to the research aims [32]. ID, rooted in constructivism, is particularly suited for clinical research, as it emphasises subjective and experiential knowledge, essential for understanding the complexities of telepalliative care [32].

The MRC framework for the development and evaluation of complex interventions (Figure 1) guides our research methodology and consists of several phases: development or identification of the intervention, feasibility, evaluation and implementation [19]. Core elements at each phase include context, development, refinement/testing of programme theory, stakeholder engagement, identification of key uncertainties, intervention refinement and economic considerations [19]. The MRC guidance recommends identifying and involving key stakeholders in the research process to enhance the relevance and quality of outcomes and is suitable for interventions characterised by multiple components requiring coordination across sectors, a hallmark of telepalliative care [19]. This study reports on the intervention identification/development phase, marked with dotted lines in Figure 1, where we developed and refined programme theory through stakeholder engagement and identification of key uncertainties.

Context and Identification of Key Stakeholders

The context for this study is the provision of specialised palliative care to adult patients and families in their homes, at two sites in Denmark. At Site 1, the SPCT is based at a regional hospital in South Jutland, covering a catchment area of 8785 km2 [33] and a population of 222,529 [34]. At Site 2, the SPCT is based at a large university hospital in an urban area and a local regional hospital on the island of Fynen, covering a catchment area of 3418 km2 [33] and a population of 507,511 [34]. Data collection occurred from October 2023 to November 2024. The complexity of telepalliative care interventions necessitates an understanding of the stakeholders involved, as these interventions are multifaceted and require collaboration among various parties [19].

Key stakeholders were identified through the literature [5, 35, 36] and the research team's clinical practice knowledge and collaboration with SPCT members. Participants were purposively selected to ensure inclusion of those with relevant knowledge and experience. These included patients with palliative care needs, family caregivers, SPCT members, allied healthcare personnel, community care nurses, IT personnel and healthcare managers from both community and tertiary care settings.

Multiple recruitment strategies were employed, utilising gatekeepers, such as nurses in specialised palliative care and oncology departments, alongside social media outreach and engagement with patient representative panels. In line with the MRC framework and recommendations for good practice in the Methods of Researching End of Life Care (MORECare) Statement [19, 37], the inclusion criteria were broad to capture a wide range of insights from those involved in palliative care. Steps were taken to facilitate the participation of vulnerable persons, such as wheelchair accessibility, parking facilities and workshop duration. Participants were required to be aged 18 years or older and deemed cognitively intact by gatekeepers.

Data Collection

Scientific workshops were the primary method of data collection [38], supplemented by participant observations and ethnographic interviews [39]. Workshops facilitate collaborative discussions that can generate innovative ideas and solutions, making them an effective tool for stakeholder engagement in complex interventions [19]. We applied the method of future workshops to the first four workshops, consisting of critique, fantasy and realist phases, which arise from a social constructivist and contextual paradigm and are, therefore, in line with ID and the MRC framework [40]. In the critique phase, the problem is presented and critiqued openly without attempts at problem‐solving [40]. In the fantasy phase, the problem is addressed from the idea of an ideal world where participants reveal hopes, dreams and ideas [40]. Finally, in the realist phase, the ideas from the fantasy phase are considered in relation to how they would be implementable in the real world [40]. Activities entailed group work in three phases and feedback and discussions in plenum. The workshop agenda for the first four workshops is available in Appendix S1. Activities in workshops 5–6 included group work to draft processual diagrams of telepalliative care consultations, presentation of group work and discussions in plenum.

Participant observations and ethnographic interviews capture social contexts and help illuminate collected data [41] and were, therefore, employed to provide further exploration of context and explore initial themes from the workshops. The combination of these methods allows for a rich, nuanced understanding of stakeholders' experiences and perspectives, essential for developing a robust programme theory for telepalliative care. In line with ID research, triangulation of data is suggested to strengthen interpretive claims [32], and MRC guidance emphasises understanding the context for which the intervention is intended [19]. Gatekeepers, who were clinicians from both sites, provided access to potential participants for workshops and participant observations [42]. Inclusion criteria were adult patients with palliative care needs and willing family members/friends/neighbours over 18 years identified by the patient as family, and members of SPCTs, and CCNs working with patients and families with palliative care needs. Other key stakeholders were recruited directly by the PI, as they were identified as key stakeholders in telepalliative care interventions by gatekeepers and through previous studies [5, 13]. Included clinicians contacted the principal investigator (PI) when telepalliative consultations were scheduled and consent to observe the consultation was provided by patient and family participants. Malterud et al.'s [43] concept of information power guided sample size during data collection. The PI and the primary research assistant (RA1) facilitated all workshops. The PI conducted the participant observations. Data collection was audio‐recorded and supplemented by field notes.

Data Analysis

The data generated from the six workshops and participant observations form the basis for the development of the programme theory. The analysis was guided by the principles of reflexive thematic analysis, a method that allows for the identification and interpretation of patterns within qualitative data [44], and is particularly useful in exploring the complexities of telepalliative care. It allows researchers to engage deeply with the data and reflect on their positionality. According to Braun and Clarke, the researchers are not neutral during data collection and analysis but subjectively apply their own values, knowledge of the topic and ontological perspective to make active, informed decisions regarding methods and theory [44]. Thus, researcher bias is inherent and not something one strives to avoid [44]. The PI, a nurse and PhD student, works with the implementation of telehealth in palliative care. Previous research and work in developing family‐focused telepalliative care provided insights into patients and families' perspectives on telepalliative care, and healthcare professionals' (HCPs) willingness to adopt this approach [5, 13, 45].

The data set consisted of transcripts and handwritten data from workshop activities, and field notes and transcripts from participant observations. While workshops 1–4 focussed on exploring participants' experiences and expectations regarding telepalliative care through future workshops, workshops 5–6 aimed at developing, refining emerging ideas and presenting and discussing preliminary findings.

The PI transcribed all audio recordings verbatim and anonymised data. Notes from activities and observations were collated, and all data were imported into NVivo 14 for data management. NVivo facilitated data familiarisation and coding, allowing systematic organisation of the data [46]. The PI and RA1 performed semantic analysis collaboratively between the second and third workshops at both sites to identify recurring patterns and inform data collection. Codes from the semantic analysis were provided at workshops to spur participants' creativity and imagination. The PI performed an RTA across the data set after data collection was completed. Following familiarisation, data were exported to Microsoft Word to generate initial themes, which were critically reviewed by the second author and refined by the PI. Final refinement was performed within the whole research group. A secondary data analysis of the analysed data identified key uncertainties following the initial RTA. Ethical Considerations

Potential participants received written and oral information about the project. Written consent was obtained, and participants were informed about the possibility of retracting their consent at any time [47]. Data were anonymised and stored securely on an encrypted server. An inquiry for permission to perform this study was submitted to the Ethical Research Board (journal number: S‐20232000‐123) but deemed unnecessary. The processing of personal data for this study is approved by the Region of Southern Denmark and listed in the internal record (23/24872) cf. Art. 20 of The European Union General Data Protection Regulation. Results are reported in accordance with the reporting guidelines for qualitative research by Braun and Clarke [48].

Results

Description of Participants

In total, forty‐nine individuals participated in six workshops. Twenty‐four professionals, including hospital clinicians, SPCT members, community care nurses (CNNs) and information technology (IT) consultants, were approached and included. Thirty‐three patients, family caregivers and patient and public representatives (PPR) were approached, and 25 were included. Two PPR and six patients and/or family members declined participation. Reasons included lack of time, interest, resources, energy and illness progression.

Five participants (one participant engaged twice) engaged in six participant observations.

The median age of patients was 68 years (range 53–80), that of family caregivers was 69 years (range 46–78), and of professionals was 50 years (range 31–75). Educational levels among patients, family caregivers and PPR ranged from lower secondary to master's level. Most family member participants were spouses, two were adult children and all participated in family caregiving. All patient participants had cancer diagnoses, reflecting the high proportion of patients with cancer referred to specialised palliative care services in Denmark, which is reported to be 90% in comparison to 10% for patients with non‐cancer diagnoses [49].

Themes

Three themes representing the programme theory are outlined below, supported by observations and citations in Table 2. The first theme is virtual care in palliative settings; adapting to new norms and evolving practices in telepalliative care. It addresses how professionals, patients and families were compelled to adapt to new norms in the virtual room without structured guidance. The second theme is balancing trust and technology: the role of face‐to‐face consultations in telepalliative care. It explores how providers and recipients of palliative care attempt to strike a balance between face‐to‐face consultations and telepalliative care. The third theme with sub‐themes is governance and equity in telepalliative care; ensuring access, sustainability and quality. It examines components that affect the implementation, sustainability and equitable access to telepalliative care services. Theme 3 is supported by infographics (Figures 3 and 4) illustrating the cyclic interactions between governance and equity components in telepalliative care. Virtual Care in Palliative Settings: Adapting to New Norms and Evolving Practices in Telepalliative Care

Several participants, including patients, family members and clinicians, described how the virtual setting provides flexibility and support, enhancing autonomy and quality of life for patients and family caregivers living with a terminal illness. Patients joined consultations from their bedrooms or living rooms, depending on how they were feeling, and SPCTs observed their surroundings to gather additional insights about their well‐being. Observation 1

As SPCTs gained experience with video consultations, they adapted to the limitations of the virtual setting by developing methods to compensate for the lack of physical examinations and proximity. These new behaviours included asking patients to perform tasks like rising from a chair and sitting again, guiding self‐examinations, adjusting camera views for visual inspection and ensuring proper lighting to assess skin colour. SPCTs also made frequent use of supplementary questions, articulated observations, paused for reflection, repeated statements and communicated non‐verbally by nodding, demonstrating actions manually or leaning toward the camera to show empathy and support. Observation 2

Over time, SPCTs became more confident in the virtual setting and discovered new ways to provide emotional support, but they also recognised their limitations and the need for further training in virtual communication. Being able to observe the emotional pain and suffering of family members without the intrusion of a physical visit was a novel professional experience. This ability allowed SPCTs to promote emotional health and well‐being for family members and led to a shift in professional practice, improving palliative care for families of patients dying at home. Citation 1

However, not all participants were comfortable with video consultations. Some patients and families reported feeling frustrated with HCPs who were impatient or irritated when technical issues arose, such as difficulties connecting to the video call. They emphasised the importance of HCPs being patient, particularly with older patients who were less familiar with digital devices. Similarly, some HCPs expressed uncertainty when assisting patients and families with technical issues, which they themselves were unsure of how to handle. Citation 2 & 3

The primary challenge was not learning new behaviours specific to the virtual setting, but rather developing digital competency to balance providing palliative care, offering technical support and troubleshooting within the constraints of time and resources.

Balancing Trust and Technology: The Role of Face‐to‐Face Consultations in Telepalliative Care

A tension exists between the flexibility and family involvement offered by video consultations and the perceived need for face‐to‐face consultations in palliative care, which assumes that face‐to‐face meetings are superior.

Video consultations, aside from saving time, travel, and energy, were valued by patients and families for enabling meaningful family participation during serious discussions. Patients recognised the importance of family involvement for support, asking questions, and receiving information directly from specialists. Citation 4

Some participants felt face‐to‐face consultations were necessary for first visits or delivering bad news. Others found video consultations superfluous, particularly if they lived close to a hospital or attended for treatment. For decisions regarding illness progression or starting new treatment, many preferred face‐to‐face consultations, with some indicating that bad news should only be delivered face‐to‐face. One patient noted that she needed to engage all her senses to process treatment advice, which she felt was impossible via video. In contrast, others were comfortable discussing serious matters virtually. Citation 5

Among clinicians, opinions varied on what could be achieved through video; some emphasised the value of visiting patients' homes to understand their situations, while others built trust through frequent, shorter video consultations. Citation 6, Observation 3

A barrier to adopting video consultations was clinicians' fear of appearing unprofessional, compounded by frustrations with complicated or time‐consuming solutions. Some patients also reported low digital competency among staff, feeling they lacked training for effective video consultations. Citations 7 & 8

Ultimately, balancing trust and technology in telepalliative care is complicated by assumptions, varying preferences, and concerns about technology, professionalism, and communication quality.

Governance and Equity in Telepalliative Care—Ensuring Access, Sustainability and Quality

Participants identified key areas to address in a telepalliative care model to ensure patient and family needs, equitable access and integration with conventional palliative care services.

Governance in Telepalliative Care

Governance in telepalliative care involves ensuring healthcare managers provide clinicians with resources, training and digital solutions, as well as fostering cross‐sector collaboration. It also requires adjustments to workflows, provision of equipment and ongoing technical support. Key governance considerations are illustrated in Figure 3.

Cross‐Sectorial Collaboration and Technical Support

One of the main governance challenges identified was the need for clear roles and effective collaboration across sectors. While CCNs expressed openness to participating in telepalliative care, they highlighted the need for clarity on their specific role, particularly in consultations between patients and SPCTs. Their involvement could enhance the coordination of care, especially in home‐based palliative care, but formal guidance and support for their participation were lacking. All participants stressed the importance of accessible and user‐friendly technology to all collaborators and readily available support to assist with problems. Citation 9 & 10

Availability and Alignment of Telehealth Solutions

Participants also pointed out that not all clinicians were aware of the availability of telepalliative care, and many patients were unaware of its potential benefits. An information campaign was deemed necessary to expand awareness of telepalliative care options, particularly in primary care settings. Citation 11

Participants reflected that needs for telepalliative care would vary significantly between different patient groups, including those in nursing homes, patients living at home and those living alone. It was particularly noted that telepalliative care could support out‐of‐hours consultations for nursing home residents, and individuals and families with palliative care needs at home, enabling access to specialists and pre‐emptive treatment outside of regular office hours. Citation 12

Ensuring a reliable technical infrastructure for telepalliative care is important for avoiding serious glitches during consultations, such as poor audio, screens freezing, etc., which negatively impact communication. Participants recommended aligning with solutions that have already been successfully implemented in certain locations. Citation 13

Training for Clinicians

Training and digital competency were central governance concerns. All participants agreed on the importance of providing clinicians with the skills to use telehealth technologies effectively. Many expressed concerns about the technical barriers that could arise, especially when clinicians lacked confidence in using the technology. Overcoming these barriers requires ongoing digital training and creating a supportive environment where clinicians feel comfortable with the technology. Furthermore, providing proper equipment and suitable physical settings is essential for smooth delivery.

Adjusting to New Workflows and Care Coordination

Telepalliative care has the potential to change the delivery of palliative care, which would require adjustments to workflows. Participants noted that telepalliative care could be a disruptive force in terms of how care is coordinated, particularly in terms of changing how consultations occur and how care is coordinated across sectors. Equity in Telepalliative Care

Healthcare managers can ensure equity in telepalliative care by supporting participation, providing guidelines for service allocation, and supporting alignment of patient and family preferences with goals of care. Efforts should also focus on improving access for underserved groups. Key aspects of equity in telepalliative care are illustrated in Figure 4.

Supporting Participation in Telepalliative Care

To ensure equity, it is essential to support individuals who wish to participate in telepalliative care, particularly those with limited digital skills or poor access to technology. Patients and family caregivers who had previous experience with telehealth emphasised the need for support, patience and empathy when conducting video consultations. Issues such as poor internet coverage and lack of digital literacy were seen as barriers to participation, and participants highlighted that guidance on using devices and applications was critical to reducing stress during consultations. Citation 15

Allocation and Tailoring of Services

Equity in telepalliative care also involves ensuring that the right service reaches the right patient. There were concerns that older patients, those living alone, and those with limited digital skills could be excluded from accessing telepalliative care. However, participants acknowledged that the benefits of telepalliative care could outweigh the burden of adapting to new technology, particularly for those with strong family support. Some clinicians expressed the need for clearer guidelines on which patient categories would be appropriate for telepalliative care, particularly in determining eligibility for video consultations and remote support. Aligning Patient Preferences With Goals of Care

Aligning patient preferences with goals of care is another key aspect of equity in telepalliative care. Some patients and family caregivers, particularly older individuals, expressed a preference for face‐to‐face consultations, though many acknowledged that they could adapt to using digital technologies with appropriate support. The process of aligning communication methods and devices with the patient's current situation is crucial. Patients and clinicians both emphasised that communication preferences could change over time, and the most appropriate method for each consultation should be adapted to the patient's evolving needs. CCNs expressed concern about the potential unintended negative effects of telepalliative care, such as the risk of cutbacks that might eliminate home visits and emphasised the need for telepalliative care to remain one of several communication options available to patients and families in palliative care. The introduction of telepalliative care offers an opportunity to bridge gaps in care for patients who may not have easy access to face‐to‐face consultations. For example, out‐of‐hours video consultations could provide essential care to patients and family caregivers at home, reducing the need for unnecessary hospital admissions. However, to ensure that telepalliative care does not exacerbate existing inequalities, it is important that the technology and support mechanisms are accessible to all patients, including those who may struggle with digital skills, lack reliable internet access or live remotely. Discussion

The aim of this study was to develop programme theory through stakeholder engagement that will contribute to the development of a contextually aligned, family‐focused model of telepalliative care. We have summarised the main results and elicited key uncertainties that merit further discussion. Our findings revealed that the virtual room in telepalliative care offers flexibility, autonomy and improved quality of life for patients and families. SPCTs need to adapt by adjusting communication strategies and compensating for the lack of physical examinations. A tension exists between the flexibility of video consultations and the need for face‐to‐face meetings, especially for delivering bad news or making serious decisions. While video consultations offer convenience and family involvement, concerns about digital competency and fear of technology undermining professionalism remain barriers to wider adoption in palliative care, similar to findings by Oelschlägel et al. [27]. Key considerations of governance and equity in telepalliative care require addressing digital competency and cross‐sector collaboration to ensure equitable access and high‐quality care, as highlighted by Gallegos‐Rejas et al. [50] in telehealth in general.

Our findings suggested that the degree of flexibility and autonomy available to patients and family caregivers in the virtual room was greatly dependent on the level of digital competency of clinicians, and the degree to which they feel comfortable in providing palliative care in this setting, regardless of patients' and families' digital competency. The more digitally competent the clinician was, the less focus they had on technology. Repeated use led to improvements in digital competency. As SPCTs attended to the patient and family caregivers' needs, new ways of providing palliative care in the virtual setting evolved. As their confidence increased, they were able to support patients and family caregivers in accessing and navigating the virtual room. These findings are mirrored by previous studies [51, 52] that found that lack of digital competency among HCPs was one of the greatest barriers to telehealth adoption. More recently, a review by Lundereng et al. [53] on HCPs' experiences and perspectives on telehealth for home‐based palliative care found that HCPs who lacked knowledge, understanding or familiarity with telehealth were less inclined to use telehealth, or encourage their patients to do so, and prior experience with technology or positive experiences with telehealth had the opposite effect. Therefore, enhancing HCPs' digital competency through education is highly relevant for increasing the adoption of telepalliative care [54]. In recognition of the shift from face‐to‐face to virtual care at the start of the COVID‐19 pandemic, the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) recommended a set of necessary generic telehealth skills for HCPs [55]. The recommendations cover areas relating to patient and practice readiness, remote clinical evaluation and care, communication, professionalism, IT, privacy and legal, ethics, patient safety and access and equity [55]. While a useful starting point, the recommended skills are generic, lacking information on speciality‐specific matters in palliative care. The holistic, team‐based, multilevel, and cross‐sectorial approach to patients with palliative care needs and their families characterises palliative care in Denmark [36], setting it apart from other specialities. Providing virtual care in palliative settings requires an appreciation of the same. While the use of video conferencing for cross‐sectorial collaboration is advised [36], no clear recommendations or clinical guidelines exist for telepalliative care in Denmark. The digital skills required by SPCTs, therefore, represent a key uncertainty that requires further investigation to uncover digital skills specific to delivering high‐quality palliative care in virtual settings. According to Leena et al. [56], HCPs' digital skills in cancer care fall into four categories:IT, ethical practice, creating a human‐oriented relationship and digital education and support. While ethical and practical guidelines are necessary, they stress the importance of assessing HCPs' digital skills and identifying their educational needs in order to raise their level of digital competency [56].

The majority of participants in this study favoured face‐to‐face visits for first visits to establish relationships and for delivering bad news, or making serious decisions, after which video consultations could be performed. Similarly, Catapan et al. [57] found that familiarity with HCPs beforehand was an important factor that contributed to trust and confidence in telehealth encounters for patients. Establishing trustful and collaborative relationships with patients and families marks the beginning of most home‐based palliative care journeys [58]. Initial coordinating consultations are often held in a patient's preferred place of care, which for many is at home [58]. These visits may coincide with transitions in care from curative to palliative care for patients and families, which can be very distressing, during which time professional support and the experience and continuity of care can be supportive factors [59]. We uncovered an underlying fear of technology interfering with the provision of high‐quality palliative care in situations where patients and families were encountering a crisis, which seemed to stem from a fear of appearing unprofessional in encounters that require sensitive and attentive care. Rosa et al. [10] described how SPCT's avoided serious palliative care topics while engaging with patients and families via telehealth in what they called “high‐stakes conversation”, but they also described the opposite and how telehealth created the opportunity to provide telepalliative care to patients and families who otherwise would not have been able to access palliative care services. They concluded that as specialists developed their digital competency and comfort in delivering telepalliative care, the role of telepalliative care would grow, especially in supporting families and promoting self‐efficacy [10]. Another plausible explanation for HCPs' hesitancy to provide telepalliative care may lie in the lens through which HCPs perceive that patients and families benefit from telepalliative care. Keenan et al. [60] found that HCPs using telepalliative care viewed patients' palliative care needs from a more biomedical perspective than from a biopsychosocial perspective. Thus, HCPs may be less aware of the meaningful aspects of telepalliative care for patients and families beyond the clinical context [60]. Therefore, preferences for face‐to‐face or virtual encounters should be based on patient preferences and a biopsychosocial perspective. Hybrid models consisting of face‐to‐face and telepalliative care may be considered for future models of telepalliative care [8].

Our findings highlighted aspects of governance and equity in access to telepalliative care. Participants displayed concerns regarding vulnerable individuals' access to telepalliative care and how cross‐sectorial collaboration may support access for marginalised individuals. Telepalliative care has the potential to minimise inequity in access to palliative care [61]. However, despite universal healthcare for citizens in Denmark, inequalities persist in Denmark and internationally [62, 63, 64]. While our results tentatively suggest that telepalliative care may play a role in providing access to palliative care for marginalised individuals, evidence for the same is lacking in a Danish context, warranting further research. Strong leadership, coupled with the provision of resources and training, was considered a key consideration of telepalliative care governance, which is in alignment with recent recommendations from international expert workshops regarding digital health in cancer care and palliative care in Europe [65]. Supporting patients with limited digital skills, tailoring services and aligning care with patient preferences were main considerations for equity in access to telepalliative care. Future research should explore whether clear guidelines for telepalliative care and accessible technology can prevent exacerbating inequalities in palliative care.

A limitation of this study was the difficulty in recruiting younger patients and their families. Access to potential participants was facilitated through nurses from oncology departments and SPCTS, which may have introduced a gatekeeping function to protect younger patients and families from unnecessarily demanding activities, a familiar phenomenon in palliative care research [66]. Efforts to mitigate this did not yield further recruitment of younger patients and families. Additionally, the prevalence of newly diagnosed cancer among people aged ≥ 60 years in 2023 was three times higher than among those aged < 60 years [67], naturally limiting the number of potential younger participants.

Conclusion

The development of programme theory and the identification of key uncertainties may help increase the contextual relevance of a family‐focused model of telepalliative care. Key considerations include the importance of face‐to‐face first visits for establishing relationships and the concern that technology may overshadow palliative care. The benefits that patients and families may gain from telepalliative care depend on clinicians' level of digital competency, highlighting the need to enhance clinicians' skills. However, the specific digital skills required to deliver palliative care in a virtual setting remain uncertain and warrant further investigation. Additionally, there is a lack of evidence regarding the role of telepalliative care in reaching marginalised populations, pointing to an important area for future research to ensure equitable access to palliative care services. The findings from this study, the programme theory and key uncertainties will theoretically inform the development of a family‐focused model of telepalliative care through stakeholder collaboration.
