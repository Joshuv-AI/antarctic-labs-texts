Fulminant cholesterol crystal embolism syndrome triggered by Vein of Marshall ethanol infusion: a case report
Authors: Bingquan Yu, Jikang Tang, Jing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCholesterol crystal embolism syndrome (CCES) represents a catastrophic complication of endovascular procedures. While mechanical trauma during left atrial catheter ablation acts as a known trigger, the specific embolic risks associated with Vein of Marshall ethanol infusion (VOM-EI) remain unexplored.Case presentationA 66-year-old male with severe atherosclerosis and chronic kidney disease underwent persistent atrial fibrillation ablation involving VOM-EI (8 mL of ≥ 99.7% dehydrated ethanol). Six hours post-procedure, he developed extensive, non-pruritic livedo reticularis. He remained hemodynamically stable (lowest blood pressure 111/78 mmHg) but rapidly progressed to anuric acute kidney injury (creatinine peaking at 542 µmol/L) and severe systemic inflammation. Diagnostic evaluation effectively ruled out contrast-induced nephropathy, hypotensive acute tubular necrosis, and autoimmune vasculitis. Given the rapid deterioration, specific eosinophiluria testing and renal biopsy were deferred; however, sterile pyuria, classic skin lesions, and recent vascular intervention established a highly probable clinical diagnosis of CCES. Treatment with intermittent hemodialysis and high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (40 mg/day) rapidly suppressed the inflammatory storm and facilitated substantial renal recovery.ConclusionsIn patients with severe aortic plaque burden, VOM-EI, alongside established factors like transseptal catheter manipulation and systemic anticoagulation, may act as a potential trigger for CCES. Although a definitive diagnosis via biopsy was not feasible, we believe that early recognition and prompt corticosteroid therapy contributed substantially to the patient’s clinical recovery.

Introduction

Catheter ablation is the cornerstone therapy for symptomatic atrial fibrillation (AF). While major complications are well-documented, cholesterol crystal embolism syndrome (CCES) is an exceptionally rare catastrophic event, with an estimated incidence of less than 0.1% following electrophysiological procedures [1, 2]. CCES is typically provoked by the mechanical scraping of vulnerable aortic atherosclerotic plaques by stiff wires or large-bore sheaths during endovascular interventions [3], triggering a massive foreign-body inflammatory response.

Recently, ethanol infusion into the Vein of Marshall (VOM-EI) has emerged as an effective adjunctive strategy for treating persistent AF [4–6]. While highly efficacious, the mechanical stress of balloon inflation and intense localized chemical phlebitis induced by ethanol in close proximity to the descending aorta pose theoretical embolic risks. Here, we present what, as far as we are aware, is the first reported case of fulminant CCES precipitated by VOM-EI and radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA), highlighting diagnostic challenges and the potential benefits of early corticosteroids.

Case report

A 66-year-old male was admitted for the management of persistent AF. His medical history included hypertension, managed with a daily single-pill combination of perindopril (10 mg) and amlodipine (5 mg), and stage 3 chronic kidney disease (baseline creatinine 129 µmol/L). Generalized atherosclerosis had previously resulted in two ischemic strokes. His daily antithrombotic and lipid-lowering regimen included 20 mg of rivaroxaban and 20 mg of atorvastatin. Preoperative carotid ultrasound revealed bilateral vulnerable plaques (Fig. 1A and B).

Under general anesthesia, the surgical team performed a transseptal puncture and cannulated the Vein of Marshall (VOM). Following baseline venography to confirm local anatomy (Fig. 1C), a balloon catheter was inflated to 5 atm, and 8 mL of medical-grade dehydrated ethanol (≥ 99.7% concentration) was infused. The operators subsequently isolated the pulmonary veins and completed extensive linear ablation. Intravenous heparin (13,000 U) maintained the activated clotting time within the target range. The procedure concluded without immediate complications, utilizing a minimal volume (< 100 mL) of iodixanol, an isosmolar contrast medium.

Six hours post-procedure, the patient developed extensive livedo reticularis on his trunk and lower extremities (Fig. 2). A dermatology consultation noted the absence of pruritus. Hemodynamically, the patient remained entirely stable; his blood pressure during the onset of the rash was 111/78 mmHg, with no documented episodes of hypotension. However, his clinical condition rapidly deteriorated. The patient developed anuria by postoperative day 1, and his serum creatinine surged to 542 µmol/L by day 2. Concurrently, a massive systemic inflammatory response erupted, characterized by a leukocyte count of 23.3 × 10⁹/L (80.7% neutrophils) and a peak high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) of 82.99 mg/L.

A comprehensive diagnostic workup was initiated to determine the etiology of the acute kidney injury. Platelet counts remained stable (181 × 10⁹/L), excluding heparin-induced thrombocytopenia. Autoimmune panels—including normal complement levels (C3, C4) and negative results for rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP, and immunoglobulins—rendered acute autoimmune vasculitis highly unlikely. A urogenital ultrasound revealed normal kidney dimensions without hydronephrosis, definitively ruling out post-renal obstruction. Routine urinalysis demonstrated sterile pyuria (27 leukocytes/µL, protein 2+, negative bacterial cultures).

Given the rapid clinical deterioration and the necessity for urgent dialysis, a confirmatory renal or skin biopsy was deemed too high-risk. Furthermore, specific Hansel staining for eosinophiluria was not performed in the emergency setting. Nevertheless, the constellation of sterile pyuria, classic atheroembolic skin lesions, stable hemodynamics, and recent complex vascular intervention established a highly probable clinical diagnosis of fulminant CCES.

Intermittent hemodialysis was initiated on day 3. To suppress the severe crystal-induced cytokine storm, intravenous methylprednisolone (40 mg/day) was administered. The patient exhibited a dramatic clinical turnaround. By day 6, urine output recovered to 2,600 mL/day, creatinine decreased to 244 µmol/L, and hs-CRP fell to 21.78 mg/L (Fig. 3), allowing for the removal of the dialysis catheter. The patient was transitioned to oral methylprednisolone (24 mg/day) and was subsequently discharged on day 16 with a stabilized creatinine level of 161 µmol/L.

Discussion

Establishing a clinical diagnosis of CCES requires the careful exclusion of alternative etiologies for postoperative AKI. In our patient, contrast-induced nephropathy was highly improbable given the minimal use of isosmolar iodixanol (< 100 mL) and the presence of systemic cutaneous manifestations. Ischemic acute tubular necrosis was excluded due to the absence of hypotension or sepsis. Finally, while acute systemic alcohol toxicity was considered, the 8 mL of dehydrated ethanol infused into the confined VOM undergoes rapid systemic dilution, lacking the biological plausibility to induce the isolated anuric renal failure and livedo reticularis observed here.

To evaluate the novelty of this presentation, we conducted a literature search of PubMed and Embase (from inception to March 2026) using keywords including “Vein of Marshall,” “ethanol infusion,” and “cholesterol crystal embolism.” The search yielded no prior reports directly linking VOM-EI to CCES. However, applying a causality framework (such as Bradford Hill considerations) reveals that while the temporal relationship is clear, the exact mechanism remains multifactorial. We postulate that a potential “chemical-mechanical” synergy may have acted as a trigger: intense localized chemical phlebitis from high-pressure ethanol combined with balloon inflation near the descending aorta may destabilize fragile plaques.

Nevertheless, it is critical to acknowledge that well-established, potent alternative triggers were simultaneously present. As summarized in Table 1, the mandatory transseptal puncture and catheter manipulation are recognized mechanical causes of atheroembolization. Furthermore, systemic anticoagulation (intravenous heparin) acts as a significant contributing factor by preventing the formation of protective thrombi over ulcerated plaques and promoting intra-plaque hemorrhage. Thus, VOM-EI should be viewed as a potential synergistic trigger rather than a solitary cause in patients with severe baseline atherosclerosis.

The subsequent organ damage in CCES is driven primarily by a severe foreign-body inflammatory response rather than mere mechanical occlusion. As highlighted by Pervaze et al., renal involvement in CCES often manifests as an acute or subacute decline in kidney function due to crystal-induced acute interstitial nephritis [7]. Clinical observations suggest that sterile pyuria frequently acts as a reliable surrogate marker for this nephritis in the absence of eosinophiluria [8, 9]. Upon embolization, macrophages engulf the cholesterol crystals, leading to the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and the massive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [10, 11].

Currently, standardized guidelines for the pharmacological management of CCES are lacking. While concerns exist regarding corticosteroid use and tissue repair, the profound inflammatory state in our patient led our team to pursue an aggressive pharmacological intervention. The administration of intravenous methylprednisolone rapidly suppressed the cytokine storm, facilitating subsequent renal recovery [12].

In conclusion, in patients with a high burden of atherosclerotic disease undergoing complex AF ablation, VOM-EI may serve as a potential trigger for CCES, particularly when compounded by catheter manipulation and systemic anticoagulation. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for atheroembolization when unexplained “skin-kidney” failure occurs. Although a definitive diagnosis via biopsy was not feasible in this acute presentation, we believe that early recognition and prompt corticosteroid therapy contributed substantially to his clinical recovery.
