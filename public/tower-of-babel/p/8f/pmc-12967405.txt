Dysregulated methylation of the human BOLL promoter hinders germ cell development from spermatogonia to spermatids
Authors: Tsung-Yen Lin, Hsing-Yi Chen, Yung-Hsuan Cheng, Chun-Wun Lu, Hsiu-Yen Ma, Hsiu-Chiung Hou, Yung-Ming Lin, Shih-Chieh Lin, Yu-Sheng Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeBOLL, a highly conserved gene crucial for meiosis in spermatogenesis, is epigenetically regulated through DNA methylation in various species. This study aimed to determine whether BOLL promoter methylation contributes to its downregulation in azoospermic men with hypospermatogenesis (HS) and to explore its potential regulatory association with human spermiogenesis.MethodsWe conducted pyrosequencing to assess methylation levels at the putative BOLL promoter on testicular samples from azoospermic men with HS versus normal spermatogenesis (NR) and evaluate correlations with spermatogenic severity and gene expression. In silico CpG island (CGI) prediction and luciferase reporter assays were used to confirm methylation-sensitive regulatory regions. Pathway enrichment analysis identified biological processes associated with BOLL. Functional assays using BOLL overexpression in HEK293T cells and GC2 cells, as well as demethylation using 5-Aza-2’-deoxycytidine (5-AZA) in GC2 cells, were conducted to explore downstream transcriptional effects.ResultsEight CpG sites (CpGs) within the BOLL promoter CGI were significantly hypermethylated in HS samples, with seven exhibiting a significant inverse correlation with the spermatogenic score, and three with BOLL transcript levels. The region from − 1434 to + 180 was confirmed as a methylation-sensitive promoter. Gene ontology analysis indicated that spermatid development and differentiation were the top BOLL-associated pathways. BOLL overexpression by plasmid transfection in HEK293T cells and GC2 cells upregulated spermatid-specific genes (TNP2 and GAPDHS), while concurrently suppressing spermatogonia-associated markers (ID4 and PIWIL4). In GC2 cells, 5-AZA treatment enhanced Boll expression and induced downstream spermatid-specific genes, including Prm2, which was not responsive to BOLL overexpression alone.ConclusionWe identified specific BOLL promoter CpGs that are hypermethylated in HS and associated with reduced gene expression. These findings suggest that promoter methylation may contribute to BOLL silencing and impaired spermatid differentiation, supporting its regulatory role in human spermiogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11033-026-11633-0.

Introduction

Infertility affects 8–12% of couples of reproductive age globally, with male factors contributing to approximately half of the cases [1–3]. Despite advancements in diagnostic technologies, 30 to 50% of male factor infertility remains idiopathic, lacking an identifiable cause [4]. Epigenetic regulation, particularly DNA methylation, has emerged as a crucial factor controlling gene expression during spermatogenesis [5, 6]. Dynamic waves of DNA methylation and demethylation are essential for germ cell development and differentiation [6–8]. Clinically, altered DNA methylation patterns have been observed in the sperm or testicular tissue of infertile men, and these changes are increasingly being linked to poor semen quality [7]. As such, epigenetic modifications are now recognized as important contributors to unexplained male infertility.

BOLL, a member of the human DAZ (Deleted in Azoospermia) gene family, is well known for its crucial role in mammalian spermatogenesis [9–12]. BOLL is highly conserved across species, and the human BOLL gene can rescue the spermatogenic defect in boll mutant flies [13, 14]. Interestingly, while no pathogenic mutations or polymorphisms in the human BOLL have been reliably associated with male infertility [15–19], several studies have reported aberrant hypermethylation of the BOLL promoter in men with spermatogenic failure, including oligoasthenoteratozoospermia (OAT), hypospermatogenesis (HS), and Sertolic cell-only syndrome (SCOS) [20–22]. These findings suggest that aberrant DNA methylation, rather than sequence-level mutations, is a key contributor to BOLL downregulation in male infertility.

Functionally, BOLL encodes an RNA-binding protein that is essential for meiotic progression during spermatogenesis [15, 23, 24]. Boll overexpression has been shown to activate meiosis-related genes such as Stra8, Vasa, and Scp3 in male goat germline stem cells [11]. Moreover, studies on mice and sheep suggest that Boll may also contribute to later stages of spermatogenesis, including spermatid differentiation [10, 12]. Notably, Boll-deficient mice can complete meiosis but arrest at the round spermatid stage [10], suggesting a regulatory role that extends beyond meiosis. However, the molecular mechanisms through which BOLL regulates spermiogenesis remain uncertain. To address this gap, we systematically investigated BOLL promoter methylation and its downstream effects in testicular samples from non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA) patients with HS.

Based on this background, we hypothesized that hypermethylation of specific CpG sites (CpGs) in the BOLL promoter region contributes to impaired spermatogenesis in azoospermic men with HS. This study investigated the association between BOLL promoter methylation and the severity of spermatogenic failure, examined whether the affected CpGs were located within a predicted CpG island (CGI), and explored the functional impact of BOLL expression using luciferase assays, gene overexpression, and demethylation approaches.

Methods

Subjects and human testicular samples

A total of 24 azoospermic men were included, comprising 16 patients diagnosed with HS and eight with obstructive azoospermia and normal spermatogenesis (NR). HS was defined as impaired spermatogenesis involving all stages of germ cells, as observed by histological evaluation of the testicular biopsy. NR was defined as normal testicular volume, histology, and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) level. All participants provided a small portion of testicular tissue for use in subsequent molecular and histological analyses.

DNA extraction, Bisulfite modification, and pyrosequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from 2 × 2 × 2 mm testicular tissue samples using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and purity were assessed with a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA), and the concentration was adjusted to 40 ng/µL. Bisulfite conversion was performed using the EZ DNA Methylation-Gold™ Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA), which converts unmethylated cytosines to uracils while preserving methylated cytosines. Bisulfite-treated DNA was amplified using primers designed for non-CpG regions (MethPrimer: http://www.urogene.org/methprimer), and methylation levels at individual CpG sites were quantified by pyrosequencing using the PyroMark™ MD system (Qiagen). Methylation percentages were calculated from the ratio of cytosine (C) to thymine (T) signals at each CpG site, and quality control was conducted using built-in software controls.

H&E stain of testicular samples and spermatogenic score

Approximately 5 mm³ of tissue from each sample was processed for histological analysis. Samples were first rinsed with PBS and fixed in Bouin’s solution at room temperature for 12 to 36 h. Following fixation, tissues were thoroughly washed in PBS, gradually dehydrated in an ethanol series from 30% to 95%, and then stored in 100% absolute ethanol for at least 30 min. After clearing with Histoclear, the specimens were embedded in molten paraffin wax and allowed to solidify at room temperature for 1–2 h. Paraffin-embedded blocks were sectioned using a Histostat Microtome (Buffalo, NY, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The sections were deparaffinized in Histoclear, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and stained sequentially with Mayer’s hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). After staining, slides were rinsed with distilled water and ethanol, and subsequently mounted for microscopic examination. The degree of spermatogenic activity was quantified using a modified scoring method based on the report by Bergmann and Kliesch [25]. Specifically, the spermatogenic score was calculated as the percentage of seminiferous tubules containing elongated spermatids: Spermatogenic score = (Number of tubules with elongated spermatids / Total number of tubules) × 10.

RNA extraction, complementary DNA synthesis, and quantitative real-time PCR

Total cellular RNA was extracted from testicular tissue using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were measured by optical density at 260 nm using a spectrophotometer. A total of 500 ng of RNA was used for complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis. Reverse transcription was performed using an RT master mix, with the reaction incubated at 42 °C for 90 min, followed by heat inactivation at 95 °C for 10 min, and then rapidly cooled to 4 °C using a programmable thermocycler (PTC-100, MJ Research, Inc., Watertown, MA, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted using the StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Each PCR reaction contained 300 nM of forward and reverse primers (sequences listed in Supplementary Table 1) and 10 µL of 2× SYBR Green I Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). Thermal cycling conditions and dissociation curve analysis were performed according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Fluorescence signals were collected and analyzed using StepOne Software v2.1 (Applied Biosystems).

BOLL CpG islands (CGI) prediction

CGI of the BOLL gene was predicted in silico, using Gardiner-Garden and Frommer’s criteria, including %GC above 50%, the ratio of observed/expected CpG (ObsCpG/ExpCpG = (Number of CpG/Number of C x Number of G) x Total number of nucleotides in the sequence) above 60%, and length of GC-rich region above 200 bp [26]. The Sequence Manipulation Suite (SMS) and GC-Profile 2.0, computational tools for searching CGI, were used and were available on the World Wide Web at https://www.bioinformatics.org/sms2/cpg_islands.html and http://tubic.org/GC-Profile2 [27, 28].

Plasmid transfection, in vitro DNA methylation, and promoter activity assay

Plasmid constructs were generated by PCR amplification using PrimerSTAR GXL DNA polymerase (TaKaRa) with genomic DNA from human testicular tissue as the template. The fidelity of the inserted sequence was verified by Sanger sequencing. Transformation was performed using Escherichia coli PIR1 competent cells. To generate methylated plasmids (Met-pCpGL-BOLL), 1 µg of plasmid DNA was incubated with 1600 µM S-adenosylmethionine and 2.5 U/µg of DNA methyltransferases (M.SssI and HhaI; New England Biolabs, Inc.) in reaction buffer, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Methylation status was confirmed by restriction enzyme digestion using methylation-sensitive enzymes (HhaI) and the methylation-insensitive enzyme MspI, followed by bisulfite modification and pyrosequencing. Plasmid DNA was extracted using phenol/chloroform, ethanol-precipitated, and quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). Both methylated and unmethylated versions of pCpGL-BOLL were transfected into PC-3 cells in parallel, along with a Renilla luciferase control plasmid, using Lipofectamine (Invitrogen). After 24 h of transfection, luciferase activity was measured using a dual-luciferase reporter assay system (Promega, USA) on a 96-well luminometer. All luciferase reporter assays were performed in at least three independent biological replicates (n = 3), each with technical triplicates.

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses

GO is a bioinformatics framework that provides a standardized vocabulary for annotating and classifying the biological functions of genes and their products across species [29]. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis is used to identify biological pathways significantly associated with a given set of genes or proteins [30]. In this study, GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were conducted for candidate BOLL-associated genes derived from the GSE9210 dataset using ShinyGO (version 0.77), an interactive gene set enrichment analysis tool [31].

BOLL expression analysis from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database

We analyzed the publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset GSE106487, which profiles human testicular cells. Cell type annotations from the original publication were used to classify major testicular cell populations, including spermatogonial stem cells, spermatocytes, spermatids, Sertoli cells, and Leydig cells. The expression levels of the BOLL gene were extracted for each defined cell type. Statistical comparisons of BOLL expression across cell types were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with significance defined as P < 0.05.

Cell culture

Human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK293T) cells and mouse spermatocyte-derived GC2-spd cells were used in this study. Both cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Corning, 10-101-CV, New York, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Culture media were supplemented with fetal bovine serum (FBS) at varying concentrations depending on experimental conditions, along with 1% Antibiotic-Antimycotic solution (100×; GeneDireX, Cat. #CC501-0100, Taiwan). Additionally, GC2 cells were cultured with 1% L-glutamine (200 mM; GeneDireX, Cat. #CC515-0100, Taiwan). Culture media were refreshed regularly throughout the incubation period. All cell culture–based experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates (n = 3).

Plasmid preparation and transfection

The human BOLL open reading frame (ORF) cDNA clone expression plasmid (Cat. #HG15415-CF) was obtained from Sino Biological TW Inc. (Beijing, China). HEK293T cells and GC2 cells were transiently transfected with 500 ng/µL of BOLL cDNA using Lipofectamine™ 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following transfection, cells were incubated in serum-free medium for the indicated durations prior to downstream analyses.

Demethylation using 5-Aza-2'-deoxycytidine

5-Aza-2’-deoxycytidine (5-AZA; Cat. #2353-33-5) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). GC2 cells were treated with 10 µM 5-AZA in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) for a duration of six weeks. During the treatment period, cells were routinely subcultured every 3 to 4 days, and the 5-AZA treatment was reapplied with each passage to maintain consistent demethylating conditions.

RNA extraction and real-time polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. #15596026, Waltham, MA, USA) and quantified using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from a defined quantity of RNA using the M-MLV reverse transcription kit (Promega, Cat. #M1701, Madison, WI, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-PCR) was performed using the FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche, Cat. #04913914001, Basel, Switzerland) on the ABI StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Gene-specific primers were purchased from Genomics (Taipei, Taiwan). For human HEK293T cells, spermatogonia-specific genes included ID4 and PIWI4, while spermatid-specific genes analyzed were PRM2, TNP2, GAPDHS, CATSPER1, and CATSPER3. In GC2 mouse cells, Prm2, Tnp2, and Gapdhs were assessed. These marker genes were selected based on stage-specific expression profiles reported in a single-cell transcriptomic study of mammalian spermatogenesis [32].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., CA, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, one-way ANOVA and two-tailed Student’s t-tests were used. For non-normally distributed data, including methylation levels and gene expression in testicular tissues, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (r) was used to assess associations between CpG methylation and either spermatogenic score or BOLL expression. Effect sizes were interpreted based on r values: 0.1–0.29 (small), 0.3–0.49 (moderate), ≥ 0.5 (strong). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The statistical method used for each figure is specified in the figure legend.

Results

Seven hypermethylated CpGs on the BOLL promoter correlate with spermatogenic failure

As shown in Fig. 1A, our previous study identified a significantly hypermethylated region (from − 735 to -676) within the BOLL promoter in HS using a methylation array [21]. To further validate and explore more potential CpGs that contribute to spermatogenic failure under a hypermethylated status, an extended promoter region (from − 1225 to -584) was selected for pyrosequencing (Supplementary Fig. S1). Regions not selected showed negative results in the initial array analysis. Of 34 CpGs examined, eight (positions − 1099, -1093, -1069, -1053, -1027, -1021, -987, and − 708) displayed significantly different methylation levels between HS and normal spermatogenesis (NR) (Fig. 1B and C). Notably, seven CpGs (positions − 1099, -1093, -1069, -1053, -1027, -1021, and − 987) exhibited a significant inverse correlation between methylation level and spermatogenic score, which were used to quantify the severity of spermatogenic failure (Fig. 1B and D).

CGI promoter plays a crucial role in regulating the BOLL promoter activity

Three CpGs (located at -1093, -1069, and − 1021) showed a significantly inverse correlation between methylation level and BOLL expression (Fig. 2A and B), suggesting that hypermethylation at these loci contributes to spermatogenic failure through transcriptional silencing of BOLL. These CpGs were located within a predicted CGI spanning from − 1225 to + 1657, with a total length of 2,882 base pairs, an observed/expected CpG ratio of 0.79, and a GC content of 65.6% (Fig. 2A). We conducted a luciferase reporter assay to validate whether the promoter region of the predicted CGI serves as the regulatory region for methylation. As shown in Fig. 2A, the BOLL construct contained a BOLL sequence from − 1434 to + 180 (detailed sequence in Supplementary Fig. S2). In vitro methylation was carried out using GCGC-specific M.HhaI and CG-specific M.SssI methyltransferases (Fig. 2C). The BOLL construct contained 10 M.HhaI and 102 M.SssI methylation sites. While M.HhaI methylation caused a slight reduction in luciferase activity that was not statistically significant, M.SssI methylation led to a significant decrease in reporter activity (P = 0.0125) (Fig. 2C), supporting the role of the CGI as a key regulatory element governing BOLL transcription via methylation-dependent silencing.

The role of BOLL in spermatid development and differentiation by gene enrichment analysis

Our data have highlighted the critical clinical relevance of BOLL DNA methylation in infertile males with HS, prompting further investigation into the downstream mechanisms of BOLL in spermatogenesis. To identify potential downstream targets, we first performed a correlation analysis between gene expression levels and BOLL expression in testicular specimens from infertile males. Genes that exhibited a significant positive correlation with BOLL expression (P < 0.05) were subsequently subjected to biological process enrichment analysis using the ShinyGO tool. This analysis revealed that BOLL is likely involved in several critical processes related to male reproduction, including spermatogenesis, spermatid development, spermatid differentiation, and cilium movement (Fig. 3A). To further elucidate BOLL’s cellular specificity, we examined its expression across different testicular cell types using single-cell RNA sequencing data from normal human testes. As shown in Fig. 3B, BOLL expression was highest in spermatocytes, followed by spermatids and spermatogonial stem cells (SSCs). The predominance of BOLL activity in spermatocytes and spermatids is consistent with our functional annotation results, supporting the notion that BOLL plays a pivotal role in spermatid development and differentiation.

Overexpression of the BOLL gene and demethylation of the BOLL promoter induce spermatid markers in mammalian cell lines

BOLL expression was first upregulated in HEK293T cells via transfection with a BOLL-expressing plasmid, which led to a marked increase in BOLL transcript levels (Fig. 4A). Following BOLL overexpression, the transcript levels of spermatogonia-specific genes, including ID4 and PIWIL4, were significantly decreased (Fig. 4B). In contrast, spermatid-specific genes, including PRM2, TNP2, GAPDHS, CATSPER1, and CATSPER3, were significantly upregulated (Fig. 4C), suggesting a shift in gene expression toward the spermatid lineage. To further explore the epigenetic regulation of Boll, GC2 cells (derived from mouse spermatocytes) were treated with the DNA demethylating agent 5-AZA, resulting in a significant increase in Boll transcript levels in GC2 cells (Fig. 5A). Boll overexpression in demethylated GC2 cells resulted in increased expression of spermatid-associated genes, including Prm2, Tnp2, and Gapdhs (Fig. 5B). In parallel, direct overexpression of Boll in GC2 cells also significantly increased Tnp2 and Gapdhs expression, while Prm2 showed no significant change (Fig. 5C and D). These results suggest that Boll is regulated by promoter methylation and contributes to the activation of spermatid-specific genes.

Discussion

For decades, reduced BOLL expression has been reported in infertile men with impaired spermatogenesis. However, no causative mutations or common polymorphisms in the BOLL gene have been identified to explain this reduction [15–19]. This longstanding paradox led researchers to consider epigenetic regulation as a more plausible mechanism instead. DNA methylation has emerged as a critical regulator of germ cell development, influencing gene expression patterns that are essential for both the early and late stages of spermatogenesis [7]. Zhang et al. reported elevated methylation levels of the BOLL promoter in sperm samples from men with OAT, although the difference did not reach statistical significance [20]. Li et al. identified several CpGs in the 5-flanking region of the BOLL gene, demonstrating significantly increased methylation in testicular tissues from infertile men with SCOS [22]. Aside from SCOS being defined by the total absence of germ cells in the human testis, the biological significance of BOLL methylation in HS has yet to be established.

We previously demonstrated that the BOLL promoter region was hypermethylated with reduced BOLL expression in infertile men with HS [21]. The present study extends the analysis by using pyrosequencing to systematically map CpG methylation across an expanded BOLL CGI promoter region and identify specific hypermethylated CpG sites. We further validated their functional relevance through correlation with transcript expression and luciferase reporter assays. To our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively characterize BOLL promoter CpG methylation patterns and examine their relationship with BOLL transcriptional regulation and impaired spermatogenesis in HS, a condition marked by reduced numbers of germ cells at multiple developmental stages, including BOLL-expressing spermatocytes and spermatids. Given that BOLL is predominantly expressed in spermatocytes, followed by spermatids, our data indicate that epigenetic silencing, rather than sequence-level mutation, may underlie or correlate with BOLL downregulation in the testis of azoospermic men with HS.

DNA methylation functions as a conserved regulatory mechanism for BOLL expression across several species. Yao et al. identified a 107-bp core promoter region in bovine Boll that was sensitive to methylation-dependent transcriptional control [33]. Similarly, Zhang et al. reported a 488-bp promoter fragment from the mouse Boll gene that, when transfected into HEK293T cells, exhibited significant transcriptional repression following in vitro methylation [20]. Consistent with these findings, our luciferase assays illustrated that the promoter region (-1434 to + 180) is the methylation-sensitive regulatory element of the human BOLL gene. Collectively, these data reinforce the concept that promoter methylation plays a pivotal and evolutionarily conserved role in regulating BOLL activity and may contribute to male infertility when dysregulated.

BOLL is known for its role in meiotic division in mammalian germ cells [11, 15, 16]. Beyond meiosis, our data suggest that BOLL might play a role in spermatid development and differentiation in the post-meiotic stage. First, gene enrichment analysis and single-cell RNA sequencing of human testicular tissue indicated that BOLL expression is most abundant in spermatocytes, followed by round spermatids, but is largely absent in spermatogonia. These findings align with prior studies that have found elevated BOLL expression in later-stage mammalian germ cells [12, 17, 34, 35]. Secondly, in HEK293T cells, the overexpression of BOLL resulted in the upregulation of specific spermatid genes, including PRM2, TNP2, GAPDHS, CATSPER1, and CATSPER3, while simultaneously downregulating spermatogonia-specific markers, such as ID4 and PIWIL4. This dual impact indicates that BOLL enhances post-meiotic differentiation while concurrently inhibiting early-stage germ cell identity, underscoring its possible function as a molecular switch in spermatogenesis.

To further investigate BOLL regulation, we used GC2 cells, mouse spermatocyte-derived cells offering a more germline-relevant model than HEK293T. After treating with the demethylating agent 5-AZA, Boll expression increased with consistent upregulation of three key spermatid-related genes, including Prm2, Tnp2, and Gapdhs. These genes are functionally essential in sperm development. TNP2 and PRM2 are involved in chromatin remodeling and condensation during spermiogenesis [36–38], while GAPDHS provides the ATP necessary for sperm motility [39]. CATSPER1 and CATSPER3, although upregulated in the HEK293T system, were not assessed in GC2 cells due to their lack of expression in mouse germ cells, as shown by single-cell transcriptomic profiling of murine spermatogenesis [32]. Although GC2 cells are derived from spermatocytes, their Boll promoter may retain physiological methylation that limits full transcriptional activity. The strong response to 5-AZA suggests that DNA demethylation relieves this repression and activates downstream spermatid genes (Fig. 5B). In parallel, direct Boll overexpression in GC2 cells upregulated Tnp2 and Gapdhs, but not Prm2 (Fig. 5D), suggesting that Prm2 expression may require both transcriptional activation and epigenetic derepression, consistent with its documented methylation sensitivity during spermatogenesis [40, 41]. This observation reinforces the role of DNA methylation in coordinating stage-specific gene expression during spermatogenesis. Together, these findings support a model in which BOLL functions as a post-meiotic transcriptional activator under epigenetic control. The complementary use of HEK293T and GC2 systems highlights the interplay between transcriptional activation and DNA methylation in driving the transition from spermatocytes to spermatids.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although histopathological classification was used to define HS and NR groups, alternative etiologies of azoospermia cannot be fully excluded, and BOLL hypermethylation should therefore be regarded as a contributory rather than exclusive mechanism. In addition, one key limitation is that genomic DNA was extracted from whole testicular biopsies without isolating specific germ cell populations. This approach may introduce variability in methylation profiles due to differences in the proportions of somatic and germ cells between samples. Future studies using germ cell-specific isolation or single-cell methylome analysis may provide more precise and cell–type–specific epigenetic data. As spermatocytes normally undergo global hypomethylation followed by selective remethylation during spermatogenesis [42], their reduction in HS testes could also contribute to the apparent hypermethylation of the BOLL promoter. Similarly, reduced bulk BOLL expression may reflect both decreased spermatocyte number and possible transcriptional repression, which cannot be fully distinguished in our study. For functional assays, we used HEK293T cells to evaluate BOLL’s transcriptional potential and GC2 cells as a complementary germline-relevant model. HEK293T cells, despite lacking germline identity, offer high transfection efficiency and a clean background. GC2 cells, while spermatocyte-derived, only partially represent human spermatogenesis. Our additional overexpression experiments in GC2 cells confirmed that BOLL can induce spermatid markers in a germline-like context. However, since 5-AZA induces global hypomethylation, its effects may not be exclusively mediated by BOLL. Further validation in more physiologically relevant models, such as human germline cells or animal systems, is warranted. These caveats do not diminish the significance of our findings but instead highlight important directions for future research, including single-cell methylome profiling, germ cell–specific functional assays, and targeted epigenome editing to establish causality.

Our findings nevertheless provide a preliminary foundation for future diagnostic and therapeutic strategies in male infertility. The hypermethylation of specific CpGs within the BOLL promoter may be a diagnostic tool for identifying epigenetically driven NOA, particularly in patients with HS who lack known genetic mutations. If validated in larger cohorts, BOLL methylation profiling using testicular or minimally invasive liquid biopsies could be integrated into clinical workflows to improve the diagnosis and classification of idiopathic male infertility. Furthermore, the reversibility of epigenetic silencing offers a promising avenue for therapeutic intervention. Targeted demethylation strategies, such as CRISPR-dCas9-TET1-based epigenome editing or small molecule demethylating agents, could potentially restore BOLL expression in affected individuals [43]. Although such approaches remain in the preclinical phase, our study provides critical mechanistic insights and a compelling rationale for exploring such technologies in future translational research. However, several challenges remain for clinical translation, including the efficient and specific delivery of editing systems to the testis, minimization of off-target effects, and ensuring the reversibility and safety of epigenetic modifications. Future in vivo studies using animal models will be essential to evaluate the therapeutic feasibility of locus-specific BOLL demethylation strategies. Beyond clinical relevance, this study enhances our understanding of how CGI promoter methylation regulates germ cell differentiation. BOLL, as a methylation-sensitive gene predominantly expressed in post-meiotic cells, may serve as a useful model for studying epigenetic control during spermatogenesis. The promoter region appeared conserved across species, suggesting an evolutionarily stable regulatory mechanism. Future research should investigate the upstream causes of BOLL hypermethylation, including environmental or developmental factors, and assess its reversibility. A broader exploration of DAZ family gene methylation may also uncover coordinated epigenetic regulation of spermatogenesis.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that hypermethylation at specific CpGs within the BOLL promoter is significantly associated with impaired spermatogenesis in men with HS. We identified three key CpGs whose methylation levels correlated with both reduced BOLL expression and the severity of germ cell depletion. Functional assays supported that methylation of the CGI promoter directly suppresses BOLL transcription. Furthermore, both BOLL overexpression and epigenetic reactivation through demethylation led to upregulation of spermatid-specific genes and downregulation of spermatogonia markers, suggesting a potential role for BOLL in promoting germ cell differentiation beyond meiosis.
