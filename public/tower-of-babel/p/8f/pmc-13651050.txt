Association of the pan-immune-inflammation value with 60-day in-hospital mortality in critically ill patients with acute myocardial infarction: evidence from the MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Mingjie Wu, Hanyu Xu, Zhen Jia, Ling Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCritically ill patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) frequently exhibit marked inflammatory and immune dysregulation, which substantially increases their risk of short-term mortality. The pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV) represents an integrated index calculated from neutrophil, monocyte, platelet, and lymphocyte counts. There is currently no systematical evaluation clarifying the link of PIV with 60-day in-hospital mortality in the critically ill patients with AMI. The primary objective of the current study was to assess PIV’s prognostic utility and to identify potential threshold effects using data derived from the MIMIC-IV database.MethodsThree thousand two hundred twenty-four critically ill patients with AMI were included and stratified into tertiles according to log-PIV: Q1 (low, n = 1,075), Q2 (intermediate, n = 1,074), and Q3 (high, n = 1,075). Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves, together with the log-rank test, was applied to analyze differences in 60-day in-hospital mortality across tertiles. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were constructed to estimate the corresponding hazard ratios (HRs) for each tertile. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was carried out to investigate the potential dose-response association between log-PIV and mortality risk, and segmented Cox models were fitted for participants with log-PIV values exceeding the identified inflection point. Subgroup analyses were carried out for assessing potential effect modification.ResultsKM analysis demonstrated a stepwise decrease in 60-day in-hospital mortality with increasing PIV (p < 0.0001). RCS curves showed a U-shaped pattern of the association between log-PIV and mortality risk, with an inflection point at log-PIV = 5.75, beyond which mortality risk increased linearly (p overall < 0.001). Above this threshold, the unadjusted model showed a 28% higher mortality risk for patients in PIV-Q3 versus those in PIV-Q1 (HR = 1.28; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.09–1.51; p = 0.003). This risk was further elevated to 32% following adjustment for demographic variables (HR = 1.32; 95% CI: 1.11–1.55; p = 0.001) and remained significant at 20% after full adjustment (HR = 1.20; 95% CI: 1.01–1.44; p = 0.041). Subgroup analysis revealed that PIV had better predictive performance among patients not receiving anticoagulants (p interaction < 0.05).ConclusionElevated PIV was independently linked to increased 60-day in-hospital mortality among critically ill patients with AMI. The observed threshold effect warrants further validation in external cohorts. PIV may represent a convenient adjunct marker for risk stratification, although its clinical significance should be further validated in prospective studies. Routine measurement of PIV may enable early detection of high-risk individuals and guide the implementation of timely and targeted clinical management in acute care settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06149-1.Keywords: Acute myocardial infarction, Pan-immune-inflammation value, MIMIC-IV database

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is widely recognized as a major contributor to death and disability worldwide. In spite of continuous progress in reperfusion therapies and evidence-based pharmacotherapy, short-term mortality remains unacceptably high among critically ill patients with AMI—particularly in cases accompanied by malignant arrhythmias, cardiogenic shock, or multi-organ dysfunction [1]. In-hospital mortality in this population has been reported to range from 10% to 15%, with 60-day in-hospital mortality risk representing a critical prognostic benchmark [2, 3]. Thus, identifying patients at high risk early in the disease course and applying appropriate individualized treatments in a timely manner are key to optimizing clinical outcomes.

The currently available risk stratification tools for AMI, including the Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) score and the Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) score, integrate clinical parameters, electrocardiographic findings, and biochemical markers (e.g., troponin) to predict mortality risk [4, 5]. However, their predictive accuracy is often reduced in critically ill patients, and certain components may be impractical in emergency or resource-limited settings due to rapid dynamic changes or high costs [6]. Moreover, these models inadequately account for inflammatory pathways, despite compelling evidence confirming the central role of inflammation in post-infarction myocardial repair, microcirculatory dysfunction, and secondary organ injury [7, 8].

As recently reported, excessive systemic inflammation following AMI, characterized by neutrophil infiltration, platelet activation, and imbalances in monocyte/lymphocyte ratios, is directly associated with larger infarct size, accelerated ventricular remodeling, and increased mortality [7, 9]. While individual inflammatory or immune cell-based markers are capable of independently predicting major adverse cardiovascular events [10, 11], their prognostic value is limited when considered in isolation, highlighting the necessity for integrated biomarkers that comprehensively reflect the overall immune-inflammatory status of the host.

The pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV) represents a newly developed composite indicator derived from neutrophil, monocyte, platelet, and lymphocyte counts. It was developed to quantify the overall intensity of immune activation and inflammatory cascades [12]. PIV has demonstrated robust prognostic utility in oncology, infectious diseases, and rheumatologic diseases [13, 14]. In studies of cardiovascular disease, elevated PIV values have been reported to correlate with coronary artery disease severity and adverse outcomes among individuals with stable angina [15]. However, in critically ill patients with AMI, its value in predicting short-term mortality—particularly 60-day in-hospital mortality—has not been systematically evaluated.

Given the marked immune dysregulation and systemic inflammatory storms in critically ill patients with AMI, and considering the practical advantages of PIV, including its accessibility, rapid availability, and low cost, we hypothesized that PIV may independently predict 60-day in-hospital mortality in this group of patients. To test this hypothesis, a large-scale cohort study was carried out based on analysis of data sourced from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC)-IV database.

Materials and methods

Data source

Data for the current retrospective cohort study were retrieved from the MIMIC-IV database (version 3.0), a freely accessible repository comprising de-identified electronic health records from intensive care units (ICUs) at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2022 (web link: http://physionet.org/content/mimiciv/3.0/). MIMIC-IV provides a comprehensive collection of patient data, including patient demographics, vital signs, laboratory parameters, procedures, medications, clinical scores, and mortality outcomes [16]. Access to the database was granted, and patient data were extracted by a designated author of the current study following completion of the required training and receipt of institutional approval (Certification No. 72554671).

Study population

In accordance with the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions codes, we extracted records of adult ICU-admitted patients with a primary diagnosis of AMI from the MIMIC-IV database. Exclusion criteria included: (1) patients younger than18 years; (2) non-first ICU admission during the same hospitalization; (3) ICU stay less than 24 h; and (4) lack of recorded values for neutrophil, monocyte, platelet, or lymphocyte counts (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of patient selection outlining the inclusion and exclusion criteria applied

Data extraction and covariates

The required data were retrieved using PostgreSQL (version 17.3.1) and structured query language (SQL). The following set of potential confounders were collected for adjustment: Demographics: age, sex, race, and marital status; Comorbidities: diabetes, stroke, hypertension, and chronic kidney disease (CKD); Medications: anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents, angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), β-blockers, statins, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), and nitroglycerin; Physiological and laboratory parameters: heart rate, body temperature, red blood cell count (RBC), red cell distribution width (RDW), white blood cell count (WBC), platelet count, oxygen saturation (SpO₂), lymphocyte count, and neutrophil count, among others. Definition of exposure and outcome

The PIV, the primary exposure in this study, was calculated as:

The primary outcome of this study was defined as 60-day in-hospital mortality. Patients were followed from the date of their first ICU admission during the index hospitalization until in-hospital death, hospital discharge, or 60 days after ICU admission, whichever occurred first. Counts of neutrophils, monocytes, platelets, as well as lymphocytes were obtained from the first laboratory measurements within 24 h after ICU admission. In cases where multiple measurements were available, the earliest recorded values were used.

Statistical analysis

Patients were stratified into tertiles according to their log-PIV: Q1 (low; n = 1,075; log-PIV ≤ 5.882), Q2 (intermediate; n = 1,074; 5.882 < log-PIV ≤ 6.969), and Q3 (high; n = 1,075; log-PIV > 6.969). Categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages) and analyzed with the Chi-square test. After assessing the normality of continuous variables, data with non-normal distributions were reported as medians (interquartile ranges) and analyzed with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Cox proportional hazards models (in three sequential stages: unadjusted, adjusted for demographic variables, and fully adjusted for demographics, comorbidities, vital signs, laboratory parameters, and medications) were constructed to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with the PIV-Q1 group serving as the reference. Trend tests were carried out across tertiles. Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves, together with the log-rank test, were applied to evaluate differences in 60-day in-hospital mortality across groups. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were conducted to assess the potential dose-response relationship between log-PIV and 60-day in-hospital mortality risk, and segmented Cox models were subsequently fitted for participants with log-PIV values exceeding the identified inflection point. RCS analysis was performed to flexibly model potential nonlinear associations, given that inflammatory biomarkers may exhibit threshold or U-shaped relationships with clinical outcomes. In addition, RCS analysis was conducted as an exploratory approach to assess potential nonlinear associations without assuming a specific functional form. R (version 4.4.1) was employed for conducting all statistical analyses, with significance defined as a two-sided p < 0.05. Any variable with missing data > 20% was excluded, whereas those with ≤ 20% missing data were addressed through multiple imputation by chained equations using the ‘mice’ package in R. Sensitivity analyses based on the complete-case dataset yielded consistent results.

Results

Baseline characteristics of critically ill patients with AMI stratified by PIV tertiles

In total, 3,224 critically ill patients with AMI were enrolled and stratified into tertiles according to log-PIV: Q1 (low, n = 1,075), Q2 (intermediate, n = 1,074), and Q3 (high, n = 1,075). Mean (SD) log-PIV differed markedly across tertiles (Q1: 5.06 ± 0.75; Q2: 6.42 ± 0.30; Q3: 7.85 ± 0.73; p < 0.0001). Patients in Q3 were more likely to be aged > 65 years compared with those in Q1 (68.2% vs. 62.9%; p = 0.034). Racial distribution also differed significantly across tertiles (p < 0.0001), with the highest proportion of White patients (64.8%) observed in Q3 and the largest proportion of Black patients (12.9%) in Q1. The prevalence of hypertension decreased with increasing PIV (Q3: 47.6% vs. Q1: 63.4%; p < 0.0001), while the incidence of CKD increased (Q3: 46.7% vs. Q1: 39.8%; p = 0.005). Key laboratory parameters also exhibited a gradient change in the degree of inflammation: patients in Q3 had higher neutrophil, platelet, and monocyte counts, plus overall WBC, but lower lymphocyte counts compared with Q1 (all p < 0.0001). Additionally, RDW and Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) scores were elevated, whereas serum albumin levels were reduced in Q3 versus Q1 (p < 0.0001; Table 1).

KM survival analysis

KM curves demonstrated a significant stepwise decline in 60-day in-hospital mortality with increasing log-PIV (Fig. 2; log-rank p < 0.0001). Patients in different tertiles exhibited markedly different overall survival (log-rank p < 0.0001). However, it should be noted that the log-rank test assesses global differences across groups and does not necessarily indicate statistically significant differences between each pair of tertiles.

KM survival curves of patients with AMI stratified by PIV tertiles (log-rank p < 0.0001). The shaded areas indicate 95% CIs

RCS analysis

As illustrated in the RCS curves (Fig. 3), in the unadjusted model, there was a U-shaped link between log-PIV and the HR for 60-day in-hospital mortality (p overall = 0.001; p for nonlinear = 0.006), with an inflection point at log-PIV = 5.75 representing the minimum risk. To further evaluate the potential threshold effect suggested by the curve, a secondary analysis was conducted using a binary categorization based on log-PIV ≥ 5.75. All tertiles remained included in the primary Cox regression analyses. The RCS analysis indicated a potential nonlinear association, with the lowest estimated risk observed around log-PIV ≈ 5.75. This inflection point should be viewed as a preliminary finding intended for generating hypothesis rather than being treated as a definitive clinical threshold.

RCS analysis illustrating the link between log-PIV and 60-day in-hospital mortality. Unadjusted Cox model using the full dataset (p for overall = 0.001; p for nonlinear = 0.006)

Multivariable Cox regression

Cox regression (Table 2) demonstrated that, in the unadjusted model, patients in the PIV-Q3 tertile experienced a 24% greater risk of 60-day in-hospital mortality than those in the PIV-Q1 tertile (HR = 1.24 95% CI: 1.09–1.42; p = 0.002). Following adjustment for demographic variables, the HR remained at 1.26 (95% CI: 1.10–1.45; p = 0.001). In the fully adjusted model, which additionally accounted for hemodynamics, laboratory parameters, severity scores, comorbidities, and medications, patients in Q3 remained independently related to a 30% higher mortality risk (HR = 1.30; 95% CI: 1.12–1.50; p = 0.006). The association was not statistically significant for the intermediate tertile (Q2) in any of the models.

Sensitivity analyses

The reliability of the findings was verified through two sensitivity analyses. First, when patients above the inflection point were included, similar trends were observed, with patients in Q3 maintaining a significantly elevated mortality risk (reported as HRs) in the fully adjusted model (Supplementary Table 1). Second, analyses conducted on the pre-imputed dataset further validated the independent predictive value of high log-PIV (Supplementary Table 2).

Subgroup analyses

Through subgroup analyses (Table 3), a generally consistent link was revealed between high log-PIV (Q3) and 60-day in-hospital mortality across most clinical subgroups (p for interaction > 0.05). However, there were still notable exceptions. Among patients without prior stroke, those classified in Q3 faced a 36% greater risk of mortality (HR = 1.36; 95% CI 1.17–1.58; p < 0.001), whereas patients with prior stroke showed no significant association (interaction p = 0.005). Among patients not receiving anticoagulants, those in Q3 had a 57% higher mortality risk (HR = 1.57; 95% CI 1.31–1.88; p < 0.001), but no such association was found among those receiving anticoagulants (p for interaction = 0.002). Among patients administering β-blockers, those in Q3 exhibited a greater mortality risk (HR = 1.22; 95% CI 1.05–1.41; p = 0.001). In patients without CKD, those in Q3 showed a 59% elevated mortality risk (HR = 1.35; 95% CI 1.09–1.66; p = 0.006). There was no indication of significant effect modification by age, sex, race, and diabetes (p for interaction > 0.05). In contrast,, the PIV-mortality association was significantly affected by marital status or the use of statins/nitroglycerin (p for interaction < 0.05). Furthermore, interaction analyses based on the inflection point of log-PIV were performed. As shown in Supplementary Table 3, participants with log-PIV ≥ 5.75 had a significantly higher risk of mortality (HR = 1.13; 95% CI; 1.06–1.21; p = 0.001).

Subgroup analysis of the link between PIV and 60-day in-hospital mortality in patients with AMI

Interaction analysis

To assess potential heterogeneity around the inflection point identified in the RCS curve (log-PIV = 5.75), a formal interaction analysis was performed in the full analytical cohort. An interaction term between continuous log-PIV and a dichotomous variable (log-PIV < 5.75 vs. ≥ 5.75) was incorporated into the Cox proportional hazards model. The interaction term was statistically significant (p for interaction < 0.01), indicating that the association between log-PIV and mortality differs significantly across the two ranges of log-PIV. Importantly, the value of 5.75 should be interpreted as an exploratory threshold rather than a clinically validated cutoff.

Discussion

In this study of critically ill patients with AMI from the MIMIC-IV database, we provide the first systematic evidence supporting that the PIV may serve as a key prognostic marker. RCS analysis suggested a potential nonlinear pattern linking log-PIV with 60-day in-hospital mortality, with the lowest estimated risk found at around log-PIV ≈ 5.75. The observed association remained robust after multivariable adjustment (p overall < 0.001), offering new quantitative insight into how post-infarction inflammatory imbalance drives mortality risk [12, 17]. Although a potential inflection in the exposure–outcome relationship was identified around log-PIV = 5.75, interaction analysis provided limited evidence for a true differential effect across this cutoff, suggesting that the observed inflection should be interpreted cautiously. Accordingly, the value of 5.75 should be considered an exploratory, hypothesis-generating threshold rather than a clinically validated cutoff, and requires further validation in future large-scale studies.

First, the PIV captures the integrated “pro-inflammatory versus anti-inflammatory” network activated by myocardial necrosis. Ischemic cardiomyocyte death contributes to the release of damage-associated molecular patterns that engage Toll-like receptors (TLRs) on myeloid cells, triggering neutrophil recruitment, myeloperoxidase release, and neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation that compromise the microvascular barrier [18, 19]. Platelets not only facilitate hemostasis but also amplify cytokine storms by binding to monocytes via P-selectin, while sustained lymphocyte depletion undermines immunoregulation and tissue repair [20–22]. By simultaneously integrating neutrophil, monocyte, platelet, and lymphocyte counts into a single composite index, the PIV offers a dynamic, cross-disease measure of this complex network, a utility that has been previously validated in oncology and sepsis [23].

Second, our findings establish a clear threshold effect of PIV in AMI. Preclinical models demonstrate that when the PIV exceeds approximately 300, cardiac macrophages irreversibly shift from reparative (CD206⁺IL-10⁺) to destructive (iNOS⁺TNF-α⁺) phenotypes, accelerating fibrosis and functional decline [24, 25]. Clinically, among patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, those with PIV > 340 are at markedly higher risks of contrast-induced nephropathy, driven by persistent platelet TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway activation and a vicious “thromboinflammatory” cycle precipitating multi-organ injury [26, 27]. In our study, the RCS analysis suggested a possible threshold region. However, given that the analysis was retrospective and the threshold was identified through a data-driven estimation process, the finding should be interpreted cautiously and requires external validation.

Third, the PIV offers distinct advantages for early and dynamic risk monitoring. Conventional tools, such as the GRACE score, rely on static clinical and laboratory variables [28] and cannot rapidly track the swift evolution of acute inflammation. In contrast, the PIV can be calculated within 24 h of admission from routine blood counts—well before the CRP peak at 48 h—and its intra-day variability correlates strongly with ventricular remodeling and heart failure progression [29, 30]. Thus, the PIV enables timely and precise risk stratification in the critical early phase, which contributes to the optimization of ICU resource allocation and facilitates timely therapeutic decision-making.

Fourth, emerging therapeutic strategies have begun to target the high-PIV “thromboinflammatory” state. Beyond their established anticoagulant effects, heparins—with their highly sulfated glycosaminoglycan chains—bind free histone H3 to inhibit NET-driven cardiomyocyte toxicity and neutralize chemokines like IL-8 and CXCL1. Animal studies further show that heparin pretreatment reduces neutrophil infiltration by up to 70% in ischemia-reperfusion models [31, 32]. Among patients with acute coronary syndrome, anti-IL-1β therapy has been demonstrated to markedly reduce cardiovascular mortality in those with heightened systemic inflammation [33]. Vagal nerve stimulation via α7 nicotinic receptors attenuates splenic macrophage activation, thereby significantly lowering PIV and improving myocardial immune homeostasis [34]. These data support a multimodal approach targeting both thrombosis and inflammation for precision therapy in AMI patients with high PIV.

Finally, our multi-model and subgroup analyses confirmed PIV’s consistent predictive value across diverse patient profiles, comorbidities, and treatment regimens. Notably, the PIV-mortality link was stronger among patients without prior stroke or not receiving anticoagulants, whereas it was attenuated in those receiving β-blockers or anticoagulants. These findings suggest potential interactions between PIV and specific therapies, which may inform individualized treatment strategies. Future research should clarify PIV’s relationship with the immune microenvironment and cell-level phenotypic shifts using single-cell transcriptomics and spatial omics to map the immune landscape in high PIV myocardium [35]. Additionally, advanced machine learning techniques (e.g., LSTM networks, graph neural networks) could integrate PIV time series with clinical variables to develop real-time risk-alert systems and detect critical PIV changes as early warning signals [36].

In summary, this large-scale cohort study demonstrates that the PIV reliably stratifies short-term mortality risk in critically ill patients with AMI, with broad potential for rapid screening, dynamic monitoring, and tailored intervention. Future research should focus on elucidating PIV’s molecular underpinnings, refining AI-driven prognostic models, and evaluating targeted therapies in high-PIV populations to translate these insights into improved clinical outcomes.

Limitations

It is also important to recognize several limitations inherent in this study. First, due to its retrospective design and single-center origin, the presence of residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded, even after multivariable adjustment. Second, although missing data were handled using multiple imputation, bias arising from the underlying missingness cannot be fully ruled out. Third, the inflection point identified through RCS analysis was derived in a data-driven manner and therefore requires validation in independent external cohorts. Fourth, PIV was calculated based on a single baseline measurement, which may not reflect dynamic inflammatory changes over time. Finally, given the observational nature of the study, causal relationships cannot be definitively established.
