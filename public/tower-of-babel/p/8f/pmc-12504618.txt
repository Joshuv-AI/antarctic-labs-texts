Experimental and numerical investigation of flow through gate valve
Authors: S L Bhilare, G A Hinge, P M Bagade, M A Kumbhalkar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFlow dynamics within gate valves must be understood in order to address issues such as turbulence, pressure loss, flow separation, and cavitation. This study examines flow behavior within standard and modified gate valves using experimental and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) methods. Water flow within a gate valve, from 25% to full opening, is simulated to analyze velocity, pressure, and turbulence behaviors. With 25% opening, abrupt velocity accelerations in the valve throat initiate extreme pressure losses and intense turbulent flow due to flow separation. These outcomes serve to underscore the need for efficient valve geometries to induce stability in flow as well as suppress energy losses. Comparison between the standard and modified gate valves at 25%, 40%, and 55% openings highlights the advantages of the modified design. The standard valve exhibits high turbulence, high recirculation areas, and high dissipation of energy, particularly at low openings. Conversely, the modified valve cuts down on turbulence, promotes uniform flow, and reduces pressure losses. By removing vortex generation and velocity oscillations, it obtains more stable flow, which increases system efficiency. Further inspection of a 55% open gate valve with different fluids, for instance, water and SAW15W-40 oil, shows the impact of viscosity on fluid flow. Water specifies a smooth velocity profile and low pressure drops, while oil experiences higher resistance, pressure gradients, and shear forces. The new design addresses these differences well, with reduced potential for cavitation and enhanced performance. All these optimizations enhance operating efficiency in applications ranging from oil and gas to water distribution and chemical processing.Keywords: Gate valves, Turbulence, Pressure loss, Computational fluid dynamics (CFD), Flow optimization, Flexible gate valvesSubject terms: Hydrology, Engineering

Introduction

Flow through a control gate valve involves intricate velocity, pressure, turbulence, and energy dissipation interaction and is thus difficult to analyze. Degree of opening in the valve significantly affects the flow separation, development of vortices, and loss of pressure. Besides other difficulties include the following:

Flow Separation and Recirculation Zones—Flow separation on the edges results from geometry changes during closure and opening. Downstream recirculation zones are created at low openings (≤ 40%), which prevent laminar-to-turbulent transition. The extent and intensity of separation depend on valve shape and upstream conditions, and predictions are not straightforward.

Overcoming these issues is important for optimizing gate valve design and improving flow efficiency. One of the promising ways to control flow separation and recirculation zones is by using rubber sleeves or a flexible pipe in the gate valve region1,2.

Research background and motivation

Control gate valves are frequently used to regulate flow in fluid transport systems in industries such as water distribution, oil and gas pipelines, and chemical processing. The flow behavior of these valves is exceedingly complicated due to the dynamic interaction of velocity, pressure, turbulence, and energy dissipation. The hydrodynamic conditions change as the valve opens, particularly the formation of flow separation zones, vortices, cavitation, and turbulent structures, which have a direct impact on system efficiency, energy losses, operational stability, and component lifespan.

Partially open valves (≤ 40%) can cause flow issues such as recirculation and sharp pressure gradients. These issues result in decreased flow control precision, energy inefficiency, mechanical vibrations, noise, and material erosion. Despite the availability of computational and experimental techniques, anticipating and mitigating these negative repercussions is difficult due to inherent fluid complexity and equipment constraints. Recent design modifications, such as the use of rubber sleeves or flexible inserts, have shown promise in terms of minimizing flow instability. However, little thorough study has been conducted to combine experimental and computational insights into how these design changes affect essential flow parameters such as turbulent kinetic energy, vorticity, pressure drop, and flow uniformity across different valve openings and fluid types.

This work addresses the lack of effective flow control and energy efficiency in traditional gate valve designs, particularly under low to moderate opening conditions (≤ 55%). It will employ CFD simulation and experimental validation to investigate how design changes, such as the insertion of rubber sleeves, can reduce flow separation and turbulence, regulate cavitation, and improve flow uniformity.

Traditional gate valves are designed to only operate in fully open or fully closed positions because partially open states can cause noise, vibrations, and significant flow field fluctuations, according to published studies. However, in certain applications, especially those that call for viscous fluids like heavy oils, greases, or honey, partially opened valves are still used3. Since these conditions might result in notable velocity fluctuations, particularly in large pipelines, further investigation is necessary. In addition to monitoring flow, the goal of this work is to propose a dual-purpose device that can function effectively in partial flow scenarios. This publication offers a comprehensive analysis of flow behavior in partially opened valve designs to advance understanding of the fluid dynamics associated with these systems.

Shape optimization of the valve (e.g., slotted/curved gates, beveled edges) reduces separation4. Flow conditioning techniques such as guide vanes and flow straighteners stabilize the flow, while gradual opening devices minimize abrupt expansions5. A recent study reported that fluctuating separation zones on either side of the gate are largely responsible for the discrepancies in flow rate1,2.

Methodology

Experimental set up

The experiment compared discharge characteristics of a standard and modified gate valve using a rubber sleeve. The experimental set up is as shown in Fig. 1. The standard metal gate valve (65 mm diameter) was installed in a recirculation pipeline with an overhead tank at a constant 1.83 m head. Discharge was measured via a piezometer at the valve location under varying pressures and rotation angles. The valve required 13 full wheel rotations for complete closure (46,800 radians), with 650 mm acrylic pipes on both sides ensuring proper flow development. The valve bonnet was marked at 900 mm intervals for alignment.

Experimental setup a with and without rubber sleeve and b piezometer at both end of valve.

Testing involved gradually closing the valve up to eight full rotations counterclockwise, recording discharge at 90° intervals in both directions. A modified valve was then tested, incorporating a rubber sleeve (made from car tyre tube material with elastic modulus = 0.1 × 109 N/m2 and tensile strength = 8–12 MPa) designed to adapt to valve movement. The sleeve, totaling 1550 mm (650 mm on each side + 228.6 mm valve length), extended 50 mm beyond each pipe end for a watertight seal.

Experimentation using piezometers for pressure differences

Piezometers recorded pressure variations in both valves under different rotation angles. The standard valve, prone to separation zones, showed discharge fluctuations. Pipes bend improved measurement accuracy, enhancing its performance as a flow meter. Discharge readings from clockwise and counterclockwise rotations were compared with theoretical models. The modified valve with a rubber sleeve improved pressure fluctuation dampening and discharge consistency1,2.

Meshing

A practically orthogonal mesh is used to attain numerical stability and accuracy. The grid consists of 853,149 nodes and 831,600 elements, and the orthogonality varies between 0.78259 and 0.99972 with a mean of 0.98666. High orthogonality minimizes cell distortion, improving gradient computations and reducing numerical diffusion42,43. Skewness values are maintained within acceptable limits, from a maximum of 0.68262 to an average of 0.10286 for solution stability and accuracy purposes25. The minimum skewness of 0.01826 elevates interpolation accuracy to improve solution resolution of flows around complex valve geometries (Fig. 6).

Solver details

A pressure-based steady-state solver is used, which is most suitable for incompressible flows for gate valve applications29. The transition k-kl-ω turbulence model predicts boundary layer transitions correctly with higher accuracy compared to the common k-ε or k-ω models26,44.

Boundary conditions

Inlet: Velocity inlet ensuring controlled inflow.

Pipe Walls: No-slip condition simulating real fluid-wall interaction.

Outlet: Pressure outlet to allow natural flow adjustment.

Numerical solution methods

Pressure-velocity coupling is treated using the SIMPLE algorithm to provide efficiency and stability28. Momentum, turbulent kinetic energy (TKE), and specific dissipation rate are treated using a second-order upwind scheme to restrict numerical diffusion and enhance accuracy27,45. The residual convergence criterion of 10−5 offers solution reliability. This turbulence model and high-resolution mesh increase CFD prediction precision, resolving pressures and flow separation precisely in gate valve simulations.

Results and discussion

Analysis of the conventional gate valve

Figure 8a illustrates the pressure gradient variation of water and SAE 15 W-40 oil across a fully open gate valve. There is a large difference in pressure gradient (dp/dx) between the two fluids due to changes in viscosity. Water experiences a large initial depression in dp/dx near the inlet, reaching a lower negative value, whereas oil experiences a continuously higher positive gradient. This is due to the higher viscosity of the oil, which opposes motion and has greater pressure losses, as well as the lower viscosity of water, allowing for a quicker formation of a stable gradient. More viscous fluids are faced with more resistance, implying greater energy dissipation as well as pump power requirements46,47. Water has a sharp negative pressure gradient (~ − 2500 Pa) at L = 0 m, leveling off at − 500 Pa at L > 0.5 m. SAE 15 W-40 oil starts with a positive gradient (~ 4000 Pa), reducing gradually to 3000 Pa at L = 2 m. This indicates water moves to developed flow quicker, while oil has a high pressure gradient due to its viscosity. This agrees with the Darcy–Weisbach equation, where greater viscosity leads to increased pressure loss46.

The variation of total pressure along the pipe length is as depicted by Fig. 8b. Both liquids have a trend of decreasing pressure, although water drops more compared to SAE 15 W-40 oil. Water’s high pressure drop rate is associated with its lower viscosity and increased turbulence, leading to greater dissipation of energy by viscous losses. The pressure drop of the oil is more gradual and represents a laminar or transient flow regime due to viscous damping. This accords with the Darcy–Weisbach equation, which provides higher pressure loss for lower viscosity fluids in turbulent flow43,48. Both liquids begin at ~ 4500 Pa at the inlet. Water drops below 3000 Pa at L = 2 m, whereas SAE 15 W-40 oil remains over 3200 Pa. Water, being more viscous, experiences more turbulent interactions and more pressure loss, while oil possesses greater pressure due to a more stable, viscous-dominated flow. This based on Moody diagram predictions for pipe flow in turbulent conditions49.

Velocity gradients are shown in Fig. 8c. Water exhibits a higher velocity maximum than SAE 15 W-40 oil, illustrating that its lower viscosity allows for increased acceleration. Oil has a highly uniform velocity profile due to its resistance to flow. Water is turbulent, before the oil, which is nearer to laminar flow. Higher velocity creates higher energy dissipation in turbulent flow, whereas oil possesses higher viscous losses48–50. Water achieves 2.4 m/s at L = 0.6 m and then tapers off at 2.2 m/s, and SAE 15 W-40 oil is close to a constant 1.4 m/s. Lower viscosity allows for acceleration more rapidly and for earlier turbulence onset, while the oil resistance suppresses turbulence and reduces the friction losses51. Figure 8d shows water having low TKE (< 0.01) initially but rising to ~ 0.02 at L = 1.5 m. SAE 15 W-40 oil has higher TKE (~ 0.05 at L = 1.5 m), indicating that while it can delay the initiation of turbulence, it contains higher energy fluctuations when turbulence exists. Higher TKE enhances mixing but raises pressure drop due to viscous dissipation (Wilcox24; Pope50). Figure 8e demonstrates trends in eddy viscosity, in which water’s eddy viscosity rises sharply after L = 0.5 m, to 1.2 at L = 2 m. SAE 15 W-40 oil is consistently below 0.1, which indicates a stable flow with negligible turbulence. Low viscosity fluids become converted into turbulent conditions at earlier states, while viscous oils avoid eddy formation, retarding turbulent diffusion losses46,51,52.

Figure 8f show vorticity profiles. Oil retained more spin motion even at its viscous condition, whereas water dispersed vorticity rapidly with the development of turbulence. Higher vorticity oil means more loss due to shear, whereas lower vorticity in water signifies effective distribution of momentum. This confirms that turbulence enhances mixing but enhances resistance to flow, increasing head losses48,52. Water peaks at vorticity ~ 20 at L = 1 m, then declines, while SAE 15 W-40 oil attains ~ 80 at L = 0.5 m and saturates. High viscosity maximizes shear stress and resistance to flow, while water loses vorticity quickly, reducing rotational losses.

The results confirm that frictional and turbulence-induced losses govern energy dissipation in pipe flows. Water’s viscosity causes turbulence at lower speeds to produce more frictional losses and greater pressures with higher velocity. SAE 15 W-40 oil keeps viscous losses over a time period due to fluid resistance against deformation under stress, thus requiring more pressure gradient. This balance forces overall pipeline system efficiency. Design considerations account for the fluid-specific losses to achieve optimum pumping efficiency to reduce cost of energy47,49. Water has greater turbulence-induced skin friction losses, while oil maintains stable but viscosity-controlled losses. Water also witnesses greater pressure drop, exhibiting greater energy dissipation, while oil, with a smoother decline, requires more input of energy in consideration of viscosity. Water loses energy by friction and turbulence, whereas oil loses energy by steady-state shear deformation. Oil pumps must overcome more powerful pressure gradients, while water flow systems must consider head losses caused by turbulence.

In comparison with water (Fig. 13), oil achieves velocities in excess of 4 m/s, while water reaches approximately 2.5 m/s. Because oil has higher viscosity, momentum diffusion is reduced closer to boundaries so that the centerline velocity gradients are more acute. Water exhibits a less heterogeneous velocity profile than oil and exhibits localized acceleration and resistance near the valve.

Water pressure is between 1000 Pa and 2500 Pa, whereas that of oil is above 30,000 Pa at the entry, exhibiting higher resistance to flow because of higher viscosity. This also generates a higher pressure drop in oil, which leads to larger energy losses. The pressure gradient also shows such effects—water has a max pressure gradient of ~ 10,000 Pa/m, whereas oil reaches − 500,000 Pa/m, revealing high internal friction and energy loss. The intense negative gradient of oil reveals great flow restriction and pressure accumulation.

Turbulence and Eddy Viscosity: TKE, the index of turbulence strength, is larger in water (~ 0.04) and extremely smaller in oil (~ 0.008). This means that turbulence is being dampened in oil due to its viscosity, which makes it closer to laminar flow. Despite this, eddy viscosity, a characterization of turbulence-stimulated momentum transfer, is far higher in oil (~ 1.5 Pa s) than in water (~ 0.1 Pa s). This means that while oil turbulence intensities are low, the viscous properties of oil promote shear-induced eddy viscosity impacts on flow resistance.

Vorticity and Shear Forces: Water registers a maximum vorticity levels of ~ 250 s–1, while oil records over 1000 s–1 in some locations with higher stronger shear forces along the valve gap. Even with reduced turbulence, oil still has localized vortices, created by its high shear resistance and viscosity interactions with flow structures. Water flows more easily, possessing a constant velocity profile, reduced pressure drop, and higher turbulence levels. Oil, by contrast, has steep pressure slopes, reduced turbulence, and higher shear forces, so that its flow is more energy-consuming and resistant. Eddy viscosity governs oil’s flow characteristics, counteracting its suppressed turbulence and influencing energy dissipation and valve operation. Understanding these differences is crucial to minimize valve design and fluid control arrangements.

Conclusion

Fluid flow via a gate valve with varying openings exhibits the complex behavior of fluid dynamics in constrained geometries. As the fluid accelerates through the narrow valve throat, its velocity increases dramatically. This rapid acceleration causes a significant pressure drop after the valve, in accordance with Bernoulli’s principle, as pressure energy is transferred into kinetic energy. The highest pressure gradient occurs around the valve throat, promoting flow separation and turbulence. Maximum eddy viscosity and vorticity are also observed around the opening, which have a direct impact on downstream flow stability. These findings emphasise the need of refined valve designs in reducing turbulence, pressure loss, and energy waste.

The experimental results demonstrate a significant variation in discharge behavior between regular and modified gate valves. Traditional designs include significant flow oscillations and turbulence, particularly at lower openings, which impairs discharge control. The improved valve, complete with a rubber sleeve, significantly lowers pressure variations and turbulence. This results in a steady discharge with only 12% variance and errors below 2%, considerably improving flow precision. CFD simulations confirm the improved flow control and performance.

A comparative study at gate openings of 55%, 40%, and 25% demonstrates the effect of geometry on valve behavior. Standard valves exhibit considerable turbulence, large recirculation zones, and rapid pressure dips, particularly under restricted flow. In contrast, the modified valve streamlines the flow channel, reduces vortex generation, and increases efficiency by stabilizing velocity profiles.

When comparing water and SAE 15 W-40 oil flow via a 55% open gate valve, fluid properties play an important role. Water flows uniformly and has negligible pressure loss, whereas oil has a higher viscosity, resulting in increased resistance, energy dissipation, and shear forces near the valve.

Improved valve design reduces cavitation risk, extends equipment life, and improves operational efficiency in a variety of industries, including oil and gas, water distribution, and chemical processing. The introduction of structural modifications like the rubber sleeve results in less turbulence, more constant pressure recovery, and less energy loss, making it ideal for high-performance fluid transport systems.
