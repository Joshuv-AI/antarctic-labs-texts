Building Research Capacity and Culture in Allied Health – A Five-Year Strategic Review
Authors: Katrina Kenah, Abby M Foster
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAllied health clinicians play a vital role in delivering evidence-based care. Engagement in research is associated with better processes of care, patient outcomes and staff satisfaction but remains underfunded and variable compared to other health and medical professions.PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate changes in research capacity and culture within allied health at a large public healthcare service, following the implementation of a comprehensive Allied Health Research Strategic Plan.MethodsA repeated cross-sectional observational study was conducted at a large public healthcare service in Melbourne, Australia. Allied health clinicians were surveyed using the validated 51-item Research Capacity and Culture (RCC) tool in 2019 and 2024 to assess changes across organizational, team and individual levels following implementation of a tailored, allied health research strategy targeting leadership, communication, collaboration, support, strategy and funding.ResultsA significant improvement in research capacity and culture was observed at the organizational and team levels (36 of 37 items, p < 0.05) in the 2024 survey (n=254) compared with the 2019 survey (n=250), with small to moderate effect sizes (r = −0.141 to −0.319). The largest improvements were seen in access to adequate research training resources, funding opportunities, expert support and mentoring (all p <0.001, r = −0.279 to −0.319). Despite these improvements, no significant changes were observed at the individual level and self-reported research skills remained lower than organizational and team domains.ConclusionThis study demonstrates the value of a tailored, multi-level approach to enhancing allied health research capacity and culture, with interventions leading to measurable improvements in organizational and team-level research engagement. The findings provide valuable insights for other healthcare organizations aiming to build sustainable research cultures to improve patient care and outcomes.

Introduction

Allied health clinicians – including physiotherapists, occupational therapists, speech pathologists, social workers, dietitians, podiatrists and allied health assistants – make up a quarter of the Australian health workforce and play a critical role in evidence-based care by integrating research evidence into clinical decision-making and service delivery.1 However, allied health research engagement – the active involvement of clinicians and organizations in conducting, supporting and applying research2 – remains variable and underfunded, particularly compared with the training, opportunities, and grant funding available to other health and medical professions.1,3

Participation in research within healthcare organizations is associated with improved processes of care (eg, a reduced length of stay, greater adherence to guidelines, enhanced care delivery) and patient outcomes (eg, lower mortality rates),4 with growing evidence of the specific value of allied health research engagement.5 Clinician and organization research engagement is underpinned by a strong research culture, defined by Harding et al6 as one in which the application of evidence is valued, clinicians are encouraged to participate in research-related activities, opportunities are available for staff to acquire skills in research, research achievements are recognized and there is an investment of resources to support research activity. The organizational benefits of a strong research culture extend beyond processes of care and patient outcomes, contributing to improved workforce satisfaction and a reduction in staff turnover.6 Recognizing the value of allied health research, the Victorian Allied Health Research Framework7 was established to support and embed a sustainable research culture within routine allied health practice. Similar policy-level initiatives, such as the Health Education England’s Allied Health Professions’ Research and Innovation Strategy for England,8 have been implemented in the UK.

Despite growing recognition of the importance of research engagement, clinicians often face barriers including limited time, competing clinical service delivery demands that constrain opportunities to engage in research, inadequate skills, and insufficient organizational support.9,10 Established research capacity building frameworks emphasize the importance of coordinated, multi-level strategies that target individual skills, team processes, and organizational systems to support sustainable research capacity within healthcare settings.11–13 Consistent with this, Matus et al12 identified that a “whole of system” approach is needed for building allied health research capacity in a public healthcare setting. Such an approach should: i) support clinicians to develop research knowledge, skills and confidence; ii) enable collaborative team-based research, and iii) ensure research is valued across all levels of the healthcare organization.12 While interventions have targeted individual or team-level capacity, few have addressed research culture and capability simultaneously across organizational, team and individual levels.14 As a result, there is limited understanding of the impact of multi-level strategies evaluated using validated tools such as the Research Capacity and Culture (RCC) survey.15,16

To address these gaps, we implemented a comprehensive, multi-level strategy aimed at enhancing allied health research capacity and culture across a large public healthcare organization, using the RCC to both inform and evaluate the strategy in a real-world, resource-constrained setting. We hypothesized that implementation of this strategy would lead to improvements in research capacity and culture at organizational and team levels, with more variable change at the individual level. The aims of this study were to: i)Evaluate changes in research capacity and culture at the organizational, team, and individual levels following a multi-level intervention.ii)Identify areas of success and ongoing challenges in improving research culture and individual skill development to refine the research strategy moving forward.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

A repeated cross-sectional observational design was used to compare changes in the research capacity and culture of allied health over a five-year timeframe.

Setting

Monash Health is the largest public healthcare service in the state of Victoria, Australia. Monash Health employs over 1463 full-time equivalent (FTE) allied health clinicians who provide services across major hospitals and a network of more than 40 locations that include adult inpatient, rehabilitation, aged care, outpatient services, community health, mental health and pediatric services.17 The Workforce, Innovation, Strategy, Education and Research (WISER) Unit at Monash Health supports the strategic growth and development of the allied health workforce. Within this unit, three allied health professionals (total 1.4 FTE) support allied health research development across the organization. Research support is also provided by allied health profession-based research leads within select professions and programs.

Participants

Allied health clinicians employed by Monash Health and operating under the governance of the Chief Allied Health Officer were eligible to participate in the study (See Supplementary Material 1 for the full list of included allied health disciplines). Despite being considered allied health professionals,18 psychologists and medical imaging professionals were excluded, as their professional governance does not fall under the Chief Allied Health Officer at Monash Health.

Intervention

An allied health research strategic plan was developed and activities addressing key priorities implemented to build research culture and capability across the organization.

Development of the Allied Health Research Strategic Plan

A multiphase process was used to develop the research strategic plan based on the Strategic Planning Guidelines for Victorian Health Services.19 The process involved: i)Analysis of the internal and external environment, followed by an evaluation of the organization’s strategic position.ii)Workshops with senior operational and research leaders within allied health to develop the strategic intent (vision), guiding principles (values) and research focus areas that form the foundation of the strategic plan.iii)Drafting of the strategic plan.iv)Consultation with stakeholders, including allied health service users.v)Finalization of the strategic plan with supporting communication strategy to assist dissemination.

Data Collection

A survey using the Research, Capacity and Culture (RCC) tool was used to measure changes in research capacity and culture within the organization.16 Results were compared to a previous survey with 250 respondents completed in 2019 as part of preliminary data gathering to inform the development of the research strategy The 2024 survey was administered via a secure online platform (SurveyMonkey, San Mateo, CA, USA), consistent with the 2019 survey. An email with a link to the survey was sent to all clinicians on an allied health distribution list by the WISER manager in early September 2024, and clinicians reminded to complete the survey prior to closing three weeks later. Ethics exemption was granted by the Monash Health Human Research Ethics Committee (Ethics Approval Number: 125440; Local Reference Number RES-26-0000-067Q). Digital informed consent was collected from all participants within the online survey.

RCC Survey Tool

The validated RCC tool includes 51 questions that examine participants’ perceptions of success or skill in a range of areas related to research capacity or culture across Organization (18 questions), Team (19 questions), and Individual (14 questions) domains.16 Each question is answered on a 10-point numeric scale, where 1 is the lowest possible level of success or skill and 10 is the highest possible level of success or skill. There is also an option of “unsure”.

Data Analysis

Survey data were extracted electronically into Microsoft Excel and imported into statistical analysis software for analysis. Responses where none of the organization, team or individual domains had been rated were removed from analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to describe the professional background and experience of survey participants. Characteristics of participants who responded to the 2019 and 2024 surveys were compared using chi-squared tests.

Descriptive statistics were used to calculate the median (IQR) for each item within the three RCC domains using available 1–10 ratings, with “unsure” responses reported separately as percentages and excluded from median calculations. Median scores were collapsed into three subcategories of low skill/success (scores 1–3.99), moderate skill/success (scores 4–6.99), and high skill/success (scores 7–10), in keeping with previous studies.9,21

Differences between median scores on the 2019 and 2024 RCC surveys were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann–Whitney) test, and changes visualized using box and whisker plots. Effect sizes (r) were calculated for non-parametric comparisons using the standardized test statistic (z) divided by the square root of the sample size (N), with values of 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively.22

All analyses were conducted using Stata (version 15; StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA), and figures were generated using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Participant Characteristics

For the 2024 survey, 302 responses were received. After excluding ineligible disciplines and responses with no ratings across the organization, team or individual domains, 254 allied health clinicians were included in the analysis, representing 15% of the total eligible allied health workforce within the organization (based on workforce data) (Table 1). Respondents were mostly female (87%) and worked in the professions of physiotherapy (30%), occupational therapy (16%) and speech pathology (15%). The majority of allied health respondents (80%) had been working in allied health for 6 or more years, and about half (49%) reported working for Monash Health for the same length of time. Over half (54%) of respondents were working in senior allied health (grade 3 or above) roles. This included 30 clinicians (12%) who were employed in grade 4 roles that typically include research as part of the position description, a notable increase from 2019. Over half of the clinicians were working in full-time permanent roles. Only a small number of clinicians (8%) had completed a research higher degree. Organizational Level Responses

A significant improvement in research capacity and culture at an organizational level was reported across all items (Figure 3), with small to moderate effect sizes (see Supplementary Table 1 for full statistical results). This change resulted in 15 of the 18 organizational items being classified in the high category, a notable increase from 7 items in 2019. The greatest areas of positive shift were seen in the areas of (i) adequate resources to support staff research training (2019 median (IQR) 5 (3–7), 2024 median (IQR) 7 (5–8), p <0.001, r = −0.319); (ii) access to funds, equipment or admin to support research activities (2019 median (IQR) 4 (2–6), 2024 median (IQR) 6 (4–7), p <0.001, r = −0.276); and (xiii) identified experts accessible for research advice (2019 median (IQR) 7 (3–8), 2024 median (IQR) 8 (7–9), p <0.001, r = −0.276). Despite these changes, (ii) access to funds, equipment or admin to support research activities was also one of the lowest rated items, along with (xi) software programs for analyzing research data (2024 median (IQR) 5 (3–8)) and a lack of (v) staff career pathways in research (2024 median (IQR) 6 (4–8)), all falling within the medium category. Discussion

Development and implementation of an organization-wide allied health research strategy, aligned to key strategic themes (leadership, communication, collaboration, support, strategy and funding), was associated with significant improvements in allied health research capacity and culture at both organizational and team levels over a five-year period. These findings highlight the benefits of implementing a multi-level strategy that is tailored to the unique context and needs of an organization.12,23 At the organizational level, activities such as the establishment of dedicated research leadership positions,15,24 a strategic research plan, research training programs,20 targeted funding mechanisms25 and annual awards may have contributed to the improvements in organizational research culture that were observed. Similarly, investment in dedicated profession- or program-specific research leads,26 embedded researcher models, and structured collaborations with academic partners, may have strengthened access to support within local research networks and team-based capability.

However, improvements at the organizational and team level did not translate into statistically significant gains in self-reported individual research skills, likely reflecting the focus of implemented activities on organizational and team-level capacity and culture rather than individual competencies. This aligns with Williams et al who similarly reported that organizational allied health research leadership positions improved team and organizational outcomes but not individual skills.15 In our sample, individual research capacity was rated lower than team and organizational domains, with clinicians reporting greater confidence in using research (eg, locating and applying evidence) than in undertaking activities required to initiate, design, and conduct research projects. This pattern has been consistently reported in allied health literature9,23,27 and contrasts with medical professions, where individual research capability is typically higher.3,28 Lower ratings may reflect differences in undergraduate research training, expectations for postgraduate research, and access to research-active environments.3,28 Individual research skill development likely requires sustained, hands-on engagement in research activities. As such, the allied health research strategy may not have provided the intensity, reach, or duration of support required to produce measurable improvements in individual research capability. Strengthening individual research capability will likely require embedding research activities into routine clinical practice, supported by targeted, discipline-specific approaches tailored to clinicians’ experience, roles, and career stages.29,30 Development of structured mentoring programs that extend beyond evidence review to support translation of research into practice may be considered as part of future capacity-building initiatives. Formal support for postgraduate study (eg, part-time scholarships) and clearly defined clinician-researcher career pathways may further support the development and retention of research expertise within health services.1

It is also important to consider the limitations of self-reported measures of research skill when interpreting these findings. As clinicians’ exposure to research increases, individuals may become more aware of the complexity of research processes and their own skill limitations, resulting in stable or conservative self-ratings despite genuine skill development – a phenomenon described as response shift.31 This effect has been demonstrated in health professions education, where learners retrospectively rated their baseline competence lower after gaining greater insight into the complexity of the assessed skills.32 Increased knowledge and insight may also lead to more critical self-appraisal.33 Within allied health professions, the strong emphasis on reflective practice and critical self-evaluation during training and professional development may further contribute to conservative self-ratings of competence.34 Consequently, the absence of statistically significant change in self-reported individual research skills may partially reflect shifts in insight and expectations rather than a lack of true capability development, suggesting that the impact of the strategy may be underestimated when relying solely on self-reported measures. Further longitudinal applications are needed to better understand the responsiveness of the RCC tool to change over time.

Finally, considerable variation in responses was observed across the allied health workforce, which may reflect differences between programs or professions. This variation highlights the challenge of enhancing research capacity and culture within a large, diverse organization spanning bed-based, community and residential care settings, each with distinct research needs. Ensuring equitable access to research opportunities and supports across settings is a key priority for sustaining organization-wide research engagement.

Strengths and Limitations

This study makes a unique contribution to the RCC literature by using the RCC to demonstrate improvements in allied health research capacity and culture following implementation of a tailored, multi-level strategy in a public healthcare organization.

However, the study has some notable limitations. First, the response rate for the 2024 survey was 15%, which may limit representativeness and introduce potential selection bias, as clinicians with greater interest in research may have been more likely to respond. Social desirability bias may also have influenced responses, particularly given the sustained organizational emphasis on research culture over the preceding five years, potentially leading to more favourable ratings. Second, the survey was anonymous, so it was not possible to link responses over the five-year period to assess within-person changes.

While this study focused on perceived changes in research capacity and culture, a more comprehensive evaluation could include objective measures of research impact, such as research outputs (eg, publications and grant funding), workforce development indicators (eg, higher degree enrolment), changes in clinical practice and service delivery, and patient-level outcomes aligned with value-based care.

Finally, improvements in research capacity and culture may not be solely attributable to the allied health research strategy, as broader organizational initiatives or access to non-allied health research supports within teams may have contributed to the observed changes and confounded the results. Additionally, team-level ratings may have been influenced by variation in how respondents interpreted the term “team,” potentially reflecting perceptions of multidisciplinary teams (eg, rehabilitation teams) rather than allied health discipline-specific teams (eg, physiotherapy).

Conclusion

Allied health research capacity and culture at organizational and team levels can be strengthened through a tailored, multi-level strategic approach informed by the Research Capacity and Culture tool and supported by dedicated research leadership and support roles. Such approaches enable clinicians to engage more effectively in conducting research and translating evidence into practice. However, further work is needed to better understand and strengthen individual research capability, including refining approaches to its measurement and implementing targeted capacity-building initiatives to support skill development. This study offers insights for other public healthcare organizations seeking to increase allied health research engagement to ultimately improve service quality, efficiency and patient outcomes.
