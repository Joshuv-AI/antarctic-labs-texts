Influence of light-polymerizing units and zirconia on the physical, chemical and biological properties of self-adhesive resin cements
Authors: Siriwong Wongsirisuwan, Narin Intarak, Sasiprapa Prommanee, Noppadol Sa-Ard-Iam, Sunporn Namano, Dusit Nantanapiboon, Thantrira Porntaveetus
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelf-adhesive resin cements (SARCs) are widely used for fixed prostheses, but incomplete cleaning near the gingival margin can cause inflammation. However, the factors influencing cement properties and the biological response of gingival fibroblasts to cement eluates are not well understood. This study examines the impact of two light-polymerizing units (LPUs) on the physical and chemical properties of two SARCs under simulated clinical conditions, as well as the subsequent response of human gingival fibroblasts (hGFs) to these eluates.MethodsDental cement discs of SARCs were polymerized using Kerr DemiPlus and 3 M Elipar DeepCure-S LED LPUs with or without a 2-mm thick zirconia screen. Physical properties (microhardness, surface roughness, residual monomers) were evaluated. hGFs’ cell viability, wound healing potency, and gene expression were assessed.ResultsBoth Maxcem and RelyX exhibited reduced microhardness and increased surface roughness when polymerized through zirconia or with DemiPlus LPU. Higher residual monomers (HEMA and GDMA in Maxcem; TEGDMA in RelyX) concentration was observed with DemiPlus and zirconia polymerization. Maxcem polymerized with DemiPlus exhibited lower cell viability, impaired healing, and altered gene expression in hGFs compared to those polymerized with Elipar LPU. Gene expression changes included downregulated NRF2 and HO-1 and upregulated CCR-3.ConclusionsLight-polymerizing Maxcem through zirconia with DemiPlus LPU compromised SARCs’ properties, leading to higher residual monomers and negatively impacting hGFs’ viability, healing, and gene expression. Careful material selection and polymerization techniques are crucial to minimize adverse effects on surrounding tissues.Clinical significanceClinicians should exercise caution when using LPUs and SARCs, especially when polymerizing through zirconia. This will help optimize the physical and chemical properties of SARCs and minimize potential adverse effects on the surrounding gingival soft tissues.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-024-04941-z.Keywords: Periimplantitis, Periodontitis, Zirconia, Prosthesis, Resin cement, Crown, HEMA, GDMA, Microhardness, Surface roughness, Residual monomer

Introduction

Self-adhesive resin cements (SARCs) are widely used in restorative and prosthodontic dentistry due to their efficiency and time-saving benefits. By eliminating the need for separate etching and bonding steps, SARCs simplify the cementation process to a single clinical step [1, 2]. Despite this simplification, SARCs maintain adequate bond strength and physical properties [3]. Additionally, SARCs enhance ease of use and reliability, making them a valuable option for clinicians looking to streamline procedures while ensuring high-quality outcomes. SARCs incorporate conventional monomers such as Bis-GMA, UDMA, HEMA, GDMA, and TEGDMA [4, 5]. These monomers influence reactivity, viscosity, shrinkage, and physical properties of the resin and are toxic to gingival fibroblasts [6–9]. Inadequate light-polymerization leading to free residual monomers can exacerbate cytotoxic and genotoxic effects on soft tissues [10–14], increasing the risk of peri-implant mucositis and peri-implantitis [15, 16]. Poorly polymerized cements can reduce gingival fibroblast viability, cause post-operative sensitivity, gap formation, and create a plaque-friendly environment, enhancing cariogenicity [17–19].

Contemporary adhesive dentistry hinges on achieving optimal bond strength, physical properties, and biocompatibility of resin cements, heavily influenced by the quality of the light-polymerizing unit (LPU) used [11]. Clinicians face challenges selecting the most suitable LPU due to variations in spectral radiant power, light tip diameter, and radiant emittance across options [20]. These differences can impact polymerization efficacy, reducing bonding strength of SARCs and potentially harming surrounding tissues [11, 21]. Adequate light-polymerization is vital for long-term stability and success of indirect dental restorations [22]. Existing evidence primarily focuses on the impact of conventional quartz-tungsten-halogen (QTH) and light-emitting diode (LED) LPUs on SARCs’ properties [23–25]. QTH LPUs are associated with several drawbacks, including excessive heat generation, broad-spectrum light emission, and inconsistent polymerization depth. These issues can result in inferior physical properties of SARCs and potential problems with monomer release [26]. Studies have shown that resin cements polymerized with QTH LPUs often exhibit a lower degree of conversion compared to those polymerized with LED LPUs, likely due to the higher energy density of the latter [27]. In contrast, LED LPUs offer several advantages, including reduced heat output, stable light intensity, and more targeted wavelengths [20]. However, resin cements have shown variable responses to different LED LPUs and polymerization protocols [28]. With the extensive range of LED LPUs currently available, there is a significant gap in understanding how various LED LPUs influence the biological responses of surrounding tissues and the physical and chemical properties of SARC, both of which are crucial for achieving optimal clinical outcomes. This study aimed to assess how two LED LPUs affect SARCs under simulated clinical scenarios, evaluating polymerized cements’ physical and chemical characteristics and SARCs’ cytotoxicity on human gingival fibroblasts (hGFs). The hypothesis was that exposure of SARCs to in vitro radiant emissions from DemiPlus, particularly when polymerizing through zirconia, would result in SARCs with inferior microhardness and surface roughness, increased residual monomers, and more adverse effects on viability, wound healing, and gene expression in hGFs compared to those exposed to Elipar.

Materials and methods

The project was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee at the Faculty of Dentistry, Chulalongkorn University (No. 090/2023, HREC-DCU 2023-079) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Cement specimen preparation

In this study, two different LPUs were used: DemiPlus (Kerr Corporation) and 3 M Elipar DeepCure-S LED Curing Light (3 M ESPE). The light output was measured using a dental radiometer (Demetron LED Radiometer; Kerr Corporation) before each independent repeat of every individual experiment. According to ISO 10650, the maximum allowable radiant exitance for light-curing units (LCUs) is 4,000 mW/cm² within a wavelength range of 380 to 515 nm, with permissible deviations of no more than ± 20% from the manufacturer’s stated value [29]. While the minimum irradiance required can vary based on material specifications, some manufacturers recommend a minimum of 500 mW/cm² [30] and others suggest up to 1,000 mW/cm² for optimal clinical conditions [31]. Both the DemiPlus and Elipar devices were confirmed to operate within these acceptable ranges. The light output measurements are detailed in Table 1.

Preparation of cement eluates

Immediately after polymerization, the prepared cement discs were immersed in Dulbecco Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 50 mg/ml streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 24 h according to ISO 10993-12 [32]. Both undiluted and diluted concentrations of cement eluates with cell culture media were used in this study.

Physical properties testing

Microhardness

Microhardness was measured by using Vickers microhardness tester (FM-810; FUTURE-TECH). Six hardness readings were taken from evenly spaced locations across the central region of each cement disc surface. Two independent experiments were conducted, with each experiment performed in duplicate [33]. The measurements were made on the surface of the cement disc that was in contact with the Mylar strip, simulating the closest proximity to the LPU tip. These measurements were taken immediately after preparing the cement discs, applying a force of 0.20 kg-force for 15 s. Surface roughness

The specimens’ average surface roughness values (Ra) were determined with a surface profilometer (Talyscan 150; Taylor Hobson Ltd.). Surface topography was analyzed using the TalyMap Universal program. The tracing area was 2 × 2 mm² with a cutoff length of 0.025 mm. The measurements were conducted on the cement disc surface that was in contact with the mylar strip. Each surface underwent 30 random measurements at 1,000 μm/s with 600 μm intervals on the y-axis. Two independent experiments were conducted, with each experiment performed in duplicate [35].

Residual monomer determination

A standard curve was established using differing concentrations of freshly prepared standard monomers as follows: Bis-GMA, UDMA, HEMA, GDMA, and TEGDMA (Sigma-Aldrich). Undiluted cement eluate and standard monomers were analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Nexera LC-40; Shimadzu) under the following conditions: column; Luna C18 (Phenomenex), column dimension; 150 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm particle size, mobile phase; methanol: acetonitrile: water (60:15:25), detection wavelength; UV 230 nm, flow rate; 0.8 ml/min; injection volume: 10 µl. Two independent experiments were conducted, with each experiment performed in duplicate [36].

Biological properties Testing

Cell culture

hGFs were isolated from gingival tissues obtained from 3 unrelated healthy donors who underwent oral surgery after approved guidelines and protocols with patients’ consent. Gingival tissues were cut into small pieces and cultured in DMEM at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cell cultures were trypsinized and subcultured after they reached confluence. 3 independent experiments were conducted using cell cultures from passages 4 to 6.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) characterization

Immunophenotyping of hGFs was performed with flow cytometry. 1 × 105 cells were stained with specific monoclonal immunoglobulin G (IgG) against CD44, CD45, CD73, CD90, and CD105 conjugated with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), phycoerythrin (PE), or peridinin chlorophyll (PerCP) protein. After a 30-minute dark incubation at room temperature, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Monoclonal FITC-IgG1 and PE-IgG1 were used as isotype controls. Fluorescent signals were measured and analyzed on a flow cytometer (BD FACSCalibur; BD Bioscience).

Cell viability assay

hGFs were selected as surrogate cells to represent gingival tissues. hGFs were seeded at a density of 5000 cells/well in 96-well plates. After 48 h of seeding, cells were treated with undiluted cement eluates for 30 min and 1:5, 1:10, 1:20, 1:40, and 1:80 dilutions of cement eluates for 24 h. When time points were reached, the eluates were discarded, each well was washed with 1X phosphate-buffered saline and then treated with 50 µl of 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-Diphenyltetrazolium Bromide assay (MTT) reagent (5 mg/ml), followed by incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 1 h in the dark. Formazan precipitations were dissolved using 100 µl of DMSO and gently shaken for 10 min. The absorbance was measured at 570 nm by using a microplate reader (Synergy H1; BioTek). Cell viability was calculated with the following equation [37]: % cell viability = Absorbance specimen - Absorbance blank / Absorbance control - Absorbance blank × 100. The results from the cell viability assay were used to select an appropriate dilution of cement eluate for further experiments, including wound healing and gene expression. Three independent experiments were conducted, each with triplicate specimens [38].

The wound scratch assay

A surrogate wound healing assay was performed as described by Grada et al., 2017 [39]. scratching the confluent hGFs monolayer. For this purpose, hGFs were seeded at a density of 50,000 cells/well in 24-well plates. After reaching more than 90% confluence growth of monolayer culture, cells were treated with the 1:20 dilution of cement eluates. The middle of the wells was scratched by using a 200 ml plastic micropipette. PBS wash was done to remove cell debris. The gaps were observed under a digital microscope (PrimoVert, Zeiss), and images were captured at identical locations after 24, 48, and 72 h. The cell-free areas were analyzed using ImageJ Version 8.0.2. and calculated using the following equation [40]: % wound closuer = (A0 - A1A0) × 100, where A0 is the area measured immediately after scratching, and A1 is the area measured after the mentioned time period. Three independent experiments were conducted, each with triplicate specimens and two measurements were taken per specimen [41].

Gene expression

hGFs were seeded at a density of 200,000 cells/well in 6-well plates. After 48 h, the cells were treated with undiluted cement eluate for 30 min and 1:20 dilution of cement eluate for 24 h. Subsequently, the eluates were removed, and RNA extraction was performed using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA specimens were later converted to cDNA using iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad) to quantify expressions of the following genes: Matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2), Nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (NRF2), Heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), and CC chemokine receptor 3 (CCR3). The mRNA level expression was determined using CFX Real-Time PCR Detection Systems (Bio-Rad). Primer sequences for quantitative reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) were provided in Supplementary Table S1. Three independent experiments were conducted, each using duplicate specimens [42].

Raw data from all experiments are provided in Supplementary File S1.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software Inc.) was used for statistical analysis. A normality test was conducted to determine the distribution of the specimens. Parametric data, including measurements of residual monomers, microhardness, and surface roughness were analyzed using One-Way ANOVA followed by Šidák multiple comparisons test. Non-parametric data, including cell viability assays, wound scratch assays, and gene expression, were evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis H test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. Statistical analysis was concluded based on P < .05 as a significance. Data were demonstrated as mean ± standard deviation.

Results

Physical properties of SARCs

The microhardness of Maxcem polymerized with DemiPlus was significantly reduced compared to Elipar, both when direct light-polymerized (P = .003) and light-polymerized through zirconia (P < .001). The same observation was noted in RelyX when directly polymerized (P < .001). Additionally, polymerizing Maxcem and RelyX through zirconia with both LPUs resulted in significantly decreased microhardness compared to directly polymerized specimens (P < .001) (Fig. 2A). The surface roughness values of both Maxcem and RelyX polymerized through zirconia were significantly higher than those of specimens directly polymerized (P < .001). Additionally, when polymerized through zirconia with DemiPlus, their surface roughness values were significantly higher than those polymerized with Elipar (P < .001) (Fig. 2B). These findings indicated that the presence of zirconia and the use of DemiPlus compromise the hardness and increase surface roughness of SARCs.

Residual resin cement monomer of polymerized SARCs

HPLC was used to quantify the amount of residual monomers in cement eluates prepared under different polymerization conditions. The retention times of each standard monomer observed in this study are demonstrated in Fig. 3A, including HEMA (2.517 min), GDMA (3.300 min), TEGDMA (4.258 min), UDMA (6.991 min), and BISGMA (13.075 min). HEMA, GDMA, and TEGDMA were detected in Maxcem and RelyX eluates after 24 h of immersion in cell culture media while UDMA and Bis-GMA were not detected in any of the cement eluates tested in this study (Fig. 3B-D). Maxcem directly polymerized with DemiPlus showed significantly higher residual concentrations of HEMA (P = .008) and GDMA (P = .035) than those in Maxcem directly polymerized with Elipar. Furthermore, polymerizing through zirconia resulted in significantly increased residual concentrations of HEMA and GDMA for both LPUs (P < .001). RelyX polymerized through zirconia with DemiPlus revealed significantly higher residual TEGDMA concentrations compared to polymerizing with Elipar (P = .003) (Fig. 3D).

Viability of hGFs exposed to SARCs eluates

The hGFs used in this study exhibited a fibroblast-like morphology and expressed characteristic MSC markers CD44, CD73, CD90, and CD105 while lacking expression of the hematopoietic marker CD45 (Supplementary Figure S1). The acute exposure of cement eluates to hGFs, simulated by treating hGFs with undiluted cement eluates for 30 min, demonstrated that direct light-polymerization of Maxcem with DemiPlus led to significantly lower viable cells compared to polymerizing with Elipar (P = .014). Additionally, the cell viability of Maxcem polymerized through zirconia by DemiPlus was not significantly different from direct light-polymerization (P > .999), whereas polymerizing through zirconia with Elipar significantly reduced cell viability compared to direct light-polymerizing (P < .001). Regarding RelyX, the analysis revealed no significant differences in cell viability between light-polymerization with both LPUs, irrespective of the polymerizing conditions (direct light-polymerization: P = .515, light-polymerization through zirconia P = .610). Under all of the polymerizing conditions, Maxcem showed fewer viable cells compared to RelyX. Specifically, the differences were significant with direct light-polymerization using Elipar (P = .021), direct light-polymerization using DemiPlus (P < .001), light-polymerization through zirconia using Elipar (P < .001), and light-polymerization through zirconia using DemiPlus (P = .002) (Fig. 4A).

The short-term effect of exposure of hGFs to cement eluates was mimicked by treating hGFs with various concentrations of cement eluates for 24 h. The results revealed that exposure of hGFs to Maxcem eluate, regardless of polymerization conditions or LPUs used, led to a significant reduction in the cell viability in a dose-dependent manner (P < .001). Additionally, DemiPlus showed a lower degree of cell viability compared to Elipar at an identical concentration of the cement eluate (P < .001). When polymerizing Maxcem through zirconia with either LPU, significantly reduced cell viability was observed even at lower cement eluate concentrations (1:5 for Elipar: P < .001, 1:10 for DemiPlus: P < .001), compared to direct curing without the zirconia screen (undiluted for Elipar: P < .001, 1:5 dilution for DemiPlus: P < .001) (Fig. 4B-C). For RelyX, cell viability at all concentrations directly polymerized with Elipar or DemiPlus was comparable to the untreated control group. However, light-polymerization through zirconia with DemiPlus significantly decreased cell viability in undiluted RelyX eluate compared to Elipar and the untreated control (P < .001) (Fig. 4D-E).

Wound healing ability of hGFs exposed to SARCS eluates

On day 1, after exposure to Maxcem eluate, both specimens polymerized with DemiPlus or Elipar showed a significantly slower wound closure rate than the control group (P < .05), as evaluated by the wound scratch assay. Additionally, Maxcem polymerized with DemiPlus resulted in significantly delayed wound closure compared to Elipar both when direct light-polymerized (P = .014) and light-polymerized through zirconia (P < .001). Zirconia also delayed wound healing of both LPUs (P < .001). For RelyX, the wound closure rate was comparable to the control, except for those polymerized with DemiPlus through zirconia, showing a significantly reduced wound closure rate on day 1 (P = .024) (Fig. 5A). On day 2, the control group demonstrated almost 100% wound closure. The results for Maxcem were similar to those on day 1, except for Maxcem directly polymerized with Elipar, which showed comparable wound healing as the controls (P > .999). In contrast, all RelyX specimens showed wound closure comparable to the controls (P > .999) (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Fig. S2). By day 3, both cements under all conditions exhibited complete or nearly complete wound closure, except for Maxcem polymerized with DemiPlus through zirconia. This group showed significantly reduced wound closure compared to the control (P < .001), directly polymerized specimens (P = .002), and specimens polymerized with Elipar (P = .014) (Fig. 5C).

Gene expression of hGFs exposed to SARCs eluates

After 30 min of exposure to cement eluates, a significant decrease in NRF2 expression was observed in hGFs treated with Maxcem polymerized with DemiPlus compared to those polymerized with Elipar (direct light-polymerized: P = .033, light-polymerized through zirconia: P < .001) (Fig. 6A). Similarly, HO-1 expression was significantly downregulated in hGFs treated with Maxcem polymerized with DemiPlus compared to those polymerized with Elipar (direct light-polymerized: P = .039, light-polymerized through zirconia: P = .007) (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, an increase in CCR3 expression was observed in Maxcem polymerized through zirconia with DemiPlus compared to those polymerized with Elipar (P = .015) (Fig. 6C). No significant differences were observed in MMP-2 expression in any cement specimens (P > .99) (Fig. 6D). After 24 h of exposure to cement eluates, no significant changes were observed between the two different LPUs used (P > .99) (Supplementary Fig. S3A-D).

Discussion

The study unequivocally shows that exposing SARCs to various LPUs and simulated clinical scenarios resulted in notable changes in their physical properties, accepting the hypothesis. Moreover, released residual monomers from SARCs adversely affected the viability, wound healing, and gene expression of hGFs, serving as a surrogate for gingival tissue (Fig. 7). This simulated clinical exposure mimicked the impact on periodontal tissues at crown margins, especially with excessive residual cement. Using a 2 mm thick zirconia screen replicated the maximum crown preparation depth, further simulating the in situ clinical environment.

Light-polymerizing Maxcem through zirconia with DemiPlus LPU compromised SARCs’ properties, leading to higher residual monomers and negatively impacting hGFs’ viability, healing, and gene expression

The 30-minute MTT assay revealed reduced cell viability in hGFs exposed to Maxcem cement polymerized with DemiPlus compared to Elipar. The cytotoxic effect appears to intensify with longer incubation periods, likely due to the gradual release of toxic monomers from the SARCs. Previous studies have shown that the cytotoxic effects of SARCs on fibroblasts increase over 72 h [37, 43]. Further research is essential to explore the cytotoxicity of SARCs over extended periods to better understand their long-term impact on gingival tissue during restorative procedures.

Maxcem demonstrated higher cytotoxicity compared to RelyX, with a dose-dependent reduction in cell viability regardless of the LPU used. Even at a low dilution of 1:10, Maxcem specimens polymerized with DemiPlus via zirconia exhibited significantly higher cytotoxicity compared to Elipar. This suggests that Maxcem polymerized with DemiPlus through zirconia was the most cytotoxic group under all conditions. According to ISO 10993-5, a reduction in cell viability of more than 30% after at least 24 h of incubation is classified as a cytotoxic effect [40]. We observed that undiluted Maxcem, whether light-cured directly or cured through zirconia, reduced the viability of hGFs to below 10%. In contrast, RelyX did not exhibit this effect, indicating that Maxcem may be cytotoxic to hGFs.

Additionally, Maxcem specimens showed delayed wound healing compared to both the control and Elipar groups at 24 h, while RelyX exhibited comparable wound healing to the control group, except when polymerized with DemiPlus through zirconia, which showed delayed healing. This supports the conclusion that RelyX has higher biocompatibility compared to Maxcem. One study reported that extending polymerization time reduces SARCs’ cytotoxicity [44], suggesting that choosing an optimal polymerization period, appropriate LPUs, and clinical parameters can reduce tissue injury from SARCs’ toxic chemicals.

Resins commonly used in restorative dentistry, such as Bis-GMA, UDMA, HEMA, GDMA, and TEGDMA, are known to be toxic to gingival fibroblasts [7–9]. Apart from their chemical composition, factors like concentration and whether they are used singly or in combination affect their cytotoxicity [5]. This study found that polymerizing with DemiPlus significantly increased concentrations of specific monomers (HEMA and GDMA in Maxcem, TEGDMA in RelyX), likely contributing to observed high cytotoxicity and impaired wound healing. In clinical practice, resin cements release residual monomers shortly after application [36], with SARCs polymerization through a zirconia increasing residual monomer release, possibly due to attenuated light transmission delaying the polymerization process [45]. The degree of conversion (DC) serves as a direct indicator of the efficiency of the polymerization process. A higher DC signifies that a greater proportion of monomers have successfully bonded to form polymer chains, reflecting a more complete polymerization reaction. Previous studies have demonstrated a negative correlation between the levels of resin monomers detected in eluates and the degree of conversion, with higher DC typically correlating with lower monomer release [46]. In our investigation, the absence of Bis-GMA, a component of RelyX, suggests that it may have been fully polymerized during the curing process. Additionally, elevated TEGDMA levels were observed when SARCs were cured through zirconia, suggesting that zirconia may interfere with the complete polymerization of these cements. This interference could result in a lower DC and potentially increase monomer release.

Gingival fibroblasts produce pro-inflammatory cytokines in response to stimuli [13]. Light-polymerized Maxcem with DemiPlus (via zirconia) was the most cytotoxic, increasing CCR3 expression, potentially attracting inflammatory cells [47]. NRF2 and HO-1 are crucial in the cellular response to oxidative stress. NRF2, a transcription factor, regulates the expression of genes involved in antioxidant defense and detoxification. During oxidative stress, NRF2 becomes activated and induces the production of protective enzymes like HO-1. HO-1, a potent antioxidant enzyme, helps mitigate oxidative damage [48]. Components within SARCs can sometimes lead to increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in target cells, resulting in oxidative stress [49, 50]. This oxidative stress can trigger an inflammatory response, potentially affecting tissue health. Previous studies have demonstrated that acute oxidative stress can suppress NRF2 expression [51]. Our findings align with this, as we observed a significant reduction in NRF2 levels in Maxcem cured with DemiPlus compared to those cured with Elipar. This suppression of NRF2 is likely due to the increased oxidative stress generated by DemiPlus during the curing process. Consequently, we also observed a significant downregulation of HO-1, a downstream target of NRF2, when Maxcem was polymerized with DemiPlus compared to Elipar at 30 min. This reduction in HO-1 is consistent with the suppression of its upstream regulator, NRF2, indicating a disruption of the cellular antioxidant response.

Vickers hardness testing is a widely used, non-destructive method for assessing the hardness of dental cements. This technique can provide indirect insights into the mechanical properties of dental cements [52]. However, the hardness values obtained can vary, influenced by factors such as the filler content in SARCs and the specific experimental conditions [53]. Previous research has documented Vickers Hardness Number (VHN) microhardness values for SARCs following light-polymerization ranging from 15 to 55 [52, 54], which is consistent with the results observed in our study. Previous research has linked the energy density of LPUs with the microhardness of dual-cure resin cements [55], supported by this study where DemiPlus-polymerized cement showed lower microhardness and increased surface roughness, especially with Maxcem. High cytotoxicity of Maxcem aligns with findings indicating that high surface roughness hampers cell viability [35]. To minimize bacterial adhesion and promote hGFs growth, the surface roughness should ideally be an Ra value of 0.2 μm or lower [56]. Although significant differences were observed among our specimen groups, all measured values remained below this threshold. Regardless of LPUs or SARCs used, a zirconia screen decreased cement microhardness, consistent with a previous study [57]. Our study used the Mylar strip to ensure a consistent and repeatable surface, facilitating a reliable evaluation of different light polymerization methods and conditions. We assessed surface roughness at the micron level and the biological response of hGFs. Significant differences were observed between SARCs cured with Elipar versus DemiPlus and between those cured with and without zirconia. These findings suggest that variations in micro surface roughness may impact the biological response of hGFs.

Elipar’s constant light emission contrasts DemiPlus’s Periodic Level Shifting (PLS), claimed to prevent overheating [58]. This study, the first to compare these two technologies on SARCs and hGFs, suggests subpar properties of DemiPlus-polymerized SARCs. Despite improved beam profile homogeneity, DemiPlus’s polymerization performance remains inferior, indicating beam characteristic may not be critical for SARCs’ optimal polymerization. DemiPlus features a turbo light guide, unlike Elipar’s conventional light guide. The turbo light guide initially increases irradiance but loses effectiveness rapidly due to light dispersion with distance. This design may contribute to poorer properties when polymerizing through a zirconia screen, increasing distance between LPU and SARCs [59]. Extending irradiation period could ensure consistent energy density across LPUs [27].

The current study findings reveal that DemiPlus-polymerized SARCs exhibited significantly inferior cytotoxicity, gene expression (NRF2, HO-1, and CCR3 in Maxcem), monomer release (HEMA, and GDMA in Maxcem; TEGDMA in RelyX), and physical properties compared to Elipar-polymerized counterparts. While the long-term success of adhesive restorations can be impacted by numerous factors, LPUs selection is critical for clinical success in terms of achieving optimal physical properties and biocompatibility of SARCs, minimizing adverse effects on the healthy gingival tissues. Furthermore, the choice of cement is also critical; RelyX appeared minimally affected by LPUs in terms of their cytotoxicity, wound healing, and gene expression, while Maxcem cement displayed relatively adverse properties especially when used with the DemiPlus pulsed LPU. Based on these findings, it appears that clinicians should judiciously consider the optimal combination of LPUs and SARCs that are used in daily practice for the best clinical outcomes.

This study evaluated only two commercially available SARCs and two LPUs, along with a single type and thickness of zirconia. This limited scope represents a potential limitation. Future studies should include a broader selection of SARCs, LPUs, and zirconia types to provide a more comprehensive understanding of their interactions and optimize clinical applications.

Conclusion

Different LPUs and polymerization methods impact physical, chemical, and biological properties of SARCs. Specifically, DemiPlus, especially when used for polymerizing through zirconia, results in SARCs with inferior microhardness, increased surface roughness, higher residual monomers, and more pronounced adverse effects on viability, wound healing, and gene expression in hGFs compared to Elipar. Clinically, this underscores the importance of carefully selecting both LPUs and SARCs, particularly when polymerizing through zirconia, to minimize potential adverse effects on the underlying gingival soft tissues.
