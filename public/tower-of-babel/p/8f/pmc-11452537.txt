Identifying the “stripe” transcription factors and cooperative binding related to DNA methylation
Authors: Ximei Luo, Quan Zou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
DNA methylation plays a critical role in gene regulation by modulating the DNA binding of transcription factors (TFs). This study integrates TFs’ ChIP-seq profiles with WGBS profiles to investigate how DNA methylation affects protein interactions. Statistical methods and a 5-letter DNA motif calling model have been developed to characterize DNA sequences bound by proteins, while considering the effects of DNA modifications. By employing these methods, 79 significant universal “stripe” TFs and cofactors (USFs), 2360 co-binding protein pairs, and distinct protein modules associated with various DNA methylation states have been identified. The USFs hint a regulatory hierarchy within these protein interactions. Proteins preferentially bind to non-CpG sites in methylated regions, indicating binding affinity is not solely CpG-dependent. Proteins involved in methylation-specific USFs and cobinding pairs play essential roles in promoting and sustaining DNA methylation through interacting with DNMTs or inhibiting TET binding. These findings underscore the interplay between protein binding and methylation, offering insights into epigenetic regulation in cellular biology.

Introduction

DNA methylation can affect transcription factor (TF) binding and histone modifications to activate or inhibit gene transcription. TF dynamic binding is determined by multiple factors, including DNA sequence specificity, DNA modifications (such as DNA methylation), and chromatin states. Previous studies have confirmed that DNA methylation can alter TF binding sequence specificity and affinity, leading to assembly into nucleosomes1–4. For instance, within the methylated DNA context, CEBPB identifies the “TTGCGCAA“5. However, upon DNA demethylation, it exhibits a preference for binding to “TTGCATAA”. During the last decade, cooperative TF binding has become a research hotspot. The technique known as CAP-SELEX6 has been employed to identify instances of cooperative TF binding. To identify the specific characteristics of cooperative TF binding, a computational framework has been developed utilizing in vitro SELEX data7. By re-evaluating previously published data, researchers have successfully identified the binding motifs of integrated transcription factors, considerably advancing the understanding of TF cobinding8–11.

However, it is important to note that many approaches used to identify cooccurring11–13 TFs rely heavily on TF motifs. Although these approaches have yielded valuable insights, they face limitations in the context of DNA epigenetic regulation. DNA epigenetic modifications, such as DNA methylation, can alter TF binding motifs and affect TF-DNA interactions14,15. Additionally, the interplay between TFs and the chromatin context can influence their binding properties to DNA, leading to complex and context-dependent regulatory outcomes. Interestingly, researchers have observed an intriguing phenomenon in vivo where a substantial number of DNA sequences exhibit TF binding motifs, but the actual binding of TFs fails to occur16. This phenomenon remains largely unexplained, suggesting that additional factors beyond the presence of the binding motif alone are required for TF binding to occur in vivo.

Indeed, relying solely on motif distributions to infer TF cooperativity is prone to instability. Previous studies have revealed that DNA methylation affects the binding preference of TFs3,17,18. Additionally, pioneering DNA-binding TFs can influence chromatin status and facilitate the recruitment of other TFs. While DNA methylation’s impact on TF binding and the extensive of TF co-occurrence are acknowledged, exploration of the diverse functions and modular behaviors of TFs under epigenetic regulation remains relatively limited. Understanding how these epigenetic mechanisms impact the functionality and coordination of TFs is an area that deserves increased attention. By unraveling the differential functions and modular behavior of TFs with epigenetic regulation, we can gain deeper insights into the intricate mechanisms governing gene expression and cellular processes. Further exploration of this research holds significant potential for advancing our understanding of transcriptional regulation.

In this study, we propose a statistical methodology to analyze 2254 DNA binding TF and protein ChIP-seq profiles and 9 WGBS profiles in the ENCODE19 and NCBI databases. Our investigation focuses on identifying universal “stripe” TFs and cofactors (USFs), exploring their cooccurrences, and pinpointing specific modules exhibiting notable sensitivity to DNA methylation. Utilizing ChIP-seq for examining co-occurrence patterns circumvents the limitations associated with TF motifs, enhancing the reliability and practicality of our research findings. In addition, we have developed a 5-letter DNA motif calling technique that incorporates epigenetic modifications to study binding efficacy. Introducing an “E” within the motif alongside the four conventional nucleotide bases represents methylated cytosine, enabling a deeper insight into epigenetic alterations present within the DNA sequence. Consequently, employing a statistical approach reinforced by experimental data obtained in an in vivo setting allows for the most effective capture of the regulatory functions of transcription factors, integrating crucial biological contextual information.

Results

The overlap binding pattern of DNA-binding proteins varies with DNA methylation state

Here, we collected a total of 2254 ChIP-seq profiles for TFs and proteins and 9 WGBS profiles for DNA methylation from 9 human cell lines, including H1-hESCs, GM12878, K562, HepG2, HeLa-S3, MCF-7, A549, SK-N-SH, and HEK293. The entire analysis process is shown in Fig. 1. First, the peaks calling from the ChIP-seq are separated based on methylation levels. The cooccurring TF and protein binding patterns are identified within different methylation states. Next, we identified the significant overlap events and calculated universal levels (the ratio of significant overlap events to total proteins). Then, we identified and compared the DNA motifs bound by USFs within different DNA methylation state contexts. USFs vary in DNA methylation states

Through a comprehensive analysis of the stripe pattern shown in Fig. 2a, we used statistical analysis to identify a set of universal “stripe” TFs and cofactors (USFs) operating in distinct DNA methylation states. These USFs exhibited significant overlap ratios, indicating a strong interaction with other proteins. The detailed results can be found in Table 1, where we identified 60 and 19 USFs in the unmethylated and methylated states, respectively. To ascertain the specific USFs in different methylation states, we performed a statistical analysis on the number of interactions for each USF. We introduced the concept of the ‘universal level’ to quantify the proportion of an individual USF’s interactions relative to all TFs in the same cell. By comparing the universal levels of USFs between the methylated and unmethylated states, we identified the specific USFs by applying two criteria: a fold change greater than 2 and a universal level exceeding 0.5. For instance, as illustrated in Fig. 2b, our analysis of the K562 cell line suggested that ZBTB33 is a methylation-specific USF in the context of DNA methylation. In fact, there have been numerous studies proving that Kaiso functions as a DNA methylation-dependent transcriptional repressor that causes local depletion of acetylated histones near its binding sites20–22. In K562, ZBTB33 exhibited significant overlap with the peaks bound by the 213 proteins under DNA methylation, accounting for 70.30% of the proteins examined in this study. ZBTB33 is also identified as methylation-specific USF in HepG2. In A549 cells, JUN, MAX, and SIN3A displayed sensitivity to DNA methylation and solely demonstrated the characteristics of USFs in unmethylated states. In our study, we identified 40 unmethylated-specific USFs, 34 of which were unique. Among the 34 USFs, 22 are TFs, and 12 are cofactors. Furthermore, 5 methylation-specific USFs were identified, 4 of which were unique, all of which were TFs. Of the 38 specific USFs identified in our study (excluding duplicates), 15 were recognized as drivers of advanced cancer processes1. The USFs identified in the 8 cell lines

Further investigation involved analyzing the DNA motifs bound by these specific USFs. For each USF, we individually identified two binding motifs in both DNA-methylated regions and unmethylated regions. Some examples are shown in Fig. 2c. Supplementary Fig. 2 shows all motifs bound by unmethylated-specific USFs. Among the 40 unmethylation-specific USFs, 15 had unmethylated motifs containing CpG, while 16 exclusively bound to “AAAAAA” motifs in unmethylated regions. Interestingly, under methylated conditions, the majority of USFs exhibited significant differences in their binding motifs compared to their unmethylated counterparts. In vitro studies have demonstrated that Kaiso binds to a methylated motif “TCTCGCGAGA”. However, our research reveals an inconsistency in in vivo settings, aligning with previous reports22. Our investigation in K562 cells demonstrates that ZBTB33 exhibits a preference for binding to the unmethylated motif “CGCCTGTATCCCAG”. In methylated regions, the ZBTB33 binding motif includes methylated CpG sites. In HepG2 cells, ZBTB38 was found to bind to 12,958 methylated peaks. Among these, 6,635 peaks contain the “CG” sequence, while 6,019 peaks involve “mCG”. Previous studies have indicated that ZBTB38 exhibits comparable affinities for binding to “mCG” and “TG” motifs23,24. However, a recent ZBTB38 ChIP-seq study defined two consensus DNA motifs of ZBTB3825, both containing mCpG sites. Notably, no TpG-containing motifs were identified. These ChIP-seq studies validated previous SELEX-based studies that identified a unique mCpG consensus motif for the ZBTB38 C-terminal zinc finger domain, which was further demonstrated in cells to exhibit methyl-dependent genomic occupancy26,27. Notably, in HepG2 cells, out of 14,501 ZBTB33 methylated peaks, 6,111 binding sites were discovered to harbor the “TGC” sequence, whereas 2,225 sites were found to contain “mCGC”. The ZBTB33 binding motif in methylated regions consists of two palindromic forms of “CCTG”. Notably, “CCTG” is a subset of the motif “TCCTGCNA”, validated in prior in vitro experiments. Therefore, we propose that in HepG2, under methylation conditions, ZBTB33 directly binds to DNA sequences by recognizing “CCTG”. Meanwhile, in K562 cells, the composite binding sequence is longer, albeit also encompassing the “CCTG” substring. These findings strongly suggest that ZBTB33’s DNA binding exhibits cell-specificity, aligning with the conclusions drawn in other research studies22,28.

While KDM2A exhibits an affinity for non-methylated CpG sites and ZNF574 for “AAAAAA” in unmethylated regions, the presence of DNA methylation notably influences the binding of these proteins to “CCTG” motifs in HepG2 cells29. Of particular interest, ZBTB33, identified as a methylation-specific USF, features a “CCTG” DNA binding motif within HepG2 cells. Notably, the substantial overlap between the methylated binding peaks of KDM2A and ZNF574 with ZBTB33 indicates a complex interaction pattern, suggesting that in the context of DNA methylation, KDM2A and ZNF574 are dependent on ZBTB33 for their interactions with DNA within HepG2 cells (see Fig. 2d). In addition, the variations in the DNA motifs bound by ZBTB33 in K562 and HepG2 cells further underscore the cell type-specificity of USF motifs, consistent with the findings of Peggy J. Farnham et al.22. The motifs of USFs may be influenced by interactions with other proteins. Moreover, the methylation-specific USFs did not exhibit significant numbers of CpGs in the DNA motifs in either the methylated or unmethylated context.

In our study, we also investigated the chromatin states of the regions bound by three USFs with different methylation statuses (as shown in Fig. 2e). In the unmethylated state, KDM2A, ZNF574, and ZBTB33 mainly bind to promoter regions in HepG2 cells. Conversely, in the methylated state, they primarily bind to the enhancer region. This finding implies that USFs may play distinct roles in various DNA methylation contexts. For instance, KDM2A primarily identifies the unmethylated region of CpG in promoters to enhance transcription. Conversely, in methylated contexts, KDM2A has been shown to promote DNA methylation by inhibiting TET2 binding30. Therefore, we speculate that this regulation potentially extends to the entire TF interaction network. Although our study successfully identified these USFs, which are crucial for interactions related to DNA methylation, they do not directly interact with DNA methylation and require other proteins or transcription factors to exert their biological effects together.

The cobinding of regulatory proteins varies with DNA methylation state

In this study, we calculated the cosine similarity between regulatory protein pairs. To improve the reliability of identification, the cobinding pairs were identified based on significant cosine values and overlap ratios through statistical analysis. These cobinding proteins were consistently involved in gene regulation. By examining cobinding events in the context of DNA methylation, we discovered a total of 884 interactions across 9 cell lines. Additionally, we identified 1476 interaction events within unmethylated states. As depicted in Table 2, each cell exhibited more protein‒protein interactions in the unmethylated state than in the methylated state. Thus, DNA methylation generally tends to suppress TF binding. Interestingly, we identified 1145 pairs (after cross-cellular deduplication, 1122) that exclusively bind to unmethylated DNA regions. Through comparison, we identified 538 unique interactions that appeared only in the DNA methylation state across 9 cell lines. To gain further insights into the interactions specific to DNA methylation, we conducted motif analysis by extracting DNA sequences from the interacting regions of 30 protein pairs that appeared in two or more cell lines. Figure 3a shows some samples. All motifs of the 30 protein pairs are shown in Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4. The cobinding protein pairs exhibit differential binding preferences concerning GC contents and CpG Obs/Exp ratios in different methylation contexts. As Fig. 3b shows, the GC contents and the CpG Obs/Exp ratios of the DNA motifs bound by the methylated-specific protein pairs were greater than those of the unmethylated-specific protein pairs. The cooperative binding modules vary in methylation states

In addition to investigating multiple TFs and cofactor cooperatives, we explored the interactions among multiple regulatory proteins by constructing a network. The network represents the maximum linked subgraph of the proteins’ cooperative (Supplementary Fig. 5). We observed the presence of common TF and cofactor groups across different methylation states. For instance, the interaction of CTCF-SMC3-RAD21 in SK-N-SH cells and CTCF-SMC3-RAD21-ZNF143 in GM12878 cells remained consistent regardless of the DNA methylation state. Conversely, specific TF groups, such as USF1-USF2-MAX-E2F6, have also been identified in the context of DNA methylation in H1 cells. Additionally, certain reports in the literature have described the coenrichment of these four TFs in methylated DNA35,36. However, it is important to note that due to limitations in the current ChIP-seq data on TFs and cofactors, we were unable to conduct a more comprehensive quantitative analysis of these TF and cofactor networks. Nevertheless, our research findings suggest the presence of distinctive TF and cofactor groups associated with DNA methylation, providing valuable insights for further investigations into the regulatory mechanisms influenced by DNA methylation.

Discussion

Previous studies have shown that DNA methylation can affect the interaction between DNA and regulatory proteins, thereby altering the sequence preference and affinity of transcription factors for binding to DNA2,17. Therefore, we inferred that DNA methylation may also affect the interactions among DNA-binding proteins. Therefore, in this study, we employed statistical methods and developed a 5-letter DNA motif recognition algorithm to investigate the interactions among TFs and cofactors within varying DNA methylation states. We identified 79 USFs and 2360 cobinding pairs based on the ChIP-seq profiles. Notably, DNA methylation may introduce variations in the DNA motif bound by the protein. Therefore, motif matching methods have limitations in identifying the cobinding pairs between proteins with DNA methylation. The method based on ChIP-seq profiles to identify the cobinding pairs among proteins can more accurately reflect the real situation in vivo. In this study, Homer’s de novo motif identification approach was utilized to extract motifs from DNA regions with varying methylation statuses. It is imperative to highlight that only the top-ranked motif was selected for thorough analysis and interpretation within this manuscript. When encountering scenarios with multiple binding motifs, the focus was placed on identifying the predominant motif. During the co-binding motif analysis, motifs identified using Homer were extended by 12 base pairs on both flanks to encompass longer motifs effectively. Nevertheless, it is essential to acknowledge that this approach may not always identify multiple motifs simultaneously when they are coexistent.

We also identified DNA methylation-specific and unmethylation-specific USFs and cobinding pairs. Through additional analysis of the DNA motifs bound by them, we observed that in the context of unmethylation, USFs typically directly bind to CpG. However, in the context of methylation, although USFs bind to methylated regions, the binding sites usually lack CpGs. In the motif analysis of the cobinding protein pairs, we also observed that these unmethylated-specific TF pairs exhibited binding regions with higher GC and CpG Obs/Exp ratios. The number of CpGs in DNA with methylation states bound by proteins is relatively low. Methylated heterochromatic regions bound by proteins are located within enhancers and the gene body and interspersed between genes rather than in regions associated with promoters (see Fig. 2e).

The behavior of TF recognition and binding to DNA methylation sites has always been of great concern. In vitro experiments have validated that TFs can directly recognize and bind methylated CpG with substantial affinity. In this study, we also found that transcription factors bind to methylated DNA in vivo. USFs and cobinding pairs were recognized in different DNA methylation states. These specific USFs interact with extensive proteins within methylated or unmethylated contexts and are key epigenetic regulators. For example, the DNA methylation-specific USF ZBTB33 was identified in K562 and HepG2 cells. Within the DNA methylation context, ZBTB33 has been shown to bind to many other proteins. Perhaps the recognition and binding of these TFs to methylated DNA requires the assistance or mediation of ZBTB33. Numerous studies have corroborated the involvement of ZBTB33 in the biological regulation of DNA methylation in K562 cells37,38. CTCF has been proven to directly bind to DNA methylation in vitro and in vivo. In our study, we also observed that CTCF can bind to methylation peaks. In K562, under DNA methylation, the overlap degree between CTCF and ZBTB33 is higher than that under non methylation. Previous reports have highlighted the interaction between CTCF and ZBTB33 in the presence of DNA methylation, demonstrating an interplay between these two factors. Moreover, research also indicates that the Kaiso-CTCF interaction can exert a negative regulatory impact on CTCF insulator activity39,40. The presence of USFs suggests that the process by which proteins bind to methylated DNA may also have synergistic and hierarchical relationships41. There may be some TFs that play a role similar to that of pioneer TFs, preferentially binding directly and then cobinding to TFs and cofactors indirectly. The USFs identified in this study are worthy of attention in the pathogenesis of future epigenetic diseases or drug development processes.

In addition, there are three distinct TF families that bind to methylated DNA, including the methyl-CpG-binding domain (MBD) family, zinc finger (ZnF) proteins and SET and RING finger-associated (SRA) domain proteins. In our study, the methylation specific USF ZBTB38 and ZBTB33 are all ZnF proteins. The TFs engaged in cobinding pairs in methylation regions are not only ZnF but also bHLH, such as USF1-USF2. The TF structure feature is crucial for the recognition and binding of DNA methylation and deserves further research. Besides, we find that proteins involved in methylation-specific cobinding protein pairs can interact with DNA methyltransferases to promote DNA methylation status or inhibit TET binding to maintain DNA methylation status. These protein pairs are designed to maintain DNA methylation and heterochromatin structure. However, due to constraints imposed by the current profiles of TF and/or protein ChIP-seq, conducting a more comprehensive investigation is not currently feasible. Our research revealed that the interactions among DNA-binding proteins are mediated by DNA methylation.

Conclusions

In this study, we conducted an integrated analysis of ChIP-seq and WGBS profiles to explore the intricate protein binding patterns within various DNA methylation states. Our investigation led to the identification of 79 notable universal “stripe” proteins that exhibited extensive interactions with other proteins. Notably, we highlighted the unique interactions of 4 proteins exclusively within the context of DNA methylation. Moreover, we delved deeper to delineate distinct protein pairs displaying methylation-specific or unmethylation-specific cobinding tendencies. Leveraging a novel 5-letter DNA motif identification model, we revealed intriguing variations in DNA sequence preferences among cobinding proteins across different methylation states, particularly by observing changes in CpG content. Interestingly, despite the binding of proteins to methylated DNA regions, we observed low CpG content in those regions, with proteins predominantly binding to non-CpG sites. Our findings suggest the presence of diverse regulatory protein binding patterns associated with distinct DNA methylation states, underscoring the complex regulatory landscape of cellular processes. Furthermore, the identification of the USF hints at a regulatory hierarchy existing within these protein interactions. Proteins involved in methylation-specific USF and cobinding pairs play crucial roles in promoting and sustaining DNA methylation through interactions with DNMTs or inhibiting TET binding mechanisms. This study sheds light on the dynamic interplay between protein binding patterns and DNA methylation states, offering valuable insights into the regulatory mechanisms governing epigenetic processes in cellular biology.

Identification of the binding and cobinding epigenetic modification motif

First, the DNA-binding peaks of one TF and/or protein are divided into two parts based on their methylation states. The corresponding DNA sequences were extracted from the binding regions. The extracted DNA sequences were then analyzed for motif calling using the “findMotifs.pl” tool. This tool identifies the presence of protein binding motifs within DNA sequences. Then, the identified motifs were matched with the original binding regions to determine the precise binding sites of the proteins. The binding site with the highest matching score is considered the most accurate depiction of protein binding.

By aligning the matched binding sites with the DNA methylation profile, the DNA methylation levels at the binding sites were determined. This information provides insights into the epigenetic modifications occurring at these sites. The DNA motifs are visualized through a 5-letter representation, with the letter “E” used to denote a methylated “C” (cytosine). Additionally, to provide a more comprehensive display, 3 base pairs (bp) of padding were added to both sides of the motif. For cobinding motifs with epigenetic modifications, DNA sequences were selected from the overlapping regions. The “findMotifs.pl” tool was used to identify the binding DNA motifs from these DNA sequences. The motif reconstruction process incorporates DNA methylation information. To enhance the display of the cobinding motif, a padding of 12 bp is added both downstream and upstream.

Identification of the regulatory protein module

After identifying the cobinding pairs, the next step was to analyze and compare the modules in different methylation states. The “max_cliques” function from the “igraph” package is used for this purpose. The maximal cliques are complete subgraphs within a larger graph where every pair of nodes is connected by an edge. By applying the “max_cliques” function to analyze the cobinding pairs, we identified modules or clusters of TFs and cofactors demonstrating a high level of cobinding within specific methylated states. This analysis helps to uncover potential regulatory networks or functional modules associated with different DNA methylation patterns. Comparisons of the composition, size, connectivity, or other characteristics of the identified modules between different methylation states were performed. This analysis provides insights into how DNA methylation influences the formation and dynamics of regulatory modules involving cobinding TFs and cofactors. By studying the variations in module composition and properties across different methylated states, researchers can acquire a more profound understanding of the regulatory mechanisms involving TFs and DNA methylation. This analysis aids in elucidating the intricate interplay between epigenetic modifications and gene regulation processes.

Statistics and reproducibility

The statistical analyses were conducted using R. The estimation of data distribution parameters and hypothesis testing was carried out utilizing the ‘fitdist()’ function in the ‘fitdistrplus’ package (version 1.1-11). Motif identification was performed using HOMER (version 3.1), applying the command ‘findMotifs.pl <fasta file > -fasta <output directory > -noknown -S 2’. Visualizations were created using ‘ggplot2’. The motif logo was generated using ‘ggseqlogo’ (version 0.1).
