Climate and land use change potentially drives southern range contraction and latitudinal shift in Caucasian Lynx
Authors: Raziyeh Shahsavarzadeh, Mahmoud-Reza Hemami, Urs Breitenmoser, Mohammad Ali Adibi, Deniz Mengüllüoğlu, Mohsen Ahmadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate and land use change are reshaping species distributions globally, yet their combined effects remain underexplored, particularly for wide-ranging carnivores. Here, we assessed the joint impact of climate and land use change on the future distribution of the Caucasian lynx (Lynx lynx dinniki) across its entire range. Using a fine-tuned MaxEnt model, we projected habitat suitability to 2050 under two socio-economic scenarios (SSP1–2.6 and SSP5–8.5), incorporating current and future climate and land cover data. Habitat patches were classified as breeding or stepping-stones based on size thresholds, and landscape metrics were used to evaluate structural changes. Our projections indicated substantial habitat loss (31–33%) and gain (14–34%), with the most severe losses expected in the southern range, particularly in the Zagros and southern Anatolian mountains. Center of gravity analysis suggested a northward shift in suitable habitats, partially compensating for southern declines. However, increased fragmentation and isolation were evident under all scenarios. These findings emphasize the urgency of conservation strategies that maintain connectivity, protect stepping-stone habitats, and support adaptive land use planning. Enhancing corridor networks and addressing conservation gaps, especially in emerging northern ranges, will be critical for ensuring the long-term viability of the Caucasian lynx in a rapidly changing landscape.Keywords: Climate change, Habitat fragmentation, Connectivity, SDM, Conservation planningSubject terms: Ecology, Zoology, Climate sciences, Ecology, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Climate change and land-use transformation are reshaping ecosystems around the globe, driving species to shift their ranges, alter behaviors, and face increased extinction risk1. While climate-driven range shifts and fragmentation have been well-documented2,3, the combined effects of changing climate and human land use, such as agriculture, infrastructure, and urban expansion, remain less explored4. Yet in a human-altered world, habitat loss and isolation compromise population persistence, reducing opportunity for movement, gene flow, and adaptation5–7. Wide-ranging species, such as large carnivores, are particularly vulnerable8. These animals depend on interconnected habitat networks to secure territories, undertake seasonal movements such as mate searching, maintain genetic diversity, and sustain their capacity to adapt to climate change across heterogeneous landscapes9,10. However, the combined pressures of climate change and land use transformation increasingly disrupt these connections, undermining the functionality of ecological corridors and restricting the ability of populations to migrate and track shifting climatic conditions11,12. However, conservation efforts often tackle climate and land-use issues separately, leaving gaps in understanding how their interaction shapes species vulnerability13,14.

The Eurasian lynx (Lynx lynx) serves as a compelling example. Its extensive range, vast home ranges, and specialist behaviors make it sensitive to landscape change15,16. Despite considerable recovery in parts of Europe, driven by intensive conservation actions, forest regeneration since the late nineteenth century, the recovery of ungulate populations (Breitenmoser et al.17,), favorable legislation, shifts in public attitudes, and coordinated management policies18,19, many lynx populations remain at risk. This recovery has also been supported by legal protection under the Bern Convention (Council of Europe, 1979) and by translocations from autochthonous east-central European populations18,20–22. As apex predators, lynx play a vital role in ecosystem function, regulating herbivore numbers and maintaining ecological balance23. Among Eurasian lynx subspecies, the Caucasian lynx (L. l. dinniki) occupies a fragmented montane range stretching from western Turkey to Caucasus and Iran24. Although the Eurasian lynx is classified as ‘Least Concern’ on the IUCN Red List, Caucasian lynx populations are regionally constrained and exposed to multiple pressures, including habitat fragmentation, poaching, retaliatory killings, and climate-driven habitat shifts in suitable habitat24,25. Most Caucasian lynx sub-populations are currently genetically diverse26–28, however, limited connectivity among some of its sub-populations might intensify isolation, contributing to reduced gene flow and marked genetic differentiation across the subspecies’ range29. Changes in climate and land use not only alter the extent of habitats, but also their internal structure, leading to smaller, more isolated patches and reduced connectivity30,31. Yet many predictive models still assume stable, homogenous habitats, ignoring spatial complexity. Tools from landscape ecology, such as landscape metrics, provide a way to understand how habitat structure and function shift over time, offering critical insights for conservation32,33.

Here, we combined species distribution modeling (SDM) with landscape ecology to assess the future distribution of the Caucasian lynx under two climate scenarios (SSP1–2.6 and SSP5–8.5) and corresponding land-use projections to 2050. We focused on how habitat suitability, fragmentation, and connectivity are likely to change, using metrics designed to quantify patch-level dynamics. By doing so, we aim to support conservation planning in anticipating future risks and promoting sustained persistence of this apex predator in a changing world.

Results

The optimized MaxEnt model for species distribution, configured with linear and quadratic features (LQ), a regularization multiplier of 1, and a AICc of 512.34, showed strong predictive performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.89 and a True Skill Statistic (TSS) of 0.62. Among all variables, annual precipitation had the highest permutation importance, followed by slope, maximum temperature of the warmest month, annual mean temperature, shrub cover, and forest cover. Response curves indicated that lynx presence was most probable in steep, shrub-dominated habitats receiving 200–300 mm of annual precipitation, whereas increasing temperatures led to reduced habitat suitability (Fig. 1).

Response curves illustrating the influence of the most important variables on the habitat suitability of the Caucasian lynx in the region. Curves were generated using MaxEnt model with LQ feature class and RM set to 1.

Geographically, the most suitable habitats were concentrated in the Alborz Mountains of northern Iran and the broader Caucasus region, forming large, continuous patches. In contrast, the Zagros Mountains in western Iran and the mountainous regions of eastern and central Anatolia in Turkey exhibited more fragmented and discontinuous habitat patterns (Fig. 2). Binary conversion of the continuous suitability map and removal of very small patches resulted in 296,800 km2 and 311,151 km2 of identified breeding and stepping-stone habitat, respectively, represented 7.38% and 7.74% of the total study area (Table 1). Under future projections to 2050, breeding patches are expected to lose 31.32% and 32.85% of their extent under SSP1–2.6 and SSP5–8.5, respectively, while gaining 18.12% and 24.21%. Stepping-stone patches showed similar patterns, with 31.61% and 33.16% habitat loss and 18.64% and 23.22% gain under the same scenarios. Notably, the more severe SSP5–8.5 scenario was associated with greater habitat gain. However, the greatest habitat losses were consistently projected for southern regions, particularly southwestern Iran and southern Turkey (Figs. 3, 4).

Discussion

Projected suitable habitats

Species distribution models often treat climate and land use/land cover (LULC) change independently, despite increasing evidence that their interaction can amplify ecological impacts34. Many projections rely solely on climate variables, assuming static landscapes or minimal influence from land use35. While this may be sufficient at broad spatial scales, it often fails to capture localized habitat fragmentation and human-driven pressures, particularly for wide-ranging species. As Gilpin36 noted, the compounded effects of climate and land use change pose a significant extinction risk, yet their combined impacts remain underexplored for large carnivores. Taken together, our results show that climate–land use interactions are reshaping the Caucasian lynx’s distribution by driving southern habitat loss, increasing fragmentation, and elevating the importance of connectivity and stepping-stone habitats for long-term persistence.

Our results demonstrated that the distribution of the Caucasian lynx is influenced by both climatic and structural landscape variables. Annual precipitation, slope, temperature extremes, and shrub cover were consistently the most important predictors of habitat suitability. These preferences reflected the lynx’s affinity for cooler, wetter, and topographically complex environments, traits shared with other regional carnivores such as the Persian leopard and brown bear37,38. While the lynx is generally found in remote, mountainous habitats with limited human access39, sensitivity to human land use varies regionally. For instance, populations in Central Europe avoid human activity during the day but tolerate it at night40. Across Europe, lynx rely more strongly on refuge habitats during daytime, when human activity is highest, particularly in open landscapes with little cover as well as in rugged terrains where effective refuges are available near human settlements41. In contrast, populations in Norway and Switzerland show relatively high tolerance to human presence throughout the day42.

The scale of analysis is likely a key factor in these observations. At the regional scale of this study, climatic factors dominated model predictions. However, at finer scales, land use may play a more prominent role in shaping resource availability, prey density, and anthropogenic disturbance43. The Caucasian lynx exhibits ecological flexibility, occupying a range of habitats, from deciduous forests and heaths to steppes and Mediterranean shrublands44–46. Despite this adaptability, our projections indicated a modest overall shift in total habitat extent, but with pronounced regional changes. The greatest habitat losses were predicted in the southern edge of the species’ range, particularly the Zagros Mountains and southern Anatolia. These declines may be partially offset by projected gains at higher latitudes, suggesting a northward shift in distribution, a pattern consistent with observations in other carnivores under climate change47,48. Similar trends have also been reported in terms of altitudinal movement8,49,50. However, range loss in the south presents serious conservation concerns. These areas already consist of fragmented and isolated patches, limiting movement and gene flow51,52. Without adequate stepping-stone habitats or functional corridors, recolonization and demographic recovery become unlikely. This represents a broader and well-recognized limitation of studies relying on land-use projections, and is particularly critical in regions experiencing rapid land use change, such as Zagros31 and southern Turkey53. The ability of the Eurasian lynx to track shifting climates and colonize newly suitable habitats will depend heavily on conservation measures that maintain functional corridors, stepping-stone areas, and, where appropriate, support future translocations54. These projected changes underscore the growing importance of landscape connectivity, a theme we address in detail below. However, potential connectivity only becomes effective when protected landscapes also ensure adequate prey availability46, retain permeable habitat, and minimize human-induced mortality30,55. In this context, protected areas play a central role. Although protected areas alone cannot fully safeguard lynx, their habitat, or their prey54, they have repeatedly been shown to be critical, particularly in regions where poaching remains a persistent threat54,56,57. Establishing new functional protected areas, combined with coordinated strategies to tackle wildlife crime and improve public attitudes toward lynx22,58, could substantially strengthen conservation outcomes.

Human infrastructure can alter the spatial behavior of both predators and their prey. In human-modified landscapes, prey species may shift their space use toward open or edge habitats in response to altered risk perception and resource availability, a pattern documented for ungulates in European systems59. In the Caucasian lynx range, however, lagomorphs, particularly hares, represent a primary prey resource, and their availability strongly influences lynx habitat use across Mediterranean, forest-steppe, and alpine ecosystems46,60. Human disturbance and hunting pressure can modify hare activity patterns and space use, potentially displacing them toward human-modified landscapes61. Such prey-mediated shifts may indirectly draw lynx into closer proximity with human land use, especially where natural habitat and prey distributions are fragmented. Although not a primary focus of our results, increased overlap between lynx activity and human-dominated areas may elevate exposure to human-related mortality risks, including conflict, poaching, and retaliatory killing, as documented across the species’ range62–65. Although our models do not explicitly include prey dynamics, these behavioral responses provide an important ecological context for interpreting projected habitat fragmentation.

Ecological challenges in newly gained habitats

The projected northward shift introduces its own set of challenges. One major concern is ecological mismatch, particularly in terms of predator–prey dynamics and interaction with large carnivores2. Within its range, the Caucasian lynx has historically coexisted with other apex carnivores such as wolves, brown bears and leopards across fragmented and human-influenced landscapes in Iran, the Caucasus, and Turkey. Although such sympatry may lead to spatial, temporal, or dietary overlap and thus potential for competition or kleptoparasitism, as observed in the Dinaric Mountains66, current evidence does not indicate clear fitness consequences for lynx arising from interspecific competition (Wang et al.67,, Martinkova68,). Examples from European lynx populations illustrate how such interactions can shape space use and behavior, but their relevance to the Caucasian lynx should be interpreted cautiously given regional differences in community composition and human pressure. Likewise, if main prey species in the Caucasian lynx range fail to shift their distributions in pace with changing climate conditions, spatial mismatches could reduce hunting efficiency, as shown in other systems69. While empirical data on prey range dynamics in this region remain scarce, the lynx’s documented dietary flexibility, including the use of lagomorphs, birds, and mid-sized ungulates, may partially buffer against localized prey shortages46,70,71. Nonetheless, shifting ecological dynamics and declining prey populations could increase human-wildlife conflict and further complicate conservation efforts72.

Because gene flow in the Caucasian lynx is mediated primarily through dispersal among habitat patches, the projected fragmentation patterns described above are most relevant at the subpopulation level. From a population genetic perspective, the primary conservation concern for the Caucasian lynx lies not in low genetic diversity or imminent inbreeding, but in the maintenance of functional connectivity among subpopulations within the subspecies’ range. Although genetically distinct from other Eurasian lynx subspecies, the Caucasian lynx retains high levels of genetic diversity, reflecting a long evolutionary history and large historical range26,28. Current evidence does not indicate widespread genetic erosion or inbreeding across studied subpopulations. However, continued habitat fragmentation may increase isolation among regional populations, potentially constraining gene flow over time. Conservation efforts should therefore prioritize connectivity among Caucasian lynx subpopulations, rather than connectivity with other subspecies, to sustain long-term genetic exchange and demographic stability. Similar within-subspecies management approaches, including targeted connectivity restoration and genetic monitoring, have proven effective for other Eurasian lynx populations, such as the Dinaric lynx, where genetic rescue and individual-based modelling have informed successful conservation strategies (Pazhenkova et al., 2025).

Landscape configuration and composition dynamics

Our landscape analysis revealed consistent trends indicative of increased fragmentation. Both breeding and stepping-stone patches experienced a reduction in mean area and an increase in patch number, reflecting subdivision of once-continuous habitats73,74. Additionally, metrics such as Euclidean nearest neighbor distance and the Splitting Index increased, signaling greater isolation and subdivision of habitat patches. The Contiguity Index, a measure of spatial connectedness, declined under future scenarios, indicating reduced internal cohesion of habitat patches. Interestingly, the aggregation index increased, suggesting that smaller patches are forming in clustered patterns. This apparent contradiction is likely due to the emergence of small patch clusters in formerly contiguous landscapes, particularly in the south. While aggregation index reflects spatial clustering, it does not equate to functional connectivity or ecological integrity. Therefore, interpreting it in isolation may be misleading75. For the Caucasian lynx, these changes translate into smaller, more isolated breeding areas and fewer effective dispersal routes, particularly for females with limited movement across unsuitable habitat.

These trends indicate increasing fragmentation within the Caucasian lynx range and mirror broader conservation concerns reported for other Eurasian lynx subspecies, many of which face similar threats from habitat fragmentation and limited connectivity24,54,76. Habitat fragmentation increases both the number and the length of habitat edges77 and is closely linked to elevated mortality risk in lynx. In Norway, Basille et al.78 showed that lynx hunting mortality increased with road density within lynx home ranges, and was often higher than would otherwise be expected close to small forest roads78–80. Premier et al.81 corroborate the prevailing wisdom that conserving habitat and preventing further fragmentation are fundamental ingredients for lynx conservation.

Dispersing individuals are particularly vulnerable, as the loss of high-quality habitat forces them to move through risky, human-dominated areas when seeking new territories. Fragmented landscapes also reduce prey availability, increase intraspecific and interspecific competition for prey, and heighten exposure to threats such as poaching, all of which contribute to increased mortality82. These impacts directly influence population dynamics and undermine long-term viability83,84. Poaching further amplifies the consequences of fragmentation. In many parts of Europe, illegal killing not only depresses population numbers but also creates a functional “barrier effect,” by forming invisible barriers to lynx dispersal81, thereby limiting movement and reducing opportunities to find mates85,86. The resulting isolation restricts gene flow and accelerates inbreeding and demographic instability, ultimately compromising individual fitness and population resilience87.

Although lynx can occupy varied habitats, their ability to move across fragmented landscapes remains constrained, especially for philopatric females that are less likely to traverse unsuitable areas76,84. Climate change compounds these issues by altering habitat quality and disrupting corridor functionality10. Ensuring long-term viability will depend on maintaining habitat linkages that support species movement and adaptation in a rapidly changing environment88. We analyzed climate and land use impacts separately for breeding and stepping-stone patches. While most landscape metrics followed similar directional trends, some key differences emerged. Breeding patches exhibited a steeper reduction in mean area, whereas stepping-stone patches showed a more marked decline in patch count. This divergence is ecologically meaningful: stepping-stone habitats are critical for functional connectivity, enabling dispersal and range shifts in fragmented landscapes89–91. Their role becomes even more pivotal under future climate and land use scenarios, particularly in landscapes shaped by human activity92.

Although this study focused on structural habitat connectivity, effective conservation of the Caucasian lynx also demands integration of demographic, behavioral, and ecological data89 and identifying variables that not only affect the social acceptability but also play a central role in the socio-political conflicts surrounding this species93. Interspecific interactions, including competition, prey availability, and predator–prey dynamics, further influence carnivore distributions16. The lynx occupies a mid-trophic position, feeding primarily on roe deer in the north and hares in the south25. Climate change could differentially affect these prey species, potentially disrupting predator–prey dynamics. Comparative studies assessing how shifting distributions impact lynx-hare-ungulates interactions would offer valuable insights into species adaptability and ecological resilience. Future research should incorporate demographic and movement data, such as dispersal distances, sex- and age-specific mobility94, and the permeability of landscape barriers, to support mechanistic dispersal modeling and the production of dispersal heatmaps. These approaches would offer deeper insight into colonization potential and functional connectivity. However, such analyses were beyond the scope of this study due to the current lack of comprehensive, range-wide demographic data for the Caucasian lynx.

Many species distribution models still emphasize climatic suitability, often neglecting spatial heterogeneity introduced by land cover or vegetation structure. By incorporating both current and projected land cover layers, this study was able to evaluate how large-scale landscape elements may either constrain or facilitate habitat suitability across time and space. This dual-layer approach provides a more comprehensive picture of the challenges and opportunities facing the Caucasian lynx in a changing environment. A key limitation of our analysis concerns the availability and resolution of future land-use projections. Although SSP1–2.6 is considered less likely in many assessments, we included it alongside SSP5–8.5 to bracket a reasonable range of socio-economic pathways and associated land-use pressures, rather than to predict any single “most probable” future. Moreover, global land-use datasets are typically produced at coarse spatial resolutions and must be resampled for use at approximately 1 kmscales, which can introduce uncertainty, particularly for fine-scale features. As a result, our projections should be interpreted as broad regional indications of habitat change and conservation concern rather than precise local forecasts. Nonetheless, coupling the best available land-use layers with climate data remains essential for capturing cumulative pressures on wide-ranging carnivores, and our findings offer a precautionary basis for identifying areas where adaptive land-use planning will be most critical for the Caucasian lynx.

Another limitation is the absence of explicit variables describing human linear infrastructure, such as roads and railways. Although we included urban and agricultural land-cover classes as coarse proxies for anthropogenic pressure, they do not fully represent the barrier effects and permeability gradients created by transportation networks, factors known to strongly influence movement and landscape connectivity in large mammals95. The lack of spatially explicit, scenario-based projections for future road and rail expansion prevented us from incorporating these effects at a comparable scale to the climate and land-use layers. Future studies should therefore integrate dynamic infrastructure scenarios to more realistically capture fine-scale connectivity constraints and their interaction with climate and land-use change.

Another source of uncertainty relates to the selection and number of background points in MaxEnt models. While the default setting of 10,000 random background points is widely used and generally appropriate for species with broad distributions, the optimal number of background points can depend on the size of the presence dataset and the extent of the study area. In cases where presence records are numerous, a higher background-to-presence ratio may better characterize environmental availability and reduce potential inflation of evaluation metrics such as AUC. In our study, we mitigated these effects by using spatial block cross-validation to account for the spatial structure of presence and background data, but we acknowledge that background point density remains a general limitation of presence-only modeling approaches and warrants further exploration in future work.

Conservation gaps and implications for long-term persistence

Our conservation gap analysis shows that less than 10% of both breeding and stepping-stone habitats for the Caucasian lynx currently fall within protected areas. Even under future climate and land-use scenarios, protection increases only marginally, leaving over 90% of suitable habitat unprotected across all projections. This limited coverage aligns with patterns observed for other large carnivores, which typically occupy extensive, heterogeneous landscapes that extend well beyond the boundaries of formal protected areas16,38. For a wide-ranging felid such as the lynx, inadequate protection of key habitats may jeopardize population viability, particularly where habitat loss, fragmentation, and human expansion are progressing rapidly37.

The lack of protected stepping-stone habitats is especially concerning. Although smaller, these patches facilitate dispersal and maintain functional connectivity between core breeding areas, processes essential for genetic exchange, demographic rescue, and long-term persistence in isolated populations30,54. Previous studies have emphasized the importance of movement corridors and intermediate patches for sustaining metapopulation structure and recolonization dynamics in fragmented mountain systems54,84. Our findings indicate that many of these critical linkage zones remain largely unprotected, suggesting that current protected area configurations may be insufficient to maintain connectivity under continued climate and land-use change. While protection percentages increase slightly under future scenarios, this pattern is driven primarily by the contraction of suitable habitat rather than by meaningful expansion of the conservation network. Similar paradoxes have been documented for other species under global change, where habitat loss inflates the apparent proportion of protected habitat without improving real conservation security31. For the Caucasian lynx, projected northward shifts and rising fragmentation may further concentrate suitable habitats in regions that currently lack formal protection, echoing trends observed for other carnivores experiencing climate-driven range reorganization96. Given these constraints, expanding formal protected areas across the entire range may be neither feasible nor sufficient, particularly in regions with strong socio-economic pressures. Instead, conservation strategies should prioritize the identification and safeguarding of key linkage zones, multispecies corridors, and climate refugia that support dispersal and long-term persistence30,88. Integrating land-use planning tools, such as ecological networks, community-managed conservation areas, and transboundary corridor initiatives, may help bridge protection gaps, especially where formal protected areas are sparse or poorly connected97.

Conclusion

The Caucasian lynx faces growing challenges from the combined effects of climate change and land use transformation across its range. By integrating climatic and land-use projections within a single species distribution modeling framework, this study provides a region-wide perspective on how these pressures may jointly reshape the future spatial context of the subspecies. Rather than indicating a simple loss of suitable conditions, our results point to a reorganization of habitat, with suitable areas shifting northward and becoming increasingly fragmented, especially toward the southern margins of the range. These changes have important conservation implications. Increasing fragmentation and isolation are likely to constrain dispersal and reduce the effectiveness of natural recolonization processes, even where climatically suitable habitat persists. In this context, maintaining functional connectivity emerges as a central challenge. Our findings highlight the disproportionate importance of stepping-stone habitats in sustaining movement pathways and buffering the impacts of landscape subdivision under future scenarios.

Looking ahead, effective conservation of the Caucasian lynx will require coordinated, landscape-scale strategies that extend beyond the boundaries of existing protected areas. Priorities include safeguarding key linkage zones, integrating climate adaptation into land-use planning, and aligning conservation actions across political boundaries. By identifying areas where habitat configuration is most likely to limit future persistence, this study provides a spatial foundation for proactive planning aimed at enhancing connectivity and resilience. Ultimately, ensuring the long-term persistence of the Caucasian lynx will depend on adaptive management approaches that anticipate change, rather than reacting to habitat loss once fragmentation thresholds have already been crossed.

Materials and methods

Study area and data collection

This study encompasses the full distribution range of the Caucasian lynx, spanning Iran, Turkey, and the Caucasus region (including Armenia, Azerbaijan, Georgia, and southern Russia), covering approximately 4,022,108 km2. The area includes two global biodiversity hotspots—the Irano-Anatolian and the Caucasus—characterized by diverse ecosystems, topography, and climates. In Iran, the Alborz and Zagros Mountains create climatic gradients from temperate Hyrcanian forests to arid central deserts98,99. Turkey’s landscapes range from the forested Black Sea coast to Mediterranean shrublands and mountainous interiors100. The Caucasus region, situated between the Black and Caspian Seas, includes both the Greater and Lesser Caucasus ranges, with climates varying from humid lowlands to alpine zones.

Species presence data were compiled from multiple sources. In Iran, most observations were recorded by rangers from the Department of Environment and collated by a study co-author (MAA). Data from Turkey and the Caucasus were gathered through field surveys, databases, and reports by other co-authors (UB, DM). Additional records were retrieved from GBIF and iNaturalist. After removing duplicates within a 5 km radius to reduce spatial clustering and autocorrelation97, we retained 359 presence points for species distribution modeling (SDM).

Environmental variables

Environmental predictors were grouped into three categories: land cover, climate, and topography. The environmental variables included in the analysis were selected based on well-documented ecological determinants of habitat suitability for Eurasian lynx and other large felids, as supported by the literature25,30,40. Land cover data for 2015 and 2050 were obtained from the Globio4 database at 10 arc-second resolution (~ 300 m)101. Future scenarios followed Shared Socioeconomic Pathways SSP1–2.6 (sustainable) and SSP5–8.5 (high emissions)102. SSPs comprise five narratives that describe alternative trajectories of global socioeconomic development and can be combined with RCP-based climate scenarios103. SSP1 (Sustainability) depicts a world emphasizing sustainable growth, equity, and strong land-use regulation. SSP2 (Middle of the Road) follows historical trends, with moderate land-use regulation, slow declines in deforestation, and delayed climate cooperation. SSP3 (Regional Rivalry) reflects a fragmented world marked by nationalism, weak land-use governance, low crop yields, and continued deforestation. SSP4 (Inequality) features stringent land-use regulation in high-income countries, but ongoing tropical deforestation in low-income regions,agricultural and land-use emissions remain only partially priced, limiting incentives for avoided deforestation and afforestation before 2030. SSP5 (Fossil-Fueled Development) envisions rapid economic growth and globalization, with incomplete land-use regulation and delayed climate action; tropical deforestation persists but declines gradually over time104.

Broadly speaking, land-use regulation is strongest under SSP1, moderate under SSP2 and SSP5, limited under SSP3, and uneven (strong in wealthier regions but weak in poorer ones) under SSP4105. To ensure consistency with climate data, land-cover layers were resampled to 30 arc-seconds (approximately 1 km2) using a nearest-neighbor resampling approach. We focused on five dominant land cover types: forest, shrub, grassland, cropland, and urban areas. Proportional coverage was calculated within a 2.5 × 2.5 km moving window using focal statistics97. Climatic variables were obtained from the CHELSA database (https://chelsa-climate.org) at 30 arc-second resolution (approximately 1 km2). We included six bioclimatic variables: annual mean temperature (Bio1), temperature seasonality (Bio4), maximum temperature of the warmest month (Bio5), minimum temperature of the coldest month (Bio6), annual precipitation (Bio12), and precipitation seasonality (Bio15). For future projections, we used ensemble means from five general circulation models (GFDL, IPSL, MPIESM, MRIESM, and UKESM) under both SSP scenarios. Topographic data were derived from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM), including elevation and elevation standard deviation (as a proxy for terrain ruggedness) within a 2.5 × 2.5 km grid39. After selecting the environmental variables according to their relevance in the movement dynamics of the species, multicollinearity among them was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) via the ‘usdm’ R package106,all variables with VIF < 6107 were retained for modeling.

Although prey availability is a major determinant of carnivore distribution, comprehensive occurrence data for the lynx’s diverse prey species (e.g., Lepus sp., Capreolus capreolus, Ovis sp.) were not available across the full study region for future projections. Consequently, we relied on land-cover classes such as forest, shrubland, and grassland as proxies for prey habitat, which are well-established correlates of lynx occurrence. The species’ documented dietary flexibility46 further suggests that areas identified as suitable based on structural and climatic variables are likely to provide adequate foraging opportunities, even if future prey communities shift in composition.

Species distribution modeling

We applied MaxEnt, a machine-learning algorithm well-suited for presence-only data and small sample sizes108. To optimize model performance, we fine-tuned two key parameters: feature classes (FC) to allow flexible transformations of predictors, and regularization multiplier (RM) to control model complexity109,110. Using the ENMeval package111, we tested five FC combinations (L, LQ, LQH, LQHP, LQHPT) and six RM values (0.5 to 3.0, in 0.5 steps). It is important to note that the RM controls the balance between model complexity and predictive performance. Increasing the regularization multiplier imposes a stronger penalty on model complexity, producing simpler (flatter) predictions,overly large values risk underfitting, which can reduce inference about habitat suitability and the relative importance of predictors112. Likewise, among the available feature classes, threshold features are generally discouraged, as they impose abrupt binary transitions in response curves, hence arbitrary response to each predictor109, that create unrealistic habitat boundaries. For these reasons, our feature class selection emphasized simpler and more ecologically interpretable combinations, including linear (L), quadratic (Q), and hinge (H) features. Product (P) features were included in two configurations to evaluate their added value, while threshold (T) features were tested only once for comparison. This strategy allowed us to explore a suitable range of model complexities while maintaining biological realism and minimizing overfitting. We used 10,000 background points and spatially partitioned the dataset into five blocks with the blockCV package113 to reduce spatial autocorrelation. Because our occurrence dataset encompasses the full geographic extent of the subspecies, retaining the default background ensured that the full diversity of environmental conditions was represented without introducing subjective spatial constraints. The best model was selected based on the lowest corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc). Variable importance was assessed through permutation importance in MaxEnt. We converted the continuous suitability output to binary presence/absence using the maximum training sensitivity plus specificity (MSS) threshold114. Model performance was evaluated using the True Skill Statistic (TSS = sensitivity + specificity – 1). Habitat patches were then classified into breeding (≥ 100 km2) and stepping-stone patches (≥ 30 km2), following previous ecological thresholds for lynx dispersal and reproduction84,115.

Assessing climate and land use impacts

To assess future impacts, we projected the current SDM to 2050 under SSP1–2.6 and SSP5–8.5 using the same climate models and land cover datasets. For each scenario, we generated binary suitability maps using the MSS threshold from the baseline model. Breeding and stepping-stone patches were identified for each scenario, and changes in area and spatial configuration were quantified.

To evaluate conservation gaps, we quantified the spatial overlap between predicted suitable breeding and stepping-stone habitats for the Caucasian lynx and the existing network of Protected areas across the region. We obtained the newest version of PA boundaries from national and international sources, including Iran’s Department of Environment, Turkey’s Ministry of Environment and Urbanization, the World Database on Protected Areas (WDPA), and the Key Biodiversity Areas (KBA) dataset. Breeding and stepping-stone habitat layers for both present conditions and future climate–land use scenarios were then intersected with these PA polygons in ArcMap 10.5 to calculate the proportion of lynx habitat currently encompassed within, versus remaining outside, formally designated conservation areas.

Landscape ecology analysis

To understand how habitat configuration might evolve, we applied landscape ecological metrics to breeding and stepping-stone patches under current and future conditions. Six metrics were calculated (Table 3), including patch number, area, contiguity, aggregation, splitting index, and nearest-neighbor distance. These were computed using the “SDMTools” package116, following definitions from McGarigal et al.117. To assess distributional shifts, we calculated the geographic center of gravity (CoG) for each patch type and time period using SDMTools. Differences in CoG latitudes were statistically tested using one-way ANOVA to evaluate potential northward shifts under climate and land use change.
