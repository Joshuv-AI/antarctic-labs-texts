Representativeness of the Danish Blood Donor Study relative to the general population: a cross-sectional assessment
Authors: Jacob Træholt, Dorte Helenius, Lea A. N. Christoffersen, Liam J. E. Quinn, Christina Mikkelsen, Nanna Brøns, Joseph Dowsett, Kathrine A. Kaspersen, Susan Mikkelsen, Torben Hansen, Bo Markussen, Mie T. Bruun, Bitten Aagaard, Thomas F. Hansen, Klaus Rostgaard, Henrik Hjalgrim, Andrew J. Schork, Thomas Werge, Christian Erikstrup, Ole B. ABSTRACT
Cohorts that are non-representative of their target population pose a critical challenge, limiting generalizability and potentially yielding misleading effect estimates. To this end, we conducted a cross-sectional assessment of the representativeness of the Danish Blood Donor Study (DBDS)—a nationwide cohort of 170,000 individuals recruited during visits to blood donation centers across Denmark—relative to the Danish general population aged 18–74 years. Using national register data, we compared demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related characteristics of DBDS participants to those of non-DBDS individuals as of December 31, 2021. Our findings revealed that DBDS participants were more likely to be well-educated, live with an adult of the opposite sex, and have two children. Although individuals living alone were generally underrepresented, young males (18–24 years) and females (18–44 years) in this group were notably overrepresented. In addition, those with high incomes or with employment in finance or insurance were strongly overrepresented, whereas immigrants and rural residents were underrepresented. Participants were less likely to have any recently recorded diagnoses or redeemed prescriptions across most major disease and treatment categories. In particular, individuals with mental, behavioral, or hematological disorders were markedly underrepresented, whereas females (25–44 years) with diagnoses related to pregnancy, childbirth, or the perinatal period—or with redeemed prescriptions for urogenital or hormonal medications—were overrepresented. This characterization of DBDS participants provides context for interpreting results obtained from the cohort and underscores the need for methodological approaches to improve the generalizability of findings derived from non-representative samples.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41941-1.

Introduction

In observational research, non-representative samples remain one of the most pervasive threats to valid inference, potentially distorting study findings and leading to biased estimates of disease risk, prognosis, and treatment outcomes. Such bias often arises when participation is voluntary, because individuals who choose to enroll differ systematically from those who do not—whether the target is a specific subgroup or the population at large. This limitation persists even in today’s large, high-profile cohort resources.

Volunteer-driven cohorts such as the UK Biobank (UKB)1 and the Danish Blood Donor Study (DBDS)2 exemplify the challenge of achieving representativeness of their respective national populations through differing recruitment and participant profiles. UKB, which comprises 500,000 participants, invited all eligible individuals aged 40–69 years residing within a 40-kilometer radius of one of 22 assessment centers across England, Wales, and Scotland. However, these participants are not fully representative of the UK general population in that age group, as they are more likely to be female, live in less socioeconomically deprived areas, and represent healthier lifestyles3. As a result, when not accounting for this, UKB may overestimate health-promoting behaviors or underestimate disease prevalence, thereby challenging its ability to draw conclusions at a population level.

DBDS, comprising 170,000 participants, recruits blood donors aged 18–74 years during their visits to donation centers across Denmark. This recruitment gives rise to a phenomenon often referred to as the healthy donor effect4–6, because only individuals meeting specific health requirements can donate blood. Consequently, the cohort initially reflects a healthier subset of the Danish general population. However, DBDS participants remain in the cohort even if they stop donating, allowing those who later develop disease or encounter circumstances that would otherwise disqualify them from donating to continue contributing data. In addition, prior work suggests that blood donors’ mortality may approach that of the general population as ‘time since last donation’ increases7,8, partially mitigating the healthy donor effect. Nonetheless, DBDS participants may still differ systematically from non-DBDS individuals.

Previous comparisons of Danish blood donors with the general population have provided useful insight into the sociodemographic profile of blood donors9. However, these were limited to comparisons of active donors, irrespective of DBDS participation, and did not incorporate health data. The present study extends this work by including both active and lapsed donors from DBDS, a volunteer cohort nested within the blood donor population. It also examines a broader set of sociodemographic variables along with register data on hospital diagnoses and redeemed prescriptions, thereby enabling a more comprehensive assessment of cohort composition and its implications for etiological research.

Accordingly, we conducted a cross-sectional assessment of the representativeness of DBDS relative to the Danish general population aged 18–74 years. Using national register data, demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related characteristics of DBDS participants were compared to those of non-DBDS individuals as of December 31, 2021. Quantifying the magnitude and direction of these differences provides an empirical foundation for bias adjustment and transparent generalization in future DBDS analyses.

Specifically, these register-based contrasts can inform generalization of DBDS findings to the Danish general population by identifying key covariates and strata that should be prioritized in adjustment, post-stratification, or reweighting approaches, and by highlighting the outcome domains in which additional robustness and sensitivity analyses are most warranted.

Materials and methods

The Danish Blood Donor Study

Established in 2010, DBDS is an ongoing prospective cohort that leverages Denmark’s existing blood bank infrastructure to recruit participants2. During visits to blood donation centers, donors are invited to participate by signing a consent form, completing a questionnaire on lifestyle and self-reported health, and providing a blood sample for research purposes, including extensive genetic testing. To ensure continuously updated and comprehensive data, DBDS introduces a new questionnaire approximately every two years, inviting participants who report for donation to complete it. In addition, DBDS periodically sends out online questionnaires on targeted health topics to all participants, regardless of current donor status.

As of April 2025, DBDS has recruited 170,000 participants, collected more than 450,000 questionnaire responses, and amassed nearly 3 million plasma samples. Additionally, DBDS includes genetic data from 135,000 participants and biomarker data for subgroups of up to 30,000 participants10,11. These data are integrated with comprehensive Danish registers, creating an extensive resource for health research, and enabling in-depth studies of early disease patterns. To be eligible for enrollment in DBDS, individuals must be active blood donors aged 18–74 years—meaning that they weigh at least 50 kg, meet health and behavioral criteria ensuring suitability for blood donation, and are able to speak and understand Danish.

National registers

Since 1968, all residents of Denmark have been registered in the Danish Civil Registration System (CRS)12. As the central population register, it assigns each individual a unique 10-digit personal identification number. This number is used across all national registers, enabling accurate and comprehensive data linkage. Managed by governmental authorities and Statistics Denmark, these registers systematically record a wide range of information on all residents, including demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related information13. However, most registers were established in different years and therefore vary in their historical depth, as seen in Supplementary Table 1.

Using the nationwide registers, we extracted a comprehensive set of 19 variables relevant to describing demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related differences between DBDS participants and non-DBDS individuals. To match the age of possible DBDS inclusion, we only retrieved information for adults aged 18–74 years residing in Denmark on December 31, 2021. This group of individuals will hereafter be referred to as the (Danish) general population.

Demographic variables such as sex, age, civil status, region of residence, and ethnicity were obtained directly from CRS. Sex was obtained from CRS as legal sex and was defined according to the individual’s recorded legal sex as of December 31, 2021. Civil status classifications followed Danish legal definitions, with civil partnerships considered equivalent to marriage. Ethnicity was determined based on Statistics Denmark’s definitions, which classify individuals as Danish origin, immigrants, or descendants, and categorize them by country of origin (see Supplementary Definitions). For this study, countries of origin were further grouped into broader categories, including MENAPT (Middle East, North Africa, Pakistan, and Turkey), following classifications by the Danish Ministry of Immigration and Integration14.

Household composition, including household size and living situation, was determined based on individuals sharing the same address in CRS. The number of children for each individual was determined by identifying those in CRS who had the person’s identification number listed as a parent, without accounting for deceased or non-resident children. Population density data were retrieved from CRS together with publicly available data from Statistics Denmark, with individuals divided into six groups based on their municipal population density (< 50 persons/km2, 50–100 persons/km2, 100–200 persons/km2, 200–1,000 persons/km2, 1,000–5,000 persons/km2, and 5,000 + persons/km2). Years lived abroad was calculated using CRS by summing periods of non-residency.

Educational level, including parental education (if available), was obtained from the Population Education Register15, using all available yearly records from 1980 onward, and linked with CRS to identify parents. Compulsory education refers to primary and lower secondary education, representing the most basic educational level in Denmark. This is followed by either general upper secondary education (lasting two to three years) or vocational education and training (typically lasting two to four years). Higher education is divided into short (around two years, e.g. professional diplomas), medium (three to four years, e.g. bachelor’s degrees), and long programs (five to six years, e.g. master’s degrees). Research education includes the PhD degree and the higher Danish doctorate degree, typically requiring at least three additional years beyond a master’s degree.

Employment status and profession data were derived from the Employment Classification Module16 and categorized according to socioeconomic and industry codes. Yearly personal income, including business income and transfer payments, was extracted from the Income Statistics Register17, and income percentiles were then calculated for the Danish general population as defined previously.

Health data were retrieved from the Danish National Patient Register18 for the full years 2019 to 2021 and from the Danish National Prescription Register19 for the full year 2021. Health conditions were classified according to the chapters of the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10), while medications were categorized using the chapters of the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) Classification System. For each chapter, individuals with at least one recorded diagnosis or redeemed prescription were identified to compare health-related characteristics of DBDS participants to those of non-DBDS individuals.

Statistical analyses

We decided a priori to stratify all analyses by sex and, for variables in which we anticipated notable age-specific effects, also by age group (18–24, 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, 55–64, and 65–74 years). For each variable, we then calculated proportions of DBDS participants and, except for health-related characteristics, estimated the corresponding (adjusted) relative risks (RRs) using a binomial generalized linear model with a log link function.

RRs were estimated relative to an appropriate reference group; for nominal variables, the reference group was specified a priori and chosen to be a substantively interpretable and sufficiently prevalent baseline category to facilitate stable comparisons. All models, except those for sex and age group, were adjusted for age as of December 31, 2021. Given the non-linear effect of age on the log-transformed probabilities of being part of the DBDS cohort, we fitted a natural cubic spline with four degrees of freedom to the age variable. Additionally, Wald 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the obtained standard errors.

To assess potential over- and under-representation of certain population segments among DBDS participants, we compared the calculated proportions to the overall, sex-specific, or sex- and age-group-specific proportions, as appropriate. For example, if 3.25% of all males were DBDS participants, we would expect approximately the same proportion in any male subgroup under random sampling. That is, if 2.31% of all males living alone were part of DBDS, this would indicate underrepresentation relative to the expected proportion.

Proportions of DBDS participants were visualized as smooth functions of age and income percentile by calculating the number of DBDS and non-DBDS individuals at each age (in years) and income percentile, followed by locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS). All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1.

Demographics

For males, the proportions of DBDS participants peaked in the 35–44 age group at 4.44% (RR = 1.314), while the highest female proportion was in the 25–34 age group (4.77%, reference). For both sexes, the lowest proportions were seen in the youngest age group (18–24 years), followed by the oldest age group (65–74 years). Individuals living with one adult of the opposite sex had the highest proportions of DBDS participants across different living arrangements (Table 2), with males at 4.35% (adjusted RR = 1.765) and females at 3.97% (adjusted RR = 1.079), compared to the reference group of those living alone (2.31% and 3.08%, respectively). Males living with one adult of the same sex had a lower proportion at 1.55% (adjusted RR = 0.729), whereas females had a higher proportion at 3.63% (adjusted RR = 1.083), with proportions dropping further for both sexes when living with two or more adults of the same sex. Individuals living with their parents exhibited the lowest proportions.

Likewise, participation varied across household sizes for both males and females (Supplementary Table 2), with the highest proportions observed among four-person households (males: 4.37%, adjusted RR = 1.742; females: 4.28%, adjusted RR = 1.022), relative to individuals living alone (2.31% and 3.08%, respectively). For both sexes, proportions were lowest in large households with six or more persons.

Finally, participation varied by the number of children for both sexes, with somewhat similar but more pronounced trends among males (Table 4). Individuals with two children had the highest proportions of DBDS participants at 4.63% (adjusted RR = 1.900) for males and 4.03% (adjusted RR = 1.106) for females, compared to those with no children (1.96% and 3.05%). Proportions then declined among individuals with three or more children, with the lowest proportions observed for those with five or more children (males: 1.50%, adjusted RR = 0.655; females: 0.87%, adjusted RR = 0.246).

Participants were overrepresented in moderately to densely populated areas (Supplementary Table 4). For males, those living in areas with 200–1,000 persons/km² had the highest proportion of DBDS participants at 4.06% (adjusted RR = 1.445), relative to the reference group (100–200 persons/km²), whereas the highest female proportion was observed in areas with 5,000 + persons/km² (4.11%, adjusted RR = 1.333), though proportions remained high in areas with > 200 persons/km². For both sexes, proportions were lowest in sparsely populated areas (< 50 persons/km²).

Among individuals of Danish origin (reference group), 3.78% and 3.99% of males and females, respectively, were represented in DBDS (Table 5). In contrast, descendants exhibited lower proportions at 0.88% (adjusted RR = 0.304) and 1.10% (adjusted RR = 0.279), while immigrants recorded the lowest proportions at 0.36% (adjusted RR = 0.084) and 0.46% (adjusted RR = 0.097). Males originating from other western countries were represented by 0.63% (adjusted RR = 0.152) in DBDS, while those from MENAPT countries and the rest of the world both had proportions of 0.31% (adjusted RRs = 0.077 and 0.075, respectively). Female participation patterns were similar, though proportions were higher among those originating from other western countries (0.97%, adjusted RR = 0.211).

Notably, individuals of Danish origin were most highly represented among younger age groups (25–44 years), although overall their representation was markedly higher than that of immigrants and descendants (Supplementary Fig. 2). Conversely, immigrants and descendants peaked among middle to older age groups (45–74 years)—a trend also observed for those originating from other western countries, MENAPT countries, and the rest of the world.

Additionally, Supplementary Table 5 and Supplementary Fig. 3 highlight that individuals who spent more than three years living abroad were underrepresented, with proportions of DBDS participants generally decreasing as years spent living abroad increased.

In summary, DBDS participation was slightly higher among females than males and peaked in early-to-mid adulthood. Participants were more frequently living with an opposite-sex adult and were overrepresented among married individuals and those with two children. While living alone was generally associated with lower participation, this pattern reversed in younger males (18–24 years) and in females (18–44 years). Participation also differed geographically, with lower representation in less densely populated areas, and was substantially lower among immigrants and descendants than among individuals of Danish origin.

Socioeconomics

Educational attainment differed significantly between DBDS participants and non-DBDS individuals for both sexes (Table 6). In general, proportions of DBDS participants tended to increase with higher education levels, with some variation across specific groups. Similarly, individuals whose parents had attained a higher education were more likely to be part of DBDS (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7).

In summary, DBDS participation increased with socioeconomic position. Participation was higher with increasing educational attainment and was also higher among individuals with higher parental education. Employed individuals were substantially more likely to be DBDS participants than other employment categories. Health

In general, DBDS participants were less likely to have a recent history of at least one recorded diagnosis in most ICD-10 chapters (Fig. 6). For both sexes, participants were markedly underrepresented in chapters D (Diseases of the blood and blood-forming organs) and F (Mental and behavioral disorders), as were male participants in chapter E (Endocrine, nutritional, and metabolic diseases). In contrast, younger females (25–44 years) were overrepresented in chapters L (Diseases of the skin and subcutaneous tissue), O (Pregnancy, childbirth and the puerperium), and P (Certain conditions originating in the perinatal period)—most notably in the last two chapters.

In summary, DBDS participants were generally underrepresented among individuals with recent hospital diagnoses and redeemed prescriptions across most ICD-10 and ATC chapters. Underrepresentation was most pronounced for mental/behavioral disorders and diseases of the blood and blood-forming organs, and for endocrine/metabolic disorders among males. A key exception was females aged 25–44 years, who were overrepresented for pregnancy- and perinatal-related diagnoses and for prescriptions related to urogenital or hormonal medications.

Discussion

This study provides the most comprehensive comparison to date of the demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related characteristics of DBDS participants relative to non-DBDS individuals, assessing the representativeness of the DBDS cohort in the context of the Danish general population. It is important to note that our analyses include both active and lapsed donors and thus reflect characteristics of individuals enrolled in DBDS at a given cross-sectional timepoint rather than of those actively meeting donor eligibility criteria. Accordingly, the observed differences should be interpreted as the net result of multiple selection mechanisms and subsequent cohort retention, rather than being attributed solely to initial cohort entry.

Overall, DBDS participants differed systematically from the Danish general population across demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related domains. Participation tended to be higher among individuals living in stable household and family structures and among those with higher socioeconomic position (e.g. education and income), whereas immigrants and residents of less densely populated areas were underrepresented. In addition, participants were less likely to have recently recorded diagnoses or redeemed prescriptions across many major disease and treatment categories, consistent with broad health-related selection. The sections below summarize these patterns and their key subgroup-specific deviations.

We observed distinct sex- and age-related patterns among DBDS participants. In contrast to UKB and other cohort studies3,20,21, females were only slightly overrepresented. Representation was lowest among the youngest (< 25 years) and oldest (> 60 years) individuals, consistent with other donor-based cohorts22,23, likely reflecting a combination of donation patterns and age-related eligibility and retention. Individuals living with others were generally overrepresented among DBDS participants, whereas young males (18–24 years) and females (18–44 years) living alone were notably overrepresented—possibly reflecting greater lifestyle flexibility and higher urban concentration among younger adults, which may facilitate blood donation. Additionally, the Region of Southern Denmark has historically invested less effort in recruiting DBDS participants, resulting in fewer participants from this region. Similarly, immigrants and descendants were strongly underrepresented, most likely because blood donors are required to be able to speak and understand Danish.

In contrast, individuals with higher educational attainment, professional occupations, higher incomes, or highly educated parents were overrepresented in DBDS—likely reflecting greater health awareness and willingness (or ability) to engage in research24. This pattern parallels findings from UKB, where participants were more likely to reside in less socioeconomically deprived areas3, and is reinforced by similar observations from other cohorts showing that volunteer participants often exhibit markers of socioeconomic advantage, such as higher educational attainment25–27.

Participants were generally underrepresented across most major ICD-10 and ATC chapters, with particularly marked underrepresentation among individuals with mental/behavioral disorders and diseases of the blood and blood-forming organs. These patterns plausibly reflect both eligibility restrictions and differential capacity or motivation to donate among individuals with poorer health. Notably, females aged 25–44 years were overrepresented for pregnancy-, childbirth-, or perinatal-related diagnoses, and for redeemed prescriptions for urogenital or hormonal medications, consistent with increased healthcare contact during reproductive years.

Although DBDS provides invaluable insights into the blood donor population and serves as a key resource for studying early risk factors for disease, our results show that the cohort differs from the Danish general population. Importantly, representativeness is not “all-or-nothing”: it varies by subgroup and by outcome domain, which means that the relevance of DBDS findings to the general population depends on the specific estimand and the extent to which associations are modified by the differences documented here.

Taken together, these findings indicate where inference to the Danish general population is most likely to require explicit adjustment. In applied DBDS studies, this can be operationalized by defining the target population and index time point, presenting key estimates overall and within relevant strata (e.g. household structure, socioeconomic position, and origin), and using post-stratification or reweighting when selection is likely to affect the estimand—thereby improving the population relevance of DBDS-based estimates. Several analytical techniques have been proposed to address systematic differences between study samples and target populations, with inverse probability weighting (IPW) showing notable promise in large volunteer cohorts. Recent UKB studies have demonstrated that IPW can shift estimates toward population-referenced benchmarks and improve external validity under plausible assumptions28–30. Applying similar approaches in DBDS could strengthen general-population inference while preserving the scientific value of its rich phenotyping (e.g. questionnaires, biomarkers, and genetics). In this context, our results provide empirical guidance on the factors such methods need to address and on where they are most likely to matter in practice.

A key strength of this study is the use of nationwide register data to provide a cross-sectional, population-wide comparison of DBDS participants to non-DBDS individuals across demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related characteristics, including both active and lapsed donors at the index date. Several limitations should also be noted. Our analyses reflect a snapshot as of December 31, 2021, and thus do not fully capture time-varying trajectories (e.g. income changes, evolving health status). Data on parental education are constrained by register completeness, particularly for older generations and immigrants. Residence and population-density measures may introduce misclassification (e.g. urban centers embedded within lower-density municipalities). Finally, analyses based on emigration data, which become less accurate over time and rely on permanent address changes, may not capture shorter or temporary stays abroad.

In conclusion, DBDS is a uniquely valuable resource, but it is not a random sample of the Danish general population—and, as a volunteer cohort nested within the donor population, its composition reflects multiple selection processes. By quantifying the magnitude and structure of these differences across major domains, this study provides both a clearer basis for interpreting DBDS findings and a practical foundation for future analyses that seek to improve generalizability to the Danish general population.
