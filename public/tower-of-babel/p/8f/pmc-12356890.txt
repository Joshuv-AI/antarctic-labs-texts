Substantial improvement of mechanical properties of Zn–Mg alloys to orthopedic implants via rotary swaging
Authors: Qing Zhao, Zonghai Liu, Tuquan Zheng, Long Zhang, Jin Li, Xiaochuan Li, Yu Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe limited strength of unalloyed zinc (Zn) hinders its use in bone repair, where materials must withstand substantial stress. Rotary swaging (RS) serves as a promising technique to enhance metal performance by modifying their internal microstructures. Herein, Zn-0.5Mg alloys undergo room-temperature RS, with their post-processing characteristics, including microstructural features, mechanical performance, degradation behaviors, and biological responses being systematically examined. Findings demonstrate remarkable microstructural optimization and breakdown of lamellar structure following RS. As strain rise, average grain sizes of cast Zn-0.5Mg alloy reduce progressively from 100 μm to 1.10 μm (RS-2), with complete refinement of second phases. This method remarkably boosts the alloy’s ultimate tensile strengths from 163 MPa of cast to 465 MPa of RS-3, while the ductility improved from 2 to 27.8%., satisfying bone implant specifications. Such enhancement stems from multiple mechanisms including grain boundary strengthening, dislocation strengthening, and second phase strengthening. Enhanced processing passes correlate with reduced degradation rates, mainly attributed to diminished localized corrosion from galvanic corrosion between Zn and Mg2Zn11 compounds. Regarding biological performance, as-swaged Zn-Mg alloys demonstrate superior cellular compatibility and bone-forming potential relative to inert titanium. These outcomes offer important references for developing robust Zn-based materials for skeletal reconstruction applications.Keywords: Zn–Mg alloys, Microstructure, Mechanical property, Osteogenesis, Rotary swagingSubject terms: Cell biology, Molecular biology, Biomaterials

Introduction

In recent years, zinc (Zn) and its derivatives have attracted significant attention in biomedical research due to their promising characteristics1,2. Among recently proposed biodegradable metallic materials (BMs), iron (Fe), magnesium (Mg), and Zn-based systems are widely regarded as the most viable candidates3,4. Notably, Zn possesses an electrochemical potential of -0.76 V, intermediate between Fe (-0.44 V) and Mg (-2.37 V), indicating a degradation rate that is neither too rapid nor excessively slow5. This balanced corrosion behavior positions Zn-based materials as potential successors to existing BMs for clinical applications.

For cardiovascular stents or orthopedic implants, ideal BMs should exhibit a yield strength exceeding 200 MPa and an ultimate tensile strength (UTS) above 300 MPa, along with sufficient ductility6,7. Unfortunately, unprocessed Zn falls short of these benchmarks, typically demonstrating a UTS below 30 MPa, necessitating further enhancement before clinical use8–11. Common strategies include elemental alloying and thermomechanical treatment. To date, multiple additives—such as Mg12–14Ca15Sr16Cu17,18Mn19–21Zr22Li23–25Ag26,27Fe28,29and rare earth elements30–36-have been incorporated into Zn-based systems, followed by processing techniques like hot extrusion, rolling, wire drawing, or severe plastic deformation (SPD).

Mg is particularly advantageous due to its biocompatibility and ability to form reinforcing secondary phases. In Zn-Mg systems, Mg2Zn11 precipitates contribute to hardening via dispersion effects37,38. However, prior studies often report a compromise between strength and ductility, primarily due to insufficient refinement of eutectic networks38,39. Jin et al. report that Zn-Mg alloys with low Mg content (< 0.1 wt%) has low tensile strength, below 300 MPa40. Mostaed et al. show that the increased content of Mg leads to the improvement of tensile strength at the expense of ductility (< 5%)39. The loss of ductility is attributed to the difficulty of refining lamellar structure completely. Researchers found that the severe plastic deformation processes, like hydrostatic extrusion and high-pressure torsion, achieve complete refinement of lamellar structure12,41. However, the interface between Zn and Mg2Zn11 particles becomes easier to be activated, resulting in a higher strength in Zn-0.5Mg alloys with lower Mg content. Thus, we finally select the Zn-0.5Mg alloy as a research object. In addition to Mg contents, advanced processing methods capable of fully optimizing these microstructures are essential. Rotary swaging (RS), a prominent SPD method, has proven effective in producing bulk ultrafine-grained (UFG) metals, particularly in Mg-based systems42,43. Given the structural similarities between Mg and Zn alloys, RS may similarly enhance microstructural homogeneity and mechanical performance in Zn-based materials. Despite this potential, reports on RS-processed Zn alloys remain scarce.

In this study, we explore the effects of RS at room temperature on as-cast Zn-0.5Mg alloys. This is because severe plastic deformation at room temperature can achieve significant grain refinement and mechanical enhancement without compromising the alloy’s integrity44,45. Microstructural evolution, mechanical behavior, degradation kinetics, and biological compatibility of Zn-0.5Mg alloys are investigated. By correlating processing parameters with structural modifications, we identify key strengthening mechanisms and corrosion pathways in the developed alloys.

Experimental procedure

Preparation of as-swaged Zn–Mg alloys

The investigated Zn-Mg materials were fabricated through a combined casting and rotary swaging approach. High-purity elemental Zn (99.99%) and Mg (99.99%) were used to produce the initial cast alloy, with ICP-OES analysis confirming a magnesium content of 0.49 wt%. Figure 1 illustrates the RS experimental setup. Initial workpieces measured 100 mm in length and 16 mm in diameter. Room-temperature processing was performed using an X16 rotary swager operating at 25 impacts per second. Workpiece advancement occurred at 1.2 mm per impact stroke. Each subsequent processing stage involved a 0.05 mm greater diameter reduction than the preceding one. Through progressive processing, the initial 16 mm diameter was sequentially decreased to 10 mm (designated RS-1), 8 mm (RS-2), and 6 mm (RS-3), corresponding to calculated true strains of 0.47, 0.69, and 0.98 respectively.

Microstructure characterization

For microstructural characterization, 2 mm-thick discs were obtained from the processed Zn-Mg materials, with their longitudinal axes aligned with the deformation direction. Specimen preparation involved sequential grinding with silicon carbide abrasives followed by final polishing to achieve optical-grade surface finish. A 4% nitric acid-alcohol mixture served as the etchant for revealing microstructural features. Microstructural evaluation was performed using a Hitachi SU8220 field-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM; ThermoFisher, USA) coupled with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy system (EDS; Bruker QUANTAX FlatQUAD, USA). The SEM images were obtained at the accelerated voltage of 5 kV and EDS was conducted at 20 kV. For quantitative analysis of crystallographic features, electron backscatter diffraction measurements (EBSD) were carried out using an Oxford Nanoanalysis EBSD detector to determine grain size distributions and defect concentrations. Transmission electron microscope (TEM; Spectra 200, ThermoFisher, USA) was further used to analyze the microstructures of the RS-3 sample. Before observation, the disk sample with diameter of 10 mm was mechanically ground to 0.1 mm of thickness. Subsequently, specimen was further thinned using a Gatan-695 C ion beam thinner.

Mechanical tests

Mechanical testing of as-swaged Zn-Mg alloys was performed using a test machine (Instron 5969, USA). The tensile specimens had a gauge length of 20 mm, thickness of 2 mm, and a strain rate of 1 × 10− 3 s− 1. The tensile fracture samples were used to observe the fracture morphologies by SEM.

Cytocompatibility

The cytocompatibility assessment of Zn-Mg materials was performed with MC3T3-E1 pre-osteoblasts (ATCC, USA), which were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) supplied with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco) in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. The disc samples have diameters of 6 mm and thickness of 2 mm, with volume ratio of 1.25 mL/cm2. Sample extracts were prepared by submerging test pieces in culture medium for 24 h, followed by replacing the growth medium with these extracts for 12 h cell exposure. Cell suspensions (10,000 cells/mL) were plated in 96-well plates at 100µL per well. Control groups received standard DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C for 72 h with daily medium replacement. For viability assays, CCK-8 solution (DOJINDO, Japan) was prepared by 1:10 dilution in DMEM, then added to wells (100µL/well) for 2 h incubation at 37 °C. Optical density measurements at 450 nm were acquired using a Bio-Rad 680 microplate analyzer (USA), with six replicates per condition. The percentage viability was calculated as: , where ODsample and ODcontrol represent the absorbance readings from test and control groups respectively. The concentrations of Zn and Mg ions in undiluted extracts were measured by using a inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES, Thermo Scientific M Series, USA).

Osteogenic properties

For alkaline phosphatase (ALP) evaluation, identical cell seeding and culture protocols were followed. The osteogenic medium was prepared by mixing 50% extract solution with ALP assay components. Cells underwent differentiation for either 3 or 7 days, with fresh medium provided every two days during the induction period. To examine calcium deposition, Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining was performed after 14- and 21-day differentiation periods. Following the specified induction time, cellular samples were fixed in 3.7% paraformaldehyde, rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and treated with 40 mM Alizarin Red S solution (pH 4.1) according to standard protocols for mineralization quantification.

Statistical analysis

The experimental results were expressed as mean ± standard deviations. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey test was carried out for variance analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results and discussions

Microstructures of Zn–0.5Mg alloys

Microstructural characterization of Zn-0.5Mg alloys in both cast and processed states is illustrated in Fig. 2. The initial cast material (Fig. 2A) exhibited dendritic zinc crystals averaging approximately 100 μm, with interconnected lamellar eutectic networks along grain boundaries. Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (Fig. 2B) confirmed preferential Mg segregation in eutectic regions, consistent with the limited solid solubility predicted by the Mg-Zn phase diagram and subsequent Mg2Zn11 phase formation47. Following RS (Fig. 2C), significant grain refinement was achieved. Processed specimens displayed elongated microstructural features aligned with the deformation axis, resulting from radial compression and axial material flow during processing. Higher magnification examination revealed equiaxed submicron grains surrounded by nanoscale second phases. Progressive deformation led to modest grain size reduction, while maintaining consistent eutectic fragment dimensions. TEM images of RS-3 sample are shown in Fig. 2D, indicating the formation of Mg2Zn11 phase by selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern. Moreover, numerous nanograins were present in Mg2Zn11 phase, which was demonstrated as MgZn2 phase in prior studies48. In Zn grains (Fig. 2E), nano-sized precipitates were observed.

Microstructures of Zn-0.5Mg alloys. (A) As-cast; (B) EDS analysis; (C) As-swaged; (D) TEM image and SAED pattern of RS-3 samples; (E) Nano-sized precipitates.

Crystallographic analysis (Fig. 3) through inverse pole figure mapping (IPF) demonstrated color-coded grain orientations: 0001 (red), 010 (blue), and 20 (green). Texture weakening was observed with increased passes, evidenced by more uniform orientation distribution. Grain size measurements showed progressive refinement from 1.46 μm (RS-1) to 1.10 μm (RS-2), with RS-3 exhibiting 1.19 μm. The population of sub-5 μm grains increased with deformation intensity. Band contrast imaging revealed deformation-induced low-angle grain boundaries (2–10°, red) formed through dislocation accumulation and reorganization. These boundaries facilitated grain subdivision, with their frequency decreasing from 8.52% (RS-1) to 4.48% (RS-2) and 4.35% (RS-3), indicating reduced dislocation density with further processing. Figure 2D shows the grain orientation spread (GOS) mappings, which are used to extract dynamically recrystallized (DRXed) grains with GOS value less than 1°. It indicated that the fractions of DRXed grains were 66.4% for RS-1, 81.8% for RS-2, and 84.0% for RS-3.

Figure 4 presents crystallographic characterization through kernel average misorientation (KAM) analysis, pole figures, and IPF mapping, revealing deformation-induced microstructural evolution in processed alloys. KAM visualization (Fig. 4A) demonstrates progressive reduction in local orientation variation, with average values declining from 0.37° (RS-1) to 0.32° (RS-2/RS-3), suggesting dislocation density reduction despite severe plastic deformation conditions49. Texture analysis (Fig. 4B) identified a characteristic deformation fiber pattern, with maximum intensity diminishing from 7.68 (RS-1) to 6.04 (RS-2) and 6.03 (RS-3) following additional processing. Crystallographic orientation mapping (Fig. 4C) revealed a progressive texture transition, where the initial 20 orientation diminished while 0001 components became predominant. Notably, 20 orientation nearly disappeared in RS-2 and RS-3 specimens. This crystallographic reorientation, particularly the shift toward < 0001 > texture, has been documented to improve mechanical characteristics in zinc-based materials50,51. The observed texture modification represents grain-level adaptation to imposed deformation forces, resulting in simultaneous enhancement of bulk material properties and microstructural refinement.

Mechanical behaviors of Zn–Mg alloys after RS process

Mechanical property evolution during rotary swaging is presented in Fig. 5A-B, revealing modest strength gains coupled with substantial ductility improvements. The yield strength (YS), ultimate tensile strength (UTS) and elongation of cast Zn-Mg alloys were 142 MPa, 163 MPa, and 2%, respectively. The processed material (RS-1) exhibited tensile properties of 422 MPa of UTS, 391 MPa of YS, and 5% of elongation. Subsequent deformation stages enhanced these values to 459 MPa (RS-2) and 465 MPa (RS-3) for UTS, accompanied by dramatic elongation increases to 26.1% and 27.8%, respectively. These findings confirm that progressive swaging effectively optimizes both strength and deformability in Zn-Mg systems. Fractographic analysis (Fig. 5C) revealed characteristic ductile failure features, with numerous dimples visible on fracture surfaces. The density of these dimple structures progressively rose with additional processing cycles, consistent with the measured ductility enhancement. This microstructural evidence substantiates the crucial role of rotary swaging in improving the plastic deformation capacity of zinc-magnesium alloys.

Corrosion behaviors of Zn–Mg alloys after RS process

To evaluate the degradation characteristics of zinc alloys, both electrochemical measurements and immersion experiments were performed (Fig. 6). The polarization behavior, illustrated in Fig. 5A, revealed similar corrosion potentials among processed specimens, with Ecorr values of -1.18 V for RS-1 and RS-3, and − 1.19 V for RS-2. Current density measurements showed a progressive decrease from 25.7 µA/cm² (RS-1) to 22.3 µA/cm² (RS-2) and 21.9 µA/cm² (RS-3). The electrochemical corrosion rates were 382.4 μm/year for RS-1, 331.8 μm/year for RS-2, and 325.9 μm/year for RS-3, indicating enhanced corrosion resistance with additional processing. This trend was confirmed by immersion testing, where degradation rates decreased from 171 μm/year to 141 μm/year and 135 μm/year for RS-1 through RS-3, respectively (Fig. 6B).

Electrochemical and immersion results of Zn-Mg alloys after RS process. (A) PDP curves; (B) Corrosion rates; (C) SEM images before and after removal of corrosion products.

Microstructural examination of corroded surfaces (Fig. 6C) identified typical degradation products including zinc oxide, hydroxycarbonate, and various phosphate compounds when exposed to simulated physiological fluid. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the corrosion products of Zn-Mg alloys consisted of ZnO, Zn5(CO3)2(OH)6, Zn3(PO4)2, and Ca3(PO4)2 compounds when immersed in Hank’s solution52. After 30-day immersion, all specimens developed surface oxide layers with particulate morphology, confirmed by elemental analysis to be oxygen-rich, as shown in Table 1. Subsequent removal of corrosion products revealed micrometer-scale pitting, with RS-1 exhibiting particularly severe localized attack characterized by larger cavities. The inverse relationship between processing intensity and pit dimensions demonstrates that increased swaging passes effectively mitigate localized degradation phenomena.

Cytocompatibility of Zn–Mg alloys after RS process

For assessing the biological compatibility of Zn-based alloys, osteogenesis-related MC3T3-E1 cells were employed with biologically inert titanium (Ti) as the control group. Figure 7A illustrates the cell survival rates following 72-hour incubation with varying extract concentrations. Initial observations indicated that RS-processed Zn-Mg specimens exhibited notably lower cellular metabolic activity compared to the Ti control when undiluted extracts (0-fold dilution) were applied. Upon 1-fold dilution, however, the cell viability metrics showed marked improvement, approaching values comparable to the Ti group. This enhanced cytocompatibility was ascribed to reduced Zn²⁺ ion concentrations within the tolerable range of the cells. When the extract concentration was halved (50% concentration), the decreased Zn²⁺ level further promoted MC3T3-E1 cell survival rates, surpassing those observed in full-strength extracts. Ionic concentration measurements for undiluted extracts are presented in Fig. 7B. The RS-1 group demonstrated a Zn²⁺ concentration of 2731 µg/L, while RS-2 and RS-3 groups exhibited slightly lower values at 2698 µg/L and 2713 µg/L respectively. Magnesium ion levels were consistently lower across all groups, registering 391 µg/L, 372 µg/L, and 378 µg/L for RS-1, RS-2, and RS-3 specimens respectively. Cellular morphological analysis using live/dead fluorescent staining is displayed in Fig. 7C. Viable cells and compromised membranes were visualized through green and red fluorescence signals respectively. Qualitative assessment revealed lower densities of viable cells in Zn-Mg alloy groups compared to pure Ti controls, with significantly more dead cells observed in the former. Quantitative analysis of live cell populations and dead/live cell ratios demonstrated that while Zn-Mg groups initially showed higher mortality rates than Ti controls, this discrepancy diminished considerably after 1-fold dilution. This further substantiates the cytotoxic effects associated with high Zn²⁺ concentrations in undiluted extracts.

Osteogenic properties of Zn–Mg alloys after RS process

The early-stage osteogenic induction potential was evaluated through ALP staining at days 3 and 7, while ARS staining at days 14 and 21 was used to assess late-stage mineralization capacity in MC3T3-E1 cells cultured with 50% extract concentration (Fig. 8). Quantitative analysis of ALP activity demonstrated that all Zn-based alloy groups exhibited superior enzymatic activity compared to the Ti control, with the RS-processed Zn-Mg variants showing comparable performance among themselves (Fig. 8A). These findings suggest that the plastically deformed Zn-Mg alloys possess robust osteogenic induction capabilities during the initial differentiation phase, potentially attributed to the synergistic effects of Zn²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions released during material degradation, which are recognized promoters of bone metabolism14,53. Consistent with these observations, ARS staining revealed enhanced mineralization capacity in Zn-Mg groups compared to bioinert Ti controls (Fig. 8B). Microscopic examination of stained cultures (Fig. 8C) showed significantly more intense purple deposits in cells treated with diluted Zn-Mg extracts, indicating elevated ALP expression levels. Parallel ARS staining patterns exhibited increased calcium nodule formation in Zn-Mg groups, further corroborating their superior osteogenic properties. This dual evidence from both early-phase enzymatic activity and late-stage matrix mineralization confirms the bioactive nature of these Zn-Mg alloys in promoting osteoblastic differentiation.

Discussions

Microstructural evolution during RS process

The RS technique represents an effective metal forming method characterized by its high-speed and severe plastic deformation features. This process demonstrates exceptional capability in grain structure refinement. Figure 3 illustrates marked grain size reduction following RS treatment. During the RS process, specimens experience continuously varying forging forces along the radial axis. Rapid fluctuations in loading direction enhance intragranular shear diversity, promoting extensive dislocation generation within grains. As RS passes accumulate, dislocation interactions and rearrangements contribute to progressive grain subdivision into finer sub-grains. Dislocation multiplication, entanglement, and reconfiguration lead to low-angle grain boundary (LAGB) formation. In Fig. 4, the reduction of fractions of LAGB indicates the increase in HAGB, of which contents are 89.9% for RS-1, 93.4% for RS-2, and 94.4% for RS-3. These results reveal systematic HAGB content elevation with increasing RS cycles, where dense HAGB networks develop through LAGB rotation54,55. Consequently, average grain sizes of Zn-0.5Mg alloys are significantly reduced after RS process. However, the average grain size of RS-3 sample (1.19 μm) was slightly increased compared to that of RS-2 sample (1.10 μm). A same case is reported in pure Zn and Zn-Ag alloys, which are processed by high-pressure torsion8,27. This is attributed to the competition between grain refinement by dynamic recrystallization and grain growth by dynamic recovery.

Apart from Zn grains, lamellar structure in as-cast Zn-Mg alloys transformed into fine particles after RS processing, as shown in Fig. 2C. The refinement of second phases depends on their mechanical properties, such as hardness. Second phases in Zn alloys are classified into two species: one is formed by combining with soluble elements, the other is formed by combining with insoluble elements. The former exhibits various morphologies and low hardness (~ 2GPa)56the latter shows particle-shape and high hardness (> 4GPa)31–33,57. The hardness of second phases is always higher than Zn matrix (< 1GPa). During RS process, soft Zn matrix prefers to deformation, resulting in the pile-up of dislocations at the interface of second phases and stress concentration. The extra stress will promote plastic deformation of second phases depends on stress level. Microhardness of Mg2Zn11 phases lies between CuZn4/LiZn4 phases and RE-containing phases, indicating the required high stress compared to the refinement of CuZn4/LiZn4 phases41. Moreover, the lamellar characteristics of Mg2Zn11 phases means the high aspect ratio, which makes it easier to generate microcracks in the phases. Previous studies have showed that increasing strain by increasing ECAP passes is an effective to refine Mg2Zn11 phases48. Our studies further demonstrated the RS process make a great progress in the refinement of Mg2Zn11 phases by altering RS passes.

Strengthening mechanism and deformation behaviors

The processed material demonstrates superior mechanical characteristics due to its refined microstructure, consisting of small grains, elevated dislocation concentrations, and homogenously dispersed eutectic phases. Following rotary swaging, both the crystalline dimensions and eutectic network undergo additional refinement. This microstructural optimization increases grain boundary area, effectively obstructing dislocation motion and thereby improving mechanical performance. The strengthening mechanism follows the Hall-Petch principle58,59: , where σy represents yield strength, K denotes a material constant, and d signifies mean grain diameter. Progressive swaging reduces d, consequently enhancing σy according to this relationship. RS-2 and RS-3 specimens display comparable grain sizes, accounting for their similar tensile properties. During plastic deformation, dislocations initially propagate within grains before piling up at boundaries, creating localized stress fields. These concentrated stresses promote the generation and movement of non-basal dislocations, which subsequently undergo annihilation through various interactions. This dislocation dynamics explains why the Zn-Mg system achieves peak strength rapidly after yielding, followed by necking initiation.

Figure 4 demonstrates a direct correlation between Kernel Average Misorientation (KAM) values and geometrically necessary dislocation (GND) density. Elevated KAM measurements correspond to enhanced plastic deformation and increased defect concentrations. The GND density (ρ_GND) is quantifiable using the formula60: , where represents mean KAM value, µ denotes EBSD step size, and b is the Burgers vector magnitude. Calculations yielded GND densities of 2.24 × 10¹⁴/m² (RS-1), 1.87 × 10¹⁴/m² (RS-2), and 1.87 × 10¹⁴/m² (RS-3) for the RS-processed Zn alloys. The GND density initially increased with cumulative RS cycles before stabilizing at higher pass numbers. Notably, elevated initial dislocation densities promote frequent dislocation interactions and entanglements during tensile loading, effectively impeding dislocation mobility and enhancing material strength61.

The secondary phase constitution also exerts critical influence on mechanical behavior. Fine secondary phase precipitates act as effective barriers to dislocation motion while generating additional lattice strain, both contributing to strength enhancement62. Multi-pass RS processing fragmented the original lamellar eutectic microstructure, as evidenced in Fig. 2. These refined particles not only impede dislocation propagation but also mitigate microcrack formation, thereby synergistically improving both ductility and strength. The Orowan strengthening mechanism provides theoretical underpinnings for this phenomenon63. During plastic deformation, dislocations traverse the matrix until encountering impenetrable second-phase particles, causing dislocation pile-up at particle-matrix interfaces. This dislocation accumulation generates significant back-stress, necessitating increased applied stress to sustain deformation and consequently elevating the yield strength (YS).

Microstructural evolution in Zn-Mg systems was examined using scanning electron microscopy and electron backscatter diffraction techniques. Plastic deformation behavior showed strong dependence on grain sizes, with average grain diameters measuring 1.46 μm (RS-1), 1.10 μm (RS-2), and 1.19 μm (RS-3). Larger grain sizes provide sufficient volume for dislocation nucleation and accumulation. In hexagonal close-packed zinc alloys, plastic deformation primarily occurs through < a>-type dislocation glide64. Activation of specific slip systems depends significantly on their respective Schmid factors. As shown in Fig. 9, basal and prismatic < a > slip systems exhibited comparable Schmid values, both lower than pyramidal < a > systems. Notably, all three alloy conditions demonstrated similar Schmid factors for < a > glide, suggesting minimal influence of processing intensity on this deformation mode. Besides < a > dislocations, < c + a > slip systems also contribute to plastic flow, displaying higher Schmid factors than their < a > counterparts. Interestingly, < c + a > Schmid values remained consistent across different processing conditions. The enhanced ductility observed with additional swaging cycles can be attributed to facilitated activation of prismatic and pyramidal < a > slip systems. In addition, the fragmentation of Mg2Zn11 phases has a huge effect on elongation. The low ductility of RS-1 sample is mainly attributed to presence of unfragmented Mg2Zn11 phases (Fig. 2C), where the stress concentration occurs.

Effects of RS process on corrosion behaviors and biocompatibility

Electrochemical assessments revealed comparable corrosion potentials and a reduction in corrosion current density within Zn alloys as the number of RS passes increased. Immersion studies further corroborated that corrosion rates diminished with more RS passes. Additionally, the emergence of pitting potential signaled localized corrosion occurrences. Upon removing corrosion byproducts, surface morphology analysis uncovered localized corrosion in the Zn alloy, primarily due to the establishment of a galvanic cell structure between the Mg2Zn11 phase and the adjacent Zn matrix. The standard corrosion potential of Mg is notably lower than that of Zn, making the Mg2Zn11 phase anodic during corrosion processes, thus causing preferential corrosion. When the Mg2Zn11 phase is minute, localized corrosion is inconspicuous, and the Zn alloy displays uniform corrosion across various immersion periods, with minimal pitting. Conversely, as the Mg2Zn11 phase dimensions were larger in the RS-1 sample, galvanic corrosion became more pronounced, leading to the development of substantial local corrosion pits surrounding the Mg2Zn11 phase. Furthermore, a minor decrease in grain size elevates grain boundary density, subsequently influencing corrosion rates. The elevated corrosion rate in the RS-1 sample is primarily due to heightened localized corrosion stemming from the larger Mg2Zn11 phase size. This expedited corrosion rate facilitates the discharge of metal ions from the alloy. Currently, since the mechanical attributes of certain high-strength degradable Zn alloys have matched those of pure titanium (Ti), multiple biological experiments have validated the bone-enhancing impact of Zn alloys. In addition to Zn ions, Mg ions liberated during degradation have been found to encourage endothelial cell movement and vascularization, with the activation of the CGRP-FAK-VEGF signaling pathway aiding bone defect revascularization65,66. Since Mg is a primary component in the human body, the discharge of Mg ions also contributes to bone repair promotion. Via the combined effects of these metal ions, the as-swaged Zn-Mg alloys demonstrate a remarkably efficient bone-enhancing impact. In this study, the Zn-Mg alloys treated by rotary swaging released Zn and Mg ions to enhance osteogenic characteristics during degradation.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the effects of rotary swaging on the microstructure, mechanical performance, corrosion characteristics, and biocompatibility of Zn-Mg alloys. The principal findings are summarized as follows:

The swaging process remarkably refined grain structure. Progressive processing reduced average grain dimensions from 1.46 μm to 1.10 μm, primarily due to complete dynamic recrystallization. Additionally, the technique effectively fragmented and homogenized eutectic networks.

Processed Zn-Mg alloys demonstrated simultaneous enhancement in both strength and ductility. The ultimate tensile strength exceeded 400 MPa, with RS-3 specimens achieving maximum elongation of 27.8%. These improvements resulted from synergistic strengthening mechanisms including grain boundary hardening, dislocation accumulation, and secondary phase reinforcement. The ductility improvement principally stemmed from favorable Schmid factors for prismatic/pyramidal slip systems and refined eutectic distribution.
