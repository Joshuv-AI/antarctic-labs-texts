Comparative analysis of the quality of fortified sweet wines made from Marselan and Petit Verdot dehydration
Authors: Qun Wang, Qiaomin Chen, Xiaoxi Zhang, Yangyang Bai, Xinyang Liu, Wenxiu Yue, Zhenan Shi, Luye Yang, Yilin Cheng, Juan Du, Fuliang Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the effects of grape dehydration on Marselan and Petit Verdot wines. The results demonstrated that dehydration modified wine quality and aging potential by altering the contents of organic acids, phenolics and aroma compounds. The specific effects varied depending on varieties and dehydration degrees. Specifically, 20% and 40% dehydration increased the total contents of monomeric anthocyanins in fresh Marselan wines by 37.4% and 28.9%, respectively, but showed the opposite trend in Petit Verdot wines. Dehydration also increased the total contents of esters in aged Marselan wines, all of which increased by more than 50%. Sensory evaluation revealed that 20% dehydration enhanced Marselan wines' palate quality, while 40% dehydration produced the more intense aroma. However, the non-dehydrated Petit Verdot exhibited greater aroma quality. In conclusion, the effects of dehydration on wines were strongly influenced by both cultivar and dehydration degree, with Marselan showing superior potential for this application.

Introduction

Since the 21st century, global climate change has led to significant alterations in the climate patterns during the harvest period of major wine-producing regions, with an increased frequency of heavy rainfall and low sunlight events (Leeuwen et al., 2019). The wine-producing regions in Northwest China, including the Eastern Foot of Helan Mountain and the Heyang regions, have also repeatedly encountered prolonged rainy spells during the grape maturation and harvest (Chen et al., 2024). Traditionally, precipitation during the harvest period can delay or hinder the ripening process and systematically disrupt the chemical composition of grapes, adversely affecting the quality and yield of grapes (de Souza et al., 2015).

During overcast and rainy weather, grape photosynthesis is suppressed while respiratory consumption increases, leading to a significant reduction in sugar content (Zhu et al., 2018). Meanwhile, sunlight is extremely limited on rainy days. As a key driver of photosynthesis and the synthesis of secondary metabolites, both the intensity and spectral quality of sunlight directly regulate the biosynthetic pathways of phenolic and aromatic compounds (He et al., 2020). Furthermore, gray mold poses another threat during the harvest period, as its incidence rate increases exponentially under conditions of continuous rainfall and high relative humidity (Hill et al., 2017). The infection leads to the production of various secondary metabolites that can contaminate wine, such as 3-octenol and other off-flavor compounds, which significantly compromise the sensory quality of the wine (Santos et al., 2022).

In response to the aforementioned challenges, harvesting grapes early and producing sweet wine through dehydration could be an effective option. More serious grape damage can be avoided by harvesting before rainy weather arrives. Meanwhile, as a key technique for producing natural sweet wines, dehydration can exert a significant impact on the quality and flavor of wines by altering the chemical composition of grapes, such as the contents of sugars and phenolic compounds (Zhang et al., 2025; Bai et al., 2023b; Chen et al., 2024). Fortified sweet wines produced using dehydration techniques not only exhibit stable color, concentrated aroma, rich taste, and high aging potential but are also rich in organic acids, amino acids, and polyphenols, suggesting potential nutritional value (Ma et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Studies have shown that organic acids can inhibit the adverse effects of fructose, and reduce hepatic lipogenesis (Liu et al., 2025). Moreover, polyphenols, particularly anthocyanins, can alleviate high-fructose diet-induced glucolipid metabolic disorders by modulating liver function and gut microbiota (Yue et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024).

Currently, the primary strategies employed in Northwest China to mitigate the impact of rainy harvest seasons on grape and wine quality include the use of rain-sheltering facilities, extra lighting, and adjusted canopy training systems (Huang et al., 2022). In contrast, research on the manufacture of fortified sweet red wines using dehydration techniques was limited, particularly in terms of determining the optimal dehydration degree for different grape varietals. Thus, raw materials for this study included two red wine grape varieties from Northwest China's wine regions, Marselan and Petit Verdot. To investigate the effects of various grape dehydration degrees on the qualities of fortified sweet wines and to evaluate if dehydration treatment can improve the quality and aging potential of wines. The findings were expected to produce novel ways for mitigating the harmful effects of increased heavy rainfall and inadequate sunshine occurrences during harvest on wine quality.

Materials and methods

Grape samples

Red grapes Marselan and Petit Verdot were harvested at the technological ripening stage in September 2021 from the Heyang Grape Demonstration Station in Shaanxi (China) and Ningxia Province (China), respectively. The sugar content of the must was about 176.83 g/L and 211.33 g/L, and the total acidity was 5.06 g/L and 7.58 g/L. Bunches of grapes were subjected to postharvest dehydration under natural conditions (20–25 °C, 65–85% relative humidity) in order to concentrate sugars. And healthy berries of Marselan and Petit Verdot grownwere collected at 0%, 20%, and 40% weight loss for winemaking, labeled Mg0, Mg20, Mg40, Pg0, Pg20 and Pg40, respectively.

Wine samples

At Northwest A&F University's experimental winery, Mg0, Mg20, Mg40, Pg0, Pg20 and Pg40 grapes were used to make the wine samples, which were branded MA0, MA20, MA40, PV0, PV20, and PV40, respectively. The vinification protocol was the same for all tests. Following the destemming and crushing of the grapes, the must was pumped into 20 L glass fermentation tanks. Each replicate added 60 mg/kg of sulfur dioxide and 30 mg/kg of pectinase. After a 96-h cold maceration period at 4 ± 1 °C, 200 mg/kg of yeast (CECA) was added. Alcoholic fermentation was conducted at a controlled temperature (20 ± 1 °C) and terminated when the specific gravity was approximately 1.020 by adding 180 mg/kg of sulfur dioxide. Continue macerating for 120 h, then press the pomace. Measure the alcohol content and add brandy to bring it down to 18–20%vol while keeping the reducing sugar content between 60 g/L and 80 g/L. The resulting wines were cold-stabilized at 4 ± 1 °C for three months before being finally bottled and stored in the cellar. During the two-year bottle aging period, samples were investigated at five time points: before bottling, and after 6, 13, 18, and 24 months of aging, which were designated as T0, T6, T13, T18, and T24, respectively.

Reagents

The organic acid standards and phenolic standards were obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China).

General wine parameters

The residual sugar, titratable acidity, alcohol, and volatile acidity levels were determined using the National Standard of the People's Republic of China GB/T 15038–2006. The pH levels were determined using an acidity meter.

Color parameters and phenolic composition

Measurement of CIELab color parameters

CIELab color parameters were determined using a UV spectrophotometer. After filtering the wine samples through the 0.45 μm filter membranes, the absorbance values at wavelengths of 450 nm, 520 nm, 570 nm and 630 nm were measured. These values were then substituted into the formula to calculate the CIELab parameters (Bai et al., 2024).

Determination of total polyphenol

Dilute the wine sample 10-fold, then take 0.2 mL of the sample. Sequentially mix it with 5 mL distilled water, 0.5 mL Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, 1.5 mL 20% saturated sodium carbonate solution, and 2.8 mL distilled water. After standing in the dark for 2 h, measure the absorbance at 765 nm. Calculate the total phenolic content using the standard curve, expressed as mg/L gallic acid equivalent (Bai et al., 2024).

Characterization of anthocyanins content

According to the HPLC method outlined by Yang et al. (2017), anthocyanins in wines were measured using an HPLC instrument (LC-20AT, Shimadzu, Japan) with a photodiode array detector (SPD-M20A, Shimadzu, China). Anthocyanins were separated chromatographically using a Synergi Hydro-RP C18 column (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA, 250 × 4.6 mm, 4 μm). The mobile phases were A = formic acid / acetonitrile / water (1:4:32, v/v/v) and B = formic acid / acetonitrile / water (1:20:16, v/v/v). The analytical settings were as follows: 1 mL/min flow rate; 35 °C column temperature; 20 μL injection volume; and 520 nm detection wavelength.

Characterization of non-anthocyanin phenolic content

The previously described approach was used to analyze the wine samples (Yang et al., 2018). Wine and ethyl acetate supernatants are collected, allowed to evaporate, and then redissolved in 0.5 mL of methanol for HPLC analysis. Using the Synergi Hydro-RP C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 4 μm) to separate the components of phenolic acids, the samples were determined using an LC-20AT HPLC equipment connected to the SPD-M20A photodiode array detector. The flow rates of the two mobile phases were 1 mL/min for mobile phase A (0.1% acetic acid in an 8:1 v/v water:acetonitrile mixture) and mobile phase B (0.1% acetic acid in a 4:5 v/v water:acetonitrile mixture). The following parameters were set: injection volume, 20 μL; detection wavelength, 280 nm; column temperature, 30 °C. Additionally, the gradient program was set up as follows: 100 to 65% A between 0 and 45 min; 65 to 0% A between 45 and 50 min; 0% A between 50 and 55 min; 0% to 100% A between 55 and 56 min; and 100% A between 56 and 62 min. Measuring the phenolic acid content using the external standard method.

Organic acids

The organic acid analysis was carried out using a photodiode array detector (SPD-M20A, Shimadzu, China) in conjunction with an HPLC equipment (LC-20AT, Shimadzu, Japan) after wine samples were filtered through 0.22 μm polyethersulfone filters (Chen et al., 2024). A Hydro-RP C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 4 μm, Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) was used in the HPLC system, and the mobile phase was an aqueous solution of 0.05 mmol/L H2SO4 at a rate of 0.45 mL/min. The temperature of the column was 55 °C, and the injection volume was 5 μL.

Aroma compositions

According to Liu et al. (2024) and Bai et al. (2024), aroma compositions in wines were analyzed by using headspace solid-phase microextraction combined with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry. After 15 min of incubation at 40 °C and 30 min of extraction, the sample was sent to the injector for five minutes of desorption at 230 °C. The carrier gas (helium) was kept at a steady flow rate of 1.5 mL/min throughout the GC run. The temperature of the injector was maintained at 250 °C. After being set to 40 °C for five minutes, the oven's temperature was increased to 130 °C and 220 °C at rates of 2 °C and 5 °C per minute, respectively. Finally, it was maintained at 220 °C for ten minutes. Electron ionization (EI) with an electron energy of 70 eV and a mass range of 35–350 amu produced GC–MS spectra. Standard retention time comparisons, retention index comparisons, and NIST 17.0 mass spectrum library queries were used for the qualitative study. Quantification was done using the internal standard-standard curve approach, the internal standard compound was 4-methyl-2-pentanol.

Sensory analysis

Twelve oenology students from Northwest A&F University's College of Enology (eight females and four males) analyzed the organoleptic qualities of wine samples using the methodology outlined by Bai et al. (2023a). Based on their basic sensory performances over the course of eight weeks of training, each judge was chosen. The formal sensory evaluation of wine samples was conducted in two parts. First, each panelist was required to evaluate the appearance, flavor, and aroma quality of the wines using the 9-point scale, with higher scores indicating greater intensity. Second, each panelist selected three to five aroma descriptors from the given list to describe the wines' aroma characteristics and rated the intensity of each descriptor on the 5-point scale. The quantification of wine aroma attributes was expressed by the M-value.

Results and discussion

Physicochemical parameters

Throughout the aging period, dehydrated wines exhibited significantly higher pH values and volatile acidity levels compared to the control samples (P < 0.05), with these increments becoming more pronounced as the degree of dehydration intensified (Tables S1 and S2). Notably, both volatile acidity and pH levels remained relatively stable over the entire aging process. This elevation may be attributed to dehydration-mediated sugar enrichment, which imposes osmotic stress on Saccharomyces cerevisiae cells. In response to such stress, a portion of pyruvic acid is diverted toward the biosynthesis of glycerol and acetic acid (Kelly et al., 2018), with the latter contributing directly to the increase in volatile acidity.

Similarly, in Petit Verdot wines, the PV40 samples (40% weight loss) exhibited significantly higher levels of citric acid, acetic acid (except T13 and T24), and ferulic acid relative to the control (P < 0.05). However, malic acid content in Petit Verdot wines was not affected by dehydration treatment. For both Matheran and Petit Verdot wines, the highest contents of most organic acids were detected in wines produced from grapes with 40% weight loss, which is consistent with the findings reported by Panceri and Bordignon-Luiz (2017). Previous research has demonstrated that organic acids can enhance the color stability and aroma persistence of aged wines (Wang et al., 2019). Therefore, dehydrated wine samples may possess improved aging potential and superior sensory properties.

In summary, dehydration treatment increased the total acidity, volatile acidity, organic acid content, and pH of the wines, which aligns with many previous studies (Mencarelli et al., 2021). The elevation in titratable acidity may result from the concentration effect of dehydration and the concurrent increase in volatile acid content (Panceri & Bordignon-Luiz, 2017). Furthermore, the increase in volatile acid content may be associated with dehydration-induced sugar enrichment. This metabolic phenomenon had also been observed in other wine types produced from high-sugar musts (Ossola et al., 2017).

The variations of polyphenols content in Marselan wines exhibited a relatively consistent pattern: the concentrations in dehydrated samples were significantly higher than those in the control group, and this trend persisted consistently throughout the entire aging period. For young Petit Verdot wines, the total phenol content of PV40 was significantly higher than that of PV0 (P < 0.05), whereas the total anthocyanin content showed the exact opposite trend (P < 0.01). The anthocyanins in young Petit Verdot wines are primarily derived from the extraction of grape skins during maceration. Several studies have shown that dehydration treatment reduces the total anthocyanin content in grape skins, thereby leading to lower anthocyanin levels in the wines (Bai et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). PV40 was produced using grapes with a 40% dehydration level, while PV0 was made from fresh grapes. Therefore, the dehydration treatment is likely the main reason why the total anthocyanin content in PV40 is lower than PV0. At other aging stages, no significant differences in total phenol and total anthocyanin were observed among different treatments, which was consistent with the variation pattern of the total tannin. Additionally, the PV40 treatment exhibited the highest total flavonoid and total flavanol contents among all groups (P < 0.05).

Monomeric anthocyanins

Nine different monomeric anthocyanins were identified in Marselan and Petit Verdot wines, including five non-acylated anthocyanins (Fig. 3), two acetylated anthocyanins (Fig. 4) and two coumaroylated anthocyanins (Fig. 5). Before bottling aging, dehydration significantly increased the total content of monomeric anthocyanins in Marselan wines, while showing the opposite trend in Petit Verdot wines. Specifically, the contents in MA20 and MA40 were 37.4% and 28.9% higher than MA0, respectively. And PV20 and PV40 exhibited contents lower than PV0 by 9.7% and 40.9%, respectively. Additionally, consistent with the trend observed in the total content of anthocyanis, the contents of non-acylated anthocyanins and acylated anthocyanins decreased during the aging period. This was most likely due to the oxidative degradation and anthocyanin polymerization (Marquez et al., 2013). Mv-3-O-Glu was the most abundant non-acylated anthocyanin in wines. As shown in Fig. 3, the content of Mv-3-O-Glu in fresh Marselan wines increased significantly with the degree of dehydration (P < 0.05). After six months of aging, the MA20 wine exhibited the highest content, which was significantly higher than MA0. Similarly, the MA20 wine displayed higher contents of Dp-3-O-Glu, Pt-3-O-Glu and Pn-3-O-Glu, and this trend continued as the wine aged. However, for fresh Petit Verdot wines, the contents of all five non-acylated anthocyanins in the dehydrated treatment groups were significantly lower than PV0, and gradually decreased with increasing dehydration degree. After six months of aging, the PV20 wine exhibited the smallest decrease in Mv-3-O-Glu content, which remained higher than PV0 and PV40 wines. These findings imply that the accumulation and preservation of non-acylated anthocyanins in Marselan wine may be facilitated by winemaking with 20% dehydration grapes, although this impact was not as noticeable in Petit Verdot wines.

When it came to acetylated anthocyanins, moderate dehydration treatment (20% water loss) significantly increased the content of Mv-3-acetylglc in fresh Marselan wines, while it had no effect on the content of Pn-3-acetylglc. In contrast, excessive dehydration (40% water loss) resulted in contents of both Mv-3-acetylglc and Pn-3-acetylglc that were significantly lower than those in the control group. After six months of aging, the contents of Mv-3-acetylglc and Pn-3-acetylglc in MA20 wines were significantly higher than those in MA0 and MA40 wines, and this trend persisted throughout the 24-month aging period. In Petit Verdot wines (T0 and T6 stages), dehydration treatment significantly reduced the contents of Mv-3-acetylglc and Pn-3-acetylglc, with the levels gradually decreasing as the degree of dehydration increased. After 13 months of aging, no significant differences were observed among the PV0, PV20, and PV40 wines. At the T24 stage, the Pn-3-acetylglc content in PV20 wines was significantly higher than that in PV0 wines.

In Marselan wines during the early stages of aging (T0, T6 and T13), Pn-3-p-coumglc and Mv-3-p-coumglc exhibited the same trend as Mv-3-acetylglc, with the highest content observed in the MA20 wine sample. After 18 months of aging, only Mv-3-p-coumglc remained significantly higher in MA20, whereas no significant differences were observed in the content of Pn-3-p-coumglc. For Petit Verdot wines, the dehydration treatment significantly increased the content of Mv-3-p-coumglc only in PV20 at the T0 stage, with no significant differences observed at other stages.

According to research, acylated anthocyanins are more stable and have a higher antioxidant activity than their non-acylated counterparts (Cruz et al., 2016), which makes them significant contributors to color stability during wine aging. And compared to their respective acetylated and original anthocyanins, coumarylated anthocyanins contributed more to color parameters (Han et al., 2008). Moderate dehydration treatment facilitated the accumulation of acylated anthocyanins in fortified sweet wines, and these maintained the highest content even after short-term aging, potentially playing a positive role in enhancing the stability of anthocyanins in fortified sweet wines.

Overall, winemaking using 20% dehydration grapes significantly promoted the accumulation of both non-acylated and acylated anthocyanins in Marselan wines, with the highest levels maintained throughout the aging process. This result is in line with our previous research on Syrah fortified sweet wines (Zhang et al., 2025), which suggests that moderate dehydration treatment of grapes may improve the stability of monomeric anthocyanins in fortified sweet wines, as well as play a certain positive role in maintaining the wines' vivid red color and improving the aging potential. In contrast, excessive dehydration induced significant degradation, resulting in lower anthocyanin content than that in the control group. Furthermore, the positive effect of dehydration treatment on anthocyanin content in Petit Verdot wines was less pronounced. It only increased the levels of certain acylated anthocyanins while reducing the content of non-acylated anthocyanins.

It is worth noting that, the effect of dehydration treatment on the hydroxycinnamic acids varies among different grape varieties. For young Marselan wines, dehydration treatment significantly increased the contents of p-coumaric acid, caffeic acid, and (E)-ferulic acid (P < 0.05). Among these, p-coumaric acid and caffeic acid maintained the same trend in aged wines. Most likely because during dehydration, hydroxycinnamates were hydrolyzed by cinnamonoyl esterases to the corresponding free phenolic acids (Panceri et al., 2015). Nonetheless, the (E)-ferulic acid content of MA0 was significantly higher than that of MA20 after 13 months of aging.

Meanwhile, dehydration treatment considerably decreased the amount of chlorogenic acid in Petit Verdot wines (P < 0.05). After 6 months of aging, the dehydrated wines' (E)-ferulic acid content was significantly higher than that of the control wines (P < 0.05), and the increase ratio was favorably connected with the level of dehydration. Consistent with the findings of Bonghi et al. (2012), the contents of catechin and quercetin increased significantly in both dehydrated Marselan and Petit Verdot wines, which may be attributed to the upregulation of flavonol synthase expression in grapes induced by the dehydration. Research have shown that quercetin can act as a copigment to form copigmentation complexes with anthocyanins, thereby improving the color stability and quality of wine (Unterkofler et al., 2020). Therefore, dehydration treatment may have certain positive effects on enhancing the color quality of wines. Meanwhile, in dehydrated Marselan wines, the contents of epicatechin and kaempferol were significantly higher than those in the control wine samples (P < 0.05). However, in Petit Verdot wines, the content of kaempferol showed an opposite trend, and there was no significant difference in epicatechin content among samples with different dehydration degrees.

In conclusion, the total phenolic and hydroxybenzoic acid contents in the dehydrated wine are higher than those in the control wine. However, the effects of dehydration treatment on different phenolic compounds vary among different grape varieties, which may be related to the grape variety, origin, and maturity. The phenolic compounds in wines were unstable and constantly change during the brewing and aging processes, including self-interaction and combination with aroma components (Perestrelo et al., 2016). The effect of dehydration treatment on phenolic compounds in wines was the result of the combined action of anabolic pathways and concentration effects during grape dehydration (Mencarelli et al., 2021). In addition, due to the degradation of cell walls and cell rupture during the dehydration process, phenolic compounds in the grape skin diffuse into the pulp, making it easier for phenolic compounds to be extracted into wines (Ossola et al., 2017).

Specifically, dehydrated Marselan wines exhibited significantly lower L⁎ values compared to the control (P < 0.05). At other aging phases, only MA40 had a significantly lower L⁎ value than MA0 (P < 0.05). This phenomenon may be attributed to the increased levels of copigments, such as hydroxybenzoic acids, induced by dehydration, which enhance color intensity through copigmentation effects (Wang et al., 2019). Although no significant differences in a⁎ values were observed at T0 and T6, the decline in a⁎ values was significantly slower in the MA40 group during aging (P < 0.05). Furthermore, after 13 months of aging, the a⁎ values of MA40 were significantly higher than those of MA0 and MA20, which may be related to its higher content of organic acids (Bai et al., 2024).

Meanwhile, the b⁎, C⁎ab and H⁎ab values of fresh Marselan wine increased significantly with the degree of dehydration, and this tendency continued throughout the aging process. These findings indicated that the dehydration treatment slowed down the rate of decline in red hue in Marselan wines, while enhancing their yellow hue and color saturation. Besides enhancing the color intensity and stability of Marselan wines, grape dehydration induced aging-like color shifts in fortified sweet wines, transforming vibrant purple hues into brick-red tones (Fan et al., 2023). This color evolution may originate from interactions among phenolic compounds, oxidation processes, and the formation of anthocyanin derivatives (Li et al., 2019).

The dehydrated Petit Verdot wine samples also exhibited the same yellowing phenomenon, with b⁎ and H⁎ab values increasing significantly with the degree of dehydration. However, the L⁎, a⁎ and C⁎ab values showed opposite trends compared to Marselan wines. This discrepancy may be related to the lower contents of monomeric anthocyanins in the dehydrated Petit Verdot wines. In addition, unlike Marselan wine, dehydration treatment reduced the contents of non-anthocyanin phenolic compounds such as chlorogenic acid and kaempferol, while compounds including p-coumaric acid and epicatechin showed no significant differences among treatments. This suggests that the hyperchromic effect caused by co-pigmentation in Petit Verdot wines may be considerably lower than that in Marselan wines.

Notably, the ΔE values for both Marselan and Petit Verdot wines exceeded 3, with higher dehydration levels corresponding to greater ΔE values relative to controls. Combined with the feature colors, it is evident that dehydration treatment indeed influenced wine color, but the specific effects differed significantly among varieties. The browning phenomenon induced by dehydration treatment was observed in both Marselan and Petit Verdot wines. However, dehydration treatment significantly enhanced the color intensity and stability only in Marselan wines, without the positive impacts on the color of Petit Verdot wines, which may be attributed to differences in phenolic composition between the two varieties.

Aroma compositions

A total of 46 aroma compounds were identified in Marselan and Petit Verdot wines, including 20 esters, 17 alcohols, 7 acids, and 2 aldehydes. Among these, esters and alcohols were present at the highest concentrations and constituted the primary aroma compounds in the wines (Table S3). The results indicated that both dehydration treatment and bottle aging exerted significant effects on the aroma compound contents of the fortified dehydrated wines, and these effects exhibited varietal specificity.

As the degree of dehydration increased, the total ester content in Marselan wines gradually rose. Specifically, during the T13 period, the MA40 treatment exhibited a significant increase of 61.7% compared to MA0. At T18, MA40 showed significant increases of 71.4% and 50.1% compared to MA0 and MA20, respectively. After 24 months of aging, MA40 exceeded MA0 and MA20 by 73.7% and 65.1%, respectively. At T18 and T24, the total ester content in Petit Verdot wines displayed a variation pattern similar to that observed in Marselan wines, while no significant differences or trends were found in other periods.

In contrast to the changes in total ester content, the total alcohol content in Marselan wines exhibited the decreasing trend with the increasing degree of dehydration. Specifically, at T13, the alcohol concentration in MA40 was significantly lower than that in MA0 and MA20 by 48.5% and 37.3%, respectively. During the T18 and T24 periods, significant differences were observed among MA0, MA20, and MA40, with MA0 showing the highest content and MA40 the lowest. For Petit Verdot wines, a significant difference in total alcohol content was observed only at T24, where PV0 was significantly higher than PV20 and PV40 by 21.6% and 20.9%, respectively.

Specifically, after 13 months of aging, the influence of dehydration treatment gradually became prominent, characterized by the following trends: the contents of ethyl acetate, isobutyl acetate, and ethyl butyrate exhibited a positive correlation with the dehydration degree. The increase in these esters may contribute to more intense fruity and sweet aroma attributes, thereby enhancing the aromatic intensity and complexity of the aged wines (Duran-Guerrero et al., 2021; Urcan et al., 2017). In contrast, the contents of 3-methyl-1-butanol, 3-Methylthio-1-propanol and phenethyl alcohol decreased significantly with the increase in dehydration degree (P < 0.05).

Dehydration treatment may not directly lead to the formation of large amounts of esters in grapes. Instead, it likely facilitates the accumulation of abundant precursor compounds for subsequent fermentation or promotes the activity of LOX and ADH, thereby promoting esterification reactions and the formation of diverse volatile compounds (Panceri et al., 2017; Urcan et al., 2017). In the later stage of aging, the increase in esters and the decrease in alcohols may be attributed to the concentration effect caused by dehydration treatment, which elevates the content of acids in the grapes (Zhang et al., 2025), thereby promoting the esterification of alcohols during aging and shifting the reaction equilibrium toward the formation of ester compounds (Wang et al., 2018).

Notably, the effects of dehydration treatment on certain aroma compounds exhibited variety-specific patterns. In Marselan wines, the concentration of ethyl lactate decreased with increasing dehydration degree and showed significant differences after 6 months of aging, whereas the opposite trend was observed in Petit Verdot wines. Furthermore, α-terpineol and 4-terpineol emerged as characteristic metabolites of dehydrated Marselan wines, while benzaldehyde was exclusively detected in dehydrated Petit Verdot samples from the T6 stage onward. The underlying mechanisms responsible for these phenomena require further investigation. In addition, the total content of acids in both Marselan and Petit Verdot wines gradually increased with the degree of dehydration, and this trend persisted throughout the entire aging process. This phenomenon was primarily attributed to the elevated content of acetic acid. The must with high sugar content resulting from dehydration typically induces osmotic stress in yeast, thereby promoting the production of yeast metabolites such as acetic acid and glycerol (Teng et al., 2020).

In Marselan wines, aroma intensity increased with dehydration level at T6 and T13. By T18, higher dehydration was associated with enhanced aroma purity, intensity, and elegance; MA40 displayed superior aromatic performance by T24. Specifically, at T13, MA40 outperformed other treatments in aroma persistence, body, and harmony. After 18 months of aging, dehydrated wines presented a richer and fuller body. At T24, all wines developed greater mouthfeel complexity, with dehydrated samples showing stronger aroma persistence. Among these, MA20 scored highest in structural perception, harmony, and aftertaste.

Aroma profiles varied notably across aging stages. At T0, MA0 was characterized by red fruit, tropical fruit, and vegetal notes; MA20 displayed more pungent spice and toasty aromas; and MA40 featured prominent black fruit and dried fruit tones. By T6, MA0 showed stronger spice and vegetal characters, MA20 exhibited enhanced toasty and dried fruit notes, and MA40 retained pronounced black fruit alongside abundant dried fruit aromas. At T13, toasty notes dominated in MA0, MA20 showed intensified red and black fruit aromas, and MA40 presented richer dried fruit characteristics. By T18, MA0 expressed more stone fruit and tropical fruit notes, MA20 had stronger toasty aromas, and MA40 displayed more intense dried fruit qualities. At T24, MA0 was marked by spice, red fruit, tropical fruit, floral, and citrus notes, whereas dehydrated Marselan wines exhibited strong black fruit, dried fruit, and mineral aromas, with MA40 additionally showing stone fruit nuances.

Unlike Marselan wines, dehydration had minimal impact on the appearance of Petit Verdot wines, aligning with CIELab color observations. Overall, PV0 maintained higher aroma quality across aging stages compared to dehydrated treatments. Notably, PV40 scored lower in structural perception and aroma harmony at T6 and T13. Specifically, for young wines, PV0 presented intense spice, fruit, and floral aromas; PV20 showed distinct stone fruit and toasty notes; and PV40, besides stronger mineral character, released undesirable odors. At T6, hydrogen sulfide was detected in all samples: PV0 retained stronger red fruit aromas, PV20 exhibited more herbal notes, and PV40 displayed clearer spice and dried fruit tones. At T18, PV0 offered more intense and diverse fruit aromas, all dehydrated wines showed prominent spice, toasty, and herbal notes, and PV40 additionally displayed clear dried fruit and tropical fruit aromas. By T24, PV0 had richer dried fruit notes, dehydrated wines showed stronger herbal and red fruit aromas, PV20 presented more abundant tropical fruit scents, and PV40 exhibited intensified floral aromas.

In summary, the sensory quality and aroma profiles of both wine varieties evolved throughout bottle aging. The effects of dehydration were variety-specific, likely due to differential impacts on volatile composition. Dehydration improved the sensory quality and aroma complexity of Marselan, with varying degrees leading to distinct aromatic signatures. In early aging (T0), dehydrated Marselan was characterized by black fruit and toasty notes, whereas dehydrated Petit Verdot featured herbal aromas. Specifically, MA20 showed more intense spice, PV20 had stronger stone fruit and toasty characters, and both MA40 and PV40 exhibited prominent dried fruit notes. During aging, dehydrated Marselan retained more intense black fruit and dried fruit aromas, while dehydrated Petit Verdot developed more prominent spice-related characteristics.

Conclusions

Based on the comprehensive analysis during bottle aging, grape dehydration had significant effects on the quality of fortified sweet wines. The application of dehydration proved beneficial for Marselan wines. Specifically, MA20 received the higher scores in body, harmony, and aftertaste. While the 40% dehydration significantly enhanced aromatic complexity and color stability, characterized by more stable a⁎ values and lower L⁎ values. In contrast, Petit Verdot wines responded less favorably to intensive dehydration, as PV0 and PV20 exhibited more pronounced fruity aromas and superior sensory quality in early aging stages (T6 and T13). After 18 months of aging, PV20 displayed enhanced pungent spice, toasty, and vegetal notes, indicating that moderate dehydration may shift the aromatic profile of Petit Verdot wine toward non-fruity characteristics. In summary, the effect of dehydration on wine quality was strongly influenced by both cultivar and degree of dehydration. It is essential to select the dehydration degree based on the grape variety.
