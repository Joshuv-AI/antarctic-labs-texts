Defusing a ticking time bomb: percutaneous intervention for a giant coronary pseudoaneurysm – a case report

Published: 2026

Introduction

Coronary artery pseudoaneurysm (PSA) post percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is a rare and potentially life-threatening complication, with real-world incidence remaining unclear due to paucity of data. The development of PSAs is postulated to be due to vessel wall damage that occurs during PCI. Other aetiologies include inflammatory disease, infection and external trauma. Furthermore, there are only a handful of documented cases of patients presenting with giant coronary PSAs (defined as being > 50 mm in diameter) [1]. We report an unusual case of a giant left main (LM) to left anterior descending (LAD) coronary PSA that we successfully treated percutaneously in a middle-aged male.

Patient information

A 62-year-old male with diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia and end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) presented initially for new-onset fast atrial fibrillation (AF). He subsequently mounted a 38.6-degree fever with blood cultures growing Methicillin Susceptible Staphylococcus Aureus (MSSA). Culture-directed intravenous antibiotics was initiated with subsequent resolution of his febrile illness with stable hemodynamics. 4 days later, he developed sudden onset chest pain with troponin elevation from 223 ng/L to > 22,000 ng/L (reference range: 0–18 ng/L). Furthermore, he became hypotensive requiring intravenous vasopressor support.

Given the evidence of acute chest pain with significant troponin elevation, the diagnosis of NSTEMI with cardiogenic shock was made. The differential diagnosis of type 2 myocardial infarction with septic shock was also considered given his initial presentation of MSSA bacteremia. However, given his hemodynamic instability and ongoing ischemic symptoms, the team decided to proceed with emergent coronary angiography. The diagnostic angiogram revealed critical LM-LAD disease with heavily calcified RCA stenoses. The operator at that time decided to proceed with definitive PCI to the LM-LAD (Fig. 1). This was performed with intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) support.Fig. 1A Caudal and Cranial views showing critical LM-LAD stenoses (). A Caudal and Cranial views showing critical LM-LAD stenoses (). B Caudal and Cranial views post PCI with drug-eluting stents to LM-LAD (red arrows). C Angiogram of the RCA

After intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) imaging, initial lesion preparation with 2.0 and 2.5 mm non-compliant (NC) balloons resulted in significant flow limiting dissections in the LM-LAD. Bailout stenting of the LM was performed with a 3.5 mm stent while the LAD was stented with 2 overlapping 3.0 mm stents. Post dilation was performed with 3.0, 3.5, 4.0 and 5.0 mm NC balloons. Drug coated balloon (DCB) angioplasty was also performed to the major diagonal branch with a 2.25 mm DCB. Final images revealed TIMI 3 flow in the LM-LAD (Fig. 1).

He was successfully weaned off vasopressor and IABP support post PCI. The transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) post procedure revealed an ejection fraction (EF) of 35% with multivessel territory wall motion abnormalities and no evidence of vegetations to suggest infective endocarditis. Repeat blood cultures eventually showed clearance of the MSSA, and he completed the course of culture-directed antibiotics.

He remained asymptomatic post-hospitalisation and was tolerating regular dialysis sessions.

Clinical findings

He presented again 8 weeks later for an elective staged PCI to the right coronary artery (RCA). However, the relook angiogram of the left coronary system revealed no flow in the LM-LAD stents with rapid contrast filling of a large circular cavity adjacent to the proximal LM-ostial LAD (Fig. 2). Different projections revealed that the cavity was not intracardiac, thus raising the possibility of a giant coronary PSA. The patient was taken off table for further evaluation with multimodality imaging.Fig. 2Relook angiogram images of the left coronary system 8 weeks post index PCI showing contrast extravasation into a large circular cavity (white outlines) adjacent to the LM with no flow into the LAD (indicating previous LM-LAD stents)

Diagnostic assessment

TTE confirms a large pseudoaneurysm adjacent to the left coronary cusp, with the presence of spontaneous echo contrast and a clear connection between the origin of the LM and the cavity on colour doppler interrogation.

Computed tomography coronary angiography (CTCA) showed the large pseudoaneurysm measuring 8.3 × 7.4 cm, for which the origin was deemed to be at the junction of the LM and LAD. A HEART team discussion was held between interventional cardiologists and cardiothoracic surgeons to determine the best option for management. The team agreed that this giant coronary PSA could not be left untreated due to the high risk of spontaneous rupture given its very large size. The surgical team assessed the patient as high-surgical risk owing to the complexity of the procedure, low LVEF and underlying ESKD. Thus, percutaneous closure with covered stents was deemed the only feasible approach. The team also concluded that PCI to the RCA should be performed first given the residual severe disease in a large dominant vessel.

Therapeutic intervention

We started with upfront insertion of a 50 cc 8 F IABP catheter. Thereafter, PCI to the RCA was first performed with implantation of 2 DES (3.5 and 4.0 mm) after calcium modification with rotational atherectomy (Boston scientific, Massachusetts, USA) and intravascular lithotripsy (Fig. 5). Minimal contrast and radiation were utilised given the expected challenges and time required for PCI to the LAD PSA. This was made possible with intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) guidance.Fig. 5RCA pre (A) and post (B) PCI

Next, we proceeded for PCI to the LM-LAD giant PSA. As there was no visualisation of LAD flow due to contrast diversion into the PSA cavity, we initially confirmed LM-LAD stent patency by means of routine wiring and distal contrast injections via a guide-extension catheter (GEC).

IVUS imaging was then performed to better characterise the extent and location of the “neck” of the PSA. This unexpectedly revealed a region of ~ 30 mm within the proximal to mid LAD and ostial LM stents showing clear breaks in the vessel wall with un-opposed stent struts (Fig. 6). This raised the possibility of multiple points of communication from the LM-LAD into the same PSA cavity, contrary to what was initially suggested on TTE and CTCA. As such, the strategy was adjusted accordingly to firstly optimize the old stents before placing the covered stents. Otherwise, this would risk inadequate expansion of the covered stents resulting in unsuccessful sealing of the PSA. Previous LM-LAD stent optimization was performed using initial dilation with 3.0 mm NC balloon, IVL with a 3.5 mm Shockwave C2 + (Shockwave medical Inc, California, USA) and finally a 4.0 mm NC balloon.

After stent optimization, 2 overlapping 3.5 × 26 mm PK Papyrus covered stents (Biotronik, Berlin, Germany) were placed from the ostial to mid LAD. These were post dilated with 3.5 and 4.0 mm NC balloons (stent expansion limit ~ 4.65 mm).

Prior to LM-LAD covered stenting, we planned to utilize the ‘jailed balloon technique’ to preserve LCx flow – adapting the strategies from bail-out PCI techniques of LM perforations. The ostial LCX (across the existing stent strut) was initially dilated. Thereafter, a 4.5 × 15 mm PK Papyrus covered stent was placed across the LM-LAD with a 2.0 mm compliant balloon position across the ostial LCx. Both balloons were inflated simultaneously and removed (Fig. 8). Prior to post dilation, fenestration of the 4.5 mm covered stent into the LCx was attempted with Conquest Pro 12 (Asahi Intecc, Japan) and Astato XS 20 (Asahi Intecc, Japan) wires mounted on a dual lumen microcatheter. We decided to prioritize optimization of the 4.5 mm LM covered stent first before risking any stent deformation from further attempts of advancing balloons into the LCx. Post-dilation of the LM covered stent was done with 4.0 and 5.0 mm NC balloons (stent expansion limit ~ 5.63 mm).

After deployment and post dilation of 3 covered stents from the LM to mid LAD, check angiogram showed improvement in LM-LAD flow but presence of multiple regions of persistent leaks mainly within the LM-ostio-proximal LAD region. As the main goal of the procedure was for sealing of the PSA, 3 more covered stents (sizes 4.0 and 5.0 mm) were deployed. This finally led to the successful sealing of all leaks and restoration of TIMI 3 flow in the LAD (Fig. 9). There was TIMI 1 flow into the LCx, which was not unexpected given the partially successful fenestration and rewiring of the vessel. Fortunately, the patient had a very dominant RCA and the reduction of LCx flow was well tolerated, with no symptomatic or hemodynamic consequences.Fig. 9Successful sealing of all leaks into the giant PSA cavity () with restoration of TIMI 3 flow down the LAD after insertion of 6 overlapping covered stents (red arrows)

A repeat TTE done 4 months later showed complete resolution of the pseudoaneurysm cavity (Fig. 10). The LVEF remained stable at 35% since the initial NSTEMI presentation with LAD territory hypokinesia.

Unfortunately, he declined a repeat CT coronary angiogram.

He was reviewed in subsequent clinic visits and was tolerating regular dialysis sessions without issues. After 1 year of being on dual anti platelet therapy (DAPT), his haemoglobin level was stable at 12.1 g/dL (ref 13.6–16.6 g/dL), and he had no bleeding manifestations. Given that he had multiple covered stents placed with risks of in-stent restenosis and thrombosis, he was continued on long term DAPT in addition to aggressive LDL lowering therapies. His LDL levels were measured to be 1.4 mmol/L on review 1 year later – an acceptable decrement from his original levels of 3.0 mmol/L.

Discussion

In this case, there was a clear indication to definitively treat the patient’s giant PSA given its rapid progression in size and presence of coronary steal phenomenon. Left untreated, the potential sequelae included spontaneous rupture with tamponade and LAD territory ischemia or infarction with subsequent malignant arrhythmias.

Given its complexity, a HEART team discussion was imperative to explore management options before embarking on the procedure. Furthermore, acquiring comprehensive information on the size and location of the PSA through multi-modality imaging also proved essential in pre-procedural planning. While the patient remained symptom free and tolerating dialysis sessions, the decision to proceed with intervention was more prophylactic given the possibility of spontaneous rupture leading to catastrophic outcomes. In the same vein, viability assessment of the LAD territory was not pursued.

The successful use of covered stents for percutaneous closure of coronary PSAs has been documented in case reports [2]. Other proposed management options include the deployment of coils. In our case, however, given the size and extent of the PSA, along with multiple communication sites, achieving a complete seal with coils would be technically unfeasible. Furthermore, the risk of coil embolization into the LM or LAD will lead to more detrimental consequences.

Despite meticulous pre-procedural planning, we were still faced with the unexpected challenge of plugging multiple leaks along the entire length of the LM to mid-LAD. Initially, it was believed that there was one “neck” originating from the LM-LAD junction, as confirmed on both TTE and CTCA. However, it was later understood in retrospect that, due to the lack of contrast flow into the proximal-mid LAD resulting from the coronary steal phenomenon, the more distal leaks beyond the level of LM could not be detected on CTCA. This could only be postulated based on IVUS findings and confirmed via GEC-assisted contrast injections. Nonetheless, we remained steadfast in the strategy of covered stenting and sealed all leaks.

The strategy for PCI to the giant LM PSA was adapted from bailout techniques in PCI of LM coronary perforations [3]. The drawback of LM covered stenting is the potential for jailing of side branches; in this case the circumflex artery (LCx). Despite utilization of the jailed balloon technique and successful fenestration of the covered stent with stiff wires, the final angiogram still showed only TIMI 1 flow in the LCx. We decided to accept that result given that the patient has a large dominant RCA and did not experience any chest pain or show any ischemic ECG changes peri or post procedurally.

Despite careful optimisation of the pre-existing LM–LAD stents and IVUS-guided deployment of 3 overlapping covered stents across the affected segment, repeat angiography demonstrated persistent extravasation. Complete sealing was only achieved following implantation of a total of 6 covered stents.

We acknowledge that this represents an unusually high number of stents with significant cost implications. We postulate that the need for multiple overlapping covered stents was multifactorial. First, the presence of extensive and multiple leaking sites along the vessel likely precluded complete sealing with a single stent. Second, severe underlying coronary calcification may have impeded optimal stent expansion and apposition, resulting in persistent microleaks that required additional stent layers to achieve adequate sealing.

Furthermore, we were cognisant of the long-term thrombotic and in-stent restenosis risks of deploying multiple covered stents. To mitigate this, the patient was placed on aggressive LDL lowering therapies and long term DAPT as tolerated. Limited data is available currently to guide decision making on the duration of DAPT required in patients with multiple covered stents. Our decision for long term DAPT in this case was extrapolated from the DAPT and PEGASUS TIMI 54 trials given that the patient naturally falls under the very high ischemic risk based on his risk factor profile compounded by the presence of multiple long covered stents. However, close follow up is necessary to ensure tolerance with no major bleeding manifestations given his ESKD.

Causes of coronary PSAs include trauma to the vessel wall during PCI, inflammation, infection and less commonly external trauma [4]. In this unique case, several of these mechanisms synergistically contributed to the development of a giant PSA.

Active MSSA bacteraemia during the index procedure likely played a significant role in the pathogenesis. The primary operator at that time felt the presence of ongoing ischemic symptoms and hemodynamic instability necessitated urgent angiography and PCI rather than deferring until the bacteraemia was adequately treated. S. Aureus is the most common pathogen in documented cases of coronary stent infections. The anatomically extensive involvement raises the possibility that the PSA may represent a sequela of an infected stent or a healed peri-stent abscess, resulting in weakening of the vessel wall and predisposing to PSA formation. Notably, the repeat TTE during the index hospital stay did not reveal any evidence of infective endocarditis, suggesting a more localised infective process.

Procedural factors from the index intervention likely further contributed. On review of the procedure, the primary operator was faced with a critically occluded, heavily calcified LM-LAD and initial pre-dilation efforts resulted in significant flow limiting dissections warranting bailout stenting. In this setting, optimal lesion preparation was necessarily compromised, increasing the likelihood of stent under-expansion although TIMI 3 flow was restored. IVUS imaging during the procedure for PCI to the PSA confirmed suboptimal stent expansion.

Taken together, the combination of deep coronary dissections, stent under-expansion in a heavily calcified vessel, and concurrent bacteraemia likely created a substrate for progressive vessel wall injury, impaired healing, and eventual PSA formation.

Conclusion

While coronary PSAs are already an uncommon complication post-PCI [5], giant PSAs are exceedingly rare. The available evidence reveals that surgery was the primary treatment modality. We present a case of an 8.3 × 7.4 cm coronary PSA that was successfully treated percutaneously with covered stents. There was another layer of complexity in that we were not only dealing with a giant PSA but one with multiple leaks/necks to be addressed.

This case highlights the potential for percutaneous techniques to address large coronary PSAs when guided by careful planning and imaging, and underscores the importance of an individualised, anatomy-driven approach in rare and high-risk scenarios.
