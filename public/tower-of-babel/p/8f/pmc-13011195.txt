N-acetylated chito-oligosaccharides and xylanase improve intestinal health and production performance in laying hens
Authors: Veerle Van Hoeck, Riet Spaepen, Ingrid Somers, Anas Abdelqader, Bart Forier, Zeinab Mahasneh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSynergistic feed additives that target the gut–immune axis offer a promising strategy to enhance health, nutrient utilization, and productivity. N-acetylated chito-oligosaccharides (NACOS) provide stimbiotic, antimicrobial, and immunomodulatory benefits, while xylanase improves non-starch polysaccharide degradation, nutrient availability, and microbial fermentation. This study evaluated a novel dual-action strategy combining NACOS, xylanase, or their combination (CXNRGY) on production performance, nutrient utilization, jejunal histomorphology, systemic immune status, and the cecal microbial community in laying hens. Forty HiSex White laying hens were randomly assigned to four dietary groups and fed either a control diet (CD) or the CD supplemented with NACOS (50 g/t), Xylanase (45,000 U/kg), or CXNRGY at the same respective doses. Dietary supplementation improved production metrics: egg weight was highest in the CXNRGY group compared with control and NACOS (P < 0.001), while egg mass increased in Xylanase, NACOS and CXNRGY groups compared to control in the second phase (P < 0.001). Xylanase and CXNRGY achieved the lowest FCR compared with NACOS and the control group. Haugh unit and albumen height were elevated in NACOS and Xylanase treatments (P < 0.05), whereas CXNRGY maximized shell thickness and breaking strength (P < 0.001). Nutrient utilization of crude fat, organic matter, and apparent metabolizable energy (AME) was significantly improved by NACOS, while Xylanase and CXNRGY further enhanced fiber and protein utilization. NACOS produced the greatest improvements in jejunal morphology (P < 0.05) and, along with Xylanase and CXNRGY, reduced serum IL-1α, TNF-α, and IL-10 (P < 0.05), while IL-1β and IL-6 were unaffected (P > 0.05). Notably, CXNRGY altered the cecal microbiota by increasing the relative abundance of Lachnospiraceae, Ruminococcaceae, Subdoligranulum, Prevotellaceae and Clostridia, while decreasing Bacteroidaceae and Negativicutes, indicating a synergistic effect on microbial community structure. Xylanase enhances nutrient utilization and prebiotic availability, while NACOS strengthens gut barrier integrity and modulates immune responses. These results indicate that combining NACOS with xylanase provides a synergistic, antibiotic-free nutritional strategy that enhances gut health, immunity, egg quality, and overall performance in laying hens.Keywords: Laying Hens, Intestinal Microbiome, N-Acetylated Chito-Oligosaccharides, Xylanase

Introduction

The concept of "intestinal health" is of major importance in animal agriculture, as it is fundamentally linked to livestock productivity, including growth, yield, and survival (Ducatelle et al., 2025). In poultry, optimizing production efficiency and health is a primary focus (Bryden et al., 2021). A key factor in this endeavor is the intestinal microbiota, whose balance critically interacts with nutrient digestion, absorption, and utilization (Abdelqader et al., 2025). To positively modulate this gut ecosystem, prebiotics and the emerging category of stimbiotics have been employed as promising feed additives. Prebiotics are defined as non-digestible dietary substrates selectively utilized by beneficial gut microorganisms, conferring health benefits to the host. In contrast, stimbiotics are low-dose feed additives that stimulate the fermentative activity of the resident microbiota, particularly fiber-degrading bacteria, without acting as fermentable substrates themselves (Lee et al., 2022). Their application has proven benefits for gut function, integrity, and overall host performance (Jha et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2022). For instance, in laying hens, the prebiotic-mediated regulation of gut microbiota can influence nutrient metabolism, thereby affecting key production parameters like egg weight, laying rate, and feed efficiency (Bryden et al., 2021). Despite these advances, the discovery and development of novel, effective stimbiotic compounds remains a critical challenge. Consequently, there is a clear need for novel nutraceutical feed additives that can simultaneously enhance intestinal health and animal performance.

Among the wide range of prebiotics, functional xylo-oligosaccharides are attracting the attention of scientific community and poultry farming as well (Morgan, 2023). The N-acetylated chito-oligosaccharide (NACOS) is a functional oligosaccharide with functional reactive groups that enhance its biological activity. The NACOS is a derivative of chitin, composed of β-(1→4)-linked N-acetyl-D-glucosamine units. Its key functional groups, primary hydroxyl (-OH), secondary hydroxyl, and the acetamido (-NHCOCH₃) group, are central to its biological activity (Guan et al., 2019; Mittal et al., 2023). In chickens, NACOS was competent to boost performance and reduction the prevalence of intestinal injuries, elevate albumen height and quality, improve egg quality, eggshell thickness, and productive performance (Gu et al., 2022; Meng et al., 2010). However, it is very important to study the acetylation degree of the chito-oligosaccharides (COS) used in the latter studies; most COS used in these studies are not well characterized and retrieved from chitosan and thus not chitin. While both chitin and its derivative chitosan are recognized for gut health benefits, their effects differ substantially due to divergent physicochemical properties. Chitin, an insoluble polymer of N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, functions primarily as a bioactive compound. In contrast, chitosan, produced by the deacetylation of chitin, is soluble in acidic conditions and possesses a net positive charge. This makes chitosan bioaccessible and enables direct mechanisms such as antimicrobial activity via electrostatic interaction with pathogen membranes and modulation of the gut microbiota, which are not prominent features of chitin (Guan et al., 2019). This implies that the resulting oligosaccharides from chitosan are not acetylated. N-acetylated chito-oligosaccharide represents a new category of non-toxic, cost-effective, sustainable, water-soluble, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant natural products (Deng et al., 2020; Hao et al., 2022; Mittal et al., 2023). The NACOS should have a degree of polymerization (DP) of 20 or less, a degree of acetylation of at least 70 %, and a polydispersity index (PDI) of 10 or less (Knaul et al., 1999). When focusing on NACOS, it is important to keep in mind that chitin, from which the NACOS originates, is one of the most important biopolymers in nature (Knaul et al., 1999). Chitin, the second most abundant natural polysaccharide, and its chemical structure serve as a basis for producing soluble, bioactive derivatives such as chitosan and chito-oligosaccharides (Guan et al., 2019). Research indicates that the antimicrobial efficacy of COS is largely dependent on their acetylated sequences. Specifically, NACOS with a higher degree of acetylation and consequently fewer free amino groups demonstrate greater antimicrobial action (Sánchez et al., 1999). Compared with conventional COS, NACOS provide superior stability, solubility, higher degree of acetylation, and bioavailability, along with stronger immunomodulatory, antimicrobial, antioxidant, and gut barrier-supportive effects (Deng et al., 2020,Hao et al., 2022; Mittal et al., 2023). NACOS exert their effect primarily through immunomodulation, acting as ligands for innate immune receptors. This interaction primes the host immune system, leading to an enhanced gut environment that selectively supports the growth and activity of beneficial bacterial communities (Deng et al., 2020). These multifunctional properties make NACOS an ideal complement to xylanase, maximizing intestinal health, performance, and resilience in laying hens. Their complementary mechanisms stem from xylanase improving the intestinal substrate landscape, degrading anti-nutritional NSPs and releasing prebiotic fibers, while NACOS enhance the host environment by fortifying the gut barrier and providing immunomodulatory priming. Together, they create a reinforcing interaction that maximizes nutrient utilization, microbial balance, and intestinal resilience, making their combined use a strategic approach for optimizing laying hen health and productivity. The global feed enzyme market is dominated by NSP-degrading enzymes, which represent more than 50 % of its share, within this category, xylanases are increasingly favored over β-glucanases (Bedford, 2022). This dominance reflects the high abundance of arabinoxylans in widely used poultry feedstuffs and the broad efficacy of xylanases in reducing viscosity, improving nutrient utilization, and supporting gut health, whereas β-glucanases have a narrower application limited mainly to barley- and oat-based diets (Bedford, 2022). Wheat has become an eminent feed ingredient in poultry diets. The absence of xylanase in wheat-based diet showed that the intestinal microbiota can shift towards the pathogenic profile of intestinal microbiota, however xylanase not only reduces the anti-nutritional properties of NSP but also improved intestinal hemostasis by enhancing the beneficial microbiota populations (Van Hoeck, et al., 2021). Previous studies on the Xylanase proved its efficiency in enhancing the nutrient utilization and performance in laying hens, as well it enhanced the growth of several intestinal beneficial microbiota (Van Hoeck, et al., 2021).

This study establishes a novel dual-pathway mechanism in which xylanase enhances nutrient release, reduces intestinal viscosity, and generates prebiotic xylo-oligosaccharides (XOS) to support beneficial microbiota, while NACOS provide precision host support through immune modulation, pathogen inhibition, and gut barrier reinforcement. Laying hens have substantially extended production cycle and are routinely exposed to long-term nutritional, metabolic, and immunological pressures. Although laying hens can tolerate higher dietary fiber levels than broilers, prolonged exposure to NSP-rich diets may compromise nutrient utilization, intestinal integrity, and microbial stability, particularly as hens age. This complementary strategy is therefore particularly relevant for laying hens, which face unique physiological challenges associated with sustained egg production, high and continuous nutrient demands for egg and eggshell formation, and age-related declines in gut barrier function and immune competence. This study introduces, for the first time, an evaluation of structurally refined NACOS as a novel alternative to conventional COS in laying hen diets. Additionally, it pioneers the investigation into the synergistic effects of combining NACOS with dietary xylanase. The present study addresses this research gap by examining the effects of dietary inclusion of NACOS, xylanase, or their combination in laying hens. It evaluates their impact on performance, nutrient digestibility, jejunal morphology, immune responses, and the cecal microbiome. Together, these findings validate a holistic nutritional approach that enhances both microbial ecology and host defense. This combined strategy represents a next-generation solution to improve nutrient utilization, egg quality, and flock resilience under antibiotic-free production systems.

Materials and methods

CXNRGY products

N-acetylated chito-oligosaccharides (NACOS) have been purified from shrimp waste material by using a drum milling equipment (Retsch TM500, Edegem, Belgium). Chitin was converted to NACOS via a catalyst-assisted mechanochemical depolymerization process. High-energy collisions and shear forces generated during drum milling disrupted chitin crystallites and induced cleavage of β-(1→4) glycosidic linkages. The combined effects of mechanical impact, localized heat generation, and catalytic action promoted controlled chain scission, effectively reducing the long chitin polymer into soluble NACOS. The catalyst enhanced reaction efficiency and selectivity, allowing NACOS production under solvent-free conditions. The extracted NACOS were analyzed using ultra-high-performance size elimination chromatography combined with refractive index and mass spectrometry detection (UHPSEC-RI-MS) to determine their molecular weight distribution and degree of polymerization (DP) and also degree of acetylation. N-acetylated chito-oligosaccharide used in this study has a DP of 1 to 20, a degree of acetylation of at least 70 %, and a PDI of 1–10. The exact profile of the product used in this study as follow: 9 % GlcNAc, 11 % GlcNAc2, 18 % GlcNAc3, 20 % GlcNAc4, 18 % GlcNAc5, 14 % GlcNAc6, 6.7 % GlcNAc7 and 2 % GlcNAc8. Xygest™ HT (Kemin Animal Nutrition and Health, Herentals, Belgium) is a thermostable, mono-component β-1,4-endo-xylanase enzyme of the GH11 family. It is produced by Pichia pastoris expressing the Thermopolyspora flexuosa gene and is formulated to enhance dietary fiber degradation and improve energy utilization of the diets. Xygest HT has a minimum xylanase action of 3,000,000 U/g on a corn starch-constructed carrier.

Birds housing and experimental design

The Animal Ethics Committee of the Department of Animal Production, University of Jordan, approved all experimental procedures. Animals were reared and treated in compliance with the EU Directive 2010/63/EU covering the protection of the animals used for investigation or other scientific purposes.

An experiment with laying hens was performed to assess the impact of including xylanase and NACOS in the diet. More specifically, the performance (egg weight, egg production, egg mass and feed conversion ratio) was observed in HiSex White laying hens from 22 weeks of age. The experiment was conducted at the poultry research facility of the University of Jordan. Hens were housed individually in enriched cages (60 × 55 × 55 cm; 3,300 cm²/hen) under a 14 h light: 10 h dark lighting program. Cage enrichments included a nest box, litter to permit pecking and scratching, perches providing ≥15 cm/hen, and a claw-shortening device. Ambient temperature was maintained at 18–24°C (mean ≈ 22°C). A total of 40 laying hens were used in this experiment (4 diets, 10 replicates, 1 bird per replicate). A priori power analysis was performed using SAS software (PROC POWER; α = 0.05), based on the mean and standard deviation (SD = 0.014) obtained from previous laying hen trials conducted in the same facility. The analysis indicated that 10 birds per treatment across four treatments were sufficient to detect biologically relevant differences, yielding a statistical power of 0.99 and confirming the adequacy of the selected sample size. Layers were allocated into four treatments; T1: Control; T2: Xylanase (15 g/t; 45,000 U/kg); T3: NACOS (50 g/t); T4: CXNRGY (15 g/t Xylanase + 50 g/t NACOS). The Xylanase and/or NACOS were added directly to the feed. The diets were centered on wheat (∼55 %), sunflower and soybean meals. Table 1 presents the diets composition used in this study. Diets were prepared as mash feed, and hens had free access to feed and water during the entire trial. The health status and mortality of the birds were checked every morning throughout the research.

Performance parameters

The study was conducted over 60 days, consisting of two distinct phases: Phase 1 (days 1–30) and Phase 2 (days 31–60). The performance data presented correspond to these two phases. Body weight was determined for each hen at the start and end of the investigational time. On a daily basis, all eggs were gathered and documented per individual cage. The rate of lay (hen-day egg production) was assessed as the number of eggs assembled divided by the number of hens, multiplied by 100. The following performance parameters were calculated: Egg mass was derived from lay percentage and average egg weight. Eggs were commercially graded by weight. Individual feed intake for each hen was recorded on a weekly basis, and then the average daily feed intake (g/hen/day) was calculated. Feed conversion ratio (FCR) was then expressed as grams of feed consumed per gram of egg mass. All eggs yielded over a 24-hour cycle were gathered, weighed for each individual cage, and graded according to commercial standards. Data of egg weight classes were presented for each phase of the trial period. All the eggs collected from the last 7 days of phase 1 and of phase 2 of the experiment were used for the egg yolk color assessment using the DSM fan technique, and for Haugh Unit calculations. Haugh unit was calculated based on the egg’s albumen height and the eggs weight as formula mentioned in our previous published study (Abdelqader et al., 2025).

Digestibility trial

The digestibility trial was conducted in the same research facility at the same house where the experiment was run. All hens were included in the digestibility trial (10 hens from each treatment: 40 hens in total). A plastic tray was fitted under each individual hen while the cage floor made of wire-mesh. Titanium dioxide (TiO2) was incorporated in all investigational diets at a level of 5 g/kg as an indigestible indicator. The apparent total tract digestibility (ATTD) coefficients of dry matter (DM), organic matter (OM), crude fat, nitrogen (N), and crude fiber (CF) were determined. Gross energy (GE) content of the feed and excreta was measured, and the apparent metabolizable energy (AME) and nitrogen-corrected apparent metabolizable energy (AMEn) were subsequently calculated as described below. The digestibility trial consisted of a 5-day adaptation (pre-collection) period followed by a 4-day excreta collection period. Titanium dioxide was included in the diets throughout both the adaptation and collection periods as an indigestible marker. Fresh excreta samples were collected from each individual cage. All voided excreta were gathered twice a day and saved at –20°C until subsequent analysis. All feathers and scales were detached from the excreta. Excreta from individual hen was collected over the whole collection period, pooled and frozen at –20°C. Following a 72-hour oven-drying period at 60°C, both the diet and excreta samples were ground through a 0.5 mm screen using a mill grinder (Retsch ZM 100, Retsch GmbH, Haan, Germany) and subsequently analyzed for dry matter (DM) content (AOAC method 930.15), OM (AOAC method 942.05), protein content by Kjeldahl (AOAC method 988.05), crude fat by Soxhlet analysis (AOAC method 920.39), and crude fiber (AOAC method 962.09) conferring to the approaches of the Association of Official Analytical Chemists protocol. The gross energy (GE) was measured using an IKA® C5001 bomb calorimeter (IKA-Werke, Germany) by measuring their heat of combustion. Intestinal morphology indices

After the trial end (day 61 of the experiment), hens (10 layers/treatment; 40 hens in total) were euthanized by cervical dislocation and directly were taken for intestinal tissues sampling from the jejunal section for later histology evaluation. A three cm section from the middle of jejunum was cut, gently flushed with cooled PBS solution, and fixed in 10 % neutral buffered formalin (50 ml) for later analyses. A microtome (Leica RM 2125RT) was used to make sectioned at 5 μm cuts from each sample. These sections were equipped with glass slides and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stain. Sections from all treatment groups were examined microscopically using image analyzer software (LAS EZ software Leica Microsystems: Leica Imaging Systems Ltd., Cambridge, UK) to quantify villus basal, villus height, apical width, and crypt depth. The mean villus height and crypt depth for each bird were determined by averaging measurements from 8–13 intact and properly aligned crypt-villus units.

Villus surface area was estimated with the formula = (2π) × (VW/2) × (VH) in which VH = villus height and VW = villus width.

Inflammation and immune responses

On day 60 of the trial, blood samples from all hens were collected into plain (non–anticoagulant) tubes. The blood was left at room temperature for 30 min to allow it to clot. The samples were then centrifuged at 2,000 g for 15 min in a refrigerated centrifuge to separate the serum. The resulting supernatant (serum) was transferred into microcentrifuge tubes using pipettes and each sample was divided into 5 aliquots. Serum was stored at –20°C until use. Five serum cytokines were measured: Interleukin-1 alpha (IL-1α), Tumor Necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), Interleukin-6 (IL-6), and Interleukin-10 (IL-10). Serum levels of the above cytokines were measured using commercially available ELISA kits specific for chickens provided by MyBioSource [MyBioSource, Inc., San Diego, CA 92195-3308, USA. www.mybiosource.com]. The procedures were executed in accordance with the manual; IL-1α (Catalog# MBS7606966), IL-1β (Catalog# MBS454453), IL-6 (Catalog# MBS288783), IL-10 (Catalog# MBS266842), and TNF-α (Catalog# MBS26).

Microbiome

The euthanized hens were used first to collect cecal digesta content. Upon euthanasia, the hens were put under highly hygienic and aseptic conditions, the abdominal cavity was opened, and the cecal digesta content of both ceca were collected. The cecal material was squeezed into Eppendorf tubes and kept at -80°C for later analyses. The QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (Qiagen) was used to extract DNA. Bacterial phylogenetic relationships and taxonomic classifications were reconstructed from 16S rRNA gene sequences. Sequencing libraries were prepared and sequenced using a paired-end 250 bp (PE250) approach on the Illumina platform, taking advantage of high-throughput sequencing technologies to obtain high-quality, high-coverage reads (Caporaso et al., 2012). Downstream bioinformatic analyses, including quality filtering, operational taxonomic unit (OTU) clustering, and taxonomic assignment, were performed to accurately profile microbial community structure and diversity. Every PCR reaction was conducted in 30 μL reactions using 0.2 μM forward and reverse primers, 15 μL of Phusion™ High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs), and about 10 ng of template DNA. The first step in thermal cycling is denaturation at 98 °C for 1 min, which is followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 50 °C for 30 s, and elongation at 72 °C for 60 s. The last phase was 72°C for five minutes. (i) PCR products quantification and qualification: Combine equal amounts of 1X loading buffer (which contains SYBER green) with PCR products, then run electrophoresis on a 2 % agarose gel to detect the results. Samples containing a single, 400–450 bp brilliant main strip were selected for additional testing. (ii) PCR products mixing and purification: equidensity ratios were used to mix the PCR products. The GeneJET Gel Extraction Kit (Thermo Scientific) was then used to purify the combined PCR products. (iii) Library preparation and sequencing: The NEB Next® UltraTM DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) was used to create sequencing libraries in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions, and index codes were added. The Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system and the Qubit@ 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific) were used to evaluate the library's quality. (iv) Sequencing data processing: by coupling particular primers with barcodes, PCR was carried out. Using the NEBNext® Ultra™ IIDNA Library Prep Kit (Cat No. E7645), the DNA libraries were constructed. Then, using the Illumina paired-end platform (Novaseq6000 platform PE250), 250 bp paired-end raw reads were generated by end repairing, adding A to tails, purification, and amplicon sequencing. These were then combined and pre-treated to produce clean tags. The effective tags, which can be utilized for further investigation, were obtained by detecting and eliminating the chimeric sequences in the clean tags.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using JMP® software (Version 15.0.0, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, 1989–2019). The numerical data were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA model. The model included the fixed main effects of NACOS, xylanase, and their interaction, with replicate included as a random effect. Data were first tested for normality and homogeneity of variances using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. The interaction between treatment and replicate was included in the model to account for potential variability among experimental units. Differences among treatment means were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05 and were separated using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post-hoc test. Replicate was treated as a random factor in all analyses. A chi square test was used to evaluate the treatment effect on proportion of dirty egg and commercial classification. To analyze species diversity for microbiome characterization, all Effective Tags were grouped (clustered) using Uparse software (Uparse v7.0.1001, http://drive5.com/uparse/). Differences in individual taxonomic abundance were confirmed using Metastats software, and LEfSe was applied for the quantitative analysis of group-specific biomarkers. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) was used to identify potential biomarkers by modeling a dependent variable as a linear combination of features, similar to ANOVA and regression. Principal component analysis (PCA), based on Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASV) relative abundances, reduces data dimensionality through orthogonal transformation, highlighting the main structures in a two-dimensional plot. In PCA, sample proximity reflects microbial community similarity, with more similar compositions clustering together.

Results

Effect of NACOS and Xylanase on performance parameters

Laying hens of all additive groups were of similar average body weight through the pre-trial period and at the start of the trial (Table 2). This trend continued toward the end of phase 1. At the end of the experiment, CXNRGY showed higher (P = 0.01) average body weight compared with the control, with no significant effect of treatments on hens’ body weights of xylanase and NACOS (Table 2). No significant NACOS × Xylanase interaction was detected on body weight (P = 0.846). Hens fed the Xylanase and CXNRGY produced heavier eggs than the control group during phase 1 of the experiment (P < 0.001). During phase 2 of the experiment, all treatments showed significant increase in eggs weight compared with the control, where the CXNRGY had the maximum (P < 0.001) egg weight. On average, NACOS had egg weight 1.6 grams heavier (P < 0.001) than the control, while Xylanase and CXNRGY had respectively 2.3 and 3.1 grams heavier (P < 0.001) eggs than the control (Table 2). Furthermore, a significant NACOS × Xylanase interaction was detected (P = 0.035). This interaction is evidenced by the differential response: the combined CXNRGY group achieved a higher egg weight than would be expected from the single additive effects, indicating a synergistic effect in the later production phase. Laying rate was improved (P = 0.005) by Xylanase and CXNRGY treatments, compared with the control. During phase 2, no significant effects of any dietary treatments were observed on laying rate. The Xylanase and CXNRGY treatments exhibited the maximum egg mass compared with the control. During the second phase, egg mass was improved (P = 0.001) in the NACOS, Xylanase, and CXNRGY groups compared with the control. Feed intake reduced significantly (P < 0.05) in Xylanase and CXNRGY treatments compared with the control. The best values of feed conversion ratios were recorded in Xylanase and CXNRGY treatments followed by the NACOS inclusion, which were significantly higher (P < 0.001) than the control. This trend was the same throughout the experimental periods (Table 2). No significant NACOS × Xylanase interaction was observed for laying rate, egg mass, or average daily feed intake (all P > 0.05). While the main effects on FCR of both NACOS and Xylanase were highly significant (P < 0.001), a significant NACOS × Xylanase interaction was detected (P < 0.001) in phase 2. The improvement in FCR from the combination (1.802, P < 0.001) was greater than would be expected from the additive effects of the individual use, indicating a synergistic enhancement of feed efficiency in the later production phase.

No significant NACOS × Xylanase interaction was detected for any egg quality parameter (P > 0.05) except for shell thickness in the first phase.

Nutrient digestibility

Values of digestibility coefficients of different nutrients are summarized in Table 5. The digestibility coefficients of organic matter, dry matter, crude fat, and AME were significantly improved by all dietary treatments. The maximum crude protein and crude fiber digestibility was observed in Xylanase, and CXNRGY treatments followed by the NACOS (P < 0.001), while all were significantly higher than the control treatment (Table 5). Enzyme supplementation significantly improved the digestibility of crude fat and fibre (P < 0.001), with notable interactions (P ≤0.001) between Xylanase and NACOS. For fat, a significant interaction (P = 0.001) reflected a plateau, where the CXNRGY numerically increased fat digestion, but combination did not significantly surpass Xylanase alone. For fibre, a strong synergistic interaction (P < 0.001) was observed; however, the numerical increase from Xylanase (23.26 %) to the CXNRGY combination (23.61 %) was not statistically significant, suggesting Xylanase was the primary driver of fiber breakdown.

Intestinal morphology

As depicted in Fig. 1, all bird groups studied displayed well-preserved epithelial cells, indicating that the mucous membranes remained intact without any degradation during preparation. The lamina and muscular layer elements developed uniformly across all chickens. Birds treated with NACOS, Xylanase, or their CXNRGY combination showed significant improvements in the villi structure compared with the control. In contrast, the control treatment often exhibited damaged villi structure, characterized by the desquamation of epithelial cells and denuded villi tips (Fig. 1). Conversely, the NACOS, Xylanase treatments, and their combination showed an integrated villi-crypt structure compared to the control. The highest villi were measured in the NACOS treatment, followed by the Xylanase, then the CXNRGY mix, while the shortest villi were in control group (Table 6). All these differences were highly significant (P < 0.001). Villi width was only increased (P =0.0125) in NACOS treatment, while Xylanase and CXNRGY had no effect on villi width, compared with the control (Table 6). Crypt depth was significantly increased (P < 0.001) in NACOS treatment compared with all other treatments. In the same manner, hens of NACOS treatment exhibited the maximum (P < 0.001) villi surface area, followed by Xylanase and CXNRGY, which all were higher (P < 0.001) than the control treatment. Gut morphology parameters (more specifically, villus height and villus surface area) showed significant NACOS × Xylanase interactions (P < 0.001). NACOS alone markedly increased villus height and surface area, but these improvements were significantly reduced when NACOS was supplemented in combination with xylanase.

Intestinal microbial

Analysis of 16S rRNA gene sequence variation to characterize the cecal microbiota revealed that dietary supplementation with NACOS substantially diminished the abundance of potentially harmful bacteria in laying hens. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) of the cecal microbiota revealed distinct bacterial taxa associated with each treatment group (Fig. 2). In the CXNRGY-supplemented group, several taxa including Clostridia (class), Lachnospirales (order), Lachnospiraceae (family), Ruminococcaceae (family), Subdoligranulum (genus), Oscillospirales (order), and Prevotellaceae (family) were significantly enriched, as indicated by negative LDA scores. Conversely, in the control group, taxa such as Bacteroides (genus), Bacteroidaceae (family), Negativicutes (class), and Selenomonadales (order) were more abundant, reflected by positive LDA scores. These LDA scores, exceeding the threshold of 4, indicate that the identified taxa could serve as potential biomarkers differentiating the microbial communities between the two groups. Principal component analysis (PCA) of the cecal microbiota revealed distinct clustering patterns between the CXNRGY and control groups (Fig. 3). The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) explained 10.27 % and 9.2 % of the total variance, respectively. Samples from the CXNRGY group clustered closely together, indicating a more consistent microbial composition within this group, whereas the control group showed greater dispersion. These results suggest that CXNRGY supplementation altered the overall microbial community structure, contributing to a shift in cecal microbiota composition compared with the control group.

Discussion

This study pioneers the in vivo application of NACOS as a refined, next-generation alternative to conventional COS in laying hens. Moreover, by uniquely combining NACOS with Xylanase, it reveals a synergistic nutritional strategy that modulates intestinal physiology and microbial ecology, offering a novel approach to enhance gut function. The present findings confirmed that combining Xylanase with NACOS provides complementary benefits to laying hens, as reflected by improvements in performance, nutrient utilization, gut morphology, and cecal microbial composition. Notably, the combined treatment consistently produced greater improvements across these parameters than either additive alone, confirming the synergistic interaction. The NACOS are a structurally refined subset of COS with a higher degree of acetylation, which enhances their biological activity. While most previous poultry studies focus on COS, the present results suggest that NACOS retain similar, and potentially amplify, core benefits related to nutrient utilization, gut morphology, immunomodulatory function, and microbiota modulation. All data have been compiled in a patent application.

The improvements observed in performance and nutrient utilization with NACOS and Xylanase supplementation can be attributed to their complementary modes of action on the intestinal environment. Xylanase likely enhanced nutrient availability by degrading arabinoxylans and reducing digesta viscosity (Van Hoeck, et al., 2021), thereby improving nutrient diffusion and absorption, which is consistent with the increased apparent digestibility observed in this study. In parallel, NACOS may have supported intestinal integrity and microbial balance (Guan et al., 2019), as reflected by the improved jejunal morphology and shifts in cecal microbiota. The greater responses observed with the combined CXNRGY treatment suggest that enhanced substrate availability from Xylanase activity may have amplified the prebiotic and immunomodulatory effects of NACOS, leading to synergistic improvements in gut function and overall performance. Because chito-oligosaccharides resist enzymatic digestion and are only partially utilized by gut microbiota, they can exert localized effects along the intestinal epithelium (Je and Kim, 2012; Lodhi et al., 2014). This characteristic may partly explain the improvements in jejunal morphology and the specific changes in cecal microbial composition observed in CXNRGY-fed hens in the present study. The enhanced antimicrobial activity reported for NACOS, can be attributed to their higher acetyl group content (Sánchez et al., 1999), and may partly explain the selective modulation of cecal microbiota observed in CXNRGY-fed hens in this study.

The body weight of the control group was slightly less than the standard weight of the HiSex strain introduced by the guideline. This was expected because the basal diet in this study is based on wheat, and the absence of xylanase would lead to a reduction in energy utilization. The findings partially align with Taylor et al. (2018), indicating that adding xylanase to the diet (at 0 or 12,000 BXU/kg) improved feed efficiency and intestinal health. The higher body weight observed in CXNRGY-fed hens may be linked to the improved nutrient digestibility measured in this study, likely driven by Xylanase-induced release of additional metabolizable energy from the diet. Consistent with the present results, Widjaja et al. (2025) reported that Xylanase supplementation in laying hens improved egg production and increased dietary apparent metabolizable energy (AMEn). In this study, the improved nutrient digestibility and performance responses observed in the Xylanase-supplemented groups support the role of Xylanase in enhancing energy availability from fibrous feed components, which likely contributed to the observed improvements in production parameters.

The performance responses observed in this trial further support the complementary actions of NACOS and Xylanase. While individual supplementation with either NACOS or Xylanase improved egg weight, the greater increases observed in the CXNRGY group suggest a synergistic effect. This response is likely related to improved nutrient availability from Xylanase activity, together with enhanced intestinal integrity and absorptive efficiency associated with NACOS, which collectively supported higher egg mass output.

The enhanced egg quality observed with NACOS, Xylanase, and CXNRGY supplementation may be linked to the improved intestinal morphology measured in this study. Increased villus height and villus height-to-crypt depth ratio likely enhanced nutrient absorption, supporting egg formation. Similar improvements in intestinal structure and nutrient digestibility have been reported with supplementation of Xylanase (Abdollahi et al., 2021) and COS (Gu et al., 2022).

The lower serum concentrations of TNF-α, IL-1α, and IL-10 observed in Xylanase-, NACOS-, and CXNRGY-fed hens suggest reduced inflammatory activation compared with the control group. These changes may be associated with improved intestinal function and reduced gut-derived immune stimulation, as COS has been shown to alleviate intestinal inflammatory responses (Lan et al., 2021). Although birds were not subjected to an experimental challenge, the elevated TNF-α and IL-1α levels observed in the control group may reflect subclinical inflammation associated with the high-NSP diet. Consistent with our results, Gu et al. (2022) showed that COS reduced inflammatory responses, alleviated intestinal barrier damage, and reduced oxidative and immunological stress in LPS-challenged laying hens.

Analysis of the cecal microbiome revealed a clear shift in the CXNRGY group compared with the control, as reflected by the distinct separation observed in the PCA. These changes align with Wang et al. (2024), who reported that xylanase supplementation increased the relative abundance of Eubacterium hallii, Parabacteroides, and Lactobacillus, while reducing Barnesiella. The inclusion of titanium dioxide as an inert marker is not expected to substantially affect the gut microbiota, as it is largely non-digestible and biologically inert. Xylanase has also been shown to enhance immunity and protect chickens against pathogenic bacteria (Zhang et al., 2025), supporting the notion that CXNRGY supplementation contributes to improved intestinal health in laying hens. Consistent with previous reports that COS can modulate gut microbiota (Zhang et al., 2020), the present study demonstrates that NACOS similarly influence cecal microbial communities. However, due to their higher degree of acetylation, NACOS may provide more selective modulation compared with COS.

Conclusions

The synergistic combination of NACOS and Xylanase (CXNRGY) represents an effective nutritional strategy for laying hens, improving gut health, enhancing nutrient utilization, and supporting productivity. Inclusion of CXNRGY in the diet is recommended to optimize egg quality, promote beneficial cecal microbiota, and modulate inflammatory responses under high fiber feeding conditions.
