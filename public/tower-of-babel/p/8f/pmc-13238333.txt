Practice of Active Waiting: Leadership Education Curricula in Uncertain Times
Authors: Ericka Roland, Erica R Wiborg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAmid rising anti‐DEI pressures and increasing programmatic instability in higher education, leadership programs grounded in critical and equity‐centered frameworks face heightened vulnerability. This article uses critical collaborative autoethnography to explore how two faculty members navigate these uncertainties through active waiting, a strategic and reflective practice for curricular decision‐making. Through dialogic engagement, we identify dimensions of active waiting to support leadership educators working within contested institutional environments.

Introduction

There is a growing body of scholarship advocating for the inclusion of academic leadership education curricula rooted in critical frameworks, social justice perspectives, and culturally responsive practices that prepare students to navigate the social, political, and ethical complexities of today's world (Alvesson and Spicer 2014; Collinson 2018; Guthrie and Chunoo 2018). In this literature, leadership educators increasingly apply critical approaches across formal leadership degree programs, minors, and certificates, framing leadership learning as a space for examining power, equity, identity, and systemic injustice rather than as neutral or solely skills‐focused efforts.

However, the current higher education landscape is shaped by two intersecting and growing uncertainties. First, the rise of the anti‐diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) movement has increased political scrutiny, legislative actions, and public backlash against curricula and programs explicitly committed to DEI (Briscoe and Jones 2024a; Conyers and Wright Fields 2025). Second, institutions are facing ongoing programmatic instability marked by the closure, consolidation, restructuring, or defunding of academic programs amid changing enrollment patterns, financial constraints, and neoliberal accountability pressures (Nyre 2025). Together, these uncertainties threaten the integrity of curricula and the viability of formal leadership education programs.

Leadership degree, minor, and certificate programs based on critical frameworks, social justice values, and commitments to inclusive and pluralistic leadership are especially vulnerable in this environment (Briscoe and Jones 2024b; Liou and Alvara 2021). These programs are often seen as nonessential, ideologically contested, or expendable, making them susceptible to political targeting and administrative cuts. Consequently, leadership educators may be required to navigate complex processes of curricular negotiation, justification, and adaptation. However, there is limited scholarship exploring these two intersecting and growing uncertainties within academic leadership programs.

Amid these political and institutional uncertainties, leadership educators and program coordinators face a crucial question: How can they sustain, adapt, or reimagine leadership curricula rooted in equity and critical perspectives while navigating anti‐DEI pressures and program instability? We argue that addressing this question requires attention not only to curricular content and design but also to the relational, interpretive, and political work through which leadership educators interpret and act within uncertain institutional environments.

This article examines how two faculty members interpret the curricula for the undergraduate leadership minor and certificate as they navigate uncertainties within their respective institutional contexts. Rooted in your analysis, we conceptualize active waiting as a reflective and strategic practice that allows curriculum creators to navigate uncertainty within complex institutional and political contexts. Rather than passive delay, active waiting involves ongoing engagement with context, deliberate decision‐making, and preparation for imaginative futures. Our dialogic engagement identifies three interrelated dimensions of active waiting: understanding context, refusal, uncritical accommodation, and imaginative futures.

In the following sections, we begin with a brief review of the literature on curricula for academic leadership education programs and the two overlapping and growing uncertainties in higher education. Then, we describe our methodological approach, critical collaborative autoethnography, including a discussion of our leadership program context. Next, we share our findings through a dialogic conversation. We conclude with a discussion and a set of reflective questions to support leadership educators involved in designing and implementing curricula for academic programs.

Literature Review

Leadership development is increasingly recognized as a central component of undergraduate and graduate education. This recognition has resulted in the proliferation of leadership‐focused majors, minors, certificates, and courses (Astin and Astin 2000; Dugan and Komives 2007; White 2006). Academic leadership programs are inherently interdisciplinary and draw from theories from education, psychology, sociology, management, and political science (Jenkins and Dugan 2013; Komives 2011), among others. Over time, leadership minors have emerged as a strategic and accessible approach to providing leadership education (Diallo and Gerhardt 2017; Dugan and Komives 2007). According to White (2006), these minors frequently complement students’ primary fields of study by imparting foundational leadership skills.

Leadership minors also address institutional challenges, as leadership programs often encounter staffing shortages and a lack of faculty expertise in leadership education (Diallo and Gerhardt 2017; Pennington 2005). Interdisciplinary minors enable institutions to utilize faculty and instructors from multiple departments or divisions while maintaining academic rigor and focus. The literature emphasizes the importance of aligning leadership minors with the institution's mission and values (Chunoo and Osteen 2016). Programs that reflect university goals are more likely to receive sustained support (Arminio 2011; White 2006). Guthrie and Devies (2025) also regarded institutional context as critical in the development of leadership programs. While an interdisciplinary approach enables programs to adapt to specific institutional contexts, it can also generate ambiguity regarding the disciplinary identity of leadership studies (Brungardt et al. 2006; White 2006). Scholars consistently argue that leadership programs are particularly susceptible to closure within neoliberal institutions, especially given the current higher education environment.

Recent anti–DEI legislation has altered faculty instructional practices. Scholars advocate for the use of critical and relational frameworks to examine how power, privilege, and institutional structures influence leadership (Alvesson and Spicer 2014; Collinson 2018; Guthrie and Chunoo 2018). Faculty teaching leadership courses are particularly vulnerable in this legislative environment. According to scholars, anti‐DEI laws increasingly frame these subjects as politically or ideologically contentious (Briscoe and Jones 2024a; Conyers and Fields 2025). Consequently, faculty frequently engage in self‐censorship, even before any curriculum mandates are solidified. Leadership educators may omit or dilute discussions of race, gender, and LGBTQ+ inclusion in leadership, despite their importance for understanding contemporary organizations (Chunoo and Guthrie 2025; Guthrie et al. 2021).

Despite these constraints, the literature identifies adaptive strategies that faculty employ to sustain meaningful leadership education. Some educators reframe critical concepts using broader terms such as student involvement, effectiveness, mentorship, belonging, or preparedness, while continuing to address influence, ethics, voice, and power (Volpe White et al. 2019). Others expand course content by incorporating cross‐cultural perspectives and interdisciplinary analyses of inequality, without explicitly referencing DEI terminology (Sicka and Hou 2023). However, scholars caution that these approaches increase faculty workload and may diminish the effectiveness of leadership education (Guthrie et al. 2021; Liu and Alvara 2021). Avoiding discussions of power and identity risks reinforcing dominant norms and failing to prepare students for the complexities of contemporary society. As faculty committed to challenging dominant norms in leadership education and learning environments, we have been challenged to consider these constraints within our own contexts.

Critical Collaborative Autoethnography

We employed a critical collaborative autoethnographic methodology to investigate how, as leadership educators, we sustain, adapt, or reimagine leadership curricula rooted in equity and critical perspectives while navigating anti‐DEI pressures and programmatic instability. Critical collaborative autoethnography integrates personal narrative, cultural critique, and analysis of social power to illuminate how individuals experience and respond to broader structural conditions (Chang et al. 2016; Todić et al. 2025). The collaborative dimension centers multiple voices through shared storytelling, co‐analysis, and critical reflection (Chang et al. 2016; Todić et al. 2025).

As Lapadat (2017) and Todić et al. (2025) observed, collaborative autoethnography enables researchers to challenge dominant narratives, generate counterstories, and cultivate networks of support that extend beyond the research context, particularly within uncertain and hostile institutional environments. Guided by this approach, we engaged in co‐writing and shared analysis to interrogate power dynamics, examine our positionalities, and envision possibilities for social action. We conceptualize critical collaborative autoethnography as a relational and dialogical practice that fosters solidarity among ourselves as we navigate these challenging conditions in higher education. Having two co‐researchers enabled our critical, collaborative autoethnographic study to produce layered, multivoiced narratives shaped by mutual trust and an openness to deeply engaging with one another's experiences (Chang et al. 2016). Bringing together multiple perspectives not only enhances the study's rigor but also foregrounds our distinct contexts, which we describe in the following section (Chang et al. 2016). Ethically, we paid particular attention to the balance of our voices and experiences, and we committed to not naming specific individuals in our public narrative, prioritizing a critique of the environment and broader structural conditions.

To enact our relational and dialogical practice, the co‐researchers met three times. The first meeting focused on sharing current campus conditions through communal dialogue and developing journal prompts grounded in literature and lived experiences as leadership educators. We then individually journaled in response to four prompts before the next meeting: (1) How did you initially interpret the impact of anti‐DEI pressures and programmatic instability on the leadership program curriculum? (2) How have you navigated these uncertainties, and with whom (e.g., colleagues, administrators, and community partners) have you engaged in this process? (3) In what ways have you sustained, adapted, or reimagined the leadership education curriculum in response to these conditions? (4) How do you understand this moment in leadership education, and what does it mean for you as both a faculty member and a leadership education scholar?

The second meeting focused on meaning‐making through storytelling. Through dialogue, we identified convergence and divergence in our interpretations and experiences, posing critical questions to deepen reflection, surface assumptions, and consider alternatives, strengthening analytic rigor and relational depth. The final meeting focused on interpreting and presenting the findings and deciding how to share our dialogic conversation. This approach resisted traditional qualitative coding practices and instead privileged narrative and context within our co‐constructed process.

Leadership Program Context

Before sharing our dialogic findings, we wanted to provide our institutional and program contexts, as they have influenced how we have navigated the current anti‐DEI pressures. As you will see from our reflections, our academic program contexts differ and include both approved and proposed curricula. We both work in states that, while different, have either enacted anti‐DEI legislation or sustained anti‐DEI pressures from political leaders or governing bodies. We also acknowledge that, given our social identities and our positionality as junior tenure‐track faculty, we engage in intentional reflexivity to navigate the precarity of academic spaces, confront potential personal and scholarly risks, and sustain a commitment to advancing leadership education grounded in equity, justice, and critical hope.

Ericka

My institutional context is a public research 1 (R1) university in Texas with a Hispanic‐Serving Institution (HSI) designation and approximately 30,000 undergraduate students. The interdisciplinary undergraduate leadership minor (ILM) and certificate program are housed in the College of Education and serve students from a broad range of academic disciplines. In 2022, I thoroughly revised the program's curriculum, transforming the ILM into a cohesive and academically rigorous program rather than a collection of loosely connected courses. The new curriculum focuses on preparing students to address DEI issues and to drive social change through leadership.

A critical leadership framework (Dugan 2017) underpins the ILM curriculum, focusing on social power analysis, critical consciousness, and action. The program intentionally combines theory and practice by drawing from various disciplinary traditions, including education, critical theory, social movement history and theory, community organizing, organizational studies, and human relations.

Shortly after the curriculum redesign, Texas passed Senate Bill 17, which bans diversity, equity, and inclusion offices and DEI‐related programs, activities, and trainings (Hazel 2025). While there has been no official communication targeting the ILM curriculum, these conditions have limited partnerships with Student Affairs, especially restricting the program's ability to recruit and promote the minor and certificate through the university's leadership center. Despite these challenges, enrollment in ILM courses has increased significantly since the curriculum was redesigned and courses shifted to an online format.

Erica

My institution is a mid‐sized public research university in Virginia with deep liberal arts roots that recently achieved R1 status. Serving roughly 6,800 undergraduates across more than 115 majors and minors, the university is exploring the creation of an interdisciplinary leadership minor housed in the School of Education. Working with colleagues across campus, I am helping develop a curriculum designed to prepare students to engage in leadership across multiple contexts by strengthening their knowledge, skills, behaviors, attitudes, and values. The curriculum emphasizes leadership as influenced by context and prevailing frameworks, with consideration of relationships, roles, and structures that shape leadership engagement.

We are using the Leadership Learning Framework (Guthrie and Jenkins 2018) to guide development of the minor, focusing on six dimensions of leadership learning: knowledge, development, training, observation, engagement, and metacognition. Because the framework positions metacognition as central, we expanded this area to include critical thinking, critical self‐reflection, social perspective‐taking, and dialectical thinking, aligning with the institution's mission to cultivate leaders who contribute to a democratic society. Early conversations with campus partners highlighted the need for a curriculum structure that is accessible to a range of student demographics and incorporates diverse disciplinary traditions, aligning with an interdisciplinary approach to leadership education.

Although Virginia does not currently ban DEI initiatives at public universities, actions by the former governor and appointed board members have contributed to efforts to curtail DEI structures across the state. In response, my institution has reorganized DEI offices and reframed administrative roles, and the governing board adopted a resolution emphasizing merit‐based excellence, opportunity, and nondiscrimination. While university leadership has affirmed commitments to legal obligations, institutional values, and academic freedom, faculty and administrators often engage in self‐censorship. Since the governing board must approve new curricula, there is heightened pressure to align program language and outcomes with the resolution.

Active Waiting: Voices in Dialogue Across Institutional Complexity

In this section, we conceptualize active waiting as an adaptive approach to leadership curriculum development in the face of uncertainty. This framework is grounded in our analysis, dialogue, and the lived experiences of faculty members navigating politically and institutionally complex environments. We analyze how the state political landscape, in conjunction with the developmental stage of our leadership minor, influences decisions regarding sustaining, adapting, or reimagining curriculum. We also investigate tensions between refusal and compliance, concluding with a reflection on imagination as preparation for evolving political conditions. Our experiences serve to illustrate, rather than fully define, the concept. By highlighting key dimensions of active waiting, we seek to clarify its conceptual and practical possibilities. This discussion establishes a foundation for understanding context as a core practice, which we explore in the following section.

Understanding Context as a Practice of Active Waiting

A core aspect of active waiting is sustained engagement with the political, institutional, and legislative contexts that shape leadership curricula. Instead of responding impulsively, this approach emphasizes attentiveness, critical reflection, and strategic action when appropriate. Understanding context required analyzing the intersections among state legislation, institutional policies, and financial structures, particularly as they relate to the leadership minor's developmental stage. We anticipated that legislative changes and administrative interpretations could impact our programs. Rather than reacting reflexively, we prioritized information gathering, risk assessment, and the preservation of curricular integrity. As administrators interpreted these shifts, we concurrently developed strategies to address uncertainty. Ericka communicated the potential impact to department‐ and college‐level leadership, given that the ILM curriculum was already in place. She stated that she was not interested in changing the curriculum's critical orientation; therefore, she sought to understand state policies and politics regarding DEI bans and the responses of university administrators.

Practicing active waiting necessitates resisting premature self‐censorship. We grappled with questions of anticipation, such as the extent to which we should proactively prepare for potential mandates that had not yet materialized. We also considered whether anticipatory compliance might contribute to a chilling effect on leadership education curricula rooted in equity, critical inquiry, and social responsibility. Instead of immediately altering the curriculum in response to suspected threats, we chose to remain alert and informed, prepared to respond if mandates were issued, while ensuring that anxiety did not dictate our actions. Erica, given that her program is currently being proposed, needed to understand the full scope of the newly proposed curriculum approval process, as she assumed that governing boards extended minor approval processes to the administration. She needed to understand the decision‐making process and confirm whether there was a heightened risk of denying approval due to the use of equity language. We subsequently recognized the necessity of strategic preparation and intentional communication.

In response to uncertainty, we asserted our roles as curriculum creators and engaged purposefully with institutional partners. This process included strengthening assessment practices and collecting evidence demonstrating that the leadership minor aligns with the university's strategic plan and long‐term institutional goals. Supplying administrators with concrete data rather than abstract commitments became necessary to protect the program and legitimize its value amid changing political and financial contexts. For example, Erica provided student data indicating that undergraduate students at her institution were interested in a minor that offered pathways to learn across differences and advance a just, equitable, and democratic society. Student engagement proved to be a significant source of both affirmation and institutional flexibility.

At Ericka's institution, enrollment in leadership courses remained robust, student feedback was consistently positive, and there were no formal complaints regarding course content. Consequently, understanding context also involves strategic preparation and evidence‐based advocacy. These efforts transformed understanding context into an ongoing practice of attentiveness, reflection, and purposeful engagement rather than passive observation. Ultimately, when curriculum developers approach context through active waiting, they are better able to navigate uncertainty, uphold integrity, and prepare for changes in the political and institutional landscape. This perspective stresses that contextual knowledge is dynamic, reflective, and constitutes ongoing curricular work.

Refusal and Uncritical Accommodation

Active waiting also involves carefully navigating the tension between refusal and uncritical accommodation. For us, refusal initially signified an uncompromising rejection of shifting mandates and political pressures, hence preserving curricular integrity. In contrast, uncritical accommodation suggested unreflective adaptation, such as preemptively altering language, learning outcomes, or content in ways that undermined the critical foundations of leadership education. Rather than seeing these options as a binary, active waiting invites a both/and approach, one that maintains curricular integrity while thoughtfully engaging with the constraints of policy, administration, and institutional politics. In other words, curriculum developers must make deliberate choices about when to uphold critical principles and when to strategically adapt to institutional pressures. Consequently, we sought to move beyond political rhetoric that limited our choices and instead create space for a both/and perspective.

This approach was not passive or evasive; rather, it required ongoing intellectual engagement, critical analysis, and a willingness to consider possible policy changes. A both/and orientation recognized that curriculum development takes place within complex systems of policy, discourse, institutional relationships, and power. It required us to remain attentive to these dynamics while supporting the core commitments of leadership education. We utilized theory and scholarship not only to establish legitimacy, but also to articulate the philosophical foundations supporting the leadership minor.

For instance, Erica received critique about citing domains of campus climate from the Culturally Relevant Leadership Learning (CRLL; Bertrand Jones et al. 2016) model in a foundational curriculum document. The inclusion of CRLL aimed to examine how campus climate domains might influence who accesses curricular leadership learning and how they influence student engagement at her institution, with the goal of organizing tangible practices to create more equitable leadership learning spaces. This brought together the institution's core focus on belonging with a model that could support the development of student outcomes for the program.

Meanwhile, Ericka connected the leadership curriculum to supporting the university's goal of career readiness. Therefore, mirroring the language that is most important at the university. This clarity enabled us to articulate the program's value to institutional stakeholders by emphasizing our commitment to core leadership principles. Both scenarios required reflection on whether responses were principled refusals or uncritical accommodations.

By adopting a both/and orientation, we moved beyond reactive decisions, instead engaging in sustained analysis, dialogue with institutional partners, and strategic alignment with university goals. This practice of active waiting includes incremental, purposeful action: refining course content, reinforcing assessment strategies, and making adjustments that protect students, support program sustainability, and preserve the philosophical foundations of leadership education. Importantly, active waiting here is proactive rather than passive; it requires continuous attention to institutional dynamics, thoughtful engagement with policy implications, and reflection on potential consequences.

Imaginative Futures

A third practice of active waiting involves creating space for imagination and forward‐thinking, even amid uncertainty. Rather than perceiving uncertainty solely as a constraint, active waiting invites curriculum developers to envision possibilities, experiment within interim spaces, and prepare for liberatory futures. Imagination becomes a form of resistance, enabling leadership programs to remain aligned with critical and equity‐oriented objectives even when immediate implementation is limited by political or institutional constraints. Scholars have observed how oppression and periods of chaos often restrict collective imagination, narrowing perceptions of possibility and limiting pathways toward liberation (Freire 1970; Kelley 2022).

During periods of heightened political restriction, dominant narratives frequently promote short‐term survival strategies that emphasize containment rather than transformation. We deliberately chose not to respond only to immediate circumstances, but to consider possibilities beyond the present moment. Cultivating imagination for liberation enabled us to resist the narrowing of possibilities and to maintain a focus on leadership education that develops critical consciousness, even when such efforts appear delayed or obscured. In her reflection, Ericka noted that students are already seeking courses focused on social issues and justice, and she therefore wants to be prepared to offer more than a traditional online course or a co‐curricular experience. In this moment, the gift lies in curriculum developers intentionally creating space for cognitive, emotional, and intellectual engagement that enables imaginative futures.

For Erica, community partnerships in student affairs, athletics, and a university academic center in DC have been central to collectively building curriculum and positioning the minor strategically in relation to institutional commitments. One idea that emerged from this group was to develop a student curriculum advisory group to provide ideas on the curriculum and feedback on the minor's direction. For her, this is an opportunity to co‐construct an academic curriculum that will impact the campus community and create an equitable learning environment. Therefore, when opportunities arise, leadership education programs can offer students opportunities to engage in social transformation.

We considered how imagination for liberation transforms the purpose of leadership education. We began to explore how future contexts may require moving beyond these frameworks toward explicit liberatory objectives. Operating within transitional periods enabled us to prepare for this shift by making incremental curricular decisions that maintain the visibility of liberation, even when it cannot be explicitly named. Ultimately, active waiting here involves preparing without rushing, experimenting without compromising core values, and sustaining readiness. It ensures that the curriculum remains responsive, student‐centered, and strategically positioned to enact social and educational transformation when opportunities arise. As such, cultivating imaginative futures transforms the in‐between moments of uncertainty into sites of possibility, innovation, and critical foresight.

Conclusion

Amid the anti‐DEI movement and ongoing institutional uncertainties in higher education, leadership educators are called not only to sustain and adapt curricula but also to engage in active waiting. This deliberate, reflective practice balances responsiveness, critical inquiry, and strategic preparation for future possibilities. Our reflections demonstrate that navigating anti‐DEI pressures and programmatic instability requires more than reactive compliance. It demands attentive engagement with institutional and political contexts, relational work with stakeholders, and thoughtful anticipation of possible challenges and opportunities.

We align with scholars who have argued that academic leadership minors occupy a distinct position within institutional degree offerings, with program sustainability closely tied to alignment with the university's mission and values (Chunoo and Osteen 2016; Diallo and Gerhardt 2017; Dugan and Komives 2007). Building on Guthrie and Devies’ (2025) assertion that institutional context is critical to leadership program development, we further emphasize the central role of the state political context. In this context, active waiting serves as an adaptive strategy that enables both/and thinking and action, assisting educators in navigating uncertainty without compromising critical principles or equity‐oriented goals (Sicka and Hou 2023; Volpe White et al. 2019).

Active waiting also offers opportunities to envision innovative curricula grounded in liberatory theories and critical frameworks. Through intentional readiness, cultivation of strategic partnerships, and reflection on institutional dynamics, leadership educators can continue to advocate for programs rooted in social justice, culturally responsive practices, and critical perspectives (Alvesson and Spicer 2014; Collinson 2018; Guthrie and Chunoo 2018).

Ultimately, in a higher education landscape marked by uncertainty and anti‐DEI pressures, active waiting enables curriculum developers to sustain, adapt, and reimagine leadership programs in ways that honor student learning, strengthen program sustainability, advance leadership education scholarship, and safeguard professional and personal ethics. Beyond immediate constraints, this practice positions leadership education as a generative space for cultivating socially just, ethical, and transformative leaders. It demonstrates that resilience, along with critical imagination, can coexist during periods of uncertainty.
