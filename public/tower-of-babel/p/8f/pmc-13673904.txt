Septic shock due to a ruptured ovarian abscess caused by hematogenous Staphylococcus aureus infection in a sexually inactive woman with atopic dermatitis: a case report
Authors: Takuya Yamaguchi, Kaname Uno, Koh Nagata, Yusuke Shigematsu, Itsuki Kajimura, Sayaka Kawashita, Yuriko Kitajima, Yuri Hasegawa, Kiyonori Miura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTubo-ovarian abscess is a severe form of pelvic inflammatory disease that is typically caused by ascending polymicrobial infections in sexually active women. However, it is extremely rare in sexually inactive women, and its pathogenesis in such cases remains poorly understood. Atopic dermatitis is associated with impaired skin barrier function and increased susceptibility to Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia. Here, we present a rare case of septic shock due to a ruptured ovarian abscess caused by S. aureus in a sexually inactive woman with atopic dermatitis.Case presentationA 44-year-old Japanese woman with no history of sexual intercourse presented with a prolonged fever lasting 4 weeks. Six weeks before admission, she developed pruritic blisters between the right index and middle fingers due to atopic dermatitis, which subsequently ruptured. Seventeen days before referral, she had watery diarrhea and was diagnosed with enteritis at a clinic. Persistent symptoms raised suspicion for viral hepatitis based on elevated C-reactive protein levels and mild liver dysfunction. Subsequently, the patient developed recurrent high-grade fever and lower abdominal pain. Imaging revealed a large pelvic abscess with ascites, and she was transferred to our hospital. On arrival, she was in septic shock, with a blood pressure of 80/40 mmHg and a pulse rate of 125 bpm. A ruptured left ovarian abscess arising from an infected mature cystic teratoma was diagnosed, and emergency laparoscopic surgery was performed. The procedure revealed severe intraperitoneal inflammation with purulent ascites. S. aureus was isolated from both blood cultures and abscess contents. Postoperatively, the patient underwent intensive care management for septic shock and acute kidney injury, gradually recovering with appropriate antibiotic therapy. She was discharged without complications and remained recurrence-free at the 1-year follow-up.ConclusionsNo gastrointestinal or gynecological source of infection was identified despite extensive evaluation, raising the possibility of a hematogenous route of infection. The patient’s atopic dermatitis may have contributed to increased susceptibility to S. aureus bacteremia through skin blistering, potentially resulting in bacterial seeding of the ovary. This case underscores the diagnostic challenges associated with atypical ovarian abscesses and highlights the importance of including them in the differential diagnosis of atypical, prolonged fever and abdominal symptoms, even in sexually inactive women, particularly those with atopic dermatitis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-026-04625-6.Keywords: Tubo-ovarian abscess, Atopic dermatitis, Staphylococcus aureus, Septic shock, Case report

Introduction

Tubo-ovarian abscess is a severe form of pelvic inflammatory disease that most commonly occurs in sexually active adult women [1, 2]. It typically results from an ascending infection, frequently caused by anaerobic bacteria such as Bacteroides spp. and Escherichia coli, and is often associated with Chlamydia trachomatis infection [3, 4]. When diagnosed early and treated appropriately, symptoms are often mild, and the prognosis is favorable [5]. However, delayed diagnosis may lead to generalized peritonitis, septic shock, and severe clinical deterioration [6]. Furthermore, severe inflammation may impair future fertility, underscoring the importance of early diagnosis and intervention [7].

Although most cases present with typical clinical histories, tubo-ovarian abscesses have been reported in women with no history of sexual intercourse [1]. Previous studies indicate that tubo-ovarian abscesses in sexually inactive women may arise from the contiguous spread of inflammation from adjacent organs, such as appendicitis or diverticulitis, or from infections associated with urogenital anomalies [8]. While E. coli and streptococcal species are the most common causative pathogens, the route of infection in some cases remains unclear [9]. Identifying the source of infection is essential for accurate diagnosis and timely management.

Atopic dermatitis is an important condition that predisposes individuals to bacteremia. Patients with atopic dermatitis present with chronically inflamed and thinned skin, which impairs barrier function and increases susceptibility to bloodstream infections [10]. Skin colonization with Staphylococcus aureus can be up to 19.7 times higher in patients with atopic dermatitis, with colonization rates of 57%–100%. Consequently, these patients are more susceptible to S. aureus bacteremia [11–14].

Mature cystic teratoma is the most common benign ovarian tumor, accounting for approximately 20% of all ovarian tumors. It is a differentiated benign neoplasm composed of tissues derived from all three germ layers, frequently including skin and hair components [15]. Although mature cystic teratomas are often asymptomatic, torsion or rupture may cause symptoms that potentially lead to peritonitis. Infection of a mature cystic teratoma is rare, occurring in approximately 1% of cases [15]. Most reported infections are associated with an ascending infection induced by gynecologic procedures such as dilation and curettage or intrauterine device insertion.

Here, we describe a severe and diagnostically challenging case of ruptured ovarian abscess caused by S. aureus infection in a sexually inactive woman with a history of atopic dermatitis. Delayed diagnosis resulted in abscess rupture and subsequent septic shock.

Case presentation

A 44-year-old Japanese woman (gravida 0) was referred to our hospital with a history of prolonged fever and abdominal pain, presenting with septic shock associated with an intra-abdominal abscess. The patient reported menarche at 15 years old and regular 30-day menstrual cycles without dysmenorrhea. She had no history of sexual intercourse. Her medical history was notable only for atopic dermatitis, which was generally well controlled without topical or systemic therapy, except for occasional pruritus involving the joint areas and interdigital spaces. She had no family history of any relevant disease, no history of substance abuse, and no recent travel abroad. Her height was 169.0 cm, and her body mass index was 20.6 kg/m2.

Approximately 6 weeks before admission, the patient developed pruritic blisters, which subsequently ruptured, between the right index and middle fingers due to atopic dermatitis. She was prescribed a topical steroid-containing cream. One month before admission, she underwent a routine dental checkup without any invasive or bleeding procedures.

Beginning approximately 1 month before her referral to our hospital, the patient experienced recurrent episodes of fever accompanied by mild diarrhea of unknown origin. Despite these symptoms, she was able to continue her usual daily activities. Seventeen days before admission, she developed a recurrent fever of 38 °C with watery diarrhea and was diagnosed with enteritis at a local clinic. Despite treatment, her symptoms persisted, and she revisited the clinic. Laboratory findings showed an elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) level of 14.68 mg/dL and mild liver dysfunction, raising suspicion for viral hepatitis. Three days before admission, CRP levels improved slightly to 11.12 mg/dL; however, her symptoms persisted.

On the day of admission, the patient developed recurrent high fever (39 °C) accompanied by lower abdominal pain and presented to the emergency department of a local hospital. On arrival, she was hypotensive, with a blood pressure of 100/64 mmHg, and tachycardic, with a heart rate of 126 bpm, suggesting systemic infection and evolving septic shock. Laboratory testing showed an elevated CRP level of 14.68 mg/dL. Abdominal computed tomography (CT) revealed a pelvic mass measuring > 12 cm in diameter, accompanied by moderate ascites. CT imaging revealed no evidence of appendicitis, diverticulitis, liver abscess, or gastrointestinal perforation. As septic shock secondary to an intra-abdominal mass was suspected, she was transferred to our hospital for further management.

On admission, the patient was alert and oriented (Glasgow Coma Scale score: E4V5M6). Her body temperature was 39.3 °C, blood pressure was 80/40 mmHg, pulse rate was 125 bpm, respiratory rate was 20 breaths/minute, and oxygen saturation was 96% in room air. She reported mild abdominal pain without localized tenderness. Digital rectal examination revealed mild diarrhea without blood. A relatively large amount of vaginal discharge was observed, along with skin erosion in the external genital area. Speculum examination showed no vaginal bleeding or visible or palpable tumors. Leukocytes and CRP were markedly elevated at 19.1 × 103/μL and 17.61 mg/dL, respectively. Serum creatinine was elevated at 1.67 mg/dL, indicating acute kidney injury. Liver enzymes were mildly elevated. Tumor markers, including CA125 and CA19-9, were not evaluated due to the patient’s life-threatening condition at presentation. Transvaginal ultrasonography revealed a 12-cm cystic mass located centrally in the pelvis without solid components. Non-enhanced CT revealed moderate ascites over the liver surface and in the Douglas pouch, as well as a 12-cm cystic pelvic lesion containing air and fluid levels indicative of an abscess (Figs. 1A and B). Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed that the cystic lesion originated from the left ovary, while the right ovary appeared normal. The cystic contents were isointense on T2-weighted images and hypointense on T1-weighted images, which are consistent with an abscess. Furthermore, the ventral portion of the cyst was hyperintense on both T1- and T2-weighted images but hypointense on fat-suppressed images, indicating the presence of fat components and raising suspicion of a mature cystic teratoma (Figs. 1C–1F).

Based on the clinical and imaging findings, the patient was diagnosed with a ruptured left ovarian abscess arising from an infected mature cystic teratoma, resulting in severe peritonitis complicated by acute kidney injury and septic shock. Given the patient’s critical condition, emergency laparoscopic surgery was planned to thoroughly remove the abscess and ascites fluid, including purulent contents.

Emergency laparoscopic left salpingo-oophorectomy with abscess drainage was performed under general anesthesia. Intraoperatively, purulent material was observed extending to the liver surface, and diffuse inflammation was present throughout the abdominal cavity (Figs. 2A and 2B). The ovarian abscess adhered firmly to the omentum, visceral peritoneum, small intestine, uterus, and retroperitoneum, requiring extensive adhesiolysis (Figs. 2C and 2D). Complete resection was performed after confirming the left adnexa as the abscess origin. The abdominal cavity was thoroughly irrigated, and a drain was placed in the Retzius space. The procedure was completed without intraoperative complications. The total operative time was 3 h and 19 min. Abscess contents were collected intraoperatively for subsequent microscopic examination and bacterial culture.

On gross examination, the resected left ovary contained a cystic lesion. Histopathological examination revealed that the cyst wall consisted of fibrous stroma and exhibited a denuded epithelial lining, extensive necrotic tissue, marked neutrophilic infiltration, and fibrin deposition, findings consistent with acute inflammation (Fig. 2E). Necrotic tissue also adhered to the serosal surface. Furthermore, adipose tissue and hair components were also identified, confirming the diagnosis of a mature cystic teratoma (Figs. 2F and 2G). No immature components or evidence of malignant transformation were identified on histopathological examination. Therefore, additional postoperative tumor marker evaluation was not considered necessary.

Postoperatively, the patient was admitted to the intensive care unit for vasopressor support with norepinephrine and continuous hemodiafiltration (CHDF) to maintain hemodynamic stability. As initial Gram staining of the abscess and blood cultures suggested gram-positive cocci consistent with S. aureus, broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy with tazobactam/piperacillin and daptomycin was initiated to provide empirical coverage for potential methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) infection, given the relatively high regional prevalence of MRSA and the patient’s life-threatening condition.

On postoperative day (POD) 1, the patient’s hemodynamic status gradually stabilized, allowing discontinuation of vasopressor support and CHDF. On POD 2, blood cultures obtained at admission yielded methicillin-sensitive S. aureus (MSSA) in both aerobic and anaerobic culture bottles. MSSA was also detected in urine and ascitic fluid. No pathogenic bacteria were detected in vaginal secretion samples. MSSA was detected in aerobic and anaerobic blood culture bottles obtained on POD 0 and remained detectable in aerobic blood culture bottles collected on POD 1 and POD 2. Details of all blood culture results are summarized in Table 1. Antibiotic therapy was subsequently de-escalated to cefazolin monotherapy. Although transient defervescence was achieved, fever subsequently recurred, prompting contrast-enhanced CT imaging from the neck to the pelvis. However, no residual or metastatic abscesses were identified. Transthoracic echocardiography showed no evidence of infective endocarditis or valvular regurgitation involving the mitral or tricuspid valves, findings that can sometimes be associated with small vegetations. Although transesophageal echocardiography was considered to exclude infective endocarditis with small vegetations, the patient’s clinical condition and inflammatory markers improved with continued cefazolin therapy (Fig. 3). The patient was subsequently transitioned to oral antibiotics and discharged on POD 32. The clinical course and interventions are summarized in Table 2. She remained asymptomatic during more than 1 year of follow-up.

Discussion and conclusions

Tubo-ovarian abscess is commonly caused by an ascending infection in sexually active women [7]. Although less frequently reported, it can also result from descending infection, such as appendicitis or tuberculous peritonitis. Tubo-ovarian abscesses in sexually inactive women are extremely rare, with only approximately 20 cases reported worldwide to date [9]. In such cases, identifying the route of infection is challenging but clinically important. Although several previous reports did not investigate the source of infection, contiguous spread from gastrointestinal inflammation or urogenital anomalies has been proposed as a potential cause [9, 10, 15]. This case also underscores the importance of heightened awareness and a high index of suspicion when evaluating women with pain in any region of the abdomen or pelvis. Further investigation with abdominal and pelvic ultrasonography should be considered in these patients [16].

In the present case, differential diagnoses, including appendicitis, diverticulitis, liver abscess, and gastrointestinal perforation, were considered because of patient’s prolonged gastrointestinal symptoms. However, no evidence of these conditions was identified on CT imaging. Common causative organisms of tubo-ovarian abscesses include Bacteroides spp., Peptostreptococcus, and E. coli. However, S. aureus was isolated from both blood cultures and the abscess in the current case. To our knowledge, only two previous reports have described tubo-ovarian abscess in women with an intact hymen caused by S. aureus, as summarized in Table 3. One patient had undergone surgery 2 months before the onset of the abscess. In both cases, skin colonization by S. aureus was suspected; however, the route of bacterial entry into the bloodstream, the presence of atopic dermatitis, and the mechanism of subsequent ovarian infection were not identified [9, 17]. Considering that sepsis has been associated with a negative economic impact, increased morbidity, extended postoperative hospital stay, readmission, and death [18], clinicians need to understand the importance of a medical history of atopic dermatitis.

S. aureus commonly colonizes the skin and nasal mucosa. In individuals with healthy skin, keratinocytes produce antimicrobial peptides that protect against infection. However, patients with atopic dermatitis exhibit reduced expression of antimicrobial peptides such as cathelicidin (LL-37) and β-defensin 2, leading to increased susceptibility to S. aureus colonization and subsequent infection [19]. In fact, colonization rates of S. aureus range from 57 to 100% in patients with atopic dermatitis, compared with substantially lower colonization rates in healthy individuals [10]. Chronic skin inflammation in atopic dermatitis impairs barrier function, thereby facilitating bacterial entry, particularly with frequent scratching. Notably, even skin that appears clinically normal may harbor histological inflammation, increasing the risk of bloodstream infection [13]. In the current patient, no history of invasive dental procedures was identified, and no apparent source of infection was found despite extensive evaluation. Therefore, the pruritic blisters between the index and middle fingers caused by atopic dermatitis were considered a possible point of entry for S. aureus bacteremia.

S. aureus bacteremia can cause severe complications, including infective endocarditis and septic arthritis, although many cases may present only transient fever. In infective endocarditis, S. aureus adheres to damaged endothelium or prosthetic material and evades host immunity through biofilm formation and activation of the coagulation system, resulting in vegetation formation [14]. A similar mechanism may have contributed to abscess formation in the present case, in which S. aureus may have adhered to skin-derived components within the mature cystic teratoma or to surrounding neovascularized tissue, leading to bacterial colonization. However, this hypothesis remains speculative. As S. aureus does not typically cause systemic gastrointestinal infections, the watery diarrhea experienced by the patient 17 days before admission may have reflected secondary gastrointestinal inflammation resulting from adjacent pelvic inflammation. The patient’s prolonged fever and gastrointestinal symptoms may have been attributable to chronic inflammation of the ovarian abscess and repeated microleakage of abscess contents caused by increasing intracystic pressure, eventually leading to complete rupture and subsequent chronic chemical peritonitis.

The most notable limitation of this case report is that genetic analysis was not performed to determine whether the S. aureus colonizing the patient’s skin was the same strain as that isolated from the abscess and blood cultures. Therefore, a hematogenous route of infection is considered plausible but remains hypothetical based on the patient’s clinical history and findings, as suggested in previous reports. Although we propose that the internal components of a mature cystic teratoma may provide a favorable environment for S. aureus infection, we were unable to directly demonstrate that components such as sebum or keratin act as a culture medium for hematogenous bacterial seeding. In addition, transesophageal echocardiography was not performed; therefore, small vegetations associated with infective endocarditis could not be completely excluded. Finally, because this report describes a single case, causal inference is limited, and retrospective bias cannot be excluded.

In conclusion, this case describes a rare occurrence of septic shock caused by a ruptured ovarian abscess due to S. aureus infection in a sexually inactive woman with atopic dermatitis. When patients present with prolonged fever of unknown origin and lower gastrointestinal symptoms with atypical or severe clinical presentations, an ovarian abscess can be considered in the differential diagnosis even in women without a history of sexual activity. In particular, S. aureus infection should be suspected in patients with atopic dermatitis, given their increased susceptibility to bloodstream infections.
