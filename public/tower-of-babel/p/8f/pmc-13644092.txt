D3aist: data-driven detection of atypical interactions in spatial transcriptomics
Authors: Teresa León, Juan Domingo, Guillermo Ayala, Angela Riffo-Campos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tissues exhibit complex spatial organization that governs cellular function and interactions, yet traditional transcriptomic methods fail to capture this context. Spatial transcriptomics preserves cellular locations while profiling gene expression, enabling high-resolution mapping of tissue architecture. Molecule-resolved spatial transcriptomics data, in which individual transcript detections are associated with spatial coordinates and gene identities, can be naturally modeled as multitype point patterns within the framework of spatial statistics. Second-order summary statistics, such as the Ripley cross K-, cross L-, and pair correlation functions, are commonly used to characterize spatial interactions; however, systematic comparison across multiple types remains challenging. We propose a data-driven method, termed D3AIST, based on empirical envelopes constructed via a leave-one-pair-out strategy over the set of observed functions. Each gene-pair type is evaluated by comparing its second-order summary functions against an empirical envelope constructed from the corresponding functions of all other pairs, excluding the one under evaluation. This allows the detection of atypical spatial interactions without relying on classical null models or asymptotic results. D3AIST provides a global comparative scheme across all type pairs, facilitating the identification of singular interaction patterns within a fully empirical framework. We applied D3AIST to transcript-level Xenium data from two publicly available studies. In a primary colorectal cancer case study based on GSE280318/GSE280314, D3AIST identified atypical spatial interaction networks across expert-defined tumor microenvironment regions. An independent external evaluation using the GSE267680 Xenium dataset further illustrated the applicability of the framework in a distinct pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia context. Controlled simulations and computational benchmarking were additionally used to evaluate empirical error behavior and scalability. Overall, D3AIST provides a descriptive and hypothesis-generating framework for prioritizing atypical transcript-level spatial associations in molecule-resolved spatial transcriptomics data.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13147-2.

Introduction

The term spatial transcriptomics was proposed by Ståhl et al. [1] to describe approaches that allow the mapping of gene activity while preserving its spatial context within tissue sections. By linking transcriptomic profiles to spatial coordinates, these approaches provide direct insight into tissue organization and cellular interactions.

Over the past decade, spatial transcriptomics has evolved into a broad collection of experimental platforms that differ substantially in their technical characteristics, including spatial resolution, gene coverage, detection sensitivity, and throughput [2]. Broadly, these platforms are often grouped into two main methodological families. Sequencing-based approaches, such as Visium HD, rely on spatial barcoding and next-generation sequencing to generate transcriptome-wide measurements across discretized spatial locations. In contrast, imaging-based approaches, such as Xenium and MERFISH, directly detect RNA molecules in situ, providing measurements at single-cell or subcellular resolution. This distinction reflects widely recognized trade-offs between transcriptome coverage and spatial resolution and is commonly used to organize the field [3, 4].

Spatial transcriptomics technologies have diverse applications in developmental biology, cancer research, neuroscience, immunology, and personalized medicine. They enable the mapping of cellular niches, tissue heterogeneity, and molecular interactions in situ. Experimental considerations, including tissue preservation, platform choice, and computational analysis, are critical for generating high-quality, interpretable data [5].

The unique characteristics of spatial transcriptomics data require specialized analytical methods and software. Emons et al. [6] provide a comprehensive overview of spatial statistical approaches: statistical methods designed to analyze the arrangement of points or events in space [7]. Within this context they introduce pasta (https://robinsonlabuzh.github.io/pasta), a detailed analysis vignette with implementations in both R and Python.

A wide range of computational tools has been developed for spatial transcriptomics and, more broadly, spatial omics analysis. In the Python ecosystem, Scanpy [8] is commonly used for single-cell transcriptomic analysis and provides a common data analysis framework on which spatial-omics tools such as Squidpy [9] build. In parallel, R-based toolboxes such as Giotto [10] and the more recent Giotto Suite [11] provide flexible environments for data processing, integration, and visualization across diverse spatial-omics workflows.

An important task in spatial-omics analysis is the identification of spatially variable features, that is, molecular signals exhibiting structured variation across tissue (typically genes in spatial transcriptomics, or regulatory regions in spatial epigenomics). Methods such as SpatialDE [12] and SPARK [13] detect spatially variable genes by modeling spatial dependence in the measured profiles. More recent approaches, including SINFONIA [14] and Descart [15], address related spatial-variability problems using alternative strategies; notably, Descart is designed for spatial epigenomics and focuses on identifying spatially variable chromatin accessibility peaks.

Most of these methods operate on measurements defined over discrete spatial units (cells, nuclei, or spots) and test individual features for structured variation with respect to spatial coordinates. While highly effective for uncovering spatially structured signals at the feature level, they typically do not explicitly target pairwise gene–gene spatial relationships, such as colocalization or segregation, which therefore often require dedicated analyses. Methods specifically designed to characterize pairwise spatial associations between genes at the single-molecule level remain comparatively limited.

In contrast, we adopt a complementary descriptive perspective tailored to molecule-resolved, imaging-based spatial transcriptomics, where each detected transcript is represented by its spatial coordinates and gene identity, yielding a multitype spatial point pattern. Tissue organization is characterized through second-order spatial associations between gene pairs, summarized across length scales using cross-type summary functions from spatial point process analysis. For each gene pair, we assess its association profile relative to an empirical leave-one-pair-out reference derived from all other pairs and identify interactions with outlying profiles. These results provide a fully empirical view of spatial transcriptional organization and are intended for hypothesis generation. Importantly, these spatial associations are descriptive summaries rather than evidence of direct regulatory or causal links. Instead, they prioritize gene pairs whose colocalization or segregation patterns may reflect shared cellular programs, tissue microenvironments, or spatial compartmentalization.

To illustrate the proposed framework, we present a primary case study based on multiple regions of interest from a published dataset, followed by an independent external evaluation in a separate study and tissue context.

The primary case study uses the colorectal cancer resource from de Oliveira et al. (GEO: GSE280318 [16]), which includes both Visium HD and Xenium measurements. Here, we analyze only the Xenium component, i.e., molecule-resolved transcript detections with spatial coordinates and gene identities, rather than Visium HD binned whole-transcriptome counts. To assess portability across studies and tissue contexts, we additionally apply D3AIST to an independent Xenium dataset from Bell et al. (GEO: GSE267680 [17]). Thus, all empirical analyses in this work are based on transcript-level coordinates compatible with the multitype point-pattern framework used by D3AIST.

Unlike recent subcellular spatial transcriptomics methods such as InSTAnT [18] and Bento [19], which explicitly model intracellular compartments, D3AIST adopts a segmentation-free and compartment-agnostic perspective. It does not rely on cell or subcellular segmentation, normalization, or feature aggregation. This design reduces sensitivity to segmentation quality and allows gene-pair spatial association analysis grounded in spatial point process methodology.

Gene–gene spatial associations, including colocalization and segregation, are characterized using methods from spatial point process theory. Specifically, we use standard second-order summary functions to quantify interaction structure as a function of inter-point distance, and identify atypical profiles relative to the empirical background defined by the ensemble of all other gene pairs. A detailed description of these functions and the underlying theoretical framework is provided in Methods section.

Finally, we evaluate both the empirical behavior and computational scalability of D3AIST. Controlled spatial simulations implemented in spatstat [20] are used to quantify false positive and false negative rates under predefined association scenarios. In addition, we perform a computational performance analysis using the colorectal cancer case with the largest number of transcriptional events among the analyzed genes, evaluating execution time and memory usage under different analysis settings, including the number of genes and the spatial window size.

Methods

Data inputs and regions of interest

D3AIST uses transcript-level spatial coordinates and associated gene identities as input. For each dataset, we used the author-released Xenium transcript tables, representing each detected transcript as a 2D point and its gene identity as the corresponding mark. The analysis is performed directly at the transcript level and does not rely on cell segmentation, cell-type annotation, spatial aggregation, expression-matrix normalization, or image-derived features.

Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined from tissue fields highlighted in the source publications. Because ROI boundary polygons were not released, each ROI was approximated by a rectangular analysis window (bounding box) in the coordinate system of the Xenium transcript locations. Upper-left and lower-right window coordinates are reported in Tables 1–2.

Gene panel metadata (gene identifiers and names) were obtained from the accompanying panel files. To enable direct comparability with the source publications and avoid post hoc feature selection, each analysis was restricted to the gene subset emphasized in the corresponding study.

The primary case study was based on the colorectal cancer Xenium resource deposited in GEO under the SuperSeries GSE280318, focusing on the Xenium SubSeries GSE280314 containing transcript-level spatial coordinates of individual transcript detections [16, 21]. We analyzed the expert-defined tumor microenvironment regions highlighted in Fig. 7 of the source publication [22]. These annotations were used only to define and contextualize ROIs; D3AIST was applied solely to transcript coordinates and gene identities. ROI bounding-box coordinates and the image coordinate convention are listed in Table 1.

For external evaluation, we used the Xenium dataset released under GEO: GSE267680 [17] and analyzed the PanIN field highlighted in Fig. 3A–B (sample PanIN-HG3) of the source publication [23]. As in the primary case study, the original annotations were used only to select and contextualize the ROI, while D3AIST was applied directly to transcript-level spatial events. Point-pattern representation and spatial point process framework

We model the data as a realization of a multitype spatial point process i.e. a multitype point pattern, in which each observation is defined by its spatial coordinates and a categorical mark corresponding to its gene identity.

By representing observed transcripts as spatial events rather than as measurements aggregated over cells or spots, D3AIST enables the use of classical second-order cross-type summary functions to characterize the spatial relationships between transcript types. This event-level formulation does not require cell segmentation, cell-type annotation, or cell-level expression summaries such as a cell-by-gene count matrix. Conversely, spot-based platforms typically report gene expression as counts aggregated over spatial regions rather than as coordinates of individual transcript detections, making them incompatible with the point-pattern framework used by D3AIST without additional modeling assumptions or preprocessing.

We next introduce the multitype point process framework and the second-order cross-type summary functions used in this work. We then show how these functions can be used to quantify gene–gene spatial associations, including colocalization and segregation, across spatial scales.

The notation and terminology used in this section follow standard references on spatial point processes, particularly Chiu et al. [24] and Baddeley et al. [25].

A spatial point process provides a probabilistic framework for event-level spatial data. In this setting, a single dataset is viewed as a point pattern, that is, a set of event locations observed within a tissue region. Different experiments, samples, or regions of interest may give rise to different point patterns, and the point process describes the distribution of such possible patterns.

When each event carries a categorical label, such as gene identity, the observed data form a marked point pattern. If the marks take values in a finite set of types, the corresponding model is a multitype point process.

In intuitive terms, the intensity of a point process describes the average density of events, that is, the expected number of points per unit area; in a multitype process, each type has its own intensity.

Multitype point processes provide a natural framework for studying cross-type spatial relationships, such as colocalization or segregation between genes, as a function of spatial scale.

Spatial relationships between point types can be characterized by the intensity (density), the cross-type K and L functions (cumulative association), and the pair correlation function g (local interactions). In what follows, we assume d=2, corresponding to planar tissue sections.

While these definitions provide a theoretical framework, in practice we work with a single observed spatial point pattern and compute empirical estimates of the intensity, and the cross-type summaries (K, L, and g) over a grid of distances. Estimation was performed using the functions Kcross, Lcross, and pcfcross from the R package spatstat.

Further details can be found in [25] and in the corresponding function documentation.

In principle, colocalization can be assessed using global envelope tests ([26]), which provide formal control of the family-wise error rate ([27], page 407) for the functional summaries just introduced. These tests compare the observed spatial interaction statistic to an envelope constructed from Monte Carlo simulations under a specified null model and reject the null hypothesis when the observed curve falls outside the envelope.

Although theoretically well-founded, global envelope tests become computationally prohibitive for omics-scale screening: obtaining calibrated p-values with simultaneous error control typically requires a large number of Monte Carlo replicates, and the procedure must be repeated over a very large number of gene pairs while evaluating the functional summary over multiple distance values.

Point-wise envelope methods, such as those implemented in spatstat [20], offer an alternative; cross-type independence can be assessed using envelopes constructed from random-shift (including toroidal-shift) simulations. By default, these envelopes provide point-wise inference in r; however, they still rely on extensive resampling and become computationally demanding when repeated over many gene pairs and distance values.

While these approaches are well established, they are not pursued further here due to their limited scalability in the omics setting, motivating the alternative comparative framework introduced in the following subsection.

D3AIST does not rely on classical null hypotheses such as complete spatial randomness or explicit independence between types. Instead, the ensemble of observed cross-type summary functions serves as an empirical reference, providing a comparative, system-wide perspective on spatial association patterns. The empirical envelopes are constructed using a leave-one-pair-out scheme, which implicitly assumes approximate exchangeability across gene pairs. This assumption is unlikely to hold strictly in practice, since gene pairs may differ in intensity, spatial scale, and biological function. Consequently, the envelopes should be interpreted as descriptive benchmarks of typical behaviour rather than as strictly calibrated inferential tools. Software implementation

Our data-processing pipeline is implemented as a set of command-line utilities written in C++. These programs handle the efficient organization and transformation of the transcriptional event data provided with the dataset and generate all intermediate files required for both visualization and statistical analysis. The first component converts the large CSV files containing gene panel information and transcriptional event data into compact binary formats, enabling substantially faster access to millions of events. The second component extracts transcription-event data for user-specified genes and regions of interest, producing per-gene location tables, binary masks representing the spatial distribution of events, and auxiliary material such as background-extraction scripts for QuPath. The final component fuses these binary masks with the histological background image to produce multichannel visualizations of gene expression.

Internally, the software organizes the Xenium-derived transcriptional event data into structured C++ classes, providing a coherent interface linking genes, clonotypes, their spatial coordinates, and associated histological information.

This allows queries such as retrieving all genes with a number of detected events above a specified threshold in a given area or computing the number of events of a gene occurring within a user-defined distance of events from other genes. Supplementary Material I provides a complete description of the C++ components, including usage formats, configuration parameters, and examples.

For each selected gene or clonotype, the software generates a dedicated file listing all detected transcription events. These files constitute the input for the subsequent statistical analysis implemented in R, described in Supplementary Material 2 and 3 (SuppMat2.pdf, SuppMat2.qmd, SuppMat3.pdf, SuppMat3.qmd and functions included in functions_srt.R), where the spatstat package [20] is used to compute the second-order summary statistics employed in our methodology.

Computational resources required for the programs in C++ are very modest. Execution times do not exceed one minute in a typical Intel/AMD modern processor at clock frequency of 4GHz. The programs in R, specifically the evaluation of the K-function using the spatstat package, are slower with execution times that, for very big areas or even the whole image, can arrive to several minutes. A benckmark with execution times and used memory is presented at Simulation study and computational performance section. Even spatstat is implemented in C at low level, it is a serial implementation and suffers from segmentation faults when executed using the parallel tools provided by R.

Simulation study

We performed a simulation study to evaluate the error profile of D3AIST under controlled conditions, focusing on type I error (false positives) and type II error (false negatives). The simulations were designed to assess whether the method can distinguish truly interacting pairs of point processes from pairs generated under spatial independence.

Three simulation settings were considered. In the first setting, a fixed number of dependent pairs of point processes was generated. For each dependent pair, the first component was simulated as a homogeneous Poisson point process, whereas the second component was obtained by applying random spatial shifts to the points of the first component. The random translations were sampled within a disc, thereby inducing local spatial association between the two components. Different numbers of dependent pairs were considered.

In the second setting, dependent pairs were generated using a Neyman–Scott cluster process. For each dependent pair, one component represented the parent process and the other represented the corresponding offspring process, inducing a clustered cross-type spatial association. As in the first setting, we varied the number of dependent pairs.

In the third setting, dependence was generated using multitype hard-core point processes, designed to induce spatial inhibition between selected types. Again, different numbers of dependent pairs were considered.

In all three settings, the remaining point patterns were generated as homogeneous Poisson point processes and therefore represented non-interacting background types. For each simulated dataset, D3AIST was applied to identify atypical cross-type spatial interactions. Type I error was computed as the proportion of independent pairs incorrectly classified as atypical, whereas type II error was computed as the proportion of truly dependent pairs not detected by the method. Full simulation results and executable code are provided in the accompanying Quarto Supplementary Material 4.

Computational performance analysis

The computational performance of the R component of the software was evaluated in terms of execution time and memory usage for several key parameters: the proportion of transcription events considered (thinning parameter), the number of analyzed genes, and the window size.

The thinning parameter defines the proportion of transcription events randomly selected (without replacement) from the complete dataset. This subsampling reduces the size of the spatial point pattern, enabling the assessment of how computational cost scales with the number of input points.

To assess computational performance, we selected the image/patient with the largest number of transcription events among the 100 analyzed genes, namely case P2CRC described in Areas d and f from P2CRC section. In all experiments, for a fixed number of genes (20, 50, and 100), all possible pairwise interactions were analyzed. Therefore, for n genes, a total of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$$\end{document} gene pairs were evaluated.

Execution times are reported in seconds (s), and memory usage (in megabytes, MB) corresponds to the largest object allocated during program execution. Numerical results are presented in Simulation study and computational performance section.

Results

Global behavior of D3AIST and empirical interaction envelopes.

The three cross-type second-order summary functions considered in this study, namely the cross-K function, the cross-L function, and the cross-type pair correlation function, provide complementary descriptions of spatial interaction between transcriptional events.

The agreement observed between the cross-K (Eq. 1) and cross-L (Eq. 2) functions is expected, as the latter is a variance-stabilizing transformation of the former and therefore conveys the same second-order interaction information. In contrast, the pair correlation function g (Eq. 3) was not fully consistent with the K and L results. This discrepancy is not unexpected, since the pair correlation function provides a local, non-cumulative description of interaction and its estimation typically exhibits higher variability, particularly at small spatial scales. Given its cumulative nature and greater stability across interaction ranges, the cross-K function was adopted as the primary basis for interpretation.

The empirical envelopes constructed from the ensemble of cross-type interaction functions defined the range of typical spatial behavior observed across all gene pairs. Atypical interactions correspond to clear departures either above or below the empirical envelopes over specific distance ranges, indicating spatial attraction or spatial repulsion, respectively.

Across the analyzed distance range, departures of the cross-K function were directionally consistent: no gene pair exhibited attraction at some distances and repulsion at others.

Data-driven detection of spatial interaction programs using D3AIST in expert-defined tumor microenvironment regions

We applied D3AIST to expert-defined tumor microenvironment regions previously characterized by de Oliveira et al. (Fig. 7 of ref. [22]).

The analysis focused on the same subset of 100 genes originally chosen to identify diverse immune populations and characterize the tumor microenvironment. Recurrent spatial interaction programs in the tumor microenvironment detected by D3AIST

To identify spatial transcriptional interaction programs consistently detected across tumor regions, we compared the atypical interaction networks identified by D3AIST in the five analyzed areas (P1CRC areas c and e, P2CRC areas d and f, and P5CRC area h). Recurrent interactions were defined as gene–gene associations showing consistent deviations of the observed cross-K functions relative to the empirical interaction envelopes across regions. Across these tumor microenvironment regions, D3AIST detected atypical spatial interaction networks involving 66 of the 100 genes included in the targeted panel. Among these, 38 genes were observed in more than one region, indicating the presence of recurrent spatial transcriptional programs across patients and tumor niches. Among the recurrent genes, CXCL10, CD27, and MZB1 were detected in all five analyzed regions, while COMP, CXCL13, CHI3L1, SPP1, CD274, and VWF were observed in four regions (Fig. 2b, red nodes).

Recurrent spatial interaction patterns were detected by comparing the atypical gene–gene associations identified across the five analyzed tumor microenvironment regions. These recurrent patterns were observed across multiple regions either as direct edges or through short paths within the corresponding interaction networks. Among the interactions observed in multiple areas, the pair CD27–MZB1 was detected in P1CRC area e, P2CRC area f, and P5CRC area h, consistently exhibiting spatial attraction as indicated by cross-K values exceeding the upper bound of the empirical envelopes. A second recurrent interaction involved the extracellular matrix remodeling genes MMP1 and MMP3, which showed atypical spatial attraction in P1CRC areas c and e and in P2CRC area d. Additional recurrent interactions included COMP–SPP1, which exhibited consistent spatial repulsion in P2CRC areas d and f and in P5CRC area h, as well as CHI3L1–REG3A, detected in P2CRC areas d and f. Other repeated associations included MUC5B–MZB1 and CXCL13–MZB1, both showing spatial repulsion across regions. These recurrent patterns define coherent transcriptional interaction modules corresponding to immune, B-cell–associated, and stromal processes within the tumor microenvironment.

Discussion

The analytical framework proposed in this study enables the identification of atypical spatial interactions between transcriptional events without relying on explicit assumptions such as spatial independence or complete spatial randomness (CSR). Instead, the method adopts an empirical comparative hypothesis: the spatial interaction of a given gene pair is evaluated relative to the variability observed among the spatial interactions of all other gene pairs in the dataset. By constructing empirical interaction envelopes derived from the ensemble of cross-type summary functions, D3AIST identifies gene pairs whose spatial behavior deviates from the range of typical interactions observed in the system. This approach allows the detection of spatial interactions between transcriptional events directly from the data while avoiding strong parametric assumptions about the underlying spatial processes.

Although D3AIST does not provide formal measures of statistical significance, the subset of gene pairs identified as atypical could, in principle, be subjected to targeted hypothesis testing (e.g., random shift tests), which would be computationally infeasible to apply exhaustively but become tractable when restricted to this reduced candidate set.

The simulation study supports the ability of D3AIST to identify atypical spatial interaction patterns under controlled conditions. Across the evaluated scenarios, the method consistently detected the injected interaction structures, including both attraction and inhibition settings, while maintaining stable behavior in the presence of non-interacting background processes. Regarding the type I error, the observed values were within acceptable ranges, with the third quartile close to the nominal level of 0.05.

Certainly, no type II errors were detected across all evaluated scenarios. However it should be noted that designing simulation studies in this context is not straightforward, due to the complexity of the underlying spatial dependence and the large number of interacting components. In particular, although simulations were conducted using classical point process models, the selection of an appropriate model remains a nuanced task given the broad range of available alternatives and their differing assumptions on spatial interaction.

Application of this framework to colorectal cancer spatial transcriptomics revealed that atypical interaction networks are a prominent feature of the tumor micro environment. Across the analyzed regions, D3AIST identified networks involving 66 of 100 genes, with 38 recurrent across multiple regions, indicating consistent spatial transcriptional programs across tumor niches and patients.

Notably, several genes identified within the interaction networks, including REG1A, CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, Clonotype1_TRA1, and SPP1, were previously reported by de Oliveira et al. [22] within the same dataset as part of expert-defined tumor regions, although without characterization of their spatial interactions. Additional genes, such as CD69, GREM1, FBP1, APOC1, CHI3L1, PIGR, IL1RN, ACTA2, KRT23, MMP7, and MMP9, were described in broader spatial profiling but were not analyzed in terms of interaction structure. Within this context, D3AIST extends the interpretation of this dataset by revealing coordinated attraction and repulsion patterns that were not captured in previous analyses, moving beyond descriptive spatial profiling toward interaction-based characterization of transcriptional programs.

The genes detected across all regions, CXCL10, CD27, and MZB1 are known to play established roles in tumor microenvironment regulation. CXCL10, through interaction with CXCR3, modulates immune responses, macrophage polarization, and stromal activation, contributing to both immune and extracellular matrix remodeling processes [28, 29]. Its consistent detection suggests that immune activation and stromal remodeling may be spatially coordinated. CD27, a marker of activated and memory B cells, reflects the presence of heterogeneous B-cell populations contributing to antigen presentation and immune modulation [30]. Its recurrent detection is consistent with the existence of spatially organized B-cell–associated niches. MZB1, involved in IgA production, regulates mucosal immune responses. Its deficiency is associated with reduced IgA, increased inflammation, and accelerated tumor progression, accompanied by elevated macrophage and neutrophil infiltration [31]. Spatial attraction with CD27 and IGHG3 is consistent with localized plasma cell–enriched niches, while interaction with CHI3L1, a protein secreted by cancer-associated fibroblasts that promotes inflammation, angiogenesis, and tumor progression, may suggest coexistence with inflammatory or stromal processes [32]. In contrast, spatial repulsion with CXCL13, a key mediator of B-cell recruitment and tertiary lymphoid structure formation [33], is indicative of potential compartmentalization between recruitment and differentiation programs within B-cell–related niches.

Additional recurrent interactions highlight stromal organization within the tumor microenvironment. The COMP–SPP1 pair exhibited consistent spatial repulsion, suggesting segregation between distinct microenvironmental programs. COMP, a matricellular protein associated with fibrosis and reduced immune infiltration, has been linked to tissue remodeling and tumor progression [34], whereas SPP1 (osteopontin) is a multifunctional cytokine involved in cell adhesion, migration, and immune modulation [35]. Their spatial repulsion may therefore reflect the separation of fibrotic stromal regions from inflammatory or immune-enriched niches. In contrast, the spatial attraction between MMP1 and MMP3, both matrix metalloproteinases involved in extracellular matrix degradation [36], tumor invasion and metastasis [37], may suggest localized proteolytic activity.

Together, these patterns suggest that stromal remodeling processes are spatially structured and organized into compartmentalized programs rather than being uniformly distributed. Importantly, they illustrate the capacity of D3AIST to resolve both spatial segregation and co-occurrence of stromal processes directly from second-order transcriptional interactions, without relying on cell-level annotations.

Collectively, these results suggest a model in which the tumor microenvironment is organized as a structured network of interacting transcriptional programs rather than a homogeneous mixture of cell populations. Within this framework, D3AIST reveals a multi-compartment architecture in which immune, stromal, and epithelial processes are both coordinated and spatially segregated. This perspective indicates that the tumor microenvironment is not only heterogeneous but intrinsically organized through spatially constrained networks of transcriptional interactions.

Importantly, these spatial interaction patterns were detected across tumor regions of different spatial extent and across different patients. The persistence of recurrent genes and interaction modules despite variations in observation windows suggests that the detected patterns reflect genuine biological spatial organization rather than artifacts related to local sampling variability. This reproducibility highlights the potential of D3AIST to uncover conserved spatial transcriptional programs within complex tissues.

The application of D3AIST to an independent Xenium spatial transcriptomics dataset further demonstrated that the framework can identify atypical transcript interaction structures across distinct biological contexts. In the PanIN region analyzed from GSE267680, D3AIST recovered a spatially structured interaction network involving transcripts associated with epithelial remodeling and PanIN progression, including MUCL3, TFF1, and TFF2. These findings are consistent with the original characterization of the dataset by Bell et al. ([23]), in which MUCL3 and TFF1 showed progressive upregulation during PanIN progression, while TFF2 was identified as part of the transcriptional signature defining neoplastic PanIN lesions. Additional transcripts identified by D3AIST, including S100A12 ([38]), BARX1 ([39]), and SERPINA1 ([40]), have been associated with stromal remodeling, inflammatory processes, and epithelial transformation during pancreatic carcinogenesis.

Interestingly, network connectivity was not directly associated with transcript abundance. For example, TFF2 and S100A12 exhibited relevant interaction structure despite relatively low transcript counts, whereas SERPINA1, although highly abundant, displayed a more spatially diffuse distribution and lower network centrality. This observation is consistent with the use of cross-type second-order summary functions, which characterize spatial association patterns across distance scales rather than transcript abundance alone. Accordingly, highly abundant transcripts need not be highly central in the D3AIST network unless their spatial association profiles are atypical relative to the empirical background.

In contrast to the colorectal cancer regions analyzed in this study, the PanIN dataset represents a distinct tissue architecture characterized by a complex premalignant pancreatic intraepithelial environment. Despite these biological differences, D3AIST identified spatially structured atypical interaction networks with highly connected transcripts and spatially localized transcriptional events distributed within the analyzed tissue region. These observations support the applicability of the framework across independent Xenium spatial transcriptomics datasets and heterogeneous tissue contexts.

Together, the analyses of the colorectal cancer ROIs and the independent PanIN ROI provide a data-driven view of spatial transcriptional organization across distinct tissue and disease contexts. These findings are best interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Importantly, the spatial associations reported here are descriptive summaries rather than evidence of direct regulatory or causal links. Instead, they prioritize gene pairs whose colocalization or segregation patterns may reflect shared cellular programs, tissue microenvironments, or spatial compartmentalization.

From a methodological perspective, D3AIST complements established spatial omics pipelines by shifting the unit of analysis from aggregated cell/spot profiles to molecule-level transcriptional events when such coordinates are available. Rather than requiring cell-type labels, signaling priors, or predefined interaction models, it estimates gene-centered spatial interaction structure directly from the data via second-order (pairwise) spatial relationships. This event-based view enables the construction of gene–gene spatial association networks from point-level transcript measurements, highlighting pairs that exhibit atypical colocalization or segregation patterns relative to the background tissue organization.

Although D3AIST is not designed to infer cell-cell communication mechanisms, the atypical gene pairs it highlights may help prioritize follow-up analyses using complementary frameworks such as ligand-receptor modeling (e.g., collectNET [41]) or cell-type-resolved deconvolution/mapping approaches (e.g., cell2location [42]).

A complementary aspect of the framework concerns its computational scalability, which is particularly relevant given the large number of transcriptional events typically generated by high-resolution spatial transcriptomics platforms.

The computational results show that memory usage increases almost linearly with the thinning parameter, reflecting the increasing number of retained transcription events. Execution time also increases monotonically with thinning, although somewhat more than proportionally. Despite the quadratic increase in the number of gene pairs with the number of analyzed genes, the observed increase in runtime was much more moderate, suggesting that the computational burden is mainly driven by the number of transcription events rather than solely by the number of pairwise gene interactions.

Restricting the analysis to smaller image windows substantially reduced both execution time and memory usage. In particular, memory usage scaled almost exactly with the number of transcription events contained in the analyzed region, while runtime also decreased markedly, with some fixed computational overhead becoming more apparent for smaller windows. Overall, the largest configuration considered, involving 100 genes (4,950 gene pairs) required 1435.53 s, i.e. almost 24 min, and about 145 MB of memory on a standard 8-core workstation.

The current implementation presents several limitations. First, the analysis was restricted to a targeted gene panel, limiting the range of detectable transcriptional programs. Extending the approach to larger panels or whole-transcriptome technologies would likely reveal additional interaction modules and refine the characterization of network architecture. Second, spatial transcriptional interactions at the transcript level do not necessarily imply direct cellular interactions, as nearby transcripts may originate from different cells or reflect diffusion effects. This limitation highlights the need for integrative approaches combining spatial interaction modeling with cell segmentation, cell-type annotation, or imaging data to better resolve the cellular basis of these programs. Because the empirical reference is constructed from the set of gene pairs included in the analysis, D3AIST results are inherently dependent on panel composition. They may also be influenced by residual heterogeneity among gene-pair second-order summary functions, since these summaries may not fully account for differences in spatial extent, non-stationarity, characteristic spatial scales, or biological function. Consequently, comparisons across datasets or panels should be interpreted cautiously unless the same gene set or a harmonized panel is used. In this sense, the detected atypical interactions provide a comparative, data-driven prioritization of spatial associations that may warrant downstream biological or statistical validation.

From a computational perspective, evaluating interactions over all observed points may become impractical for large datasets. In such cases, random thinning provides a principled approximation, preserving second-order characteristics such as the cross-K function while reducing computational complexity.

Finally, alternative strategies such as grouping genes into functional categories (e.g., via gene ontology) could enable the analysis of higher-level interaction patterns, facilitating biological interpretation while improving scalability.
