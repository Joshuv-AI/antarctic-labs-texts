Biomimetic climbing robot design inspired by geckos and cats for rough wall applications
Authors: Mohammed K. Jodah, Mofeed Turky Rashid, Ali Hassan Abdulaali, Bilal Naji Alhasnawi, Alžběta Dočekalová, Vladimír Bureš
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This research presents the design of a quadrupedal robot capable of climbing rough surfaces. The robot’s shape, body, arms, and locomotion mechanism were inspired by the gecko, while its limbs were inspired by the limbs cats used to climb trees and grip rough surfaces. The robot has three degrees of freedom. Its grasping limbs were designed in an arched shape, with each limb equipped with seven claws so that each hook moves independently on the wall without overlapping, The arms were designed in a parallelogram shape and at the same level as the robot’s body to ensure stability during climbing and maintain the robot’s center of gravity close to the wall surface, enhancing gripping force and reducing the risk of slipping or falling. These features enable the robot to grasp, cling, and climb rough surfaces with greater ease. The motion algorithm applied to the climbing robot was inspired by the gecko algorithm, and a kinematic model was developed to calculate the displacement and position of the robot arm’s end-receiver. The robot’s body was designed using SolidWorks, and the robot’s body was fabricated and implemented using a 3D printer. Some system kinematics calculations were performed using MATLAB. Several climbing experiments were conducted on rough surfaces at 75° and 85° inclinations. In 15 trials on a 75° slope, the robot achieved a success rate of 93% demonstrating reliable performance. The robot’s climbing speed ranged from 20 to 60 cm/min. The experiments demonstrated the robot’s ability to climb and navigate on rough surfaces and uneven terrain efficiently and with stable performance.

Introduction

In recent years, robotics has advanced significantly, extending beyond industrial fields to include areas such as the military, surveillance, and monitoring. Among these robots, the climbing robot is the most important. The significance of using climbing robots on various vertical surfaces lies in their ability to perform tasks that are difficult to reach due to the risks associated with high places, such as the walls of tall buildings, which pose serious threats to workers, or because of the high costs. Robots can handle hazardous environments that put individuals at risk1–5.

One of the most prominent challenges facing researchers in the field of wall-climbing robots is achieving sufficient adhesion on various surfaces while ensuring a reliable level of effectiveness. Consequently, diverse adhesion methods offer significant opportunities and key areas of focus for scientific research in wall-climbing robots. Thus, wall-climbing robots can be categorized according to their adhesion methods into several groups, including magnetic adhesion, vacuum suction pads, and biologically inspired systems6–12.

Biologically inspired methods can be applied to various surfaces and do not necessarily require specific means to create adhesion. One type of method inspired by nature for climbing and grasping rough surfaces is the claw-like technique used by cats, as well as the climbing mechanisms of geckos. Through continuous observation and monitoring of these climbing animals, which are renowned for their ability to navigate rough terrains, researchers have developed new adhesion techniques and methods inspired by their unique adaptations. Robotic limbs are equipped with fishing hooks that mimic claws, enabling them to grasp, grip, and climb over rough surfaces such as concrete and brick. For instance, a robot with limbs containing miniature spikes can ascend rough walls by embedding the spikes into the uneven terrain. Climbing robots equipped with claws to traverse these challenging surfaces represent advanced technology that allows robots to adapt to irregular terrains that pose difficulties for conventional robots. Such irregular surfaces, characterized by roughness and unevenness, like rocks and trees, can complicate movement. Claws provide the necessary frictional force to stabilize the robot on these surfaces, thereby enhancing stability during climbing. Consequently, robots that utilize claws for climbing offer promising opportunities in various fields, particularly in rescue, inspection, and surveillance operations, thereby expanding the horizons of scientific research2,3,7,13–19.

Some animals with the ability to climb and cling have become an inspiration source for researchers, as bionic technologies such as claws on cats are a typical technique for creating adhesion20. We will provide an overview of the mechanical adhesion method used in climbing robots to a group of researchers who have developed or used wall-climbing robotic technologies.

Aihong Ji et al. designed a quadruped robot with claws capable of climbing rough vertical surfaces. Its legs feature flexible pads equipped with claws. This robot boasts lightweight, high stability, and excellent climbing ability on rough surfaces. However, the robot’s body has eight degrees of freedom, which complicates the design and implementation process13. Xu et al. proposed a novel grasping method based on a mechanism using a claw-like hook. They proposed two types of mechanical models for the interactions between sharp claws and micro-protrusions and outlined the design method for sharp claw tips. Based on these, an eight-limbed wall-climbing robot was designed and tested on a concrete wall model. This design is lightweight and highly stable. However, having such many robot limbs increases the complexity and high cost of the design21. Haynes and Rizzi designed a six-legged, bioengineered robot capable of climbing rough vertical surfaces. The robot uses fine, stacked spines on its six feet, enabling it to adhere to rough, hard vertical surfaces, such as plaster. The robot has six legs, each with two joints and twelve degrees of freedom. The robot contains 12 servo motors, excluding the tail. This design increased complexity and implementation capacity due to its high cost and heavy weight 2.8 kg22. Hassan et al. proposed a hybrid robot for climbing rough surfaces, dubbed CLARC, which consists of an arm resembling a gecko’s arm but relies on a cat’s claw mechanism for gripping rough surfaces. In this design, the researcher was able to reduce the number of servo motors in the joints of each arm, thanks to the proposed motion mechanism based on the gecko’s arm movement. The design shows that the robot is not rigid and that the gripper is attached laterally at the effective end of the arm, reducing pressure on the wall and thus weakening the grip process. Furthermore, the robot failed to climb rough surfaces and was limited to the metal clip only8,23.

Spenko et al. proposed a bioengineered hexapod robot equipped with small hooks that enable it to cling to walls. It was used to climb trees, as well as vertical building surfaces such as plaster and crushed stone, and to move on the ground. These capabilities were achieved by combining bioengineered design with traditional methods. The proposed weight of the robot was 2.5 kg, but this was not achieved due to the heavy weight of the batteries, which increased the total weight of the robot to 3.8 kg. Furthermore, the robot’s limbs were equipped with spiny feet without the use of rigid materials or claws, which affects the overall climbing process, in addition to its high cost24. Sintov et al. presented a design for a claw-inspired robot (CLIBO), capable of climbing rough surfaces. The robot features four limbs, each with four degrees of freedom, which means a total of 16 actuators are used to control the arm movements. The proposed design was tested on a rough surface model for climbing trials, and the experiments demonstrated high reliability in the grasping and clinging process, as well as the efficiency of the movement algorithm. However, one issue with this design is the slow movement due to the robot’s instability when increasing the speed of the arms and grippers. Another problem is the slow communication and long delays between movements, which limit the robot’s speed to a maximum of 12 cm/min25. Nadan et al.26 presented a new climbing robot (LORIS), used for surveillance. It can climb irregular vertical surfaces, using a small passive gripper with micro-spikes that can bear varying loads. The robot weighed 3.2 kg and measured approximately 660 mm in length including its tail, and 180 mm width. The LORIS robot successfully climbed irregular vertical surfaces and rocky surfaces in the laboratory. Some drawbacks of the LORIS climbing robot include its relatively heavy weight and long, and its reliability is insufficient for practical applications. Zi et al.27 presented a quadrupedal climbing robot designed for rocky terrain. The robot uses SPASAS grippers, claw-like end effectors inspired by beetles, arboreal birds, and ungulates, providing high gripping force and passive adaptability to uneven surfaces. Unlike conventional climbing systems, experimental results demonstrated successful multimodal locomotion—walking on flat terrain and climbing vertical rock structures—without the need to change end effectors. However, limitations remain, including reliance on laboratory artificial terrain, manual foot placement, and limited autonomy despite the integration of an RGB-D camera. Furthermore, occasional climbing failures (slipping and fall) occurred due to weak gripping force and delayed force redistribution.

This paper presents the design and implementation of a bio-inspired climbing robot, modeled after the anatomical structure and locomotion of a gecko, particularly its body and limb articulation. The gripping mechanism, however, draws inspiration from a cat’s paw and incorporates curved fishing hooks affixed to the limb ends, functioning as claws to enable strong and reliable adhesion to rough surfaces7. A mathematical model has been developed to accurately characterize the robot’s climbing dynamics, and its validity has been confirmed through simulations in MATLAB. The robot is driven by only 12 motors and is constructed using 3D printing, achieving a lightweight and structurally efficient design that reduces mechanical complexity while ensuring functional effectiveness. Experimental trials were conducted on a rough wall surface model to simulate real-world climbing conditions. The proposed system demonstrates notable advantages in terms of affordability, locomotion flexibility, and gripping strength on uneven surfaces. Moreover, the robot is capable of maintaining stable positions over extended durations, a critical feature for applications requiring long-term positional stability. Throughout this paper, we will explore several key areas of the design and development process. Initially, we will provide an overview of the general design of the robot, followed by a detailed discussion of the mathematical and kinematic principles behind its movement. In the second section, we will delve into the algorithm used to control the arm’s movements and the underlying mechanisms that facilitate its operation. The third section will focus on the practical implementation of the robot’s design, followed by a comprehensive discussion of the verification process and the experimental results obtained from testing the proposed design on a rough vertical surface model.

Overall, these contributions represent a focused integrative engineering advancement. Rather than introducing a new adhesion or locomotion principle, this study integrates and optimizes established bio-inspired mechanisms—specifically, the gecko-inspired crawling gait and body posture combined with the cat-inspired claw-based grasping—within a unified robotic system. The primary contribution lies in the engineering design, achieving a lightweight, mechanically simple climbing robot with a minimal number of actuators, reliable performance on rough surfaces, and manufacturability via 3D printing. This integrated approach effectively addresses key practical limitations that restrict the deployment of climbing robots in real-world inspection and surveillance tasks.

Structural design of the climbing robot

The original idea for designing this robot was inspired by nature. Special focus was placed on the movements and mechanisms of geckos, known for their amazing ability to climb and cling to rough, vertical surfaces due to the unique structure of their feet, enabling the robot to navigate difficult surfaces with ease. The robot’s limbs were designed to imitate those of cats and felines, which possess agile and flexible limbs and are characterized by their skill in climbing and clinging to walls and trees effortlessly. Each component of the robot was carefully designed, ensuring that its movements and accessories were precisely tuned for optimal performance. This level of precision in design enabled the robot to successfully adhere to and climb rough, uneven surfaces, proving its effectiveness in various environments. When designing a climbing robot, several factors must be considered. First, the robot’s size must be optimized to be suitable for the application. The robot should be small enough to navigate tight spaces and fit within various environments while maintaining the strength needed to perform its tasks. Second, the robot must effectively climb and maneuver on vertical surfaces. If it cannot perform these movements accurately, it will lose adhesion to the surface and fall, which poses the main challenge, as a lack of maneuverability will prevent the robot from achieving its primary purpose28. Third, the robot must be capable of carrying and transporting small, practical objects while ensuring that it does not lose its ability to cling to surfaces, as weight limits must be precisely defined to maintain a balance between performance and capability.

The robot consists of four arms identical in design and shape, with each pair sharing the same timing and mechanical motion. Each arm has three degrees of freedom, as shown in Fig. 2, which is explained in the proposed arm design diagram. Each arm consists of four links arranged in a parallel geometric configuration, with each link connected to the others by a 5 mm diameter steel rod to form a joint, allowing for more flexible rotational motion.Fig. 2The robot arm design by SolidWorks.

The robot arm design by SolidWorks.

Link A is fixed and immovable, and the upper part is attached to Motor1 (which is responsible for moving the arm upward and downward). Likewise, Motor2 is fixed at the lower part of link A, which is responsible for moving the arm forward and backward. Link A is connected at its ends to links B and D through a steel shaft. Link C is connected to links B and D via the steel shaft, while its other end is connected to Motor3, which is responsible for controlling the gripper movement. The arm is equipped with three powerful torque servo motors and a metal motion system. The first motor is dedicated to the shoulder, with an axis of rotation parallel to the wall. It moves the arm away from or toward the wall to prevent friction between the arm and the wall surface during climbing and ascending, while also helping to limit the level of the robot relative to the wall. The second motor has an axis perpendicular to the wall, and it is responsible for the forward and backward movement of the arm, in other words, it moves the arm toward or away from the ground, while at the same time performing another task and with the help of the third motor in pushing the body of the robot to up away from the ground.

The third motor, which has axes parallel to the wall, is responsible for the movement of the gripper and performs two functions at the same time: the first is to push the gripper towards the wall to insert and fix the claws in the wall’s topography and rough surfaces, and the second is to control the distance between the robot and the wall. Therefore, the second and third motors are responsible for the movement of the robot. In addition, the arm was designed in a rectangular shape to prevent any tension or breakage that may occur in the corners and joints of the arm during climbing. Figure 2 shows the schematic design of the robot arm using SolidWorks.

The gripper located at the end of the arm, which is connected to the Motor3, is designed in a way that mimics the way cats cling and climb rough surfaces as shown in Fig. 3, and is a device specifically designed for the climbing robot that enables it to grip cracks or rough topography in walls while climbing. The gripper consists of seven steel fishing hooks placed inside longitudinal grooves to prevent interference between them. The hooks are fixed to the gripper body with iron nails, and behind each hook, there is a spring that performs the task of pushing the hook and allowing it to move back and forth in one direction. This design provides the gripper with high gripping strength and allows it to carry weights of up to 2.5 kg7. Therefore, the common design of both the leg and the gripper gives the robot a strong ability to cling, climb, and carry heavy weights.Fig. 3Proposed design of Robot Arm gripper. (a) Cat’s limb. The use of seven independent claws represents a deliberate engineering adaptation of the biological claw principle. While many adept animal climbers (e.g., birds, arboreal mammals) utilize two opposing claws—an efficient solution evolved for weight saving and dynamic force control—our design prioritizes robustness and reliability for a passive robotic system. Multiple claws provide essential redundancy when engaging the discrete and unpredictable asperities of artificial rough surfaces. This increases the probability of successful engagement, distributes load to prevent localized overstress, and reduces the need for precise gripper alignment compared to a dual-claw pincer mechanism. Thus, the gripper design is bio-inspired in its fundamental hook-and-pull mechanism but bio-adapted in its multi-claw architecture to meet specific robotic constraints.

Modelling of the robot arm

Kinematics is used to analyze and study the motion of objects without considering their causes, such as force and torque. Robot kinematics refers to the analytical study of a robot’s motor motion and is essential for understanding its behavior. Robot kinematics is categorized into two types: forward kinematics and reverse kinematics29,30.

Forward kinematics

Forward kinematics refers to the relationship between the effective end of the robot arm (EE) and the base, involving the determination of the homogeneous transformation matrix that describes the position and orientation of the effective end frame relative to the base frame8.

Research in this field began in the middle of the last century when Denavit and Hartenberg introduced a concise formulation for specifying the coordinate frames and the relationship between the effective end of the arm and the base, which later became known as the D-H notation. The D-H method provides the kinematic model for executing a transformation from one coordinate frame to an adjacent frame using the homogeneous transformation matrix. For objects that include multiple frames, such as a robot arm, multiplying the matrices of these frames together will yield a homogeneous transformation matrix that transforms the coordinates from the EE frame to the base frame. The resulting transformation matrix is called the arm matrix, which contains the position and direction of EE (X, Y, and Z)29–31. To determine the shape of the link and its joint motion, four kinematic parameters are defined, widely known as the D-H parameters. The shape of the link is described by the following two parameters, namely the length of the ligament and the twist of the ligament, while the motion of the joint is described by the remaining two parameters, namely the joint displacement and the joint angle29,31.

It has been observed that applying the DH parameter method to closed-loop kinematic chains leads to inconsistencies in the corresponding kinematic model. When applying this method to mechanisms containing triple, quadruple, etc. links with more than two points of contact, the multi-parameter set problem arises. Khalil and Kleinfinger point out that there is uncertainty in the case of applying DH to closed-loop robotic arms26,28. However, Siciliano et al. have shown that the DH parameter method may apply to closed-loop manipulators by roughly breaking one of the joints, which leads to the formation of an equivalent open chain with a tree structure to which the DH notation can be applied32,33.

The kinematic description of the closed-loop manipulator robot is complex compared to the open-loop manipulator robot. If these two classes are combined, the resulting offspring may be a hybrid, and these chains are referred to as hybrid manipulators with a more complex structure compared to their counterparts30.

Based on the above, in this research we will resort to deducing the relationship between the effective end of the arm relative to the base, which represents the forward movement of the arm, by using both the rotation and the transformation matrix for each joint relative to the previous joint, and finding their product, taking into account the displacement between the two joints along the axis29.

Inverse kinematics

It is known that the equation for forward motion represents the relationship between joint variables and the position and direction of the end effector. In contrast, inverse kinematics involves the equations for joint variables that correspond to the position and direction of the end effector. If we know the position and direction of the robot arm’s end actuator, along with the parameters of its joints and connections, we need to find the corresponding joint angles so that the end actuator can be positioned as desired. In other words, we need to determine (q1, q2, and q3) in relation to the position and direction of the arm’s end effector, namely (X, Y, and Z). Solving the inverse motion is crucial for translating the motion specifications of the end effector in operational space into the necessary joint space kinematics that allow the desired motion to be executed. Several methods exist for solving the arm’s inverse motion, including geometric and algebraic methods. To simplify the process, the second method will be utilized, which is referred to as the "Algebraic Solution." This method is commonly applied to arms with multiple joints29,33,34.

Leg motion algorithm

The mechanism of gecko leg movement will be demonstrated during a climbing process for a single rotation, allowing us to understand the optimal design of robotic locomotion inspired by gecko movement. The mechanism will be explained in stages to enhance understanding of gecko locomotion. It is important to recognize that geckos use a locomotion mechanism known as "alternating walking," a diagonal movement in which the front and hind feet alternate (diagonally) to balance and propel themselves upward while climbing. At the beginning of climbing, assume that all four legs of the gecko are attached to the wall, as shown in Fig. 6a, while the left foreleg and right hind leg converge.Fig. 6Gecko locomotion gait while climbing on the wall.

Gecko locomotion gait while climbing on the wall.

Stage 1: Detachment phase (left foreleg and right hindleg) from the wall surface (see Fig. 6b):

Careful observation of the leg movements revealed that the left foreleg detached and lifted first, immediately followed by the detachment and lifting of the right hindleg, as indicated by the red arrows. However, the detachment of both the left foreleg and right hindleg from the wall appears to occur nearly simultaneously. Meanwhile, the right foreleg and left hindleg remain attached to the wall.

Stage 2: The upward movement of the left foreleg and right hindleg is accompanied by a simultaneous change in the elbow joint position of the right foreleg and left hindleg to increase pressure on the wall to generate action and reaction. Their downward movement also forces the gecko’s body to bend midway, helping it propels upward (see Fig. 6c). Therefore, the attached limbs are important factors in generating body movement.

Stage 3: The left foreleg and right hindleg are fixed to the wall:

The left foreleg reaches the wall surface and is fixed to it shortly before the right hindleg, approximately one millisecond. Now, the gecko has all its limbs fixed to the wall surface (see Fig. 6d). The previous stages (detachment and movement) are repeated, but with the right foreleg and the left hindleg (see Figs. 6e–f). With this pattern, the gecko has completed a full cycle.

After fully understanding how the gecko moves its legs while climbing, and because the proposed design mimics climbing animals, the optimal number of arms for the robot would be four, with three degrees of freedom to control climbing and prevent falling. Motor1 (see Fig. 5) will be used for the shoulder, motor2 for the wrist, and motor3 for the gripper. The robot’s arms will move diagonally, also known as alternating walking, where two arms will be in motion and ascending while the other two cling to the wall. The robot must be secured to the wall with the arms in a resting state, meaning that the motors are powered but not actively moving. The robot first moves the gripper away from the wall. Then, it raises each arm a fixed distance from the ground. Next, it moves the arm toward the wall, keeping it very close to avoid friction. The gripping process begins by positioning the gripper against the wall surface and attaching the claws to it. The robot then pulls the arm down toward the ground. With the help of the gripper, the robot pushes upward. This process is repeated, as shown in Algorithm (1) below.

To evaluate gripping performance, a balance analysis and mechanical testing were conducted using a rough wall surface to enhance adhesion, minimize slippage during climbing, and examine structural responses such as hook bending and distortion underload. Results indicated that a single hook exhibited slippage or deformation under increased loads. To address this, the number of hooks in the gripper was increased, allowing it to support a load of up to 2.4 kg without significant deformation or slippage7.

These tests ensure that the gripper can withstand the weight of the robot, particularly since two arms are permanently attached to the wall during climbing, while the other two arms are in motion. This arrangement provides sufficient stability for the gripper. Practical experiments were conducted on the robot without additional loads, with a total weight, including accessories, of approximately 1.8 kg. The robot successfully climbed. Based on the proposed algorithm for the robot’s climbing motion, the movement mechanism was programmed into the ATmega2560 microcontroller.

The climbing robot model was initially tested on a rough surface with an inclination angle of 75 degrees. This angle was selected for the initial rigorous validation phase as it imposes a significant gravitational load while providing a margin of safety for algorithm tuning. Furthermore, from a static mechanics perspective, a 75° slope presents a distinct challenge for passive claw adhesion: the normal force component (m·g·cosθ) pressing the claws against the surface is reduced compared to a vertical wall, testing the strength of the mechanical interlocking. Subsequent to the initial tests, the robot’s performance was also validated on a semi vertical (85°) wall of the same material, this confirmed its ability to climb successfully under maximum gravitational torque.

Consequently, the motion of one arm pair is representative of the others, differing only in temporal sequencing. During movement, when one pair of arms detaches from the wall, the remaining two pairs maintain contact, clinging to the surface to stabilize the robot and prevent it from falling. To illustrate the mechanism of the robot’s climbing cycle, the process begins with all of the robot’s arms attached to the wall, as shown in Fig. 12a. The cycle starts with the coordinated movement of the right forward arm (RFA) and the left rear arm (LRA). First, the grippers are detached from the wall (Fig. 12b), while the grippers of the other two arms, the left forward arm (LFA) and the right rear arm (RRA) remain firmly attached. The detached arms (RFA & LRA) are then raised upward to a safe distance from the wall surface to avoid any collision (Fig. 12c). After this, two simultaneous actions take place: the RFA and LRA are moved forward, while the other two arms (LFA and RRA), which are still attached to the wall surface, are pulled backward simultaneously, as illustrated in Fig. 12d. The pulling action of the arms gripping the wall provides the necessary reaction force to propel the robot upward, thereby ensuring a stable and controlled climbing motion. Next comes the stage of reattaching the grippers of the RFA and LRA to the wall surface (Fig. 12e). After that, the robot detaches the grippers of the LFA and RRA from the wall (Fig. 12f). The robot now performs two simultaneous movements: pulling the (RFA) and the (LRA) backward, while simultaneously moving the (LFA) and the (RRA) forward (Fig. 12g). Finally, the grippers of the LFA and RRA are reattached to the wall surface, returning the robot to the initial configuration shown in Fig. 12h. The observed reliable adhesion stems from this multi-claw, passively adaptive design. This highlights a key divergence from pure biomimetics toward principle-informed engineering. The animal model solves climbing with minimal claws through active neuromuscular control and energy-optimal morphology. For a statically stable, power-constrained robot, we instead employ multi-point redundancy and statistical mechanics. Each claw acts as an independent probe; the system’s strength arises from the collective engagement of several claws, ensuring stability even if individual claws slip or find poor purchase. This explains the high success rate despite variations in surface topography.

The adaptation of each claw to the irregular surface wall allowed the robot to maintain its distance from the wall, in addition to adopting a crawling position. This, in turn, allowed the robot to maintain its center of gravity very close to the surface, reducing the risk of slipping and ensuring it remained stable at all times, even in the state of a power outage. This significantly contributed to the overall efficiency of the robot. Some tests were conducted on the robot while it climbed a surface specifically designed for testing the climbing robot. The robot’s arm movement speed reached approximately 250 cm/min. However, several factors forced us to reduce the robot’s arm speed; this included the fact that the process of separating the gripper from the wall at this speed led to unwanted vibrations, negatively affecting the robot’s stability during climbing. Therefore, the arm speed was reduced to 150 cm/min to ensure safer and more stable climbing.

Although the arm’s speed is high, the robot’s overall speed is lower than that of the arm. This is due to several factors, including the sequence of movements of the arm components (specifically the shoulder, wrist, and gripper) and the time intervals between them, as well as the intervals between the movements of the robot’s two arms. These combined time delays caused the robot’s overall speed to decrease to 20–60 cm/min. Experimental protocol and performance metrics

The robot’s task efficiency is defined as the ratio of successful climbing trials to the total number of attempted trials. Based on this definition, a unified experimental protocol was established to quantitatively evaluate the robot’s robustness and climbing performance. Each trial consisted of an independent autonomous attempt to climb a fixed vertical distance of 2 m on the designated test surface. A trial was classified as successful only if the robot completed the entire distance without experiencing a catastrophic failure, such as a fall or an unrecoverable slip requiring external intervention. Temporary slips that were autonomously corrected within subsequent climb cycles were not considered failures.

For the primary evaluation conducted at a 75° inclination, 15 trials were performed, resulting in a task efficiency of 93% (14 successful trials out of 15) and a measured climbing speed ranging from 20 to 60 cm/min. To further assess performance under increased gravitational loading, an additional set of 15 trials was carried out on a semi-vertical surface inclined at 85° using the same material. Under these more demanding conditions, the robot maintained a success rate of 80% (12 out of 15 trials) with a comparable speed range of 20–60 cm/min. The observed reduction in overall climbing speed is primarily attributed to the temporal intervals between successive motions of individual arm components, as well as the sequential coordination among multiple arms. The overall climbing efficiency reported in the abstract and discussion refers to the results obtained at the 75° inclination. High success rates observed at both inclination angles demonstrate the reliability of the proposed design. All reported climbing speed values are calculated exclusively from successful trials.

One of the robot’s most notable features is its ability to stay attached and cling to vertical surfaces without electrical power. This is because of the careful design of the gripper, the pattern of hooks arranged in an arc, and the independence and flexibility of each hook. This guarantees an effective grip even during a power outage and without external help. It significantly boosts the robot’s safety and reliability, especially when working on uneven and complex terrain. Another feature that increases the robot’s efficiency is the design of the arm and structure, which mimics a reptile (gecko). This reduces the gap between the robot’s body and the wall, keeping the robot’s center of gravity close to the surface. This improves its grip strength when climbing and allows it to stay attached for longer periods.

Another characteristic of the robot is its flexibility and smoothness of movement. This is achieved by dividing the robot’s body into two parts: a front and a rear, connected via a rotating joint. Additionally, there are two push-type springs located on either side of the robot, between the two body parts. The flexibility the robot has while climbing greatly helps mimic the movement mechanism of geckos in terms of body structure, and this feature supports arm movement and enables the robot to push upward while climbing.

Therefore, the above study demonstrates the effectiveness of an integrative, optimization-based design philosophy for climbing robots. By combining a gecko-inspired parallel arm mechanism (for stability and a low center of gravity) with a cat-inspired multi-claw gripping mechanism (for strong, passive adhesion), and implementing it via lightweight, 3D-printed structures, this system was developed to prioritize functional reliability and ease of practical deployment. The key outcome is not a novel discovery in the field of biomimicry, but rather a proven engineering solution that achieves an ideal balance between performance, complexity, cost, and ease of fabrication, as compared with previous studies shown in Table 7.

Conclusion

This paper presents details the design and implementation of a wall‑climbing robot tailored for navigating rugged vertical environments. Its external body and arm‑actuation systems are inspired by geckos, while the gripper mechanism is modeled on feline limb adaptability, providing effective adhesion on rough terrains such as rock faces and tree bark. By adopting a gecko‑like, low‑crawling posture, the robot keeps its center of gravity close to the wall, enhancing both grip strength and stability. This posture is crucial not only for reliable adhesion and upward but also for maintaining balance during unexpected power interruptions. A mathematical kinematic model of the robot’s arm was developed using MATLAB, enabling precise computation of required displacements. Experimental validation occurred on a custom-built test wall exceeding two meters in height. Climbing performance was evaluated by tracking displacement and measuring lateral deviations during ascent. The results confirm that wall surface roughness and claw geometry play a dominant role in gripping stability: higher surface roughness combined with smaller claw tip size significantly improves mechanical interlocking and adhesion, enhancing climbing efficiency, maneuverability, and adaptability across diverse terrain.

Notably, the robot demonstrated high reliability and safety during extended operation, with its ability to remain attached even during power loss, fundamentals feature for autonomous or long-duration missions. Further investigation revealed that the detachment speed of the gripper influences overall system stability: excessively rapid separation induces undesirable vibrations and destabilizes the structure. To counteract this, we intentionally reduced arm movement speed, thereby achieving smoother transitions and maintaining structural equilibrium throughout climbing.

Additionally, temporal delays between sequential motions of arm segments (e.g., gripper, wrist, shoulder) were observed to reduce overall ascent speed. These sequencing constraints, while limiting high velocity, did not compromise climbing adequacy: the robot maintained a robust and satisfactory climbing rate suitable for rough-surface applications. Together, these results confirm the effectiveness of the proposed bio-inspired design and control approach in delivering stable, efficient wall locomotion under practical conditions.

The observed reliable adhesion stems from this multi-claw, passively adaptive design. This highlights a key divergence from pure biomimetics toward principle-informed engineering. The animal model solves climbing with minimal claws through active neuromuscular control and energy-optimal morphology. For a statically stable, power-constrained robot, we instead employ multi-point redundancy and statistical mechanics. Each claw acts as an independent probe; the system’s strength arises from the collective engagement of several claws, ensuring stability even if individual claws slip or find poor purchase. This explains the high success rate despite variations in surface topography.
