Overcoming Implementation Barriers in Frailty Management in Cardiology
Authors: Shuting Liu, Qing Yang, Xin Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: barriers, cardiology, frailty, implementation, policy

The progressive aging of the global population, projected to reach 2.1 billion individuals aged 60 and older by 2050, poses frailty as a paramount challenge in contemporary health care, with substantial implications for cardiology clinical practice. For cardiovascular disease, frailty alters disease trajectories, increases mortality risks, and reduces treatment efficacy.1 Frailty, defined as a multidimensional syndrome involving physical, cognitive, nutritional, and psychosocial vulnerabilities, exhibits a bidirectional relationship with cardiovascular disease.2,3 This interaction is supported by shared risk factors, including inflammation, sarcopenia, and autonomic dysfunction.4 The most prevalent cardiovascular comorbidity associated with frailty is heart failure. Frailty is more prevalent in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction compared with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, and it is similarly associated with adverse outcomes, including heightened risks of all-cause death and hospitalizations across these subtypes.5 Clinical trials of finerenone for heart failure with mildly reduced or preserved ejection fraction indicate consistent benefits irrespective of frailty levels, pointing to viable treatment strategies.6 In community-dwelling older adults, research reveals heightened frailty risks after cardiovascular events, highlighting the urgency of early preventive measures.7

The 2022 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) consensus statement on frailty in cardiology represented a significant advancement, providing clinicians with comprehensive guidance on its assessment and management.8 It recommended routine screening, multidisciplinary interventions, and individualized treatment plans to mitigate the impact of frailty. However, a follow-up survey conducted by Wong and colleagues9 1 year later identified persistent knowledge gaps and suboptimal practices among cardiologists, indicating that more than half rarely or never screened older patients for frailty before invasive procedures or during acute cardiovascular events. Building on this, in this issue of JACC: Asia, Wong et al10 extend their investigation through a validated 29-question survey, guided by the ESC consensus and prior analyses of implementation barriers. The respondents, primarily from the Asia-Pacific region and comprising 59 cardiologists, 114 physicians from other specialties, 57 nurses, and 8 allied health professionals, reported barriers to implementation across 7 domains: demographics, health care provider factors, guideline and workflow factors, multidisciplinary team factors, patient and caregiver factors, institutional factors, and resource factors.10 The key findings reveal widespread acknowledgment of frailty’s importance, with 93.3% of respondents agreeing that it is essential in patient care, alongside significant practical limitations. Specifically, 22.7% lacked confidence in identifying frailty, and 28.2% in managing it, whereas approximately one-quarter were unaware of appropriate screening candidates or tools. These gaps were more pronounced among cardiologists, who exhibited lower awareness of screening protocols than other physicians (74.6% vs 88.6% awareness of patients requiring screening, P = 0.018) and were more likely to report time constraints (strongly agreeing they were too busy: 22.0% vs 10.5%, P = 0.041).

Responses concerning multidisciplinary team factors revealed distinct insights across professional groups. Nurses reported significantly fewer communication challenges with multidisciplinary teams compared with cardiologists (1.8% vs 18.6%, P = 0.003) and were less likely to agree that the primary treating physician should lead frailty care efforts. In addition, fewer nurses reported a higher workload than cardiologists. These findings suggest the potential for designating an alternative frailty care leader, such as a nurse-led team, to enhance frailty management. Although 96.6% of respondents considered multidisciplinary teams beneficial, 54.2% encountered communication obstacles and 21.0% experienced difficulties in accessing interdisciplinary support. Regarding patient and caregiver factors, 21.8% indicated unreceptiveness to frailty measures, whereas 93.8% recommended augmented educational initiatives. At the institutional level, 39.9% perceived frailty management as inadequate, and 39.5% reported insufficient collaborations with community care services. In terms of resources, 50.8% cited excessive clinical demands, 47.1% viewed frailty assessments as excessively time-consuming, and 36.1% highlighted inadequate resource allocation. Notably, most respondents (92.0%) supported the integration of digital health technologies to facilitate frailty care.

Previous studies have examined barriers to implementing health interventions for older adults in community and acute care settings. A systematic integrative review of Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment for community-dwelling older adults identifies primarily organizational and systemic barriers, such as limited resources (eg, funding, personnel, time) in diverse policy contexts, interdisciplinary communication gaps, and inadequate coordinated care plans, which hinder adoption and sustainability.11 Another qualitative meta-synthesis on dietary nutrition interventions for sarcopenic older adults in community settings highlights multilevel barriers, including complex protocols (eg, time-consuming meal preparation, lack of tailoring for chronic illnesses) and insufficient provider training or counseling, leading to noncompliance.12 A qualitative study of frailty screening and management among acute care providers in Singapore reveals provider-level barriers like inconsistent frailty knowledge among non-geriatric specialists and differing views on screening tools (eg, Fried's Phenotype deemed time-consuming by emergency physicians but simple by orthopedists), alongside systemic issues such as time/manpower shortages and lack of post-screening guidelines, resulting in fragmented implementation.13

The ongoing commitment of the ESC, as exemplified in the FOCUS on Management of Cardiovascular Disease in the Elderly,14 incorporates frailty assessment alongside digital health innovations, examining the potential of technologies to enhance management. This aligns with the advocacy of Wong et al10 for digital solutions, such as remote monitoring and artificial intelligence–driven tools, to address resource constraints. However, the absence of an updated ESC consensus on frailty since 2022, despite mentions in related documents such as the 2023 European Heart Rhythm Association statement on arrhythmias in frail patients,15 reflects a delay in guideline updating. Resource innovations, particularly digital health, hold promise. Mobile apps for SMS-based screening, AI chatbots, wearables for remote monitoring, and virtual frailty wards could alleviate staffing strains and enable timely interventions. Collaborations with tech firms and academia could harness infrastructure, while training in digital literacy ensures adoption.

Addressing these barriers necessitates a multifaceted, stakeholder-specific approach (Figure 1). At the level of individual health care providers, enhancing education is essential. The study by Wong et al10 indicates that 95.4% of respondents would benefit from additional training, aligning with systematic reviews that endorse multidisciplinary programs combining e-learning and in-person workshops to improve competence. Frailty champions, such as specialized nurses or geriatric cardiologists, could facilitate knowledge dissemination and peer mentoring, while incorporating frailty assessments into routine clinical tasks.

Improvements in clinical workflows are equally essential. Unclear objectives affected 19.3% of respondents and one-third remained unaware of appropriate actions following frailty screening. Previous study showed that the implementation of automated clinical pathways within electronic health records could standardize procedures by auto-populating frailty scores from existing data, thereby reducing redundancy.16 In the field of cardiology, where procedural demands exacerbate time pressures, delegating responsibilities to nurses or allied health professionals may yield substantial benefits. Nurse-led multidisciplinary interventions have been shown to improve advance care planning for vulnerable older adults by leveraging bedside interactions to facilitate early identification and appropriate referrals.17

Multidisciplinary collaboration constitutes a foundational element in frailty management, as the ESC advocates for specialized frailty teams to formulate integrated care plans.8 Frailty Intervention Teams have demonstrated improvements in patients’ quality of life while mitigating caregiver burden. In resource-limited regions of Asia, where the prevalence of frailty surpasses that observed in Western countries and cultural stigmas often impede health-seeking behaviors, effective collaborations between hospitals and community care services are essential. The survey's revelation that 39.5% of respondents viewed such collaborations as inadequate highlights a key opportunity for policy-driven improvements.

The study by Wong et al10 is timely, highlighting the need for tailored strategies to integrate frailty management into cardiology, a field in which frailty is highly prevalent among older adults with cardiovascular diseases. Its Asia-focused perspective effectively addresses region-specific challenges, such as elevated frailty rates in socioeconomically disadvantaged communities and barriers to accessing publicly funded services. Overall, the findings underscore the imperative to advance from mere awareness to practical implementation, leveraging multidisciplinary expertise, innovative technologies, and targeted education to alleviate frailty's burden and promote healthy aging. Nonetheless, limitations—including self-reported biases, underrepresentation of allied health professionals, and geographic skew—restrict generalizability. Future research should prioritize implementation trials to validate proposed strategies, investigate frailty trajectories across diverse populations, and adapt assessment tools like the Fried Phenotype or Clinical Frailty Scale to context-specific settings.
