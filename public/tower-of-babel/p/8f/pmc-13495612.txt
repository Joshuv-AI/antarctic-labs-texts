Quantifying manual editing time in autocontouring via audit log analysis
Authors: Geert De Kerf, Gabriele Balletti, Nina Menten, Michaël Claessens, Thibaut D'homme, Joachim Marichal, Mark J Gooding, Dirk Verellen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and purposeArtificial intelligence (AI)-based contouring reduces delineation workload, yet manual corrections remain required. Reliable, scalable quantification of structure-level editing time is needed to evaluate clinical usability beyond geometric similarity metrics as geometric metrics may not reflect how tooling impacts edit time.Materials and methodsThis retrospective observational study analysed 3083 AI-generated, edited structures created in routine practice. Structure-modifying actions were extracted from the treatment planning system audit log database and converted to per-structure editing time by summing inter-event intervals. Automatic times were benchmarked against manual time recordings for 54 structures across 8 patient cases using Lin's concordance correlation coefficient (CCC). Added path length (APL) between AI and corrected contours was computed, and the associations between editing time, APL and tooling (interpolation use) were investigated using Spearman's correlation coefficient.ResultsAudit log–derived editing time agreed well with manual timing (CCC = 0.93). In the full cohort, 3083/24970 structures were edited (12% correction rate). Brush tools were used in 80% and contour interpolation in 42% of edited structures. Median editing time and APL were higher with interpolation than without (81 s vs 58 s; 232 mm vs 18 mm; both p < 0.05), and correlation between editing time and APL was fair (r = 0.38 vs 0.51).ConclusionsVendor audit logs could be translated into accurate structure-level editing time estimates, enabling low-burden monitoring of manual workload. Editing strategy influenced time–geometry relationships, supporting log-based time endpoints alongside geometric metrics for meaningful auto-contouring evaluation.Keywords: Auto-contouring, Auto-segmentation, AI monitoring, edit time, audit log, added path length

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI)–assisted segmentation has become a cornerstone of radiotherapy workflows, reducing the manual contouring workload and improving delineation consistency. Nevertheless, human oversight remains essential, as clinicians must routinely refine AI-generated contours to ensure anatomical accuracy, resulting in a measurable but variable editing workload [1], [2]. Accurately quantifying this editing effort is therefore crucial for evaluating AI tools, and for reporting clinically meaningful performance metrics.

A growing consensus is being established that geometric, dose-related, and time-related evaluations are all important for assessing model performance [3], [4]. However, how these metrics should be measured, and what should be considered clinically relevant, remains unclear. Nevertheless, combining both quantitative and qualitative metrics is likely to improve the reliability of evaluations, as previously suggested for model commissioning [3], [4]. While qualitative assessments involve expert reviews of model outputs to quantify the level of usability, quantitative metrics, such as Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) or added path length, assess the geometric accuracy. Although time-based measures do not directly reflect model accuracy, their combination with geometric metrics can provide insights into clinical usability. While time savings have been demonstrated at the task level [5], data at the contour level remains limited.

Traditionally, editing time is quantified using self-reported manual recordings, a method that is impractical for clinical adoption and has consequently been applied to only a small number of patients [6], [7], [8], [9]. In some studies, additional actions, such as adjusting window settings and navigating between image slices, were included in the time registration and dedicated software was used [10], [11], [12]. In the past, dedicated software allowed for automatic time recording by measuring the time interval from when the CT scan was first displayed to the final submission of a contour and subtracting interruptions longer than five minutes from the editing time [13]. To overcome the burden of manual time recording, often surface-based metrics, such as added path length (APL) or surface DSC were often reported as a proxies for correction time [14], [15]. However, these surface-based metrics fundamentally measure distance, not time, and cannot fully capture the complexity of real editing behaviour [16]. Factors such as the number of editing operations, the types of tools used, case complexity, and user experience can significantly influence editing time, but are not reflected in APL [17]. When correction tools become even more sophisticated [18], or when models actively learn [19], direct monitoring of edit time may yield more meaningful insight into efficiency gains, whereas geometric metrics may become less reliable proxies for correction time. This underscores the necessity of automatically registering the actual editing time rather than relying on derivative metrics.

The use of audit log data already proved its potential to quantify time spent in complex digital workflows such as the electronic health record [20]. Also, within radiotherapy, audit log data is available in commercial systems and might offer the possibility to analyse time-stamped records of user interactions during segmentation editing, even in a multi-centric context. However, cross-centre, and meaningful system-to-system comparisons are only possible if the definitions of the underlying metrics and thresholds are aligned, both for audit log–based time extraction and for the computation of geometric metrics [21].

Despite the availability of this rich data source, few studies have explored the use of audit log data to investigate the human-software interaction. Nevertheless, these insights can potentially provide useful information within a context of model performance monitoring. This study investigated if audit log data can provide reliable edit times and how tooling impacts geometric and temporal measurements. Consequently, a clear framework was proposed, including thresholds, to use widely available audit log data in commercial software to assess real edit time of Regions of Interests (ROI). The automatic time logging was benchmarked against a manual time registration, and a large-scale analysis was performed combining segmentation outputs, APL measurements, and fine-grained audit log data to quantify how tooling influenced the editing time of AI-generated contours.

Material and methods

Data sources and cohort

This retrospective observational study was conducted to evaluate segmentation editing sessions in routine clinical practice, independent of anatomical region or treatment site. All data was collected between March 2023 and June 2025 and 1967 patients were included, covering 24,970 AI-generated structures of which 3083 were edited and used for analysis. Each datapoint represents an AI-generated segmentation that was subsequently reviewed, edited if required, and eventually approved by a radiation oncologist, with all editing actions performed using the treatment planning software (TPS) RayStation 2024 A (RaySearch Laboratories, Stockholm, Sweden).

Both initial AI segmentations and user-corrected versions were stored by default within the TPS, which allowed for quantitative comparison using volumetric DSC and APL (at a 2 mm threshold) metrics calculated by an in-house Python script based on an open-source implementation [22]. Additionally, all performed TPS actions were automatically stored within an audit log database, capturing detailed information about the editing activity such as the identifier of the user who performed the edits, the editing tool used, and an action timestamp.

Audit log data extraction

For each AI-generated structure within the study period, detailed audit log data were extracted from the SQL database associated with the TPS software. The audit log contained all structure-modifying actions performed during contour editing (e.g., 2D/3D brush tools, interpolation tools, and related editing operations), each recorded with a timestamp. Consequently, every active use of an editing tool generated a corresponding audit log event, and the time difference between consecutive events was aggregated to estimate the contour editing time Tedit. A schematic representation of the data extraction is provided in Fig. 1.

Schematic workflow for deriving structure-level correction time from treatment planning system audit logs. The event message and timestamp fields are used as input. Structure-modifying actions and the corresponding ROI are classified from the event message, and the duration of each action is calculated as the time interval between the current event and the subsequent event. Event durations are then aggregated per structure and summarized by action type to obtain the total correction time.

To ensure that editing time reflected active interaction rather than pauses, correction time was calculated from the elapsed time between consecutive structure-editing events within each user session. To account for possible interruptions, the maximum allowable interval (“cap time”) between consecutive logged structure-editing events within each user session was adjusted in increments of 30 s, ranging from 30 to 300 s. For each cap time setting, every interval was assigned to the structure associated with the preceding event and capped accordingly.

The accuracy of the audit log–based time extraction method was validated by comparing automatically computed editing times, using a range of cap times from 30 to 300 s, with manual reference recordings. An independent dataset, obtained during the correction and validation of an abdominal AI model, was used and consisted of 54 structures from 8 patient cases, spanning 17 unique structures. Manual timing was recorded per structure, starting when editing began and stopping when it ended. Pauses were excluded by pausing the timer, and all actions required to complete the edits, including scrolling, zooming, and image review, were included. Agreement between the two methods was evaluated using Lin's CCC, which measures the extent to which the observations align with the 45-degree line of identity ($y = x$), as shown in Fig. 2.

Agreement between Measured and Estimated Times Tedit when using a cap time of 60 s.

Finally, a cap time of 60 s was chosen for the remainder of this study, aiming to maximise the agreement with manual reference recordings while limiting the influence of non-active periods. Supplementary Table S1 shows the correlation for different cap times to support the chosen cap time decision.

Data analysis

The dataset was partitioned into sequential subsets, as shown in Fig. 3. First, ROIs were classified as edited or unedited to calculate the correction rate. This classification was based on audit log data indicating whether a ROI had undergone at least one manual edit. For edited ROIs, tool-use frequencies were quantified to support stratification by interpolation use, as this tooling may affect the relationship between editing time and contour-change magnitude and both subgroups contained sufficient data for meaningful comparison. A post-hoc analysis of the correlation between interpolation use and volume was conducted.

For all ROIs, median edit time (T∼edit), median APL (APL∼), and their correlation by category were calculated. All analyses assumed non-normal data distributions and calculations were performed using SciPy.

Results

Validation of the audit log-based time extraction against manual time recording showed strong agreement (Lin's CCC = 0.93) when a cap time of 60 s was chosen. In the retrospective cohort, 3083 of 24,970 ROIs were manually edited, corresponding to an overall correction rate of 12%. A total of 23 radiation oncologists performed the corrections, with individual review volumes ranging from 8 to 360 patients. The most frequently used editing tools within an editing session were the brush tool (80% of edited ROIs) and contour interpolation (42%). Smart brush, 3D deformation, and spline drawing were used in 15%, 6%, and 4% of edited ROIs, respectively. Multiple tools could be used within the same ROI editing session. Among the 3083 edited ROIs, 1790 were corrected without interpolation (T∼editnointerp = 58 s (IQR 25 s – 110 s); APL∼nointerp = 18 mm (IQR 4 mm – 71 mm); r = 0.51), whereas 1293 were corrected with interpolation (T∼editinterp = 81 s (IQR 39 s – 165 s); APL∼interp = 232 mm (IQR 72 mm – 468 mm); r = 0.38). Editing time and APL differed significantly between the interpolation and non-interpolation groups (Mann-Whitney U: Tedit U = 928,822, p < 0.05; APL U = 385,788, p < 0.05).

In the organ-specific analysis, the correction rate was highest for the mandible (52%) and lowest for the lungs left and right (1%). Additionally, median editing time was highest for mandible (T∼edit= 374 s (IQR 198 s – 556 s); APL∼ = 95 mm (IQR 49 mm – 155 mm); n = 69) and lowest for the left and right cochlea (respectively T∼edit = 16 s (IQR 10 s – 25 s); APL∼ = 16 mm (IQR 10 mm – 25 mm); n = 8 and T∼edit = 13 s (IQR 9 s – 43 s); APL∼ = 13 mm (IQR 9 mm – 43 mm); n = 6). Considering all corrected ROIs, a very strong correlation was found for 2 organs (ipsilateral breast right, and larynx) with correlations up to 0.90, a moderate correlation was found for 16 ROIs, fair correlation for 22, poor correlation for 10 (left and right axillary lymph nodes L4 and L1, left and right femur head, oral cavity, liver, left kidney and left eye). For some structures, such as the heart, the use of interpolation did not meaningfully impact the correlation between Tedit and APL. A fair correlation (r = 0.51; n = 68) remained fair for corrections using interpolation (r = 0.53; n = 13) and without interpolation (r = 0.53; n = 55). For the bladder, the overall Spearman correlation was moderate (r = 0.64; n = 289) and changed to fair with interpolation (r = 0.47; n = 87) and remained moderate without interpolation (r = 0.68; n = 202). For the spinal cord, the overall fair correlation (r = 0.41; n = 81) changed to poor without interpolation (r = 0.21; n = 29) and remained fair with interpolation (r = 0.38; n = 52).

Discussion

In this study, we found that routinely collected audit log data from a commercial treatment planning system could be translated into a robust, structure-level estimate of manual editing time for AI-generated contours. By reconstructing active editing from time-stamped events (with an inter-action cap) and validating the approach against manual time recording, we provided a practical framework to quantify a clinically meaningful endpoint: how much hands-on interaction is still required after auto-contouring, without adding workload for clinicians.

A key observation was that editing strategy, particularly the use of contour interpolation, altered both the magnitude of geometric change and the time required to perform it. In our cohort, interpolation was used in 42% of edited ROIs and was associated with significantly higher T∼editinterp and substantially higher APL∼interp. These findings support the intuitive notion that interpolation was associated with larger, but not necessarily faster, corrections. This is further reinforced by the lack of correlation of interpolation with structure volume, which may indicate that the amount of editing is the primary determinator of interpolation use rather than the structure size. In routine practice, brush tools were mainly used for small local edits, whereas larger corrections were often performed by deleting most contour slices, retaining only every third or fifth slice, correcting those remaining slices, and subsequently applying interpolation. This approach provided an efficient means of improving contour quality. Importantly, stratifying by interpolation use reduced the correlation between APL and edit time, illustrating that APL captured the extent of boundary change but did not reflect the interaction complexity introduced by different tools, consistent with earlier observations [16].

Placing our structure-level editing times in context remained challenging because few studies reported edit time at ROI granularity. Most publications focused on relative time savings or use surface-based surrogates rather than direct time measurement. Among the limited ROI-level evidence, Poel et al. (2025) provided one of the few references that reported manual correction time per structure [9]. However, their manually timed values were summarized as averages, ranging from 33 s to 209 s and reported for a limited number of structures in the head-and-neck region. Nevertheless, these edit times, in line with our reported time stamps, strengthened the idea that our chosen methodology can provide reliable data. Nevertheless, discrepancies between our reported timings and theirs are present and highlighted the need for harmonized reporting conventions (e.g., whether navigation, review, and interruptions were included) and strengthened the motivation for an automated, clearly defined logging approach that can be adopted consistently across centres [20].

Our correlation analysis further demonstrated that geometric discrepancy metrics could not be assumed to be reliable proxies for human effort. While prior work reported stronger relationships between APL-like measures and correction time, we observed predominantly fair correlations across ROIs, with only a small subset showing very strong correlations (up to r = 0.90). This lower correlation could explained by the choice of APL tolerance applied leading to missed edits (e.g. for the spinal cord, the 2 mm tolerance lead to an APL∼nointerp of zero but a T∼editnointerpof 36 s), and the impact of the tolerance on the correlation merits further investigation. However, our results also indicated that the choice of tooling impacted the correlation, suggesting that accurate evaluation of manual editing effort requires more than geometric agreement alone, particularly when different correction strategies were used.

A major strength of this work was the direct benchmarking of audit log–derived editing time against a manual reference standard. The high concordance suggested that, despite being designed for traceability rather than time-and-motion analysis, vendor audit logs can yield a reliable surrogate for the time clinicians actively spend correcting structures. In addition, these logs enabled tracking ROI correction rates, which were relatively low and consistent with previous studies [2], [24]. Consequently, this audit log analysis may enable large-scale, low-burden monitoring of editing workload over time, across ROI categories, across users, and potentially across software versions or model updates, as institutions seek to evaluate clinical impact beyond geometric accuracy alone [22]. In addition, audit log–based time analysis may help streamline editing workflows by identifying the most efficient correction tools in routine practice. At the same time, true clinical editing effort remains difficult to capture using audit log data, because the extracted time reflected a combination of active correction, contour review, scrolling, and other interactions. For example, a prolonged review may result in only a minor contour adjustment, whereas larger intermediate edits may partly cancel each other out before the final contour is saved. Together, these considerations highlight that audit log-based edit time and geometric metrics, such as APL, provide complementary information: the former reflects the actual user interaction required to correct an AI-generated structure, whereas the latter reflects the geometric extent of the correction and the theoretical effort needed to create that structure.

This study also had limitations that point to clear opportunities for refinement. First of all, the approach is limited to TPS systems that do support audit log-based activity tracking [25]. In the multicentre study by Walker et al. (2022), log-based data was used by only one centre and only at the task level. This coarser level of measurement likely led to substantially higher reported time values. Secondly, because audit logs were not purpose-built for quantifying editing time, our reconstruction depended on assumptions about how inter-action intervals relate to user activity. The 60 s cap was chosen to mitigate the influence of interruptions, but this threshold is tuneable and may need recalibration for other clinics, tasks, anatomical regions, or user groups [20]. However, Trimpl et al. (2024) used a comparable cap time of 85 s, which may serve as an indicative starting point for other centres implementing similar audit log–based analyses. Nevertheless, the validation dataset in this study remained relatively small, containing only 54 abdominal structures [11]. Just like for geometric evaluation, clearly defined measures and ground truth validation will be needed to make audit log-based time extractions more reliable and to allow for multi-centric validations [21]. In addition, the absence of review actions in the current audit log prevented the applied cap time from fully disentangling observation time from active editing time, potentially underestimating total review time. In addition, we did not model user experience or case complexity explicitly. Incorporating these factors could help disentangle workflow effects from contour quality effects and further improve the interpretability of correction rate and time-based performance monitoring.

In conclusion, audit log data provided a feasible and scalable way to measure structure-level manual editing time in routine clinical practice, and our validation indicated that this approach could approximate true active interaction with high accuracy. Our results showed that editing time depends strongly on correction strategy (notably interpolation use) and that commonly used geometric metrics, such as APL, were complementary. As AI auto-contouring continues to evolve, integrating standardized, log-based time endpoints alongside geometric accuracy metrics will be essential for meaningful performance monitoring, benchmarking, and clinical decision-making.
