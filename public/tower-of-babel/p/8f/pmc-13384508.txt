Determinants of Early Mortality After Congenital Heart Surgery: A 7-Year Single-center Experience in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Saud A. Bahaidarah, Jowana M. Aljinaid, Amal A. Alzahrani, Jana M. Banjer, Lujain A. Kofiyah, Nesmah A. Noaman, Rahaf A. Badkook, Raghad A. Aljabri, Raghad S. Alghamdi, Renad M. Alamoudi, Sarah S. Bawazir, Gaser A. Abdelmohsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo determine predictors of early mortality among pediatric patients undergoing congenital heart surgery at a tertiary care center in Saudi Arabia.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 955 pediatric patients who underwent congenital heart surgery at King Abdulaziz University Hospital, Jeddah, between January 2017 and December 2023. Demographic, clinical, operative, and postoperative variables were collected.ResultsOverall mortality was 4.9%. Mortality was higher among neonates and infants and among patients with RACHS categories 4–6. Non-survivors had longer cardiopulmonary bypass times and more frequent delayed chest closure. Postoperative complications, including the need for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), depressed cardiac function, acute kidney injury, central nervous system (CNS) insult, infection, and prolonged mechanical ventilation and intensive care unit (ICU) stay, were strongly associated with mortality. In multivariate analysis, independent predictors of mortality were prolonged ICU stay, longer postoperative mechanical ventilation, postoperative ECMO support, and postoperative CNS insult.ConclusionEarly mortality was comparable to international benchmarks. Postoperative physiological deterioration, particularly ECMO requirement, neurological injury, prolonged ventilation, and extended ICU stay, was independently associated with death, underscoring the importance of early detection and targeted perioperative management.

Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common congenital anomaly and a leading cause of infant and childhood mortality worldwide. Advances in prenatal diagnosis, surgical techniques, cardiopulmonary bypass, and postoperative intensive care have improved early survival after congenital cardiac surgery, including in patients with highly complex lesions [1]. Despite these advances, mortality following congenital heart surgery remains clinically significant and heterogeneously distributed. Neonates, infants with critical CHD, and patients with complex anatomical abnormalities or associated comorbidities continue to experience the highest risk. Large cohort and registry-based studies have demonstrated that mortality is influenced by multiple interacting factors, including low birth weight, prematurity, genetic or chromosomal abnormalities, poor preoperative physiological status, and lesion complexity. Modern risk-adjustment models derived from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons database incorporate patient-specific characteristics, such as syndromic and extracardiac anomalies. This approach improves the accuracy of mortality prediction and shows that genetic and noncardiac conditions contribute differently to risk rather than having a uniform impact across patients [2,3].

Despite these advances, mortality prediction remains imperfect, especially across different healthcare settings, and outcomes vary significantly among institutions, even after adjusting for case mix and surgical complexity. Furthermore, excess mortality extends beyond the early postoperative period, and long-term survival remaining inferior to that of the general population even among patients with successful surgical repair [1,4]. In this context, identifying reliable predictors of mortality after congenital cardiac surgery is critical to enhance risk stratification, optimize perioperative care, and support targeted quality improvement. Despite extensive research on CHD-related mortality, regional data on postoperative outcomes in Saudi Arabia are limited. Accordingly, this study aimed to identify predictors of postoperative mortality among pediatric patients undergoing congenital heart surgery at King Abdulaziz University Hospital (KAUH), Jeddah.

Methods

This retrospective study included 955 pediatric patients with congenital heart disease who underwent cardiac surgery at KAUH in Jeddah between January 2017 and December 2023. The study approved by Unit of Biomedical Ethics - Research Ethics Committee (REC) at KAUH. The investigators extracted demographic, clinical, and operative data from the electronic medical records using a standardized tool. Patients diagnosed with hypoplastic left heart syndrome or congenital complete heart block were excluded. Patients who underwent more than one cardiac surgical procedure during the same hospital admission were also excluded to avoid duplication of outcomes. The variables included patient characteristics, disease type, surgical complexity, operative details, postoperative course, complications, and outcomes. The investigators obtained ethical approval and analyzed the data in accordance with institutional confidentiality standards.

Age was categorized into the following pre-defined developmental groups: neonatal period (birth to 28 days), infancy (1 month–1 year), toddler age (1–3 years), preschool age (3-years), school age (6–12 years), and adolescence (12–18 years). Acute kidney injury (AKI) was defined as a postoperative increase in serum creatinine to more than 2 times the upper limit of normal (normal range: 0.3–0.7 mg/dL). Hepatic injury was defined as an elevation in aspartate aminotransferase (AST) or alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels to more than 2 times the upper limit of normal (normal range: AST, 8–40 IU/L; ALT, 7–40 IU/L; hepatic injury: AST or ALT, >80 IU/L). Elevated AST levels within the first 24 h postoperatively were not considered indicative of hepatic dysfunction because they likely reflect hemolysis related to cardiopulmonary bypass. Chylous effusion was diagnosed when triglyceride levels in chest tube drainage exceeded 1.25 mmol/L or when the patient required a medium-chain triglyceride–based formula. Postoperative bloodstream infection was defined as a positive blood culture after surgery [5].

2.1. Statistical analysis

The investigators performed statistical analysis using Jamovi software (version 2.6; Jamovi Project, 2024). The investigators assessed the normality of continuous variables using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The variables showed a non-normal distribution. Continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (25th–75th percentiles), and categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. The investigators compared survivors and mortality using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

To identify the factors associated with operative mortality, the investigators conducted univariate logistic regression analysis. Variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis were entered into a stepwise multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent factors associated with mortality. The results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals. All tests were two-sided, and P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 955 pediatric patients who underwent cardiac surgery were included in the analysis. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study cohort are summarized in Table 1. The median weight was 6.5 kg (IQR 4.0–12.5). The age distribution of the study population is shown in Fig. 1. Infants represented the largest subgroup, followed by patients beyond infancy and neonates (Fig. 1). Syndromic features were identified in 15.3% of patients, with Down syndrome being the most prevalent (13.4%). The spectrum of congenital heart disease was included both cyanotic (49.8%) and acyanotic (50.2%) lesions. Biventricular repair accounted for 84.2% of surgical procedures, whereas 15.8% of patients underwent univentricular palliation at various stages, most commonly the bidirectional Glenn and Fontan procedures.

3.1. Survival and perioperative factors

The overall survival rate was 95.1% (908/955), and the mortality rate was 4.9% (47/955). Comparisons between survivors and nonsurvivors are shown in Table 2. Nonsurvivors had a significantly lower median weight than survivors (3.4 vs 6.7 kg, P < 0.001). Mortality was inversely related to age. The highest proportion occurred among neonates (11.6%), followed by infants (5.3%), and children beyond infancy (2.3%) (P < 0.001). Higher surgical complexity was also associated with mortality. Patients with RACHS categories 4–6 had higher mortality than those with RACHS 1–3 (17.5% vs 4.1%, P < 0.001).

Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) duration was longer in nonsurvivors (median 86.0 vs 63.0 min, P = 0.020). Aortic cross-clamp time tended to be longer among nonsurvivors, but the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.06). Delayed chest closure was more frequent in nonsurvivors than in survivors (14.3% vs 3.3%; P < 0.001). Neither the presence of syndromic features nor the type of surgery (univentricular vs biventricular) was significantly associated with mortality.

3.2. Postoperative complications and outcomes

Postoperative morbidity differed between survivors and nonsurvivors (Table 3). Nonsurvivors had longer ICU stays (median 15.0 vs 7.0 days, P < 0.001) and longer postoperative mechanical ventilation (12.0 vs 2.0 days, P < 0.001). Postoperative Extra Corporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO) was strongly associated with mortality. Nearly half of the patients requiring ECMO support did not survive (47.5%, P < 0.001).

Depressed postoperative cardiac function occurred more frequently in nonsurvivors than in survivors (9.6% vs 1.3%, P < 0.001). Mortality was higher in patients who developed postoperative complications, including nosocomial infection (P = 0.014), seizures (P < 0.001), central nervous system injury (P < 0.001), acute kidney injury (P < 0.001), dialysis requirement (P < 0.001), postoperative sepsis (P < 0.001), pulmonary edema (P < 0.001), and chylothorax (P = 0.012). Postoperative phrenic nerve palsy was not associated with mortality (P = 0.753).

3.3. Predictors of mortality

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses identifying predictors of mortality are shown in Table 4. In univariate analysis, lower weight, longer CPB time, prolonged ICU stay, prolonged mechanical ventilation, delayed chest closure, higher RACHS category, younger age, postoperative ECMO support, depressed postoperative cardiac function, and multiple postoperative complications were significantly associated with mortality.

After multivariate adjustment, independent predictors of mortality were prolonged ICU stay (OR 1.04 per day; 95% CI: 1.01–1.07; P < 0.001), prolonged postoperative mechanical ventilation (OR 1.14 per day; 95% CI 1.07–1.20; P < 0.001), postoperative ECMO support (OR 11.20; 95% CI 4.27–29.42; P < 0.001), and postoperative central nervous system (CNS) insult (OR 6.35; 95% CI 1.56–25.82; P = 0.010). Other variables, including weight, CPB time, RACHS category, delayed chest closure, and postoperative acute kidney injury, were not significant after multivariate adjustment.

Discussion

4.1. Overall operative mortality and institutional outcomes

The overall operative mortality rate in our cohort was 4.9%, which aligns with internationally reported ranges when interpreted in the context of case mix and surgical complexity. Contemporary data from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) database typically report operative mortality rates between 2% and 4%, with outcomes strongly influenced by the patient risk profile and procedural category [1,2,4]. In our cohort, higher RACHS categories and a substantial proportion of neonatal and complex cases likely contributed to the observed mortality. As a tertiary referral center managing a significant burden of high-risk congenital heart disease, our findings reflect real-world institutional outcomes within our regional context.

4.2. Age, weight, and developmental vulnerability

Mortality in our cohort followed a clear age gradient, with the highest risk among neonates, followed by infants, and the lowest risk among older children. Nonsurvivors also had significantly lower body weights, highlighting the interaction between age and growth status in determining surgical vulnerability. These findings are consistent with large registry analyses demonstrating that earlier gestational age and lower developmental maturity are associated with increased mortality and postoperative complications after congenital heart surgery [6,7]. A key biological explanation is that neonates and small infants have limited physiological reserves. Immature myocardial calcium handling, reduced ventricular compliance, increased pulmonary vascular reactivity, and imbalance in the inflammatory and coagulation systems can increase vulnerability to cardiopulmonary bypass, ischemia–reperfusion injury, and low postoperative cardiac output. In practice, these maturational constraints increase susceptibility to multi-organ dysfunction, prolonged ventilation, and the need for advanced support pathways, which are strongly linked to mortality. In our cohort, younger age and lower body weight were associated with mortality. However, their effects were mainly mediated by postoperative severity and organ injury variables (ECMO, CNS insult, ventilation duration, and ICU stay) in the multivariable analysis. This finding is consistent with international evidence that developmental maturity influences vulnerability through complications and the need for advanced support.

4.3. Surgical complexity and risk stratification

Higher surgical complexity was significantly associated with mortality in the univariate analysis. Patients in RACHS categories 4–6 had greater mortality risk than those in lower categories. These findings align with established risk-adjustment models from large databases that show a stepwise increase in mortality with increasing procedural complexity [2,8,9]. However, surgical complexity was not an independent predictor in the multivariate analysis. The effect of surgical complexity may be mediated through postoperative complications and physiological instability rather than acting directly once downstream factors are considered.

4.4. Operative factors: CPB time and delayed chest closure

Longer cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) duration was associated with mortality in the univariate analysis. Prolonged CPB has been linked to increased mortality and organ dysfunction in pediatric cardiac surgery. It may reflect procedural complexity and a partially causal effect mediated by systemic inflammatory activation, hemodilution, and ischemia–reperfusion injury [10,11].

Delayed chest closure was also associated with mortality but is better interpreted as a marker of postoperative hemodynamic instability and critical illness rather than a direct cause of death. However, neither CPB duration nor delayed chest closure remained independent predictors in the multivariate analysis. This suggests that postoperative complications and physiological deterioration primarily mediate their impact [12,13].

4.5. Postoperative organ dysfunction and mortality

Postoperative organ dysfunction was the central determinant of mortality in our cohort study. Postoperative cardiac dysfunction is a major contributor to mortality after congenital heart surgery. Early low cardiac output significantly increases in-hospital mortality and is often associated with multi-organ dysfunction. Over time, residual structural lesions and persistent ventricular dysfunction can lead to progressive heart failure and late mortality.

High-risk patients typically include younger children, those with complex or single-ventricular anatomy, prolonged bypass and cross-clamp times, residual defects, and greater organ dysfunction. In our cohort, depressed postoperative cardiac function was strongly associated with mortality in the univariate analysis, although its effect was attenuated after adjustment, likely reflecting its close association with downstream severity markers, such as ECMO use and prolonged ventilation [14,15].

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is one of the most frequent complications of congenital cardiac surgery and is consistently associated with increased in-hospital mortality. Large pediatric meta-analyses have demonstrated that cardiac surgery–associated AKI confers approximately sevenfold higher risk of in-hospital death, with a markedly increased need for renal replacement therapy. In neonatal cohorts undergoing surgery for critical congenital heart disease, AKI has been reported in more than one-third of the patients, with mortality exceeding 50% among those affected; severe (stage 3) AKI is particularly lethal and independently associated with death [16,17]. Although AKI was strongly associated with mortality in our univariate analysis, it did not retain independence after adjustment, suggesting that renal dysfunction may represent part of a broader cascade of multi-organ failure rather than an isolated driver of mortality.

Hepatic dysfunction exhibited a similar pattern. Mild postoperative elevation of liver enzyme levels is common and often reflects transient hepatic stress related to cardiopulmonary bypass, low cardiac output, and hepatic venous congestion. However, marked or persistent elevations, particularly those associated with severe hepatopathy and conjugated hyperbilirubinemia, have been associated with substantially increased perioperative and intermediate-term mortality, especially in neonates and those with complex CHD [18]. The identified risk factors included postoperative ventricular dysfunction, residual lesions requiring re-intervention, infection, pleural effusions, arrhythmias, and prolonged parenteral nutrition. These findings underscore the importance of vigilant hepatic monitoring and aggressive hemodynamic optimization.

Hospital-acquired infections (HAIs) are also common after congenital cardiac surgery, occurring in approximately 5–15% of pediatric patients, and are associated with higher in-hospital mortality, prolonged ventilation, extended ICU stay, younger age, low weight, higher surgical complexity, syndromic conditions, prolonged mechanical ventilation, delayed sternal closure, ECMO support, and extended CPB times increase infection risk. However, as in AKI, infection may function more as a marker of severity than as an independent determinant of mortality after full adjustment [19–21].

Collectively, these observations suggest that postoperative organ dysfunction reflects the severity of the systemic insult and hemodynamic instability, serving as both a consequence and an amplifier of critical illness. Early identification and proactive management of multi-organ dysfunction remain essential strategies for improving survival after congenital cardiac surgery.

4.6. Mechanical ventilation and ICU stay

In our cohort, longer postoperative mechanical ventilation and prolonged ICU stay were independently associated with mortality. Clinically, these variables are best interpreted as integrative markers of physiological instability that capture the combined burden of residual lesions, ventricular dysfunction, pulmonary vascular reactivity, inflammatory response, and organ dysfunction. Contemporary reviews of pediatric cardiac surgery emphasize that prolonged mechanical ventilation is tightly linked to adverse postoperative trajectories (e.g., low cardiac output, elevated lactate levels, high vasoactive requirements, infection, and organ injury) and consistently associated with worse outcomes.

At the same time, a key interpretive nuance is reverse causality: prolonged ventilation and ICU stay rarely “cause” mortality in isolation; instead, they often reflect the severity of the underlying postoperative course (e.g., LCOS, sepsis, neurologic injury). This is important when translating regression outputs into bedside strategy; these variables function as “red flags” for escalating support and reassessing residual lesions, rather than purely preventable exposures.

4.7. ECMO as a marker of refractory circulatory failure

Postoperative ECMO was the strongest independent predictor of mortality in our multivariate model (OR 11.20). ECMO after congenital cardiac surgery is associated with a high mortality rate, particularly in high-risk patients. Studies have indicated that although ECMO can be a life-saving intervention, it is often linked to severe complications and high mortality, especially in cases requiring postcardiotomy support. The following sections outline key findings regarding ECMO and mortality. The in-hospital mortality rate of patients receiving ECMO after congenital heart surgery is approximately 56.8% [22]. Specific studies have reported that 58% of the patients requiring postoperative ECMO support die either on ECMO or shortly after weaning [23]. In a cohort of 510 patients who underwent cardiac surgery, 22% of those receiving ECMO died, with higher mortality rates linked to factors such as pre-ECMO lactate levels, residual cardiac lesions, prolonged ECMO duration, renal failure, residual anatomical malformations, high pre-ECMO lactate levels, single ventricle physiology, and bleeding complications [24]. Therefore, ECMO should be interpreted as a marker of disease severity within the postoperative cascade. Although it remains a life-saving modality in selected patients, its requirement signals advanced circulatory collapse and identifies a population in whom aggressive hemodynamic optimization, early assessment of residual lesions, and organ-protective strategies are essential to improve survival.

4.8. Neurological injury as a predictor of mortality

Postoperative neurological injury is an uncommon, but high-impact complication of congenital heart surgery. Although the reported incidence is relatively low (approximately 2.8 %) in a recent pediatric surgical series [25], children who develop CNS complications experience significantly higher early mortality and prolonged ICU and hospital stays. Ischemic brain injury, particularly global hypoxic–ischemic injury, is the most frequent type and carries the highest risk of mortality [26,27]. The risk factors include right-to-left shunt, single-ventricle physiology, aortic arch obstruction, reoperation, and prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass duration. Neurological complications are also more frequent in patients requiring ECMO support, with a reported incidence ranging from 12% to 26% depending on the type of ECMO and patient population [28,29]. In our cohort, postoperative CNS injury remained an independent predictor of mortality. These findings emphasize the importance of meticulous cerebral perfusion management, embolic risk reduction, and early neurological surveillance as key strategies to improve postoperative survival.

4.9. Clinical implications for perioperative strategy

Our findings emphasize the importance of the early recognition of postoperative deterioration, particularly in patients at risk of prolonged ventilation, ECMO requirement, or neurological injury. Strengthening neuroprotective strategies, optimizing cerebral perfusion, and implementing structured neurological monitoring are essential components of care.

Early extubation protocols and minimizing the need for prolonged mechanical ventilation may reduce the incidence of secondary complications. In addition, standardized organ protection strategies, including renal, myocardial, and infection prevention measures, are critical for limiting postoperative organ dysfunction. Therefore, a proactive multidisciplinary perioperative approach may help reduce mortality and improve the outcomes of congenital heart surgery.

4.10. Strengths, limitations, and future directions

This study benefited from a large 7-year cohort of 955 pediatric patients, providing contemporary single-center data within a tertiary referral setting. The use of standardized clinical definitions and multivariate modeling strengthened the validity of the findings and allowed the identification of independent predictors of mortality.

However, the retrospective, single-center design may limit the generalizability and introduce a selection bias. The analysis focused on early operative mortality only and included postoperative variables in the regression model, which may reflect severity rather than purely predictive risk. Residual confounders from the unmeasured factors cannot be excluded.

Future efforts should focus on establishing prospective multicenter registries to enable national benchmarking and risk-adjusted quality assessments. Long-term follow-up studies are needed to understand survival patterns and guide targeted strategies to reduce mortality in congenital heart surgery.

Conclusion

In this 7-year single-center cohort of 955 pediatric patients who underwent congenital heart surgery, the early operative mortality rate was 4.9%. Although younger age, lower weight, and a higher RACHS category were associated with mortality in the univariate analysis, postoperative variables were the primary independent predictors after multivariate adjustment. Prolonged ICU stay, extended mechanical ventilation, postoperative extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support, and central nervous system insults were independently associated with death. These findings highlight the effect of postoperative physiological instability and organ dysfunction on early outcomes. Continued efforts toward early recognition of deterioration, optimized perioperative management, and structured outcome monitoring may contribute to improved survival in pediatric congenital heart surgery.
