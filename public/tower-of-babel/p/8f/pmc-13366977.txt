Dynamic proteomics of Nasonia vitripennis pupal development reveals testis-specific regulation of new proteins (orphans) likely related to exo-α-sialidases
Authors: Charlotte Lécureuil, Christine Almunia, Vanessa Guérin, Jean Armengaud, Thierry Moreau, Sophie Fouchécourt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpermatogenesis is a highly complex developmental process requiring coordinated expression of a large fraction of the genome, and the testis displays one of the highest proportions of tissue-specific proteins across animal species. However, proteomic data capturing testis development over time remain limited, particularly in non-model organisms. In Nasonia vitripennis, spermatogenesis occurs as a single synchronized wave during pupal development, providing a valuable framework to investigate stage-specific protein dynamics underlying testis differentiation.ResultsWe quantified protein abundance in heads, legs, and testes across four successive pupal stages (white, red-eyed white, bicolor, and black pupae), identifying 3,130 proteins. A large ubiquitous core proteome (69.4%) was detected alongside clear tissue- and stage-specific expression patterns. The testis showed the earliest proteomic divergence between early and late pupal stages, indicating an earlier onset of differentiation compared to heads and legs. Clustering of protein abundance trajectories revealed sequential molecular programs associated with cytoskeletal remodeling, meiotic processes, and spermiogenesis, highlighting both conserved pathways and lineage-specific features. Among 175 proteins showing at least a two-fold change in abundance during testis development, 11 were uncharacterized. Four of these belong to a novel family of six-bladed β-propeller–like proteins identified by three-dimensional structure prediction and showing similarity to exo-α-sialidases.ConclusionsThese findings provide a comprehensive view of proteome dynamics during synchronized spermatogenesis in N. vitripennis and reveal candidate proteins potentially involved in testis differentiation. The identification of Hymenoptera-specific β-propeller–like proteins related to sialidases suggests roles in glycan processing, sperm surface modification, and reproductive function, opening new perspectives for understanding the evolution and specialization of reproductive proteins.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12918-1.Keywords: Pupa, Development, Testis, Proteomics, Structure prediction, Orphan protein, Hymenoptera, Nasonia vitripennis

Introduction

In all animal species, spermatogenesis process requires the sequential and coordinated expression of thousands of genes. Current gene/protein catalogues list ≈ 19—20 K human protein-coding genes/proteins (i.e. > 18 000). Several studies and the Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/humanproteome/tissue/testis) report that the testis expresses a very large fraction of the human proteome – approximately 82—84% of all human proteins – and that 220—2300 genes/proteins show elevated expression in testis compared with other tissues [1, 2]. In animals, efforts were performed to construct proteome ATLAS as in human (see https://peptideatlas.org/ where there are pig, horse and chicken proteomes). Interestingly, in most species, the testis is the organ containing the highest number of tissue-specific protein-coding genes (human [3], rodents [4, 5] and chicken [6]). Also, numerous studies have focused on characterizing the proteome of spermatozoa or seminal plasma separately, most of the time in the context of human pathology ([7], review), farm animal sperm conservation or quantification according to fertility (bovine [8], stallion [9], or in model species (mouse [10], bee [11]), but few in the developmental testis.

Most genes and mechanisms involved in spermatogenesis are relatively well- conserved even among phylogenetically distant animal species [12, 13]. In 2021, we conducted phylogeny analyses that revealed that 81% of the testis genes are conserved between vertebrates and invertebrates [14]. Furthermore, the strong conservation of genes involved in gametogenesis across metazoan lineages, including Hymenoptera, suggests that key aspects of spermatogenesis can be effectively studied using a single species as a representative model system [14, 15]. To gain deeper insights into spermatogenesis and to attribute protein functions for each specific cell types, it is essential to establish the protein abundances at different developmental stages of germ cells. While isolated and enriched populations of testicular cells have been used to generate such data in mice (e.g., [16, 17]), stage-specific testicular proteome datasets remain scarce in other species (e.g., Drosophila [18], ram [19]), due to the challenge of obtaining sufficient material from these tiny animals. The parasitoid wasp Nasonia vitripennis serves as an excellent and valuable model organism for studying the role of proteins during spermatogenesis given our existing knowledge about its reproduction. Parasitoid wasps are of significant ecological and agronomic interest. Sex determination in these insects follows a haplodiploid system, in which males are haploid and females are diploid [20]. N. vitripennis spermatogenesis has been extensively characterized by microscopy [21–24]. It occurs within synchronously differentiating germline cysts, in which all germ cells within a cyst progress through the same developmental stage. This process follows a single developmental wave, resulting in a highly coordinated progression of all cysts through spermatogenesis and revealing a unique feature: a single wave of cystic spermatogenesis. This property provides a distinct advantage for identifying proteins involved in the critical stages of germ cell differentiation during pupal development. At the molecular level, transcriptomic sequencing analyses of the testis, have allowed the identification of sex-biased genes [25, 26], as well as genes uniquely/specifically expressed in the testis [14, 27].

In this study, we aim to enhance our understanding of the spermatogenesis process in the parasitoid wasp Nasonia vitripennis by integrating an analysis of proteomic traits associated with spermatogenesis and conducting a comprehensive examination of the testis proteome by shotgun proteogenomics. Proteogenomics for non-model species is the combined use of genomics and proteomics to identify, validate and prioritize proteins synthesized in different conditions, thereby linking gene sequences to functional proteins in real-world ecological contexts [28, 29]. Tandem mass spectrometry on proteomes, assisted by a Nasonia specific transcriptomic database, allowed the identification and quantitation of proteins. Protein specific to the testis was obtained with the proteome comparison to somatic tissues (head and legs). The proteins regulated during the four pupal stages were monitored, thus across the germ cell development. In a second stage, we investigated the putative 3D structure and functions of orphan proteins among the modulated testis proteins, aiming to uncover novel candidates potentially involved in testis differentiation and spermatogenesis. Our analysis offers insights into protein modulation during spermatogenesis with identification of putative new key molecular players in Hymenoptera.

Material & methods

Samples

For each developmental stage (white pupal stage: WP, red-eyed white pupal stage: REP, bicolor pupal stage: BiP, black pupal stage: BP), tissues were collected from pools of 64 wasps. Each pool comprised 64 heads, 128 testes and 384 legs dissected from the same individuals and pooled by tissue. Three independent biological replicates (64 individuals per replicate) were generated per stage, resulting in a total of 768 wasps analyzed. Predominant spermatogenetic cell types (see [22, 30]]) correspond to primary spermatocytes at the WP stage and secondary spermatocytes at the REP stage, while later stages (BiP and BP) are characterized by more advanced stages of spermatogenesis, including spermatids and differentiating spermatozoa (Fig. 1).

Protein extraction

The frozen tissues were crushed in liquid nitrogen and proteins were dissolved with 100µL of a PBS ice-cold lysis buffer containing SDS 0.1%, and a mix of protease inhibitors (Sigma Aldrich #P8849). Protein concentration was assessed using the Bradford method. A total of 15 µg of each sample were loaded into a 10-well SDS-PAGE gel (4—10% polyacrylamide). The electrophoretic migration was conducted for 5 min at 200 V, using MOPS/SDS running buffer. Each whole-cell proteome sample, which appeared as a single band, was stained with SimplyBlue™ SafeStain (Thermo Fisher LC6065), and then cut for further trypsin digestion in presence of ProteaseMax surfactant (Promega) to release peptides as previously described [31]. The resulting peptides were characterized by tandem mass spectrometry.

NanoLC-MS/MS analysis

A volume of 5 µL of each peptide sample was analyzed with a Q-Exactive HF tandem mass spectrometer (Thermofisher) coupled with a PepMap™ C18 trapping column (5 µm, 100 Å, 300 µm × 5 mm) and a column PepMap™ C18, (3 μm, 0.075 mm × 500 mm) purchased from Thermofisher. The 90 mn gradient was performed with elution buffers A (0.1% formic acid in H2O) and B (80% Acetonitrile 0.1% formic acid in H2O). The mass spectrometer was operated in data dependent acquisition mode with parameters essentially as previously described [32].

Interpretation of MS/MS spectra

MS/MS spectra generated were assigned with the Mascot search engine that searches for the corresponding mass to the peptides in the protein database from the Nasonia vitripennis reference genome GCF_000002325.3 available at https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/refseq/annotation_euk/Nasonia_vitripennis/102/. This corresponds to the protein database we named MS19—027_Nasonia_Protein.FASTA in PRIDE (PXD073770) (24,846 sequences). Searches were performed with full-trypsin specificity including a maximum of two miss-cleavages and considering a mass tolerance of 5 ppm on the ion parent and 0.02 Da on the MS/MS spectra. Proteins were identified with at least two non-ambiguous peptides validated with a p-value of 0.05, resulting in a protein identification FDR of 0.01 as evaluated with the reverse decoy MASCOT option. Searches took into account possible chemical modifications including carboxyamidomethylated cysteine (+ 57.0215) as static modification and oxidized methionine (+ 15.9949) and deamidation of asparagine and glutamine (+ 0.9848) as dynamic modifications.

The numbers of peptide-to-spectrum assignations observed for the different detected peptides of the same polypeptide were summed providing spectral count (SC) values for each replicate and condition.

Differential proteomics

The modulation between development stages and tissue was analyzed as previously recommended [33] using the PatternLab strategy relying on T-fold (Fold change calculated with the ratio SC sample versusSC reference and p value calculated with a t-test. Among the whole proteins, only proteins that were significantly downregulated (T-fold < −1) or proteins up regulated (T-Fold > 1) and p value below 0.05 were selected for further analyses using multivariate statistics in R, with the associate work package MixOmics. PLS-DA was used to demonstrate that protein abundance discriminated between tissue and development stages. Normalized spectral abundance factor (NSAF) was used to classify proteins by their relative abundance considering the total protein spectral counts analyzed in experiments and corrected by their molecular weight. GO enrichment and pathway analysis was performed with the DIAMONDS alignment tool. PCA-biplot was performed with the R packages “FactoMineR” and “factoextra”.

3D structure similarity searches

3D Structure databases

We considered the Protein Data Bank (PDB) database containing experimental 3D structures of proteins (RCSB-PDB, https://www.rcsb.org/, ([34], with summaries included in PDBsum (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/thornton-srv/databases/pdbsum/ [35]). In addition, we also considered the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (AFDB, last update AlphaFold DB version 2022—11—01, created with the AlphaFold Monomer v2.0 pipeline)), which gives the scientific community access to the AlphaFold2 (AF2) predictions [36–38] for a broad part of the UniProt database, i.e. over 200 million of protein 3D structures models. Interestingly, AF2 predictions provide a per amino acid measure of local confidence, called pLDDT (predicted local distance difference test [37–39]). Average pLDDT values were calculated over the whole length of the proteins or over the length of protein segments when necessary. Average pLDDT values ≥ 70 reflect confident predictions, whereas values < 70 are considered less reliable.

Foldseek search (FSS)

The 3D structures extracted from AFDB were searched for structural similarities against the whole set of experimental and predicted 3D structures using the Foldseek Search tool (FSS, https://search.foldseek.com/search) [40, 41]. Foldseek aligns 3D structures by considering a structural alphabet to describe spatial amino acid interactions. It was used here to identify putative distant homologs in various species, thereby increasing the sensitivity compared to sequence similarity search.

ChimeraX for 3D structure visualization

3D structure visualization and analysis were carried out using UCSF ChimeraX Version 1.8 (https://www.rbvi.ucsf.edu/chimerax) [43]. Root mean square deviations (RMSD) were calculated based on superimposition of C-alpha (Ca) atoms.

BLASTP analysis

BLASTP searches were run on NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) with default parameters. Searches were performed against the non-redundant (nr) protein sequence databases, by combining all organisms or excluding/targeting species/clades to increase statistical robustness. If necessary, reciprocal BLASTP analyses were performed.

Phylogeny

The phylogenetic trees from NCBI protein sequences were performed with http://www.phylogeny.fr/alacarte.cgi (MUSCLE for Multiple Alignment; Gblocks for Alignment curation; construction of phylogenetic tree with PhyML; visualisation of phylogenetic tree with TreeDyn). Phylogenetic reconstruction was performed using the maximum likelihood method implemented in PhyML via the Phylogeny.fr platform. The LG amino acid substitution model was used to estimate substitution rates between amino acids based on observed protein data, with among-site rate heterogeneity modeled using a gamma distribution (+ G) and a proportion of invariant sites (+ I). The bootstrap values were estimated with 1000 replications and the trees were rooted using midpoint rooting method.

Results

Strategy to establish the organ proteomes

We applied a global proteomics strategy to characterize organ-specific and developmental proteome dynamics across four pupal stages: white pupa (WP), red-eyed white pupa (REP), intermediary white pupa with black head (Bicolored Pupa, BiP), and black pupa (BP) (Fig. 1A). Legs, heads, and testes were analyzed in triplicate at four successive stages (WP, REP, BiP, BP), yielding 36 samples analyzed by NanoLC–ESI–MS/MS. The whole dataset acquired for the 36 samples comprises 2,076,904 MS/MS spectra. A total of 1,333,855 Peptide-to-Spectrum Matches (PSMs) were assigned. They allowed the identification of 46,441 peptide sequences and a total of 3,130 confident proteins (suppl Table S1). Downstream analyses combined tissue-resolved expression profiling and stage-dependent differential expression to investigate proteome remodeling during development, supported by clustering, functional annotation, and comparative analyses.

The protein catalogues were clustered using hierarchical clustering (Fig. 1B). Replicates grouped consistently by tissue, confirming both the reproducibility of the analysis and tissue-specificity. In testes, early stages (WP, REP) are clearly separated from later stages (BiP, BP), whereas in heads and legs divergence only appears after the WP stage. At the developmental level, BP forms the most distinct cluster, well separated from the others. The method effectively distinguished developmental stages, with the dendrogram reflecting developmental chronology globally and according to the tissue.

Of the 3,130 confidently identified proteins, 2,529 were considered present in at least one tissue as they exhibited a spectral count strictly greater than 2 in at least one of the 36 samples analyzed, independently of tissue type or developmental stage. Across the cohort, the number of proteins detected per sample varied according to tissue type, ranging from approximately 1,100 to 2,050 proteins. Head samples showed the highest proteome coverage, with an average of ~ 1,770 proteins detected per sample, followed by testis samples with an average of ~ 1,450 proteins, while leg samples displayed a lower but consistent coverage, averaging ~ 1,330 proteins per sample. Within each tissue, proteome depth was highly comparable across pupal stages (WP, REP, BiP and BP) and across the three biological replicates. Although no analytical replicates were included, this consistency indicates a robust and reproducible proteomic workflow across tissues and developmental stages.

Among the 2,529 detected proteins, 1,754 were present in all three tissues (independently of the stage), while the remainders were either tissue-specific or shared between two tissues (Fig. 2A; suppl Table S2). Of these, 204 proteins were specific to the testis, 184 to the head, and 11 to legs. Among the proteins uniquely present in testis, several are well known to play critical roles in spermatogenesis as TSSK3 (XP_008216122), Courtless (NP_001154931.1), and tubuline glycylase 3 A (XP_001601458.2), which were detected despite very low relative abundance, as reflected by their %NSAF values in the 10⁻5 range, highlighting the depth and sensitivity of the proteomic analysis. Within the whole set of 2,116 proteins detected in testis, 599 (including the 204 testis-specific) were relatively more abundant in this tissue than in the two others (i.e., the sum of their abundance across testes samples exceeded that in each of the other tissues). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of this subset highlighted 'cilium' as the most overrepresented cellular component term, consistent with the central role of spermatogenesis in testis function (Fig. 2B). A similar result was obtained with the testis-specific subset. Interestingly, we noticed that approximately 10% of all identified proteins were annotated as uncharacterized, highlighting the lack of current knowledge for functional annotation of insect proteomes in general. This proportion increased to 26% among testis-specific proteins (χ2(1) = 44.87, p < 0.0001), pointing to a potential reservoir of novel proteins with testis-specific roles that would require functional studies.

Developmental modulation of testicular proteins

Clustering of proteins based on testicular abundance kinetics

To identify molecular signatures of testis differentiation, proteins were grouped according to the dynamics of their relative abundance between stages, yielding four clusters: three of which (38 proteins) displayed distinct profiles (the fourth cluster gathering the other proteins, without any specific/notable dynamic global profile) (Fig. 3). These three clusters correspond to sequential stages of testis development, from early remodeling to late spermiogenesis. Notably, 10 of these 38 proteins remain uncharacterized and without any GO annotation.

Protein clustering during testis development in Nasonia vitripennis. A Proteins were grouped into four clusters based on their developmental abundance profiles, three of which displayed distinct and biologically relevant patterns: cluster 2, abundant in REP (15 proteins), cluster 1, abundant in BiP (16 proteins), cluster 3 increased between BiP and BP (7 proteins). B Their significantly enriched GO terms (p < 0.01)

Cluster 2 (15 proteins) is described before CL1 as it corresponds to the early stages. Its abundance reached maximum at the REP stage and declined thereafter (Fig. 3A). This profile indicates that these proteins initiate the structural remodeling required for subsequent differentiation. The most enriched GO term (Fig. 3B) was “cilium” (× 9), alongside “microtubule organization” and “chromosome segregation” (× 4.5), highlighting cytoskeletal and meiotic processes. Proteins specifically activated at REN include dyneins (XP_008208864.1; XP_008206675.1), coinciding with the onset of filament rearrangement and microtubule elongation, and plectin (XP_008211049.1), which maintains microtubule integrity. Nuclear reorganization also begins at this stage, supported by centrosome-associated proteins XP_008203946.1 and XP_003427104.1, and XP_016840937.1, predicted to be homologous to a hydrocephalus-inducing protein involved in axonemal assembly. Altogether, Cluster 2 proteins set the stage for nuclear shaping and tail precursor formation, paving the way for the BiN-associated events captured in Cluster 1.

Cluster 1 (16 proteins) showed increasing abundance later from WP to BiP, peaking at BiP before declining at BP (Fig. 3A). This pattern suggests a central role in mid-stages of testis differentiation. GO enrichment revealed “GTPase activity,” “supramolecular fiber,” and “cytoskeleton” (enrichment × 9, × 6, and × 4, respectively) (Fig. 3B). Proteins in this cluster include cytoskeletal regulators such as tubulins and tilB homolog (dynein assembly), the mitochondrial ES1 homolog, and modulators of DNA/RNA/protein synthesis. Two histone phosphorylation proteins, XP_003425592.1 (predicted SH3 domain–containing kinase-binding protein 1-like isoform X1) and XP_001604497.1 (predicted NCK-interacting protein with SH3 domain isoform X2), peaked at BiP and BP, respectively, consistent with chromatin condensation and microtubule enrichment observed at the BiP stage. This stage marks the onset of spermiogenesis, when nuclei become formed and elongated microtubules start filling the sperm tails.

Cluster 3 (7 proteins) appeared later, with abundance steadily increasing until BP, pointing to a role in the terminal phases of testis differentiation (Fig. 3A). GO enrichment highlighted “cell killing” (× 23), a process often linked to late spermatogenic remodeling (Fig. 3B). This cluster can be divided into three groups: (i) fatty acid synthases XP_008207677.2 and XP_008207459.2, known markers of sperm quality; (ii) mitochondrial regulators XP_008207245.1 (MICOS subunit Mic60 isoform X1) and NP_001165992.1 (cytochrome P450 4G43), essential for oxidative phosphorylation and sperm metabolism; (iii) metabolic and structural proteins such as XP_001604902.1 (purine nucleoside phosphorylase-like, meiosis) and NP_001166087.1 (serine protease 70 precursor, spermiogenesis). Both enzymes are directly implicated in chromatin remodeling and sperm morphology, with potential consequences for fertility if impaired. Interestingly, a predicted toll-like receptor 3 protein was also detected with a low abundance (1.6% of the NSAF). Although generally linked to immunity, studies in insects indicate that related receptors are often expressed in the male germline, where their function remains largely unknown and may vary between species [44]. Overall, Cluster 3 reflects the final maturation of spermatozoa, including mitochondrial activation, metabolic tuning, and structural shaping of the gamete.

Proteome dynamics along the pupal stages

We analyzed the dynamics of the testicular proteome by analyzing the successive stage transitions. This approach improves the resolution of intermediary phases. Indeed, this complementary approach allowed the identification of 175 proteins showing at least a twofold change, thereby capturing stage-specific molecular shifts in testis differentiation (Fig. 4, suppl Table S3). Because spermatogenesis in N. vitripennis is synchronized, PCA of intracellular localization, biological processes, and molecular functions provided a chronological overview of cellular events during testis development. The 175 modulated proteins were grouped to allow to track molecular dynamics across stages (Fig. S1 and suppl. Table S4). The most modulated proteins shifted progressively from categories related to chromosomes and nuclei in early stages to the plasma membrane, cytoplasm, and finally mitochondria as differentiation advanced (suppl Fig. 1). The associated biological processes followed the same temporal pattern, beginning with chromosome organization and morphogenesis, then transitioning to protein assembly, catabolic activity, and ultimately cell maintenance (Fig. 5). This temporal progression in protein function is consistent with the abundance-level transitions revealed by PCA. The 175 proteins were distributed across the three stage transitions as described in Fig. 6: 51 between WP and REP, 50 between REP and BiP, and 74 between BiP and BP (see also suppl. Table S3). GO enrichment showed the highest number of terms in the REP-to-BiP transition, notably 'cilium' (4.3-fold) and 'chromosome' (3.1-fold), consistent with dendrogram analysis and marking this transition as a critical phase of testis reorganization. Among the 175 proteins modulated during testis development in N. vitripennis, 128 (73%) have at least one ortholog in Drosophila (171 orthologs in total) (suppl Table 3 columns N to BA). Of these, 124 Drosophila orthologs (72%) also have at least one ortholog in Ciona, highlighting the deep evolutionary conservation of testis-related proteins (suppl Table S5). Regarding functional annotation, a subset of 11 uncharacterized proteins lacked GO annotation, or any other information in UniProt (suppl Table S3, columns I and J; Table 1 and paragraph below).

Functional prediction of uncharacterized proteins modulated between two pupal stages in Nasonia vitripennis testis

We thus focused on uncharacterized proteins not associated with any GO terms (Table 1). Given the lack of functional information for these proteins, we searched for predicted 3D structures in the AFDB (Table 1): 1/No structural prediction was available for XP_031781367; 2/Three proteins (XP_008203399, XP_008209339, XP_008215448) displayed low-confidence models (i.e., pLDDT < 70). 3/Among the seven having confident predictions (pLDDT ≥ 70), four (XP_016841576.1, XP_016836869.1, XP_016845589.1, XP_001604384.1) shared comparable lengths (between 408 and 424 AA) and obvious structural similarities (Fig. 7A), suggesting they may form a protein family. The remaining three proteins with confident predictions showed no obvious structural relationship (not shown) and are not described in the present paper. In terms of molecular abundance, XP_001604384.1 and XP_016836869.1 are the most highly represented proteins within this group, with higher spectral counts (1049 and 524 spectra, respectively) and correspondingly elevated %NSAF values (1.4 × 10⁻3 and 7.2 × 10⁻4). However, these %NSAF values indicate that they remain of relatively low abundance at the scale of the whole proteome. In contrast, XP_016845589.1 and XP_016841576.1 exhibit lower abundance both within the group and globally, as reflected by their lower spectral counts (133 and 91 spectra) and lower %NSAF values in the 10⁻4 range. Concerning their 3D structure, the four uncharacterized proteins are globular with a dense and spherical folding containing an alternance of β-sheets linked by disordered loops with low local pLDDT values (as shown by orange/yellow colors in AF2 3D structures, Fig. 7A). Also, their very proximal N-terminal part (AA 1 to 19/27) is unconfident, local average pLDDT being < 60. These N-terminal parts being predicted as signal peptides (suppl Fig. S2), this suggests that the proteins may be secreted or attached to the membrane. The four 3D predicted structures superimposed with low RMSD two by two (Fig. 7B): between 1.03 and 1.3 Å. We noticed that three of the proteins, AF-A0A7M7J0H0-F1, AF-A0A7M7IP25-F1 and AF-A0A7M7QMN3-F1, showed a greater degree of superimposition with one another than with the fourth protein, AF-A0A7M7G992-F1 (as explained in legend in Fig. 8B). This latter exhibits a supplemental disordered linker that likely decreases the matches with the three others (red arrows in Fig. 7A/B). Using FSS to search similarities in the PDB database of experimental 3D structures, we identified structural analogs for each of the four uncharacterized proteins (raw results in suppl Table S6A-D: one tab per interrogation). We then applied the sorting procedure based on parameter relevance and significance, as described in Material and Methods, before searching candidates common to the four searches. This analysis highlighted a single common target (and two of its mutants) (Table 2): the PDB 7fh6-assembly1_A (UniProt Id A0A1Y0K711) corresponding to the Friedel–Crafts alkylation enzyme, CylK, previously described in the cyanobacteria Cylindrospermum licheniforme [45]. The best FSS match was obtained with AF-A0A7M7QMN3-F1 (TM = 0.58; e-value = 7.85e-6) and concerns 89% of the whole length of the uncharacterized protein and 57% of the whole length of CylK (see query/target positions: columns H&I in suppl Table S6 A-D). Results are very similar for the three other uncharacterized proteins but with lower matches (Table 2). CylK is a globular protein of 676 AA composed of two domains (suppl Fig. S3 and [45]): an N-terminal Ca2+- binding domain and a C-terminal β-propeller domain that consists of six 4-stranded β-sheet motifs (blades) radially arranged (wheel-shaped symmetry). This latter (from AA 252 to 635) superimposed with the uncharacterized protein AF-A0A7M7QMN3-F1 (from AA 40 to the end) with a RMSD of 1.27 Å (Fig. 8; suppl Fig. S4). As the two domains of CylK are necessary for its enzymatic activity (see discussion), it is unlikely that the four N. vitripennis proteins exhibit this enzymatic activity. We thus expanded our search using FSS interrogating the AFDB50 database of predicted 3D structures. For each of the four uncharacterized proteins, one thousand matches were obtained. By retaining the most significant hits according to the FSS threshold, (see M&M) corresponding to proteins (between 89 and 153 proteins) with a name and functional annotation (suppl Table 7), we identified 10 different protein families that were common to the four searches (suppl Table S7). Each family included at least two representative proteins identified in different prokaryotic species (mainly bacteria and cyanobacteria), while none were detected in eukaryotes. For each of the ten families and each of the four searches, we selected the result with (suppl Table S8): the highest E-value, TM > 0.5 and the target with pLDDT ≥ 70 (suppl Fig. S5A-I). All ten protein families exhibit the six-bladed β-propeller domain. Notably, only one family, exo-α-sialidase family (Fig. 9), had members consisting of a single β-propeller domain, whereas the other proteins were larger with the domain linked to one or several unrelated domains (suppl Fig. S5). Importantly, “sialidase/neuraminidase” appeared in PDB100 results (~ 30 times in Suppl. Table 6A-D) for the four proteins, whereas the other nine families are absent in these results, supporting AFDB results. All together these results suggest that the four uncharacterized proteins 1/share a similar 3D structure; 2/this structure corresponds to a globular six-bladed β-propeller domain like the CylK protein from Cylindrospermum licheniforme; 3/3D similarity searches further revealed similarity with several predicted prokaryotic proteins carrying six-bladed β-propeller domain like. Interestingly, in most of these predicted structures the domain β-propeller is linked to additional modules, except in most exo-α-sialidases identified.

Concerning the phylogeny of the four uncharacterized proteins, our observations suggest that they likely belong to a family with multiple paralogs and orthologs that appear restricted to Hymenoptera. Indeed, in each of the four FSS interrogations of the AFDB50 database performed above, there were two Hymenoptera species (no other insects) other than N. vitripennis found with significant matches: Trichomalopsis sarcophagae and Trichogramma brassicae (suppl Table S9). This was confirmed by NCBI BLASTP with each of the four protein sequences: when restricted to Hymenoptera other than Nasonia, we found 50 homologous proteins (all being hypothetical or uncharacterized proteins) dispatched in four species of parasitoid bees including the two above (suppl Table S10). In contrary, NCBI BLASTP restricted to Drosophila melanogaster or Bombyx mori or Tribolium casteneum did not find any homolog for any of the four proteins. Interestingly, the FSS/AFDB50 investigation pointed out significant matches with 14 confident targets in Nasonia (suppl Table S9), suggesting that the family is larger than the 4 proteins of the present study; the result was confirmed by NCBI BLASTP (suppl Table S11), with even more (21) homologs (putative paralogs, see their phylogenetic tree Fig. 10). The four Hymenoptera species exhibit many paralogs like N. vitripennis, and phylogeny analysis suggested duplication occurring after speciation (suppl Fig. S6). Finally, we have identified in N. vitripennis testis four orphan proteins sharing a common three-dimensional fold, characterized by a six-bladed β-propeller domain. According to current sequence databases, these proteins appear to be restricted to a limited number of Hymenoptera species, supporting their classification as orphan proteins. Although six-bladed β-propeller domain-containing proteins constitute a large and functionally diverse protein family, structural similarity analyses suggest a potential relationship between these orphan proteins and exo-sialidases. Discussion

In this study, we provide the first proteome-wide characterization of tissue-specific and developmental protein dynamics during Nasonia vitripennis development. Across heads, legs, and testes at four successive pupal stages, we identified 3,130 proteins present, including a large ubiquitous core but also clear signatures of tissue- and stage-specific expression. One of our aims was to focus on testis differentiation, and the originality of our study is based on two singular points corresponding to lacunar data in the literature: 1/N. vitripennis is a model for synchronized testis (one differentiation stage at one time), 2/the comparison with two somatic organs: legs and heads. Moreover, to our knowledge, no data are available on the proteome of synchronized testes. In Apis mellifera, studies have investigated various reproductive organs or cells (sperm, accessory glands, seminal vesicles, seminal fluid [46–49]), but not the testis itself. Likewise, in other vertebrate species with synchronized testis, i.e. fish models such as zebrafish, salmon and rainbow trout, proteomic analyses have focused on adult testes or spermatozoa [50], with no studies on larval stages.

According to our protein profiles, testes displayed the earliest divergence from other tissues, with abundance patterns separating early from late pupal stages, consistent with the progressive onset of spermatogenesis. Clustering of abundance developmental profiles further resolved a tightly orchestrated sequence of molecular events underlying spermatogenesis sequential molecular programs. We identified three clusters corresponding to early, mid, and late phases of testis differentiation, highlighting the sequential activation of cytoskeletal remodeling, nuclear organization, mitochondrial and metabolic regulation, and chromatin modification. These differential analyses across consecutive stages confirmed that the REP-to-BiP transition represents a critical inflection point of testicular reorganization, marked by enrichment of ciliary and chromosomal functions. They are consistent with histological and cytological studies showing that spermatogenesis in N. vitripennis is highly synchronized, completed before male emergence, and follows an abortive meiotic program typical of Hymenoptera [21, 22]. Our proteomic data therefore provide a molecular complement to these observations, by highlighting candidate proteins likely involved in these coordinated processes.

Focus on known proteins with testicular function

Among the proteins uniquely expressed in testis, several are key regulators of spermatogenesis. For instance, TSSK3 (testis-specific serine/threonine protein kinase 3; XP_008216122), a conserved protein essential for male fertility in mouse [51] and the unbiquitin-conjugating enzyme courtless or crl (NP_001154931.1) whose loss disrupts meiosis and sperm formation in Drosophila [52] are both present in our dataset. We also detected TTLL3B, a tubulin glycylase (XP_001601458.2) required for axoneme stability and male fertility [53, 54], previously identified as testis-specific in N. vitripennis [26]. Phylogenetic analysis using Biomart shows that most proteins modulated during cyst-synchronized spermatogenesis in Nasonia are conserved in Drosophila and, to a lesser extent, in Ciona, while a subset of orphan proteins lacking GO annotation points to potential Hymenoptera-specific innovations. Consistent with our previous work showing that 81% of genes important for testicular function in Drosophila are conserved in vertebrates [15], these findings are further supported by stage-specific proteomic shifts in cyst-synchronized Drosophila testes [18]. Together, our results highlight both the evolutionary conservation of key testis proteins and lineage-specific diversification, linking molecular dynamics to functional stages of germ cell differentiation.

Identification of an orphan family regulated during pupal differentiation testis

We aimed to focus on developmentally regulated proteins lacking annotation in NCBI, UniProt, and Ensembl databases. Orphan genes with no obvious phylogenetic link (annotated as « uncharacterized» in data banks like NCBI) are restricted to a single species or within a taxonomical group [55]. They have important roles in phenotypic and functional evolution by diversifying biological structures and processes (for review [56]). To decipher orphan proteins from Bacteria to human, scientific world develops large methodologic efforts [57], thanks to artificial intelligence based tools that have been revolutionizing 3D structure protein predictions (in human: [55], in Drosophila [58]). Thus, orphan proteins are challenging to study, yet these genes can play essential roles in reproduction, as recently shown for several Drosophila testis genes—Atlas [59], Goddard [60], katherine johnson (kj) [61]. To reach our aim, we relied on recent AFDB/FSS tools to predict their 3D structures. As shown in Drosophila melanogaster [40, 62, 63], AlphaFold2 can reveal novel protein folds still absent in the PDB, making it a valuable approach for our study. However, many orphan proteins lacked reliable 3D models (pLDDT < 70; in our work, it was the case for some of uncharacterized proteins, Table 1), a limitation also noted for de novo testis proteins in Drosophila [64]. This likely reflects both their intrinsically disordered nature, as well as lack of knowledge due to lacunar information in databases and current algorithm limits due to insufficient alignments [38, 65]. FSS is a rapid and easy-to-use method, effective even for distant or partial structural similarities [41], and AFDB50 database interrogation usefully completes PDB100. Even if robust, models require experimental validation, since AF2 does not account for dynamic interactions, multimeric states or disulfide bounds [39, 66], though AF3 is expected to address these limitations [67].

Among the set of 11 testicular developmental proteins in N. vitripennis pupae that are uncharacterized, we focused on four orphans with similar (robust) 3D predicted structures with length between 410 and 420 AA. After structural superimposition, we searched for similarities using FSS. In PDB100, a significant hit corresponded to a six-bladed β-propeller domain, exemplified by one domain of the Friedel–Crafts alkylation enzyme (CylK) involved in cyanobacterial biosynthesis [45, 68, 69]. Cylk ensures Friedel–Crafts alkylation (attaching alkyl groups to aromatic rings).As said in the results, it is unlikely that the four N. vitripennis orphans exhibit the same enzymatic activity as Cylk: they lack the second domain of CylK (Ca-binding site) necessary for its enzymatic activity, as shown by site mutagenesis experiments by Braffman et al. [45]. Six-β propeller domain is a fold also found in various other proteins, forming a large family of β propeller domain-containing proteins with various functions such as structural components, receptor modules, actors of signaling pathways, as well as enzymes [70–72]. The diversity of functions associated with six bladed β-propeller fold is also well documented in the SCOP database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/pdbe/scop/search?t=txt;q=propeller).

Our FSS searches in AFDB50 are coherent with these data, showing that the six-bladed β-propeller fold is broadly distributed, with 10 functional families found common to the four orphans (suppl Fig. S5A-I). Among these, exo-α-sialidases (sialidases SCOP ID: 3,001,607) emerged as the best match, based on size (with a single β-propeller domain) and the frequent occurrence of “sialidase/neuraminidase” in PDB100 results (~ 30 times vs. no occurence for other families; Suppl. Table 6A-D). Previous works have investigated the relationship structure-enzymatic activity of various sialidases [73–76]. Beside three Arg distributed throughout the sequence interacting with the carboxylate group of sialic acid [76, 77], a nucleophilic tyrosine forming a covalent sialosyl–enzyme intermediate and an acid/base Asp/Glu pair are involved in enzymatic function [78, 79]. We looked at the position of these AA in the hypothetical catalytic site, but their distribution along the cavity (not shown) makes any analysis speculative. The protein sequence alignment was not informative because of the low level of Id (9—10%). These lacks of evidence however do not exclude an exo-sialidase-like activity of the four orphan proteins, as the previous work of Hsciao et al. [80] demonstrated that the conformation and shape of neuraminidase active sites may differ significantly from one neuraminidase to another, even though the enzymes having a similar fold. Nevertheless, the hypothesis of exo-sialidase-like activity is in high coherence with our physiological context of developmental testis, sialylation being important for spermatogenesis as described in the paragraph below. However, this hypothesis needs to be verified with further studies.

Sialidases are found in species as distant as bacteria and vertebrates. In bacteria, sialidases are involved in interaction and attachment with the host cell, thus promoting pathogenicity [81]; they are promising targets for the development of new antimicrobial treatments [82]. In vertebrates, sialylation, the covalent addition of sialic acids to glycoproteins, glycolipids or polysaccharides, and its reversal by sialidases, plays a central role in glycan turnover [83]. These modifications, like other post-translational events, are crucial for neurodevelopment, cell reprogramming, embryogenesis, oncogenesis, and immune responses (for review [84]). Mammalian sialidases, including human enzymes, share the six-bladed β-propeller fold typical of microbial counterparts [83] and their tightly regulated activity varies across cell types and developmental stages. In human, defects in exo-α-sialidases are linked to severe pathologies such as sialidosis and galactosialidosis [85]. In insects, sialylation has been suggested to occur [86]. In Drosophila melanogaster, enzymes such as CMP-Sia synthase are present [87], with sialic acids present only during early development and under tissue- and stage-specific regulations [88, 89]. Elements of this pathway have also been reported in several insect species including sialidases in Bombyx mori [90], sialidase activity in Triatoma infestans [91] and sialic acids in adult Aedes aegypti [92] and Galleria mellonella [93]. Interestingly, the detection of exo-α-sialidase-like proteins in N. vitripennis pupae, across head, leg, and testis, is coherent with the extensive cell remodeling and tight regulation of this pathway during pupal development.

Interestingly, we identified two sialic acid transporters (sialin-isoform X2, testis specific XP_001608) and XP_031783207.1 in our testicular proteome (suppl Table S2). Even if they are not modulated during development, their presence suggests the functionality of a sialic acid pathway. In humans, sialin mRNA is detected in early spermatids protein atlas (Human protein atlas—https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000119899-SLC17A5), suggesting a conserved role. Altogether, these data support the existence of an active sialylation/desialylation process in N. vitripennis testes during pupal development. In vertebrates, such glycan modifications—often described as the sperm sialome—are crucial for sperm maturation, capacitation, survival in the female tract, and oocyte binding [94] (for reviews: [95, 96]). Of course, at this stage of our study, the cellular and physiological functions of N. vitripennis sialidases-like remain unclear and need to be explored and validated with further works (including functional genomic [97]). In a first step, it will be informative to determine their precise cellular origin (germinal versus somatic) and stage-specific expression. Investigations in somatic tissues, head and legs, will also be interesting as the modulation of these sialidases throughout larval development suggests a finely regulated role in their differentiation. This study therefore provides a first framework for candidate selection and establishes a basis for future targeted experimental validation of key testis-specific proteins identified in our proteomic dataset.
