Implementing continuous glucose monitoring in public primary care for type 2 diabetes management: a convergent mixed-methods study of patient and provider experiences in Singapore
Authors: Shilpa Tyagi, Keith Sng, David Wei Liang Ng, Valerie Hui Ying Teo, Si Min Thio, Jeremy Cong En He, Chun Yen Beh, Ming Hann Cheah, Nooradlin Marina Binti Mohammad Junaidi See Toh, Jiawei Chen, Gerald Choon-Huat Koh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundType 2 diabetes (T2D) is highly prevalent in Singapore, yet glycemic control remains suboptimal in primary care. Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) offers real-time feedback that may improve self-management, but adoption in Asian primary care settings is limited by cost and system integration challenges. This study evaluated the feasibility, acceptability, and perceived impact of CGM implementation in Singapore’s public primary care clinics using a convergent mixed-methods design.MethodsA CGM pilot program was conducted across five public polyclinics. We studied a purposive sample of 12 adults with T2D and 10 primary care providers. Patients used CGM sensors for two cycles over a three- to six-month period. Quantitative surveys assessed usability, satisfaction, and perceived outcomes. Semi-structured interviews with patients and providers explored user experiences and implementation barriers. Data were analysed independently and then integrated for a comprehensive assessment.ResultsAmong the study participants, over 80% of patients found the CGM device easy to use, comfortable, and useful in understanding daily glucose fluctuations. Real-time feedback prompted immediate behaviour changes, 83% reported healthier diets and more frequent glucose monitoring, and 67% felt more confident managing diabetes. Providers unanimously reported that CGM improved clinical decision-making and enhanced patient engagement, enabling more timely and tailored treatment adjustments. Key barriers identified included cost (only 58% of patients were willing to pay out-of-pocket; 89% of providers cited funding concerns), added workload for data interpretation and patient education, and difficulties integrating CGM data into existing workflows. Nonetheless, 67% of patients expressed willingness to reuse CGM if affordable, and providers endorsed its clinical value.ConclusionCGM use in Singapore’s public primary care was feasible and acceptable, supporting individualized diabetes care. Broader adoption will require addressing cost, IT integration, and provider support to realize CGM’s full potential in routine T2D management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-025-03001-x.Keywords: User-experience, Qualitative research, Quantitative research, Diabetes, Continuous glucose monitoring, Telemonitoring

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) affects approximately 8.5% of adults in Singapore [1], above the global average and remains a major public health challenge with rising complication rates [2, 3]. A recent study of over 220,000 patients (2013–2020) reported rising rates of both macrovascular and microvascular complications in diabetes, including heart disease, stroke, and diabetes-related eye, kidney, and nerve conditions [4]. By 2050, projections estimate sharp increases in annual cases of myocardial infarction from 9,300 to 16,400, stroke from 7,300 to 12,800, and end-stage renal disease from 1,700 to 2,700 [5]. Glycemic control is essential but often difficult to achieve, especially in primary care where most T2D patients are managed [6–8]. Traditional monitoring methods like self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) and HbA1c testing have limitations, offering only intermittent or averaged data that may miss daily fluctuations [9–12]. Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) provides real-time data, trend indicators, and metrics like Time in Range (TIR), allowing for more responsive and informed self-management [13, 14]. Clinical trials have shown CGM can improve HbA1c and self-care behaviours in T2D, particularly when paired with education [15, 16], while qualitative studies highlight its motivational and educational benefits [14, 17].

Despite these advantages, CGM uptake in T2D remains low, particularly in general practice and in Asia, due to cost, limited provider familiarity, and lack of integration into routine workflows [12, 18–22]. Most CGM research has focused on specialist settings or single stakeholder perspectives, leaving a gap in understanding how CGM is experienced and implemented in primary care. Mixed-methods studies that combine patient and provider perspectives are especially rare but crucial for informing scalable, patient-centred models of care. To address these gaps, we conducted a convergent mixed-methods study to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and user experience of a pilot CGM program in Singapore’s public primary care system. Our study aimed to (1) quantify patient and provider perceptions of CGM use, (2) explore facilitators and barriers to implementation through in-depth interviews, and (3) integrate findings to generate practical insights for wider adoption in primary care.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a concurrent mixed methods design with equal emphasis on quantitative and qualitative components to evaluate the feasibility and user experience of CGM for type 2 diabetes management in primary care [23]. Quantitative (survey) and qualitative (interview) data were collected in parallel and analysed independently, followed by integration during the interpretation phase. The mixed-methods approach was chosen to capture both broad usability metrics and in-depth perspectives, providing a comprehensive understanding of the CGM user experience. The methods are reported in accordance with Good Reporting of A Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS) guidelines [24], and the qualitative strand follows the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [25].

Study setting and participants

The study was conducted across five polyclinics from all three public healthcare clusters in Singapore, capturing a diverse sample of public primary care. In Singapore, polyclinics are government-subsidized primary care centres that deliver a wide spectrum of healthcare services, including treatment for acute and chronic conditions, maternal and child health services, and dental care [26, 27]. These integrated "one-stop" clinics are staffed by multidisciplinary teams comprising doctors, nurses, and allied health professionals, and they also provide onsite access to diagnostic services such as laboratory tests and medical imaging [28]. Their affordability and convenience make them a preferred choice for patients managing chronic illnesses [28]. Nursing staff involved in team-based care in polyclinics are referred as care managers. Care managers provide chronic disease care, counselling, patient education and follow-up in polyclinic setting. For this CGM pilot, care managers took on training and support tasks, for example, applying sensors, educating patients on using the CGM app, and answering queries throughout the program. Some polyclinics also have a non-clinically trained staff member referred to as care coordinator who supports team-based care by reviewing and recommending vaccinations, advising on health screenings, and assisting patients with appointment bookings and care navigation. For this CGM pilot, they provided administrative support to the care managers during the recruitment and onboarding of eligible patients. Participants included (1) adult patients with type 2 diabetes enrolled in a CGM pilot under the Primary Tech-Enhanced Care Diabetes Mellitus (PTEC-DM) program, and (2) healthcare providers involved in diabetes care at these sites (e.g., physicians, care managers, care coordinators). As part of this CGM pilot program led by the Ministry of Health Office for Healthcare Transformation (MOHT) (a national agency driving innovation and care model transformation in Singapore) [29] in collaboration with the three polyclinic clusters in Singapore, patients with type 2 diabetes were enrolled by their primary care teams at selected polyclinics. Eligible patients, deemed clinically suitable by their care team, received two CGM patches free of charge and underwent in-clinic review of their glucose data. Diabetes management was delivered by a multidisciplinary team, at minimum involving a doctor and nurse, with possible support from care coordinators or allied health professionals such as dietitians. While CGM devices were provided at no cost, patients paid standard rates for clinical consultations and services received during the pilot. For the purpose of this PTEC-DM CGM pilot, CGM devices were sponsored and provided by MOHT through the PTEC-DM program while the device vendor’s direct role was limited to initial hands-on or practical staff training. Subsequently, all patient training and device setup were performed by the clinic care team. Eligible participants were those involved in this CGM pilot, English-speaking, and not pregnant (for patients). All participants provided informed consent; interviewees also consented to audio-recording. Participation was voluntary.

Data collection

Data collection for both quantitative and qualitative strands was conducted concurrently during a 3 to 6 month CGM pilot implementation period. To ensure participants had sufficient experience with the CGM device before providing feedback, data collection was timed mid-course of the pilot. Patient surveys and initial interviews were initiated after approximately 1.5 months of the patient using CGM. Provider surveys and interviews were conducted 3 to 4.5 months after the start of CGM pilot. Both quantitative surveys and qualitative interview guides were adapted from existing literature [13, 19, 30].

Each patient participant was invited to complete one survey and one follow-up interview (with an option for a second interview, though none opted for it). Surveys were self-administered via a secure online link sent by text message, with no identifying information collected. After survey completion, participants could access a separate form to review the interview information sheet and indicate interest in an interview. Those who consented provided contact details and were later contacted by the study team to schedule an interview at their convenience. Interviews were conducted either in person at a private location or via secure video conference (Zoom), based on participant preference.

A list of eligible primary care team members at each participating clinic was obtained via MOHT [29]. All identified providers were invited by email to complete an online user experience survey, which included study information. The same group was later invited to participate in a qualitative interview, all of which were conducted via secure videoconferencing (Zoom) for convenience.

For both patients and providers, interviews were in-depth, semi-structured conversations guided by a topic framework developed from literature and expert input, and pilot-tested for clarity (Supplementary File 1). Topics included CGM benefits, usability, barriers, and implementation recommendations. All interviews were conducted in English, lasted 25 to 45 min, and were audio-recorded with consent. Field notes captured contextual details, and no repeat interviews were needed.

Quantitative strand (Survey)

The quantitative component involved self-administered user experience surveys, with separate but analogous versions for patients and providers. The HCP quantitative survey assessed provider perspectives on implementing CGM within the PTEC DM Program, using the i-PARiHS framework to explore four domains: innovation characteristics, context, recipients, and implementation facilitation. Most items were closed-ended (Yes/No), capturing perceived facilitators and barriers, with open-ended questions addressing additional feedback, clinical indications, and experiences with multimodal monitoring (if applicable). One item evaluated the perceived helpfulness of prior CGM training. The patient survey explored CGM user experience across domains of usability, impact, challenges, and acceptability. It included device identification, Likert-scale items on usability and health impact, multiple-choice and scaled questions on physical and practical challenges, and Yes/No items on training, reuse, and willingness to pay for CGM with teleconsultation. Open-ended responses captured qualitative feedback on user experience (Supplementary File 1). Further details of survey questions along with mapping to the listed constructs is provided in Supplementary Table 1 under the Supplementary File 2. Considering the lack of a single instrument measuring the desired constructs for both HCPs and patients, the above quantitative surveys were adapted from existing literature. While extensive validation of the survey instruments was not performed considering the feasibility assessment scope of current pilot project, these survey tools were reviewed by clinical experts and pilot-tested for face validity. Responses were collected anonymously via a secure online platform.

Qualitative strand (Interviews)

For the qualitative strand, all survey participants were invited to consider an interview. Among those who volunteered, we conducted interviews with participants spanning a range of clinics and roles to ensure diverse perspectives for in-depth, semi-structured interviews. One interview per participant was conducted by a team of three female researchers: a senior researcher (ST) with a PhD in health services research and extensive qualitative experience, and two undergraduate researchers (YC and SM). The interviewers had no clinical roles or prior relationships with participants. At the start of each interview, they introduced themselves as independent researchers, clarified their role, and emphasized they were not part of the clinic care team to encourage open sharing.

Interviews were conducted in private settings or via secure video calls, depending on participant preference. Using a flexible interview guide, the team explored CGM experiences, benefits, challenges, provider support, and suggestions for improvement. All interviews were audio-recorded with consent, transcribed verbatim, and anonymized. Field notes were taken to supplement the transcripts; transcripts were not returned to participants due to time constraints.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

Survey data were analysed using descriptive statistics to characterize the sample and summarize user experience metrics. Data from the online survey platform were exported to StataSE 17 for analysis [31]. We calculated frequencies and proportions for categorical responses and means (with standard deviations) for continuous or Likert scale items, as appropriate. No inferential statistical comparisons were planned given the primarily exploratory aim and the modest sample size of this pilot. Results of the quantitative analysis were reviewed to identify prevalent trends or noteworthy observations. These quantitative results were later juxtaposed with qualitative findings during integration to contextualize the numbers with narrative insights.

Qualitative analysis

Qualitative data were analysed using a thematic analysis approach, following the Braun and Clarke six-phase framework (familiarization, coding, theme development, review, definition, and write-up) [32]. The analysis was primarily inductive, allowing themes to emerge from the data, though the interview guide topics provided an initial organizing structure. The senior researcher (ST) independently read each transcript and performed open coding to label significant phrases or ideas. After coding the first few transcripts, the research team met to compare codes and develop a preliminary coding framework (codebook) that captured the key concepts arising from the data. This codebook was then applied to subsequent transcripts, with flexibility to introduce new codes or refine definitions as needed in an iterative manner (constant comparison was used to continuously compare data segments across different transcripts and participant groups). NVivo qualitative analysis software (QSR International, Version 12) [33] was used to manage the transcripts, codes and memos, complemented by use of Microsoft Word and Excel.

Throughout the analysis, particular attention was given to comparing perspectives between patients and providers and across the different polyclinic sites. We noted that many themes (e.g. perceptions of CGM benefits and challenges) were common to both patients and healthcare providers. Therefore, qualitative findings were largely analysed and reported collectively for all participants, while highlighting any nuances unique to one group where applicable. We also compared narratives from different healthcare clusters to identify any site-specific differences in implementation experiences (using a constant comparative approach across cases). The coding and analysis continued until thematic saturation was achieved, indicated by no new themes or insights emerging from the last few interviews. We determined that saturation had occurred when three consecutive interviews (toward the end of data collection) yielded no substantively new codes or ideas. The final result of the qualitative analysis was a set of major themes and subthemes describing user experiences and implementation barriers/facilitators of CGM, with representative quotations selected to illustrate each theme.

Data integration

Following separate quantitative and qualitative analyses, findings were integrated using a side-by-side comparison and joint display approach to support the mixed-methods design [34, 35]. Quantitative results were compared with qualitative themes to assess convergence or divergence, and findings were discussed in parallel within the text. A joint display table was also created to align key survey results with illustrative participant quotes. This integration enabled a deeper understanding of CGM feasibility and acceptability, with areas of alignment strengthening conclusions and discrepancies offering contextual insights. The process was guided by the GRAMMS framework to ensure coherent mixed-methods interpretation [24].

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the National University of Singapore Institutional Review Board (NUS-IRB-2024–283) and conducted in accordance with local regulations and the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. For surveys, consent was implied by proceeding after reading the online information sheet. Patients interested in interviews provided separate consent to be contacted. Written consent, including for audio-recording, was obtained before each interview. All responses were kept confidential, and survey data were collected and stored in de-identified form. Participants were reimbursed for the interviews only and not surveys.

For the qualitative interviews, 14 HCPs and 8 patients expressed interest and were contacted; all agreed to participate. A total of 13 HCP and 7 patient interviews were completed with a respective response rate of 76% and 32%, with one no-show in each group (Table 1). At data closure, no further participants remained to be interviewed.

User experience survey findings (for patient participants)

Patient-reported challenges

Despite overall positive experiences, patients reported several challenges with CGM use (Table 3). The most common was remembering to log complementary data (58.3%). Physical discomfort was minimal: 8.3% reported skin irritation or pain, 16.7% noted some discomfort, and one patient experienced a sensor-related infection. A minority (16.7%) found the device limiting for activities or were bothered by alerts. Importantly, all patients found the CGM data understandable, most reported little to no interference with daily routines. Two-thirds (66.7%) would use CGM again, while a third were unsure or unwilling, often due to cost concerns, and only 58.3% were willing to pay out of pocket. All received training, and 75% discussed their CGM data with a provider, though a quarter did not recall this interaction, suggesting room to improve follow-up.

Challenges encountered by patient participants

Healthcare provider UE survey

Nine primary care providers completed a questionnaire on the CGM pilot, highlighting key facilitators and barriers (Table 4). All (100%) agreed CGM improved clinical decision-making, personalized therapy, timely interventions, and patient self-management. They also noted better engagement, and more focused clinic visits due to real-time glucose data. Major barriers included cost and lack of financial support (88.9%), time constraints and education workload (66.7%), and difficulty integrating CGM into routine care or IT systems (55.6%). Other issues included sensor failures (55.6%), download inefficiencies (44.4%), and alert fatigue (33.3%). Less common barriers were inconsistent implementation (22.2%) and limited leadership support (11.1%). Overall, while CGM was unanimously valued, providers emphasized addressing cost, workload, training, leadership, and system integration for sustainable use.

Theme 1: care model characteristics

Sub-theme 1.1: program structure

The sub-theme of program structure includes CGM program implementation, care team composition across sites, patient journey, and adopted care protocols. The care team mainly included doctors and care managers, supported by care coordinators. Doctors identified patients and adjusted treatment, while care managers handled counselling, device setup, and app use. Dietitians were involved by referral. One doctor noted that care managers largely led the project, with doctors entrusting onboarding and monitoring to them.

“For this particular project… it is mainly the care manager… And then… we get them to see my nurse… she’ll explain a bit more… And then usually you’ll see them again three months later for the first round.” (IDH04, doctor, implementation site A).

Care managers often became the point of contact for technical issues and patient queries throughout the CGM cycles. A care manager from another clinic explained her role encompassed counselling and follow-up:

“We see patients with diabetes… do the counselling… start off using insulin… checking insulin technique and teaching them how to use the glucometer. For CGM, we also put on the patches and set up the app.” (IDH11, care manager, implementation site C).

The program followed a structured two-phase CGM process. Doctors screened patients for suitability, after which care managers handled consent, device application, and education. Patients wore the first CGM for 10–14 days, followed by about three months of usual care. At the next routine doctor visit, first-phase data was reviewed and a second CGM applied. The timeline aligned with regular appointments to reduce extra visits.

“We try to make it convenient… book for them to come back at their next doctor appointment. Then… come and see CM (care manager) as well.” (IDH10, Care Manager, implementation site C)

After the second CGM cycle, doctors conducted a final review to discuss results and adjust management. The program offered intermittent CGM use over several months, which patients found structured and helpful, though some felt the 3-month gap between cycles was too long. While eligibility was broadly defined for patients with sub-optimally controlled type 2 diabetes, providers also considered age, technology comfort, and caregiver support. One doctor emphasized motivation and willingness as key inclusion factors over age or technology skills.

“As long as the patient is willing, I think that is the most important… even the elderly who are not tech savvy, but if they’re willing to learn and have a caregiver to help them (we include them).” (IDH04, doctor, implementation site A)

Providers assessed practical factors like smartphone compatibility for the CGM app and excluded frail or disinterested patients. Patients wore the sensor for 10–14 days, accessed their readings via the app, and in some sites, clinic staff and caregivers could view data remotely. The model integrated CGM into routine care with minimal disruption, relying on nurses for implementation and doctors for data review during regular visits.

Sub-theme 1.2: patient education and support

Under this theme, technical training was a critical component. At enrolment, patients received hands-on guidance from care managers or coordinators on CGM app setup, account registration, and interpreting glucose graphs. Nurses applied the first sensor in-clinic while teaching proper use and troubleshooting. One coordinator noted that step-by-step setup could be time-consuming for patients less familiar with smartphones.

“When you are doing the app download, create the account, then… make sure patient (can use it). The savvy, fast ones can be as quick as 20 min.” (IDH03, care manager, implementation site B)

For less tech-savvy patients, staff spent extra time to ensure understanding. One clinic used creative solutions, such as video calls to guide home application of the second sensor, with care managers demonstrating the process on themselves.

“We do a video call… I’ll put the patch (on myself)… show them: I clean the area and put it here, then release it. We showed them the entire process.” (IDP05, patient, implementation site D)

Remote coaching empowered patients to use the device independently while feeling supported. Technical training reduced device issues, and the program reinforced diabetes self-care. Care managers used CGM data to guide diet and exercise adjustments, often encouraging food diaries to help patients link meals with glucose patterns.

“I tell them the benefit is it’s feedback to the patient… I usually encourage them to write a food diary because it only makes sense (with the CGM data).” (IDH04, doctor, implementation site A)

Patients were thus guided to use the CGM experience as a learning tool about their lifestyle. In practice, not everyone maintained a food log, and not all sessions included comprehensive education due to time constraints. One patient noted he received verbal diet advice but did not strictly follow it, instead self-adjusting behaviour based on the CGM readings alone:

“They tell me (what to eat)… but I never follow. I only look at the CGM – when the reading high I stop eating, go low I start eating. I follow the reading.” (IDP07, Patient, implementation site E)

This quote illustrates that while education was offered, some patients adopted their own interpretation, essentially using real-time feedback to modulate intake (fasting when glucose was high and eating when it dropped). Education was discussion-based, with most guidance delivered verbally. Few received printed resources, and providers acknowledged the lack of structured tools like food diaries. Support emphasized personalized counselling and real-time troubleshooting. Some suggested older patients could benefit from refresher materials, given the long interval between CGM cycles. Patient engagement varied, motivated individuals explored their data in-depth, while others absorbed only on basic device use.

Theme 2: implementation experience

Sub-theme 2.1: healthcare provider experience

Providers noted that implementing the CGM program in busy public primary care setting significantly increased workload. Enrolment and follow-ups were time-intensive, with care managers spending up to 30 min per patient for screening, consent, device application, and app training, far exceeding standard appointment durations (around 10 min for doctor consults in polyclinic setting). Highly engaged patients required even more time, straining clinic schedules. Additional burdens included unscheduled patient calls, coordinating second sensor applications, and managing follow-ups alongside other programs. Despite these challenges, providers valued the program's clinical benefits and professional growth, with many undergoing CGM training and wearing the devices themselves to better support patients.

“I think it's good if everyone can have a chance to have this device attached to them first and try it themselves before… for patients.” (IDH01, care manager, implementation site D)

Clinics supported experiential learning through vendor-led training and internal protocols, which providers appreciated for enhancing their diabetes care skills. One nurse noted the CGM pilot helped her refresh glucose pattern interpretation and patient education, contributing to capacity building. Staff were excited to pioneer new technology in primary care, though some felt they needed more training in efficiently interpreting CGM data during busy clinics. Overall, the program brought both increased workload and meaningful professional growth, blending strain with skill development and job satisfaction.

Sub-theme 2.2: patient experience and engagement

Patients’ CGM experience depended on their acceptance of the technology and engagement with the process. While overall acceptance was positive, it varied by age, tech comfort, and language. Many appreciated avoiding daily finger-pricks and found the sensor comfortable after initial use. Smartphone users adapted quickly, while older or non-English-speaking patients often needed support. Despite occasional issues like loose sensors or erratic readings, most found the device reliable and engaging. Real-time glucose data prompted lifestyle changes, with patients becoming more diet-conscious, with some adjusting meal choices or portions after seeing spikes. Overall, CGM use encouraged reflection and behaviour change, reinforcing its value in diabetes self-management.

“Once I see the alert so high, I scared, I stop eating… I wait until it drops. Normally when it’s high, I don’t feel hungry. When it drops below the low level… then I start to get hungry, then I eat.” (IDP07, patient, implementation site E)

CGM use prompted real-time and retrospective behaviour changes, including reduced food intake, portion control, and increased physical activity. While some patients maintained these habits, others reverted after device removal, highlighting the motivational role of continuous feedback. A few reported lasting mindset shifts, like improved awareness of dietary impacts. Overall, CGM improved diabetes understanding, though long-term behaviour changes varied.

Patient engagement also differed. Most were motivated, adherent, and appreciated the support, with some actively seeking help to interpret data. A few declined the second cycle due to inconvenience or low perceived benefit, but most completed at least one cycle, with no major dropouts or adverse events. Supportive clinic staff played a crucial role in sustaining participation. In summary, CGM was well-received and often eye-opening for patients, though sustaining engagement and change, especially among less motivated individuals, remained a challenge.

Theme 3: implementation factors

Sub-theme 3.1: facilitators

Several facilitating factors enabled the CGM program to be implemented relatively smoothly. Organizational support was evident through flexible scheduling for CGM appointments and backing for staff training. A strong culture of peer support also emerged, with experienced team members guiding others during the initial rollout, such as one coordinator learning from a colleague familiar with the device.

“I was doing for the first time… my colleague assisted me because I never had the experience… she explained, is very fast when she knows, she already experienced it” (IDH02, care manager, implementation site D)

Peer experiential learning boosted staff confidence in troubleshooting CGM use, fostering shared knowledge and support. Multidisciplinary meetings enhanced coordination, allowing teams to align on patient selection and follow-ups, integrating CGM into routine care. Technological infrastructure also supported implementation, the CGM system’s cloud syncing enabled easy access to patient data, allowing providers to download and print glucose reports during appointments.

One patient explained that after initial setup, “all my reports update will just go automatically to them… so they can review it even when… my 10 days is finished but I’m not there, they can pull the report and have a look” (IDP05, patient, implementation site D)

Seamless data sharing enabled care teams to efficiently review CGM results during clinic visits, while real-time app feedback motivated patients. The real time continuous glucose monitoring (RT-CGM) sensors were reliable, and the user-friendly app supported patient engagement, especially for tech-savvy users. Implementation sites prepared with spare sensors and IT support for troubleshooting, enabling smooth integration. Clinically, the program was embedded into routine workflows, with multidisciplinary teams, including nurses, doctors, and sometimes dietitians, collaborating to provide comprehensive, coordinated CGM reviews.

One patient participant recounted, “With nurses first, then dietitian, then doctor, all of them look at the readings, but they talk to you about different things”, demonstrating a coordinated approach where each provider addressed a facet of diabetes management (IDP05, patient, implementation site D)

Team-based consultations enhanced CGM’s value by offering integrated feedback on medication, lifestyle, and diet in one session. Consistent care coordinators supported continuity and trust, while providers’ personal commitment, for example, extra calls and one-on-one reviews, boosted patient success. Strong teamwork, seamless data integration (at some sites), and supportive staff attitudes were key to overcoming early implementation challenges.

Sub-theme 3.2: barriers

Despite facilitators, several barriers to CGM implementation emerged. Resource constraints were key, busy clinics struggled with extended consults, and existing staff often had to manage CGM tasks alongside regular duties. Limited trained personnel affected continuity when staff rotated or were unavailable. Financial concerns also emerged. While devices were provided during the study, patients and providers questioned long-term affordability without subsidies, highlighting cost as a barrier to scale-up.

One patient bluntly stated that without subsidies the program wouldn’t be sustainable for many: “The government must subsidise… you cannot expect the company to keep giving… it’s helpful to patients (but expensive)” (IDP07, patient, implementation site D)

Technical limitations included phone compatibility, which excluded patients with unsupported or outdated devices. The CGM app’s English-only interface and limited food logging features further restricted usability, especially for older or non-English-speaking patients. Minor issues like sensor dislodgement, battery drain, and Wi-Fi dependency were noted but less critical. Patient factors also influenced success, older adults with low technology literacy often used the device passively, some felt anxious about high readings, and others found it disruptive. Critically, patients uninterested in lifestyle change were less responsive to CGM feedback. In summary, while CGM technology itself was effective, challenges such as staffing, cost, digital literacy, and patient motivation limited full impact suggesting the need for targeted support and careful patient selection in future implementations.

Theme 4: program outcomes

Sub-theme 4.1: clinical impact

Even in this short pilot, notable clinical outcomes were reported. CGM use led to perceived improvements in glycaemic control, with providers noting better glucose trends and HbA1c reductions in some patients. Continuous monitoring enabled timely interventions, nurses responded to hyperglycaemia patterns between visits, and CGM data uncovered issues like post-lunch spikes or risk of hypoglycaemia, prompting medication adjustments.

Beyond clinical metrics, patients gained a deeper understanding of how their behaviours affected glucose levels. Real-time feedback supported informed decisions and better self-management, benefits not achievable with quarterly HbA1c tests alone. Convenience and peace of mind were also significant; patients appreciated fewer finger pricks and the reassurance of being continuously monitored, sometimes even by family via linked apps. The program also enhanced patient-provider interaction, increasing support and accountability. While a few felt overwhelmed by the data, most reported greater engagement and empowerment. Overall, CGM enabled more proactive care with promising early outcomes, despite the pilot not being a formal clinical trial.

Sub-theme 4.2: process outcomes

Beyond clinical outcomes, the CGM program revealed key process outcomes related to integrating new technology into public primary care workflows. Clinics developed new pathways, for instance, pairing nurse and doctor appointments to align CGM application with consultations, improving coordination and minimizing extra visits. Some sites enabled doctors to review CGM data before the patient arrived, streamlining consultations.

However, the program also exposed workflow gaps. For example, dietitians sometimes saw patients before CGM data was available, limiting the effectiveness of those sessions. These challenges highlighted the need for better sequencing and real-time data access. Overall, the initiative led to improved scheduling practices and protocol development, while also surfacing integration issues offering valuable insights for future technology adoption in primary care.

Theme 5: future considerations

Sub-theme 5.1: scalability requirements

To scale and sustain the CGM program, providers and patients emphasized the need for adequate resources. Expansion to routine care would require more trained staff, protected time for CGM-related tasks, and broader training for all diabetes nurses and primary care doctors. Integrating CGM data into electronic medical records was seen as essential to manage larger patient volumes efficiently.

Financial sustainability was a key concern. Without subsidies, sensor costs would deter patient uptake, making long-term funding via government or insurance critical. Process improvements were also suggested. Many felt that two short CGM wear periods over three months were insufficient for lasting behaviour change. Longer program durations (e.g., six months) or more frequent sensor cycles could enhance outcomes. Reducing the interval between CGM cycles and potentially adding a third sensor round were proposed to maintain momentum and reinforce learning. In sum, scaling will require investment in staffing, training, IT, funding, and refined protocols to ensure long-term effectiveness and reach.

Sub-theme 5.2: program improvements

Interviewees suggested improving CGM targeting by focusing on motivated patients with poorly controlled diabetes, particularly those in their 40s–50s or newly diagnosed, who are more receptive to behaviour change. In contrast, long-time insulin users with entrenched habits were seen as less responsive. Providers emphasized selecting patients who are both open to change and tech-savvy, or have caregiver support to manage the app. To strengthen implementation, recommendations included providing multilingual materials and boosting patient engagement between visits through weekly tips or a midway check-in to maintain motivation and support behaviour change.

Discussion

This convergent mixed-methods study of a pilot CGM program in Singapore primary care found high patient acceptability and strong perceived clinical value. Patients reported better understanding and short-term behaviour changes, while providers highlighted timely interventions and more personalized care. However, sustaining behaviour change post-CGM use was challenging, suggesting the need for ongoing reinforcement or extended use. While not a controlled trial, both patients and providers perceived improved glycemic control, reinforcing CGM’s potential in routine diabetes care. Key barriers included clinic workload, limited long-term funding, and technology access issues. Overall, findings support CGM’s promise in primary care, contingent on adequate training, infrastructure, and financial support.

Our findings resonate with and add to the growing literature on CGM use in type 2 diabetes, particularly in primary care settings.

CGM drives behaviour change and self-management

Our study reinforces growing evidence that CGM is a powerful catalyst for behaviour change and self-management in patients with type 2 diabetes. Consistent with prior studies [14, 17, 36], patients reported that real-time data prompted immediate lifestyle adjustments, helping them link behaviours to glucose fluctuations and make more conscious food choices. Shared data review also strengthened patient-provider relationships. However, our findings also highlight the nuanced reality that CGM’s impact is not uniformly positive. A few patients reported data-related anxiety or felt overwhelmed, mirroring concerns noted in other studies [22, 37]. This underscores the need for ongoing support and tailored coaching to ensure CGM is used constructively. With proper guidance, CGM remains a valuable tool for enabling diabetes self-management and behaviour change.

Perceived clinical outcomes

Although clinical outcomes like HbA1c or quality of life were not objectively measured, both patients and providers reported perceived improvements in glucose control and day-to-day diabetes management. Providers used CGM data to tailor treatment, identifying postprandial spikes or avoiding nocturnal lows while patients felt more empowered by real-time feedback. These perceived benefits align with literature on intermittent CGM, especially when paired with education or coaching [15, 38].

Importantly, our findings also echo studies suggesting that CGM’s value in type 2 diabetes may lie less in reducing hypoglycaemia, especially for patients on oral medications with low baseline hypo risk [13], and more in minimizing hyperglycaemia, reducing variability, and enhancing time-in-range. While not measured quantitatively, participants noted fewer glucose swings and greater confidence in self-management. Consistent with existing literature [13, 19], CGM was generally well-received, reducing anxiety for many by offering continuous insight. Though a few patients found certain readings stressful, most felt CGM reduced uncertainty and improved confidence. Overall, CGM emerges as a valuable complement to patient-centred, responsive diabetes care in primary care settings.

Cost as a barrier to uptake

Cost emerged as a major barrier to sustained CGM use, echoing both local and international literature. Patients and providers alike expressed concern about affordability once study-provided sensors ended, with many unwilling to continue without subsidies. This reinforces findings from Yap and colleagues [12], who identified cost as the top barrier among Singapore-based providers, and adds the patient perspective. Similar concerns have been raised internationally, where high out-of-pocket costs and lack of insurance coverage hinder CGM adoption in type 2 diabetes [19, 22].

Importantly, cost-related barriers risk exacerbating health inequities. In our public primary care setting, patients of lower socioeconomic status, who may stand to benefit most from CGM, were often least able to afford it, a trend noted in broader discussions on diabetes technology disparities [39]. Indirect costs, like needing a compatible smartphone and data plan, add further challenges. Participants and providers called for targeted subsidies or public funding to support CGM for patients with poor glycemic control, in line with previously reported recommendations [12]. Without addressing these financial barriers, CGM’s potential to improve care in real-world settings will remain limited to those who can afford it, underscoring the need for equitable implementation strategies.

Implementation in primary care: feasibility and challenges

Our study provides real-world insights into implementing CGM in public primary care settings, particularly in an Asian context. Consistent with Johansson and colleagues [38], we found CGM can be successfully integrated into routine care with training and workflow adjustments. Nurses and care coordinators played central roles in sensor application, education, and data review, reflecting global trends promoting allied health involvement in tech-enabled chronic care. However, context-specific barriers, such as app-language and phone compatibility issues in multi-ethnic populations, were more prominent than in Western literature.

Key implementation challenges included time burden, coordination logistics, and provider confidence in CGM data interpretation. Consults were extended, aligning with prior workload concerns [19], and provider hesitation around ambulatory glucose profile interpretation echoed earlier findings [12], underscoring the need for capacity-building. Patient factors also mattered, those more motivated and digitally literate benefited more, while others struggled, as noted by Tanenbaum and colleagues [22]. These insights suggest that successful CGM integration in primary care depends on targeted training, thoughtful workflow redesign, and readiness screening to match patients with appropriate support.

Mixed methods approach to user experience

A key strength of our study was the concurrent mixed-methods design, integrating patient and provider perspectives for a holistic evaluation of the CGM program. While quantitative data captured overall satisfaction and perceived benefits, qualitative insights revealed important nuances, such as digital literacy challenges, provider workflow adaptations, and mismatched expectations around data use. This approach allowed us to identify both reinforcing patterns and practical gaps, such as the need for clearer communication and streamlined CGM reports. Aligning with prior recommendations [13, 38], our findings demonstrate the value of mixed-methods in capturing the full scope of user experience and implementation challenges, supporting more patient-centred and effective adoption of health technologies.

This study has several key strengths. The convergent mixed-methods design enabled both validation and depth, quantitative surveys quantified attitudes, while qualitative interviews explained them and uncovered additional insights, with strong triangulation enhancing credibility. Including both patient and provider perspectives allowed a more comprehensive evaluation of how CGM affected care dynamics, a feature often missing in single-perspective studies. Conducted across multiple public primary care clinics with typical patients and frontline staff, the study reflects real-world conditions and enhances generalizability. Finally, by focusing on Asian primary care context, it contributes novel evidence to an area underrepresented in CGM research.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. The small sample size (12 patients, 9 providers) within the context of a feasibility pilot limits generalizability, and survey percentages should be interpreted cautiously. As a pilot, findings are descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. Selection bias is possible, as participants may have been more motivated or tech-inclined than the general population, potentially overstating acceptability. The short study duration captured only initial experiences, leaving long-term adoption and outcomes unknown. Importantly, we did not collect objective clinical data (e.g., HbA1c), so perceived improvements cannot confirm clinical efficacy. No participants opted for a second interview, possibly because one interview felt sufficient given the short pilot duration; thus, we were unable to capture longer-term reflections. Lastly, findings are context-specific to Singapore’s public primary care system and may not fully apply to other settings with different funding models or CGM technologies. Nonetheless, many insights, such as the need for training, workflow adaptation, and patient support, are likely relevant across health systems.

Conclusion

To make CGM a feasible and effective tool in primary care, systemic support is essential. First, capacity-building through staff training and the designation of CGM “champions” in clinics can ease the burden on individual providers and support broader patient reach. Second, integrating CGM technology into existing clinical systems will streamline workflows and reduce technical frustrations. Third, financial barriers must be addressed, our findings support subsidizing CGM for suitable patients, similar to how chronic disease medications are subsidized. Fourth, patient selection is critical; targeting motivated individuals who need better control, while supporting those with language or tech barriers, can maximize impact and equity. Finally, offering longer or repeated CGM use with education may foster more lasting behaviour change than short-term exposure. These recommendations aim to enhance CGM program effectiveness and address the practical challenges identified in our study.

In conclusion, this mixed-methods study provides valuable early insights into CGM implementation in Southeast Asian public primary care, an underrepresented context. While limited in scale, our findings reinforce CGM’s benefits in self-management and engagement while highlighting barriers such as cost and workflow burden. Importantly, we capture both patient and provider perspectives in primary care, adding depth to the largely specialist-focused discourse. Our findings underscore CGM’s transformative potential when supported appropriately and outline practical considerations like system integration and equitable access crucial for successful scale-up. These insights lay the groundwork for future research and real-world adoption.
