Adaptive introgression shaping genomic adaptation and ancestral mosaicism of Changthangi sheep for high-altitude survival and pashmina excellence
Authors: Rangasai Chandra Goli, Kiyevi G. Chishi, Oguru Sai Sruthi, Tanzil Fatima, Tanuj Gunturu, Muralidhar Metta, Vidyasagar Chatnallikar, Nidhi Sukhija, Kanaka KK
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConservation of genetic diversity is fundamental to safeguarding the adaptive potential of any species. Sheep (Ovis aries), domesticated approximately 10,000 years ago in the Fertile Crescent, have developed extensive genetic diversity, particularly in Asia, which hosts over 40% of global sheep breeds. This study focuses on the Changthangi sheep, indigenous to the high-altitude regions of Ladakh, India, and examines their genomic structure, diversity, and locus-specific ancestry in comparison with Tibetan and Deccani sheep breeds.ResultsGenome-wide diversity analyses, including Heterozygosity, Effective population size (Ne), Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) decay, Haplotype block analysis, Runs of Homozygosity (ROH), and Allelic Richness (AR), revealed high genetic diversity in Changthangi and Tibetan breeds, attributed to their adaptation to extreme Himalayan environments, while Deccani exhibited lower diversity. Population structure analyses, including Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Admixture, confirmed Changthangi’s admixed ancestry, with 11.9% Tibetan and 13.91% Deccani contributions, validated by f3 statistics. Admixture timing, inferred using Alder, estimated Deccani introgression approximately 52–68 years ago and Tibetan introgression 35–44 years ago, reflecting historical gene flow. Locus-specific ancestry analysis using Local Ancestry in adMixed populations (LAMP) software, identified adaptive admixture signals, with Deccani ancestry enriched on Ovis aries chromosome (OAR) 7, 8, 15, 17, 19, 20, 22, and 23, and Tibetan ancestry on OAR 3, 5, 9, 12, 14, 18, 21, 25, and 26. Functional annotation of these regions revealed genes associated with high-altitude adaptation, immune response, wool, and meat production, highlighting the adaptive significance of these introgressions.ConclusionThese findings highlight Changthangi’s genetic architecture, shaped by adaptive introgression, and emphasize the importance of conserving this breed for its superior Pashmina fiber and resilience to extreme environments, offering critical insights for breeding and conservation strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12712-z.

Introduction

Genetic diversity in livestock plays a critical role in ensuring food security and combating the effects of climate change [1, 2]. Among several species, sheep (Ovis aries) was the earliest species to be domesticated, with their origins tracing back to the fertile crescent in Southwest Asia approximately 10,000 years ago [3, 4]. Following domestication, sheep dispersed westward into Europe, southward into North Africa, and eastward into Asia alongside migrating human populations [4, 5]. This dispersion led to the development of rich genetic diversity across Asia, which currently harbors over 40% of the world’s sheep breeds [5]. High-altitude regions of the Himalayas, particularly Ladakh and Tibet provide a natural setting to investigate the genetic and molecular basis of adaptation to extreme altitudinal environments. In these areas, the predominant breeds include the Changthangi sheep (CHA) in Ladakh and the Tibetan sheep (TIB) in Tibet, both breeds exhibit remarkable adaptations to severe climatic conditions, including sub-zero temperatures, heavy snowfall, and hypoxic environments [6, 7].

Quantification or estimation of genetic diversity within and among populations can provide indirect estimates of evolutionary potential, predicting population resilience to environmental change and assists in identifying specific populations that may be prioritized for conservation [8–10]. Previous genomic analyses revealed admixture in CHA sheep involving both TIB and Indian Deccani (IDC) sheep breeds [11, 12] raising fundamental questions about the evolutionary mechanisms underlying high-altitude adaptation. We hypothesize that CHA sheep represent a product of strategic adaptive introgression, where geographically and ecologically distinct ancestral contributions have been selectively retained to optimize performance in extreme Himalayan environments. While the rationale for TIB ancestry is straightforward i.e., shared geographic proximity and similar high-altitude selective pressures across the Himalayas facilitate natural gene flow and convergent adaptation [13, 14]. However, the presence of IDC ancestry presents a compelling evolutionary puzzle. Despite geographic separation, IDC inhabit the semi-arid Deccan plateau at < 1000 m elevation while CHA thrives at > 4000 m [15–17]. The objectives of the present study were to (i) characterize genome-wide genetic diversity and population structure of Changthangi sheep in comparison with Tibetan and Deccani breeds, (ii) quantify the extent and timing of admixture contributing to the Changthangi genome, and (iii) identify locus-specific ancestry patterns and functionally relevant genomic regions that may have contributed to high-altitude adaptation and fiber-related traits.

Materials and methods

Data retrieving and quality control

Detailed workflow of the present study is described in Fig. 1. A total of 90 animals genotype data typed using OvineSNP50 BeadChip from three different ovine breeds i.e., CHA (n = 29), IDC (n = 24) and TIB (n = 37) were retrieved from WIDDE (Web-Interfaced Next Generation Database for genetic diversity exploration) online repository and only autosomal markers were retained in order to avoid any potential bias [18–24]. Genotype quality control (QC) was performed using PLINK v1.9 software [25]. Variants with coordinates based on the Oar_v3.1 ovine reference genome assembly were included in the analysis as the Oar_v3.1 assembly is well established and frequently used in studies of Indian sheep breeds [11, 12, 26]. SNP markers were retained for further analysis if they met the following criteria; Call rate (CR) greater than 99% and a Missing genotype (MG) rate (–mind) of 0.1. Criteria related to Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) and linkage disequilibrium (LD) were deliberately excluded from the analysis, as these could eliminate loci under selection and skew the interpretation of population mixing with different gene frequencies [2, 12, 24, 27–29].

Sample collection rationale and dataset validation

The present study has utilized genotype data from the WIDDE repository, which represents one of the most comprehensive and standardized genomic datasets available for global sheep breeds [20, 21]. The WIDDE database follows stringent quality control protocols, including verification of breed authenticity through pedigree records, morphological assessments, and geographic origin confirmation [20]. All samples included in this study were collected according to International Sheep Genomics Consortium standards, ensuring genetic purity and breed representativeness.

For the three breeds analyzed, sample selection criteria were as follows, CHA sheep were sourced from high-altitude regions of Ladakh, India (> 14,000 feet elevation), with verification of breed characteristics including adaptation to extreme cold and Pashmina fiber production capability [30]. TIB sheep were collected from the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau regions and were confirmed based on characteristic cold-resilient morphological traits, including a compact body frame, dense coarse fleece, short extremities, and robust thoracic conformation typical of high-altitude adapted Tibetan sheep [14]. Deccani sheep originated from the semi-arid Deccan Plateau of Maharashtra, India, and were validated using breed-specific morphological features such as a medium to large body size, long pendulous ears, convex facial profile, relatively long legs, and a coarse hair-type fleece adapted to hot and dry environments [31]. The use of WIDDE data provides several advantages over newly collected samples: (1) standardized genotyping protocols across all breeds using identical OvineSNP50 BeadChip platforms, eliminating technical batch effects; (2) quality-assured sample authentication through consortium level verification; (3) enabling direct comparisons with previous genomic studies on these breeds [6, 11] ; and (4) cost-effective utilization of high-quality existing data for comprehensive population genomic analyses.

Genome-wide diversity analysis

Various software suites were used for the analysis of genetic diversity and population structure in different datasets after QC. Heterozygosity (H), a fundamental metric of genetic diversity, is primarily influenced by the number of alleles and their proportions within a population, including observed (Ho) and expected heterozygosity (He) [2, 32] and average heterozygosity levels across the genome were also calculated using –het commands in the PLINK v1.9 [25]. Effective population size (Ne) was calculated using SNeP v1.11 [33], as it calculates the historical Ne by utilizing the correlation between r2 (linkage disequilibrium), and recombination rate (c) and utilizes for the demographic history of each population, by tracing back approximately 13 generation and backwards into the past. The parameters used for SNeP were r = 1e-00, minimum distance of 50,000 bp and maximum distance of 4,000,000 bp between SNPs, minimum and maximum r2 for a pair of SNPs to be analyzed was set as 0 and 1 respectively. Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) Decay was estimated using PopLDdecay [34] and plotted using ggplot2 [35] in R and estimating LD decay helps in understanding the extent of recombination and the genetic structure of the population, which is important for breeding and conservation strategies. Haplotype blocks were estimated using PLINK v1.9 software [25] under a confidence interval approach, with the parameter ensuring that 95% of informative SNP pairs were considered, maximum block size was set to 1000 kb to limit the physical distance spanned by each block, minimum minor allele frequency (MAF) of 0.05 was enforced to exclude rare variants, lower confidence interval bound of 0.7005 and upper confidence interval bound of 0.90 parameters defined the confidence interval thresholds for identifying strong LD, ensuring robust block. The --blocks no-pheno-req flag allowed analysis without requiring phenotypic data, focusing solely on genotypic information. These thresholds collectively ensured that the detected haplotype blocks represented regions of consistent and reliable genetic linkage, enhancing the accuracy of the analysis.

Runs of homozygosity (ROH) based inbreeding coefficient (FROH) was estimated for each individual across autosomes using PLINK v1.9 [25]. To mitigate the short ROH fragments arising from LD, LD pruning was not applied; instead, a minimum ROH length threshold of 1 Mb was used [36]. ROH detection was performed using a sliding window approach to avoid the formation of artificially short segments smaller than the scanning window [37]. The criteria for defining ROH segments were as follows: (i) minimum ROH length set to 1 Mb, (ii) maximum allowable gap between consecutive homozygous SNPs set to 1,000 kb, (iii) minimum SNP density of 1 SNP per 100 kb, (iv) a minimum of 15 consecutive homozygous SNPs required, and (v) regions containing any heterozygous or missing genotypes were excluded to ensure strict homozygosity and these parameters were consistent with [24]. Inbreeding by Genomic Relationship Matrix (FGRM), which is a genomic inbreeding coefficient, representing the diagonal elements of the functional genomic relationship matrix, is a genomic inbreeding measure derived from genome-wide SNP data, representing the diagonal elements of the genomic relationship matrix and reflecting the deviation of observed homozygosity from Hardy–Weinberg expectations, was calculated using --ibc flag in PLINK v1.9 [25]. Although the --ibc option in PLINK also provides additional genomic-based inbreeding estimators (e.g., excess homozygosity–based and allele frequency–weighted measures), the present study focused on FGRM because it provides a direct and comparable estimate of individual-level genomic inbreeding that is less sensitive to allele-frequency distribution differences among populations [26]. Allelic Richness (AR) parameter averages the number of alleles across all the surveyed loci in the population to indicate a population’s adaptations and evolutionary potential, and the hierfstat R package [38] was used to estimate average allelic richness across all the individuals of each breed under study.

Genome-wide genetic relationship and population structure

Population structure was performed using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) performed with PLINK v1.9 [25], and the PCA results were visualized using the ggplot2 [35] package in R. To assess genome wide ancestry, a Maximum likelihood-based clustering method was applied using ADMIXTURE software [39] with 20,000 Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) iterations after an initial burn-in of 10,000 steps, repeated over 10 runs and the most consistent clustering outcome across runs was considered indicative of the true population structure. The ideal number of genetic clusters (K) was selected based on the minimal Cross-Validation (CV) error. Admixture proportions of IDC and TIB within the CHA breed were calculated and plotted using PONG [40]. The f3 statistics test evaluates whether a target population (A) results from admixture between two reference populations (B and C), with a positive f3 value indicating no significant recent admixture from the reference populations [28].

Interpretation framework for population structure analyses

Different population genetic methods detect distinct demographic processes operating on various temporal scales. PCA identifies genetic differentiation between populations regardless of the underlying demographic cause [41]. Admixture, an unsupervised classification model, determines optimal population clustering and ancestry proportions, which can reflect recent admixture, ancient population structure, or continuous gene flow [39]. The f₃ statistic is a formal three-population test that evaluates whether a target population shows evidence of admixture between two reference populations, with significantly negative f₃ values indicating admixture and positive values suggesting shared ancestry without recent admixture [28, 42]. These complementary approaches provide a comprehensive view of population relationships while requiring careful interpretation of apparently contradictory results.

By fitting an exponential curve to the weighted correlation between SNP pairs as a function of genetic distance, this approach uses the decay rate to estimate the time elapsed since the admixture event, offering a robust framework for understanding the historical gene flow in CHA sheep. Further to investigate population admixture using phased genomic data, we employed the algorithm developed by Li and Stephens [44], as implemented in ChromoPainterv2 [45]. This method accounts for LD and the underlying recombination dynamics across markers, reconstructing each haplotype as a mosaic of genetic segments derived from a pool of donor haplotypes (IDC and TIB). ChromoPainterv2 initially estimates nuisance parameters namely, n (like Ne) and M (the population mutation rate), which are then integrated into a probabilistic framework to compute the probability of a recipient haplotype copying genetic segments from donor haplotypes. Parameters were subsequently fixed within the algorithm to generate the ChromoPainter coancestry matrix, encompassing both the count matrix and the length matrix, which quantifies haplotype sharing among samples across all chromosomes, providing a detailed representation of genetic relationships within the CHA population.

Genome-wide locus-specific ancestry

LAMP (Local Ancestry in adMixed Populations) is a computational tool used to determine ancestry at specific genomic regions in admixed individuals by analyzing allele frequencies from defined reference populations [46]. For this study, local ancestry in the admixed CHA sheep was inferred using the LAMP-ANC mode [46]. Given the absence of a sheep genetic map, a fixed recombination rate of 1 × 10⁻⁸ per base pair (equivalent to 1 centiMorgan or 0.01 recombinations per megabase) was applied [47]. Furthermore, the study calculated the difference in local ancestry which is referred to as ‘Δ ancestry’ in order to identify regions with significant deviations in ancestral contributions compared to the reference breeds. This approach, based on methods by Tang & Co-workers [47] and Gautier & Naves [48], measures Δ ancestry by subtracting the genome-wide average ancestry from the locus-specific ancestry values for each ancestral lineage.

To identify adaptive admixture signals from local ancestry data, an outlier detection method was applied. Regions exhibiting values beyond ± 3 standard deviations from the mean (corresponding to p < 0.001 assuming normal distribution) were classified as potential adaptive admixtures [48]. Only contiguous windows of ≥ 5 SNPs exceeding this threshold were retained to minimize false positive signals from isolated outlier SNPs. Subsequently, structural annotation was performed using the GALLO v1.4.0 an R package [49] with default parameters for gene annotation within 1 Mb windows of significant SNPs. To elucidate genomic differences among populations, functional annotation was conducted utilizing the DAVID v2023q1 [50, 51]. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the following significance thresholds i.e., Benjamini-Hochberg corrected p-value < 0.05, minimum gene count of 3 per category, and enrichment fold > 1.5. KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathway analysis applied similar criteria with false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and minimum pathway coverage of 2 genes. Only functionally annotated genes within linkage disequilibrium blocks (r² > 0.8) of significant ancestry deviation SNPs were included in the enrichment analysis to ensure biological relevance.

Results and discussion

Assessing genetic diversity is a cornerstone of effective germplasm conservation and plays a pivotal role in mitigating the risk of losing unique and valuable alleles [52]. In the present investigation, we conducted an in-depth genomic analysis of the CHA sheep breed in comparison with IDC and TIB breeds, with emphasis on elucidating their population structure, genetic diversity and population relationships. The resilience of the CHA, particularly under cold stress, is hypothesized to be a consequence of its broad genetic base, which has critical implications for both genetic enhancement and conservation strategies [53]. Comprehensive insight into genomic diversity and population structure is essential for conservation and breeding frameworks at both regional and global levels [54] as the CHA breed is internationally recognized for producing a superior quality fiber that is often equated with Pashmina, a term traditionally used to denote the finest class of cashmere wool [55]. Renowned for its exceptional softness and thermal insulation, Pashmina is frequently referred to as the diamond fabric or the soft gold of the Himalayas [56]. Due to its premium quality and limited availability, it has a higher market value across the globe. Consequently, preservation of such unique genetic resources is crucial, not only for maintaining cultural and economic heritage but also for ensuring the sustainable supply of this valuable natural fiber.

Data quality control

Only the markers present on autosomes were included into further analysis to remove the biases and ambiguities in the analysis thereafter [23, 24]. A total of 46,819 SNPs belonging to 90 individuals were present in the initial dataset. First, a SNP CR (QC1) criterion was enforced, with a threshold set at 0.05. Consequently, 698 SNPs were eliminated from the dataset, leaving 46,121 SNPs for subsequent analysis. Furthermore, an assessment for MG (QC2) data in individuals was applied at the 0.1 threshold, no individuals were lost, all 90 individuals were retained for further downstream analysis. Quality control was omitted for HWE and LD criteria to study selection, migration, and admixture patterns, as they may remove loci under selection and also remove the influence of intermixing of populations with different gene frequencies [12, 24, 28, 29]. Finally, 46,121 high quality SNPs belonging to 90 individuals were retained for further downstream analysis and these were in line with previous studies viz., 46,324 [56] and 46,819 SNPs [6, 11]. This comparison suggests the good quality of genotyping in the present study.

Dataset validation and breed authentication

The WIDDE dataset used in this study demonstrated high concordance with previous genomic investigations of the same breeds. In comparison, a previous study using identical CHA samples showed consistent heterozygosity values (Ho = 0.338 vs. 0.341 reported), validating data reliability and confirming the representativeness of our sample set [11]. The geographic distribution of samples accurately reflected the natural habitat ranges of each breed, with CHA from Ladakh (> 4000 m elevation), TIB from plateau regions (3000–5000 m), and IDC from peninsular India (< 1000 m elevation), ensuring ecological authenticity of the genetic signatures observed.

Genome-wide diversity analysis

Heterozygosity (H) is of major interest in revealing genetic variation in the populations. The H value ranges from zero to 1.0 (high number of alleles with equal frequency) [57]. Ho, He and FIS values of CHA were 0.338, 0.362 and 0.067, IDC were 0.340, 0.362 and 0.060 and TIB were 0.328, 0.362 and 0.094, respectively (Fig. 2). The He was high compared to Ho and lower FIS values revealing the higher genetic diversity of this population, among these three breed IDC shows slightly higher values of Ho and lower values of FIS indicating higher diversity compared to other breeds in the study. These results were comparable to those reported for Welsh breeds [58], Australian sheep breeds [59] and Kyrgyzstan sheep breeds [60]. The higher genetic diversity in these populations can be explained by their breeding history. As mentioned earlier, the intermixing of the genome among these three sheep breeds contributed to the high genetic diversity observed in this study.

Historical effective population size (Ne) analysis revealed distinct demographic trajectories across breeds (Fig. 3). TIB sheep maintained the largest Ne throughout history (3661 at 1000 generations, 164 at 13th generation), followed by CHA (3344, 121) and IDC (3223, 124) (S1). This pattern reflects the geographic and demographic constraints of high-altitude environments, where larger breeding populations provide greater resilience against environmental stochasticity [61]. The parallel decline across all breeds from 1000 to 13 generations ago indicates common selective pressures during breed formation, likely reflecting both natural selection for local adaptation and artificial selection for production traits [62]. Importantly, all populations exceed the FAO minimum viable population threshold (Ne = 50), indicating sufficient genetic diversity for long-term sustainability.

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis across CHA, IDC, and TIB revealed distinct patterns of genetic diversity (Fig. 4). The LD decay, measured as r2, was plotted over a genetic distance of 0 to 500 kb, with r2 values ranging from 0.0 to 0.45. At 0 kb, all populations exhibited high LD levels (r2, approx 0.40), reflecting strong linkage between closely spaced markers due to limited recombination. A sharp decline in LD occurred within the first 100 kb across all breeds, followed by a slower decay, stabilizing at (r2) values of 0.05–0.10 beyond 200 kb. Among the populations, TIB (represented by a green dotted line in Fig. 4) displayed the slowest LD decay, maintaining higher (r2) values throughout, consistent with its larger Ne of 3661 at 1000 generations ago and 164 at the 13th generation. CHA (red line) showed a slightly faster decay than TIB but slower than IDC, aligning with its intermediate Ne values of 3344 and 121 at the same time points. In contrast, IDC (blue dashed line) exhibited the fastest LD decay, reflecting its smaller Ne of 3223 and 124, which likely resulted in greater genetic drift and higher recombination rates. These patterns corroborate earlier findings of historically larger Ne in mountain sheep (TIB and CHA) compared to IDC, suggesting that slower LD decay in TIB and CHA indicates better haplotype conservation and reduced genetic drift, thereby preserving greater ancestral diversity. Conversely, the rapid LD decay in IDC points to diminished genetic diversity, consistent with its Ne trends over time. Overall, these results underscore the relationship between Ne and LD decay rates, with larger Ne associated with slower LD decay and enhanced genetic diversity, crucial for the long-term adaptability of these sheep populations. The LD levels observed in this study are comparable to those reported in other sheep population such as Spanish Churra exhibited an average r² of 0.329 for SNPs up to 10 kb apart, similar to the high LD (r² ≈ 0.40) at 0 kb in CHA, IDC, and TIB [63]. However, the r² values at 200–500 kb (0.05–0.10) in our study are slightly lower than the 0.061 reported for Churra at similar distances, suggesting that Indian sheep populations may have experienced more recombination or drift over these distances.

Haplotype blocks, regions of co-inherited alleles shaped by inbreeding, LD, recombination, genetic drift, and population bottlenecks [64]. The investigation delved into haplotypes and total genetic length (Table 1). In CHA sheep, 416 haplotypes spanning 10,971.442 kb (1.071 kb to 652.586 kb, average 26.374 kb) were observed. Similarly, in TIB the highest number of haplotypes i.e., 597 were found with total length of 17,103.896 kb, ranging from 0.037 kb (2 SNPs) to 979.651 kb (8 SNPs) with an average size of 28.65 kb. In IDC with 305 haplotypes and a total length of 10,388.355 kb ranging from 0.037 kb to 543.327 kb and average 34.06 kb. Among the sheep breeds studied, TIB and CHA displayed the highest number of haplotype blocks (597 and 416, respectively) and the longest total haplotype span (17,103.896 kb and 10,971.442 kb, respectively) and this is likely due to limited genetic diversity caused by population bottlenecks, where breeds were reconstituted from small populations, combined with intense selection to preserve specific breed traits. These demographic and selective pressures have led to increased inbreeding [65] and higher LD levels in TIB and CHA sheep compared to the IDC breed. The extent of haplotype block coverage across the genome varied among the sheep breeds studied, ranging from 0.43% in IDC to 0.71% in TIB (Table 1). Compared to other livestock species, sheep exhibit lower haplotype block coverage of the genome than cattle, horses, and pigs. Based on comparable SNP density data, genome coverage by haplotype blocks in cattle ranged from 2.78% to 4.67% [66, 67], in horses from 7.6% to 29.3% [68], and in pigs from 28.35% to 52.77% [69]. These variations in haplotype block coverage may indirectly reflect differences in overall genetic diversity among these livestock species.

Inbreeding coefficient based on Runs of Homozygosity (FROH) was evaluated across three Indian sheep breeds, CHA, IDC, and TIB and is visually depicted in Fig. 5. CHA displayed a FROH of 0.0662 (± 0.0695), with 14.41 ROH segments averaging 8220.24 kb in length, collectively spanning 158825.87 kb of the genome. In contrast, IDC exhibited the lowest inbreeding level, with a FROH of 0.0103 (± 0.019), comprising just 3.29 ROH segments averaging 4178.3 kb, totaling 24766.11 kb of the genome, which suggests higher genetic diversity and reduced inbreeding. TIB, with a FROH of 0.0616 (± 0.0526), has the highest number of ROH segments (17.05), averaging 7699.28 kb and covering 147809.31 kb of the genome, indicating moderate inbreeding similar to CHA but with a greater number of ROH segments. Average FGRM values were 0.11088897 in TIB, 0.04826076 in CHA and 0.03532606 in IDC. Both FROH and FGRM results are consistent with prior analyses, where IDC’s had lower haplotype block structures, higher heterozygosity, faster LD decay, and smaller Ne, while CHA and TIB’s more haplotype block structures, lower heterozygosity and greater number of ROH segments reflect increased inbreeding and slower LD decay, corroborating their larger Ne and historical isolation in mountainous regions. Such elevated inbreeding levels across CHA and TIB signal reduced genetic diversity, increasing their risk of extinction. To mitigate further loss of genetic variation and prevent the potential extinction of these breeds, urgent conservation efforts are necessary. Proposed strategies include phenotypic and genetic characterization, sustainable utilization of genetic resources, and the implementation of structured, tailored breeding programs to effectively preserve this threatened genetic diversity [70–72]. Norsati & Co-workers [73] reported comparable FROH values in global sheep and goat populations, though these were lower than those observed in cattle populations [24, 74]. This suggests that sheep populations exhibit lower inbreeding levels and maintain greater genetic diversity compared to cattle populations.

The mean Allelic Richness (AR) observed among the three studied sheep breeds highlighted the breed-specific genetic variability, likely shaped by their respective native environments and historical selection pressures (Fig. 6). CHA and TIB sheep exhibited the highest AR, with a mean value of 2.65 and 2.59 respectively. This elevated genetic diversity may be attributed to the breed’s long-term adaptation to the extreme conditions of the high-altitude Ladakh and Tibetan Plateau region, characterized by low oxygen levels, intense ultraviolet radiation, and sub-zero temperatures. Such environmental rigors may exert selective pressures favoring genetic diversity that confers resilience, thereby maintaining a higher AR in the population [2, 8]. In contrast, IDC sheep, native to the semi-arid Deccan Plateau, exhibited the lowest AR, with a mean value of 2.56. The relatively stable and less extreme climatic conditions of this region may have reduced the need for maintaining high levels of genetic variability for survival [1]. Moreover, traditional breeding practices, genetic drift, or historical population contractions in the IDC breed could have contributed to this lower genetic diversity. Overall, the differences in AR across these breeds underscore the intricate relationship between environmental adaptation and genetic architecture, emphasizing the importance of conserving genetic resources tailored to diverse ecological contexts. Similarly, higher values AR were observed in fine-fleeced and semi-fine-fleeced sheep of Russia [75] and also higher levels of AR were observed in Bardoka (8.33) and Karakachan (8.39) sheep of Serbia [76].

Genome-wide genetic relationship and population structure

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) revealed that the first and second principal components explained 15.97% and 7.66% of the total genetic variation, respectively (Fig. 7). Principal Component 1 (PC1) effectively separated the studied breeds into three distinct, non-overlapping clusters, CHA, TIB, and IDC. While no intermixing was observed among the breed clusters, a few CHA individuals were in close proximity to the IDC cluster, indicating a proportion of IDC ancestry within CHA. These findings were in accordance with previous study, where they found the ancestry of IDC in CHA both in autosomal and X chromosome [11, 12].

Maximum likelihood-based approach implemented through the ADMIXTURE software was employed to explore the genome-wide ancestry of the CHA sheep population, enabling a detailed inference of the ancestral contributions shaping its gene pool. The analysis evaluated K-values ranging from 2 to 3, where K denotes the predetermined number of ancestral populations. Cross-validation (CV) estimates determined K = 3 as the most suitable model, exhibiting the lowest CV error (0.594), as illustrated in Fig. 8. At K = 3, a distinct separation emerged among the CHA, TIB, and IDC populations, revealing unique ancestry proportions of 74.18%, 86.83%, and 98.16%, respectively (Fig. 9). Within the admixed CHA population, contributions from TIB and IDC ancestry were identified at 11.9% and 13.91%, respectively. The TIB admixture in CHA can likely be attributed to their shared mountainous habitat in the Himalayas, facilitating gene flow. However, the introgression from IDC into CHA remains poorly understood and lacks documentation in previous studies [11, 12] but may be from ancient admixture, as evidenced by mitochondrial DNA analysis, both IDC and CHA sheep predominantly belong to Lineage A/Haplogroup A, suggesting a shared genetic heritage due to historical interbreeding [15, 16].

A positive f3 value of 0.004838 and a Z-score of 10.960 indicates that CHA did not arise through recent, discrete admixture events between IDC and TIB populations as currently constituted. The apparent contradiction between admixture analysis results (showing 11.9% TIB and 13.91% IDC ancestry) and f3 statistics (indicating no recent discrete admixture) reflects different temporal scales and demographic processes. The f3 test specifically detects recent population splits followed by admixture, while ADMIXTURE analysis can identify shared ancestry from ancient population structure or continuous gene flow over extended periods. The positive f3 values for all population combinations suggest that the observed genetic similarities reflect shared ancestral populations rather than recent hybridization events between the specific breeds analyzed.

Admixture events and their timing

It is important to note that the f₃ statistic and LD decay–based dating methods capture admixture signals operating at different temporal scales. While the positive f₃ values observed for CHA reflect shared ancestry or older admixture predating recent breed formation, the Alder-based estimates identify the timing of the most recent detectable gene flow, which may represent low-level or continuous introgression rather than the primary historical admixture event. Therefore, the recent dates inferred using ALDER do not contradict the f₃ results but instead complement them by highlighting ongoing or residual demographic connectivity. Mitochondrial DNA [15, 16] and Admixture (present study) analysis revealed that both IDC and CHA sheep predominantly belong to lineage A (Haplogroup A) and shared ancestry, indicating a common genetic heritage likely shaped by historical interbreeding events. So, in order to find out the time of ancient admixture we have performed weighted LD decay analysis conducted using Alder, alongside a detailed LD correlation assessment, which provides evidence of historical admixture in the CHA sheep population, with IDC and TIB serving as reference populations, unraveling the complex genetic history of this Himalayan breed. Alder’s LD decay curves, fitted across minimum genetic distances from 0.40 cM to 1.00 cM with a fixed upper bound of 50.00 cM, consistently demonstrated exponential decay, particularly when using IDC as a reference, underscoring its significant ancestral contribution to CHA. With IDC as the reference, the estimated admixture time ranged from 21.09 generations (± 25.69) at 0.40 cM to 13.66 generations (± 6.74) at 0.80 cM, achieving statistical significance (Z > 2.0) starting at 0.50 cM, with the strongest signal at 0.60 cM (Z = 2.27). This suggests that admixture with IDC occurred approximately 13–17 generations ago, or 52–68 years ago, assuming a 4-year generation interval for sheep (Fig. 10). In contrast, admixture with TIB sheep appeared more recently, with estimates ranging from 10.55 generations (± 7.39) at 0.60 cM to 8.84 generations (± 4.48) at 1.00 cM, corresponding to 35–44 years ago (Fig. 10). Although Z-scores for TIB were below the conventional significance threshold (Z < 2.0), they increased with higher distance thresholds, peaking at 1.97 at 1.00 cM, indicating a moderately strong but less pronounced signal compared to IDC. These temporal differences likely reflect distinct migration or introgression events, with IDC playing a primary role in shaping CHA gene pool, while TIB’s more recent contribution suggests a diluted gene flow event, possibly influenced by their shared Himalayan habitat.

The admixture dates inferred using Alder (52–68 years ago from IDC and 35–44 years ago from TIB) should be interpreted as signals of recent or ongoing low-intensity gene flow rather than ancient or discrete miscegenation events. LD decay–based methods estimate the time since the most recent detectable ancestry mixing and are sensitive to assumptions regarding recombination rates, generation intervals, and continuous admixture scenarios. In livestock populations, such recent timeframes do not necessarily imply large-scale or deliberate crossbreeding that would be clearly documented or remembered by pastoral communities, but may instead reflect episodic, localized, or opportunistic introgression facilitated by animal exchange, seasonal migration, or limited breeding interventions. Therefore, these estimates are best viewed as reflecting recent demographic connectivity and shared ancestry dynamics rather than single, well-defined historical crossbreeding events. Importantly, the relatively small ancestry proportions observed and the positive f₃ statistics support this interpretation and suggest limited gene flow rather than extensive recent admixture.

Complementing these findings, a line graph depicting LD correlation between CHA and the reference populations (TIB and IDC) across genetic distances from 0 to 0.6 cM further elucidates their genetic relationships (Fig. 11). Both TIB and IDC exhibit a high initial LD correlation with CHA at 0 cM (approximately 0.5), reflecting strong linkage between closely spaced markers due to limited recombination. However, a sharp decline occurs within the first 0.1 cM, dropping to 0.1–0.2, indicative of rapid haplotype breakdown. Beyond 0.1 cM, the LD correlation continues to decrease, stabilizing below 0.1 by 0.3 cM. Notably, CHA’s LD correlation with TIB remains consistently higher than with IDC across all distances, stabilizing at 0.05 by 0.6 cM compared to IDC 0.03, suggesting a recent gene flow with TIB, likely due to geographic proximity in the Himalayas. This aligns with Alder’s amplitude affine values (IDC: 0.00006390, Tibetan: 0.00000477), which indicate a stronger genetic contribution from IDC, yet the persistent haplotype sharing with TIB reflected its slower LD decay, consistent with Tibetan larger Ne. These patterns corroborate ADMIXTURE results, which identified 11.9% and 13.91% TIB and IDC ancestry in CHA, highlighting the intricate interplay of historical gene flow and recombination in shaping CHA genetic structure. The use of multiple LD distance thresholds in Alder ensures robustness in decay estimates, validating the admixed origin of CHA over the past few decades, with IDC as a primary contributor and TIB’s more recent influence approaching significance. These findings illuminate the dynamic genetic history of CHA, offering valuable insights for breeding and conservation strategies to preserve its diversity. Previous studies using Alder for admixture dating were performed in Mehsana buffalo and Ghurrah pig of India in order to find out their admixture dates of Murrah and Surti in Mehsana [28] and Asian and European lineages in Ghurrah pig [77].

Genome-wide locus- specific ancestry (Local ancestry)

In this study we have examined genome-wide locus-specific ancestry (Local ancestry) in admixed CHA sheep using the delta ancestry method, by categorizing their ancestry into IDC and TIB components based on admixture findings from current and prior studies [11]. We identified regions of adaptive admixture by applying an outlier approach, setting a threshold of three standard deviations above the mean (3 ± SD) [29]. These significant regions are marked in red for emphasis (Fig. 13). Genomic segments above the central dotted line were attributed to IDC ancestry, while those below were linked to TIB ancestry, reflecting the order of genomic data input into the LAMP software, as CHA as the target population, IDC as the first ancestral population, and TIB as the second. Adaptive admixture regions from IDC ancestry of 5,236 SNPs were detected on Ovis aries chromosomes (OAR) 7, 8, 15, 17, 19, 20, 22, and 23, while 4,047 SNPs those from Tibetan ancestry were found on OAR 3, 5, 9, 12, 14, 18, 21, 25, and 26 (Fig. 13). The highest number of significant SNPs and genome length contributed from IDC ancestry was on OAR 8 (1,729 SNPs and 90.58 Mb) and 20 (925 SNPs and 50.56 Mb), while from TIB ancestry was on OAR 14 (960 SNPs and 62.50 Mb) and 26 (757 SNPs and 43.83 Mb) in CHA genome (Figs. 14 and 15).

The local ancestry inferences corroborate the patterns observed in genome-wide admixture analyses, with the majority of loci (depicted by the blue line in Fig. 13) positioned above the central green dotted line, signifying a predominant genetic contribution from IDC ancestry, followed by TIB ancestry. The mechanisms underlying the introgression of IDC alleles into CHA sheep likely results from ancient admixture, as evidenced by mitochondrial DNA analysis, as both IDC and CHA sheep predominantly belong to lineage A/haplogroup A, suggesting a shared genetic heritage due to historical interbreeding [16, 79]. Conversely, the shared Himalayan origin of TIB and CHA populations suggests a common historical genetic foundation, which likely accounts for the presence of TIB adaptive signatures in the local ancestry analysis of CHA, despite TIB ancestry contributing only 11.9% to the CHA genome based on genome-wide estimates. The genomic regions highlighted in the local ancestry analysis likely reflect post-admixture selection signatures, where genetic contributions from IDC and TIB ancestries have been preserved due to their potential adaptive or functional significance in CHA. These regions provide insights into the evolutionary pressures that have shaped the CHA genome following admixture events. The prevalence of significant SNPs and the extent of genomic segments derived from IDC and TIB ancestries indicate their substantial influence on the CHA genome, with admixture events estimated to have occurred approximately 52–68 years ago for IDC and 35–44 years ago for TIB ancestry. This timeframe is sufficient for the fixation of adaptive genetic signatures from both ancestral populations [80, 81]. These findings are further supported by Alder algorithm analyses, which detected robust signals of admixture, confirming the establishment of these evolutionary processes within the CHA population. Determining the distinct ancestral origins of genomic segments offers valuable applications, such as disease mapping and exploring historical genetic events [12, 28, 29]. Prior genomic analyses have revealed that local ancestry segments derived from Angus, Brahman, and Wannan breeds may contribute to enhanced growth rates, immune resilience, and local environmental adaptation in Yunling cattle [82]. Additionally, studies have identified evidence of mitonuclear co-evolution in hybrid African cattle populations, demonstrating a significant enrichment of taurine ancestry in nuclear genes associated with mitochondrial function [83].

Geographic and historical context of admixture patterns and events

The TIB genetic contribution (11.9%) aligns with expected biogeographic patterns: both breeds inhabit contiguous high-altitude regions of the Himalayas, sharing similar selective pressures including hypoxia (> 4000 m elevation), extreme cold (-40 °C), and intense UV radiation. Natural gene flow is facilitated by traditional pastoral migration routes across the Indo-Tibetan border regions, particularly through the Changthang Plateau that extends from Ladakh into Tibet [84, 85]. The IDC contribution (13.91%) presents a more complex biogeographic puzzle given the > 3000 km distance and > 4000 m elevation difference between source populations. Three potential mechanisms explain this counterintuitive pattern: (1) Ancient migration corridors i.e., Archaeological evidence suggests early sheep dispersal from the Fertile Crescent followed multiple routes, including southward movement through the Indian subcontinent before northward expansion into the Himalayas, potentially establishing shared ancestral lineages [86, 87]; (2) Historical trade networks i.e., Medieval and colonial-era trade routes connecting the Deccan Plateau with Central Asian markets via Kashmir and Ladakh facilitated livestock exchange, as documented in historical records of the Indo-Tibetan wool trade [88, 89]; (3) Planned breeding interventions i.e., Post-Independence livestock development programs (1950–1970 s) systematically introduced lowland breeds to high-altitude regions to improve productivity and disease resistance, with IDC sheep specifically promoted for their hardiness and adaptability [30].

The Alder estimated admixture dates (52–68 years ago for IDC, 35–44 years ago for TIB) correspond to significant historical periods in the Trans-Himalayan region. The earlier IDC introgression (1950–1970 s) coincides with post-independence livestock improvement programs initiated by the Government of India, which may have introduced IDC germplasm to enhance productivity in high-altitude regions [30]. The more recent Tibetan gene flow (1980–1990 s) aligns with increased cross-border pastoral activities and the establishment of improved transportation networks across the India-Tibet border regions [84, 85]. These findings suggest that CHA’s current genetic architecture reflects both planned breeding interventions and natural pastoral gene flow, rather than random genetic drift.

Reconciling population structure analyses: ancient vs. recent admixture

The integration of multiple analytical approaches reveals a complex demographic history for CHA sheep that requires careful interpretation. While ADMIXTURE analysis suggests genetic contributions from TIB (11.9%) and IDC (13.91%) ancestries, the positive f3 statistics (f3 = 0.004838, Z = 10.960) indicate these breeds did not arise through recent discrete admixture events. This apparent discrepancy reflects fundamental differences in what these methods detect [45, 90].

The ADMIXTURE algorithm identifies optimal clustering solutions based on allele frequency patterns across populations, which can reflect both recent admixture and ancient population structure [39]. The observed ancestry proportions likely represent: (1) shared ancestral populations that contributed to multiple modern breeds, (2) ancient gene flow predating breed formation, or (3) continuous low-level migration over extended periods rather than discrete hybridization events [91].

The Alder based dating analysis provides crucial context, estimating admixture events 52–68 years ago (IDC) and 35–44 years ago (TIB). These relatively recent timeframes, combined with positive f3 statistics, suggest that the genetic signatures detected represent limited gene flow or shared ancestry from related populations rather than extensive hybridization between the specific breeds analyzed [43]. This interpretation is consistent with the geographic isolation of CHA in high-altitude Ladakh, where controlled breeding practices would limit extensive outcrossing with distant breeds. The local ancestry analysis using LAMP further supports this interpretation by identifying specific genomic regions showing excess ancestry from TIB and IDC lineages. The functional enrichment of these regions for traits related to high-altitude adaptation, immune response, and fiber production suggests that observed genetic similarities reflect adaptive introgression of beneficial alleles rather than random hybridization [46, 47].

It is acknowledged that population genomic inferences can, in principle, be influenced by underlying bloodline or family structure effects, particularly when datasets are derived from structured or historically managed populations. However, the samples used in the present study were sourced from a well-curated public repository and represent geographically distinct and unrelated individuals within each breed, minimizing the likelihood of close kinship bias. Moreover, the concordant patterns observed across multiple independent analytical frameworks, including genome-wide diversity indices, PCA, ADMIXTURE, LD decay, and Local ancestry analyses suggest that the reported signals reflect stable population-level genetic structure rather than artefacts driven by specific lineages. Nonetheless, subtle residual bloodline effects cannot be completely excluded and should be considered when interpreting fine-scale ancestry proportions. Future studies incorporating larger sample sizes and explicit pedigree or kinship-based corrections would further refine these estimates.

Mechanistic links between historical admixture and modern Changthangi phenotypes

The temporal sequence of admixture events reveals a strategic genetic architecture underlying CHA’s superior trait. The earlier IDC introgression (52–68 years ago) established foundational thermotolerance mechanisms through heat shock proteins (HSPA1A) and oxidative stress defense systems (SOD2), enabling survival in Ladakh’s extreme temperature fluctuations (-40 °C to + 35 °C) [85] (Fig. 16). Subsequently, Tibetan gene flow (35–44 years ago) introduced specialized high-altitude adaptations, particularly hypoxia-inducible factor regulation (EGLN2) and enhanced glucose metabolism (HK1, HKDC1), optimizing oxygen utilization at altitudes exceeding 16,000 feet [92, 93] (Fig. 17).

The synergistic effect of these temporal introgressions explains CHA’s unique position as the world’s finest Pashmina producer. IDC derived follicle development genes (PI16, EDN1) established the structural framework for fine fiber production, while Tibetan derived metabolic genes (MVD, sphingolipid pathway) enabled the lipid biosynthesis required for cashmere’s characteristic softness and thermal insulation properties. This dual-ancestry contribution results in fibers averaging 12–16 microns diameter—significantly finer than other cashmere breeds (18–22 microns) and approaching the theoretical minimum for mammalian hair [56].

OAR 14 (62.50 Mb, 960 SNPs): Metabolic Adaptation Hub, this region represents the largest TIB ancestry contribution and contains critical genes for high-altitude metabolic adaptation. EGLN2 (Egl-9 Family Hypoxia Inducible Factor 2) encodes a prolyl hydroxylase that directly regulates HIF-1α degradation under normoxic conditions, making it a master regulator of cellular oxygen sensing [94]. At high altitudes, EGLN2 variants allow fine-tuned hypoxia responses without triggering excessive erythropoiesis, preventing altitude sickness while maintaining oxygen delivery. HK1 and HKDC1 (hexokinase genes) catalyze the rate-limiting step of glycolysis, critical for maintaining glucose metabolism under hypoxic conditions where oxidative phosphorylation becomes less efficient [95]. These genes cluster within a 15 Mb region showing near complete TIB ancestry (> 95%), indicating strong positive selection.

OAR 26 (43.83 Mb, 757 SNPs): Immune-Metabolic Integration, this region shows the second highest TIB contribution and contains genes bridging immune function and metabolic adaptation. MVD (mevalonate diphosphate decarboxylase) is essential for cholesterol biosynthesis and isoprenoid production, directly affecting cell membrane composition and hormone synthesis under temperature extremes [96]. NOD2 provides pathogen recognition crucial for high-altitude environments where pathogen diversity varies dramatically with elevation, while PIK3R2 regulates PI3K-Akt signalling, integrating growth, survival, and metabolic signals [97]. The co-localization of these functionally related genes suggests this region underwent coordinated selection for integrated physiological responses.

OAR 25 (34.70 Mb): Cellular protection network, the third largest TIB contribution centres on cellular protection mechanisms. RBX1 and EP300 form a regulatory network controlling protein degradation and chromatin remodelling, essential for managing cellular stress under extreme conditions [98, 99]. INSR (insulin receptor) variants in this region likely contribute to metabolic efficiency under nutrientlimited conditions typical of high-altitude pastoral systems [100, 101].

These three chromosomal regions account for 58% of total TIB ancestry contribution (140.03 Mb of 242.5 Mb total) and demonstrate coordinated adaptive responses targeting hypoxia tolerance, immune function, and metabolic efficiency—the three primary physiological challenges of extreme high-altitude environments (S2 and S3).

Integrated adaptive landscape of Changthangi sheep

High-altitude physiological adaptation: Tibetan-derived variants in hypoxia response (EGLN2, HIF pathway) and metabolic efficiency (HK1, HKDC1, mTOR pathway) genes provide the physiological foundation for thriving at extreme altitudes. These findings parallel high-altitude adaptation signatures identified in other Himalayan species [101–103]. Superior fiber production: Both Tibetan and IDC ancestries contribute to wool quality enhancement through different mechanisms—Tibetan variants affect follicle morphogenesis (MAP3K7, EDN1), while IDC variants influence fibre structure and thermal properties (PI16, HSPA1A). This dual contribution explains CHA’s unique position as the premier Pashmina producer globally (S4 and S5).

Environmental resilience: The enrichment of stress response (HSPA1A, SOD2) and immune function genes (NOD2, MSR1, AIF1) from both ancestral sources creates a robust defense system against the multiple stressors of high-altitude environments, including hypoxia, temperature extremes, and pathogen variability.

Support for the adaptive relevance of the identified ancestry-enriched regions is further strengthened by functional annotation and gene ontology enrichment analyses. Several genes located within significant local ancestry segments are functionally linked to hypoxia response, metabolic regulation, and cellular stress tolerance. Notably, TIB-enriched regions harbor key hypoxia-related and metabolic genes such as EGLN2, HK1, and HKDC1, which are involved in oxygen sensing and glycolytic regulation under low-oxygen conditions. Additional genes including MVD, PIK3R2, and INSR are associated with lipid metabolism and energy homeostasis, processes critical for survival in high-altitude environments. IDC–derived ancestry regions were enriched for genes related to stress response and fibre biology, including HSPA1A and SOD2, which play roles in cellular protection against thermal and oxidative stress, as well as PI16 and EDN1, which have been implicated in skin structure and follicle development. Immune-related genes such as NOD2 and MSR1 were also detected within ancestry-deviated regions, suggesting a contribution to pathogen defense under extreme environmental conditions. Collectively, the functional coherence of these genes and their enrichment in biologically relevant pathways supports the interpretation that the observed ancestry deviations reflect adaptive introgression rather than neutral population structure.

Conservation and economic implications

The genetic architecture revealed here has immediate conservation and economic implications. CHA’s adaptive introgression events created a breed generating US$200–400 million annually in Pashmina trade, supporting > 200,000 pastoral families across Ladakh [56]. However, the elevated inbreeding coefficients (FROH = 0.0662) indicate genetic erosion threatening this economic resource. Conservation strategies must maintain the critical TIB-IDC ancestry balance while expanding the breeding population to reduce inbreeding. The locus-specific ancestry maps provide genomic targets for selective breeding programs aimed at preserving adaptive variants while increasing overall genetic diversity.

Study limitations and future directions

While the WIDDE dataset provides robust and standardized genomic data, several limitations should be acknowledged. The sample sizes, though adequate for population genomic analyses [20], represent a subset of the total genetic diversity within each breed. Future studies would benefit from expanded sampling across broader geographic ranges, particularly for CHA sheep from different valleys within Ladakh. Additionally, while the OvineSNP50 array provides genome-wide coverage, whole-genome sequencing approaches could reveal structural variations and rare alleles not captured by SNP arrays [28]. The temporal aspects of sample collection (spanning multiple years in WIDDE) may introduce minor temporal genetic drift effects, though these are likely minimal given the time scales involved. Despite these limitations, the current study provides the first comprehensive analysis of locus-specific ancestry in CHA sheep, establishing a foundation for future genomic investigations. The identification of adaptive admixture signals offers valuable insights for conservation planning and breeding programs, while the methodological framework developed here can be applied to other high-altitude adapted livestock breeds globally.

Conclusion

This study provides the first comprehensive genomic characterization of CHA sheep, revealing a sophisticated genetic architecture shaped by strategic adaptive introgression events that directly underpin the breed’s exceptional high-altitude performance and superior Pashmina production. Our integrated population genomic analysis demonstrates how temporal admixture events created a uniquely adapted livestock breed through the synergistic combination of Tibetan (11.9%) and IDC (13.91%) ancestries. The temporal sequence of introgression reveals a two-phase adaptive strategy: earlier IDC gene flow (52–68 years ago) established foundational thermotolerance through heat shock proteins (HSPA1A) and oxidative stress defense systems (SOD2), enabling survival in Ladakh’s extreme temperature fluctuations (-40 °C to + 35 °C), while subsequent Tibetan introgression (35–44 years ago) introduced specialized high-altitude adaptations, particularly hypoxia response genes (EGLN2) and enhanced metabolic efficiency (HK1, HKDC1), optimizing oxygen utilization at altitudes exceeding 16,000 feet. This dual-ancestry contribution produces quantifiable phenotypic advantages: CHA sheep maintain 85–90% oxygen saturation at altitudes where other breeds experience 60–70%, while producing Pashmina fibers averaging 12–16 microns diameter—significantly finer than competing breeds (18–22 microns) and commanding 300–500% higher market values. Despite their adaptive success, elevated inbreeding coefficients (FROH = 0.0662) indicate genetic erosion threatening long-term viability, necessitating urgent conservation strategies that utilize the locus-specific ancestry maps generated here as precise genomic targets for selective breeding programs. These findings establish the mechanistic foundation for CHA’s dual importance as both a genetic reservoir for high-altitude adaptation research and an economically critical breed supporting the global Pashmina industry, while providing a framework for evidence-based conservation of high-altitude livestock breeds worldwide.
