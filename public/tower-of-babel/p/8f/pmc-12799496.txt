A Global Survey of Follow Up Imaging After EVAR Reminds Us That We Are Still Speaking Different Languages
Authors: Ruth A Benson
Published: 2025

The FOREVAR Survey1 reached surgeons and radiologists in 65 countries via their English language survey. This breadth is notable, a testament to our colleagues in the European Vascular Research Collaborative, with a snapshot capturing our ongoing reliance on protocols alongside the variation in those protocols required to adapt to real world practice.

Endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR), alongside its complex (cEVAR) and thoracic (TEVAR) cousins, remains a transformative advance in our ability to treat the spectrum of dilating aortic disease with lower immediate morbidity and mortality rates than their open surgical equivalents.2,3 However, whichever way we look at it, the longevity of these grafts remains below par, reflected in reported reduced long term survival and higher rates of re-intervention due to limb or endograft occlusion, endoleaks and re-intervention in around 20% of patients, and late rupture in around 3.5% at five years.4,5 Although patients should be fully consented for this, the implications and logistics are impossible to fully plan for. Guidelines promote a selective approach to post-operative care, on a foundation of routine follow up, with long term imaging guided by early markers of low complication risk (class 2a level B).6

The authors found consensus amongst participants on the need for surveillance; nearly all respondents (96–98%) reported having a standardised departmental protocol. However, follow up imaging strategies differed markedly, for example: less than 50% of respondents performed imaging pre-discharge.

Ultrasound and computed tomography angiography (CTA) remain the mainstay after EVAR, as has been reported in previous consensus stuies.7 The first year is the most intense for most patients, and in more than half of centres it “never ceases”. Surgeons are encouraged to perform holistic patient care, so it seems like a waste of resources to blindly continue surveillance if a patient is not a candidate for any kind of intervention due to frailty or comorbidities. This is partly because surveillance with CTA (the most frequently used modality here) entails repeated radiation exposure. Consider the current entry age for some aortic screening programmes of 65 years, and the significant accumulation of radiation over a patient's lifetime becomes something to consider. Moreover, high frequency imaging drives healthcare costs, not only in direct expenditures but also through indirect effects such as patient anxiety, time off work, and follow up procedures triggered by false positives or incidental findings. Given the rising prevalence of EVAR worldwide, these resource implications are non-trivial at a population level.

The survey also reports on follow up practices for cEVAR and TEVAR, procedures that address more challenging aneurysm anatomies or thoracic segments. The finding that a greater proportion of patients receive CTA during long term follow up after cEVAR (44–74%) possibly reflects this need for detailed imaging of complex repairs, or perhaps a difference in the nature of patient selection and consent.

One of the benefits of reading this paper is being able to see where our own centres fit into this spectrum. We are all aware that the variation in protocols from centre to centre probably reflects many factors: local resource availability, healthcare cost constraints, patient preference, and patient financial resources. Therefore, a key limitation to this paper is the lack of detail around the nature of centres (urban or rural), secondary or tertiary), their economic global standing, and the nature of healthcare provision (public, private, or mixed care).

Can this study be used to guide a centre's ongoing surveillance practice? There are a few outstanding questions, including how successful centres' post-operative surveillance programmes are, and what proportion of patients complete or remain on surveillance post-operatively. The apparently low rates of adherence to the European Journal of Vascular and Endovascular Surgery's suggested algorithm might also lead to a pause for thought, and not for the first time.7,8 While nearly universal adoption of some version of a surveillance protocol affirms universal recognition of the risks of late complications, the disparity in imaging modality use, frequency, and duration, raises concerns about patient experiences of radiation exposure, impact of patient born cost (depending on the health system), and other unreported patient related outcomes.9

Emerging technologies (such as low dose CT protocols, contrast enhanced ultrasound, or advanced magnetic resonance imaging sequences) could identify superior strategies for younger patients; however, we must accept that these techniques require expertise and often develop in departments with resources that support early adoption of technologies. Additionally, patient preferences and quality of life impacts of intensive imaging regimens warrant investigation, which may signal a return to using Xray and ultrasound, even in the setting of more complex grafts; of course many centres never abandoned the practice.

Moving forward, developing evidence based, patient centred, and resource conscious guidelines will be the challenge for those performing ever growing numbers of endovascular aortic repair.
