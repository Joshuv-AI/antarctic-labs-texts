Harnessing 50 years of tick population genetics: Choosing the right molecular tool for contemporary research
Authors: Xavier Barton, Joseph B Fontaine, Shanan S Tobe, Charlotte L Oskam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract Ticks are ectoparasites of major medical, veterinary and ecological importance, transmitting a wide range of pathogens to humans, companion animals and livestock. Understanding the population structure of ticks is essential for uncovering patterns of pathogen transmission, and population genetics provides a powerful method for this purpose.Tick population studies are uniquely challenging as their biology is shaped by complex interactions between hosts, microbiome and environmental factors. The choice of population genetic tools is crucial, as different methods offer varying levels of cost, throughput, resolution and accessibility, which can significantly influence the quality and scope of results.This review traces the evolution of molecular tools in tick population genetics, from early allozyme electrophoresis in the 1970s to advanced whole genome sequencing (WGS) technologies. It critically evaluates key methodologies, including allozyme electrophoresis, random amplified polymorphic DNA (RAPD), microsatellites (STRs), amplified fragment length polymorphisms (AFLP), sequence typing, restriction‐site associated DNA sequencing (RADseq) and WGS, highlighting their strengths, limitations and applications.By offering a practical guide to these tools, this review helps researchers select the most appropriate methods for their studies and allows interpretation of results from older tools in the context of modern research. Sequence typing and RADseq currently provide the best balance of cost and practicality, while WGS has great potential once sequencing costs decline. This resource empowers researchers to make informed decisions, maximise the impact of their work and gain deeper insights into disease vector population structure. Keywords: climate change, ecology, molecular markers, population structure, sequencing tools, vectors

THE VALUE OF POPULATION GENETICS

Population genetics is a valuable method for understanding the genetic relationships within and between populations of organisms. By examining genetic structure, researchers can infer patterns of relatedness and gene flow which, when combined with geographic (Benham et al., 2021), environmental (Raghavan et al., 2021), temporal (Tran et al., 2021) and anthropogenic factors (Multini et al., 2020), reveal drivers shaping population structure.

One practical application of population genetics is the study of disease vectors and the pathogens they transmit. By providing insights into genetic variability within vector populations, population genetics can assess the potential of vectors to invade new ecosystems (McGaughran et al., 2024), introducing novel pathogens or increasing their capacity to vector diseases within their current range (Cator et al., 2020). As anthropogenic factors such as land use and climate continue to shift, understanding population structure can aid in predicting how easily vector populations may adapt to new habitats, influencing pathogen transmission and the ecological impact of invasive species (Arnott et al., 2012).

However, the rapid evolution of population genetic tools (Figure 1) necessitates periodic reviews to guide researchers. With a wide range of both legacy and modern tools available, each offering distinct trade‐offs in scope, cost, ease of use and time efficiency, navigating these choices can be overwhelming for both researchers new to the field of population genetics and experienced researchers alike. While older tools may become obsolete as new methods emerge, understanding the historical context and significance of these tools is essential for interpreting literature and comparing findings across studies. Moreover, familiarity with both outdated and contemporary methods helps researchers select the most appropriate tools for their specific research objectives, avoiding inefficiencies and ensuring that chosen approaches align with the desired outcomes of a project.

Therefore, in this review, we describe and evaluate frequently used molecular population genetic tools developed over the past 50 years, examining each based on key criteria such as cost, reproducibility, resolution and throughput, while also underscoring their historical relevance. Our comprehensive evaluation provides a summary of each tool's strengths and limitations, equipping researchers to make informed choices suited to their specific project needs and to better interpret the literature.

TICKS AND THEIR IMPACTS

Ticks are hematophagous ectoparasites belonging to the class Arachnida and are found on every continent. They belong to the Order Ixodida, which includes hard ticks (Ixodidae), soft ticks (Argasidae) and a monotypic family (Nuttalliellidae). There are 742 recognised species of Ixodidae and at least 193 recognised species of Argasidae globally (Guglielmone et al., 2020).

Hard ticks have a four‐stage life cycle, consisting of distinct on‐host and off‐host periods. The life cycle strategies of hard ticks also vary by species, with some completing their development on a single host (one‐host ticks), while others require two or three different hosts (two‐host and three‐host ticks) to progress through their stages. Overall, these factors contribute to intricate and dynamic interactions between ticks, hosts, microbes and the environment, forming a complex mechanism where each component can influence the others in multifaceted ways (Figure 2).

Key Figure

The complex interactions between factors that affect ticks and their lifecycle. Understanding these interactions is crucial for developing effective strategies for tick control and disease prevention. Population genetics can be a highly effective tool to measure and explain which interactions are resulting in an existing population structure.

Lifecycle—The life cycle of ixodid (hard) ticks involves four instars: Egg, larva, nymph and adult. After hatching, ticks go through each stage by feeding on blood from vertebrate hosts. Hard ticks can follow a one, two or three host life cycle in which a host may be changed between each moult (Troughton & Levin, 2007).

Tick populations—Tick populations refer to the overall abundance and distribution of ticks within a given environment. Factors such as the availability of hosts and environmental conditions influence tick population dynamics.

Climate change—Anthropogenic climate change can significantly impact tick ecology. Alterations in conditions such as temperature and precipitation can affect tick survival, reproduction and geographic distribution (Backus et al., 2021; Ogden et al., 2021). Climate change can extend peak tick season and expand their habitat range.

Land use—Changes in land use practices, such as deforestation, urbanisation and agriculture expansion, can influence tick populations. These land use changes cause altered habitats, reduced natural hosts and increase human–animal interactions which impact tick abundance, distribution and potential for pathogen transmission (Diuk‐Wasser et al., 2021; Hansford et al., 2023).

Livestock—Livestock, including cattle, sheep and poultry, serve as hosts for ticks. Ticks feed on livestock, affecting animal health and productivity. Livestock management practices can influence tick exposure and transmission of tick‐borne diseases. Additionally, movement of livestock in the environment through trade results in tick populations mixing and increased pathogen transmission (Foughali et al., 2021; Saleem et al., 2019).

Wildlife—Wildlife are key hosts for ticks and are reservoirs for pathogens they transmit, providing essential blood meals for tick survival and reproduction and allowing pathogen populations to be maintained (Egan et al., 2020, 2021). The movement and behaviour of wildlife can influence tick distribution and the spread of pathogens. Agricultural lands can also amplify this role by serving as interfaces where wildlife and livestock interact, facilitating further pathogen maintenance and transmission.

Companion animals—Companion animals, such as dogs and cats, can be hosts for ticks (Greay et al., 2016). Ticks can attach to pets during outdoor activities with people, increasing the risk of tick‐borne pathogen transmission to both animals and humans (Irwin & Beadle, 2022).

People—Humans can inadvertently become hosts for ticks during outdoor activities. During tick feeding, pathogens can be transmitted, which may lead to diseases, including Lyme disease, babesiosis and anaplasmosis (Mac et al., 2019; Madison‐Antenucci et al., 2020). Human behaviour, such as recreational activities and awareness of preventive measures, can influence tick exposure and pathogen transmission. Normally, people are considered an end‐stage host for ticks, as ticks are not able to take on a full blood meal before being discovered and removed.

Pathogens—Ticks transmit a wide variety of pathogens that can cause diseases in human and animal hosts. Pathogens transmitted by ticks include bacteria (Marques et al., 2021), viruses (Jenkins et al., 2022) and hemoprotozoa (Hussain et al., 2022). Tick populations and their interaction with hosts contribute to the spread of these pathogens.

Endosymbionts—Tick endosymbionts are microorganisms that live within ticks and can have symbiotic relationships with them. These microorganisms may influence tick biology, development and their ability to transmit pathogens.

Ticks pose a significant disease risk to humans, livestock, companion animals and wildlife, primarily due to pathogens they carry (i.e. bacteria, viruses and protozoan parasites). Additionally, ticks are also associated with non‐pathogenic conditions (i.e. alpha‐gal allergy and tick paralysis) (van Nunen, 2015). Due to the risks they pose, ticks have driven the growth of a billion‐dollar industry focused on both preventive measures (e.g. biosecurity and prophylactic treatments) and reactive care (e.g. treatments for tick‐borne diseases and infestations) for both humans and animals (Oskam et al., 2021).

Lyme disease, caused by the bacterial complex Borrelia burgdorferi sensu lato, is among the most recognised tick‐borne diseases (TBD) affecting humans, particularly in North America and Europe. The disease can present in two stages. In the acute stage, it often presents with erythema migrans (a characteristic rash) and influenza‐like symptoms (Steere et al., 2016). However, challenges arise in diagnosing a chronic stage due to indeterminate symptoms, such as persistent fatigue, myalgia and neurological impairment (Lee et al., 2023). The primary vectors for B. burgdorferi include Ixodes scapularis and Ixodes pacificus in North America and Ixodes ricinus and Ixodes persulcatus in Europe and Asia (Marques et al., 2021). These tick species have been observed to expand their geographical range, coinciding with an increase in the incidence of Lyme disease and are predicted to expand further (Robinson et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022). This growing prevalence highlights the disease's significant public health burden (Bobe et al., 2021). In the United States alone, the financial burden associated with Lyme disease is estimated to range between $345 and $968 million USD annually (Hook et al., 2022).

In the livestock industry, tick infestations are a major concern due to associated commercial losses from disease treatment, prevention and environmental tick control. One emerging TBD is bovine anaemia due to Theileria orientalis group (BATOG), caused by the intracellular hemoprotozoan parasite Theileria orientalis, resulting in cattle with severe anaemia which can lead to death (Forshaw et al., 2020). At this time, there are few preventative measures that can be taken; these are limited to acaricide treatments and biosecurity practices. As well as the limited preventative measures, treatments for sick animals, such as blood transfusions, are costly, and drugs such as imidocarb, erythromycin and oxytetracycline are ineffective (Jenkins, 2018). The outcome of this poorly managed disease results in an estimated $20 million dollars of cost per year to the Australian cattle industry (Lane et al., 2015; Perera et al., 2014). Emerging diseases such as BATOG highlight the need for effective tick and TBD monitoring.

Ticks provide a compelling case study for investigating advancements and utilisation of population genetic tools. Their intricate life history, involving feeding on multiple moving hosts, prolonged life cycles spanning multiple years and their ability to transmit pathogenic diseases to humans and animals, render ticks an invaluable focal point for assessing progress made in population genetic tool development.

POPULATION STRUCTURE IN THE FACE OF ENVIRONMENTAL SHIFTS

Studies show that interactions between people and ticks have increased, partly due to landscape changes, with human activity further encroaching on natural habitats, resulting in an increased incidence of TBDs (Stone et al., 2017). Climate change has also impacted ticks, affecting their host range, reproduction and behaviour. Furthermore, as livestock movements increase in scale, so do the opportunities for ticks to disperse across regions. In this section, we explore the ways in which changing landscapes, climate change and livestock movements are affecting tick populations and the spread of TBDs.

Climate change

Global warming continues to accelerate (Masson‐Delmotte et al., 2021), leading to a range of direct effects, such as heatwaves, droughts and flooding, as well as indirect effects including increased wildfire prevalence and pathogen outbreaks (Pörtner & Roberts, 2022). These effects, combined with ongoing anthropogenic impacts such as habitat fragmentation and human population expansion (Potapov et al., 2017; Thomson et al., 2022), are intensifying pressures on ecosystems and biodiversity (Ruthrof et al., 2018). As a result, there is increasing contact between humans, their companion animals and wildlife (both native and introduced). This increased interaction also extends to pathogens within wildlife reservoirs (Byers, 2021; Rocklöv & Dubrow, 2020; Semenza & Suk, 2018). These indirect effects of climate change and their subtle interactions are broadly underappreciated but will be the source of major economic costs and conservation challenges in the coming years (Pörtner & Roberts, 2022).

Climate change impacts tick populations by affecting both ticks and their hosts. Increased precipitation and warming temperatures enhance tick survival and reproduction, while changes in host survival and movement patterns enable ticks to expand their range. Warmer local climates have allowed I. ricinus to survive in areas previously too cold, contributing to higher incidences of the viral disease, tick‐borne encephalitis (Lindgren & Gustafson, 2001). Additionally, milder winters may extend the breeding period for ticks, reducing winter mortality (Githeko et al., 2000). In tropical climates, conditions have been linked to more generations of Rhipicephalus (Boophilus) microplus per year, signalling potential for increased abundance and range expansion as warming continues (Cruz et al., 2020). These findings highlight an urgent need to monitor tick populations closely, as climate change continues to shape population dynamics and, consequently, the potential spread of tick‐borne diseases.

Livestock movements

Ticks are a significant concern for the livestock industry due to their impact on animal health and productivity. Infestations can result in production losses, including reduced body weight, increased disease treatment costs and hide damage (Saleem et al., 2019). Furthermore, the movement of livestock across the globe poses a risk to biosecurity standards, with numerous instances of livestock trade leading to uncontrolled tick dispersal (Al‐Deeb & Muzaffar, 2020; Busch et al., 2014; Kanduma et al., 2016).

A pertinent example is of Haemaphysalis longicornis, first described by Louis Georges Neumann in 1901 and commonly referred to as the Asian long‐horned tick, the bush tick in Australia and the New Zealand cattle tick in New Zealand (Heath, 2016; Neumann, 1901). H. longicornis is native to east and central Asia and can be found in China, Japan, North and South Korea and southern Russia.

From its original distribution, H. longicornis has expanded in range to many other regions, including Australia, New Zealand and most recently, the United States of America, following a recent incursion event in 2017 (Rainey et al., 2018). H. longicornis is hypothesised to have entered Australia through the movement of cattle from northern Japan, where it then spread from Australia to New Zealand (Hoogstraal et al., 1968). However, despite the increasing number of publications focusing on H. longicornis, there is a lack of modern research on the dispersal pathways of this tick species throughout the globe, particularly utilising molecular population genetic techniques (Egizi et al., 2020).

The recent invasion of H. longicornis into North America has raised significant attention towards this tick species (Myers & Scimeca, 2024; Rainey et al., 2018). Based on data collected from hosts and tick collections in the area, it appears that the species is starting to disperse beyond its initial point of discovery in New Jersey and can now be found in many states (Egizi et al., 2020). This recent invasion has revealed that the species is parasitising a range of animals including Ovis aries (sheep), Odocoileus virginianus (white‐tailed deer), Procyon lotor (raccoons) and Didelphis virginiana (Virginia opossums) (Tufts et al., 2019, 2021). The example of the H. longicornis ongoing global spread is a growing concern for ecologists and biosecurity authorities, given its potential to transmit pathogens to naïve human and domestic animal populations. Integrating molecular population genetic tools into studies of these invasions can provide valuable insights into transmission pathways. This information is essential for developing effective intervention strategies, such as establishing barriers at ports, which are key incursion sites. Ultimately, this underscores the critical need for enhanced tick population monitoring and proactive management strategies.

Human environmental disturbance

Increasing anthropogenic activities in natural habitats are altering the populations and distribution of both ticks and their hosts. Human actions such as deforestation, urbanisation, mining and pollution lead to destruction and fragmentation of natural landscapes. However, the impacts of these changes on tick populations remain poorly explored, with the available studies concentrated in the Americas and Europe.

Land clearing is one example of how land use changes can affect tick populations. As suburban developments increase along an urban–wildland interface, proximity between people and wildlife diminishes, potentially increasing the likelihood of ticks attaching to humans and companion animals (MacDonald et al., 2020). Additionally, as urban expansion forces many wildlife hosts into designated reserves, this creates more opportunities for ticks to encounter hosts. This pattern is illustrated by a study on Amblyomma sculptum abundance on Hydrochoerus hydrochaeris (capybaras), which found higher tick densities on capybaras in proximity to urban environments compared to those further away (Dias et al., 2020).

Several studies have examined the impact of controlled burning practices and wildfires on tick populations. Gleim et al. (2019) found that large‐scale burns significantly reduced encounters with ticks, leading to fewer ticks carrying pathogenic bacteria. Factors such as changes in microclimate and host movements likely influenced this reduction. Tick levels remained lower for up to 2 years after the fire (Gleim et al., 2019). Similarly, Mather et al. (1993) observed a decline in the number of nymphal Ixodes dammini ticks following burning. However, this reduction did not necessarily correlate with a decreased likelihood of encountering ticks carrying Borrelia burgdorferi. This suggests that the displacement of host species due to fire plays a significant role in determining the effectiveness of burning in reducing disease transmission by ticks (Mather et al., 1993). Integrating population genetics into studies like these could help address knowledge gaps about how tick populations are shifting in response to burning practices, providing deeper insights into the underlying causes of these effects.

As evidence grows that changes in climate and the environment affect tick populations, often leading to increased tick activity and survival, monitoring these populations over time, alongside field‐based data collection, will provide valuable insights into the most effective ways to control ticks and mitigate potential disease outbreaks.

A TIMELINE OF WIDELY USED MOLECULAR POPULATION GENETIC TOOLS

In the field of population genetics, numerous methods have been employed to study genetic variation among individuals and populations. Some of these techniques, such as allozyme electrophoresis, random amplified polymorphic DNA (RAPD), microsatellite markers, amplified fragment length polymorphism (AFLP), sequence typing, restriction‐site associated DNA sequencing (RAD‐seq) and whole genome sequencing (WGS), have been commonly used both historically and in current research (Figure 1). Each of these methods has its own advantages and disadvantages, offering varying levels of resolution, throughput, cost‐effectiveness (Table 1) and ease of workflow (Figure 3). The following section will explore how each method works to produce results, their historical applications and their respective advantages and limitations. This review aims to assist researchers in selecting the most appropriate tools for their projects and in interpreting findings derived from previously utilised methodologies.

Allozyme electrophoresis

Allozyme electrophoresis is a technique that uses gel electrophoresis to observe variation in protein expression between individuals. This is performed by homogenising tissue with extraction buffer and using an enzyme stain to identify changes in band size, travel and number of bands on a gel caused by differences in protein size and charge.

The use of allozyme electrophoresis for population structure studies began in the 1970s, with the technique being applied to laboratory studies on Drosophila (Kojima et al., 1970) and subsequently on field studies on plants (Efron et al., 1973), reptiles (Taylor & Gorman, 1975) and other insects (Jelnes, 1975).

This technique was first applied to tick population structure studies in 1976, where Bothriocroton hydrosauri from two climatically similar regions located 280 km apart in South Australia were investigated. Using allozyme electrophoresis, the study provided evidence suggesting that the gene flow between B. hydrosauri populations was larger than previously predicted based on observational data alone (Bull & Sara, 1976).

Other studies using allozyme electrophoresis on ticks have revealed varying levels of population differentiation. For instance, while Delaye et al. (1997) found little to no divergence in I. ricinus populations in Switzerland, Kain et al. (1997) identified noticeable geographical population differentiation in I. pacificus in California, United States.

Allozyme electrophoresis emerged as a more time‐efficient alternative to the traditional capture–mark–release method for studying populations in natural environments. Unlike the labour‐intensive and costly process of capturing, marking, releasing and recapturing organisms to generate results, allozyme electrophoresis bypassed these steps by directly analysing a captured organism. This method also enabled the study of organisms that could not undergo the capture–release process, where recapture opportunities are unlikely due to the organism being too small or the population density too high.

The limitations of allozyme electrophoresis have led to its replacement by DNA‐based methods. These limitations include its low sensitivity to variation between individuals, often necessitating multiple experiments to assess different loci, which can waste valuable specimens, resources and lower throughput (Rahman et al., 2013). Delaye et al. (1997) emphasised that more powerful genetic markers are better suited for identifying population differentiation. Additionally, as the analysis focuses on actively expressed proteins, the results are susceptible to selection pressures (Parker et al., 1998). Finally, this technique depends on the target enzymes remaining active for effective staining, which can be challenging due to the need to keep specimens fresh until snap freezing (Richardson et al., 1986). Moreover, inconsistent enzyme expression by the organism can lead to unreliable outcomes, further limiting the method's effectiveness.

While allozyme electrophoresis provided an innovative and efficient method for studying population structure in its time, its limitations in sensitivity, consistency and scope have rendered it largely obsolete in favour of the rapidly developing DNA‐based techniques of the time. Despite its historical significance and early contributions to understanding individual variation, the challenges associated with wet‐lab workflow and limited resolution highlight the need for more advanced methods to address the complexities of population genetics in modern research.

Random amplified polymorphic DNA

Random amplified polymorphic DNA (RAPD) is a population genetic technique that uses a multiplex of short (~10 bp) generalised PCR primers which arbitrarily amplify segments of DNA (Lynch & Milligan, 1994). This amplification of random DNA fragments creates a unique ‘fingerprint’ of amplification sites, which may change depending on the organism or individual and will become more differentiated with more taxonomically distant organisms.

The RAPD methodology gained traction in the early 1990s alongside the increasing adoption of PCR technology (Williams et al., 1990). One of the first applications with ticks was described in a short communication by Hernandez et al. (1998), where RAPD was used to genetically fingerprint different strains of R. microplus. This approach facilitated the detection of acaricide resistance by identifying distinct genetic profiles within the species. Compared to traditional methods that required labour‐intensive pesticide dose–response experiments on tick populations, RAPD offered a more efficient alternative.

Use of RAPD grew rapidly as it required little to no a priori knowledge of the target organism's genomic content, making it broadly accessible before the advent of inexpensive and more reliable sequencing technologies like Sanger sequencing (Black & DuTeau, 1997). Additionally, because the primers bind indiscriminately throughout the genome, this technique generates a random sample of variation sites across the genome with minimal bias.

RAPD detects variation through multiple mechanisms, which are reflected as differences in band patterns on gel electrophoresis. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) can affect primer binding, either by preventing binding and causing the absence of a DNA fragment or slightly shifting binding sites to alter band size. Deletions of entire priming sites can also inhibit primer binding, leading to the absence of specific bands. Additionally, insertions or deletions between primer sites can result in changes to band size, creating further variation in the gel patterns.

Several other studies have employed RAPD to assess genetic diversity of ticks with differing results. Overall, most studies showed high levels of genetic diversity within tested samples (Paulauskas et al., 2006; Radzijevskaja et al., 2005; Velayutham et al., 2012). While these studies all report high genetic diversity, reliance on random primer binding can introduce methodological biases based on variations in PCR experiments, potentially leading to overestimation of genetic variation. Some tick species have been shown to exhibit high genetic variability, while others demonstrate more moderate levels (Ogrzewalska et al., 2016; Tian et al., 2022). However, the consistent trend of RAPD studies reporting relatively high diversity raises concerns about the accuracy of these findings. This pattern may indicate inherent issues with the RAPD methodology, as the random primer binding approach might exaggerate genetic variability, regardless of the true underlying diversity.

Although an RAPD experiment is simple and relatively inexpensive (Table 1) to perform and develop, there are concerns about the reproducibility of the method. As the method depends on PCR primers binding or not binding to create a unique profile, slight variances in PCR conditions and reagents may lead to differences in how the primers bind and therefore what profiles are created (Hadrys et al., 1992; Micheli et al., 1994). As well as this, markers may overlap due to travelling the same distance on a gel, meaning that present genetic variances may not be visible. Finally, as the primers bind randomly to any DNA in the sample, microbial, host or environmental contaminations in the DNA may lead to the presence of bands that would not be present in the target organism. Due to the increase in more accurate and reliable genetic profiling methods, the use of the RAPD technique has largely been superseded.

Microsatellite markers

Microsatellites or short tandem repeats (STRs) are short DNA motifs (usually less than 8 bp) that repeat many times within sections of non‐coding DNA. During DNA replication, repeats can be added or lost from the microsatellite region due to polymerase slippage, and as they do not code for genes there are no adverse effects (Selkoe & Toonen, 2006). The mutation rate of a microsatellite region is relatively higher than coding regions of DNA and is in the scale of 10−2 and 10−6 mutations per locus per generation (Schlötterer, 2000).

Microsatellite markers are detected using primer sequences designed to target specific STR regions within a genome. These STR regions, flanked by the primers, are amplified via PCR, and the resulting products can be visualised using gel electrophoresis or, more commonly, capillary electrophoresis (CE), to measure the fragment size or determine the sequence. Variation is detected as differences in band size, which reflect differences in STR length (number of copies of the STR) between individuals (Selkoe & Toonen, 2006). Since microsatellites are located in non‐coding regions and exhibit higher mutation rates, their lengths can vary significantly among individuals and populations. The greater the genetic distance between individuals or populations, the larger the differences in STR lengths are likely to be (Schlötterer, 2000).

The development of PCR in the mid‐1980s significantly advanced the use of DNA‐based population genetic markers by allowing small amounts of DNA to be quickly and efficiently amplified (Mullis & Faloona, 1987; Saiki et al., 1988). This breakthrough facilitated the use of microsatellites as genetic markers, establishing them as an essential tool for studying genetic variation due to their high polymorphism (Ashley & Dow, 1994).

Microsatellite markers have been widely used in tick population genetic research since the early 2000s. One of the first applications was to differentiate strains of R. microplus, comparing populations from South America and Australia to understand evolutionary divergence and its implications for the development of tick vaccines and acaricides (de la Fuente et al., 2000). Other studies have applied microsatellites to investigate speciation events (Labruna et al., 2009), divergence related to host use (Kempf et al., 2009; McCoy et al., 2003) and changes in tick populations across geographic regions and over time (Dharmarajan et al., 2010).

Microsatellite markers offer significant advantages due to the simplicity and efficiency of experimentation. They rely on widely accessible and cost‐effective PCR technology, which is quick to perform (typically within a day), requires only a small cost (Table 1) and produces consistent, replicable results. Additionally, microsatellite markers have high information content because of their high mutation rates. This makes them particularly valuable for detecting recent evolutionary events, such as population bottlenecks or vector movements, due to their high allelic diversity (Chevillon et al., 2013). However, this same property limits their utility for studying more distant evolutionary events, as the rapid mutation of short tandem repeats (STRs) can obscure older genetic relationships (Selkoe & Toonen, 2006).

Despite the simplicity of using microsatellite markers in experiments, their initial development has traditionally been a labour‐intensive process. Early methods for generating these markers involved several steps, including DNA extraction from the target population, random fragment amplification, fragment purification, cloning and sequencing (Gardner et al., 2008; Guzinski et al., 2008; Koffi et al., 2006).

In recent years, the process of developing microsatellite markers has become more efficient due to advances in genomic technologies. Current methods utilise genomic data to detect STR regions and design primers entirely in silico, significantly reducing the time and labour involved (Barton, 2021; Hoffman & Nichols, 2011).

However, a key limitation of microsatellite markers is their high specificity to the species or population for which they were developed. Studies have shown that microsatellites often perform poorly when applied to closely related species, with few markers yielding comparable results to those observed in the original population (Mccoy & Tirard, 2000). While this specificity ensures reliable long‐term use within the target population, it limits the broader applicability of microsatellite markers to other species, reducing their versatility in comparative studies.

While microsatellite markers offer high genetic resolution and have provided significant insights into tick population genetics, their development has historically been labour‐intensive, and their specificity limits broader applicability to other species. Advances in genomic technologies have simplified marker creation, increasing their accessibility and utility. Despite their limitations, microsatellites remain a valuable tool for understanding population dynamics and recent evolutionary changes in target populations.

Amplified fragment length polymorphisms

Amplified fragment length polymorphism (AFLP) is a method for detecting genetic variation that involves digesting the total DNA of a target organism with restriction enzymes, ligating adapters to the resulting fragments and selectively amplifying these fragments based on the number of marker points desired (Vos et al., 1995). The amplified fragments are then analysed using gel electrophoresis, producing a unique banding pattern that can be used to differentiate individuals and populations.

At the time of writing, only one study has employed AFLP to analyse tick population genetics. In 2013, Araya‐Anchetta et al. used AFLP to investigate populations of Dermacentor ticks (predominantly Dermacentor variabilis and Dermacentor andersoni) in North America. The study aimed to assess potential hybridisation events between these species, a hypothesis arising from the western expansion of D. variabilis and its interaction with D. andersoni. Morphological methods alone were insufficient for this analysis, as distinguishing species and hybrids based on physical traits can be challenging, particularly in areas with poor taxonomic keys (Huynh et al., 2021). The use of AFLP provided evidence for hybridisation in regions where the populations overlapped, consistent with observations from laboratory experiments (Araya‐Anchetta et al., 2013).

A key advantage of AFLP is that it does not require prior genetic information about the target organism, making it particularly valuable for non‐model organisms. This allows researchers to efficiently genotype individuals and assess population structure without relying on a reference genome. Additionally, the technique's use of enzymatic digestion to generate DNA fragments provides a random sample of loci across the genome, enabling unbiased variation analysis and reducing dependence on specific genomic regions.

Despite these strengths, AFLP has notable limitations. One significant drawback is its inability to determine heterozygosity, a critical metric for many population genetic studies (Zhang et al., 2014). Furthermore, the decreasing cost of high‐throughput sequencing has rendered gel electrophoresis‐based methods like AFLP less desirable. These methods are inherently more qualitative, providing categorical rather than precise quantitative data. Compared to modern sequencing technologies, AFLP lacks the specificity and resolution necessary for addressing many contemporary genetic research questions, limiting its broader applicability.

Sequence typing

Sequence typing (focusing on a single locus) and multilocus sequence typing (MLST) (focusing on multiple loci), collectively referred to as DNA barcoding, are widely utilised genotyping approaches to detect variation between individuals. These methods involve sequencing specific loci using Sanger sequencing or, now commonly, next‐generation sequencing (NGS). Genetic variation is detected by comparing nucleotide sequences of the selected loci and comparing them across individuals or populations. The difference in the nucleotide sequence indicates how closely related samples may be, with more differences indicating a more distant relationship. The loci used are often conserved regions that facilitate the detection of variation through SNPs. For studies on tick populations, commonly targeted loci are derived from either the nuclear or mitochondrial genomes. Mitochondrial markers often include cytochrome c oxidase I (COI), 12S rRNA (12S) and 16S rRNA (16S) genes, while nuclear markers typically include the internal transcribed spacer 2 (ITS2) and 18S rRNA genes (Evans, 2018).

Sequence typing has been widely employed for its ability to generate a reproducible and standardised catalogue of genetic variation, enabling comparisons across studies, taxa and geographic regions. One of the earliest uses of sequence typing in ticks was by Wesson et al. (1993), who used the ITS1 and ITS2 loci to resolve the taxonomic ambiguity surrounding I. dammini and I. scapularis in North America. By targeting highly variable loci, the researchers confirmed that I. dammini was not a distinct species, demonstrating the method's effectiveness in addressing species‐level questions (Wesson et al., 1993).

More recently, Nadolny et al. (2015) used the mitochondrial 16S gene to study the population structure of Ixodes affinis and Amblyomma maculatum in the mid‐Atlantic region of the United States. The relatively conserved nature of the 16S gene made it well suited for examining population structure on a broad geographic scale, enabling the authors to infer migration patterns and historical population dynamics of these species (Nadolny et al., 2015).

Similarly, Amzati et al. (2018) employed COI and 12S loci to uncover two distinct lineages of Rhipicephalus appendiculatus in the African Great Lakes region. The researchers concluded that one lineage resulted from a recent founder event, while the other reflected a more established population. Their study also revealed significant population differentiation between highland and lowland regions, providing insights into ecological and evolutionary processes shaping tick populations (Amzati et al., 2018).

In another study, Bitencourth et al. (2016) used cytochrome oxidase II (COII) and 12S loci to investigate genetic diversity in A. sculptum populations in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil. Despite identifying high genetic diversity, they observed no spatial population structure, suggesting long‐distance dispersal of ticks facilitated by host movement. This study highlighted the value of these loci for exploring host‐mediated gene flow in tick populations (Bitencourth et al., 2016).

One significant advantage of sequence typing is the ability to design primer sets tailored to target specific genes, which can reliably identify species and even differentiate subspecies or strains (Brophy et al., 2022). These primer sets are designed based on conserved regions of the genome, making them robust tools for long‐term use in population studies. Once established, they provide a highly standardised and reproducible method for assessing population structure on a global scale (Lv et al., 2014). Additionally, as sequence typing utilises either Sanger or NGS technologies, the declining costs of these sequencing methods over the past 10–15 years (Athanasopoulou et al., 2021) have made sequence typing more affordable (Table 1) and accessible to a wider group of researchers.

The use of multiple loci in MLST offers significantly higher resolution compared to single‐locus approaches. This improvement arises from the increased amount of sequence data available for detecting genetic variation. By analysing multiple loci, researchers can achieve more detailed differentiation not only between populations but also within them, providing deeper insights into fine‐scale population structures (Sakamoto et al., 2014).

Additionally, once primer sequences have been developed for genes of interest, the experimental workflow is relatively straightforward. It involves amplifying and sequencing the target loci, followed by in silico comparisons across individuals and populations. This standardised process simplifies cross‐study comparisons and ensures consistent results.

However, sequence typing methods are particularly suited to population genetic studies that address broad‐scale temporal or geographic questions. The relatively low mutation rates of commonly used genes compared to other population genetic markers with higher mutation rates (i.e. microsatellites) limit their ability to detect fine‐scale variation, making these methods more effective for exploring broad patterns of tick dispersal or large‐scale population interactions. For smaller scale studies, population genetic tools that utilise loci with higher mutation rates, such as non‐conserved neutral markers, can provide greater resolution.

A drawback of sequence typing lies in the inherent bias introduced by focusing on highly conserved pre‐selected loci. This standardisation, while useful for reproducibility, may overlook variation in other parts of the genome. To address this limitation, genome‐wide approaches that randomly sample markers across the genome can provide a more comprehensive and unbiased assessment of genetic diversity.

Restriction site‐associated DNA sequencing

Restriction site‐associated DNA sequencing (RADseq) is part of a broader category of techniques known as reduced representation sequencing (RRS). These methods are referred to by various names and forms, including genotype‐by‐sequencing (GBS), RADseq, double digest RADseq (ddRADseq), triple digest RADseq (tdRADseq) and type‐IIB RADseq (2bRADseq), each involving slight variations in methodology. Despite these differences, they all operate on the same principle: using an untargeted subset of the genome to identify SNPs (Andrews et al., 2016; Chambers et al., 2023).

These methods involve the digestion of genomic DNA using one (RADseq), two (ddRADseq) or more (tdRADseq) restriction enzymes. Double and triple digest RADseq typically use enzymes with varying densities of cut sites across the genome, ensuring unique and uniform DNA fragmentation. After digestion, fragments of a selected size (usually 200–300 bp) are ligated with adapters and barcodes, enabling multiplexing for NGS. Once sequencing is completed, SNPs can be identified in the generated catalogue of loci, providing insights into genetic relatedness (Davey & Blaxter, 2010). A newer variation, 2bRAD, simplifies the workflow by eliminating the size selection step. It employs a type IIb restriction enzyme that cuts on both sides of its recognition site, producing uniform fragments. These fragments can be directly ligated with indexed adapters for sequencing, streamlining the laboratory workflow (Chambers et al., 2023).

RADseq, introduced by Baird et al. (2008), represented a significant advancement over earlier microarray‐based RAD methods by incorporating NGS. This innovation made RADseq a powerful tool for studying genetic variation at high resolution unbiasedly across the genome. The adaptability of RADseq has enabled researchers to address complex questions in population genetics, especially for non‐model organisms such as ticks (Baird et al., 2008).

For instance, Lado et al. (2019) revisited previously inferred population structures in D. variabilis using ddRADseq. The high resolution of ddRADseq provided robust evidence for East–West population differentiation, which was consistent across multiple analytical tools. Importantly, ddRADseq enabled the detection of finer scale genetic structure within the Eastern clade, revealing two distinct subgroups that mitochondrial markers had failed to identify. This demonstrated the utility of ddRADseq in uncovering subtle genetic variations, offering a clearer understanding of population structure (Lado et al., 2019).

Similarly, Tietjen et al. (2021) utilised ddRADseq to investigate potential host‐associated differentiation in D. variabilis. While a homogeneous population structure was observed among ticks collected from vegetation, distinct genetic clusters were identified between ticks associated with different host animals. This finding suggested potential host‐associated differentiation within the species. However, the study also highlighted a limitation of RADseq techniques: DNA extracted from whole ticks, including their blood meals, may inadvertently include host or microbial genetic material, complicating the interpretation of the SNP catalogue (Tietjen et al., 2021).

The benefits of RADseq lie in its ability to generate reliable and specific SNP data due to the use of NGS technology. Additionally, because the restriction enzymes used to cut the genomic DNA at random sites, the resulting markers are distributed throughout the genome with minimal bias. Furthermore, as a reduced representation sequencing approach, RADseq keeps costs relatively low (Table 1) by avoiding the need to sequence the entire genome.

However, RADseq does have some limitations. One drawback is the need for an initial development protocol to test which restriction enzymes are most effective for the target organism. While this step adds complexity, it is still relatively minor compared to the challenges of developing some other methods. This issue can also be bypassed with techniques that do not require genome reduction, such as whole genome sequencing. Another limitation is the requirement for high‐quality and sufficient quantities of input DNA to ensure effective enzymatic fragmentation. Degraded or insufficient DNA can negatively impact the efficiency of restriction enzyme digestion and subsequent SNP detection, potentially compromising results (Bayona‐Vásquez et al., 2019).

Whole genome sequencing

Whole genome sequencing (WGS) is a powerful method for sequencing the entire genome of an organism or multiple genomes simultaneously. Using NGS technologies, genomic DNA is fragmented into short regions, typically 150–500 bp in length, which are then sequenced and organised in silico (Park & Kim, 2016). Unlike reduced representation methods such as RADseq, WGS provides comprehensive genomic coverage, making it a versatile tool for studying genetic variation, population structure, and evolutionary history.

Currently, research using WGS in tick population genetics is limited. Poli et al. (2020) utilised WGS to genotype 192 SNPs in the species I. ricinus that had previously been identified using other methods. WGS was chosen for this study because of its ability to validate and expand upon existing SNP data by sequencing the entire genome. This approach ensured that all identified SNPs could be confirmed and accurately placed within the genomic context, which would have been challenging with reduced representation methods like RADseq that only sequence a subset of the genome (Poli et al., 2020).

Schoville et al. (2024) employed a low‐coverage WGS strategy to reduce sequencing costs while still capturing a genome‐wide scope of SNP loci for I. scapularis. This approach was chosen to balance cost efficiency with the need for comprehensive data to assess population structure across large geographic scales. The results were able to demonstrate clear genetic differentiation among specimens collected across multiple states in the eastern United States, revealing evidence of local adaptation and isolation by distance (Schoville et al., 2024).

One significant advantage of WGS is its adaptability to various sequencing platforms. While short‐read technologies like Illumina have traditionally been favoured for their high accuracy, long‐read platforms such as Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) and Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) now offer comparable sequencing quality, broadening the options for genome analysis. Long‐read sequencing is particularly advantageous for tick genomes, which often contain large amounts of repetitive sequences (Guerrero et al., 2010). The longer reads simplify genome assembly, overcoming challenges that arise with short‐read data.

Another benefit of WGS is its ability to generate data without prior knowledge of the target organism's genome. Because the entire genome is sequenced without a target, the resulting data can be used for reference‐based mapping or de novo genome assembly, depending on the study's goals. Furthermore, the genetic information obtained is not limited to population genetics; it can also be repurposed for other applications, such as identifying structural variations, studying functional genomics or detecting associated pathogens.

However, WGS does come with some challenges. The high cost of sequencing (Table 1), particularly for organisms with large or complex genomes, can be a limiting factor. This is compounded by the need for a high sequencing depth to minimise errors; insufficient depth can result in incomplete or unreliable data. Additionally, long read WGS requires high‐quality and sufficient quantities of DNA for successful sequencing and subsequent analysis. The computational resources required for genome assembly, SNP calling, and downstream analyses also add to the overall cost and complexity. Despite these challenges, the decreasing costs of sequencing and advancements in computational tools are steadily increasing the accessibility of WGS.

INTEGRATING CONTEXTUAL DATA FOR COMPREHENSIVE POPULATION GENETIC INSIGHTS

In the era of omics‐based research, understanding the full context of biological samples has become both feasible and essential for understanding population genetic structure. For instance, traditionally, DNA extracted from organisms such as ticks has posed challenges due to contamination from non‐target (non‐tick) DNA, leading to issues such as inhibition or competition during analysis (Reifenberger et al., 2022). However, multifaceted approaches provide an opportunity for a holistic framework, leveraging diverse datasets to gain a comprehensive understanding of an organism's interactions with its internal and external environment. This integrative strategy not only elucidates current population structure but also enables more accurate assessments of why a population is structured how it is and how that might change in the future.

To achieve a comprehensive understanding, researchers can employ complementary techniques that extend beyond traditional population genetic analysis. First, the DNA sample itself can be analysed not only to uncover population structure but also to provide insights into microbial composition and host identity (Egan et al., 2021; Wodecka et al., 2014). This is best achieved by carrying out additional targeted sequencing of the microbiome using the same DNA purification method. However, certain population genetic tools, such as RADseq (Holmes & Davis Rabosky, 2018) or WGS (Pérez‐Cobas et al., 2020), may also provide sensitivity for analysing both host and microbiome (Figure 3). These additional data layers are invaluable for understanding the ecological and pathological roles of associated microbes and for monitoring potential pathogen introductions.

The DNA extraction methods used in tick population genetic studies can also be effectively applied to pathogen detection. For example, Ehrlichia canis, a bacterium responsible for canine monocytic ehrlichiosis in dogs, causes symptoms such as fever, lethargy, inappetence, enlarged lymph nodes, bleeding disorders (including haemorrhaging and nosebleeds), anaemia and weight loss, which regularly become fatal (Irwin & Beadle, 2022). This pathogen was unexpectedly identified for the first time in Australia in 2020 during the height of the COVID‐19 pandemic, following a government investigation into multiple cases of severely diseased dogs in the Kimberley region of Western Australia (Neave et al., 2022).

The discovery highlights a missed opportunity for earlier detection through active tick population monitoring in northern Australia. Such monitoring could have potentially mitigated the outbreak, sparing numerous dogs in affected communities. By combining assessments of tick population structure with the detection of E. canis prevalence through DNA extraction, more effective management strategies could have been implemented to reduce the risk of pathogen transmission.

Additionally, extracted DNA enables the investigation of host populations, facilitating population genetic analyses to uncover evolutionary history, migration patterns and insights into how hosts colonise target areas and their potential for future spread (Collini et al., 2016).

Environmental data from the location where the source organism was collected can provide valuable information for understanding the ecological and evolutionary context of population genetic structure. Factors such as temperature, rainfall and host movement can significantly influence population movement and behaviour (Backus et al., 2021; Ogden et al., 2021). Collecting and analysing these data provides valuable insights into the environmental pressures and historical events that have shaped population dynamics in the past and how they may do so in the future.

Access to historical data of populations and understanding the type of results provided by older tools is beneficial for understanding the previous history and migration patterns of a target organism. Historical records such as observational studies, animal tracking and older molecular techniques can provide important insights into the factors that have shaped the structure of populations over time, leading to improvements for formulating hypotheses in future studies.

Finally, by utilising the previously described features of populations, researchers can create models to predict the consequences of current and future environmental changes on population structures. Predictive modelling techniques allow scientists to assess how populations are likely to respond to factors such as climate change or habitat fragmentation, offering valuable insights into the potential impacts of these changes (Gilbert, 2021). For example, Clark et al. (2022) developed a model to identify environmental attributes that may trigger an increase in hospitalisations of companion animals due to paralysis caused by the tick species I. holocyclus and I. cornuatus. Their findings demonstrated that temperature anomalies, vegetation moisture levels and the Southern Oscillation Index are strong predictors of elevated risks for tick paralysis. These insights can inform preventive measures and reduce risks to animals by identifying environmental conditions that heighten the danger of tick paralysis (Clark et al., 2022). Such predictive models provide valuable tools for understanding how environmental changes may shape population dynamics, enabling researchers to develop informed strategies for mitigating potential risks of disease or conserving biodiversity.

The accuracy of predictive models that incorporate population structure information can depend on the resolution of the molecular population tool chosen for data generation, as described in previous sections. Analytical tools should, however, be carefully considered and aligned with the resolution of population genetic data needed, ensuring concordance with research objectives and study design. Similar to variation of molecular population genetic tools, various analysis pipelines also warrant further investigation and a dedicated review.

RECOMMENDATIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

We are working in a time of unprecedented advancements in sequencing technology, where the ability to generate large‐scale, high‐resolution data has far outpaced the capabilities of traditional methods (Athanasopoulou et al., 2021). This progress has not only transformed population genetic research but has also expanded the toolkit available to researchers, with each method offering unique strengths tailored to specific research objectives.

Although allozyme electrophoresis and RAPD provided more informative data for population genetic studies than earlier catch‐and‐release‐style methods, they have since been replaced by sequencing‐based techniques. Modern approaches offer significantly more loci at a lower cost per locus, along with streamlined workflows, higher throughput and far more information‐dense data. Importantly, these modern markers deliver much higher resolution, allowing researchers to analyse populations with lower genetic diversity and distinguish closely related individuals with high precision (Figure 3). Despite this, the results produced by these earlier techniques remain valuable for understanding historical studies and forming a foundation for current and emerging research. They provide important context and help in shaping hypotheses. However, these tools are no longer practical or suitable for generating the level of detail and information required for modern research and are, therefore, not recommended for future studies.

Microsatellites have long been a mainstay in population genetics due to their ease of use, cost‐effectiveness (Table 1), and the availability of established protocols and primer sets. As highly polymorphic markers, they are well suited for investigating fine‐scale population structure (Araya‐Anchetta et al., 2015). This approach is especially useful for studies focusing on small geographic areas or on tick species with rapid dispersal mechanisms (such as those utilising avian hosts (McCoy et al., 2003)). In such cases, other genetic markers like SNPs, which have a slower mutation rate, may not have accumulated sufficient variation to reveal genetic differentiation. Microsatellites, with their higher mutation rate, can resolve these differences more effectively.

However, the reliance on a relatively small number of loci limits genomic coverage compared to sequencing‐based methods, which can generate data with genome‐wide inference. The limited coverage of microsatellites may introduce sampling bias and also limit power to detect subtle or complex patterns of genetic variation, especially across the genome. Despite these limitations, microsatellites remain a viable option for researchers prioritising cost‐efficiency and straightforward workflows, particularly in studies where the focus is on genetic differentiation at finer spatiotemporal scales without the need for comprehensive genomic coverage.

AFLP, while innovative at its introduction, was quickly superseded by sequencing‐based methods that offer more quantitative and reproducible data with similar workflows. As a result, AFLP is now rarely used and is generally not recommended for new studies, given the superior options available through modern sequencing technologies.

Sequence typing remains a gold standard for investigating broad evolutionary relationships among organisms (Nadolny et al., 2015). Its widespread adoption is facilitated by the availability of standardised primer sets across numerous taxa, streamlining assay development and making it both practical and efficient for researchers. The method is well suited to rapidly generating data via Sanger sequencing and has been further enhanced through adaptation to high‐throughput NGS technologies, enabling the parallel sequencing of multiple samples and loci. Such developments have significantly improved throughput and efficiency, allowing researchers to apply sequence typing to projects involving hundreds of individual samples at much lower cost compared to methods such as WGS (Table 1). Sequence typing is particularly valuable for studies aiming to reconstruct the evolutionary history of a tick species or group of ticks. Owing to the slower mutation rates of commonly targeted loci, sequencing typing provides a robust baseline for understanding how, where and when globally invasive species such as H. longicornis may have been introduced to new regions over centuries of cattle trade. However, the conserved nature and slower mutation rates of these gene regions limit the utility of sequence typing for fine‐scale population genetic analyses. Instead, its strengths are most evident in research focused on broad evolutionary patterns or the generation of robust comparative datasets.

More recently, RADseq has emerged as an efficient and cost‐effective method for generating numerous unbiased genetic markers (Andrews et al., 2016). However, the development process for RADseq can be more complex than that of sequence typing or short‐read WGS, often requiring careful optimisation of restriction enzymes and higher quality DNA input to ensure successful library preparation and sequencing (Chambers et al., 2023). DNA quality and quantity requirements can pose challenges for some researchers, particularly when working with degraded DNA or field‐collected samples. RADseq is best suited to projects where researchers can control sample storage and DNA extraction protocols, thereby maintaining DNA integrity for optimal results. Where these conditions are met, RADseq provides access to a wide catalogue of SNP loci, enabling researchers with limited budgets to sequence more samples than would be feasible with WGS. Access to more SNP loci is particularly advantageous for studies requiring fine‐scale resolution at spatial scales as low as tens of kilometres and individual populations, as opposed to the global scale typically addressed by sequence typing. For smaller research projects, sample collections at such broad scales are often not practical or required given the research question, making RADseq a valuable option for investigating population structure at more localised levels. Despite some threshold limitations, RADseq remains a valuable choice when DNA quality requirements can be met and the necessary development processes can be implemented, offering a practical balance between cost and genomic coverage for studies that require finer resolution than traditional marker‐based approaches but do not have the budget or resources for WGS.

As the cost of sequencing continues to decline, WGS is becoming increasingly feasible for a broader range of applications. Older tools that provide less comprehensive data are likely to become obsolete in many contexts. Over the past 50 years, population genetic tools have consistently evolved to maximise information, reproducibility and specificity while minimising cost and effort. Once WGS becomes widely accessible and efficient, the main limitations in population genetics will no longer stem from the genetic markers themselves but from practical constraints, such as the collection, DNA purification and sequencing of large numbers of samples.

At the time of writing (July 2025), WGS methods remain prohibitively expensive for many research projects (Table 1). However, where funding is available, WGS can enable a broader study scope, as discussed in Section 5. For example, if a researcher wanted to examine not only the genetics of a tick but also investigate the host blood meal and microbiome of the same set of individuals, metagenomic WGS approaches could provide a viable pathway.

In addition, the large volumes of data generated by second‐ and third‐generation sequencing technologies provide opportunities for advancement of in silico analysis of population genetic data (Wang et al., 2021). The development of new software and analytical techniques to assess and determine population structure will enhance our understanding of target organisms. Moreover, advancements in high‐performance computing will enable the processing of larger and more complex genomic datasets, further improving the resolution and depth of population genetic studies (Jiang et al., 2021).

Finally, once comprehensive genetic data for population structure analysis are available through WGS, it will be crucial to apply this information to practical, real‐world scenarios. For tick populations, this includes predicting their movement in response to changing environmental conditions and understanding how pathogens spread through these populations. Such analyses require more than just population genetic data; improvements in environmental data collection, such as climate change models, livestock and/or wildlife movement patterns, and other ecological factors are essential. These insights will help us understand the drivers of population structure and enable the development of strategies to protect people, pets, livestock and wildlife from ticks and TBDs.

While WGS offers an unparalleled quantity of information, its high costs (Table 1), substantial computational demands and the need for advanced bioinformatics expertise currently limit its widespread application. In contrast, methods like sequence typing and RADseq strike a practical balance between cost and accessibility, providing robust insights into population genetics within the constraints of many research environments. However, with the rapid decline in sequencing costs and continuous advancements in computational tools, WGS is poised to become an increasingly viable and transformative option for a broader range of studies. Researchers can use this review as a guide to navigate the strengths and limitations of these tools, enabling them to make the best use of their resources and tailor their approaches to their specific objectives. In doing so, they can maximise the impact of their work and deepen their understanding of the ecology of their study organism.
