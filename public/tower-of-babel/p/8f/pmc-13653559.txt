Exploration of the vein to vein blood transfusion system in three Kenyan counties: a qualitative study
Authors: Linda S Barnes, Jennifer Makin, Jose R Paiz, Jana Macleod, Cindy Mical Makanga, Tecla Kivuli Chelagat, Alejandro Munoz-Valencia, Ejemai Eboreime, Robert Kamu Kaburu, Abdirahman Musa, Carolyne Njoki, Cavin Otieno Opiyo, Kristina E Rudd, Mark Yazer, Bopaya Bidanda, Juan Carlos Puyana, Pratap Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundSevere shortages of blood for transfusion are a persistent global health crisis, with the burden disproportionately concentrated in low-income countries. Diverse stakeholder perspectives on blood availability might inform improvements. We aimed to use a comprehensive approach to identify the challenges, facilitators, and innovations within the blood transfusion continuum (BTC) in three different Kenyan settings.MethodsIn this qualitative study, we interrogated the BTC in Siaya, Nakuru, and Turkana counties as part of the Pathways for Innovation in Blood Transfusion Systems in Kenya (PITS Kenya) mixed methods research study. We used purposive sampling, and through interviews and focus group discussions, we solicited perspectives about the challenges, barriers, and facilitating features of the BTC, including any innovations, from health-care workers, hospital administration, government officials, transfused patients, and blood donors aged 18 years and older, who were familiar with the BTC, from Aug 5, 2021, to May 9, 2022. We thematically analysed interview and discussion transcriptions using an iterative approach to achieve saturation of the thematic content. The findings were validated via member checking and stakeholder meetings in Nairobi that included research participants, non-governmental organisations, and Kenyan health authorities.Findings197 participants (84 [43%] female and 113 [57%] male) participated in 74 interviews (76 participants) and 18 focus group discussions (121 participants). Seven themes emerged: (1) massive shortages of blood, (2) mismatches between where blood is stored and where patients need it, (3) reliance on family replacement donors, (4) delays in patient care, (5) stresses to the health-care system and workforce hardship, (6) variable knowledge and attitudes towards blood donation and transfusion, and (7) limited resources. Facilitation of blood transfusion involved local champions through self-organised and community engagement. Innovations advocated for improved resource allocation, education, and collaborative efforts.InterpretationThis study identified BTC challenges, facilitators, and innovations. The participants described the BTC as fragmented in these settings. The most poignant findings revealed how the lack of blood availability caused profound stress on the health system, contributing to delays, inefficiencies, and worry for patient wellbeing. Innovations encouraged collective stewardship and governance of the BTC, bridging communities and health-care facilities.FundingThe US National Institutes of Health National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute.

Introduction

Blood transfusions enable essential medical care, reducing morbidity and mortality for patients affected by chronic anaemia to acute haemorrhage. 61% of countries do not have adequate blood supply to deliver this care, with an estimated shortage of 102 million red blood cell units annually.1,2 Low-income and middle-income countries experience the brunt of the deficit.2 Nearly every country in sub-Saharan Africa faces chronically severe blood shortages.3–5 Blood availability depends upon a reliable donor population and the ability to process, test, store, and deliver blood, along with the use of evidence-based transfusion practices for patient care. This so-called vein-to-vein series of steps, from blood donation to transfusion, comprise the blood transfusion continuum (BTC).1

Previous research describes many factors contributing to insufficient blood availability in Kenya, including high rates of chronic anaemia and transfusion transmitted infections, community fears and mistrust, low levels of voluntary blood donation, and reliance on school-aged children for blood drives.6 Other challenges include complex logistics associated with storage, transportation, testing, and processing of blood.7 Hospital staffing and supply deficits also impair timely blood transfusion.8,9 Few studies detail the perspectives of health-care professionals who encounter daily difficulties fulfilling the clinical demand for blood transfusion at the hospital level, or the broader view of community stakeholders and health system authorities engaged in the BTC.

We sought to explore diverse stakeholder viewpoints using qualitative research methods in three Kenyan counties: Siaya, Nakuru, and Turkana. The objectives were to gather information on the challenges and opportunities in providing a sufficient and reliable blood supply from health-care, government, and community members experienced in the BTC, considering benefits and harms. We hypothesised that health-care workers, hospital administration, government officials, transfused patients, and blood donors were best situated to (1) describe critical challenges in blood donation, processing, and delivery; (2) identify facilitators contributing to possible improvements; and (3) inform novel innovations to close gaps in blood transfusion availability. Given the complexity of the BTC, we anticipated that a holistic view would expose opportunities for increased performance of the system overall.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a qualitative study using an exploratory design of the Kenyan blood transfusion system as part of the ongoing Pathways for Innovation in Blood Transfusion Systems in Kenya (PITS Kenya) mixed methods research study that started on July 1, 2020.10 We obtained Ethical Review Board approval from Strathmore University (Nairobi, Kenya; SU-IERC0992/21), the National Commission for Science, Technology and Innovation (Nairobi, Kenya; NACOSTI/P/21/9976), the University of Minnesota (Minneapolis, MN, USA), and the University of Pittsburgh (Pittsburgh, PA, USA; STUDY21020040). Our previously published protocol paper describes the design, setting, and metatheoretical framework.10

Participants

We used a purposive sampling method to guide participant selection across the range of the BTC. We applied a sampling matrix that targeted representatives from each stakeholder category to ensure balanced representation from multiple levels of the health system, officials, and community members (appendix 1 p 1). We sought at least 120 adults with experience in the BTC within Nakuru, Turkana, and Siaya counties to include in the study. We purposefully selected health-care facilities within the targeted counties to include both smaller peripheral hospitals and larger referral centres. Study personnel approached prospective participants in three separate ways, with consideration given to potential for adverse impact or harm. (1) We contacted clinicians and administrators within the selected facilities considering their professional roles within the BTC through department rosters and directly invited their participation. We selected various clinical specialties and officials to capture a full spectrum of the BTC across the health-care system. (2) We invited transfused patients through ward census reviews and approached adults (aged ≥18 years) capable of consenting after their acute clinical needs resolved. (3) We asked for blood donors through managers at satellite blood donation centres and referrals to community blood donor groups. This multilevel and role-based sampling approach ensured representation of BTC perspectives across the health system, community, and government.

Procedures

Interview tools consisted of a semi-structured guide with questions informed by the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR).11 The topics explored, in the form of open-ended questions, are summarised in appendix 1 (pp 2–3). The questions and prompts focused on the participant’s perspective and experience within the BTC to acquire their perceptions of challenges, barriers, and enabling features, including any innovations that each participant might offer. For an innovation, participants were asked about acceptability, feasibility, and appropriateness within their setting. The tools were developed by blood transfusion experts and clinicians with practical experience in Kenya (JMak, JMac, MY, PK, and JCP). We pilot tested the questions with nine research team members, health-care workers, and community representatives in Kenya, who were uninvolved in the qualitative part of the research. We refined the tools before starting the formal study by clarifying the questions and prompts. As well as individual interviews, we conducted focus group discussions, which involved community members (blood donors) or clinicians (medical providers or nurses).

Three teams of trained research staff, one per county, performed data collection. Each team consisted of a team leader and two research assistants. Participants gave individual written consent in either English, Swahili, Luo, or Turkana. De-identified recordings, restricted to the research team, were uploaded to Otter.ai and transcripts were downloaded to secure institutional storage. Files were removed from Otter.ai after transcription in compliance with the study’s data management procedures.10 For interviews conducted in Swahili, Luo, or Turkana, research team members (TKC, CMM, RKK, and AM) fluent in these languages transcribed the audio and then translated them into English. All transcriptions were verified against the audio recording and minimally refined by a second member of the research team to improve linguistic clarity, remove vocal disfluencies or hesitations, and incorporate verbal emphases that were not evident in the transcripts alone.

Data analysis

The qualitative analysis followed a structured and iterative approach focused on thematic domains without predefined outcomes (appendix 1 p 4). To provide consistent interpretation of participant voices, a codebook captured key constructs from the CFIR conceptual framework and definitions. The research team (LSB, TKC, AM, CMM, JMac, JMak, AM-V, and PK) augmented this initial codebook by reviewing the literature before data analysis, then improving the definitions through a workshop that incorporated a debrief of field notes to ensure relevance and applicability. We performed a preliminary analysis on a selection of 15 transcripts, five from each county, to refine the codebook and definitions. The researchers (LSB, TKC, CMM, JMac, JMak, AM-V, and PK) met three times to resolve differences in application of codes allowing for further refinement of the codebook, definitions, and incorporation of emerging concepts informed by the transcribed data. Subsequent rounds of analysis included the entire body of 92 transcripts.

We used computer-assisted qualitative data analysis software MAXQDA for data analysis. Six researchers (LSB, TKC, CMM, AM-V, JMac, and JMak) trained in qualitative research performed the analysis using a systematic, thematic approach to identify and interpret key content and patterns related to the BTC. The researchers reviewed interview transcripts repeatedly to gain familiarity with the data. To assess intercoder reliability, we conducted paired analysis on a series of transcripts and resolved the discrepancies through interactive discussions to achieve high concordance. We applied an inductive approach to the initial conceptual framework guided by the coding process and a deductive approach to iteratively incorporate new insights, allowing for both a priori and emergent themes to surface. Memos summarising contemporaneous researcher impressions of the data informed emergent codes, patterns, and themes. The process of identifying emerging themes, with data coded into broad categories, led to the refinement of specific subthemes.

Summary themes were developed for constructs and subconstructs that were related to facilitators, barriers, and innovations, then were exported to Microsoft Excel. The themes were organised into tables to provide a comprehensive overview of the findings and implications. The final stage of analysis involved synthesising these themes into actionable recommendations aimed at improving the BTC. Throughout the analysis, attention was given to the context of the collected data, and themes were cross-checked for consistency across the types of data (recordings, memos, and transcripts). The research team discussed the themes from the preliminary findings with participants during structured site visits and conducted an external validation of the emerging themes at a stakeholder meeting held in Nairobi comprising research participants, non-governmental organisation partners, and health authorities. These activities occurred before comprehensive analysis and peer debriefing with other knowledgeable experts in the BTC. The comprehensive analysis involved regular research team meetings to achieve thematic saturation of the concepts conveyed by participants, address potential biases, and ensure the accuracy of interpretation.

Role of the funding source

The funder of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

Between Aug 5, 2021, and May 9, 2022, 197 participants (84 [43%] female and 113 [57%] male) took part in 74 interviews (76 participants) and 18 focus group discussions (121 participants; table). Individual interviews ranged from 10 min to 141 min. Focus group discussions, each comprising five to eight participants by discipline, included medical officers, clinical officers, inpatient and outpatient nurses, and blood donors, and ranged in duration from 35 min to 123 min. The recordings captured 4669 min of content.

Seven overarching themes arose from the data, detailing critical challenges and barriers contributing to the lack of blood availability in the three counties (panel 1). (1) Massive deficit in blood availability. “Today there is blood and tomorrow we don’t have any.” Focus group discussions, clinicians, 131110 (2) Mismatch between where blood is stored and where it is needed, reflecting supply chain challenges. (3) An impractical reliance on family replacement donors. (4) Blood shortages impact patient care and lead to poor health outcomes; donors, patients, and health-care workers frequently discussed prolonged hospital stays, not transfusing the desired amount of blood required for the degree of anaemia and haemostasis, or patient safety, or both. (5) Concern for patient outcomes causes stress to the health-care system. (6) Knowledge, attitudes, perceptions, and beliefs about transfusion and donation differ by site and influence the willingness of donors to donate blood and providers to prioritise patients for transfusion. (7) Lack of equipment and resources limits the ability to collect, process, store, transport, and transfuse blood.

The adverse consequences of these challenges ranged from creating a strain on the health system, health-care workers, patients, and their families to a low public willingness to donate and a lack of resources. These themes illustrate the complexity of governance over the BTC and the fragmented yet interconnected nature of blood supply and transfusion demand.

Multiple factors contributed positively to the functioning of the BTC, both in the health-care facility and community setting (panel 2). A recurring facilitator across all sites was the motivation and perseverance of the health-care workforce. Health-care workers and administrators described having to push hard to hunt for blood, even seeking blood in the community for emergencies. “I went to a nearby school. I went to the principal, and I requested…give me only five students who can donate blood…it was screened [tested] then I took it by myself to ICU [intensive care unit].” Interview, administration, 1301094

In many instances, health-care workers personally visited the laboratory to advocate for their patients, called other facilities to locate blood, or sourced petrol and vehicles to transport necessary blood products. On a routine basis, health-care workers described educating patients and their families regarding the safety and need for blood donations. Teamwork between departments and across facilities contributed to successful transfusions. Individuals created informal supply chains between public and private facilities and blood banks. Family replacement donors and individuals with relationships to their patients described a compelling motivation to donate blood. Close physical proximity to the facilities capable of collecting and testing blood appeared vital, and facilities without these resources struggled with transportation delays. Reliance on schools as blood drive locations contributed high yields of willing donors when in session. Notably, community governance interactions allowed for cocreation of campaigns from stakeholders through multisector collaboration, and social accountability mechanisms that engaged the communities with the need for blood transfusion in the local health-care settings.

Participants identified innovations and adaptations across the BTC (panel 2). The most common innovation was adapting to scarcity. “Blood is never enough, but we’ve learnt over years to work with what you can get. Common suggestions included community education and empowerment, such as advocating for donations from community leaders, educating the public to reduce fear of donating, and promoting healthy behaviours and lifestyles. A specific example of successful community mobilisation came from a sports club in Turkana, where members organised and participated in multiple blood drives. Many participants emphasised the need to implement quality standards and develop a governance system for estimating the region’s blood supply and demand. Fluctuations in peak demand, during seasonal malaria, and low blood donation periods, such as school holidays, were highlighted. A proposed strategy was to establish databases to track repeat blood donors to enable their re-engagement and donation when needed, particularly through digital platforms such as WhatsApp and Damu Sasa. Several participants also advocated increasing the number of regional blood drives and increasing convenient public access points to donate blood conveniently. Additionally, many suggested replicating the example seen in Nakuru by establishing local blood donation centres able to perform blood collection and on-site processing and testing of blood products within the hospital facility. “Our facility is a bit different we have a blood bank, a small one, so if we need [blood], especially [for] the patients in the ward that can wait, we ask the relatives to come and donate, they come, they donate.” Focus group discussions, clinicians, 131086

Participants hypothesised that bringing blood donors and the resultant blood products closer to recipients reduced transportation costs and the risk of delays. Overall, these innovations aim to address the fragmented nature of the BTC and better align the blood supply with the demand for transfusions. These examples showed that integration of community stewardship for the blood supply, including self-organised campaigns, multisector collaboration, and social accountability to align stakeholders.

The research team sorted and refined the emerging themes in panels 1 and 2 to develop a conceptual framework to articulate the recommendations for improving the BTC (figure). Within this figure, we categorised the overarching themes comprising challenges, the contextual innovations, and facilitators of blood transfusion availability. To improve the BTC, participants recommended strengthening the linkages between communities and health-care facilities through more coordinated and collaborative actions to balance available blood supplies. Additionally, resources to overcome limitations in equipment, transportation, and workforce constraints are important. These innovations aim to enhance the overall efficiency of the BTC by closing existing gaps and ensuring a more integrated, responsive system.

Discussion

Through the voices of transfused patients, blood donors, health-care professionals, and governing officials in three diverse Kenyan locations, this qualitative study shows that inadequate blood availability is not only a profound and pervasive problem of insufficient blood donation, but a fragmented BTC in which weak linkages between community mobilisation and local health-care facilities yield delays, inefficiencies, and distress across the entire system. Our methods led to a deep contextual understanding, informing a conceptual framework to collectively overcome challenges, thus thwarting the expectation of scarcity or desperate helplessness.12,13 Participants described both established and novel solutions, their perspectives on acceptability, and the barriers and facilitators to implementation. Overall, the findings provide a comprehensive view of the current state of the BTC in Kenya and suggest actionable pathways for future progress, meeting the three primary objectives of identifying key challenges, facilitators, and innovations.

Importantly, the participants in this study perceived the BTC as discontinuous in these settings. The community connects to the blood bank through desperate individuals pressing for semi-structured, self-organised donor mobilisation activities, such as family replacement donors or infrequent blood drives, while blood banks maintain unsteady linkages to hospital laboratories where clinical demands for transfusion are acutely arbitrated, patient by patient, considering exhausted blood inventories. This fragmentation between the community and health-care facilities creates gaps in the supply and demand for blood, making effective mediation of blood availability difficult. The challenges of the BTC in Kenya were consistent with known barriers of limited infrastructure and resources.14 Participants acknowledged the difficulty in securing adequate and sustainable support to strengthen the BTC. Yet no single discipline or stakeholder category was able to fully articulate the complexity of the problem. The prevailing impression revealed a fractured system characterised by mismatches between supply and demand described as disruptions or delays between where blood is available and where it is most needed. There was a general sense of mistrust and exhaustion among participants, stemming from their own observed challenges, misinformation, and broader knowledge gaps, which contributed to this concept of fragmentation. The overarching theme across all interviews was that blood availability consistently failed to meet the demand for transfusions, aligning with the inadequate blood supply documented across sub-Saharan Africa.15–17

Participants described facilitation of blood availability as being enabled by individual stakeholders, advocates, or local champions who acted as pillars supporting the BTC. Other publications also found local champions to be vital catalysts.18,19 However, we found dependence on singular advocates to be both a facilitator and a barrier when each stakeholder exists in isolation. A health-care worker who leverages political influence or creates informal supply chains is a facilitator; however, their reliance on personal effort and the risk of health-care worker burnout underscores the limitations of depending on individual will. Our data suggest there are inordinate pressures and indirect costs put on families of family replacement donors. For example, a family replacement donor who answers the call to donate blood and mobilises their network is an important facilitator to motivate blood donation directed to a patient. Like findings from other settings, reliance on this method of blood supply in this region has proven unsustainable because the need for blood transfusion might be urgent and precede blood donation (eg, trauma or haemorrhage).20 The families and patients expressed resentment and the hospital staff and patients were desperate to receive timely blood for emergent transfusion. Participants advanced a systems-thinking approach that fostered coordination, collaboration, and partnership across the BTC.21 We suggest moving away from sole reliance on individual champions or family replacement donors towards developing sustainable system level supports such as dedicated transfusion coordinator roles, blood demand forecasting tools, and interhospital blood sharing platforms.22,23 Policies ideally reinforce coordinated governance across national, county, facility, and community levels, with clear roles and accountability mechanisms. Embedding structured, community-led approaches within policy frameworks might enhance sustained blood donation and better align local participation with system needs.

Emerging innovations identified modern technologies to increase awareness, including digital and social media platforms. The innovations sought transparency and shared ownership, connecting the community and hospital with the mutual governance of the BTC, to overcome barriers and improve blood availability. Participants described the need for interoperable digital systems enabling real-time data sharing on donor availability, blood stocks, and demand forecasting, as well as investments in systems level change, including resourcing local blood testing and processing capabilities. The emphasis on expanding community-based blood donation and blood drives generated the most enthusiasm and support from the stakeholders. Community engagement has been a core pillar of blood transfusion improvement endorsed by WHO Regional Office for Africa strategies since 2001 that call for community-based mobilisation and voluntary blood donation.24 Community walking blood banks such as those implemented in Turkana County might help to close emergency gaps in blood availability.25 However, the most universal, sustainable, and powerful solutions, according to the participants, leveraged teamwork, networking, and civic engagement, fostering stewardship of the BTC to fulfil a sufficient blood supply for patients requiring blood transfusion.26,27 For blood donation to take on real, practical significance, it must become an integral part of the community.13,28,29 Participants articulated the importance of connecting and uniting each component of the BTC over optimising any single aspect or working heroically in isolation.

Limitations of this study include that, despite the large number of participants and diversity of their perspectives, the purposeful sampling might not represent all perspectives on the BTC. Further, participants might have provided what they thought was socially desirable information, contributing to bias. Participants might also have been biased by their most recent or worst experience with blood transfusion or donation, and this recall bias could have affected their answers and general opinion of the BTC. Measures taken to reduce researcher bias included paired coding comparisons, memoing (documenting contemporaneous impressions), and refining definitions through a group process that involved peer debriefing, member checking, and stakeholder input. Future research might facilitate the transferability of these findings beyond the settings included in the study. This study does not account for changes over time in the study settings.

Despite their challenges within the BTC, stakeholders worked tirelessly. The resilience and passion of these individuals and their cooperative efforts offer optimism for a stronger, more robust BTC in the future. Although many of the innovations identified by participants would undoubtedly benefit the system, no single technology or solution was viewed as a panacea.6,25 The participants’ vision of the BTC underscores that achieving an adequate blood supply demands more than technical solutions; it depends on innovation that bridges communities and health-care delivery. Collective stewardship and shared governance of the BTC are essential to reliably realising a safe, sufficient, and sustainable blood supply from vein to vein.
