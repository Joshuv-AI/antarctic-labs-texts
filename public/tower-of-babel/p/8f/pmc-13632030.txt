Effects of PCSK9 inhibitors on coronary atheroma morphology in patients with coronary artery disease treated with statins: a meta-analysis and systematic review
Authors: Priyesh Thakurathi, Agnes S Kim, Michael G Nanna, Khagendra Dahal, Shiva S Aryal, Quinn Pack, Dinesh K Kalra, Raymond G McKay, Paul D Thompson, Andrew M Goldsweig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitors (PCSK9i) reduce adverse cardiovascular events when added to statins, but their effects on coronary plaque morphology remain inadequately defined.MethodsWe searched PubMed via MEDLINE, Cochrane CENTRAL, Scopus, Web of Science for randomized controlled trials comparing the effects of PCSK9i plus statins versus statins alone on coronary atheroma morphology in patients with coronary artery disease, including total atheroma volume (TAV), percent atheroma volume (PAV), fibrous cap thickness (FCT), minimal lumen area (MLA), maximum lipid arc (MLArc), and lipid parameters. Random-effects models pooled mean differences (MDs).ResultsOf 1,472 abstracts screened, eight randomized controlled trials (n = 1,898; PCSK9i+statin n = 950; statins alone n = 948) met inclusion criteria with mean follow-up of 59.4 ± 21.4 weeks. Compared with statins alone, PCSK9i plus statins increased FCT (MD 26.48 um, 95% CI 14.00 to 38.96) and reduced MLArc (MD -16.97°, 95% CI -30.90 to -3.04). In IVUS studies, PCSK9i reduced PAV (MD -0.86%, 95% CI -1.16 to -0.55) and TAV (MD -8.95 mm3, 95% CI -15.81 to -2.10); MLA did not differ. PCSK9i also produced larger reductions in LDL-C, total cholesterol, non-HDL-C, triglycerides, ApoB, and Lp(a), with a modest increase in HDL-C.ConclusionsThe addition of PCSK9i to statin therapy improved coronary atheroma composition and morphology in patients with coronary artery disease.Keywords: PCSK9 inhibitors, Coronary artery disease, Atherosclerotic plaque morphology, Percent atheroma volume, Fibrous cap thickness, Optical coherence tomography, Intravascular ultrasound

Introduction

Statins led to significant reductions in the incidence and mortality of coronary artery disease (CAD) [1,2], but CAD remains a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality. Atherosclerotic plaque progression plays a central role in adverse cardiovascular events [3]. Statins and ezetimibe stabilize coronary plaques by reducing atheroma volume and increasing fibrous cap thickness [4,5]. Still, considerable residual cardiovascular risk persists, prompting the development of novel therapeutic agents such as proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitors (PCSK9 inhibitors/PCSK9is). By increasing low-density lipoprotein (LDL-C) receptor availability, PCSK9is lower LDL-C and reduce major adverse cardiovascular events [[6], [7], [8], [9]].

Statins improve atheroma characteristics [10,11], but direct effects of PCSK9i on coronary plaque morphology have not been well described. Some recent trials have explored if PCSK9is confer incremental benefits to statin treatment in plaque stabilization and regression when assessed by intracoronary imaging modalities. Intracoronary imaging using optical coherence tomography (OCT) and intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) provides detailed assessments of plaque composition and morphology, offering critical insights into the therapeutic effects on atheroma volume, fibrous cap thickness, and lipid content [12].

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the effects of PCSK9is, in combination with statins, on coronary atheroma morphology compared with statin therapy alone. We sought to determine whether PCSK9i leads to more favorable changes in key atheroma characteristics.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted following the methods outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [13] and adhered to PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [14]. The study protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews; Registration No: CRD420251112168).

A comprehensive systematic search was performed in collaboration with a medical librarian using PubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane CENTRAL, Scopus, and Web of Science from database inception through January 2026. Search strategies combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keyword terms related to PCSK9i, coronary artery disease, plaque morphology, intravascular ultrasound (IVUS), and optical coherence tomography (OCT). Searches were restricted to human studies published in English. Additional studies were identified through manual review of reference lists from relevant articles. Detailed search strategies and database-specific queries are provided in Supplemental Tables 1 and 2.

Study selection and outcomes

Eligible studies were RCTs enrolling patients with established CAD who received either PCSK9i (alirocumab/evolocumab) plus statins or statins alone. Enrolled patients were undergoing left heart catheterization either for an elective indication or in the emergent setting when presenting with acute coronary syndrome.

Plaque morphology outcomes of interest included percent atheroma volume (PAV) and total atheroma volume (TAV) assessed by intravascular ultrasound (IVUS),and fibrous cap thickness (FCT), minimal lumen area (MLA) and maximum lipid arc (MLArc) assessed by optical coherence tomography (OCT). Non-randomized studies, case reports, reviews, and studies lacking a control arm were excluded.

Three independent reviewers (PT, SS, KD) screened studies, extracted data, and assessed risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB-2) for RCTs [13], with each study being reviewed by at least two reviewers and discrepancies resolved through consensus or consultation with the third reviewer. Extracted data included study design, enrollment criteria of individual studies (Supplemental Table 3), patient demographics, plaque characteristics, and lipid profiles.

Statistical analysis

Random effects models were used, assuming heterogeneity in the population and setting under study, to pool continuous outcomes as mean differences (MDs) with standard deviations (SDs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with inverse variance through the DerSimonian and Laird method to account for clinical and methodological heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was quantified using Higgins’ I² statistic, with values < 25%, 25–50%, 50%-75% and >75 % indicating low, moderate, substantial and considerable heterogeneity, respectively [13]. Sensitivity analyses were performed by restricting the analysis to double-blind RCTs. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

When primary studies reported medians with interquartile ranges, data were converted to means and SDs with the appropriate formulae [16]. Apart from random-effects meta-analyses, data from multiple RCTs were pooled based on their weighted averages and proportions of their sample size to calculate baseline characteristics and changes in lipid profiles, with pooled SDs accounting for within-study variability and between-study dispersion. These pooled estimates were descriptive and not used for inferential meta-analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan) 5.4 (Cochrane Collaboration, London, UK) [15].

Results

A total of 1,472 records were identified through database searching, including 195 from PubMed/MEDLINE, 235 from Scopus, 71 from Cochrane CENTRAL, and 971 from Web of Science. After duplicate removal and screening, eight randomized controlled trials involving 1,898 participants met inclusion criteria (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram showing the selection process of the included studies.

Across the included trials, eligibility criteria required patients with established coronary artery disease undergoing intracoronary imaging with IVUS and/or OCT while receiving guideline-directed statin therapy. Four studies enrolled patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS), whereas the remaining studies included both ACS and stable CAD populations. Imaging eligibility required suitable non-obstructive target vessels for serial assessment. Common exclusion criteria included severe renal dysfunction, significant heart failure, prior coronary artery bypass grafting, uncontrolled arrhythmias, and conditions limiting repeat intracoronary imaging.

The studies were conducted across multinational, multisite, and single-center settings, with sample sizes ranging from 24 to 968 participants (Table 1). Four trials were double-blinded and placebo-controlled (GLAGOV [17], PACMAN-AMI [20], HUYGENS [21], FITTER [23]), while four were open-label (ODYSSEY J-IVUS [18], Adage-Joto [24], ALTAIR [22], Gao [19]). The imaging follow-up period across studies ranged from 12 to 78 weeks, with a weighted mean follow-up duration of 59.4 ± 21.4 weeks (13.7± 4.9 months). IVUS was used in five trials, OCT in five, and adjunctive near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) in two, with PACMAN-AMI, HUYGENS and FITTER integrating multimodality imaging. Four studies evaluated alirocumab use and the other four studies evaluated evolocumab use. Control groups in all studies received statin therapy alone, most commonly atorvastatin or rosuvastatin, either with placebo or open-label background therapy. Included populations varied between acute coronary syndrome and stable coronary artery disease cohorts. Seven RCTs reported baseline and follow-up lipid profile information.

Baseline characteristics were well balanced between the PCSK9i plus statin group and the statins alone group (Table 2). The mean age was 60.3 years in both arms, with a similar proportion of males (75.5% vs. 74.9%) and comparable rates of hypertension, diabetes, prior acute coronary syndrome, and other comorbidities. Use of high-intensity statins (∼45%) and other cardiovascular therapies were also consistent.

Pooled analysis of OCT imaging outcomes demonstrated that PCSK9i use was associated with a significant increase in FCT compared with statins alone (MD: 26.48 µm, 95% CI 14.00 to 38.96, p < 0.0001), with considerable heterogeneity across studies (I² = 93%). Likewise, there was a significant reduction in MLArc favoring PCSK9i (MD: −16.97°, 95% CI −30.90 to −3.04, P = 0.03), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 67%). However, no significant difference was observed in the minimal lumen area.

In the IVUS outcomes, PCSK9i use was associated with significant reductions in PAV (MD: −0.86%, 95% CI −1.16 to −0.55, P < 0.00001) and TAV (MD: −8.95 mm³, 95% CI −15.81 to −2.10, P = 0.01). These findings were consistent across multiple RCTs, with low to considerable heterogeneity respectively (I² = 17% for PAV, I² = 80% for TAV). Further sensitivity analyses were performed by restricting the dataset to double-blinded RCTs using a leave-one-out approach. These analyses did not materially alter the effect estimates. For PAV, heterogeneity was eliminated (I²: 17% to 0%) while maintaining statistical significance (P < 0.00001). For TAV, statistical significance strengthened (P = 0.01 to P < 0.00001), accompanied by a modest reduction in heterogeneity (I²: 80% to 78%) . Similarly, subgroup analysis limited to patients presenting with ACS demonstrated a modest reduction in heterogeneity for TAV (I²: 80% to 75%), and persistent statistical significance (P = 0.02), and for FCT (I²: 93% to 80%) without altering the overall direction or magnitude of effect for the studied variables (Supplemental Fig. 1).

Across seven RCTs, the addition of PCSK9i to statins significantly improved lipid parameters compared with statins alone. PCSK9i therapy led to larger reductions in total cholesterol (MD −53.11 mg/dL, 95% CI −61.69 to −44.53, p < 0.00001), LDL-C (MD −44.72 mg/dL, 95% CI −56.42 to −33.02, p < 0.00001), non-HDL-C (MD −58.46 mg/dL, 95% CI −64.84 to −52.09, p < 0.00001) and triglycerides (MD −17.51 mg/dL, 95% CI −25.11 to −9.91, p < 0.00001). On the other hand, the PCSK9i arm showed a higher increase in HDL-C compared to the statins alone arm (MD 2.59 mg/dL, 95% CI 2.29 to 2.88, p < 0.00001). Comparatively, there were also significant reductions in both ApoB (MD −40.28 mg/dL, 95% CI −40.73 to −39.83, p < 0.00001) and Lp(a) (MD −4.90 mg/dL, 95% CI −9.21 to −0.58, P = 0.03) in the PCSK9i arm (Fig. 3 and Supplemental Fig. 2).

Forest plots showing the pooled treatment effect estimates of PCSK9i compared with statins alone and the corresponding changes in LDL-C (A), HDL-C (B), ApoB (C) and Lp(a) (D) in the two groups.

Pooled and weighted lipid parameter values from the seven RCTs also revealed a decrease in Total cholesterol, LDL-C, Triglycerides, ApoB and Lp(a) to be more pronounced in the PCSK9i arm and a bigger increase in HDL-C (Table 3). The risk of bias of the included studies ranged between “low” to “some concerns” as represented in the traffic light plots (Supplemental Fig. 3). Formal assessment of publication bias using funnel plots was not performed because fewer than 10 studies were included, consistent with established methodological recommendations. Each RCT had low concern over most risk of bias domains, except missing outcome data at the end of follow-up (range 5-20%). To address this, the included studies employed established methods such as sensitivity analyses excluding participants with missing data and multiple imputation techniques. Some instances of missing outcomes were random and were considered unlikely to meaningfully influence the pooled effect estimates or overall confidence in the findings.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of eight RCTs including 1,898 participants, the addition of PCSK9i to statin therapy resulted in significant favorable changes in coronary plaque morphology and lipid parameters. Imaging endpoints consistently showed plaque stabilization, with increases in FCT, reductions in PAV and TAV, and improvements in MLArc with PCSK9i use compared to statins alone. PCSK9i therapy also led to greater reductions in LDL-C, total cholesterol, non-HDL-C, ApoB, triglycerides, and Lp(a), along with modest increases in HDL-C. These findings suggest that intensive lipid lowering with PCSK9i may be associated with favorable changes in plaque morphology and composition.

Atheroma morphology changes

The evolution of an unstable coronary atheroma starts with pathological intimal thickening of a coronary artery. The intimal thickening consists of smooth muscle cells (SMCs) with focal areas where SMCs are replaced by extracellular matrix (ECM) interspersed with lipids and cholesterol. When macrophages infiltrate this area, the intimal thickening transitions into fibroatheromas rich in lipid-filled necrotic core. As the lesion progresses, ECM content decreases while lipid content increases, eventually forming a large necrotic core. Likewise, the thick fibrous cap of an early fibroatheroma transforms into a thin-cap fibroatheroma [25,26]. Ultimately, advanced plaques rupture, causing thrombosis and precipitating ACS. Intracoronary imaging such as OCT and IVUS provides a window into this progression, revealing thin fibrous caps, necrotic cores, and macrophage infiltration- features that correspond to increases in PAV and TAV; their use has demonstrated superiority over angiography-guided procedures for stent sizing, expansion, and clinical outcomes [[27], [28], [29], [30], [31]], and served as a sensitive tool to detect disease-modifying effects of PCSK9 inhibition.

In this analysis, PCSK9i therapy consistently increased FCT across all included studies, reflecting biologically meaningful plaque stabilization. However, plaque regression in itself is only a surrogate marker of atherosclerosis and not direct evidence of clinical benefit in CAD. At the same time, emerging evidence suggests that PCSK9 influences atherosclerosis beyond LDL-C lowering, through pathways involving inflammation, macrophage lipid uptake, and smooth muscle cell cholesterol trafficking [32].

The vascular benefits of PCSK9i extend to non-coronary vessels. FOURIER and ODYSSEY Outcomes demonstrated that LDL-C lowering with PCSK9i corresponded with reductions in ischemic stroke [7,8]. The plaque-stabilizing effects observed here provide biological plausibility for these cerebrovascular benefits. This is reflected in the 2024 Primary Prevention of Stroke Guideline, which assigns a Class IIb recommendation for PCSK9i when additional LDL-C lowering is required or statins are not tolerated [33].

Lipid profile changes

The lipid-lowering efficacy of PCSK9i was seen in FOURIER trial, where evolocumab lowered LDL-C from 92 mg/dL to 30 mg/dL and reduced MACE by 15% over 2.2 years [8]. ODYSSEY Outcomes demonstrated reductions in recurrent ischemic events and all-cause mortality in post-ACS patients treated with alirocumab [7]. This study’s findings extend these results by linking intensive LDL-C reductions with measurable regression and stabilization of coronary atheroma, providing mechanistic insight into clinical benefit. Despite background statin therapy, PCSK9i therapy in our meta-analysis produced absolute LDL-C reductions of ∼50 mg/dL and consistent improvements in other lipid parameters. These reductions exceed those observed with ezetimibe and directly explain the incremental plaque regression observed. Current guidelines endorse PCSK9i for patients at very high ASCVD risk who do not achieve LDL-C targets on maximally tolerated statins [34], and the 2025 ACS Guideline provides a Class IIa recommendation for adding PCSK9i in ACS patients with LDL-C 55–69 mg/dL [35].

CAD can develop due to risk factors apart from elevated LDL-C such as genetics, inflammation, or elevated Lp(a) [32,36]. PCSK9 loss-of-function variants confer lifelong reductions in LDL-C and up to 88% reductions in ASCVD risk [37]. The ATHEROMA-IVUS study demonstrated that circulating PCSK9 levels were linearly associated with necrotic core burden independently of LDL-C or statin use [38]. Elevated ApoB reflects the total burden of atherogenic particles and correlates more strongly with ASCVD risk than LDL-C alone [39], while Lp(a) is now recognized as a causal factor based on Mendelian randomization and large scale epidemiological data [40,41]. This analysis showed significant reductions in ApoB and Lp(a) with PCSK9i, changes that are not typically achieved with statins. Statins minimally affect Lp(a), whereas PCSK9i reduce it by ∼20–30%, offering meaningful therapeutic potential in high-risk patients with elevated Lp(a).

Future directions

Future studies should clarify whether the observed imaging changes translate into sustained reductions in clinical events, define the durability of plaque stabilization after treatment discontinuation, and standardize imaging protocols and follow-up intervals. Implementation research addressing access, adherence, and cost may help determine how best to deploy PCSK9i in patients with residual risk despite statin therapy. Early evidence from the PURSUIT Phase II trial of an oral PCSK9i and interim results from the Phase III CORALreef Outcomes trial involving the oral agent enlicitide suggest that effective oral PCSK9 inhibition may soon be feasible, potentially increasing access [42,43].

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the included studies varied in clinical and imaging follow-up duration (12–78 weeks) and sample sizes; and while they spanned three continents, when considering each study six of the eight studies were limited to specific geographic and homogenous population cohorts. These factors may have introduced the significant heterogeneity observed in some of the key atheroma characteristics reported in this analysis. We performed subgroup and sensitivity analysis to address the heterogeneity as reported above. Second, trials enrolled patients with established coronary artery disease on high-intensity statins, limiting generalizability to lower-risk populations (such as for primary prevention) or statin-naïve individuals. Third, lack of individual patient data precluded adjustment for potential confounders (e.g., adherence, lifestyle changes). Finally, while PCSK9i use showed significant improvement in atheroma parameters with statistical significance, long-term effects of plaque changes on objective clinical outcomes (e.g., myocardial infarction, mortality) remain to be fully studied, as the included trials focused on surrogate endpoints of atheroma parameters and lipid values.

Conclusion

The addition of PCSK9is to statin therapy in patients with CAD was associated with favorable changes in coronary composition and lipid profile compared to statins alone.
