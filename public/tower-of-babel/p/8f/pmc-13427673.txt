Design and results of a pilot community screening strategy for hepatitis B and hepatitis C to promote early access to treatment among migrant populations from high-prevalence countries residing in Catalonia (MiCatC)
Authors: Venus Forero, Sandra Manzanares-Laya, Hakima Ouaarab, Prat Jordi Gómez i, Anna Not, Marcos Montoro, Esteve Muntada, Jordi Casabona, Elisa Martró, Mayara Torres, María Serrano Gregori, Manuel Gómez-Barrera, Raquel Domínguez-Hernández, Xavier Majó, Joan Colom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Community based interventions are key strategies in achieving viral hepatitis elimination among migrant population.•Health interventions should address the gender perspective by focusing on migrant women.•Multidisciplinary community strategies simplify hepatitis diagnosis and treatment.Keywords: Hepatitis B virus, hepatitis C virus, community screening, migrant, intervention

Introduction

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) and hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection represents a significant public health problem that requires efforts for prevention, diagnosis and access to treatment. The lack of early diagnosis causes the disease to progress, resulting in serious liver complications and even death (World Health Organization, 2024). Treatments in current use have made it possible to control the disease in the case of hepatitis B (Rodríguez et al., 2020) and to cure it in the case of hepatitis C (Calleja et al., 2018).

The World Health Organization (WHO) has set the goal of eliminating viral hepatitis by 2030 (World Health Organization 2016). One recommendation for efforts to achieve this goal pertains to the screening of hepatitis B and C among populations whose countries of origin are characterised by high (≥5%) or medium prevalence rates (between 2-5%) (World Health Organization 2016).

In Europe, the prevalence in the general population is 1.2% for hepatitis B virus surface antigen (HBsAg) and 0.9% for HCV-RNA, (World Health Organization, 2024), whereas these conditions reach 12% and 4.5% (anti-HCV positive), respectively, among refugees and asylum seekers in the host country (Eiset and Wejse, 2017). In Spain, a systematic review estimated that the global HCV seroprevalence among the migrant population was approximately 1.6% (0–7.1%, 95% CI) (Lazarus et al., 2019) but varied according to individuals’ regions of origin (i.e., Eastern Europe, sub-Saharan Africa, North Africa and Asia). With respect to populations from highly endemic countries such as Romania, Pakistan, Nigeria and Senegal, the seroprevalence of HCV ranges between 0.7% and 3.3%, whereas that of HBsAg ranges between 2.2% and 10.0% (Polaris Observatory Collaborators 2023, Polaris Observatory HCV Collaborators 2017).

Recent community screening studies have revealed an HBsAg prevalence of 9.2% in migrant population from West Africa in Spain (Picchio et al., 2023) and of 3.6% in Mongolia as well as prevalences of anti-HCV of 5,8% and of 0.5% HCV-RNA among this population (Palom et al., 2023). At the beginning of 2021, 15.88% of the population of Catalonia had a foreign origin; this fact increased to 17.24% in 2023 (Idescat, 2024). A cross-sectional study estimated that, in Catalonia in 2017, the prevalence of HBsAg was 0.5% and that of anti-HCV was 1.1% (Rodríguez-Tajes et al., 2020). Although few studies have investigated the prevalence of these infections in the host country, a study conducted in Catalonia to investigate a community intervention among migrants from West Africa reported a prevalence of HBsAg of 9.2%, while another study that focused on the Pakistani population reported a prevalence of anti-HCV + 4.6% and RNA-HCV + of 1.4% (Martró et al., 2022).

One challenge involved in the task of eliminating viral hepatitis pertains to the difficulty of reaching populations that find it difficult to access the health system, such as the migrant population (Gimeno-Feliu et al., 2011). These difficulties pertain to language, cultural, socioeconomic and administrative barriers (Moonen et al., 2023, Falla et al., 2018). Alongside the asymptomatic nature of these infections, this issue contributes to the failure to detect these individuals until they reach a more advanced stage of liver disease (World Health Organization, 2017), thus increasing the number of cases of hidden infection and the corresponding complications.

Therefore, the different action plans developed to address viral hepatitis in the European region include prioritising community-based actions with the goal of improving efforts to prevent new infections and intensify screening and treatment among these populations (World Health Organization, 2017, European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, 2016) as well as to reduce existing barriers to improved health outcomes (Alarcón et al., 2019, Ahmad et al., 2018). A study that estimated the number of HCV cases among the migrant population in Spain and the impacts of different microelimination strategies in this context highlighted the need to develop community screening strategies for populations from endemic countries who could benefit from access to treatment (Alarcón et al., 2019).

The general objective of the MiCatC project was to design and implement a pilot community-based screening intervention pertaining to health care and early access to treatment for HBV and HCV for immigrant population from high-prevalence countries residing in Catalonia. The specific objectives of this pilot project were (1) to design and implement an invitation, screening and referral circuit for migrants with hepatitis B and C in Catalonia; (2) to describe the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, country origin, and HBV/HCV prevalence in migrants participating in the strategy; and (3) to increase the knowledge and self-awareness of these individuals with regard to viral hepatitis

Methodology

Design of the strategy

The strategy involved an intervention aimed at increasing knowledge on hepatitis B and C and self-awareness of infection status by hosting educational workshops including screening for HCV and HBV in different community settings, such as religious sites, associations, or community centres in different territories: the coastal area of the city of Barcelona (Barcelona Litoral), Hospitalet de Llobregat and its surroundings (like the south metropolitan area), and the cities of Tarragona and Lleida.

Selection of the relevant population and territories

Countries with a medium or high prevalence of HCV were first identified through a review of the literature being Romania, Pakistan, Nigeria, Senegal and Gambia (European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, 2016, Alarcón et al., 2019).

Subsequently, information from the most recent national population census in Catalonia was consulted to determine the number of people per municipality for each of the selected nationalities. Population density data were used to identify the distribution of migrant adults residing in Catalonia according to their places of origin, and to define the nationalities to be screened in each territory.

Finally, thresholds were established as criteria to define the territories where the intervention would take place: a seroprevalence >3% in the country of origin among populations with more than 1000 inhabitants per municipality, and >5% in municipalities with fewer than 1000 inhabitants from the same country of origin. The objective of this process was to identify approximately 500 or more individuals per target population, a target established a priori based on pragmatic considerations to ensure a balanced distribution of recruitment efforts and contribute to the overall study sample.

Consequently, the countries of origin by region were as follows: Romania and Nigeria were selected for the Tarragona region, Senegal and the Gambia for Lleida, and Pakistan for the southern metropolitan area and Barcelona Litoral.

In addition, the inclusion criteria for the selected populations were to be aged ≥18 years and to have resided in Catalonia for at least 3 months. Individuals under 18 years of age, those residing in Catalonia for less than 3 months, and visitors without intention to reside were excluded.

Intervention

HBV and HCV screening

CE-IVD marked rapid detection tests for HCV (anti-HCV; Turklab Tibbi Malzemeler San. Ve TIC. A.S.) and HBV (HBsAg; (DETERMINE HBsAg 2, Abbott Molecular; analytical sensitivity of 0.1 IU/mL) were performed. In anti-HCV positive participants capillary blood was collected as dried blood spots (DBS) as previously reported (Not et al., 2025) for confirmatory HCV-RNA testing. DBS samples were shipped at room temperature to a central laboratory. One spot was eluted and analysed in the m2000 system with the Abbott RealTime HCV assay (Abbott Molecular) according to the manufacturer's instructions. This assay has been approved for in vitro diagnostics using DBS samples (CE-IVD) and has a lower limit of detection of 462 IU/mL.

Follow-up after the intervention

After DBS testing in the laboratory, which was accomplished in less than two weeks, the community nurse contacted participants with viraemic HCV infection to schedule appointments with the hepatology service of the referral hospital. All participants with a positive HBsAg rapid test were also referred to the hepatologist. Each territory featured its own circuit for diagnosis, referral and access to treatment, which were established through agreements with the hepatology service of each hospital.

Follow-up of patients in the hospital varied depending on the nature of the infection in question. In HCV infection, an initial consultation with the hepatology clinic was made to perform complementary tests and initiate treatment, while a follow-up visit was scheduled 3 months after the end of treatment with the aim of assessing the extent of the cure of the disease by determining the viral load and the therapeutic attitude in cases involving recurrent viremia. In hepatitis B cases, after the initial assessment visit, at which complementary tests were performed and treatment was considered, a follow-up visit was proposed at 6 months, except for some cases in which, owing to the nature of the infection, the individuals in question were referred to primary care for follow-up. In addition, for all patients who received a negative result for any of these viruses, attendance to primary care was recommended to enable them to check their vaccination status for HBV and the administration of the vaccine for HBV if they did not have immunity due to previous exposure or vaccination (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

Implementation

Planning of the intervention groups

A session involving all the relevant actors was hosted to present the programme and establish the work plans that had been adapted to each territory. Multidisciplinary working groups were established; these groups included peer educators, community agents, nurses, hepatologists, and public and community health personnel. Similarly, peer educators and case manager nurse were trained in their roles for the project, and care circuits were established with the hepatology services.

The fieldwork teams in each territory consisted of two peer educators from the nationalities that were included in this intervention alongside a case manager nurse. The peer educator was a person of the same sociocultural origin as the population targeted by the intervention and was the individual who was responsible for the community activities of this project. The case manager nurse was primarily responsible for coordinating the fieldwork team in each territory as well as managing referrals and subsequent follow-up of positive cases. The tasks assigned to each agent involved in this project are defined in Table S1.

Design of the educational session

The informative and educational materials that were necessary for data collection used as part of this intervention were prepared and adapted to each population; furthermore, the referral circuit for the positive cases was created and refined for each territory.

The peer educators were responsible for contacting and organising meetings with members of associations or groups of migrants, religious sites, and cultural or meeting centres as well as with mediators or civil or religious community leaders associated with each of the immigrant populations in each territory; the aim of this process was to obtain access to these populations and present the project as well as to facilitate recruitment.

Recruitment was done by peer-to-peer communication, through social media, or via informal invitations posted in bars or stores frequented by the target audience.

Each intervention involved a 2-hour group session or workshop or an individual session lasting 20-25 minutes among these organizations, interest groups or places of congregation for immigrants of the same origin. In these sessions, an informative brochure concerning the project was provided to all persons who were invited to participate with the goal of ensuring informed consent; information concerning the intervention and the treatment of the data was also provided. All the documentation and materials used was made available in several languages (i.e., Catalan, Spanish, Urdu, French, Wolof, and Romanian). Consent was provided in writing with the help of the peer educator or community agent, and each participant was assigned an identification number that served to pseudonymise their data. At the time of the intervention, a baseline epidemiological questionnaire was administered to all participants in an electronic format, and sociodemographic, clinical and health information as well as risk factors were collected. Information was also collected concerning the characteristics of the nonparticipants and their reasons for rejection. This information and the corresponding documentation were collected online via REDCap. In addition, to empower the population, the interactive digital tool HEPARJOC (Ouaarab et al., 2020) was used in the workshop; the purpose of this tool is to acquire and share knowledge regarding viral hepatitis as well as to highlight the importance of early diagnosis.

Ethical aspects

This project was implemented in accordance with Organic Law 3/2018 on Data Protection as well as with Regulation (EU) 2016/679 of the European Parliament (Reglamento (UE) 2016/679, 2016); furthermore, it was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Jordi Gol i Gurina Foundation under code 20/218-P.

Statistical analysis

The results were shown in terms of the number and percentage of people who agreed to participate and the prevalence rates of HBV and HCV, and their 95% confidence intervals (IC); furthermore, they are segmented by gender and country of origin as well as the clinical characteristics of the participants and the corresponding impacts on the cascade of care with respect to each of the viruses.

Self-awareness of disease status was evaluated by comparing test results with self-reported data.

Results

Reach

As part of the MiCatC project 1,505 people were screened over a period of 10 months.

Of the total number of migrants who attended the workshops 3.7% (58/1563) refused to participate; the most frequent reasons for rejection were “I don't want to take the test now”, which accounted for 19 cases (32.8%), “I am afraid of the result”, which accounted for 12 cases (20.7%), and "I'm not prepared", which accounted for 6 cases (10.3%). The characteristics of these individuals and their reasons for rejection are detailed in Table S2.

Among the 1,505 people screened as part of this intervention (including 71.4% men and 28.6% women), the majority were of Pakistani origin and were screened in Barcelona Littoral or in the southern metropolitan area. Among members of most nationalities, the rate of participation was higher for men than for women, with the exception of Romania, in which context women accounted for 58.8% (87/148) of the total participants. The nationality that exhibited the lowest level of representation of women was Senegal (15%, 36/235) (Table 1).

Most of the participants (52.1%) had obtained a secondary education. In terms of gender, 21.9% of the women had participated in higher or university-level studies, whereas only 8.7% of the men had done so. In contrast, 23.4% of the women were employed (48.6% focused on housework), whereas 68.4% of the men were employed. The main reasons for these individuals migration to another country also differed between genders: 63.9% of these individuals had migrated for reasons pertaining to work (including 80.8% men), whereas 27.4% reported family reunification (including 67.2% women). Effectiveness

HBV testing results and linkage to care

Among the total number of people screened for HBV infection, 3.0% (95% CI 2.2-4.0) (n=44) were HBsAg positive (91% men); the countries that exhibited the highest prevalence in this context were Senegal and Gambia, thus maintaining the pattern of gender differences observed across all the countries of origin (Fig. 3, Table S3). Lleida exhibited the highest number of HBsAg positive people related to having the major population of Senegal on its territory (Table S4). Most of the HBsAg-positive people were screened in religious sites, except for Senegal origin who were screened in the community centre, and Pakistan in religious sites. A total of 88.6% of the individuals who tested positive had a health card, and 97.7% had resided in Catalonia for more than 1 year. With respect to these individuals’ history of exposure, 16% of the HBsAg-positive individuals had undergone surgery or injectable treatments, while 97.7% denied having been vaccinated for HBV or did not remember having been vaccinated. Of the total number of HBsAg-positive participants 11 reported a previous diagnosis, while 33 (75%) were new diagnoses, who improved their understanding of their condition (self-awareness of disease status).

HBsAg: hepatitis B surface antigen

Regarding the follow-up, 65.7% of the HBsAg positive people attended the hepatology consultation; only one of these individuals was a woman, and 2 of these individuals fulfilled the indications for antiviral treatment. No cases of advanced fibrosis or hepatocellular carcinoma were observed, nor were any persons coinfected with HDV.

HCV testing results and linkage to care

A total of 3.3% (95% CI 2.42-4.28) (n=49) of the people screened were anti-HCV positive (84% men), and Pakistan being the place of origin that exhibited the highest representation in this context (93.9%; 46/49 cases) (Fig. 4, Table S5). In terms of gender, the trend for men to receive more representation than women were observed among people born in Pakistan, unlike Romania, where more than half of the people screened were women; an HCV infection was detected in one such individual. Barcelona Littoral and the southern metropolitan area accounted for more than 95% of the positive cases detected in this context. Among the people with anti-HCV-positive result, the majority were screened in religious sites, 98% had an individual health card, and 94% had resided in Catalonia for more than 1 year.

Of the total number of positive for HCV RNA (16 participants), 13 attended the first hepatology consultation and initiated treatment, while 12 completed the clinical follow-up and achieved a sustained virological response 12 weeks after the end of the treatment. Among the liver elastograms performed (n=5), only 20% (n=1) indicated stage F4, and none indicated hepatocellular carcinoma. All viremic participants were newly diagnosed (they were unaware of their status) and, therefore, they went on to improve their self-awareness thanks to the strategy.

Adoption

Approximately, 50 entities and community leaders were contacted to promote engagement and facilitate the implementation of the screening and referral circuit.

Implementation

A total of 150 community screening interventions were implemented from 2021-2022, of which more than half (55.4%) took place in religious sites, 11.9% in community centres, 10.9% in associations, 8.9% in private homes, and the remaining 13% in other locations. Individuals from Pakistan, Nigeria and Romania were screened mostly in religious sites and, in the first two cases, also in associations.

During the project, 11 training sessions were delivered to peer educators and case manager nurses.

Maintenance

The intervention was a pilot study, so it was not continued. However, data from this study and the HepBClink study (Not et al., 2025) have subsequently been used for cost-effectiveness analyses to inform public health implementation in the future (Martró et al., 2026).

Discussion

The results of this pilot community screening strategy (MiCatC) reinforce the need, feasibility and relevance of reproducing this type of community intervention, which focused on viral hepatitis and another infectious diseases (Pottie et al., 2011). This strategy demonstrated that establishments specific circuits on the basis of the health system to facilitate cultural adaptation to vulnerable migrant populations with help of community assets, are key in this context. The implementation of this strategy allowed 1,505 people to be screened through numerous screening interventions, which linked relevant actors with community leaders. Although we do not have data regarding the total number of people who received information from the workshops, with regard to the migrants who attended these workshops, only 58/1563 (3.7%) refused to participate. The low rejection rate and the presence of new HBV and HCV diagnoses among attendees suggests a high acceptability of the intervention among people who accessed the community education and screening events and evidence an increase in self-awareness about the infection status among participants.

Among the study populations, Romanian and Pakistani nationals were among the five most common foreign populations in Catalonia at the time of the intervention. Furthermore, the Pakistani population is highly concentrated in this region within Spain as a whole. Given that both groups are among the largest migrant-origin populations in Catalonia in terms of population size, the study can be considered broadly representative.

Despite possible administrative barriers, most of the people screened possessed an individual health card (96%), a phenomenon which is associated with universal health coverage policies in Catalonia. (Burón Pust, 2012). These findings highlight the importance of integrating these groups into the healthcare system and of strengthening community-based initiatives among migrant populations. Although the majority held a health card, we found people with infections who had not been diagnosed or were not being monitored. In addition, the exploration of integration into the host country on the basis of knowledge of the two official languages of Catalonia revealed that more than half of these individuals (53.4%) reported speaking Spanish well and fluently, with slightly higher rates of men exhibiting skill in Spanish than women as well as, to a lesser extent (12.8%), Catalan. If we account for the fact that more than 70% of the participants in this research had lived in Catalonia for more than 5 years, the low level of knowledge of the official languages can represent as a barrier to their ability to access health services (Burón Pust, 2012).

On the other hand, despite the feminisation of migration (Idescat, 2024), the population screened in this context was mostly male (3 out of 4 people were male), with the exceptions of the populations of Romania and Gambia, in which a slightly higher percentage of participants were women, which may have been due to the fact that the peer educators were women or to the space in which screening was offered. In the Senegalese population, the percentage of males was higher (ratio of 3 to 1), perhaps because the main asset pertaining to this community, that is, the leader, was a man (Idescat, 2024). Additionally, we observed differences in the levels of studies and work. Although women exhibited higher levels of education than did men, in terms of work, approximately half of the women screened were engaged in household chores (4% men); in contrast, most men were employed. In terms of these individuals’ history of exposure, we also observed differences between the genders, such that women with higher levels of exposure through surgeries, injection treatments or transfusions than did men. More than 80% of the participants denied having received previous HBV tests; in this context, the percentage of women was higher than that of men. Although some of these data coincide with the ones reported in previous studies (Idescat, 2024), these results highlight the need to incorporate the gender perspective into community-type health interventions, especially when these interventions involve migrant populations, where other axes can interact with gender inequality, such as ethnicity and social class (Artazcoz et al., 2018).

Regarding the results concerning HBV, the prevalence among the Senegalese population of Lleida was the highest among all the populations screened at 7.23 (95% IC, 4.40 -11.53). However, this percentage was lower than those that have been reported in similar studies conducted in Catalonia, including 8.2% (Martró et al., 2022, Not et al., 2025) and 9.2% (95% CI, 6.8–12.4%) (Picchio et al., 2023), as well as to the estimated value for the country of origin (10.0%) (Polaris Observatory Collaborators 2023). Despite the low number of people screened in the Gambian community (15 people), 2 individuals (13.3%) were HBsAg positive; this rate was higher than the expected prevalence in the country of origin (6.0%) (Polaris Observatory Collaborators 2023). Another recent study conducted in Catalonia estimated that the prevalence of HBsAg was 18.8%, (95% CI 14.4–23.2), in a sub-Saharan population that attended a health centre. In this case, the population was different from that on which our study focused, which represented a “healthy” migrant population (as opposed to individuals attending a health centre), which could explain the higher prevalence observed in that context (Cruz et al., 2024). Although the Pakistani population was the most representative, it exhibited a prevalence of positive HBsAg of 1.7% (95% CI, 1.01–2.72%), which was higher than published results in another study that was conducted under the same conditions (0.9%) (Martró et al., 2022, Not et al., 2025) ; however, it was lower than the expected prevalence (2.2%) (Polaris Observatory Collaborators 2023) as well as that of the Nigerian community, which was 1.3% (as compared with the expected 8.1%) (Polaris Observatory Collaborators 2023). Finally, the Romanian community exhibited a prevalence of HBsAg of 4.7% (95% CI, 2,09–9,87%), which was much higher than another study conducted in Catalonia (1.4%) (Martró et al., 2022, Not et al., 2025) but close to prevalence in the country of origin (4.4%) (Polaris Observatory Collaborators 2023).

In our analysis, no cases of positive anti-HCV results were observed among in the Senegalese, Gambian, and Nigerian community, although prevalences of 5.3% (95% CI, 2.9–9.1) (European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control, 2016), 0.8% (Polaris Observatory HCV Collaborators 2017), and 0.7% (Polaris Observatory HCV Collaborators 2017) were expected, respectively. In comparison with other studies, Martró et al. reported an anti-HCV prevalence of 0.6% among the Senegalese population (Martró et al., 2022, Not et al., 2025), while Cruz et al. reported a prevalence of 1.1% in sub-Saharan population with a lower limit of 0 in terms of its 95% CI (Cruz et al., 2024); which is consistent with the prevalence we observed in our study. Similarly, the seroprevalence observed among the Pakistani population in our intervention (4.5%; 95% CI, 3.3-5,6), with an active infection rate of 1.6%) is consistent with the previously published results of seroprevalences ranging from 3.7–4.6% (1.4% active infection) with respect to a similar community intervention in Catalonia (Martró et al., 2022, Not et al., 2025). Similarly, the Romanian community exhibited an anti-HCV prevalence of 0.7% (95% CI, 0.02-3.71), which was clearly lower than the expected value of 1.4% (95% CI, 1.0-1.9) (Hleyhel et al., 2024) as well as the reported value in another community study (1.4%) (Martró et al., 2022, Not et al., 2025).

The prevalences observed as part of the MiCatC project reinforce the claim that adapting health interventions to the characteristics of each migrant population community. This allows members of these populations to approach the health system, thus expanding the scope of these interventions. On the other hand, the variation among the prevalences observed in this context may be due, among other factors, to the sustainability of community interventions among each population, in which context the Pakistani population was the most representative (68% of the total screened). This prevalence was similar to that described in the bibliography, possibly due to the previous performance of community work in this territory (Martró et al., 2022). This contrasts with other screened populations where the community work and the driving group were joined in the beginning of the project without establishing trust or solid relationships with the previous health system. Accordingly, the MiCatC project made it possible to describe the characteristics of these populations and thus identify their needs, which could facilitate more effective adaptations of community interventions and enable individuals to become familiar with this type of intervention and its benefits. Regarding the effectiveness of this strategy in the care cascade (linkage to care and access to treatment), our study is in line with previous strategies that have focused specifically on the detection and treatment of HCV cases in Catalonia (Martró et al., 2022, Not et al., 2025).

One limitation of this study pertains to the difficulties involved in the process of establishing different teams consisting of peer educators and community nurses in territories in which community care teams have not yet been established for the migrant population. The low rates of participation in the intervention on the part of women highlights the need to prioritise a gender approach to the design, implementation, monitoring and evaluation of this intervention. Additionally, the participation of women should be enhanced by considering the role that they play in each culture as well as in the process of selecting members of the driving group and ensuring that they have the capacity to establish a balance in the interventions. In addition, due to cultural barriers and the fact that locations included religious sites, and some questions regarding exposure to risk factors related to sexual practices or drug use could not be asked in this context; thus, no information could be obtained.

Among the different strengths of the intervention, the peer educator, as part of the driving group, could inspire low levels of rejection and promote high levels of awareness among the groups analysed in this context once they had been accessed. The two main reasons for rejection were reported to be "I do not want to take the test" and "I am afraid of the result"; thus, the need for health education was reinforced by people who had knowledge of relevant cultural, socioeconomic, or educational as well as customs that could facilitate trust and establish a solid bond with the goal of overcoming various barriers to migrants’ participation in the intervention and their subsequent adherence to treatment.

This intervention aimed to reduce the barriers between the health system and members of the migrant population who exhibit vulnerable conditions while taking into account language adaptation, since this issue represents one of the greatest obstacles in the process of implementing this type of strategy (Brown et al., 2023). In our case, all the documentation and material available were translated into the main official languages of the intervention in line with the linguistic and cultural domain of the peer educator. In addition, despite the good results regarding access to the individual health card, a direct circuit was developed to manage the health card associated with the intervention, which offered the possibility of reaching people who lacked access to the public health system due either to ignorance or other limitations.

Finally, the integration of the different types of care and interdisciplinary work alongside various health assets was essential to guarantee transversal and coordinated work at all levels, including centralised DBS analysis in a single clinical microbiology laboratory and the multiple hepatology services depending on each location. In addition, the simplification of the diagnostic circuit made it possible to perform the tests and obtain initial results in the same place where the intervention was implemented, thus allowing a greater number of participants to be captured and a guided and controlled follow-up process to be implemented to avoid the loss of people who received a positive test.

Conclusions

The MiCatC project has proven to be an effective community strategy with regard to the microelimination of HBV and HCV among migrant populations from endemic countries, including by establishing a simplified model of diagnosis and access to treatment. This project represents an integrative and community model of public health in Catalonia, whose purpose is to guarantee equal access to treatment and care for viral hepatitis, thereby ensuring universal access to health services among members of all populations.

The participation of an interdisciplinary team and their collaboration with influential actors within migrant communities facilitated transversal and cross-cultural work, which enabled these populations to be integrated into the health system more effectively. As in the case of other health strategies, viral hepatitis elimination strategies must maintain a focus on gender, including by promoting the inclusion of women from high-prevalence countries and adapting to their specific needs to guarantee equitable access to care.

Future research should determine whether HBV and HCV infection occurs in migrants’ country of origin or during the process of migration or whether it is related to reception conditions in the country of destination. In addition, it is advisable to conduct a specific analysis according to migrants’ different places of origin. In light of the sample size of the populations included in our intervention, it was not possible to conduct an analysis that could identify cultural differences among exposure factors and the included populations that could influence the results and follow-up. Therefore, future research can analyse these differences more effectively, thus allowing more specific tailored interventions to be developed. Similarly, it is essential to continue exploring new tools that can be used to bring the health system closer to these populations through the establishment multidisciplinary teams, including considering the social determinants that affect the migrant population, such as their socioeconomic situations, ability to access health services and relevant cultural barriers.
