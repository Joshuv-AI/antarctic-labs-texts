Expression of miRNAs Associated With Embryo Development and DNA Damage Response in Porcine Embryos
Authors: Gabriel Pineda, Fernanda Luiza Facioli, Mariana Priotto de Macedo, Vanessa Guay, Luke Currin, Karina Gutierrez, Vilceu Bordignon, Werner Giehl Glanzner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEmbryo genome activation (EGA) is a crucial event implicated in proper embryonic development. Similarly, the DNA Damage Response (DDR) is essential for correcting or preventing occasional errors during embryonic cell division that could result in embryo development arrest or the propagation of genetic abnormalities. Although both EGA and DDR are regulated by epigenetic mechanisms, the role of microRNAs (miRNAs) in these processes has not been explored in pig embryos. Herein, we assessed the abundance of miRNAs linked to embryo development and DDR across four developmental stages: Day 2 (D2), Day 3 (D3), and Day 4 (D4), and at the blastocyst stage, as well as after DNA damage induction. mRNA levels of EGA‐related genes confirmed our timepoints as representing EGA timeframe, while immunofluorescence for γH2AX validated DNA damage induction. Significant decrease in blastocyst rate and total cell number per blastocyst was detected in UV‐exposed embryos. Analysis of miRNA abundance revealed increased levels of miR‐200a‐5p on D3, which were partially maintained on D4. Both miR‐15a and miR‐24‐3p increased on D4, but their levels were downregulated in UV‐exposed embryos at the same stage of development. In blastocysts, UV exposure upregulated miR‐29a‐3p and miR‐344b‐3p. Together, these findings provide the first characterization of miRNAs expression in porcine embryos during EGA and following DNA damage induction.

Introduction

Reproductive success and improved fertility are of great importance for the agricultural sector. However, intensive animal selection, to which livestock have been subjected, is often associated with a decline in fertility and reproductive performance (Brito et al. 2021; Pryce et al. 2004). As infertility rates increase, the use of assisted reproductive technologies (ARTs), such as in vitro fertilization (IVF), has become more widespread. Therefore, improving ARTs plays a central role in animal agriculture (Hernandez Gifford and Gifford 2013; Menchaca 2023). Optimizing ARTs requires a deep understanding of embryo biology and culture conditions, as these factors are critical for the generation of high‐quality embryos (Marsico et al. 2023; Simopoulou et al. 2018). Hence, elucidating the mechanisms regulating early embryo development is crucial for improving ART outcomes and, ultimately, improving fertility.

The development of a healthy embryo relies on the interaction of multiple molecular and regulatory factors that modulate proper programming and ensure normal progression (de Macedo et al. 2021). Among these processes, embryo genome activation (EGA) and the DNA damage response (DDR) are two of the most critical events during early development (Liu and Grosshans 2017; Musson et al. 2022). EGA marks the transition from maternal to embryonic control of development, when the embryo initiates transcription of its own genome (Lee et al. 2014). This event is tightly regulated by epigenetic mechanisms such as histone modification enzymes, transcription factors, chromatin remodelers, and genome stability regulators (De Iaco et al. 2017; Eckersley‐Maslin et al. 2018; Glanzner et al. 2018, 2020; Østrup et al. 2013; Reik 2007). Failure in EGA and proper epigenetic programming results in embryonic arrest (Glanzner et al. 2020; Hernandez Mora et al. 2023). Hence, successful EGA is essential for establishing a healthy embryo, sustaining pregnancy, and producing viable offspring. Similarly, DDR is essential for maintaining genome integrity and stability during embryo development (Bohrer et al. 2018; Dicks et al. 2017, 2020). To mitigate DNA damage, cells employ specific pathways, such as homologous recombination (HR) and nonhomologous end joining, thereby preserving genomic integrity (Karran 2000). In embryos, HR is more frequently utilized, since the use of the identical sister chromatid reduces the risks of introducing errors into the DNA sequence (Bohrer et al. 2018; Chapman et al. 2012). These repair pathways rely on numerous components, including kinases, transcription factors, epigenetic regulators, and DNA repair proteins such as ataxia telangiectasia mutated (ATM), ataxia telangiectasia and Rad3‐related (ATR), RAD51, and breast cancer gene 1 (BRCA1) (Kujjo et al. 2012; Stiff et al. 2004, 2006). Proper regulation of these factors is vital to ensure embryonic survival and normal development.

While the transcriptional regulation of both EGA and DDR remains not fully understood, the contribution of major regulators of gene expression, such as microRNAs (miRNAs), has not been investigated in the context of porcine early embryogenesis. miRNAs are small noncoding RNAs that regulate gene expression post‐transcriptionally (Bartel 2004, 2009; Shang et al. 2023). They are known to regulate several DNA repair mechanisms through repression of transcription factors, kinases, or other DNA components (Li et al. 2022; Liao et al. 2015; Mocavini et al. 2019; Rahman et al. 2014). Evidence suggests that miRNAs are required for oocyte growth, maturation, and early embryogenesis in several species, including pigs (Kaczmarek et al. 2020; Prather et al. 2009; Rosa and Brivanlou 2017). In bovine, miRNAs have been shown to be critical for preimplantation embryo survival (Paulson et al. 2022). Moreover, miRNAs contribute to maternal mRNA clearance, a process crucial for EGA and proper chromatin configuration (Bushati et al. 2008; Lund et al. 2009; Rosa and Brivanlou 2017).

Given the importance of miRNAs in cell biology and embryonic development, and the crucial roles of EGA and DDR in ensuring normal embryo progression, this study aimed to evaluate the abundance levels of miRNAs previously associated with embryo development, EGA, and DDR processes during early development of porcine embryos. miRNA profiles were assessed during the EGA timeframe (D2–D4) and at the blastocyst stage (D7), in embryos exposed or not to UV radiation to induce DSBs and activate DDR. Validation of the EGA timeframe and DDR model was performed by assessing the mRNA levels of EGA‐related genes and the presence of the DSBs marker γH2AX in UV‐exposed embryos.

Materials and Methods

All chemicals were purchased from Sigma Chemicals Company (Sigma‐Aldrich, Oakville, ON, Canada), unless otherwise mentioned.

Oocyte Collection and In Vitro Maturation (IVM)

Porcine ovaries were collected at a local abattoir (CBCo Alliance Inc., Les Cédres, Quebec, Canada) and transported to the laboratory in saline solution containing 100 UI/mL penicillin and 10 mg/mL streptomycin at 30°C–35°C. Upon arrival, cumulus‐oocyte complexes (COCs) were aspirated from follicles of 3 to 6 mm in size using a 10 mL syringe and 20‐gauge needle. COCs were selected for IVM based on the presence of a homogenous granulated cytoplasm and at least three layers of cumulus cells. Pools of ~30 COCs were matured at 38.5°C in 5% CO2 and 95% air for 22 h in 90 µL drops of in‐vitro maturation media consisting of TCM‐199 (Life technologies, Burlington, ON, Canada), supplemented with 20% porcine follicular fluid, 0.1 mg/mL Cysteine, 10 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (Life Technologies), 0.91 mM sodium pyruvate, 3.05 mM D‐glucose, 20 µg/mL gentamicin, 1 mM cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), 5 IU/mL hCG (Intervet Canada Corp, Kirkland, QC, Canada), 10 µg/mL FSH (Vetoquinol, Lavaltrie, QC, Canada). After 22 h of IVM, COCs were transferred to a similar medium without cAMP, FSH, and LH, for an additional 22 h.

Parthenogenetic Activation and Embryo Culture

After 44 h of IVM, COCs had their cumulus cells removed by vortexing in TCM‐199 HEPES‐buffered medium supplemented with 0.1% hyaluronidase. Denuded oocytes were parthenogenetically activated as described by de Macedo et al. (2018). Briefly, oocytes were exposed to 15 µM Ionomycin for 5 min, followed by 15 min in 200 µM Zn2+ chelator N,N,N0,N0‐Tetrakis (2‐pyridylmethyl) ethylenediamine (TPEN). Oocytes were then cultured in Pig Zygotic Media 3 (PZM‐3) supplemented with 3 mg/mL (0.3%) bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 7.5 µg/mL cytochalasin B for 4 h. After activation, presumptive zygotes were cultured in 60 µL drops of IVC media (PZM‐3 plus 0.3% BSA) at 38.5°C with an atmospheric air of 5% CO2. On Day 5 of development, culture media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Embryo cleavage was determined 48 h post‐activation, and development to blastocyst was assessed on Day 7.

DNA Damage Induction

Pools of ~30 cleaved embryos were equally distributed on D2 of development to ensure homogeneity across experimental groups. DNA damage was induced as previously described (da Silva et al. 2022). Briefly, embryos at Day 2 (D2), Day 3 (D3), Day 4 (D4) or Day 7 (blastocyst stage) of development were placed in a 35 mm plate containing 1 mL of pre‐warmed IVC medium and exposed to UV radiation for 10 s in a biosafety cabinet (1300 Series Class II; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States). UV irradiation was performed using a 30 W UV‐C lamp (Philips, Eindhoven, The Netherlands) with a wavelength of 253.7 nm, located approximately 60 cm above the culture dishes containing the embryos. After UV exposure, embryos were returned to culture for an additional 2 h to allow for the DDR. Control embryos followed the same manipulation but were not exposed to UV light. Following this period, embryos (pools of 20 embryos per group for D2, D3, and D4, or 10 blastocysts) were rinsed in PBS solution and collected in 100 µL of TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and then stored at −80°C for subsequent RNA extraction and purification.

RNA Extraction and Reverse Transcription Quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR)

Total RNA, including small RNAs, was isolated from pooled embryos using the column‐based Direct‐zol RNA Microprep Kit (Zymo Research) according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNase digestion was performed to degrade any contaminating DNA. Reverse transcription of miRNAs and mRNA was performed by using miScript II RT Kit (QIAGEN) and SuperScript VILO cDNA Synthesis Kit (Life Technologies), respectively. For the miScript II RT Kit (QIAGEN), the 5X HiSpec Buffer was selected for the quantification of mature miRNAs. RT‐qPCR reactions were performed in a CFX 384 real‐time PCR system (BioRad, Hercules, CA, United States) and employing the qPCR master mix (Wisent Bioproducts, StBruno, QC, Canada). For miRNA amplification, universal reverse primer (miScript SYBR Green PCR Kit) was used in combination with the forward primer (mature miRNA sequence; Table S1). For mRNA amplification, the primers utilized in this study were designed using the accession number or previously characterized by our group (Table S2). Relative abundance for the genes and miRNAs was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The gene H2A and the miRNA miR‐93 (Mahdipour et al. 2015; Timoneda et al. 2012) were used as endogenous controls for mRNA and miRNA, respectively.

Immunostaining for γH2AX and Cell Counting Analyses

Developing embryos were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and permeabilized with 1% Triton X‐100 in PBS for 1 h at 37°C. For immunostaining, samples were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in blocking solution (3% BSA and 0.2% Tween‐20 in PBS), then incubated overnight with primary antibody anti‐γH2AX (EMD Millipore – 05‐636), diluted (1:500) in blocking solution. Negative control samples were incubated in blocking solution overnight in the absence of the primary antibody. Samples were then rinsed three times for 30 min each in blocking solution before incubation in the dark for 1 h at room temperature in the presence of the secondary antibody, anti‐mouse IgG Cy3‐conjugated (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories – 115‐165‐146, West Grove, PA, United States; 1:1000) diluted in blocking solution. Embryos were then incubated in 10 µg/mL of DAPI for 10 min, followed by a rinsing step with permeabilization solution. Finally, embryos were mounted on microscopic slides using Mowiol Mounting Medium. Slides were then examined using a Nikon Eclipse 80i microscope (Nikon). Finally, images were captured at 40X using a Retiga 2000R monochrome digital camera (QImaging, Surrey, BC, Canada), and blastocyst cells were counted using the ImageJ software.

Statistical Analyses

Data were obtained from 4 independent replicates. Statistical analysis was performed using JMP Pro 17 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, United States). Differences in mRNA and miRNA abundance across more than two groups were analyzed by ANOVA, followed by the LSMeans Tukey‐Kramer Honestly Significant Difference test. Comparisons between control and UV‐treated groups were performed using the t‐test. Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Results are presented as means ± SEM, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. A total of four replicates were performed in all experiments.

Results

Validation of the EGA Embryo Model and Expression of miRNA Biogenesis‐Related Genes

To confirm that the time points of embryo collection accurately reflect the EGA timeframe (pre‐EGA, D2; EGA, D3; and post‐EGA, D4), we initially evaluated the mRNA abundance of EGA‐related genes (e.g., Dppa2, Dppa4, Eif1ax, and Kdm5b) at these specific time points. We observed an increase in mRNA levels of Eif1ax and Kdm5b on D3, as well as a tendency for an increase in Dppa2 and Dppa4 transcripts, although not statistically significant (Figure 1A). These results confirm that our sampling timepoints align with the onset of EGA. We then assessed mRNA levels of the miRNA biogenesis‐related genes Dgcr8 and Dicer and observed a decreasing trend from D2 to D4 of development (Figure 1B).

Expression Profile of miRNAs During the Onset of EGA

Since embryos collected at D2, D3, and D4 showed upregulation of EGA markers at the expected onset of EGA (D3), we further analyzed the abundance of mature miRNAs previously assessed during embryo development and EGA or known to regulate EGA genes in pigs or in other species (humans, mice, rats). Out of 12 selected miRNAs reported to play a role in embryo development or EGA (ssc‐miR‐381‐5p, ssc‐miR‐381‐3p, ssc‐miR‐486, ssc‐miR‐21‐5p, ssc‐miR‐29a‐3p, hsa‐miR‐29c‐3p, ssc‐miR‐200b, hsa‐miR‐200c‐5p, hsa‐miR‐200c‐3p, hsa‐miR‐200a‐5p, rno‐miR‐344b‐5p, and rno‐miR‐344b‐3p), four (ssc‐miR‐486, ssc‐miR‐29a‐3p, hsa‐miR‐200a‐5p, and rno‐miR‐344b‐3p) resulted in quantifiable amplification (Figure 2A). Among these, only hsa‐miR‐200a‐5p presented a bell‐shaped expression pattern, with higher levels on D3 compared to D2, partially maintained through D4 (Figure 2A). We also selected 12 DDR‐related mature miRNAs (ssc‐miR‐383, ssc‐miR‐421‐5p, ssc‐miR‐101, ssc‐miR‐24‐3p, ssc‐miR‐15a, ssc‐miR‐18a, ssc‐miR‐185, ssc‐miR‐421‐3p, ssc‐miR‐374b‐5p, ssc‐miR‐374b‐3p, ssc‐miR‐21‐5p, and ssc‐miR‐15b) and evaluated their abundance at D2, D3, and D4 of development. Of these, seven (ssc‐miR‐15a, ssc‐miR‐18a, ssc‐miR‐24‐3p, ssc‐miR‐101, ssc‐miR‐185, ssc‐miR‐383, and ssc‐miR‐421‐5p) showed quantifiable amplification (data not shown). Only two (ssc‐miR‐15a and ssc‐miR‐24‐3p) showed differential expression, with both showing higher levels on D4 compared to D3 (Figure 2B).

UV Exposure Impairs Embryo Development and Quality

To further investigate miRNA expression in DDR, we initially characterized embryo responses to UV exposure. Oocytes were parthenogenetically activated (resulting in an average cleavage rate of 62.9% ± 5.4% in four replicates) and then either exposed or not to UV light for 10 s at D2, D3, D4, or the blastocyst stage. Embryos were collected 2 h after UV treatment for mRNA and miRNA expression and immunostaining analyses. A proportion of embryos was cultured until D7 to assess blastocyst rates. UV exposure markedly impaired embryo development, with a ~3‐fold reduction on D2 (15.8% × 45.5%), a ~6‐fold reduction on D3 (6.4% × 42.6%), and a ~5‐fold reduction on D4 (8.6% × 48.9%) compared to control embryos not exposed to UV (Figure 3A). Embryo quality, assessed by total cell number at the blastocyst stage, was significantly reduced by UV exposure at all stages (Figure 2B). Immunostaining for γH2AX confirmed increased incidence of DSBs in embryos 2 h after UV exposure (Figure 3C).

After validating our UV‐exposure model, we analyzed the expression of seven DDR‐related miRNAs (ssc‐miR‐15a, ssc‐miR‐18a, ssc‐miR‐24‐3p, ssc‐miR‐101, ssc‐miR‐185, ssc‐miR‐383, and ssc‐miR‐421‐5p) in embryos exposed or not to UV. Notably, ssc‐miR‐15a and ssc‐miR‐24‐3p presented lower levels in embryos exposed to UV on D4 (Figure 4). Expression Profile of miRNAs in Blastocysts Exposed or Not to UV

Finally, we evaluated the expression of all selected miRNAs (EGA‐ and DDR‐related) in D7 blastocysts exposed or not to UV. Of the 24 miRNAs tested, 12 showed quantifiable amplification (Figure 5). Among embryo development/EGA‐related miRNAs, ssc‐miR‐29a‐3p and rno‐miR‐344b‐3p were upregulated in UV‐treated blastocysts compared to controls (Figure 5A). Interestingly, only two DDR‐related miRNAs (ssc‐miR‐15a and ssc‐miR‐15b) were detected at this stage, with no obvious differences in abundance between UV‐exposed and control embryos (Figure 5B).

Discussion

Embryo development is characterized by numerous critical events, one of the most important being EGA, which marks the beginning of transcriptional autonomy. During EGA, several epigenetic regulators, including demethylases, acetylases, and other chromatin‐modifying factors, are required to modulate chromatin and promote transcription (de Macedo et al. 2021; Eckersley‐Maslin et al. 2018; Glanzner et al. 2018, 2020; Liu and Grosshans 2017; Reik 2007). Proper regulation of epigenetic marks and effectors is essential for normal embryo development. Genome stability is also crucial, making DDR and efficient DNA repair mechanisms vital for successful embryogenesis and species perpetuation (Musson et al. 2022). DNA damage has been shown to negatively affect embryonic developmental potential (Bohrer et al. 2013, 2015; Dicks et al. 2017), with the HR pathway playing a key role in maintaining genome integrity and enabling normal embryo development (Bohrer et al. 2018). Although the importance of both EGA and DDR is well established, the role of miRNAs in these processes remains poorly understood in porcine embryos. Our study attempted to address this gap by assessing the expression profile of miRNAs during EGA and DDR. A total of 24 miRNAs were selected based on reported associations with embryo development, EGA, or DDR in pigs and other species (Brunner et al. 2012; Galluzzi et al. 2010; Hu et al. 2010; Liao et al. 2015; Manoel‐Caetano et al. 2019; Rahman et al. 2014; Rezaeian et al. 2020; Rosa and Brivanlou 2017; Shen and Houghton 2013; Song et al. 2011; Wang et al. 2013; Wu et al. 2013). All DDR‐related miRNAs analyzed have been previously described in human cancer cell types and are known to interfere with or participate in the ATM/ATR signaling or DNA repair pathway. Regarding embryo‐related miRNAs, the selected candidates (e.g., miR‐200 family, miR‐344, miR‐29a, miR‐486) have been described to play roles in embryonic stem cell functions, embryo development, cell differentiation, or regulation of genes involved in EGA across multiple species, including human, mice, and rats (Douvris et al. 2022; Kim et al. 2017; Mocavini et al. 2019; Sundararajan et al. 2022; Sweat et al. 2021; Tokumaru et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2020). Since some of those miRNAs are not annotated in pigs, we tested homologous miRNAs from humans, mice, and rats, some of which were successfully amplified. The selected miRNAs were screened for amplification, and those with quantifiable amplification were further analyzed.

Before quantifying miRNA abundance, we validated the EGA timeframe in porcine embryos, which occurs between the 4‐ and 8‐cell stages (Telford et al. 1990), and confirmed it by the upregulation of EGA‐associated genes (de Macedo et al. 2021; Glanzner et al. 2018). Recently, several markers of EGA have been described, including KDM5B (Glanzner et al. 2020), DPPA2 and DPP4 (De Iaco et al. 2019; Eckersley‐Maslin 2018; Eckersley‐Maslin et al. 2019), and EI1FAX (Magnani et al. 2008). We confirmed EGA by evaluating the expression of these markers, which peaked around D3, corresponding to the expected EGA window at ~72 h post‐fertilization during the four‐cell stage (Tománek et al. 1989). We also assessed the mRNA expression of Dicer and DGCR8, which are critical components of the miRNA machinery (Bartel 2004; Carthew and Sontheimer 2009; Shang et al. 2023). Both DGCR8 and Dicer transcripts were detected from D2 to D4, albeit with decreasing levels, consistent with maternal mRNA degradation (Paulson et al. 2022). However, additional studies should characterize the expression of these miRNA‐biogenesis regulators at the later stages of porcine embryo development. Interestingly, DGCR8 also has a miRNA‐independent role in UV resistance through phosphorylation at serine 153 (Calses et al. 2017), although in our study, DGCR8 mRNA levels were not altered by UV exposure (data not shown).

Among the embryo development/EGA‐related miRNAs analyzed, only miR‐200a‐5p showed differential expression in the EGA window (D2‐D4). We observed an upregulation of this miRNA, consistent with the known role of the miR‐200 family in ectodermal development and cell proliferation (Sweat et al. 2021). These miRNAs are regulated by histone modifications (H3K4me3 and H3K27me3) near their genomic regions (Enkhbaatar et al. 2013). We observed that both miR‐200a‐5p and Kdm5b were upregulated, underscoring the need for further investigation to determine whether there is a link between KDM5B and miR‐200 cluster expression. Knockdown experiments will be needed to clarify their exact role and relationship in the regulation of porcine EGA. In addition, miR‐486 and miR‐29a‐3p, known repressors of KDM5B (Mocavini et al. 2019; Tokumaru et al. 2021), showed similar expression patterns, suggesting potential regulatory interactions.

To explore the role of miRNAs in DDR, we initially confirmed that UV exposure during the EGA window reduced embryo development and total cell number, consistent with our previous studies (Bohrer et al. 2018; da Silva et al. 2022; Dicks et al. 2020; Glanzner et al. 2020). UV‐induced DNA damage was validated by increased γH2AX foci, a marker of DSBs (Bonner et al. 2008; Rogakou et al. 1998). Since porcine embryos primarily repair DNA using the HR pathway (Bohrer et al. 2018), we evaluated the expression of DDR‐associated miRNAs related to this pathway. Both miR‐15a and miR‐24‐3p were downregulated by UV exposure at D4 of development. This downregulation is in line with a role in facilitating DDR, as miR‐24‐3p represses H2AX and ATM (Brunner et al. 2012; Lal et al. 2009), while miR‐15a inhibits the HR repair pathway (Hater et al. 2022), and its downregulation enhances DNA repair (Patel et al. 2017). The downregulation of these miRNAs likely reflects an embryonic attempt to augment DNA repair. In line with this, miR‐185 and miR‐383, which are repressors of ATR kinase (Liao et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2013), showed similar trends for downregulation following UV exposure, but without statistical significance.

The exposure of blastocysts to UV light altered the abundance of miR‐29a‐3p and miR‐344b‐3p. We found that only five miRNAs (miR‐200a‐5p, miR‐344b‐3p, miR‐29a‐3p, miR‐15a, and miR‐486) were consistently expressed both at the EGA window and at the blastocyst stage. MiR‐29‐3p is of particular interest as it has been implicated in recurrent miscarriages (Yang et al. 2018) and gestational diabetes mellitus (Gillet et al. 2019), suggesting a role in placental development and as a potential biomarker of embryo stress. On the other hand, miR‐344 has been linked to the activation of two‐cell‐like transcriptional states in mouse embryonic stem cells (Yang et al. 2020), and miR‐344b‐3p has been associated with neuronal morphogenesis in mice (Liu et al. 2014), but their roles in the context of DDR have not been investigated. These observations suggest a possible role for these miRNAs during early embryogenesis.

Despite the promising findings from this study, it is important to acknowledge some unavoidable limitations. First, only a single endogenous miRNA (miR‐93) was used for normalization, which was selected for its reported stability across embryo developmental stages (Mahdipour et al. 2015; Timoneda et al. 2012). Attempts to amplify other reference candidates (e.g., miR‐26a and miR‐191) were unsuccessful. Second, our experimental design did not account for paternal (sperm‐derived) miRNAs, which are increasingly recognized as important regulators of early embryonic development (González and González 2025; Isacson et al. 2025; Yuan et al. 2016). Sperm‐borne miRNAs, such as miR‐34c, have been shown to regulate maternal RNA degradation, cell metabolism, cell proliferation, and embryo implantation in mice (Cui et al. 2023). In humans, sperm miRNAs have been proposed as biomarkers for embryo quality in IVF (Isacson et al. 2025), while in bulls, they are suggested to influence the developmental competence of two‐cell embryos and metabolic activity in blastocysts (Wu et al. 2020). In pigs, sperm miRNAs have been linked to sperm motility and morphology (Curry et al. 2011); however, their specific contributions to porcine embryo development remain not clearly described. Future studies in porcine embryos should integrate both maternal and paternal miRNA contributions to better elucidate their roles in EGA, DDR, and overall embryonic development.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study is the first to assess the expression patterns of miRNAs associated with regulators of EGA and DDR in porcine embryos. We found that miR‐200a‐5p is relatively more abundant on Day 3 of development (the time of EGA). In addition, differences in expression were observed for miR‐15a and miR‐24‐3p on Day 4, and for miR‐29a‐3p and miR‐344b‐3p at the blastocyst stage when comparing control and UV‐exposed embryos. These findings suggest that miRNAs may play important roles in embryonic processes such as EGA and DDR. Further studies investigating both maternal and paternal contributions will provide deeper insights into the role of miRNAs in the epigenetic regulation of early embryonic development.
