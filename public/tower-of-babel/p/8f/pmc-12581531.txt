“Ringleader who has no power”: a qualitative study of parent uncertainty during a NICU admission unrelated to prematurity
Authors: Erin Rholl, Steven R. Leuthner, Kathryn J. Malin, Joanne Lagatta, Krisjon R. Olson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParents of preterm infants experience stress related to uncertainty which impacts parent health outcomes. Less is known about the experience of parents whose infants are admitted for reasons other than prematurity. In this study we sought to describe (1) the experience of parents whose infants are admitted to a level IV NICU for reasons unrelated to prematurity in relation to uncertainty and (2) the effect of social and structural determinants on parent experience with uncertainty.MethodsThis single center qualitative study utilized interviews, questionnaires, and chart review at 2 time points: 2 weeks after admission and 3 months after discharge. Parents enrolled in a prospective study of parent mental health related to infant medical complexity were purposively selected to achieve a sample representative of the study NICU. Interviews occurred between July 2019 – September 2020.Results49 parents of 35 infants completed initial interviews and 26 completed follow up interviews. Parents experienced uncertainty related to diagnostic and prognostic trajectory as well as experiences prior to admission and NICU process of care. Themes included: (1) Parents of infants with medically complex presentations experience significant uncertainty. (2) Lived experience of structural vulnerability helped parents manage medical uncertainty. Some parents more readily accepted uncertainty due to prior experiences though often had fewer resources for managing needs after discharge. (3) Discrimination created mistrust preventing adaptation to uncertainty. (4) Provider counseling impacted parent adaptation. Counseling accounting for families lived experiences and focusing on practical aspects of a NICU admission such as team lead, how multidisciplinary teams arrive at decisions and steps to decrease uncertainty increased parent acceptance.ConclusionParents of NICU infants admitted for reasons other than prematurity experience uncertainty related to both medical and non-medical events during their NICU stay. Communication that practically guides parents through NICU uncertainty, assesses structural vulnerability, and fosters trust in the medical system may help parents adapt to NICU uncertainty.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40748-025-00232-9.

Introduction

While extensive research has examined the challenges faced by parents of preterm infants in the NICU, much less is known about the experiences of parents whose infants are admitted for reasons unrelated to prematurity [1, 2, 3]. These parents often face a different set of uncertainties, as outcomes and length of stay may not be tied to a maturing postnatal age. This distinction is critical, as studies suggest that parents of non-preterm NICU infants report higher rates of depression and lower health-related quality of life (HRQL) compared to parents of preterm infants [3, 4].

Beyond medical care, social and structural determinants of health (SDOH) may influence how parents experience uncertainty in the NICU [5]. While structural vulnerabilities, such as financial instability or discrimination, are known to impact health outcomes, they may also inform how people navigate uncertainty [6, 7, 8]. Understanding these interactions is essential to improving support for families during and after NICU stays.

Objectives: This study aimed to describe (1) the experience of parents whose infants are admitted to a level IV NICU for reasons unrelated to prematurity in relation to uncertainty and (2) the effect of social and structural determinants on parent experience with uncertainty.

Methods

This Institutional Review Board approved this qualitative study conducted at a level IV NICU utilizing semi-structured interviews, surveys, and chart review. We screened for eligible cases as patients were considered for enrollment in a prospective cohort study between 2019 and 2020 [9, 10]. The original cohort included parents of NICU infants with a length of stay > 14 days and excluded parents who would not be caring for the infant after discharge or were not fluent in English or if the infant’s death appeared imminent [9, 10]. Parents were eligible for our study if their infants had a primary admission diagnoses unrelated to prematurity. This included any term infant admission, as well as any premature infant admission to the study NICU specifically for congenital conditions, such as gastroschisis, CDH or cardiomyopathy. Eligible parents were given an informational handout about the interview study after enrollment in the cohort study. If they indicated interest, they were approached for enrollment in person (E.R.). Purposive sampling allowed for a representative sample of study NICU along demographics and diagnoses [11] (Table 1, eTable 1). Participants were given a $30 check for after completing interviews. Enrollment was stopped once thematic saturation was reached as determined by iterative analysis [11, 12].

Data collection

Data collection occurred twice: at study enrollment and 3 months after NICU discharge from July 2019-September 2020. Participants answers to demographic questions and Protocol for Responding to and Assessing Patient’s Assets, Risks and Experiences (PRAPARE) were reviewed to assess SDOH [13]. Chart review included demographics and notes (neonatology, consultants, social work, security). Interviews were in person at enrollment and via phone after discharge. We developed an interview guide using a qualitative approach (Supplemental Material) [12, 14]. Open-ended questions examined experiences significant to the NICU and medical uncertainty. To assess parent socioeconomic background, we utilized the Structural Vulnerability Assessment Tool (SVAT), assessing 8 domains of structural determinants (financial, residential, environment, food, social network, legal, education, discrimination) [6]. During the interview phase, we adopted an iterative approach analyzing the interviews, refocusing our questions, and conducting additional interviews. We also asked spontaneously generated questions, specific in each case, to probe for clarification of participants’ responses and to follow new, relevant topics that participants raised. Parents in a relationship were interviewed separately or together per preference. Interviews were in English (one Spanish), transcribed, and de-identified. Coding and analysis followed COREQ guidelines [15].

Data analysis

Investigators (ER, KO) coded separately and performed iterative thematic analysis using NVivo 12 [12]. Validity was checked by triangulating questionnaires, chart review, and interviews [16]. To analyze parent social and structural background, interviewers assessed structural vulnerability assigning values of 1–5 to each of the SVAT domains based on coding of interviews, charts and questionnaires. Scores of ≥ 20 were considered high structural vulnerability. Participants were stratified into high (significant) and low (not significant) experiences with structural vulnerability and compared experiences with uncertainty. Cohen’s Kappa coefficient measured inter-rater reliability as 0.84, indicating excellent agreement [17].

Results

Thirty-five families (49 parents) completed initial interviews and 24 families (26 parents) completed follow up interviews (Fig. 1).

Our interviews demonstrated that parent experience with medical uncertainty was not driven by medical aspects like diagnosis and prognosis alone. The process of receiving medical care and parents’ prior life experiences also contributed to their experience with uncertainty. Within this framework, four key themes emerged: (1) Parents of infants with medically complex presentations experience significant uncertainty, (2) lived experience of structural vulnerability helped parents manage medical uncertainty, (3) discriminatory bias prevents therapeutic relationships and adaptation, and (4) provider counseling impacts parent adaptation.

Theme 1: Parents of infants with medically complex presentations experience significant uncertainty

Parents reported uncertainties regarding diagnosis, prognosis, and hospitalization logistics (e.g. length of stay and team composition). Most parents’ uncertainty gradually decreased. For others, uncertainty about their infant’s prognosis remained high until they transitioned home and established relationships with outpatient providers. Some reported their infant’s trajectory was more uncertain than premature infants who improve over time, such as this mother whose infant received a neurologic diagnosis.“Premature babies, they just have to grow. They just have to get bigger. My baby, who knows?” C8.

“Premature babies, they just have to grow. They just have to get bigger. My baby, who knows?” C8.

While her infant’s condition is expected to become clearer over time, it was not tied to postnatal gestational age making it more difficult for her to anticipate time points for reassessment.

In general, parents whose infants had medical diagnoses described trial-and-error processes such as this mother whose infant with cystic fibrosis was working through feeding challenges:

“Their plan is to start formula again tomorrow through an NG tube…But it’s all just a lot of unknowns…And so, it’s all just kind of trial and error.”C23 Her experience was shared by other parents who had difficulty accepting fluid care plans as medical conditions evolved.

As medical complexity increased and more consultants became involved, parents more often perceived a lack of plan, no clear team leader and inadequate communication. One mother interfacing with 6 consultant teams noted:“Kind of difficult [with multiple teams] you feel like the ringleader who has no power… sometimes you don’t get all the information and that’s kind of frustrating…I kind of thought that like our core team would maybe more so have been [the ringleader], or maybe more so be able to help guide us through that information.” C34.

As more teams became involved, her uncertainty about her infant’s condition and care plan logistics increased.

Additionally, parents with unexpected admission described significant and enduring uncertainty, a focus on survival, less team connection, and less social support due to lack of preparation. This mother whose infant had postnatal concerns for choanal atresia noted:“He was not a preemie. He was raced to NICU because of his breathing situation…Those first few days I honestly thought he was dead, and nobody was trying to tell me.” C9.

“He was not a preemie. He was raced to NICU because of his breathing situation…Those first few days I honestly thought he was dead, and nobody was trying to tell me.” C9.

She was worried about her infant’s survival and did not understand what the NICU team was evaluating for her infant.

Of note, parents receiving prenatal diagnoses reported less initial uncertainty. Even for cases with medical complexity, a prenatal diagnosis allowed parents to understand how the teams will communicate and develop care plans as well as prepare their finances, work, and connect with their social network. In addition, parents of infants with primarily surgical diagnoses also reported less uncertainty about their conditions. They often received a prenatal diagnosis, perceived the lesion as “fixable,” described a concrete plan and identified a leader.

Theme 2: Lived experience of structural vulnerability helped parents manage medical uncertainty

Parents with structural vulnerability – who had routinely experienced uncertainty related to circumstances such as finances, immigration status and educational barriers – reported integrating medical uncertainty into their lives more quickly than those without structural vulnerability. Some described medical uncertainty as manageable or less worrisome than everyday issues of changing relationships, employment, or housing insecurity. One mother whose son had multiple anomalies reflected on how her refugee experience eased her acceptance of uncertainty in the NICU:“When you have something so traumatic, I think it like changed a lot of my views …Whatever’s going to happen, and what do you do? You just kind of get through it.” C33.

“When you have something so traumatic, I think it like changed a lot of my views …Whatever’s going to happen, and what do you do? You just kind of get through it.” C33.

A father who spent time in prison reflected similarly on how his past experiences allowed him to accept uncertainty in the NICU.“It’s hard to be [patient] at times, but I think I’ve mastered it. [Being] in prison humbled me, made me humble myself. It taught me patience…It’s hard to have patience, especially dealing with stuff like this, but like I said, when you got five and a half years to lay on a bunk and you ain’t going nowhere, you learn all the patience in the world.” C19.

“It’s hard to be [patient] at times, but I think I’ve mastered it. [Being] in prison humbled me, made me humble myself. It taught me patience…It’s hard to have patience, especially dealing with stuff like this, but like I said, when you got five and a half years to lay on a bunk and you ain’t going nowhere, you learn all the patience in the world.” C19.

Parents who had not previously experienced structural vulnerability often focused on uncertainties related to prognosis and daily management plans, dedicated to changing their infant’s outcome. This mother with no significant prior life uncertainties described:“I’ve always felt like life’s kind of gone in my favor. I haven’t had a lot of like really too hard of challenges and [the NICU] has just like thrown me for a total loop. Because it’s just something you so can’t control and you’re waiting on everyone to tell you what’s going on…I’m guilty of going on Google when [the team] tells us something and then I think I become this expert asking “But what about, but what about?” C15.

She had prolonged difficulty integrating medical uncertainty and accepting her lack of ability to change her infant’s outcome.

In contrast, parents with structural vulnerability more often reported that while they had no control over their infant’s condition, they were able to focus on other immediate life concerns if the team was adequately managing urgent medical matters. A mother whose infant required a vesicostomy, and potentially needed dialysis, reported her primary uncertainties in the NICU as being able to work and pay rent rather than her infant’s medical course:“Since I had to deliver early…the middle of the month, I had worried about covering my rent for July…I was like how am I going to pay for this? But hopefully it all works out. I’m hoping because the job I was at, I was there for seven months. So, I think I am qualified to try to apply for unemployment, so I have taken that step. So, we’ll see.” C6.

Her son’s prognostic uncertainty was less concerning than financially supporting him once discharged, highlighting overlapping complexities that families in the NICU often navigate.

Other parents reported similar logistical barriers they deemed more worrisome than their infant’s uncertain prognosis which they could not change. A Spanish-speaking father to an infant with a neurologic diagnosis reported more frustration with lack of access to Spanish language services than concern over prognostic uncertainty. His partner, who spoke English, reviewed a conversation with a potential home nurse:“[Nurse said] ‘If [father] ever has questions, he can just message me in Spanish, and I’ll get translation.’ But what if it’s something emergent? I’m going to bring her home with oxygen and a G-tube, how’s that going to work?” C14.

While the NICU team focused discussions on prognostic uncertainty, the more significant uncertainty for the parents surrounded services after discharge and the father’s immigration status. This left the parents reporting isolation.

Of note, documentation of life stressors varied across charts and questionnaires compared to interviews. Examples include housing insecurity (reported in 6 interviews, 0 questionnaires, 2 charts) and temporary legal status (reported in 6 interviews, 1 questionnaire, 2 charts). Housing insecurity was not reported in the chart or questionnaire for two mothers reporting that as their primary concern during interviews.

Theme 3: Discriminatory bias prevents therapeutic relationships and parent adaptation

Parents reported discriminatory bias related to race or ethnicity ((10/49), 20%), primary language ((3/49), 6%), education ((2/49), 4%), and lifestyle ((4/49), 8%). Discrimination occurred at transferring hospitals and the study hospital, both impacting the experiences of parents at the study hospital. Discrimination was not limited to parents with low socioeconomic status (SES). This Black mother with a graduate degree and leadership position at her company shared:“I don’t expect everyone to fully embrace me or minorities as a whole. I can’t speak for my entire race, but I just feel like, common courtesy. I thought I had to, you know, shut up and put up…That’s why a lot of times I just ask to be alone, you know. I got my necessity things and I’m like, no, I don’t need you for anything else” C9.

“I don’t expect everyone to fully embrace me or minorities as a whole. I can’t speak for my entire race, but I just feel like, common courtesy. I thought I had to, you know, shut up and put up…That’s why a lot of times I just ask to be alone, you know. I got my necessity things and I’m like, no, I don’t need you for anything else” C9.

She experienced discrimination at her delivery hospital, impacting her initial experiences with the NICU team. Experiences of discrimination contributed to poor communication and mistrust leading to decreased parent understanding of the team’s management.

Parents reporting discrimination followed by positive interactions described repaired relationships. One Black couple reported feeling discriminated against regarding their race and education level at their transferring NICU. They described an improved relationship with the study NICU team after positive interactions:

“I had some nurses in there that were like, I mean they were horrible…[One time] I was having a panic attack…[the nurse] wasn’t listening to me. She’s like, ‘I have your narcos.’…she was just trying to feed me pills and didn’t care about my concerns…I [also] felt like the doctors were kind of like trying to dummy down information to me, like the way that I was told here that he has muscle tone issues, when over there, they just said, “Well, he’s just too chill.” I’m like, “What does that mean? I don’t understand that. What does ‘too chill’ mean?” You know, they withheld a lot of information…Here every decision that’s been made, it was brought to us first, even if y’all made it prior, y’all still brought it to our attention.” C17.

They shared that communicating in an open and unbiased way allowed for a better relationship with the study NICU.

Some experiencing discrimination described permanently damaged relationships with the team, especially if hospital security was involved. This was true even when security was called for staff support rather than de-escalation as reported by this Black mother:“[Dad] showed his frustration a little bit, but he walked out which was the right thing to do [per behavior contract due to prior staff concerns about marijuana smell]. Then as we were walking out, we see security coming up here. That was unnecessary and uncalled for, because he did the right thing which was to walk away. And security was still called for what reason? Just why, after the first incident it’s just been stuff constantly, people saying stuff to us constantly like something we’re doing wrong. You get down this path where it’s like hard to get back. It’s kind of weird right now, but hopefully we’re going home soon.” C26.

Theme 4: Provider counseling impacts parent adaptation

When reflecting on counseling specifically surrounding medical care, parents described aspects that fostered or hindered their adaptation to the NICU. Parents developed more agency as they learned more about their infant’s condition and NICU process of care. Helpful information included the team structure, identification of a team leader, and team expertise. If consultant teams were involved, parents appreciated descriptions of how teams communicate and arrive at decisions. When there was uncertainty around diagnosis or prognosis, valuable information included evidence used to direct care, concrete plans, balance of known versus unknown and suspected trajectory. Parents receiving that counseling felt more comfortable with medical care:“It was still a lot of gray area just because you never know what to expect but they did the best that they could to tell us, ‘Okay, like this is what to expect.’ They were pretty brief in the long-term plan, but they gave us a general idea about how surgery works, kind of what to expect in the NICU…it was a vague description and understanding that I felt comfortable with.” C2.

“It was still a lot of gray area just because you never know what to expect but they did the best that they could to tell us, ‘Okay, like this is what to expect.’ They were pretty brief in the long-term plan, but they gave us a general idea about how surgery works, kind of what to expect in the NICU…it was a vague description and understanding that I felt comfortable with.” C2.

This parent understood there was still going to be uncertainty around the outcome but appreciated the description of how the medical teams would approach that uncertainty. As parents gained acceptance, their ability to focus on their infant’s condition increased. However, parents receiving counseling centered around medical uncertainty without specific plans to address uncertainty and descriptions of what was known by the medical teams felt distressed and burdened with assisting the team in finding a path forward. One mother whose son was diagnosed with cardiomyopathy expressed frustration with counseling focused solely on uncertainty:“Just have people keep coming in here to tell you ‘I don’t know. I don’t know? I’m just getting so unbelievably frustrated for you to keep telling me you don’t know. What do you know?’” C1.

“Just have people keep coming in here to tell you ‘I don’t know. I don’t know? I’m just getting so unbelievably frustrated for you to keep telling me you don’t know. What do you know?’” C1.

This mother did not understand the information being used to direct care leading to difficulty accepting the uncertainty surrounding her son’s condition. Parents perceiving deficits in communication, team leadership or expertise lacked confidence in the team and took responsibility to become an expert. One mother of an infant with congenital hyperinsulinism described:“It felt like, oh my God, this is so much more because she has this and I don’t know everything. And some people know even less than me…So, how is this going to work? I felt a lot of pressure to educate myself as best as I could.” C15.

“It felt like, oh my God, this is so much more because she has this and I don’t know everything. And some people know even less than me…So, how is this going to work? I felt a lot of pressure to educate myself as best as I could.” C15.

This mother’s limited experience with healthcare concerns, coupled with a lack of clear guidance from the teams, led to anxiety around uncertainty and the self-imposed burden of trying to educate the care teams.

Discussion

This study evaluated parents of infants admitted to a level IV NICU for reasons other than prematurity. A key finding is that parent experience with medical uncertainty is impacted by lived experience prior to admission and NICU processes of care in addition to medical aspects such as diagnosis and prognosis. This finding is similar to Han et al.’s taxonomy of uncertainty which outlines that issues of uncertainty can range from various aspects of the encounter with the healthcare system such as medical aspects, processes of care, and individual circumstances [18]. Within this framework, we found parents at risk for delayed adaptation based on infant’s characteristics and interactions in the NICU. We also describe counseling practices that helped parents through their experience.

Parents experienced uncertainty related to medical factors (diagnosis, prognosis) as well as related to NICU structures and processes (team composition, how decisions are made). While most parents adapted, the trajectory depended on several factors. Parents receiving a prenatal or primary surgical diagnosis adapted earlier, while those with medically complex infants interacting with multiple specialists adapted later, sometimes after discharge. We suspect this delayed adaptation to uncertainty, often related to the multiple teams and perception of “trial and error” plan, is one reason parents of infants with primarily nonsurgical congenital anomalies and those seeing more specialists may have lower HRQL [19].

Parents without prior experience with structural vulnerability had more difficulty accepting NICU uncertainty and things they could not change in the hospitalization. This aligns with Krick et al.’s study of primarily White, well-educated parents reporting existing coping mechanisms were inadequate for a NICU stay [5]. While these parents had initial difficulty with uncertainty, they more easily accessed resources, allowing some control over time. Parents with lived experience of chronic structural vulnerability often accepted the uncertainty of the NICU admission more quickly. Drawing from prior experiences with challenging circumstances, many recognized the NICU or their child’s prognosis as beyond their control and focused on other important life uncertainties while their child received needed medical care. While this finding was surprising to us, similar patterns of conserving focus have been described as a response for managing uncertainties unrelated to medicine [7, 8, 20]. Parents with higher structural vulnerability may also not have reported distress with medical uncertainty while focusing on other stressors. Since these parents may require more support especially after NICU discharge, structural vulnerability assessments are critical to determining NICU and post-discharge needs, which may allow these parents to participate more fully in their infant’s care as Gorsky et al. describes [21]. Importantly, we did not measure factors such as resilience and mental health disorders that likely impact parent experience with uncertainty. The overlap of uncertainty, structural vulnerability and resiliency deserves further study.

Parents reporting discriminatory bias were also at risk for delayed acceptance of medical uncertainty in part due to alienation from staff and mistrust. Experiences of discrimination, whether occurring in the study NICU or from prior experiences, negatively impacted adaptation. Discrimination in our study occurred across different racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic backgrounds, though most often by Black parents. This finding is concerning and not limited to this study [22, 23, 24, 25]. Our interviews revealed relationships can improve after positive interactions set the stage for trust. Powell et al. also reported improved relationships starting with one interaction [26]. As healthcare systems target systemic biases, providers must also improve individual parent-provider relationships to prevent isolation and mistrust.

Recommendations for care

Parents in our interviews described practices that helped them adapt to a NICU stay.

Incorporating lived experience

Counseling that incorporated family circumstances outside of the medical encounter was crucial. Parents did not fully engage in discussions with providers if there was misalignment between parent and team concerns or feelings of mistrust. To help the team better understand potential impact of parent prior experiences, we recommend eliciting a robust social history. Our interviews captured more structural vulnerability than charts or questionnaires which is not unique to our NICU, as determinants other than employment are often underassessed by NICU SDOH screens [27]. Different techniques should be utilized throughout a NICU stay to identify families’ most pressing concerns. Techniques may include SDOH screens, incorporating questions from the SVAT into NICU intake and utilizing support staff with expertise in these areas such as social workers and psychologists [6]. Extended NICU stays provide an opportunity to obtain information over time. Some families may share sensitive information only after trust is established and for some their circumstances around housing and employment change during an admission.

Directional counseling

Neonatologists largely agree that uncertainty should be shared with families [21]. However, some providers struggle with how to approach uncertainty with families often focusing on the unknowns related to diagnosis and prognosis [27, 28, 29]. Pediatric and neonatal intensivists caring for critically ill children often focus more on scientific based uncertainties until they are exhausted often then transitioning to more practical uncertainties [30]. Importantly, our study found that purely sharing uncertainty without a guiding framework was unhelpful and hindered parents’ acceptance of the clinical situation. We suggest that addressing practical uncertainties earlier for all NICU infants is beneficial for parents. Parents in our study shared practices that helped them adjust to an uncertain NICU course, regardless of prior experiences, such as communication surrounding logistical structures and processes of care and the ways that the teams approached uncertainty. Examples included communicating information about potential outcomes, information used to guide the care plan and how teams work together to arrive at decisions. Descriptions of how care decisions are made were specifically important as more specialty teams became involved. Consistent and practical communication demonstrated to parents the medical teams had the collaboration and expertise to care for their child. Early targeted counseling is vital as higher initial and persistent parent uncertainty is associated with perinatal posttraumatic stress disorder [9].

Limitations

This was a single-center, qualitative study. Additionally, single-center findings may not be generalizable to other centers. However, communication and discrimination themes align with prior work; thus, we expect our findings represent experiences for many NICU families. Although we sought diverse participants, our cohort was primarily White. Experiences like discrimination may be underrepresented. We were unable to reach 11 families for follow-up; even though no families reporting significant experiences with structural vulnerability or discrimination were lost to follow-up, it is possible those lost to follow-up had different experiences than the families that completed two interviews. We acknowledge that the time frame for this study was during a period of Covid visitation restrictions. During this time, we continued to allow parents to visit, yet it could possibly lead to follow up issues or contributed in some way to how parents dealt with uncertainty even though this was not directly mentioned in any of the interviews. While our study had more mothers (35) than fathers (14), fathers are a routinely underrepresented voice in qualitative parent studies and we feel that their inclusion in this study is valuable.

Conclusion

Parents of NICU infants admitted for reasons other than prematurity experience uncertainty related to both medical and non-medical circumstances during their NICU stay. Parents whose infants are medically complex especially those with no prenatal counseling are at risk for delayed adaptation to uncertainty. Counseling the family about NICU processes, team structure and how the team will approach uncertainty, as well as periodic assessment for structural vulnerability during the NICU stay, may improve the experience of parents in the NICU.
