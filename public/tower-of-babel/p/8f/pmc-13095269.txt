Long-term trends of lipid concentrations and lipid control in South Korea, 2007 to 2023: A nationwide representative study
Authors: Hyunyi Yoo, Yesol Yim, Ji Ho Kim, Hyunjeong Kim, Selin Woo, Jaeyu Park, Jee Myung Yang, Yerin Hwang, Dong Keon Yon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Long-term trend studies on lipid concentrations and lipid control among Korean adults up to 2023 are limited. Therefore, this study aimed to assess nationwide trends in lipid concentrations and lipid control, including subgroup disparities, over a 17-year period. We used nationally representative data from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey from 2007 to 2023. Lipid control was defined as total cholesterol (TC) <200 mg/dL among lipid-lowering medication users. Primary outcomes were age-standardized blood concentrations of TC, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and triglycerides (TG), and the proportion achieving lipid control. Survey-weighted linear regression was used to estimate mean differences in lipid concentrations with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and survey-weighted logistic regression was applied to assess subgroup differences in lipid control. A total of 97,129 nonpregnant adults aged ≥20 years were included in the analyses (female, 56.69%). Age-standardized mean TC levels increased in the overall population, from 184.7 mg/dL (95% CI, 183.8–185.6) in 2007–2009 to 189.7 mg/dL (188.6–190.7) in 2022–2023 (mean difference, 5.0 mg/dL [3.6–6.3]). In contrast, HDL-C improved, LDL-C declined, and TG levels showed no significant change. Among lipid-lowering medication users, mean TC decreased from 164.1 mg/dL (95% CI, 161.3–167.0) in 2007–2009 to 152.2 mg/dL (150.8–153.6) in 2022–2023, and lipid control rates increased from 72.97% (67.99–77.95) to 88.09% (86.33–89.85), respectively. However, several groups had significantly lower odds of lipid control: adults aged 20 to 44 years (weighted odds ratio, 0.39 [95% CI, 0.26–0.57]), individuals with high school education or less (0.82 [0.70–0.95]), and those with insufficient muscle strengthening exercise (0.75 [0.59–0.96]), compared with their respective reference groups. Lipid concentrations in Korean adults improved from 2007–2009 to 2022–2023, characterized by lower LDL-C, higher HDL-C, and stable TG levels. Lipid control among adults receiving lipid-lowering therapy also increased steadily over 17 years. However, disparities among younger adults and those with lower education underscore the need for targeted strategies to achieve more equitable improvements in lipid control.

1. Introduction

Since about 2008, declines in US cardiovascular mortality have stagnated, fueling concern that the trend reflects increasing burdens of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia.[1] Many Asian countries have likewise experienced substantial increases in the prevalence and burden of cardiovascular disease and related metabolic conditions (including dyslipidemia, hypertension, diabetes, and obesity).[2] A previous study in East and Southeast Asia showed marked increases in both total cholesterol (TC) and non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels.[3] These shifts impose significant socioeconomic costs, including higher national health care expenditures, reduced labor productivity, and widening disparities in access to care. Early, appropriate lipid management is therefore essential to reduce premature mortality and relieve pressure on public health systems.[1]

However, population-level research on lipid concentrations and lipid control remains relatively limited (particularly in Asian countries). Previous studies have focused largely on Western populations, especially the US and Europe, where long-term surveillance systems and national lipid guidelines are well established.[4] In Asia, several countries such as India and China have reported secular trends in lipid concentrations and the use of lipid-lowering medications; however, many of these studies are restricted to specific age groups, span shorter periods, or lack detailed subgroup analyses.[5] In South Korea, although national surveillance data from the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) have been used to characterize overall trends in lipid concentrations, no study to date has systematically examined trajectories of lipid control among adults receiving lipid-lowering therapy using data through 2023.

Therefore, this study evaluates changes in lipid concentrations among Korean adults from 2007 to 2023, both overall and within subgroups; assesses trends in lipid concentrations and lipid control among adults taking lipid-lowering medication over the same period; and examines differences in lipid control across demographic groups. We hypothesized that lipid profiles have improved over time but with persistent demographic disparities. Among individuals taking lipid-lowering medication, lipid control was defined as TC <200 mg/dL per Korean guidelines,[6] with TC chosen for its consistent measurement across all survey years. These analyses provide current evidence on lipid trends in Korea.

2. Methods

2.1. Survey design and participants

This study used data from the KNHANES, an annual, nationally representative survey administered by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency from 2007 to 2023.[7] KNHANES employs a complex, stratified cluster sampling method to ensure representativeness and minimize bias.[7] Sampling weights are applied to accurately reflect the Korean population structure, enabling reliable national estimates for health and nutrition indicators. For the present analysis, we initially pooled 103,765 nonpregnant adults aged ≥20 years.[1,8] We excluded 6636 individuals with missing values for any lipid fraction (TC, HDL-C, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C], or triglycerides [TG]) or analytic covariates, using complete-case analysis. The excluded participants had missing data for education level (n = 5678), household income (n = 940), weighted values (n = 11), pregnancy information (n = 19), smoking status (n = 24), or private insurance status (n = 21), yielding a final analytic sample of 97,129 individuals (Fig. 1).

The survey spanned 17 cycles (2007–2023). After applying the above inclusion criteria, eligible participants were grouped into 6 analytic periods, with the following sample sizes: 2007 to 2009 (n = 16,570); 2010 to 2012 (n = 17,560); 2013 to 2016 (n = 22,121); 2017 to 2019 (n = 18,387); 2020 to 2021 (n = 11,419); and 2022 to 2023 (n = 11,072). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted using the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

For individuals taking lipid-lowering medication, lipid control was defined as a total-cholesterol concentration <200 mg/dL, consistent with the control threshold used in the 2022 Dyslipidemia Fact Sheets in Korea based on KNHANES data, which aligns with current Korean Society of Lipid and Atherosclerosis guidelines.[6]

2.3. Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on established evidence, previous studies, and relevant clinical or methodological considerations regarding their potential association with lipid-related diseases.[10,11] These covariates included sex, age (20–44, 45–64, 65–79, and ≥80 years),[1] region of residence (urban and rural),[12] body mass index (BMI; underweight, normal, overweight, and obese), private insurance status (yes or no), weight change status (no change, weight loss, and weight gain), smoking status (yes or no),[13] drinking (yes or no), muscle strengthening exercise (inactive, active, and highly active),[14] level of education (high school or lower education, and college or higher education),[13] household income (lowest and second quartile, and third and highest quartile),[13] and lipid-lowering medication use (yes or no).

BMI categories were classified according to the Asian–Pacific guidelines into 4 groups: underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–22.9 kg/m2), overweight (23.0–24.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥25.0 kg/m2).[12] Weight change status was categorized as “no change (within ± 3 kg)”, “weight loss”, “weight gain”, and “unknown.” Muscle strengthening exercise was classified into 3 groups according to weekly frequency: inactive (<2 days per week), active (2–3 days per week), and highly active (≥4 days per week).[14]

2.4. Statistical analysis

Lipid concentration and control trends were analyzed using data from the KNHANES from 2007 to 2023. For trend analysis, the survey years were grouped into 6 analytic periods: 2007 to 2009, 2010 to 2012, 2013 to 2016, 2017 to 2019, 2020 to 2021, and 2022 to 2023. Weighted means and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for age-standardized lipid concentrations of TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, and TG for each analytic period. Survey-weighted linear regression analysis was performed to assess temporal trends and to estimate mean differences in lipid concentrations with 95% CIs and corresponding P-values between the first (2007–2009) and last (2022–2023) periods.[15] Trends and period comparisons were further evaluated for lipid control (defined as the proportion of individuals achieving target levels), with results reported as mean differences with 95% CI and p-values. For the analysis of lipid control across subgroups, survey-weighted logistic regression models were used to estimate weighted odds ratios (wORs) and 95% CIs, adjusted for age. Subgroup analyses were conducted stratified by sex, age, region of residence, BMI, private insurance status, weight change status, smoking status, level of education, and household income. All analyses accounted for the complex survey design and applied appropriate sampling weights to ensure nationally representative estimates, minimizing bias due to annual variations in sample size. Model adequacy was assessed by including a quadratic term for year; as no statistically significant associations were observed for any outcome, linear models were used for all analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary), applying two-sided tests; P-values <.05 were considered statistically significant.

2.5. Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of LDL-C estimation, we conducted sensitivity analyses comparing the LDL-C values calculated using our primary method with those derived from the Martin-Hopkins equation. The Martin-Hopkins equation has been shown to provide more accurate estimates, particularly in individuals with low LDL-C or high TG levels.[16] The β-coefficients were estimated using survey-weighted linear regression models across the entire study period to evaluate temporal trends in LDL-C concentrations, overall and within subgroups by sex, age, BMI, region, and other covariates. The mean LDL-C levels and their temporal slopes showed no meaningful differences between the 2 estimation methods, indicating high concordance. The results showed consistent temporal patterns and comparable β-coefficients across all estimation methods, confirming the robustness of our findings (Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R706).

4. Discussion

4.1. Key finding

This study presents a large-scale analysis and long-term trends in TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, and TG concentrations over 17 years, from 2007 to 2023, involving 97,129 participants. Over the study period, TC showed a slight increase, but other lipid concentrations improved, with LDL-C decreasing, HDL-C increasing, and TG remaining stable. These trends were generally observed in both males and females. Among adults taking lipid-lowering medication, TC showed a continuous decline, and the rates of lipid control steadily increased, indicating more favorable management patterns over time. However, even within the population taking lipid-lowering medication, certain subgroups (particularly younger age groups, those with lower levels of education, and those inactive in muscle strengthening exercise) had relatively low rates of lipid control. These findings suggest that despite overall improvements, disparities among these groups highlight the need for targeted strategies to close gaps in preventive cardiovascular care.

4.2. Comparison with previous studies

International studies on lipid trends have shown varied patterns across populations. Recent U.S. data demonstrate declining mean lipid concentrations, yet lipid control among treated adults remains suboptimal, with evidence of persistent undertreatment. This treatment gap is attributed to limited uptake of lipid-lowering therapy overall and particularly low utilization of non-statin agents.[17] Large-scale surveys from China and India have observed concurrent increases in TC and non-HDL-C levels with expanding use of lipid-lowering therapy.[18] However, these studies offer limited insight into patterns of lipid control, including effectiveness among those taking lipid-lowering medication or differences across demographic and clinical subgroups over time. In this regard, our study extends the evidence by providing long-term, nationally representative data from Korea through 2023 and detailed subgroup analyses.

In addition, previous KNHANES-based studies have reported increasing prevalence of hypercholesterolemia and changes in lipid concentrations.[19,20] However, these studies primarily focused on overall population trends, with limited attention to lipid control among lipid-lowering medication users and disparities within subgroups.[6,21] In contrast, our study characterizes lipid trends and control rates among adults taking lipid-lowering medication and highlights disparities by age and education level. Notably, even within the population taking lipid-lowering medication, relatively low control rates were observed among younger age groups, those with lower educational attainment, and those inactive in muscle strengthening exercise, which may reflect factors such as reduced health literacy, lower perceived cardiovascular risk in younger age groups, differences in adherence and monitoring practices, and limited engagement in resistance exercise that has been associated with modest improvements in lipid concentrations.[22,23] This subgroup focus enables more targeted insights into inequities in preventive cardiovascular care.

4.3. Plausible underlying mechanisms

The favorable changes in lipid profiles observed in our study likely reflect both pharmacological interventions, such as increased use of lipid-lowering therapy,[21] and non-pharmacological factors, including dietary shifts,[24] reduced smoking,[25] and greater health awareness.[20] The 2013 American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology guidelines marked a shift from a cholesterol target-based approach to one emphasizing broader statin use, including high-intensity statins for higher-risk populations and expanded treatment indications.[1] Although these guidelines were developed in the US, they influenced clinical practice globally. It is plausible that similar expansions in statin use and treatment indications in Korea reflected these broader trends, thereby contributing to the observed improvements in lipid control. Supporting this possibility, a nationwide study of Korean patients with myocardial infarction from 2011 to 2020 reported increases in statin prescription rates (from 92.9–97.0%) and high-intensity statin use (from 12.8–69.3%).[21] These therapeutic shifts were accompanied by significant improvements in LDL-cholesterol goal attainment, suggesting that pharmacological interventions played a central role in enhancing lipid control.[26] Taken together, these systemic and contextual factors likely underlie the improvements in lipid control observed in this study and highlight the importance of national-level treatment strategies in addressing lipid management.[27]

4.4. Clinical and policy implications

Effective lipid management is a public health priority given its central role in preventing cardiovascular disease. Several initiatives provide valuable models: healthy people 2030 highlights cholesterol control as a measurable national target,[28] the National Cholesterol Education Program offers structured clinical guidelines that translate policy into practice, and the UN Sustainable Development Goals underscore lipid control as part of global efforts to reduce premature mortality from noncommunicable diseases.[29] Together, these programs have raised awareness and created essential frameworks, yet gaps in implementation and equity remain.

Our findings suggest that strengthening these initiatives through routine lipid screening in primary care, timely intervention for high-risk groups, and integration with equity-driven public health campaigns could help close persistent gaps. In particular, prioritized packages for younger adults and those with lower educational attainment (such as pharmacist-led medication management, fixed-dose combination therapy, and low-cost text-message reminders) may enhance uptake and adherence.[30,31] Embedding such strategies into national health systems and global frameworks has the potential to reduce disparities in lipid control and substantially lower the burden of cardiovascular disease worldwide.[32]

4.5. Strengths and limitations

This study comprehensively examined long-term trends in lipid concentrations and lipid control using 17 years of nationally representative data. Moreover, focusing on individuals taking lipid-lowering medication, the study identifies subgroups (especially younger adults and those with lower educational attainment) with relatively poorer control, thereby informing targeted interventions for more efficient disease management and reduced long-term socioeconomic costs.[20,33]

This study has several limitations. First, some variables, including lipid-lowering medication use and level of education, were collected through self-reported data, which may introduce recall bias, response bias, or other forms of information bias.[20,34] Such biases could affect the accuracy of the variable and potentially influence the study results.[35,36] Second, although lipid-lowering medication use was identified, detailed information on medication class (e.g., statins, ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors), dosage, or adherence was not available.[37] The absence of these factors may limit the ability to evaluate the relationship between lipid management and medication use comprehensively.[37] Medication adherence, in particular, may influence treatment effectiveness and outcomes, and incomplete measurement of adherence could obscure variability within statin users.[38] To mitigate this, we focused on analyzing long-term trends and subgroup disparities, emphasizing population-level patterns rather than individual-level treatment effects. Third, certain lifestyle factors known to impact lipid concentrations such as dietary habits were not measured or adjusted for in the analysis. These unmeasured confounders could bias observed associations due to residual confounding.[39] Nonetheless, we incorporated available relevant covariates such as drinking status, smoking status and BMI in our models to partially account for lifestyle influences, and the large sample size helps to buffer some of the bias arising from unmeasured variables.[40] Fourth, LDL-C concentrations were estimated using calculated values rather than directly measured laboratory data. Although sensitivity analyses comparing LDL-C values derived from the Friedewald equation for TG levels <400 mg/dL and the Sampson equation for TG levels ≥400 mg/dL with those obtained using the Martin-Hopkins equation showed consistent trends, the use of calculated LDL-C may still introduce estimation bias, particularly in individuals with high TG levels. Finally, as this study focused solely on the Korean population, its findings may have limited generalizability to global lipid management trends. Nonetheless, the use of large-scale, nationally representative data over 17 years provides valuable insights into metabolic health patterns and may inform future policy tailored to evolving public health needs.

5. Conclusion

Using nationally representative data from 2007 to 2023, this study examined long-term trends in lipid concentrations among Korean adults. During this period, TC increased slightly, while LDL-C decreased, HDL-C increased, and TG remained stable, reflecting overall improvements in lipid profiles. Among lipid-lowering medication users, lipid control also improved over time. However, subgroups such as younger age groups, those with lower education levels, and those inactive in muscle strengthening exercise showed lower control rates, indicating disparities in lipid management despite overall progress. These subgroup differences suggest that improvements in lipid control were not evenly distributed. Disparity-reducing strategies (including pharmacist-led adherence support, fixed-dose combinations, and text reminders in primary care) could improve lipid management across population subgroups.
