DNA origami assembled spheroid for evaluating cytotoxicity and infiltration of chimeric antigen receptor macrophage (CAR-M)
Authors: Qinyao Zhu, Xiaofang He, Junhua Liu, Heming Wang, Xiaojiao Shan, Guangqi Song, Luo Zhang, Yicheng Zhao, Xiushan Yin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapies have shown remarkable results in patients with hematological malignancies. However, their success in treating solid tumors has been limited. As an alternative candidate for the CAR therapy, CAR-macrophages (CAR-M) have demonstrated activation and phagocytosis directed by tumor-associated antigen (TAA), showing promise in the treatment of solid tumors. Nevertheless, the mechanisms by which CARs direct tumor chemotaxis and invasion of CAR-M remain poorly understood. In this study, we aim to investigate the role of CARs in CAR-M attachment and infiltration using 3D tumor spheroids, which were created by utilizing a novel self-assembling nucleic acid nanostructure decorated living cells (NAC). Our results demonstrated that CAR-M exhibited higher invasion and killing capacity in 2D model and 3D tumor spheroids. In summary, the 3D NAC assembled tumor spheroid model provides a suitable platform for target screening and pharmacodynamic evaluation of CAR-M.

Introduction

In recent years, immunotherapy has revolutionized cancer treatment. CAR-T, as the first personalized cellular therapeutic strategy, has successfully been employed in treating refractory hematologic malignancies, particularly targeting CD191,2. However, significant challenges persist in treating solid tumors, such as low infiltration and high heterogeneity in the tumor microenvironments (TME)3. To address this, CAR-NK (Natural Killer) cell therapy has emerged as a promising alternative strategy, demonstrating efficacy in targeting solid tumor antigens in clinical trials4–6. Another alternative strategy is CAR-M (CAR-macrophage) therapy, which offers advantages in immune cell trafficking and infiltration7,8. For instance, Michael Klichinsky and colleagues delivered CAR structures into human primary macrophages using adenovirus transduction, resulting in a distinctive phagocytic effect, enhanced expression of proinflammatory factors, and cross-presentation of tumor antigens to bystander T cells in vivo9.

Other studies have focused on enhancing phagocytosis to improve the anti-tumor activity of CAR-macrophages10–12. This includes combining anti-CD47 antibodies to disrupt CD47-Sirpα axis interaction and enhance phagocytosis11, optimizing intracellular PI3K-signaling/Megf7 domain11 and MerTK12, and using exogenous chemicals to induce a proinflammatory M1 phenotype in CAR-M, thereby enhancing phagocytosis, tumor killing capability, and cytokine release on target cells13. In summary, CAR-macrophages show promise as a potential treatment for solid tumors.

Animal tumor models are commonly utilized for evaluating the efficacy and safety of cell therapies in a preclinical setting. However, these models are time-consuming, expensive, and raise ethical concerns. As an alternative, 3D tumor spheroids that simulate the tumor microenvironment in vitro have emerged as promising models for preclinical tumor treatment studies. Extensive research suggests that 3D tumor spheroid/organoid models are effective tools for preclinical drug screening and verification14,15. Various 3D tumor spheroid/organoid models have been developed for different solid tumors, including pancreatic cancer16, ovarian cancer17,18, colon cancer19, liver cancer20, prostate cancer21, and bladder cancer22. However, there are limited reports on the use of 3D tumor spheroid models to evaluate the efficacy of CAR-mediated immune cell therapy23–25. One study demonstrated the co-culture of CAR-T cells with spheroids derived from a colorectal cancer cell line to simulate the complex morphology of solid tumors, albeit limited to specific adherent cell lines23. To address these challenges, we propose a novel approach called the NAC strategy, which utilizes DNA origami for assembling a 3D tumor spheroid system26. NAC strategy represents an innovative bioengineering approach for constructing 3D in vitro models, leveraging NAC-Linker as a key building block to achieve efficient cell-to-cell connectivity. This enables the creation of functionally complex and structurally refined in vitro biological models27,28. Given the high uniformity and rapid construction enabled by the NAC strategy, our study aims to investigate the role of CARs in CAR-M attachment and infiltration using 3D tumor spheroids based on the origami assembly technique.

In this study, we employed a novel NACs-based origami assembly technique to model 3D tumor spheroids. We evaluated the biological functional characteristics of CAR-M not only in 2D well/plate-surface cultures but also in a 3D tumor spheroid model. Our experimental data confirmed the targeted recruitment, infiltration, and killing of CAR-M on tumor spheroids within a complex and organized tumor microenvironment. In conclusion, the 3D NACs-origami assembled tumor spheroid model is highly suitable for evaluating pharmaceutical efficacy and screening targets for CAR-M therapy.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

RAW264.7 (RAW), ovarian cancer cell SKOV3 and 293FT cells were purchased from ATCC. RAW, RAW GFP, and RAW CAR-HER2 GFP cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Corning), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Lonza) and 100 U/ml penicillin/streptomycin (Life Technologies). 293FT, SKOV3, and SKOV3-Fluc cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Corning), also supplemented with 10% FBS and 100 U/ml penicillin/streptomycin. SKBR3 and SKBR3 HER2 KO cell lines were provided by our partners and maintained in our laboratory, which was cultured in McCoy’s 5A (Modified) medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 100 U/ml penicillin/streptomycin, and their HER2 protein expression was verified by Western Blot. Above all cells were cultured in 5% CO2 incubators at 37 °C. For the subsequent cell analysis experiment, RAW cells were collected by direct pipetting, and the other adherent cells were digested by 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Gibco).

Human monocyte-derived macrophages (hMDM) in the study were isolated from normal human donor blood using CD14 magnetic beads (Miltenyi Biotec), and peripheral blood was provided by Chinese PLA General Hospital. These CD14+ cells were cultured for 5 days using 100 ng/ml of M-SCF to induce macrophages. Collection and analysis of human donor blood were approved by the Roc Rock Biotechnology ethics committee. The experiment using human blood was agreed upon under the medial and rules for each participating individual and the written informed consent was waived.

The mouse PBMCs in our study were purchased from Oricells company, and the finished product was namely mouse PB-MNC (MOSPB-001F). They were cultured in a complete RPMI medium to ensure their vitality.

Viral production and infection

To construct CAR-M cells based on hMDM cells, a genetically engineered modified adenoviral vector, Ad5F35 adenovirus, was selected as a mediator of gene delivery. The circular pAd5F35-CAR-HER2-mCherry adenoviral vector was linearized by PacI enzyme digestion and transfected into 293A cells for adenoviral packaging using jetPRIME. 293A cells were collected after the CPE phenomenon was observed on day 10. Adenovirus particles can be obtained from 293A cells after two freeze-thaws. Then, it was filtered by a 0.45 μm PVDF filter and concentrated by ultracentrifugation over the CsCl column. The purified adenovirus obtained can be added directly to the cells or stored in the refrigerator at −80 °C.

Preparation of CAR-M cells (based on RAW cells) and SKOV3-Fluc cells were transduced by CAR and luciferase lentivirus particles, respectively. The lentiviruses were produced by transfecting 293FT cells with a third-generation plasmid system, including pMD2.G, psPAX2, and a lentiviral expression vector. The 293FT cells were seeded and transfected using the jetPRIME transfection reagent (Polyplus). 48 h and 72 h post-transfection, cell supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 250 × g for 5 min to remove cell debris. The above lentivirus supernatant was then filtered through a 0.45 μm PVDF filter and concentrated using LentiX (Takara Biosciences). The final lentivirus can be added directly to the cells or stored in a −80 °C refrigerator.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the Eastep Super Total RNA Extraction Kit (Promega) following standard procedures. Subsequently, RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the Reverse Transcription Kit (Promega). Next, the experiments were performed on an RT-qPCR detector (Bio-Rad) using SYBR Green Mix (Vazyme). The relative mRNA expression levels of the target genes were calculated by normalizing for GAPDH in each sample and comparing the cycle threshold (CT) values to a calibration curve. All primer sequences used for real-time PCR analysis are in Table S1.

Flow cytometry analysis

Multi-parameter flow cytometry (BD FACSCanto II) was utilized to analyze CAR expression. For the detection of CAR expression in CAR-M cells, CAR-M cells were prepared and the Fc receptor was blocked with anti-moCD16/32 antibody (1:100, 14-0161-82, Thermo Fisher) or Human TruStain FcX™ (1:20, 422301, BioLegend) for 10 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with Myc tag-APC antibody (1:100, 47029, CST) or HER2-FITC antibody (1:100, 342823, Thermo fisher) on ice for 30 min in the dark. After washing with the FACS buffer, the cells were measured on the flow cytometry machine.

Western blot analysis (WB)

Cell pellets were lysed by RIPA buffer containing PMSF on ice. The total protein concentration was quantified by a BCA kit (Beyotime). The lysates were then mixed with 5× loading buffer and denatured by boiling water. Each protein sample was separated on SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membrane was blocked by 5% skim milk in TBST buffer, and followed by incubation overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, anti-mouse Myc tag antibody (1:1000, #2272, CST), anti-β-actin monoclonal antibody (1:1000, AF0003, Beyotime) or anti-rabbit HER2 monoclonal antibody (1:1000, #4290, CST). On the second day, the membranes were washed three times and incubated with HRP-labeled Goat Anti-Mouse (1:2000, A0216, Beyotime) or HRP-labeled Goat Anti-Rabbit (1:2000, A0208, Beyotime) for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, the membranes were developed with chemistry high-sig ECL Western Blotting substrate (Tanon) and analyzed with the ChemiDoc XR (BIO-RAD).

Live cell staining

Cells were collected according to the routine procedures and centrifuged at 250 × g for 5 min. The final cell pellet was resuspended in PBS or serum-free culture medium. Next, CellTracker Deep Red Dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific, C34565) or CellTracker Green CMFDA Dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific, C2925) was added to the suspension solution at a concentration of 1 μM per 106 cells/ml. The cell-dye mixtures were incubated at 37 °C in the dark. After staining for 30 min, the cells were washed twice with culture medium and can be used for co-culture experiments with CAR or untreated (UTD) macrophages.

Phagocytosis assay

Celltracker fluorescent dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to label macrophages and target cells with different fluorescent colors to distinguish different cells in the co-culture system. Macrophages are able to be identified by flow cytometry by phagocytosis or trogocytosis of the target cell, which results in the intracellular inclusion of two different fluorescent markers. By counting the proportion of FITC and APC double-positive cells in the macrophage cell population, it was possible to accurately quantify the level of phagocytosis of target cells by different macrophages.

Since CAR-M cells are inherently labeled with GFP (RAW CAR) or mCherry (hMDM CAR) fluorescent proteins. CellTracker Deep Red (co-cultured with RAW CAR cells) or CellTracker Green CMFDA Dye (co-cultured with hMDM CAR cells) was used to label tumor cells. Approximately 3 × 105 CAR-M and their control macrophages were pre-inoculated in 6-well plates. Subsequently, an appropriate amount of stained tumor cells was added to achieve a 1:3 ratio of effector to target cells (E/T). After 6 h of co-culture in the dark, fluorescence microscopy imaging and flow cytometry analyses were performed, respectively. FCS data files exported by the BD Diva software were further analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.8.1). Finally, the phagocytic efficiency of CAR-M cells was assessed by double-positive events of FITC and APC.

Tumor killing assay

Based on the Flyfire Luciferase fluorescence reporting system for evaluating killing efficiency, CAR-M and their control macrophages were pre-seeded into a 96-well black plate (Beyotime) at a density of 104 cells/100 μl medium per well. Then target cells were added at different E/T ratios (100 μl/well) and co-cultured at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator for 24 h or 48 h. After incubation, each well was replaced with a 100 μl cell medium containing D-Luciferin (substrate) at a working concentration recommended as 0.3 mg/ml. Next, the 96-well plates were incubated at 37 °C for 10 min without light and then transferred to a universal microplate reader for bioluminescence detection (Cytation1, Agilent). Construction of 3D spheroid model

The construction of human 3D spheroids was achieved using NAC strategy26. In brief, cells were incubated separately with NAC-Linker A and NAC-Linker B (Puhengtec, NAC001), with gentle tapping during incubation to ensure uniform mixing. After incubation, the cells treated with NAC-Linker A and NAC-Linker B were combined in a single tube. The cell suspension was then diluted to a concentration of 30,000 cells/ml and added to a pre-treated 96-well plate (Puhengtec, S-9601) at 100 μL per well. The plate was placed in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C for further culture. After 24 h, the formation of a 3D spheroid was observed, and subsequent experiments were conducted following spheroid formation.

Construction of 3D co-cultivation model

Utilizing the NAC strategy, a 3D model was meticulously constructed. PBMCs were collected, serving as the source of immune cells essential for the establishment of an immune response component. These PBMCs were subsequently introduced into the 3D tumor model culture medium to ensure their co-existence with tumor cells. Simultaneously, immune-modulating agents, namely the immune suppressor BLZ945 (MCE, HY-12768) and the immune recruiter SEW2871 (MCE, HY-W008947) were incorporated to emulate an immunomodulatory environment29,30. Subsequently, the co-cultured 3D model was placed in a controlled cell culture incubator and incubated for 24 h. During this incubation period, regular monitoring and recording of PBMCs’ invasive capability were conducted, alongside an assessment of the potential influence of immune-modulating agents. These observations served to evaluate the intricate cellular behaviors and interactions within the model system.

LDH assay

A volume of 100 μl of supernatant was carefully collected and placed into a transparent 96-well plate, ensuring uniform distribution across all wells. Subsequently, the LDH content in the supernatant was assayed using an LDH assay kit (CK12, provided by Tongren Chemical). Following this, absorbance readings were obtained at 490 nm using a spectrophotometer. The LDH’s relative concentration was determined by measuring the absorbance values and subsequently calculating the LDH concentration based on the standard curve provided by the assay kit.

Bio-Lite Luciferase Assay System

The requisite number of tumor spheroid samples was aspirated and carefully placed into a clear 96-well plate. Subsequently, an equivalent volume of D-Luciferin (substrate) reagent (Vazyme, DD1201) was added and thoroughly mixed with the samples. Following the addition of the assay reagent, the plate was placed on a shaker and agitated at 600 rpm under subdued lighting conditions for a duration of 30 min. After the incubation, the luminescence intensity of each well was measured using a microplate reader at the appropriate wavelength. Given that the generated chemical luminescence is directly proportional to viable cell counts, the number of viable cells in each sample was determined by measuring the luminescence intensity.

Histopathology

Tumor spheroid samples from the 3D model were meticulously collected, followed by overnight fixation in 4 °C paraformaldehyde (PFA). Subsequently, a dehydration and embedding procedure was conducted. The samples were sequentially immersed in varying concentrations of alcohol to gradually remove moisture. Following this, the tumor spheroid samples were embedded in paraffin to ensure structural fixation and preservation of integrity. In preparation for subsequent analysis, 4 μm continuous sections were meticulously obtained using a microtome. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was employed for observing the overall spheroid structure, DAPI staining was used to delineate cellular nuclei, and immunofluorescence staining was performed for the detection of specific proteins or antigens.

Fluorescence imaging and quantification

To demonstrate macrophage movement, macrophages were stained using CellTracker fluorescent dye and then co-cultured with 3D spheroids. Images were captured in both the bright field and at the 488 nm fluorescence channel. The fluorescence intensity of cells that infiltrated the model was quantified using ImageJ software. The obtained data were plotted and statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. Statistical differences between different groups were compared using Student’s t-test.

Results

Generation of CAR-Ms and phenotype evaluation

To evaluate the phagocytic and killing capabilities of CAR-M in vitro in 2D well/plate-surface culture, we initially designed a CAR construct, featuring an extracellular Myc tag and anti-HER2 scFv domain, a hinged CD8 region transmembrane domain and an intracellular CD3ζ costimulatory domain (Fig. 1A). This CAR-HER2 gene cassette was inserted into adenoviral vectors (with mCherry fluorescent tags) and lentiviral vectors (with GFP fluorescent tags), respectively. Subsequently, hMDMs were transduced with the CAR-HER2 gene using the Ad5F35 vector known for its efficient gene transduction capability, resulting in hMDM CAR cells (Fig. 1B). Then, we transduced the CAR construct into the macrophage cell line (RAW) using the lentiviral system (Fig. 1C). Validation of CAR-M in 2D surface culture

SKOV3 and its fluc cell line with firefly luciferase reporter gene, which highly expresses HER2, were selected as target cells (Fig. 1H, I). In order to evaluate the phagocytosis of HER2+ cells by CAR-M cells at the 2D level, SKOV3 cells were labeled with CellTracker Deep Red Dye (co-cultured with RAW CAR cells) or CellTracker Green CMFDA Dye (co-cultured with hMDM CAR cells). The stained SKOV3 cells were co-cultured with hMDM CAR and RAW CAR cells at an E:T ratio of 1:3, respectively. The results showed that after co-culturing CAR-M with SKOV3 tumor cells, more phagocytosis events were observed in randomly selected fields of view by fluorescence microscopy in both the hMDM CAR and RAW CAR cell groups than in control macrophages (Fig. 2A, B). Flow cytometry analysis showed that hMDM CAR cells exhibited a significantly enhanced phagocytosis of SKOV3 cells with a phagocytosis efficiency of 19.6%, compared to 3.9% for the unmodified control hMDM UTD cells, which was almost five-fold higher (Fig. 2C). Notably, RAW CAR cells demonstrated a significant increase in phagocytic efficiency, with RAW CAR cells exhibiting a phagocytic efficiency of 9.71% against SKOV3 cells, compared to just 2.97% for the control RAW UTD macrophages (Fig. 2D). The results of flow cytometry were impacted by different gating strategies, but a consistent trend was observed when analyzing the data using strategies that included all cell populations (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). The human-derived hMDM CAR exhibited superior killing effects than the murine-derived RAW CAR cells. These findings not only strengthen the potential of CAR-M cells in cancer immunotherapy but also suggest the unique advantages and significant value of human-derived CAR-M in clinical translation.

Interaction between macrophage and 3D tumor spheroids

To replicate the invasion characteristics of tumor tissue more accurately by CAR-M cells, we utilized the NAC strategy to construct a 3D tumor immune microenvironment model (Fig. 3A). Considering the vital importance of cell activity in the success of experiments in the tumor model, we used PI staining to label dead cells and calcein-AM staining to label live cells (Fig. 3B), which revealed that there were almost no dead cells in the tumor model. Furthermore, we monitored the activity of tumor cells over a span of 5 days (Fig. 3C) and observed that ATP levels gradually increased over time, indicating that our tumor model possessed stable cell activity and continued to grow. To facilitate efficient CAR-M screening, we built an ovarian cancer tumor immune microenvironment model on a 384-well plate. Within just 24 h, we observed a significant invasion of PBMC into the solid tumor spheroids (Supplementary Fig. 2). It is worth noting that our model also demonstrated that the invasion capability of PBMC into the tumor model can be regulated by immunomodulatory drugs. BLZ945, a CSF-1R inhibitor, reduced immune cell invasion into the tumor, while SEW2871, an S1P1 agonist, enhanced immune cell recruitment. After BLZ945 treatment, PBMC invasion into the model significantly decreased compared to the control group, whereas the SEW2871 treatment group showed a substantial increase. Additionally, we observed that macrophages can infiltrate SKOV3 spheroids using a live cell real-time imaging system (Supplementary video). Validation of CAR-M targeting, infiltration, and cytotoxicity in 3D spheroids model

To validate the recognition, penetration, and cytotoxicity of CAR-HER2 macrophages against solid tumors, we established a solid tumor model using ovarian cancer SKOV3-Fluc cells. After successfully constructing the model, we co-cultured hMDM UTD and hMDM CAR with the 3D spheroid. To determine the optimal ratio and time for interaction between tumor spheroid and hMDM CAR cells, we evaluated the infiltration and cytotoxic effects of macrophages at different ratios of tumor cells to macrophages (Fig. 3D, E, Supplementary Fig. 3A). CAR-HER2 macrophages effectively infiltrated the tumor spheroid at various ratios, with the E/T ratio of 10:1 showing the most significant effect. In contrast, UTD macrophages exhibited only mild targeting and seldom reached the depths of the tumor spheroid. At an E/T ratio of 10:1, CAR macrophages demonstrated a significant cytotoxic effect on solid tumor spheroids compared to UTD macrophages (Fig. 3F, G). We also validated the effect of the RAW cell for SKOV3. The modified RAW CAR-macrophages were effective in targeting and killing SKOV3 cells with high HER2 (Fig. 3H–K, Supplementary Fig. 3B). Furthermore, we validated whether CAR-M has targeting and killing effects in breast cancer cell lines. With the addition of CAR-M, we observed an invasive and cytotoxic effect on HER2+ cells (Supplementary Fig. 4). To verify the targeting effect of CAR on HER2, we used SKBR3 cells with the HER2 knocked out (Supplementary Fig. 4A). We found that after HER2 knockout, the invasive and cytotoxic effect of CAR-M was reduced (Supplementary Fig. 4B–D).

Furthermore, we explored the infiltration and cytotoxic effects of CAR-M macrophages on tumor spheroids at different time points and found that both infiltration and cytotoxic effects of CAR-M macrophages on the tumor spheroids increased progressively over time (Fig. 4A–D, Supplementary Fig. 5). H&E and DAPI staining, along with observation of macrophage GFP fluorescence, confirmed that CAR-M macrophages successfully penetrated the interior of the tumor spheroids and killed SKOV3 cells (Fig. 4E–G). Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated the effectiveness of CAR-M therapy from two aspects: a conventional 2D co-culture model and a newly developed 3D tumor spheroid model based on our 3D NAC strategy. CAR-M therapy is increasingly recognized as a promising treatment strategy for solid tumors, particularly in terms of trafficking and infiltration. Our results from the conventional well/plate surface co-culture experiments revealed that CAR-mediated macrophages exhibited a significant phagocytic effect compared to macrophages alone. And the killing effect varied depending on different ratios of effector to target (E/T) or the effect time. In particular, we found that the most optimal efficiency was achieved when CAR-macrophages and tumor cells interacted with each other for 6 h with an E/T ratio of 10:1 in the phagocytosis and killing assay. Furthermore, we are actively exploring ways to optimize the CAR structure through a structure-function mechanism and investigating its potential combination with other cancer therapy methods via this new 3D NACs-origami platform. These findings suggest that CAR-M therapy holds substantial potential for further optimization and clinical application.

It was well known that periodical drug development cannot be separated from in vitro and in vivo animal experiments, and as a matter of fact, animal modeling was the most time-consuming and labor-intensive. Hence, we propose a kind of novel 3D tumor spheroids model utilizing the NAC strategy. In this study, we demonstrated the feasibility of using 3D NACs-origami assembled tumor spheroids to assess the cytotoxicity and infiltration of chimeric antigen receptor macrophages. This approach serves as a link or bridge between animal and cell tests, offering the following advantages: (1) High survival rate: The 3D NACs-origami assembled tumor spheroids exhibit a high survival rate. Our results showed that these spheroids can survive for at least 5 days. We have also successfully constructed spheroids from different cell types, such as hepatoma and glioma (data not shown). (2) Affinity with immune cells: The 3D NACs-origami assembled tumor spheroids exhibit excellent bio-affinity with immune cells. Our data demonstrates that peripheral blood mononuclear cells can effectively participate in and invade the tumor spheroids. (3) Mimicking solid tumor microenvironment: The 3D NACs-origami assembled tumor spheroids mimic a simplified version of the solid tumor microenvironment. Obviously, trafficking and infiltration barrier and tumor heterogeneity in solid tumors partially resulted in the failure of CAR-T31.In this respect, our study showed that CAR-M cells can infiltrate these tumor spheroids and target solid tumors more effectively than macrophages alone. Meanwhile, CAR-M co-cultured with 3D tumor spheroids exhibited an E/T ratio- and time-dependent increase manner in targeting and infiltration efficiency, and we also found that the E/T ratio at 10:1 could reach an optimal state, which is consistent with the 2D co-culture level. However, these tumor spheroids do not fully capture the complexity of solid tumors due to the absence of heterogeneous cells. Incorporating various stromal cells, such as fibroblasts, immunosuppressive cells, and T cells, could enhance the spheroids’ resemblance to the solid tumor microenvironment. (4) Alternative in vitro evaluation: The 3D NACs-origami assembled tumor spheroids offer an alternative in vitro evaluation approach before preclinical animal experiments, especially in saving time and costs. Combining this model with automation devices could simplify and broaden its applications, given certain conditions. Additionally, we validated the CAR-M system. The modified CAR-M cells effectively targeted and killed hepatocellular carcinoma cells with high GPC3 expression32.

In general, whether in the traditional 2D co-culture system or the 3D tumor spheroids system, our CAR-Ms exhibited excellent targeting, infiltration, and killing capabilities against solid tumors. We also believe that this 3D NACs-based tumor spheroids system can be utilized in other areas related to CAR-M, such as exploring combination CAR-M therapy with chemotherapy or immunotherapy (e.g. anti-CD47 or Siglec proteins). Thus, the integration of these technologies accelerates CAR-M clinical research.

Conclusion

In summary, we have established a 3D tumor spheroid system-based NACs-origami assembled technique for assessing CAR-Ms in vitro. More importantly, this platform is conducive to the verification of multi-targets or multi-tumors of CAR-M and also provides reliable and favorable evidence for future clinical treatment.
