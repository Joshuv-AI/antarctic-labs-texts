Mavacamten and atrial fibrillation: assessing the risk through FAERS and meta-analysis
Authors: Lingqing Ding, Xinhang Liao, Yongkuan Yang, Congqin Chen, Jie Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMavacamten is a novel selective cardiac myosin inhibitor indicated for patients with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (oHCM). Given its negative inotropic properties, which theoretically reduce myocardial contractility and affect left atrial systolic function, concerns have arisen about its potential association with an increased incidence of atrial fibrillation (AF). The study aims to assess this potential association.MethodsWe employed a dual approach combining real-world data analysis and evidence synthesis. First, disproportionality and time-to-onset analyses were performed using the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) across two models: the general population (Model 1) and an HCM-specific cohort (Model 2). Independent risk factors for AF were identified using logistic regression. Second, a meta-analysis of placebo-controlled trials was conducted to evaluate the safety profile of mavacamten, with pooled risk ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) .ResultsIn the disproportionality analysis, Model 1 revealed a signal for AF with mavacamten (adjusted ROR 18.54, 95% CI 11.53–29.80, P < 0.05; EB05 11.55). In contrast, no significant disproportionality signal was observed in Model 2 (aROR 4.28, 95% CI 1.30–14.07, P = 0.017; EB05 0.74). The time-to-onset analysis indicated a random failure type for AF occurrence in mavacamten-treated patients. The meta-analysis included five RCTs and one retrospective cohort study. Compared with placebo, mavacamten was not associated with an increased risk of AF (RR = 0.92, 95% CI 0.80–1.06, P = 0.12).ConclusionOur findings did not identify a significant association between mavacamten and AF in patients with HCM. These results should be interpreted with caution due to the limited number of studies and small sample sizes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05699-8.Keywords: mavacamten, atrial fibrillation, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, pharmacovigilance study, meta-analysis

Introduction

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is a common inherited myocardial disease, primarily caused by pathogenic variants in sarcomeric protein-coding genes, with a general population prevalence of 1:200-1:500 [1, 2]. HCM is commonly classified into two major subtypes: obstructive HCM (oHCM), characterized by left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction, and nonobstructive HCM (nHCM), characterized by the absence of LVOT obstruction either at rest or with provocation [3, 4]. Mavacamten, a first-in-class selective cardiac myosin inhibitor, has been proven to be an effective treatment for symptomatic oHCM. Evidence from phase 3 clinical trials, including EXPLORER-HCM [5] and VALOR-HCM [6], demonstrated that mavacamten resulted in substantial reductions in LVOT gradients, improvements in NYHA (New York Heart Association) functional class, and favorable functional capacity.

Although demonstrated to be effective, over the years that mavacamten has been used, reports of atrial fibrillation (AF) adverse events (AEs) have emerged [7, 8]. Observational studies further highlighted a high incidence of new-onset and recurrent AF, some leading to rapid decline in left ventricular ejection fraction or drug discontinuation [9–11]. Castrichini et al. observed a notable AF incidence that 32% of oHCM patients experienced new-onset or recurrent AF after a median of 8 months of mavacamten treatment [9]. In contrast, randomized controlled trials [5, 12] or real-world studies [2, 13] reported relatively low incidences of AF. Given that HCM patients with AF face higher risk of thromboembolism and poorer prognosis [14], and as the use of mavacamten extends beyond HCM centers, rigorous evaluations are needed. Therefore, in this work, we integrated pharmacovigilance study based on real-world data from the Food and Drug Administration’s Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS), a large spontaneous-reporting database, and a systematic review and meta-analysis of existing studies, to evaluate the association between mavacamten and AF.

Materials and methods

Study design

A pharmacovigilance study was initially conducted using the FAERS database to examine the risk of AF in real-world clinical settings. Then, a meta-analysis was conducted to further investigate the risk.

Pharmacovigilance analysis

Data source

A retrospective, observational pharmacovigilance analysis was conducted using publicly available, de-identified data from the FAERS database between Q1 2014 to Q2 2025. To enhance the reliability and validity of the results, only adverse event reports submitted by healthcare professionals were retained. The raw data were processed through extraction, cleaning, standardization, and de-duplication using SQL Server Data Mining techniques.

Definition of adverse events and drugs of interest

In the FAERS database, AEs are coded using preferred term (PT) codes from the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA). Two AF-related PTs were considered: “atrial fibrillation” and “atrial flutter”. Both terms were selected given the clinical and reporting difficulties in clearly distinguishing between these two arrhythmias [15, 16]. The drug of interest was mavacamten (Camzyos, brand name). The study encompassed all cases where mavacamten was classified as PS (primary suspect) or SS (second suspect) for the AE.

Selection of cases and non-cases

Only reports involving adult patients (≥ 18 years) were retained. AF is more common in individuals with HCM than in the general population, with an annual incidence of 2% to 7% [17]. As AF is more common in individuals with HCM than in the general population, and limiting the study population may help reduce indication bias [18], we stratified the study population into two analytical models. Model 1 included all reports without indication restrictions, in which the cases involved patients exposed to mavacamten and the reference group consisted of reports without mavacamten exposure. Model 2 focused on reports with an indication of HCM, in which cases involved patients exposed to mavacamten and the reference group comprised reports unexposed to mavacamten. This stratification was designed to minimize the potential impact of the underlying HCM disease process on AF outcomes and to ensure balance in baseline characteristics across the study population.

Covariates

In this study, each AE was treated as the objective variable, while age, sex, HCM indication, concomitant diseases and concomitant drugs served as explanatory variables. The age variable was stratified into two groups: 0–65 years and ≥ 65 years, with the latter designated as the reference category. Female was selected as the reference group. We also included information on the presence of HCM, along with comobitities (e.g. hypertension, peri-/myocarditis, and congenital heart disease) and common drugs that may be associated with AF [19]. These variables were incorporated into the logistic regression models to control for potential confounding effects.

Disproportionality analysis

In pharmacovigilance signal detection, methods capable of identifying all the potential safety signals across numerous drug-event combinations may suffer from low specificity. Therefore, effective signal detection methodologies should balance both specificity and sensitivity to minimize false results. In this context, we employed two disproportionality measures to assess the risk of AF reporting for mavacamten: the reporting odds ratio (ROR), multi-item gamma Poisson shrinker (MGPS) algorithm. Both methods are based on 2 × 2 contingency tables (Supplementary material 1), where “a” represents the number of AF reports for mavacamten, “b” represents the reports for mavacamten without AF-relate AEs, “c” represents the number of AF reports for the control groups, and “d” represents the reports for the control groups without AF-related AEs [20].

The ROR signal was defined by the criterion of the lower limit of the 95% confidence interval (CI) of the ROR > 1 and the number of reports a > 3. Furthermore, the ROR was estimated using a logistic regression model that adjusted for potential confounders, including age, sex, the presence of HCM, comorbidities, and the use of medications known to be associated with AF (as explained above). To improve robustness to sparse counts, the MGPS was used in parallel with the ROR. While the ROR provides a crude measure of disproportionality, the MGPS systematically adjusts large estimates from small counts and calculates the Empirical Bayes Geometric Mean (EBGM) with two-sided 95% CI (EB05 < EBGM< EB95) for drug-event combinations [21]. The lower confidence limit of the EBGM, defined by EB05, is typically set with a conventional cutoff of EB05 ≥ 2 for signal detection, indicating that a drug-event combination occurs at least twice as often as expected relative to comparator drug-event combinations in the database.

Note that only drug-event pairs meeting the predefined criteria for both adjusted RORs (aRORs) and MGPS are considered to represent an association.

Risk factors for AF

To account for those potential influencing factors [22, 23], we performed both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses to adjust for variables such as gender, age, HCM indication, comorbidities, and concomitant medications, separately for the total population and the HCM-specific population. Given the number of patients with missing data for age and gender, we applied multiple imputation methods to address these missing variables in the sensitivity analysis. In total, five datasets were imputed using a multiple chained equations approach, based on the assumption that the data were missing at random, and the pooled results were used for the final analysis. Covariates were adjusted to estimate the aRORs and 95% CIs.

Reported time to onset analysis

Time-to-onset analysis has been proposed as a method for detecting signals of AEs in FAERS [24, 25]. Time-to‐onset is defined as the period from the initiation of drug treatment to the onset of events. In this study, the Weibull shape parameter (WSP) test was conducted by fitting a Weibull model to time-to-event data, as the WSP test can determine the varying ratio of incidence of adverse events in similar pharmacovigilance studies [26].

The Weibull distribution has two parameters: the scale parameter α and shape parameter β. The scale parameter α of the Weibull distribution indicates the number of A E occurrences at which approximately 63.2% of the total expected adverse events have occurred. The shape parameter β of the Weibull distribution determines the shape of the distribution function: when the shape parameter β was < 1 and its 95% CI was < 1, the AE risk was considered to have decreased over time (indicative of a early failure-type pattern); when the shape parameter β was equal to or nearly 1 and its 95% CI included the value 1, the risk was assumed to occur uniformly throughout the observation period (indicative of a random failure‐type pattern); and when the shape parameter β was > 1 and its 95% CI excluded the value 1, the risk was deemed to increase over time (indicative of a wear‐out failure‐type pattern) [27].

Statement of ethics

Because the information of the included reports in this public database was anonymous and only identified by a unique primaryid, there was no need to require informed consent and ethics.

Meta analysis

This meta analysis has been registered as CRD420251174864 on the PROSPERO website and has been reported in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) checklist .

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive search of the Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, and Clinical Trials databases up to November 2025. The search algorithm is detailed in the Supplementary material 2. Case reports, case series, editorials, and meta-analyses, were excluded from the search. The screening and data collection were performed independently by two authors (DLQ and YYK), and any disagreements were resolved through consultation with a third author(CCQ).

Types of interventions and comparisons (PICO)

In accordance with PRISMA guidelines, all included studies met the PICO criteria (Participants, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome). The participants consisted of patients with HCM (both HCM and HOCM). The intervention group included mavacamten, with no restrictions on dosage or treatment duration. The comparison group comprised patients receiving either a placebo or standard medical treatment. The outcome of interest was any grade or type of cardiac adverse events related to treatment. For data extraction, the focus was specifically on treatment-related AF adverse events, including atrial fibrillation and atrial flutter.

Quality assessment

The quality of the included RCT studies was assessed using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2), a recommended tool to evaluate the risk of bias in RCTs [28]. The quality of the included observational studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [29].

Data analysis

In the systematic review, baseline information from the five included studies was extracted and presented in tables. Meta-analyses were performed using Review Manager 5.4, with statistical significance defined by a two-sided P value of less than 0.05. Data were presented as risk ratio (RR) with 95% CI. Heterogeneity was assessed using the Higgins I2 statistic value. with I² values interpreted as follows: low (< 25%), low to moderate (25–50%), moderate to substantial (50–75%), or substantial (> 75%). In the presence of significant heterogeneity was detected, a random-effects model was used; otherwise, a fixed-effect model was applied. Additionally, subgroup analysis was based on obstructive versus non-obstructive HCM.

Model 2

Overview of adverse events reported in the FAERS database, 2014Q1–2025Q2

In model 2, we filtered the query based on the indication and identified 845 reports diagnosed with HCM. Among these, 660 cases were associated with mavacamten, and 85 of these mavacamten-associated cases were related to AF.

General characteristics of mavacamten-associated AF reports

Overall, females comprised the majority of reported cases (31 reports, 28.97%). Patients aged 25–64 years and those older than 74 years represented similar proportions (13.08% and 10.28%, respectively). The United States had the highest number of reported cases (74 reports, 69.16%). Following FDA approval of mavacamten in April 2022, reports of AF-related AEs became notably more frequent starting from Q2 2022, with a subsequent increasing annual trend. Mavacamten-associated AF outcomes frequently led to hospitalization (12.38%).

Disproportionality analysis

To minimize indication bias, we not only included the HCM indication as an explanatory variable in Model 1, but also restricted the analysis to patients with HCM in Model 2. Among patients with an HCM indication, 92.39% (85/92) of AF cases were associated with mavacamten. No AF over-reporting signal was concluded for mavacamten under the combined ROR+MGPS criteria both in the unadjusted and multivariate analysis: the ROR was 3.76 (95% CI 1.71–8.27, P < 0.001) in the unadjusted analysis and 4.28 (95% CI 1.30–14.07, P = 0.017) in the multivariable-adjusted logistic regression analysis; however, MGPS was not supportive (EB05 = 0.54), and therefore the combined criterion was not met. Notably, the multivariate analysis in Model 1 revealed that HCM indication was a risk factor for AF among patients receiving mavacamten; and when the analysis was restricted to patients with HCM in Model 2, no safety signal for mavacamten was detected. Taken together, these results implied that population heterogeneity, rather than a drug-specific effect, may explain the over-reporting of AF events in patients treated with mavacamten detected in Model 1.

Risk factors for AF

The multivariate logistic regression in Model 2 suggested that no specific risk factors for AF were identified among HCM patients receiving mavacamten, and the results are shown in Table 2.

Time to onset

Overall, the median time-to-onset of AF following mavacamten initiation was 42.30 days (IQR: 18.95–79.68 days). The Anderson-Darling goodness of fit test indicated that the data conformed to a Weibull distribution (Anderson-Darling (Adjusted) 1.040). Based on the Weibull distribution model and valid cases, the shape parameter for mavacamten was 1.09 (95% CI, 0.76–1.57), suggesting a constant hazard rate (random failure type).This indicates that AF events predominantly occur at any stage following mavacamten treatment. In the included studies, AF occurred in 179 patients (16.11%) in the mavacamten group and 3,309 patients (22.95%) in the control group. Discussion

This study analyzed the association between AF and mavacamten by combining pharmacovigilance data with a meta-analysis. The pharmacovigilance data, based on FAERS, show that AF was not over-reported in HCM patients receiving mavacamten. Similarly, the meta-analysis suggests that mavacamten is not associated with a significantly increased risk of AF in patients with HCM compared to placebo. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate pharmacovigilance and meta-analysis to assess the hypothesis of pseudo-arrhythmia versus true AF in patients using mavacamten.

Theoretical mechanisms for mavacamten-induced AF have been proposed, primarily based on the established atrial pathophysiology in HCM. Previous studies have shown that HCM patients who develop AF generally exhibit larger left atrial (LA) diameters and impaired LA function compared with those without AF [34–36]. On this basis, several imaging-based studies have hypothesized that the negative inotropic effect of mavacamten might further impair LA systolic function, promoting LA enlargement and structural remodeling, which in turn predispose patients to electrical conduction abnormalities and AF [37, 38]. For example, Wessly et al. reported significant declines in LA reservoir strain, pump strain, and atrial ejection force in 15 HCM patients one month after initiating mavacamten, potentially reflecting negative inotropic effects [37]. However, other data suggest that mavacamten may instead exert neutral or even beneficial effects on LA structure and function [39–42]. A echocardiography analyses suggested mavacamten might decrease AF incidence through enhanced LA function and remodeling [43]. Animal studies also support potential protective effects [44]. Consistent with this, the long-term MAVA-LTE extension study reported sustained reductions in LA volume index compared with baseline at week 48 and beyond in patients who had completed EXPLORER-HCM [45, 46]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the net effect of mavacamten on AF risk is mechanistically complex and remains uncertain.In clinical practice, emphasis should be placed on baseline AF risk assessment and appropriate rhythm monitoring during treatment, particularly in higher-risk HCM subgroups, rather than attributing AF events directly to mavacamten exposure. This may help avoid unnecessary treatment discontinuation while supporting individualized benefit–risk evaluation and management.

In recent years, the association between mavacamten and AF has been debated in both small prospective [5, 12, 31] and large retrospective cohort studies [9–11, 13]. A previous pharmacovigilance study [47] assessed mavacamten-related adverse events using FAERS data from January 1, 2022, to September 30, 2023, concluding that mavacamten was associated with a disproportionately high rate of AF-related adverse event reports (ROR 16.63, 95% CI 12.72–21.75; EB05 = 11.96). Similarly, real-world single-center or small-size studies have shown that incident or progressive AF occurred more frequently in real-world cohorts compared to randomized trials, which reported a generally low incidence of AF [9–11]. Results from the Week 128 analysis of the VALOR-HCM trial [48] found that new-onset AF occurred in 11 out of 108 patients (10.2%), equating to 4.55 cases per 100 patient-years. This rate was substantially higher than that observed in the treatment arms of the EXPLORER-HCM [5](2%) or VALOR-HCM [6] (3.6%) clinical trials. The observed discrepancy in AF incidence following mavacamten initiation between clinical practice and clinical trials may be attributed to the more stringent inclusion criteria and more frequent rhythm monitoring in clinical trials.

In the context of the inherently high prevalence of AF among HCM patients (approximately 5%–33%), the higher background incidence of AF in this population may introduce substantial confounding. This, in turn, raises an important question: does exposure to mavacamten genuinely increase the risk of clinically relevant AF? To address this concern, we stratified the reports into two distinct groups based on HCM status. Our updated pharmacovigilance analysis found that, after multivariate adjustment in Model 1, the presence of HCM was identified as an independent risk factor for AF; however, when the analysis was restricted to patients with HCM in Model 2, no over-reporting of AF-related adverse events was found. Taken together, these results suggest that disease heterogeneity, rather than a drug-specific effect, may account for the findings. We suggest that, in clinical practice, emphasis should be placed on baseline AF risk assessment and appropriate rhythm monitoring during treatment, particularly in higher-risk HCM subgroups.

Our updated meta-analysis—showing no statistically significant increase in the risk of AF with mavacamten compared with placebo—is consistent with findings from previous clinical trials and earlier meta-analyses after we increased our sample size [49–51].These findings are consistent with a recent comprehensive meta-analysis of cardiac myosin inhibitors [51], which also reported no excess AF risk across six RCTs and an overall short-term AF incidence of roughly 4–5 cases per 100 person-years in HCM patients treated with mavacamten and aficamten.Taken together, current trial-level evidence suggests that, although mavacamten can reduce LA contractile function in some patients, this does not translate into a clearly measurable increase in AF events over the short follow-up of existing studies. Longer-term data are needed to determine the inherent AF risk for mavacamten.

Previous studies have highlighted gender differences in AF risk among patients with HCM, with females often presenting at older ages and more advanced disease stages, leading to worse clinical outcomes [22, 23]. Additionally, female gender has been identified as an independent risk factor for AF in both the general and HCM populations [52]. However, our analysis in mavacamten-treated HCM patients found no statistically significant increase in the adjusted odds ratio (aROR) for AF in Model 2, suggesting that the arrhythmic safety profiles for males and females treated with mavacamten are comparable. This finding aligns with clinical trial results showing similar safety and efficacy outcomes across genders, with women demonstrating sustained improvements in LVOT gradients and functional capacity [53, 54]. Our findings indicate that older age did not significantly influence the risk of AF (patients aged ≥ 65 years vs. patients aged < 65 years). This is consistent with the exploratory analysis of the EXPLORER-HCM trial, which observed similar AF rates in patients aged ≤ 60 and > 60 years (2.3% vs. 2.4%) [55]. Unfortunately, our meta-analysis did not include a gender- or age-specific subgroup analyses, as no clear association between gender/age and AF-related adverse events was reported. To note, our findings warrant further validation through future large-scale, well-designed RCTs to confirm the impact of potential patient-level factors.

Among the limited cases with complete time-stamp data in our study, although the small sample size precludes definitive conclusions, our time-to-onset analysis suggested that AF events occurred at any stage during the mavacamten treatment period, indicating a random failure type. This is consistent with findings from previous research [9]. In the VALOR-HCM [48], 11 patients (10.2%) developed new-onset AF over a 128-week follow-up, with 7 cases (6.5%) emerging during the latter half of the study (weeks 56–128), suggesting a potential for late-onset events. It is important to note that missing date information for drug initiation and event occurrence within the FAERS database limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions, underscoring the need for more comprehensive, prospective time-to-event data collection.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first to combine pharmacovigilance data with meta-analysis to explore the associations between AF and mavacamten. Additionally, by restricting the analysis to HCM patients, we minimized baseline disease risk heterogeneity, thereby enhancing the interpretability of AF signal. Furthermore, we used multivariable logistic regression to adjust for key confounding factors when examining the association between mavacamten exposure and AF.

However, there are several limitations in our study. First, as with all spontaneous reporting systems, the use of the FAERS database has some inherent limitations, including susceptibility to notoriety bias, selection bias, underreporting, follow-up time bias, and exposure misclassification. To mitigate these limitations, we applied standardized data cleaning and deduplication procedures, used established disproportionality methods for signal detection, and interpreted the findings as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory evidence. Second, FAERS lacks denominator data (i.e., the total number of drug-exposed individuals), which prevents the calculation of true incidence rates. Third, AF is inherently prevalent in the HCM population, complicating the distinction between background disease-related risks and potential drug-associated effects. To address this, we stratified the reports into two groups—total population and HCM population. Fourth, while the literature on whether mavacamten dosing or pre-existing AF history influences AF risk is inconsistent, the absence of detailed dosing information [9] and baseline AF history in the FAERS reports hindered our ability to conduct multivariate analyses based on these factors. The last, although our meta-analysis expanded the sample size compared to previous studies, only six RCTs met our inclusion criteria, largely because mavacamten is a newly marketed drug and not all RCTs reported relevant outcome indicators. This limited the sample size for certain outcomes. Consequently, more large-scale RCTs are needed to provide more definitive conclusions.

Conclusions

The results found no significant association between mavacamten and AF in patients with HCM. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the limitations of spontaneous reporting systems and the small number of studies included in the meta-analysis. Further prospective, large-scale studies with continuous rhythm monitoring are needed to confirm these findings and guide individualized safety assessments for long-term mavacamten therapy.
