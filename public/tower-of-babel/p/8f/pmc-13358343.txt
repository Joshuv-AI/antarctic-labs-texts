The impact of Métis Nation of Ontario citizens’ housing satisfaction on self-reported health: a population-based retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Tatiana Baziw, Carolyn Lacka, Robynn Sadler, Abigail J Simms, Noel Tsui, Amy Mersereau, Cindi Rye, David Crenna, Jeff Evenson, Sarah A Edwards
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDistinction-based research on the relationship between housing and health for Métis people is limited, despite a recognised housing crisis for Indigenous Peoples in Canada. This study examines the association between housing conditions and repairs with self-rated physical, mental, and overall balance of health in citizens of the Métis Nation of Ontario (MNO).MethodsAn MNO-led, population-based online survey was conducted between 6 May 2022 and 13 June 2022. Census sampling was used to invite all MNO citizens, aged 16 and older, with a valid email on file, to participate. Respondents were asked to rate their physical, mental and overall balance of health, and satisfaction with their housing conditions on five-point Likert scales. Level of repair (major, minor or none) and sociodemographic information were also collected. Bivariate analyses and logistic regression models were used.ResultsIn total, 4164 (30%) MNO citizens completed the survey. Housing satisfaction and level of repair were strongly associated with self-reported physical, mental and overall balance of health. The adjusted odds of reporting good physical health (OR=3.3; 95% CI 2.61 to 4.21), mental health (OR=4.1; 95% CI 3.19 to 5.20), and overall balance of health (OR=3.8; 95% CI 2.51 to 5.68) were significantly higher among those satisfied with their housing conditions versus dissatisfied. Similar associations were observed with repair.ConclusionsOur results contribute to closing the gap in Métis health research and provide the MNO citizen-specific housing information to inform the planning and evaluation of Métis-specific housing programmes.Keywords: Public Health, Mental Health, Epidemiology

Introduction

One’s housing conditions play a significant role in determining health and well-being.1 The home is where health and well-being begin, offering a safe space for individuals to gain a sense of self and identity.1 Factors such as overcrowding, needed repairs, access to water, internet and electricity, proper ventilation, and safety from pests and contaminants define one’s housing conditions.2 Additionally, proximity to amenities such as healthcare, grocery stores, schools, and social and recreational activities contributes to housing quality.2 When housing conditions are substandard, mental, physical, emotional, spiritual, and social/cultural health can be greatly impacted.3 In a systemic review by Alidoust and Huang,4 several associations between poor health and substandard housing quality were found. When living in housing that is overcrowded or in need of serious repair, occupants experienced psychological distress, reduced well-being, and lower self-reported mental health while having a higher prevalence of respiratory conditions and chronic illness.4 When individuals live in safe neighbourhoods, outside of major urban centres but with adequate access to amenities and social outlets, they experience lower levels of stress and higher levels of satisfaction and emotional well-being.4

Due to the historical and ongoing impacts of colonization, which include disproportionately negative impacts on socioeconomic status, educational attainment, and overall health outcomes, one in six Indigenous people is more likely to live in unsuitable, overcrowded housing conditions.5–7 According to a recent Métis Nation of Ontario (MNO) housing census, 6% of MNO citizens live in overcrowded conditions and 23% live in a dwelling that needs major repairs.8 This is consistent with other resources reporting Indigenous people being three times more likely to live in housing in need of major repairs.5

To date, most research investigating Indigenous health and well-being in association with housing conditions focuses on Indigenous Peoples as one group, rather than viewing First Nations, Métis, and Inuit as separate Nations.9 Métis people are a distinct Indigenous group with an unique history as affirmed under Section 35 of the Canadian Constitution Act, 1982.10 Métis people came to be through a process of ethnogenesis starting with intermarriages between First Nations women and European men via the fur trade.10 Moreover, there is a recognized gap for Métis-specific health research.9 As described by the Métis National Council (MNC), one of the key indicators of Métis well-being is housing.11 12 Thus, it is vital that further investigation into how housing conditions affect the holistic health of Métis people be conducted. Holistic health for Métis, as defined by the MNC, encompasses the physical, emotional, spiritual, mental, and social/cultural aspect of the self as they relate to interpersonal relationships, community, and the overall Métis Nation.13 This study was conducted as part of a larger study with the MNO who are the only federally and provincially recognized self-governing body of Métis people within Ontario.10 10

The MNO was created in 1993, with the purpose of preserving Métis culture, improving the social and economic well-being of Métis citizens in Ontario, and furthering the self-determination rights of the Métis.10 The MNO is governed by the Provisional Council of the Métis Nation of Ontario (PCMNO), with province-wide elections held every 4 years. Through these elections, citizens elect regional representatives from nine Ontario regions, as well as the President, Chair, Vice-Chair, Secretary-Treasurer, and Executive Senator. Ontario is Canada’s most populous province, and the MNO is one of several Métis governments across Canada, alongside the Manitoba Métis Federation, Métis Nation-Saskatchewan, the Otipemisiwak Métis Government (formerly the Métis Nation of Alberta), and the Métis Nation British Columbia. Currently, the MNO serves over 28 000 Métis citizens across 30 chartered community councils, offering more than 30 programmes and services.14

The objectives of this study are (a) to describe the current state of housing conditions and housing quality for MNO citizens and (b) to determine the association between housing conditions, required repairs and self-reported physical health, mental health and overall balance of health, adjusting for socio-demographic variables (eg, age, gender, education, income, employment and community belonging).

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted as part of an MNO-led, population-based online survey which began 6 May 2022 and closed 13 June 2022. The MNO maintains the only recognized provincial registry for Métis people in Ontario. At the time of this survey, there were 27 780 registered MNO citizens. Census sampling was used to invite all MNO citizens aged 16 and older with a valid email on file with the MNO Registry (n=15 214) to complete the survey (see figure 1), which was distributed via Qualtrics. Prior to receiving the email invitation, all eligible MNO citizens with a valid phone number with the MNO Registry were notified about the survey through an automated phone call. This survey was also advertised through the MNO website and pre-existing social media platforms such as X (formerly Twitter), Facebook and Instagram. All respondents received a CA$5 coffee gift card on completion of the survey and were entered into a draw for a chance to win 1 of 50 CA$100 Visa gift cards.

Breakdown of MNO citizens’ recruitment to complete the survey deployed between May 6 2022 and June 13 2022.

This survey was adapted from a population-level survey developed by the Otipemisiwak Métis Government and academic partners at the University of Alberta. Specifically, the housing questions were pulled from existing national level housing surveys run by Statistics Canada which have been validated in the Canadian population, including Indigenous Peoples. Similarly, the questions on physical and mental health were obtained from the Canadian Community Health Survey which is also run by Statistics Canada and the question on overall balance was sourced from the First Nations Information Governance Centre. Prior to distributing the survey, it underwent content review and face validity checks by MNO leadership (who were also MNO citizens) and staff members (who were also MNO citizens), as well as three MNO Senators. Field testing of the online survey to confirm skip patterns was done by the research team and MNO staff members.

Measures

The primary outcome was MNO citizens’ self-reported physical, mental, and overall balance of health, to represent holistic health. Respondents were asked the following for physical health: ‘In general, how would you describe your physical health?’ with options of excellent, very good, good, fair, and poor. Excellent, very good, and good were grouped for good physical health, and fair and poor were grouped for less good physical health. Similarly, respondents were asked the following for mental health: ‘In general, how would you describe your mental health?’ with options of excellent, very good, good, fair, and poor. Excellent, very good, and good were grouped for good physical health, and fair and poor were grouped for less good mental health. Both questions also included an ‘I don’t know’ option. Respondents were also asked: ‘How often do you feel in balance physically, emotionally, mentally and spiritually?’ with options of all of the time, most of the time, some of the time, almost none of the time, and none of the time. ‘Prefer not to say’ was an option for all questions. The all of the time and most of the time options were grouped together for in balance, and the some of the time, almost none of the time, and none of the time options were grouped for not in balance.

The primary independent variables of interest included a question on housing condition and whether their home needed repairs. Respondents were asked: ‘How would you rate your level of satisfaction with your housing conditions?’ with response options of very satisfied, satisfied, neither satisfied nor dissatisfied, and very dissatisfied. For repairs, respondents were asked: ‘Is your dwelling in need of any repairs?’ with response options of ‘no, only regular maintenance’, ‘yes, minor repairs’, or ‘yes, major repairs’. A ‘prefer not to say’ option was also provided.

Sociodemographic information, including gender, education, income, employment, and relationship status, was also collected. Age was linked from the MNO Registry. In addition, a question related to Métis culture was included which asked: ‘How would you describe your sense of belonging to your Métis community? Would you say it is…?’ with the options of very strong, somewhat strong, somewhat weak, and very weak. This question also included ‘not sure’ and ‘prefer not to say’ as options.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the sample of MNO citizens. Bivariate analyses, including χ2 tests, were used to examine the association of housing condition and housing repairs, sociodemographic variables, and community belonging with self-reported health outcomes. Logistic regression models were used to describe the associations between housing condition and housing repairs with self-reported physical health, mental health, and overall balance of health holistically, adjusting for sociodemographic variables and sense of community belonging.

Effect modification by education level and income was examined using stratified analyses for both housing condition and repairs. Stratum-specific adjusted ORs and 95% CIs were estimated. All stratified models were adjusted for age group, sense of belonging, gender, employment status, and relationship status.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Respondents

In total, 4780 (31%) MNO citizens followed the survey link; 4209 (28%) completed informed consent, and 4164 (27%) completed the survey. The overall proportion of respondents by age, and MNO Regions resembled the proportions for the full MNO citizenship population 16 years and older.

Comparisons by age, sex, and MNO regions between survey respondents and the full MNO population are presented in online supplemental table S1. More than half of the respondents were women (59.6%). Most respondents were in a relationship or married and living together (69.3%). Just over half were currently employed full-time or self-employed (52.9%). Stratified analyses demonstrated that the association between need for housing repairs and self-reported health varied by both educational attainment and household income. For physical health, stronger associations were observed with increasing educational attainment, with the largest effects among individuals with graduate or professional degrees (see online supplemental table S2), while stratification by income revealed pronounced effects among MNO citizens in both the lowest (≤CA$49 999) and highest (≥CA$100 000) income groups (see online supplemental table S3). For mental health and balance of health, associations were strongest among MNO citizens with trade certification and college or university education, with attenuation among those with graduate-level education (see online supplemental table S4 and S6). Stratification by income indicated increasing associations for both mental health and balance of health with higher household income, with the strongest effects observed in the highest income group (see online supplemental table S5 and S7). Across most strata, a dose–response relationship was evident, with progressively better health outcomes associated with improved housing conditions. Stratified analyses also showed associations for self-reported housing satisfaction across physical, mental and balance of health outcomes varied by both education and household income (online supplemental tables S8–S13).

For physical health, MNO citizens who reported being satisfied with their housing had substantially higher odds of good physical health compared with those who were dissatisfied across all education and income groups. ORs ranged from 2.2 to 4.7, with the strongest associations observed among individuals with graduate education and those in the lowest income category. For balance of health, housing satisfaction was also strongly associated with education and income strata. MNO citizens who were satisfied with their housing had between threefold and sevenfold higher odds of good balance of health compared with those who were dissatisfied. Particularly large effects were observed among individuals with trade certification and among low-income and high-income households, although estimates in some strata were imprecise due to small sample sizes. For mental health, housing satisfaction demonstrated the strongest associations. Across education groups, satisfied MNO citizens had threefold to 10-fold higher odds of good mental health compared with those who were dissatisfied, with the largest effects observed among individuals with trade certification. Stratification by income also revealed consistently strong associations, with ORs ranging from 3.2 to 8.0 and the largest effects observed in the highest income group. Overall, associations between housing satisfaction and health outcomes were stronger than those observed for structural repair status, particularly for mental health and balance of health.

Bivariate analyses found a statistically significant difference when comparing self-reported satisfaction with housing conditions (p<0.0001) and housing repairs (p<0.0001) between MNO citizens reporting good versus less good physical health (table 2). Similarly, bivariate analyses found statistically significant differences across all demographic categories, except for sense of belonging to the Métis community (p=0.76), when comparing MNO citizens reporting good versus less good physical health.

Both the unadjusted and adjusted models for sociodemographic variables found satisfaction with housing conditions was strongly associated with self-reported physical health (table 3). The odds of MNO citizens reporting good physical health were 3.3 times higher (OR=3.29; 95% CI 2.54 to 4.19; p<0.0001) for MNO citizens satisfied with their housing conditions versus dissatisfied and 1.4 times higher (OR=1.24; 95% CI 1.00 to 1.66; p=0.01) for MNO citizens satisfied with their housing conditions versus neither satisfied nor dissatisfied (table 3), after adjusting for sociodemographic variables. Similarly, housing repairs were also strongly associated with self-reported physical health. The odds of MNO citizens reporting good physical health were 2.4 times higher (OR=2.42; 95% CI 1.91 to 3.07; p<0.0001) for MNO citizens with no major repairs needed versus major repairs and 1.3 times higher (OR=1.28; 95% CI 1.00 to 1.60; p=0.05) for MNO citizens with minor versus major repairs, after adjusting for sociodemographic variables.

Both the unadjusted and adjusted models for sociodemographic variables found that satisfaction with housing conditions and housing repairs was strongly associated with self-reported mental health (table 3). The odds of MNO citizens reporting good mental health were 4.2 times higher (OR=4.22; 95% CI 3.22 to 5.38; p<0.0001) for MNO citizens satisfied with their housing conditions versus dissatisfied and 2.3 times higher (OR=2.35; 95% CI 1.67 to 3.12; p<0.0001) for MNO citizens satisfied with their housing conditions versus neither satisfied nor dissatisfied (table 3), after adjusting for sociodemographic variables. Similarly, housing repairs were also strongly associated with self-reported mental health. The odds of MNO citizens reporting good mental health were 2.4 times higher (OR=2.39; 95% CI 1.90 to 2.99; p<0.0001) for MNO citizens with no major repairs needed versus major repairs and 1.7 times higher (OR=1.68; 95% CI 1.32 to 2.10; p<0.0001) for MNO citizens with minor versus major repairs, after adjusting for sociodemographic variables.

Physical, emotional, spiritual, and mental balance

Bivariate analyses found a statistically significant difference when comparing self-reported satisfaction with housing conditions (p<0.0001) and housing repairs (p<0.0001) between MNO citizens who were in balance physically, emotionally, spiritually, and mentally versus not in balance (table 4). Similarly, bivariate analyses found statistically significant differences across all demographic categories when comparing MNO citizens in balance versus not in balance.

Both the unadjusted and adjusted models for sociodemographic variables found satisfaction with housing conditions was strongly associated with physical, emotional, spiritual, and mental balance (table 3). The odds of MNO citizens reporting being in balance were 3.8 times higher (OR=3.81; 95% CI 2.51 to 5.68; p<0.0001) for MNO citizens satisfied with their housing conditions versus dissatisfied and 1.3 times higher (OR=1.33; 95% CI 1.00 to 2.17; p=0.04) for MNO citizens satisfied with their housing conditions versus neither satisfied nor dissatisfied (table 3), after adjusting for sociodemographic variables. Similarly, having no repairs needed relative to major repairs required was strongly associated with physical, emotional, spiritual, and mental balance, whereas no repairs relative to requiring minor repairs were less strongly associated (did not reach statistical significance). The odds of MNO citizens reporting being in balance were 2.3 times higher (OR=2.32; 95% CI 1.85 to 2.83; p<0.0001) for MNO citizens with no repairs required versus major repairs required to their dwelling and 1.3 times higher (OR=1.27; 95% CI 0.98 to 1.80; p=0.13) for MNO citizens with minor repairs required versus major repairs required to their dwelling, after adjusting for sociodemographic variables.

Discussion

Satisfaction with housing conditions was strongly associated with self-reported health, including physical, mental, and a holistic measure of health that included being in balance physically, mentally, spiritually, and emotionally in MNO citizens. This is in line with previous research that has shown the conditions one lives in are associated with the state of their health and well-being.1 4

Housing conditions were found to show a strong positive association with self-reported physical and mental health for MNO citizens. Satisfaction with one’s housing conditions was associated with a 3.3 times higher rating of positive mental health and a 4.1 times higher rating of physical health. This association was also found across various housing and health studies examining general and Indigenous populations. When Indigenous Peoples live in substandard housing, they experience poorer physical and mental health outcomes.15 Higher rates of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma seen in MNO citizens may be linked to inadequate housing conditions.16 Additionally, our findings align with a 2021 systematic review results indicating that adequate housing quality and environment supports one’s well-being and health.4 Our study also demonstrated that housing conditions and state of repair were associated with overall balance of health. Although there were no other studies examining holistic health outcomes related to housing, exploring this outcome is consistent with the Métis worldview as health is a ‘state of balance and interconnected relationships between physical, mental, social, spiritual, environmental, and cultural well-being’.13

Overall, housing quality was considered satisfactory among 73% of MNO citizen respondents. This proportion is in line with the 72% of Indigenous renter households captured in the 2018 Canadian Housing Survey, which was lower than 85% Indigenous owner households.17 This MNO housing census found that the proportion of MNO citizen’s dwellings in need of major repairs was 17.3%, which was higher than the 10% reported by self-identified Métis respondents across Canada in the 2021 Canadian Census.5 This larger proportion of houses in need of major or minor repairs in the MNO citizen population is consistent with interview data collected from Métis people across Canada where home repairs were a major theme identified.12 Within the Indigenous context in Canada, poor housing conditions are rooted in longstanding structural factors, including colonial policies and chronic underfunding of housing for Indigenous peoples.18 Existing Indigenous housing research consistently highlights that inadequate housing and the impacts this has on Indigenous peoples’ health.5 15 17 Our study contributes to this literature by quantifying this relationship within a Métis population.

Our findings from the stratified analyses align with broader evidence that housing insecurity and poor quality environments can amplify stress and reduce physical and mental well-being, particularly among socioeconomically vulnerable groups.19–21 Evidence from both the main effect models and stratified analyses underscores that improvements in housing quality are not just a matter of addressing physical conditions but also of reducing socioeconomic inequalities in health outcomes. These findings highlight that both objective (houses in need of major repairs) and subjective (dissatisfaction with housing conditions) dimensions of housing quality contribute to self-reported health. Targeted approaches that consider socioeconomic context may be necessary to maximise health benefits and reduce housing-related health inequalities for MNO citizens.

The data collected from MNO citizens and Métis people across Canada demonstrate there is room for improvement in their housing conditions. These findings support calls for Métis-led, community-controlled approaches to housing, along with sustained investments. Research consistently shows that addressing both the physical quality of housing and the broader social environment is essential for improving health and well-being.22 Our results contribute to filling the gap in Métis health research and provide the MNO with citizen-specific housing information to inform the planning and evaluation of housing programs. These results emphasize the need for continued sustainable funding for the MNO’s emergency repair and housing stabilization programs.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is that it is the first to look at the association between housing conditions and health in a Métis population with a population-based approach. Current research lacks distinction-based approaches; thus, it was critical to examine Métis people specifically in collaboration with the MNO. Further research is required to grasp a full scope of Métis housing conditions. In addition, this study was led by the MNO; the MNO was involved throughout the entire research process, which aligns with the ethical principles of Métis research,23 Indigenous data sovereignty, and the rights of Indigenous Peoples to have control over various aspects of data, including collection, storage, ownership, access and consents, and application.24 Additionally, ‘holistic’ health was captured for MNO citizens which is a strength as viewing human health through as encompassing physical, emotional, spiritual, mental, and social/cultural experiences aligns with a Métis worldview.13

Our study also had some limitations. The respondents only represent a sample of MNO citizens, which introduces the potential for both non-response and exclusion bias, particularly for those living in poor housing conditions. We were only able to compare the respondents to the full MNO population by age and gender but not other important sociodemographic variables like education or income. The online format of the survey and the inclusion of only citizens with a valid email on file may potentially exclude citizens who do not have internet access or those with lower socioeconomic status or who may have forgotten to update their contact information with the MNO Registry. Although the survey captured many sociodemographic variables, including community belonging, it did not ask questions probing all Métis social determinants of health.11 Given this study is cross-sectional, housing condition and self-reported health were measured at the same time. As a result, we cannot determine temporality or causality, as reverse causation is possible in that poorer physical health may have led to lower socioeconomic status including poorer housing conditions for MNO citizens. In addition, single general items of self-reported health are limited, in particular, for mental health. Although single item mental health measures have demonstrated acceptable validity for population level monitoring, they do not capture specific symptom domains or diagnoses. Our results may also be a conservative estimate as the MNO registry represents Métis people living in Ontario who are eligible and have chosen to register with the MNO, and not necessarily all Métis people living in Ontario. Some Métis citizens may choose to retain their registration with Métis governments in their home provinces or not to register at all. Lastly, not all MNO citizens reside within Ontario, and thus some respondents included in the study may not reside in Ontario. This is presumed to be a small number based on available MNO registry data.

Conclusion

Overall housing quality among MNO citizens was assessed and a positive association between housing satisfaction and citizens’ mental and physical health status as well as overall holistic balance of health. The findings from this survey, along with results from the qualitative component of this study, were shared back with the survey respondents and the broader MNO community through an article in the 2023 Métis Voyageur newspaper. These findings contribute to filling the gaps in Métis health research and provide the MNO with citizen-specific housing data to inform the planning and evaluation of housing programmes.
