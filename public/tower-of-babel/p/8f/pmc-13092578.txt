Comparative performance of air sampling methods in a broiler house: implications for use and recovery of bacteria
Authors: JT Porzeinski, LA Wythe, AP McElroy, MB Farnell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate quantification of airborne bacteria in broiler houses is essential for assessing exposure risks to workers and animals, as well as for developing informed sanitation and surveillance strategies. Numerous sampling methods and equipment with differing collection physics, sampling parameters, processing, and handling have been developed in order to collect and quantify bacteria within bioaerosols. The objective of this study was to conduct a field comparison of three commonly used air sampling methods, including a cyclonic liquid impinger, a single-stage impactor, and gelatin filters housed in two-piece cassettes. Sampling was performed in an active broiler house using a balanced design (n = 6, k = 3, days = 5, N = 90) across five days (broiler ages D37, D38, D39, D40, and D44), with variation across sampling days evaluated descriptively. Liquid impinger and gelatin filter samples were collected from 100 L of air, whereas impactor samples were collected from 10 L to maintain countable colony densities. All counts were standardized to log10 cfu/m3 before statistical analysis. Sampler type was found to have a statistically significant effect on bacteria recovery (P < 0.05), with method effects evaluated independently for total aerobic bacteria and Staphylococcus spp. cultured on tryptic soy agar and mannitol salt agar using a linear mixed-effects model that accounted for day level and block level variability. MacConkey agar results yielded frequent non-detections, with only sporadic recoveries on 100 L impactor samples, indicating that Gram-negative bacteria including coliforms were present at concentrations near or below method specific limits of detection. Although differences between samplers were modest compared to variability between days (accounting for 34 to 51% of total estimated variance across media), the impactor consistently yielded the highest recoveries across both tryptic soy agar and mannitol salt agar (4.47 and 4.18 log10 cfu/m3, respectively), with recoveries from the liquid impinger and gelatin filters overlapping across media. These results indicate that sampler choice and associated processing can produce statistically significant but modest shifts in culturable airborne bacterial estimates that should be treated as a controlled methodological factor when interpreting and comparing field data. However, given the relative comparability between methods, ease of use and intended sampling parameters should guide sampling method selection rather than expectations of differing bacterial recovery.Keywords: Air sampling, Bioaerosol, Broiler house, Method comparison

Introduction

Bioaerosols in commercial broiler houses consist of complex mixtures of microorganisms and organic particulates derived from litter, feather, and dander fragments. Bird activity drives higher organic dust loads, and a high relative humidity is associated with increased concentrations of viable airborne bacteria (Ravić et al., 2024). Genomic analyses have further demonstrated the wide variety of microorganisms that exist within bioaerosols in broiler production facilities (O’Brien et al., 2016), including species of heightened concern to both birds and personnel such as bacterial pathogens and viruses with documented zoonotic or food safety concerns (Górny et al., 2023; Smith and King, 2023).

Mechanically ventilated poultry houses exhibit extensive spatial and temporal heterogeneity in bioaerosol concentrations. Variations in airflow, temperature gradients, relative humidity, and bird age contribute to fluctuations in airborne bacteria and dust levels (Du et al., 2019; Redwine et al., 2002; Wheeler et al., 2006). Broiler houses present a unique environment for bioaerosol sampling, as production factors including pen layout, ventilation design, stocking density, bird age, breed, and season can greatly influence bioaerosol concentrations and community composition. Environmental factors including temperature, relative humidity, time of day, and season also significantly affect airborne bacterial communities, with spatial distribution patterns varying across locations within facilities (Chen et al., 2022; Smith and King, 2023; Xu et al., 2022; Yan et al., 2023).

Poultry housing type significantly influences microbial community characteristics, with floor-housed operations producing higher airborne dust and bacterial concentrations than cage-housed facilities, supporting litter disturbance as a primary driver of bioaerosol generation (Just et al., 2011; St-Germain et al., 2025). Conventional broiler production facilities may be particularly susceptible as birds are reared directly on litter throughout the production cycle. Particulate matter concentrations and microbial community composition shift significantly across growth stages, with concentrations peaking during mid-growth attributed to increased fecal output, bird activity, and feather accumulation (Liu et al., 2026).

The three air sampling methods used in this study have multiple differences not only in operation, but also in the principles used to capture bacteria. Cyclonic liquid impingers are designed to minimize desiccation stress and particle bounce by continuously collecting particles into a liquid medium, improving recovery of viable microorganisms, including relatively fragile cells (Lin et al., 1999; Willeke et al., 1998). Single-stage inertial impactors collect particles by accelerating airflow through drilled orifices and impacting them directly onto agar surfaces for culture based analysis. Filter based cassette samplers with gelatin membranes have a high moisture content and are single use consumables. The gelatin filter captures bioaerosols through a combination of inertial impaction and diffusion within the filter matrix. Following sampling, gelatin membranes can be dissolved to allow complete transfer of collected microorganisms without mechanical extraction. However, because collection occurs on a semi-solid surface and sampling durations are constrained by temperature and humidity, culturability loss may still occur, particularly for stress sensitive organisms (Mainelis, 2020).

The objective of this study was to quantify method dependent differences in three populations of culturable airborne bacteria, measured using three commonly used bioaerosol sampling approaches under active broiler house conditions. Mixed-effects modeling was used to estimate the magnitude of method associated shifts in recovered concentrations relative to day level variance, providing a field relevant framework for interpreting culture based bioaerosol data across studies.

Materials and methods

Experimental setup

All air sampling was conducted in a mechanically ventilated broiler house at the Texas A&M University Poultry Science Research Center. Birds were 37 to 44 days of age over the sampling period, and sampling was performed on five days. The house, where sampling occurred, contained 80 pens each measuring 0.9 m x 1.8 m (3 ft by 6 ft) with 18 broilers per pen, corresponding to a stocking density of approximately 0.09 m2 (1 ft2) per bird. Litter consisted of fresh pine shavings with no litter amendments. Days were labeled D37 to D44 corresponding with bird ages at the time of sampling. Sampling days D37 through D40 were conducted consecutively, followed by D44 four days later, to capture both short term and between day variability under otherwise similar production conditions.

Sample size and replication

A total of 30 air samples were collected for both the liquid impinger and gelatin filter methods. Each consisted of six replicate collections spread across three blocks per day over five sampling days. For each sample, aliquots were plated onto tryptic soy agar (TSA), mannitol salt agar (MSA), and MacConkey agar (MCA; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). The impactor was operated under the same daily sampling schedule; however, because collection occurred directly onto agar surfaces without dilution or a secondary plating step, each impactor plate constituted an independent replicate for the corresponding media (n = 30 per media).

Media selection

Tryptic soy agar, MSA, and MCA were used to target specific populations of the culturable airborne bacterial community. Tryptic soy agar served as a non-selective medium for total aerobic culturable bacteria, MSA was selected to culture salt tolerant Gram-positive organisms, and MCA targeted Gram-negative enteric bacteria or coliforms. HardyCHROM ECC complete agar (Hardy Diagnostics, Santa Maria, CA, USA) was evaluated as a potential substitute for MCA, but comparable results led to the retention of MCA as the representative agar for coliforms.

Minimization of Bias

To reduce temporal and spatial bias, all air sampling began between 10:00 and 10:15 AM and concluded by 1:00 PM. Each sampling day, samples were collected in three cycled blocks of two technical replicates per method (replicates 1 and 2, 3 and 4, and 5 and 6), with the order of sampling methods repeated within each block to minimize within day time dependent effects. This was done as full randomization of replicate order was limited by pump availability and flow-rate requirements. Samples were collected from the same within-house location each day. The sampling table was positioned approximately 21.3 m (70 ft) from each end of the house, between two rows of pens, with its center located 3.0 m (10 ft) from one sidewall and 6.1 m (20 ft) from the other. Samplers were arranged on the table at 56 cm (22 in) spacing, 76 cm (30 in) above the house floor, and oriented directly into unobstructed airflow except for the impactor, which was oriented upward in accordance with manufacturer specifications. Sampler positions were also rotated across sampling days, ensuring that no sampler consistently occupied the same table location throughout the study.

Air sampling equipment

Three bioaerosol sampling methods were represented by the BioSampler Bioaerosol Collection Device, BioStage single-stage impactor, and gelatin filters housed in two-piece SureSeal cassettes (SKC Inc., Eighty Four, PA, USA). Sampling parameters were selected to permit practical comparisons among methods while adhering to manufacturer recommendations and field conditions.

Liquid impinger

The cyclonic liquid impinger operated at a calibrated flow rate of 12.5 L/min for 8 min, corresponding to a total sampled air volume of 100 L. The collection chamber was filled with 20 mL of sterile PBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) as the collection liquid. Following sampling, the collection liquid was stored within a wet ice cooler before transport to the lab for processing. Final sample volumes differed by less than 5% from the initial fill volume, resulting in no volume based correction factors being applied.

Single-stage impactor

For the single-stage impactor, a calibrated flow rate of 28.3 L/min was used. Sampling duration was set to 21.2 s, sampling a total air volume of 10 L through 400 jets. The sampled air was impacted directly onto agar plates with a 100 mm diameter containing TSA, MSA, or MCA. Agar plates for the impactor were stored at room temperature overnight alongside those used for liquid impinger and gelatin filter quantification. On each sampling day, impactor agar plates were transported to the sampling location at ambient temperature within a clean, hard sided container where they remained until sampling. Plates were loaded into the impactor within a separate container to shield it from oncoming wind and dust. To prevent condensation, plates were kept at ambient temperature post sampling until they were placed in the incubator. Plates were handled directly in the field rather than within a biosafety cabinet; use of a biosafety cabinet was not feasible given the number of samples collected per day and the need to minimize time variability between sampling events.

The final sampled air volume of 10 L was used for the impactor as opposed to the 100 L volume of the other methods. This was due to preliminary testing on D37 which indicated larger sampling volumes, such as 25 and 100 L, resulted in excessive colony overlap and plate overloading under barn conditions. Six plates for each media were sampled at 100 L, 25 L and 10 L on day 37. All 100 L TSA plates were too numerous to count with all 400 orifices showing growth, and in many cases multiple colonies growing from the same orifices. The 25 L plates showed countable values for MSA, however TSA plates were approaching or exceeding over the upper limit of quantification. Lowering the sampled air volume to 10 L helped maintain countable colony densities while minimizing particle coincidence and heavy reliance on positive hole correction associated with high impaction densities.

Gelatin filter

The gelatin filter air sampling method used 37 mm gelatin membrane filters housed in two-piece filter cassettes with a cellulose support pad, operated at a calibrated flow rate of 4 L/min for 25 min, corresponding to a total sampled air volume of 100 L. Gelatin filters were received sealed from the manufacturer and stored at 4°C until use. Prior to sampling, filters were aseptically loaded into two-piece SureSeal cassettes within a biosafety cabinet and returned to 4°C storage. On each sampling day, sealed cassettes were transported to the field within a wet ice cooler and only removed immediately prior to sampling. Following collection, cassettes were resealed and returned to the wet ice cooler until transport to the laboratory. In the laboratory, gelatin filters were allowed to warm to room temperature for 10 min before being disassembled in a biosafety cabinet. The filters were then placed in Whirl-Pak bags (Filtration Group, Pleasant Prairie, WI, USA) containing 2 mL of sterile PBS, and were homogenized for 30 s. The PBS was pre-warmed to 37°C, as recommended by the manufacturer, to promote complete dissolution while minimizing thermal stress on the collected microorganisms.

Sample processing and quantification

Liquid impinger samples and gelatin filter samples were homogenized by gentle vortexing or stomaching, respectively. Samples were serially diluted in sterile PBS to obtain countable colony densities. Aliquots of each sample and subsequent dilutions were spread plated onto TSA, MSA, or MCA. On day 37, 0.1 ml was plated for the liquid impinger samples whereas for the gelatin filter samples, 0.3 ml was plated. For all subsequent days 0.3 mL was plated for both liquid impinger and gelatin filter samples. Impactor samples were collected directly onto agar plates and processed without dilution. Agar plates for all three methods were incubated at 37°C for 24 h before quantification.

Enrichment cultures

Enrichment cultures were performed on all liquid impinger and gelatin filter samples to assess microbial culturability in the event of low detectability. Each sample was diluted 1:10 into tryptic soy broth, MacConkey broth (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), and mannitol salt broth (HiMedia Laboratories, Mumbai, India), and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. This was followed by plating and an additional 24 h incubation. Because impactor samples could not be enriched using conventional liquid based methods, duplicate 100 L air samples were collected daily for each medium in addition to the original 10 L samples. These 100 L samples were used as a proxy for enrichment based assessment to lower the limit of detection. Growth for all enrichments was assessed as positive or negative.

Blanks and quality control

Method blanks were collected daily for each sampler type and media to assess for potential contamination associated with sampler handling, transport, and operation (n = 5 per method and medium). Media blanks were also prepared daily for TSA, MSA, and MCA to verify sterility (n = 5 per medium).

On D44, growth was observed on a single TSA liquid impinger method blank and all corresponding media blanks remained negative. Because the corresponding media blank remained negative, contamination of the growth medium itself was ruled out. The same batch of media was used across all sampling days, and method blanks from all other sampling days remained negative, indicating that the isolated occurrence on D44 was consistent with an incidental handling event rather than a systematic workflow issue. Affected samples were therefore retained and interpreted conservatively. No additional method or media blanks exhibited growth during the study. One gelatin filter sample on day 38 was excluded from analysis due to contamination caused by a fly entering the cassette during sampling.

Sanitization during sampling

The impactor face was sanitized with 70% ethanol between media changes, and the liquid impinger collection vessel was rinsed thoroughly with sterile PBS between samples. The gelatin filter cassette used consumable components that were handled within a biosafety cabinet to prevent contamination.

Flow-rate calibration and pumps

Sampling flow rates were calibrated prior to sampling using a Chek-Mate Flowmeter for the AirChek Touch pump (gelatin filter), and an SKC field rotameter for the BioLite+ pump (liquid impinger and impactor; SKC Inc., Eighty Four, PA, USA).

Environmental monitoring

Environmental conditions within the house were recorded using a Kestrel 5200 Professional Environmental Meter at the sampling location and included ambient temperature, relative humidity, and air speed (Kestrel Instruments, Boothwyn, PA, USA). Outdoor environmental data were obtained from the Easterwood Airport meteorological station located adjacent to the facility.

Limits of detection and quantification

Limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) were calculated separately for each sampling method based on method specific air volumes, final sample volumes, and minimum detectable colony counts. Final sample volume (Vf) represents the total liquid volume containing the recovered microorganisms, including the collection liquid for the liquid impinger and the dissolution volume for the gelatin filter. The limit of detection was defined as the detection of a single colony, and LOQ as the minimum count meeting standard countability criteria. Method specific formulas are provided below.

Positive-hole correction

For inertial impactor samples, raw colony counts were corrected using the positive-hole correction method prior to normalization to log10 cfu/m3. In single-stage impactors, multiple particles can impact through the same jet orifice during a sampling event, resulting in the formation of multiple colonies occupying the same location on the agar surface. This causes underestimation of true particle collection, with the degree of underestimation increasing as overall colony density increases. The positive-hole correction method uses raw count correction tables based on probability statistics that there will be two or more coincident particles within a single jet of the impactor. This offsets bias toward low end counts that may occur as raw counts increase, and allows for more accurate reporting of culturable cfu. The positive-hole corrected counts were then normalized for use in statistical analysis. This study was conducted using an impactor with 400 jets which has a corresponding table outlined in Macher (1989).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using JMP Student Edition 18.2.1 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Linear mixed-effects models were fit separately for TSA and MSA data. To evaluate overall differences between sampling methods, method was included as a fixed effect with sampling day as well as replicate blocks nested within day, set as random effects. Random effects covariance parameter estimates were extracted from the models to quantify the relative contributions of sampling day, replicate block and residual error to total variability. All analyses were conducted on and reported in log10 cfu/m3 transformed airborne bacterial concentrations.

When dilution plates fell outside the standard 30 to 300 cfu countability range, the dilution closest to this range was used for quantification. For all methods, plates were counted when colonies were discrete and countable. Observations below the 30 cfu countability threshold were retained because they represent true detections above method specific LOD and excluding them would bias method comparisons. For inertial impaction, plates with counts slightly above or below the 30 to 300 cfu range were retained when colonies remained discrete and positive-hole correction could be reliably applied.

Environmental variables (temperature, relative humidity, and air speed) were evaluated descriptively to confirm environmental consistency across sampling days but were not included as fixed effects in statistical models to avoid inflation of type I error and multicollinearity with day level random effects.

Results

Experiment overview

A total of 30 air samples for each method were collected across five sampling days and plated on three media, resulting in 270 data points (3 methods × 5 days × 3 blocks × 2 replicates × 3 media = 270 observations). Following the exclusion of one gelatin filter replicate due to quality control findings, TSA and MSA datasets were analyzed using separate linear mixed-effects models on the log10 cfu/m3 scale. MacConkey agar results were not modeled due to a high proportion of non-detections and were interpreted descriptively relative to method specific limits of detection. The single positive observation on a gelatin filter MCA plate did not yield growth upon enrichment. Observed enrichment culture growth coincided with colony counts for all remaining replicates and was therefore not included in statistical modeling.

Sampling method effects within each media

Sampling method significantly influenced recovery for both TSA and MSA following application of positive-hole correction (TSA: F2,72.5 = 4.54, P = 0.0139; MSA: F2,72.3 = 6.24, P = 0.0032). For TSA, the impactor yielded the highest LS-mean recovery (4.47 ± 0.10 log10 cfu/m3), followed by the liquid impinger (4.36 ± 0.10 log10 cfu/m3) and the gelatin filter (4.31 ± 0.10 log10 cfu/m3; Table 1). Pairwise comparisons indicated that impactor recoveries were significantly greater than those of the gelatin filter, whereas differences between the gelatin filter and liquid impinger were not statistically significant. For MSA, the impactor again produced the highest recoveries (4.18 ± 0.08 log10 cfu/m3), followed by the gelatin filter (4.06 ± 0.08 log10 cfu/m3) and the liquid impinger (3.98 ± 0.08 log10 cfu/m3; Table 1). As opposed to TSA, MSA impactor recoveries were statistically significantly greater than those of the liquid impinger, and differences between the gelatin filter and the liquid impinger were not statistically significant.

MacConkey agar results

MacConkey agar results showed a high frequency of non-detections, with growth observed in only one gelatin filter replicate. All liquid impinger and gelatin filter MCA enrichment cultures were negative, and 100 L impactor MCA plates showed sporadic growth. These results were not suitable for mixed-effects modeling and were instead evaluated in relation to method specific limits of detection.

Random effects and variance partitioning

Variance component estimates indicated that sampling day accounted for approximately 51% of total variance in log10 cfu/m3 for TSA and 34% for MSA, with residual variance comprising the remaining 49% for TSA and 65% for MSA. Replicate blocks nested within day contributed 0% of total variance for TSA and 1.3% for MSA.

Between day stability within methods

Day level mean airborne bacterial concentrations for each method and medium are presented in Table 2. Across both media, the impactor exhibited the largest between day ranges for TSA (4.20 to 5.04 log10 cfu/m3) and MSA (3.78 to 4.62 log10 cfu/m3), indicating greater sensitivity to temporal variation in barn bioaerosol concentrations. For TSA, the gelatin filter produced the most stable recoveries across sampling days, with day level means ranging from 4.08 to 4.60 log10 cfu/m3, while the liquid impinger showed slightly greater variability ranging from 4.03 to 4.58 log10 cfu/m3. For MSA, the liquid impinger produced the most stable recoveries, with day level means ranging from 3.81 to 4.12 log10 cfu/m3, whereas the gelatin filter was more variable with means ranging between 3.82 to 4.32 log10 cfu/m3.

Environmental monitoring

Environmental metadata, including temperature, relative humidity, and air speed remained within relatively narrow ranges across sampling days due to consistent morning sampling and stable mechanical ventilation. These variables were therefore used to verify environmental consistency rather than as predictors. Environmental covariates were intentionally excluded from mixed-effects modeling to avoid inflation of type I error. Conditions on D40 were an exception to the otherwise consistent pattern, with comparatively lower relative humidity ranging from 33.2 to 37.7% RH compared to 60.6 to 78.6% across all other sampling days, as well as reduced mechanical ventilation and moderately elevated bacterial concentrations.

Discussion

Summary

This study demonstrated that between day environmental variability exerted a stronger influence on recoverable airborne bacterial concentrations than sampler type, and differences among sampling devices were comparatively modest.

Across all methods, TSA consistently yielded higher recoveries than MSA, reflecting its non-selective nature and support for aerobic bacterial growth. On the other hand, MCA recoveries were largely below detection limits (LOD), consistent with the low ambient abundance and stress sensitivity of Gram-negative bacteria in poultry house bioaerosols (Zhao et al., 2014).

Mixed-effects modeling revealed substantial between day variability in airborne bacterial concentrations, consistent with the inherent temporal heterogeneity of bioaerosols in commercial poultry houses (Liu et al., 2026). Replicate block level variance was minimal, indicating that the blocking and sampler rotation strategy effectively controlled short term temporal and locational biases.

Sampler performance

Sampler dependent effects were statistically detectable but relatively small. Following application of positive-hole correction, the impactor yielded slightly higher recoveries than the gelatin filter on TSA and the liquid impinger on MSA. However, observed differences should be interpreted as differences across collection, processing, and analytical workflows rather than as differences in collection efficiency alone (Dybwad et al., 2014; Macher and Macher, 1997; Mainelis, 2020; Wythe et al., 2026).

Another point of interest was the recoveries obtained using the liquid impinger and gelatin filter, which were statistically similar on TSA and MSA. This could indicate that despite differences in collection mechanics and processing, both liquid impingement and gelatin filter based sampling can provide comparable quantitative estimates of total culturable airborne bacteria when sampling duration, flow control, and downstream processing are appropriately managed.

The elevated impactor recoveries observed on D40 may be partially explained by the lower relative humidity recorded on that day (33.2 to 37.7%) compared to all other sampling days (60.6 to 78.6%). Under reduced humidity conditions, desiccation stress within the liquid impinger and gelatin filter methods could increase physiological stress on collected microorganisms, potentially suppressing culturability in these methods relative to the impactor, which collects directly onto agar without a liquid intermediate (Li, 1999; Siller et al., 2024; Tseng and Li, 2005). Reduced humidity may also alter the aerodynamic characteristics of bioaerosol particles, potentially shifting particle size distribution in ways that could affect collection efficiency across sampler types, though this mechanism cannot be confirmed without concurrent particle size measurements.

The gelatin filter was included as it retains moisture and enables the transfer of bioaerosols by complete dissolution of the filter, avoiding the incomplete mechanical extraction and desiccation of bacteria that can limit dry filter methods. However, gelatin filters are not universally exempt from desiccation stress, as prior aerosol physics work shows that gelatin can dehydrate during extended sampling as the filter dries out, reducing recovery of fragile organisms under low humidity or long duration conditions (Li, 1999; Tseng and Li, 2005). Neither issue occurred under the conditions evaluated here, as relative humidity was high on four of five sampling days and sampling durations were kept within manufacturer recommended limits. This also distinguishes gelatin filter sampling from other filter collection approaches previously evaluated in poultry settings with dry surfaces that required mechanical extraction of bacteria from the filter after sample collection (Woodward et al., 2004).

Gram-negative recovery and media selectivity

In some instances (three positive detections out of 14 total 100 L collections), isolated colonies of bacteria were recovered on individual impactor 100 L MCA plates. Detection of a single colony for the impactor following a 100 L air sampling corresponds to an estimated airborne concentration of approximately 1.00 log10 cfu/m3. The absence of consistent recovery across replicates or sampling days indicates that these isolated detections reflect rare airborne Gram-negative cells rather than stable or quantifiable populations within the house air.

This is consistent with previous reports in which airborne coliform concentrations varied significantly by operation type and sampling location. For example, a study sampling cattle operations reported coliform concentrations at or below 1.00 log10 cfu/m3, levels that would be near or below the detection limit of impactor sampling under the present study design. In the same study, higher coliform concentrations were reported in poultry systems at exhaust fan locations, where emitted bioaerosols could be concentrated from the entire house, rather than in ambient interior air (Ruiz-Llacsahuanga et al., 2024). A study analyzing spatial concentrations of total aerobic bacteria in a layer house found that concentrations of bacteria were significantly higher near exhaust locations than elsewhere in the house, with a gradient increase in airborne cfu longitudinally down the house (Zhang et al., 2025). This could explain why other studies sampling air near exhaust locations found greater concentrations of coliforms. Moreover, on top of a low baseline concentration, Gram-negative bacteria could face greater susceptibility to environmental stress and exist in a viable but not culturable state when sampled from the environment, further lowering the threshold of detection (Fakruddin et al., 2013).

The lack of consistent quantitative recovery on selective media should not be interpreted as true absence, but rather as an indication that airborne coliform concentrations under routine interior broiler house conditions fluctuate around the lower boundary of detection, or decreased Gram-negative viability due to handling/desiccation stress, resulting in sporadic detections.

Quality control and detection limits

Daily method blanks, media blanks, and enrichment cultures confirmed the integrity of the sampling workflow and processing, and the reliability of resulting log10 cfu/m3 estimates. With the exception of two colonies on a single liquid impinger TSA blank, which was attributed to handling related contamination, no evidence of method or media blank contamination was observed. These quality control measures increase confidence that observed differences among samplers and media reflect true methodological and biological effects rather than procedural contamination.

Differences in method specific LODs and LOQs reflect variation in sampled air volume and dilution rather than collection efficiency (Table 3). As such, LOD and LOQ values should be interpreted as operational characteristics of each sampling workflow and can be manipulated depending on study design with some limitations depending on the method used.

Logistical considerations and field handling

Beyond bacterial recovery, sampler selection in field settings is strongly influenced by logistical handling requirements, contamination risk, and practical constraints on sampling duration and flow rate. These considerations are particularly relevant in active agricultural production environments where access, setup time, and aseptic handling can be limiting factors. Therefore, although all three sampling approaches evaluated here were capable of producing interpretable quantitative data under the conditions studied, each presents distinct operational tradeoffs that influence method suitability depending on study objectives and environmental context.

Gelatin filters within the two-piece cassettes offered the greatest logistical simplicity, with assembly and disassembly performed entirely within a biosafety cabinet using sterile, single-use components that minimized contamination risk during handling. The filters also completely dissolve, allowing processing flexibility that direct impaction lacks. However, gelatin filters are constrained by manufacturer recommended limits on sampling duration, restricting collections to 30 min to prevent filter dehydration. Therefore, with a maximum flow rate of 4 L/min, the total sampled air volume may be insufficient to achieve quantifiable recovery in low concentration environments, effectively limiting applicability to locations with relatively high bioaerosol density.

The liquid impinger enables longer sampling durations and flexible processing, including dilution, enrichment, and molecular analyses. Additionally, because of the 20 mL collection liquid volume used for its operation, microorganisms are substantially diluted, requiring larger sampled air volumes to achieve comparable detectable concentrations. The liquid impinger also operates at a lower effective flow rate compared to the impactor, which necessitates longer sampling times. This can be beneficial for time integrated sampling in shifting environments, but can also be limiting for point based applications such as disinfection efficacy assessments, where rapid sampling at defined locations is required. Field deployment also requires careful handling and transport to prevent damage to the delicate and expensive glass components. Moreover, liquid handling and disinfection of the liquid impinger are difficult in field environments when multiple samples are collected sequentially. These factors increase the logistical complexity and potential contamination risk relative to disposable filter based and stage impactor methods.

Inertial impaction with the single-stage impactor provides rapid sampling and direct collection onto culture media at a high flow rate, facilitating efficient recovery without the need for post collection processing. However, impactors must be loaded with agar plates prior to or during field deployment and may be directly exposed to contaminants during sampling and handling. High bioaerosol concentrations can also necessitate short sampling durations to avoid plate overloading, requiring prior knowledge, estimation, or preliminary testing of concentrations to select appropriate sampling times and volumes. This limitation was encountered during preliminary testing on D37, where sampled volumes of 100 and 25 L resulted in plate overloading and colony coincidence, necessitating the reduction of sampled volume to 10 L. In an environment where bacterial concentrations are relatively consistent (within 1 log), the impactor can be used reliably, however, as concentrations fluctuate beyond what the sampling volume can reliably capture, reliability decreases. This highlights a definitive drawback of the impactor compared to its liquid impinger and gelatin filter counterparts that can be diluted to increase the range of quantifiable concentrations.

Moreover, quantitative interpretation of impactor data requires application of positive-hole correction to account for coincident particle deposition, introducing an additional analytical step compared to liquid or filter based methods (Macher, 1989). When coupled with the issue of a limited quantitative range due to no post sample dilution, higher impactor counts become increasingly reliant on positive-hole correction to account for greater particle coincidence.

Sampler selection should be guided not only by recovery efficiency and sensitivity, but also by practical considerations including bioaerosol concentrations, allowable sampling duration, air volume, contamination risk, processing, and field handling constraints.

Future directions

While this study focused on culturable airborne bacteria, culturable microorganisms represent a small fraction of the total microbial load within bioaerosols. Viable but non culturable bacteria may still contribute to health risks while being undetectable by culture based methods. Even so, culture based methods can contribute to health risk assessment through quantification of approximate bacterial concentrations and the detection of indicator organisms. Future research could extend this study's framework by integrating culture based and molecular approaches, particle size distribution measurements, or mechanistic bioaerosol transport modeling to further resolve sampler biases and improve characterization of low abundance or stress sensitive species. Application of this approach across additional agricultural and occupational environments would also further test its generalizability and support development of standardized methodologies for bioaerosol surveillance for animal and worker health and biosecurity.
