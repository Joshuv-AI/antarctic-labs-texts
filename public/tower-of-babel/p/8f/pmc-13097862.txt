Extreme weather events and malaria prevention in pregnancy: a mediation analysis of antenatal care and sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine use in two malaria-endemic counties of Kenya
Authors: Dennis Juma Matanda, Martina Mchenga, Jacinter Aluoch Amadi, Zoe Nakuya, Oscar Okoth, Brenda Otieno, Francis Obare
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClimate change threatens maternal, and newborn health, particularly by exacerbating climate-sensitive diseases like malaria. Malaria in pregnancy (MiP) contributes to maternal anaemia, stillbirth, preterm delivery, and low birth weight. In Kenya’s Lake Victoria basin, recurrent floods and droughts disrupt antenatal care (ANC), the main delivery platform for intermittent preventive treatment of MiP with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (IPTp-SP). The extent to which these extreme weather events affect IPTp-SP uptake through ANC attendance remains unexplored.MethodsData are drawn from a cross-sectional household survey conducted under the revive IPTp-SP project among women who had given birth to a live baby in the last 24 months preceding the interview in malaria-endemic counties of Kisumu and Migori. Exposure was self-reported extreme weather events in the past 12 months; the outcome was completion of three or more IPTp-SP doses (IPTp₃ +); and the mediator was attendance of ≥ 4 ANC visits (ANC4+). Using a counterfactual mediation framework, we decomposed the total effect of climate shock on IPTp₃ + into natural direct and indirect effects via ANC4+, adjusting for covariates. Models accounted for the complex survey design, with bootstrapping to estimate 95% confidence intervals (CI) for indirect effects.ResultsIPTp₃ + coverage was lower among shock-exposed pregnancies (49.5%) versus unexposed (58.8%). Extreme weather events were associated with reduction in ANC4+ attendance (− 0.23, 95% CI − 0.41 to − 0.05). ANC4+ completion strongly predicted IPTp₃ + uptake (1.71, 95% CI 1.42–2.00). The total effect of shocks on IPTp₃ + was -0.45 (95% CI − 0.68 to − 0.23), of which 76.6% was mediated through ANC. After adjustment, the total effect attenuated (-0.27, 95% CI − 0.56 to 0.02) and was no longer significant. Effects were stronger in flood-prone Kisumu than in Migori.ConclusionsExtreme weather events reduced IPTp₃ + uptake primarily through ANC disruption in unadjusted models, with effects concentrated in Kisumu. However, these associations attenuated after adjustment, suggesting the role of underlying socioeconomic and contextual vulnerabilities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05859-3.Keywords: Malaria in pregnancy, Climate change, Antenatal care, Sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine, Kenya

Background

Climate change is not only an environmental emergency but a growing threat to maternal, newborn, and child health particularly in low- and middle-income countries [1, 2]. Rising temperatures altered rainfall patterns, and more frequent extreme weather events are projected to exacerbate existing health inequities and undermine decades of progress towards Sustainable Development Goal 3 that aims to ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages [3]. In sub-Saharan Africa, climate-sensitive vector borne diseases such as malaria remain leading causes of morbidity and mortality among pregnant women and newborns [4]. Malaria in pregnancy (MiP) is associated with maternal anaemia, stillbirth, preterm delivery, and low birth weight, contributing substantially to neonatal mortality [5]. In 2023, approximately 35 million pregnancies in malaria-endemic African countries were at risk of adverse birth outcomes [4].

Over the last 2.5 decades, major floods have occurred in Kenya, destroying essential infrastructure and services (1997–1998, 2002, 2003, 2006, 2008, 2010, 2012 and 2015) [2]. Kenya’s Lake Victoria basin, which includes Kisumu and Migori counties, is among the country’s highest malaria transmission zones [6], making it an important setting to examine the interaction between climate variability and malaria prevention in pregnancy. Changes in rainfall patterns, prolonged flooding, and occasional droughts have altered mosquito breeding habitats and shifted seasonal transmission peaks [7]. According to the Kenya Meteorological Department, recurrent flooding along the Lake Victoria shoreline and in low-lying farmland in these counties damages crops, displaces communities, and disrupts transport networks, limiting access to essential maternal health services, including antenatal care (ANC) [8, 9].

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends a package of proven interventions to prevent malaria in pregnancy in areas of moderate-to-high transmission, which includes the use of insecticide-treated mosquito nets (ITNs), prompt diagnosis and effective case management of malaria illness, and intermittent preventive treatment with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (IPTp-SP) administered at scheduled ANC visits [10]. IPTp-SP is recommended to be started as early as possible in the second trimester, with doses given at least one month apart until delivery to achieve optimal protection for both the mother and the foetus [10]. In Kenya, IPTp-SP is delivered through facility-based ANC services under direct observation by skilled health personnel, making ANC attendance the primary gateway for access to this intervention [11].

Disruptions to ANC attendance during climatic shocks have been documented in several contexts [12–15]. In Kenya, climate variability has been shown to drive fluctuations in malaria transmission and strain health service delivery. Nyawanda et al. [16], identified climate variability as a key factor influencing malaria parasite prevalence between 2008 and 2019, and a subsequent analysis confirmed its continued role in shaping malaria incidence in western Kenya despite intervention scale-up [17]. Complementing these findings, the Kenya Climate Change Impacts on Health and Livelihoods report [18], underscores how recurrent floods, droughts, and infrastructure damage further disrupt access to essential health services.

Despite this evidence base, no previous research has quantified the indirect effects of climate shocks on IPTp-SP uptake through ANC utilisation in high-malaria settings such as Kisumu and Migori counties. This study addresses that gap by applying a mediation analysis framework to assess whether, and to what extent, climate shocks reduce IPTp-SP coverage via their impact on ANC attendance. By disentangling these pathways, the findings aim to inform climate-resilient maternal health strategies that safeguard ANC continuity and IPTp-SP delivery during environmental disruptions.

Methods

Study aim, design and setting

The study aims to investigate the effect of climate shocks on the uptake IPTp-SP in Kenya’s Lake Victoria basin, with a particular focus on how ANC attendance mediates this relationship. We conducted an analysis of a cross-sectional survey data from the endline survey of the Revive IPTp-SP project in Kisumu and Migori counties in western Kenya. The Revive IPTp-SP project is a health systems strengthening intervention (2020–2025) aimed at revitalizing uptake of the recommended three or more doses of IPTp-SP in malaria-endemic regions [19]. The project focused on Kisumu and Migori, two adjacent counties around Lake Victoria with high malaria transmission and historically low IPTp coverage [19]. Kisumu county is primarily urban and around 15 m above the water level of Lake Victoria, and Migori is predominantly rural bordering Tanzania and 250 m above the lake level [20].

The endline survey was carried out from November to December in 2024 among women of reproductive age (15–49) who had given birth to a live baby in the last 24 months preceding the survey. Using a two-stage cluster sampling design, the survey obtained a representative sample of women from communities living in Kisumu and Migori. Strata were defined by county (and further by sub-county), and primary sampling units (PSUs) were sub-locations (the smallest administrative units) selected with probability proportional to size [15, 21]. Within each selected cluster, households were enumerated, and eligible women interviewed. Survey weights were computed to account for the sampling probabilities and non-response, allowing generalization to the population of women in the study area.

The analysis focuses on women who had a pregnancy and gave birth to a live baby within the last 24 months preceding the survey. These respondents provided retrospective information about their most recent pregnancy, ANC attendance, and malaria prevention during that pregnancy. Our analytic sample was restricted to women who had complete data on exposure, mediator, and outcome variables.

Measures

Outcome: the outcome measure of the present study was uptake of IPTp-SP, defined as receipt of three or more doses of IPTp (IPTp₃ + uptake). We constructed a binary indicator coded as 1 if the woman received three or more doses of sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) and 0 if they received 2 or less. This measure was primarily derived from entries recorded in the woman’s ANC booklet. Where the booklet was unavailable, self-reported data were used.

Exposure: Climate shock related events during last pregnancy is the main exposure of interest. In the survey, women were asked about whether during their last pregnancy, they ever failed to attend a clinic or get to the hospital due to any of the following extreme weather events (heavy rains, flooding, drought and hot sun) (see appendix Table 1A). We operationalized extreme weather event as a binary variable indicating whether the woman was exposed to at least one significant climate shock during her most recent pregnancy period.

Mediator: Adequate ANC attendance was defined as attending at least four antenatal care contacts during the pregnancy. The number of ANC contacts was obtained primarily from the woman’s ANC booklet; where the booklet was unavailable or incomplete, self-reported visit counts were used. Documentation of ANC contacts in routine records is known to be imperfect, particularly for higher-order visits, and misclassification is more likely as visit thresholds increase.

We therefore applied the four-visit benchmark in line with the earlier WHO focused ANC model [22], which remains a widely reported indicator at national and global levels and is less sensitive to documentation gaps. Although WHO now recommends a minimum of eight contacts [23], only approximately 4% of women in our analytic sample achieved eight or more ANC contacts (see Fig. 1 in the appendix), limiting the feasibility and interpretability of ANC8 as a mediator in this setting.

Mediation framework showing the hypothesised pathways linking climate shock, ANC4 + attendance, and IPTp₃ + uptake

Covariates: We controlled for a set of potential confounding factors in the mediation analysis. These included maternal age (15–19; 20–24; 25–34 and 35 +), parity (number of prior births), education level (no education, primary, secondary and higher), socio-economic status (created from an asset index adapted from demographic health survey [24] and divided into five quintiles including poorest, poor, middle, rich and richest) and empowerment indicator, measures the woman’s agency in making decisions related to social life, health and household purchases (full item definition and coding are detailed in the appendix).

We also adjusted for marital status and whether the woman slept under a mosquito net throughout her pregnancy. These covariates were selected a priori based on their association with both exposure and outcome. For example, poorer women might be more likely to be affected by climate shocks and have lower healthcare utilization [11].

Statistical analysis

A counterfactual mediation framework was applied to decompose the effect of the exposure (climate shock) on the outcome (IPTp₃ + uptake) into: (1) the total effect (TE) of climate shock on IPTp₃ + ; (2) the natural direct effect (NDE), representing the pathway not mediated by ANC attendance; and (3) the natural indirect effect (NIE), quantifying the portion mediated through attendance of four or more ANC visits. This framework follows the definitions outlined by VanderWeele [25] and is illustrated in Fig. 1.

In the mediation diagram, climate shock (X) represents the exposure, ANC4+ (M) is the mediator, and IPTp₃ + uptake (Y) is the outcome. Path a captures the effect of climate shock on ANC attendance, path b represents the effect of ANC attendance on IPTp₃ + uptake, and path c´ represents the direct effect of climate shock on IPTp₃ + uptake, independent of ANC4+ attendance. The total effect c is the sum of the direct and indirect components.

In this context, the NDE represents the estimated effect of climate shock if ANC4+ were held constant at the level it would have been without exposure, thereby isolating the direct influence of the shock (e.g., through other pathways such as health facility disruptions or altered malaria risk). These pathways may include health facility disruptions as well as changes in malaria transmission risk induced by extreme weather events. Climate shocks can modify malaria risk by altering vector breeding conditions and transmission intensity, which may in turn influence IPTp₃ + uptake among ANC attendees through provider behaviour (e.g., heightened emphasis on IPTp during periods of perceived high risk), supply prioritisation, or women’s risk perceptions and acceptance of preventive treatment. The NIE captures the component of the effect transmitted through changes in ANC attendance.

For estimation, we estimated the mediation model as a generalised least-equations path model with two equations fit simultaneously: (1) a logit link for the binary mediator (ANC4⁺) as a function of climate shock and covariates (path a), and (2) a logit link for the binary outcome (IPTp₃ +) as a function of ANC4⁺, climate shock and covariates (paths b and c′). Joint estimation allows the two equations to be fit within one system, accommodating the path structure and the covariance between equation errors. Survey design features (strata, PSU) and sampling weights were incorporated, and standard errors were obtained with a design-robust (sandwich) estimator. Survey design variables (strata, PSU) and sampling weights using design-adjusted estimation (e.g., generalised linear models with survey weights) were incorporated.

We obtained the indirect effect using standard mediation formulas, calculated as the product of the coefficients for climate shock → ANC4⁺ and ANC4⁺ → IPTp₃ + pathways, assuming no exposure-mediator interaction. Variance estimation for indirect effects was performed via nonparametric bootstrapping, resampling at the cluster level to preserve the multistage sampling structure. A total of 1,000 bootstrap replications were used to derive percentile-based 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the NIE and NDE. All hypothesis tests were two-sided, with a significance level of α = 0.05.

The mediation analysis assumes no unmeasured confounding of the exposure-outcome, exposure-mediator, and mediator-outcome relationships. This was addressed by controlling for relevant observed covariates, though residual confounding remains possible. We estimated both unadjusted models, including only exposure, mediator, and outcome and adjusted models, controlling for all covariates listed above including county fixed effects for pooled analysis. All models incorporated survey weights, strata, and PSU design variables. Statistical significance thresholds were defined a priori as: p < 0.01: strong evidence; p < 0.05: moderate evidence; and p < 0.10: marginal evidence. Missing data were minimal and largely attributable to questionnaire skip patterns. Information on ANC attendance was available for 3,220 women, and IPTp/Fansidar use was available for 3,125 women, where climate shock information was also available for all women in the sample. Stepwise deletion was applied, such that each model equation was estimated using all observations with complete data for the variables included in that equation. Analyses were conducted in Stata 16 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Overall, 57% reported receiving at least three doses of intermittent preventive treatment for malaria in pregnancy (IPTp₃ +), while approximately 20% experienced at least one extreme weather event (flooding, unusually heavy rainfall, severe drought, or extreme heat) during their most recent pregnancy. The types of shocks differed by county: floods predominated in Kisumu (41%), whereas Migori was more affected by unusually heavy rains (42%) and extreme heat (38%) (see Appendix Fig. 1A). Completion of four or more ANC visits was reported by 73% of women.

Most women accessed ANC in public health facilities (83%) and reported consistent mosquito net use during pregnancy (92%). Nearly two-fifths of the sample had only primary education or less, and the majority were aged 20–34 years (77%) and married (81%). More than 70% had two or more prior births. The distribution across household wealth quintiles was broad, though the wealthiest quintile accounted for a disproportionately large share (36%). Religious affiliation was predominantly Protestant (76%), followed by Catholic (14%) and other faiths (10%). Roughly one-third of women were classified as empowered (31%). Geographically, more respondents resided in Migori (53%) than in Kisumu (47%).

Mediation analysis: pathways linking climate shocks, ANC4+, and IPTp₃ + uptake

Aggregated results

Table 2 summarises the aggregated logistic‐regression mediation models. In the unadjusted specification, exposure to extreme whether events during pregnancy was associated with a significant reduction in attaining ANC4+ (path a = − 0.23, 95% CI − 0.41 to − 0.05, p = 0.016). Attaining ANC4+, in turn, was strongly associated with higher odds of IPTp₃ + completion (path b = 1.71, 95% CI 1.42 to 2.00, p < 0.001). The resulting indirect (mediated) effect of climate shock on IPTp₃ + completion was negative and statistically significant (NIE = − 0.35, 95% CI − 0.61 to -0.09, p = 0.009), accounting for 76.6% of the total effect (TE = − 0.45, 95% CI − 0.68 to − 0.23, p < 0.001). The residual (direct) effect was non-significant (NDE = − 0.11, 95% CI − 0.26 to − 0.05, p = 0.181). On the odds-ratio scale, the indirect-to-direct ratio is 0.78, and the total-to-direct ratio is 0.71.

After adjustment for covariates, the association between climate shock and ANC4+ attendance (path a) remained negative but was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (− 0.15; 95% CI − 0.38 to 0.07; p = 0.172). Neither the natural direct effect of climate shock on achieving IPTp₃ + uptake (NDE = -0.06; 95% CI − 0.23 to 0.11; p = 0.484) nor the natural indirect effect mediated through ANC4+ attendance (NIE = − 0.21; 95% CI − 0.51 to 0.09; p = 0.166) was statistically significant. In contrast, ANC4+ attendance remained strongly and positively associated with IPTp₃ + uptake (path b = 1.66; 95% CI 1.40 to 1.93; p < 0.001). Although the total effect of climate shock on IPTp₃ + uptake was negative, it was not statistically significant (TE = − 0.27; 95% CI − 0.56 to 0.02; p = 0.068). On the odds-ratio scale, the indirect-to-direct effect ratio was 0.94, while the total-to-direct effect ratio was 0.81.

Beyond climate shocks, several sociodemographic and contextual characteristics were significantly associated with ANC4+ attendance and IPTp₃ + uptake (as shown in the appendix Tables 2A and 3A). Women with tertiary education, those aged 20–24, married women, Protestants, and those in the wealthiest households were more likely to complete four or more ANC visits. By contrast, multiparous women and those residing in Migori were less likely to achieve ANC4+. With respect to IPTp₃ + uptake, marital status remained an important determinant, while women who sought ANC services from private facilities were less likely to complete three or more doses. Similarly, Muslim women and those from Migori reported lower odds of attaining the recommended three IPTp doses.

County-level mediation impact

We conducted stratified mediation analyses for Migori and Kisumu counties (Table 3) to assess heterogeneity in the effects of extreme weather events. In Migori, there was no evidence of an association between extreme weather events and either ANC4+ attendance or IPTp₃ + uptake. The climate shock → ANC4 + path (a) was negative but insignificant (unadjusted a = -0.13, 95% CI: -0.43 to 0.17, p = 0.393; adjusted a = − 0.11, 95% CI − 0.42 to 0.19, p = 0.460). Completion of ANC4+ nonetheless remained a strong predictor of IPTp₃ + uptake in Migori (adjusted b = 1.42, 95% CI 1.09–1.74, p < 0.001), mirroring the pooled analysis. Given the insignificant path a, the indirect effect was also non-significant (adjusted NIE = − 0.17, 95% CI − 0.58 to 0.24, p = 0.398). Neither the direct effect (adjusted NDE = 0.02, 95% CI − 0.24 to 0.28, p = 0.881) nor the total effect (adjusted TE = − 0.15, 95% CI − 0.58 to 0.28, p = 0.487) were significant.

Discussion

This study suggests that extreme weather events may undermine IPTp₃ + uptake largely through their effects on ANC attendance, although the strength of this relationship depends on model specification and local context. In pooled analyses, unadjusted models indicated that disruptions to ANC utilisation accounted for most of the observed association between climate shocks and reduced IPTp uptake, but these effects were attenuated and no longer statistically robust after covariate adjustment. Subcounty analyses revealed important heterogeneity: evidence of climate-related disruptions to IPTp uptake was observed in Kisumu, whereas no corresponding effects were detected in Migori. Taken together, these findings indicate that climate shocks have the potential to disrupt maternal malaria prevention, but their impacts are uneven and shaped by place-specific factors, including geographic exposure and underlying system vulnerabilities.

The absence of a statistically significant direct effect of climate shocks on IPTp₃ + uptake is consistent with how malaria prevention services during pregnancy are organised in Kenya. IPTp is delivered almost exclusively through routine ANC visits, with limited alternative delivery channels outside the formal health system. As a result, climate shocks are unlikely to influence IPTp uptake independently of ANC utilisation. Even when extreme weather alters malaria transmission risk or heightens perceived vulnerability, pregnant women must still attend ANC in order to receive Fansidar. When ANC attendance is held constant, as in the estimation of the natural direct effect, there is therefore limited scope for climate shocks to affect IPTp uptake through other pathways. This structural dependence helps explain why the estimated effects operate primarily through ANC attendance rather than via a direct pathway.

County-level differences further illuminate how geography and infrastructure condition the relationship between climate shocks and maternal malaria prevention.

In Kisumu, floods predominated (41%), consistent with its lakeshore geography and fragile road networks, whereas Migori experienced more heavy rains and extreme heat but fewer floods. This aligns with our mediation results: shocks were more disruptive to ANC attendance and IPTp₃ + uptake in flood-prone Kisumu than in Migori, where effects were negligible. Adjustment attenuated these effects, likely due to confounding by sociodemographic or geographic factors, yet the pattern is consistent with evidence that Kisumu’s infrastructure vulnerabilities limit access during heavy rains [26]. By contrast, Migori showed no significant associations, possibly reflecting its slightly higher altitude (about 250 m above lake level) and reduced exposure to flooding compared with Kisumu.

These divergent findings underscore the context-specific nature of climate impacts on maternal health services. The extent to which environmental shocks disrupt care depends not only on exposure but also on geography, infrastructure quality, disaster preparedness, and health system flexibility [27]. Evidence from systematic reviews and implementation studies in sub-Saharan Africa indicates that community-based IPTp delivery and ANC outreach can increase IPTp coverage and maintain engagement with antenatal services under routine conditions; however, their effectiveness specifically during acute climate shocks remains underexplored [28, 29]. Moreover, facility-led outreach may itself be constrained by flooding and transport disruption, underscoring the importance of distinguishing between outreach models that rely on mobile health teams and those delivered by community-resident providers. Future research should explicitly test whether community-resident delivery of selected ANC components, including IPTp, offers greater resilience to climate shocks than facility-based delivery or mobile outreach, and assess complementary innovations such as decentralised drug distribution or remote logistics support. In flood-prone settings such as Kisumu, these design considerations are particularly salient for sustaining IPTp coverage amid increasing climatic volatility.

Flooding has long been recognized as a major barrier to maternal health access in Kenya. When floods cut off roads and damage infrastructure, pregnant women face significant challenges reaching facilities [30]. In extreme cases, women on Kenya’s inland lakes have been forced to give birth in boats after being cut off from care [31]. Similar experiences have been reported elsewhere in the region. For example, in Zambia’s Barotse Floodplain, seasonal floods substantially reduced women’s walking access to maternal health services [12]. These findings reinforce the notion that geography and infrastructure interact to amplify the impact of climate shocks on maternal care.

Our results are consistent with broader evidence that adverse climatic events disrupt maternal healthcare utilization through multiple pathways. Studies across sub-Saharan Africa have documented that floods, droughts, and heatwaves reduce service use, leading to incomplete ANC and lower skilled birth attendance [18, 32, 33]. Pathways include damaged transport networks, income loss and competing livelihood needs, displacement, and weakened health systems [8]. For instance, following Cyclone Idai in Mozambique, maternal health visits declined sharply [13], while in Malawi precipitation variability disrupted ANC attendance for an estimated 2.5% of pregnancies annually [14]. In South Asia, recurrent flooding in Bangladesh was linked to significant declines in skilled maternity care use [34, 35]. A recent scoping review further highlighted that climate shocks not only reduce service demand but also strain fragile health systems by destroying facilities and disrupting medical supply chains [15].

From a policy perspective, these findings point to several priorities for malaria-in-pregnancy programming in climate-sensitive settings. First, while our analysis does not directly measure disruptions to facility operations or supply chains, the observed reductions in ANC attendance during climate shocks highlight the vulnerability of access-dependent service delivery models. Strengthening the climate resilience of health infrastructure, including flood-resistant facilities, protected transport corridors, and contingency referral mechanisms, may therefore help sustain physical access to ANC services during extreme weather events [27].

Second, greater flexibility in service delivery platforms may help buffer short-term access disruptions. Complementing facility-based ANC with community-level delivery of IPTp by appropriately trained health worker cadres has been shown to increase coverage under routine conditions in sub-Saharan Africa [28]. However, the extent to which such approaches enhance resilience during climate shocks is likely to depend on implementation design, including whether delivery agents are community-resident, whether IPTp stocks are pre-positioned, and whether outreach relies on mobile teams or local providers [36]. These considerations suggest that community-based strategies may mitigate transient access barriers, but their effectiveness under prolonged or severe climatic disruptions remains an important empirical question.

Finally, integrating climate and health surveillance systems could improve preparedness and response. Linking meteorological forecasts with routine health service data may enable anticipatory planning, such as pre-positioning commodities or targeting outreach in high-risk areas, thereby reducing avoidable interruptions to ANC attendance and IPTp delivery during forecasted floods or heavy rainfall [27].

Strengths and limitations

This is among the first studies to quantify the ANC-mediated effects of climate shocks on IPTp₃ + uptake in a high malaria-transmission African setting, using a robust counterfactual mediation framework and county-disaggregated analysis. The use of survey design-adjusted estimation enhances representativeness.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional survey design precludes strong causal claims beyond the mediation framework’s identifying assumptions, and residual confounding remains possible despite adjustment for key sociodemographic, geographic, and clinical factors. Second, while these covariates were included to control for confounding, the analysis does not explicitly estimate heterogeneity in climate-related vulnerability across population subgroups. Climate shocks are likely to interact with factors such as poverty, remoteness, housing quality, and baseline access to services, producing unequal impacts on ANC attendance and IPTp uptake. Disentangling these differential vulnerabilities was beyond the scope of the present study, but represents an important priority for future research to inform more targeted adaptation strategies.

Third, women who experienced stillbirths were not included because the endline survey eligibility was restricted to women with a live birth in the preceding 24 months. As a result, our estimates apply to pregnancies ending in live births and may not be fully generalisable to all pregnancies. Exclusion of stillbirths could bias estimates if climate shocks, ANC attendance, or IPTp-SP uptake are jointly associated with foetal loss. If extreme weather events increase the risk of stillbirth through reduced ANC access or malaria exposure, our analysis may underestimate the total adverse effect of climate shocks on IPTp-SP uptake and ANC utilisation. However, because ANC attendance and IPTp-SP receipt typically occur earlier in pregnancy, prior to pregnancy outcome, the direction of mediation pathways is unlikely to be reversed. We therefore interpret our findings as conservative estimates for surviving pregnancies.

Finally, the exposure measure, based on self-reported climate shocks, may be subject to recall bias, although its consistency with Kenya Meteorological Department hazard reports supports construct validity. Future research integrating high-resolution climate data, longitudinal service records, and spatial accessibility modelling could more precisely characterise pathways of disruption, identify populations at greatest risk, and evaluate the effectiveness of adaptive service delivery strategies under climate stress.

Conclusions

This study provides quantitative evidence that climate shocks in malaria-endemic western Kenya reduce uptake of IPTp₃ + , with most of the effect operating through disrupted ANC attendance. The mediation framework reveals that ANC continuity is the primary gateway through which environmental disruptions undermine malaria prevention in pregnancy, particularly in flood-prone Kisumu. These results highlight the dual vulnerability of maternal health services to climate variability-physical inaccessibility of care during shocks and the cascading impact on essential interventions delivered through ANC.

The county-level heterogeneity underscores that the magnitude and pathways of disruption are context-specific, shaped by geographical location and local infrastructure. Strengthening the climate resilience of ANC service delivery, through flood-proof facilities, mobile outreach and community-based IPTp distribution, offers a critical opportunity to safeguard maternal and neonatal health in the face of intensifying climate extremes.
