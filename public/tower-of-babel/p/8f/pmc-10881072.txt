DARESOME enables concurrent profiling of multiple DNA modifications with restriction enzymes in single cells and cell-free DNA
Authors: Ramya Viswanathan, Elsie Cheruba, Pui-Mun Wong, Yao Yi, Shaun Ngang, Dawn Qingqing Chong, Yuin-Han Loh, Iain Beehuat Tan, Lih Feng Cheow
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
5-Methylcytosine (5mC) and 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) are the most abundant DNA modifications that have important roles in gene regulation. Detailed studies of these different epigenetic marks aimed at understanding their combined effects and dynamic interconversion are, however, hampered by the inability of current methods to simultaneously measure both modifications, particularly in samples with limited quantities. We present DNA analysis by restriction enzyme for simultaneous detection of multiple epigenomic states (DARESOME), an assay based on modification-sensitive restriction digest and sequential tag ligation that can concurrently perform quantitative profiling of unmodified cytosine, 5mC, and 5hmC in CCGG sites genome-wide. DARESOME reveals the opposing roles of 5mC and 5hmC in gene expression regulation as well as their interconversion during aging in mouse brain. Implementation of DARESOME in single cells demonstrates pronounced 5hmC strand bias that reflects the semiconservative replication of DNA. Last, we showed that DARESOME enables integrative genomic, 5mC, and 5hmC profiling of cell-free DNA that uncovered multiomics cancer signatures in liquid biopsy.

INTRODUCTION

As the most abundant DNA modification, 5-methylcytosines (5mCs) are referred to as the fifth base due to their very important biological roles including regulation of tissue-specific gene expression and retroviral element silencing (1–3). In recent years, other DNA modifications have been discovered and characterized. 5-Hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC), in particular, has received tremendous attention and is widely accepted as the sixth base in the mammalian genome (4–6). As the second most abundant DNA modification after 5mC, 5hmC was initially thought to be a mere by-product of DNA demethylation, but latest studies have established 5hmC as a distinct epigenetic mark with contrasting function to 5mC (7, 8). There is considerable interest in studying the distinct roles of these different DNA modifications and, ultimately, how these different layers of information are combined to fine-tune cellular functions.

The development of analytical technologies to elucidate the biological roles of these modified bases has thus become an intense area of research. It was found that bisulfite sequencing, traditionally used for 5mC measurement, cannot discriminate between 5mC and 5hmC (9, 10). Thus, many previous 5mC studies with bisulfite sequencing may have been confounded by the presence of 5hmC. Recent methods such as oxidative bisulfite sequencing (oxBS-seq) and Tet-assisted bisulfite sequencing (TAB-seq) could indirectly provide separate measurement of 5mC and 5hmC (11, 12). In oxBS-seq 5hmC (but not 5mC) is converted into uracil, while in TAB-seq 5mC (but not 5hmC) is converted into uracil. The relative abundance of 5mC and 5hmC can be inferred from oxBS-seq or TAB-seq with a parallel bisulfite sequencing assay. These assays are challenging because of not only the bioinformatic complexities to obtain information about each epigenetic state but also the potential biases that may result from different degrees of DNA recovery in the separate assays. Last, chemical and enzymatic conversion processes are not 100% efficient, and this may introduce biases to epigenetic state measurements.

An interesting alternative to chemical-based methods is restriction enzyme–based assays for detecting DNA modifications. Methylation-sensitive restriction enzymes have exquisite specificity for particular DNA modification at their cognate target sites and are much more amenable to small sample amounts due to the gentle reaction conditions. Genome-wide 5mC profiling has been achieved using comparative isoschizomer profiling assays such as Hpa II tiny fragment enrichment by ligation-mediated polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (HELP) (13) and digital restriction enzyme analysis of methylation (DREAM) (14) assays. We recently reported on the single-tube restriction enzyme–based DARE assay that allowed genome-wide 5mC profiling at single-cell resolution (15). Meanwhile, hydroxymethylation-sensitive restriction enzymes such as PvuRts I and Aba SI have been used to detect 5hmC even in single cells (16, 17). Multiple enzymes such as β-glucosyltransferase and Msp I have also been used in tandem for detection of 5hmC such as in HELP-GT (18) and reduced representation hydroxymethylation profiling (RRHP) (19). Last, physical methods such as nanopore sequencing have been proposed to distinguish different base modifications (20). There were notable advances in being able to detect 5mC using this approach, but the developmental efforts to detect 5hmC had lagged behind. A method known as SignalAlign (21) used a hidden Markov model to predict 5mC and 5hmC to 79% accuracy, but this has only been shown in synthetic DNA substrates.

Thus, to date, none of the chemical- or restriction enzyme–based approaches are capable of simultaneously measuring 5mC and 5hmC. This is a challenge toward investigating the integrative roles of the fifth and sixth base on cellular epigenetics. Here, we report on a method, DNA analysis by restriction enzyme for simultaneous detection of multiple epigenomic states (DARESOME), that is able to simultaneously detect unmodified cytosine, 5mC, and 5hmC in a single reaction. Founded on the basis on the DARE approach that we previously reported (15), DARESOME can perform comprehensive epigenomic state profiling in CCGG sites genome-wide. Using DARESOME, we demonstrated the correlation of 5hmC/5mC ratio in gene bodies to gene expression, and the distinctive age-related 5mC/5hmC interconversion in mouse brain.

DARESOME is an enabling technology for profiling multiple epigenomic states, particularly when it is infeasible to split limited amount of sample for parallel analysis. We showed that the implementation of DARESOME in single cells (scDARESOME) enabled detection of differential DNA methylation between cells. 5hmC analysis in dividing embryonic stem cells additionally revealed chromosome strand asymmetry in 5hmC abundance among single cells that was masked in bulk analysis.

Last, we showed that application of DARESOME for cell-free DNA (cfDNA) profiling (cfDARESOME) enabled integrated multiomics [C, 5mC, 5hmC, copy number variation (CNV)] detection of cancer signatures. While copy number and 5mC analysis provide information about the genetic aberration and tumor fraction of the underlying cancer, we showed that a global decrease in 5hmC level in cfDNA could be a biomarker for colorectal cancer (CRC). Together, the capability of DARESOME to profile the most abundant six “bases” of the human genome provides unique opportunities to investigate the complex interplay among these epigenetic marks. Integrating these complementary metrics into multiomics data brings a more comprehensive picture of biological regulation that can fuel discovery of biomarkers in diseases.

RESULTS

DARESOME workflow

To date, assays that measure DNA epigenetic changes have relied on specific “tagging” of nucleotides that are modified. In chemical-based (e.g., bisulfite and oxidative bisulfite) conversion methods, this is achieved by selective protection of a modified cytosine (5mC or 5hmC) from conversion into uracil. Regardless of the method variations, only one form of DNA modification can be measured at any one time as there is only one “tag” (cytosine/uracil) available.

Instead of directly converting a nucleotide, DARE method attaches a different DNA barcode to a DNA fragment depending on the methylation state of a particular CpG site in the CCGG context. While only two DNA barcodes were used to distinguish between the methylated and unmethylated states in the original DARE assay, it is conceivable to use three or more DNA barcodes to simultaneously denote multiple DNA modification states in the same assay. This conceptual advance will be referred to as DARESOME to reflect its extended capability compared to the original DARE assay.

Here, we adapted the DARESOME concept to simultaneously detect 5hmC, 5mC, and unmodified cytosine in CCGG sites across the entire genome. This assay makes use of the following modification-sensitive restriction enzyme reactions: (i) Hpa II can only digest unmodified CCGG sites; (ii) Msp I can digest CCGG sites containing unmodified cytosine, 5mC, or 5hmC; and (iii) Msp I cannot digest CCGG sites containing glucosylated 5hmC (5ghmC) in both fully methylated and hemi-methylated states (22). We also designed three adapter sequences, U-tag, M-tag, and H-tag, to perform differential tagging of unmodified cytosine-, 5mC-, and 5hmC-containing CCGG sites. A schematic of the DARESOME assay is shown in Fig. 1A, and the detailed workflow is presented in Materials and Methods. Briefly, unmethylated CCGG sites are first digested with Hpa II and ligated with U-tag adapters. Subsequently, all the remaining CCGG sites are digested with Msp I, followed by ligation of H-tag adapters. Next, the sample is treated with β-glucosyltransferase to glucosylate all the 5hmC bases. Last, the samples are redigested with Msp I and ligated with M-tag, effectively tagging fragments containing 5mC in CCGG (fragments containing 5ghmC in CCGG sites retain the H-tag, as they are protected from the second Msp I digestion). Similar to the DARE assay, genomic DNA is also fragmented with a frequent-cutting enzyme Nla III and ligated to a partial Illumina universal read 2 sequencing adapter (N-tag) to facilitate uniform representation of CCGG-containing fragments across the genome. As a result, this workflow yields short fragments at deterministic locations that can be sequenced to uniquely identify (through the ligated tags) the DNA modification states (unmodified cytosine, 5mC, and 5hmC) of CCGG sites across the genome.

To assess the ability of DARESOME to distinguish the three cytosine modification states, we performed the assay on three sets of control synthetic double-stranded DNA containing unmodified cytosine, 5mC, and 5hmC, respectively (table S1). In a DARESOME experiment, control DNAs with unmodified cytosines were tagged with U-tag, control DNAs with 5mC were tagged with M-tag, and control DNAs with 5hmC were tagged with H-tag (Fig. 1B). We additionally performed DARESOME on 10 separate mixtures of control amplicons with different cytosine modification states and demonstrated accurate quantification of all modification states (fig. S1A). Our results validate the working principle of DARESOME and showed that DARESOME is highly specific and accurate.

Genome-wide profiling of 5mC and 5hmC distribution in mouse brain

Neuronal cells in the brain undergo highly dynamic epigenetic changes, particularly during early development. One of the most exciting properties of the brain, its plasticity, is thought to be highly dependent on epigenetic modifications. Studies have found that apart from the typical role of 5mC in silencing gene expression, stable and elevated 5hmC levels in brain cells are associated with specific gene activation (23, 24). Extensive studies have been performed to separately elucidate the roles of these different DNA modifications in the brain, but these different epigenetic markers have not been studied concurrently in a single assay because of technical challenges.

To demonstrate the utility of DARESOME for comprehensive epigenomic profiling, we performed DARESOME assay on genomic DNA extracted from the cerebellum of adult mice (n = 2) (32 weeks). To evaluate the technical accuracy of DARESOME assay on biological samples, we included several control experiments. First, synthetic control DNAs (see the previous section) were spiked into genomic DNA to assess the specificity of tag ligation. Our results showed that the specificity of synthetic control DNA tagging in spike-in experiment was comparable to the stand-alone experiment (fig. S1B), validating the accuracy of the assay. The reproducibility of DARESOME assay was also excellent, as shown by the high Pearson correlation in 5mC (r = 0.93) and 5hmC (r = 0.92) measurements between two biological replicates (Fig. 1, C and D). DARESOME is built on the foundation of the DARE assay, which we have extensively validated in the previous work. To benchmark the DARESOME assay, we performed parallel DARE and DARESOME assay on the same mouse cerebellum DNA sample. The genomic coverage of DARE and DARESOME is identical, as both assays make use of the same set of restriction enzymes (fig. S1C). Nonetheless, similar to bisulfite sequencing, the “methylated cytosines” in the DARE assay do not distinguish between 5mC and 5hmC. Thus, we separately compared the methylated cytosines in DARE with 5mC or 5mC + 5hmC measurements in DARESOME for the same sample (fig. S2). The high correlation (r = 0.90) of 5mC + 5hmC measurement in DARESOME compared to methylated measurements in DARE confirmed its accuracy (fig. S2B).

It has been reported that different cytosine modifications are preferentially localized at specific genomic regions, suggesting their different roles in gene regulation (24). Using DARESOME, we measured the distribution of each cytosine state within different genomic regions of adult mouse cerebellum sample (Fig. 2A). We observed enrichment of unmodified cytosines in the CpG context in CpG islands and promoters, while 5mC is enriched in introns and repeat regions. This is consistent with current understanding that promoters containing CpG islands are typically unmethylated to facilitate transcription factor binding and gene expression, while repetitive regions are silenced by heavy deposition of 5mC. 5hmC has a unique genomic distribution, being high in gene body and enhancers but almost absent in CpG islands and promoters. We also investigated whether the distribution of different cytosine modifications has any relationship to local CpG density (Fig. 2B). Unmodified cytosines and 5mC displayed opposing trends: Regions with high CpG density are highly enriched with unmodified cytosines, while 5mCs are predominantly localized in regions with low and intermediate CpG density. In contrast, 5hmC displayed a unique trend, being relatively enriched in the intermediate CpG density regions instead. These observations lend credence to the notion that 5hmC is not only an intermediate by-product of DNA demethylation but also a distinctive epigenetic mark with unique distribution and characteristic.

5hmC is well established as an oxidation product of 5mC. To explore the relationship between these epigenetic marks, we sorted genes by their average 5mC levels (10 groups of 0.1 bin interval) and plotted their corresponding 5hmC levels. We observed a clear negative correlation between 5hmC and 5mC levels in gene bodies (Fig. 2C). The replacement of high-affinity 5mC binding sites with low-affinity 5hmC binding sites has been suggested as a mechanism to increase gene expression by removing the repressive effect of MeCP2 occupancy over the transcription unit (25). We found that highly expressed genes in brain cells are associated with high 5hmC/5mC ratio in gene bodies (Fig. 2D). Separately, gene body 5hmC is highly associated with increased gene expression but gene body 5mC is negatively correlated with gene expression (fig. S3). Our results highlight the importance to be able to distinguish 5mC and 5hmC, to reveal their opposing roles in regulating gene expression. While this observation is generally consistent with previous studies reporting on the relationship of gene body 5hmC and 5mC to gene expression (1, 26), DARESOME can establish this in a single assay because of its ability to concurrently measure absolute 5mC and 5hmC levels.

Studies have shown that neuronal cells in the brain undergo dynamic epigenetic changes with aging. To better understand the changes in different DNA modifications with aging, we performed DARESOME assay to compare the epigenetic profile of cerebellum DNA from young (21 days, n = 3) and adult (32 weeks, n = 2) mice. The average ratios of all three epigenetic states (5C, 5mC, and 5hmC) in gene bodies are represented in ternary plots for the young and adult mice samples (Fig. 2E). Both the 5mC and 5hmC measurements showed higher correlation among samples of the same age groups (fig. S4). The most notable global difference is in 5hmC levels (fig. S5, A and D). Gene set overlap analysis showed that the genes with differential 5hmC levels were overrepresented by Gene Ontology (GO) terms relating to synapse and neurons (fig. S5B), suggesting active epigenetic regulation of neuronal development in the brain over this period. On the other hand, there is only a slight corresponding decrease in global unmodified cytosine and 5mC levels (fig. S5, A and D). These trends are consistent at the individual chromosome levels as well (fig. S5C). In contrast, a conventional bisulfite sequencing approach would have erroneously concluded that global DNA methylation in adult mice had increased compared to young mice (bisulfite sequencing measures 5mC + 5hmC levels) when there is a net decrease in 5mC and a net increase in 5hmC (fig. S5D). This highlights the ability to concurrently profile 5mC, and 5hmC is important to reflect accurate epigenetic changes in biological systems. On a genomic region basis (Fig. 2F), we found that the increase of 5hmC with age is most pronounced in distal enhancers where the highest density of 5hmC is found. Conversely, minimal changes in 5hmC are observed in gene promoter regions. Figure 2F also suggests that the primary epigenetic changes in the brain during aging are in the conversion of 5mC to 5hmC, while unmodified cytosine distribution remains relatively unperturbed. This is further corroborated by the negative correlation between 5hmC and 5mC changes during aging, and the absence of correlation between 5hmC and 5C changes during the same interval (fig. S6).

Following earlier observations that gene body 5hmC correlates positively with gene expression, we investigated whether gene expression changes between young and older mice (27) could result from 5hmC changes. We partitioned genes into those that had high gain (>2×), low gain (<2×), and no gain (<1×) of 5hmC during aging and plotted the fold change in gene expression as reported previously (Fig. 2G). Genes that had high gain in gene body 5hmC during aging has significantly higher fold change in gene expression compared to other groups. Together, these results suggest that 5mC to 5hmC conversion could be an important epigenetic mechanism of gene regulation in the brain during aging.

Our results showed that DARESOME can simultaneously interrogate 5mC, 5hmC, and unmodified cytosine. Nevertheless, it should be noted that the analysis is limited to CCGG sites for fragments bound by CCGG and CATG within specific size range [32 to 272 base pairs (bp)]. There are 1,036,265 CCGG sites that satisfy this condition in the mouse genome (mm10), constituting ~10% of all CpG sites. In essence, DARESOME can be considered as a reduced-representation methylation profiling method. The number of CpGs covered by DARESOME is comparable to the well-established reduced-representation bisulfite sequencing (28) that can theoretically profile 1,291,138 CpG sites (standard conditions). Regional methylation information can also be inferred from a subset of CpG methylation state measurements. Using publicly available datasets of TAB-seq and oxBS-seq (GSE97568) (29), we compared the actual regional methylation level from all CpG sites and average regional methylation level from CCGG sites only. 5mC at CpG islands and 5hmC at gene bodies have established functional relevance to gene regulation. We observed that the overall 5mC in CpG islands and 5hmC in gene bodies are well represented by selective measurements at CCGG sites (Pearson correlation > 0.9) (fig. S7, A and B). Meanwhile, overall 5mC and 5hmC levels in 10-kbp genomic windows can also be accurately inferred with measurements at CCGG sites only (Pearson correlation > 0.9) (fig. S7, C and D). These results highlight the utility of DARESOME for investigating epigenetic regulation at functional elements in the genome.

Multimodal analysis of epigenetic heterogeneity in single cells

Studies have shown that epigenetic heterogeneity in single cells could have important biological consequences. To date, however, single-cell epigenetic measurements have only been demonstrated for single DNA modification. Having established the ability of DARESOME to perform comprehensive epigenetic profiling on bulk samples, we next investigated whether DARESOME can be adapted to measure epigenetic differences in single cells. The ability to perform concurrent analysis of multiple DNA modifications in a single assay is particularly pertinent in single-cell analysis as it would not be feasible to split a single cell for parallel analysis.

We successfully adapted the DARESOME protocol for single-cell analysis (scDARESOME) with slight modifications (Materials and Methods). We applied scDARESOME on five H1 embryonic stem cells (H1 ES) and five HepG2 cancer cells. On average, we covered 687,000 unique CCGG sites in single cells. At the single-cell level, distribution of cytosine modifications is noticeably different between single H1 ES cells and HepG2 cells across different genomic regions (Fig. 3A). Single HepG2 cell displayed global hypomethylation and hypohydroxymethylation compared to single H1 ES cells, a trend that is also reflected in bulk DARESOME assay (fig. S8A). The methylation pattern across genes revealed the characteristic pattern of high methylation level in gene bodies coupled with a sharp drop in methylation around the transcription start site (TSS) in single cells (Fig. 3B), demonstrating the ability of scDARESOME to measure epigenetic differences in different gene regions that could correlate with their specific functions. Despite the global depletion of 5mC in HepG2, we could detect both consistently hypomethylated and hypermethylated promoters compared to H1 ES cells with scDARESOME, demonstrating cell type–specific methylation (Fig. 3C). Because of the known roles of promoter methylation in regulating gene expression, we anticipate that scDARESOME would be invaluable for investigating epigenetic basis of cellular phenotypic heterogeneity.

On the other hand, due to the far lower abundance of 5hmC, base-resolution or genic-resolution comparison of 5hmC between single cells is more challenging. Nevertheless, when we investigated 5hmC distribution at a larger spatial scale, we observed highly interesting features that can only be seen at the single-cell level. DARESOME (and scDARESOME) assay could yield strand-specific information of hydroxymethylation modification. If a site was hemihydroxymethylated, only the fragment containing the original 5hmC would be protected from second digestion. While maintenance DNA methyltransferase is responsible for mostly symmetrical 5mC modifications on both strands of the same DNA, no such enzymes have been reported for 5hmC. Previous reports on single-cell 5hmC assay had shown that asymmetric strand distribution of 5hmC could be observed at the single-cell chromosomal level as a result of semiconservative DNA replication during cell division (30). Upon investigation of the strand distribution of 5hmC in dividing H1 ES cells at 10-Mb resolution, we observed that the strand distribution of 5hmC can be highly asymmetric in some chromosomes in single cells (Fig. 3D and fig. S8B). Specifically, we observed the phenomena that strand distribution of 5hmC can be highly skewed in either direction within chromosome of the same cell. The same chromosome between different cells could be skewed in opposite directions. This effect is only observed in a single cell but absent in 5hmC analysis from bulk cells (Fig. 3D).

We investigated whether this 5hmC strand asymmetry phenomenon observed in scDARESOME is consistent with semiconservative DNA replication and random segregation of parental DNA strands within daughter cells. A newly divided diploid cell would have a pair of each autosomal chromosome. Assuming that each chromosome replicates semiconservatively in the parent cell and is randomly distributed among the two daughter cells, each daughter cell is expected to receive either one original positive and one original negative chromosome strand (50%), two original positive chromosome strands (25%), or two original negative chromosome strands (25%). We calculated the chromosome-level 5hmC strand asymmetry in five H1 ES cells and analyzed their distribution (Fig. 3E). The violin plot showed that while most chromosomes showed approximately symmetrical 5hmC strand distribution (~0.5), there is a notable number of chromosomes that displayed either positive (>0.5) or negative (<0.5) biased strand distribution (Fig. 3F). The percentages of chromosomes that have a 5hmC positive-strand bias (>0.55), are nonbiased (0.45 to 0.55), and have negative-strand bias (<0.45) are calculated to be 29%:45%:25%, closely tracking the theoretical expected distribution of 25%:50%:25%. As a control, we reassigned the stranded 5hmC measurements in single cells to random chromosomes and observed no biased distribution, supporting that 5hmC strand bias is linked within chromosome in single cells. Last, 5hmC strand bias is also masked in bulk cell analysis, likely due to the averaging effects of this random inheritance from many cells (Fig. 3, E and F). Our analysis showed that scDARESOME can enable concurrent profiling of 5mC and 5hmC in single cells to reveal important differences and heterogeneity that would be masked in bulk cell analysis.

Multiomics characterization of cfDNA

Last, we investigate the utility of DARESOME for multiomics profiling in cfDNA for noninvasive liquid biopsy. To date, it has been shown that the various genetic and epigenetic features of cfDNA are immensely useful for detection and characterization of diseases such as cancer. Genomic information such as mutations and CNVs in cfDNA can identify circulating tumor DNA and can be useful for understanding the underlying cancer drivers and subtypes as well as tailoring treatment (31–34). 5mC profiling in cfDNA has been widely used for detecting multiple cancers, because of their pervasive disease-specific alterations. Meanwhile, 5hmC profiling in cfDNA is gaining attention because of their reported stage and tissue-specific changes that can be used to further characterize the disease (35, 36). The integration of these measurements is highly valuable as each of these provides complementary information about the underlying disease. However, due to the limited amount of cfDNA that can be collected from patients [~5 to 10 ng/ml (37)], it is desirable that a single assay can provide multiomics information rather than having to split the sample into multiple smaller amounts for separate assay that will inevitably reduce the sensitivity.

There have been limited successes in multiomics profiling of genetic aberration and DNA modifications from cfDNA. The bisulfite conversion process needed for DNA modification profiling leads to considerable DNA damage and fragmentation, in addition to loss of complexity that hinders genetic profiling (e.g., difficulty in identifying DNA mutations as bisulfite conversion introduces base changes, challenges in copy number profiling due to uneven fragmentation, and amplification of bisulfite-converted DNA). Some previous reports have shown that cfDNA profiling with methylation-dependent restriction enzymes can enable 5mC profiling with minimal DNA loss, but these approaches only amplify selective regions (methylated or unmethylated) and are unsuitable for genome-wide profiling of genetic aberrations such as CNV (38, 39). Last, 5hmC profiling in cfDNA is only achievable through 5hmC pull-down methods that lacked single-base resolution and not compatible with concurrent 5mC and genome-wide genetic analysis (40, 41).

Because of the demonstrated ability of DARESOME to perform genome-wide 5mC and 5hmC profiling, it is attractive to consider its utility for cfDNA multiomics profiling. cfDARESOME is an adaptation of the DARESOME protocol for performing multiomics profiling in small quantities of cfDNA (fig. S9A and Materials and Methods). Because cfDNAs are naturally fragmented, Nla III digestion is not necessary. Instead, sequencing adapters are ligated to both ends of the cfDNA fragments, and the sequential restriction enzyme digestion and tag ligation steps are carried out. In the end, only fragments that contain a methylation status tag on one end and sequencing adapter on the other end are sequenced.

Because of the unique library preparation of cfDARESOME, each sequenced fragment is anchored on a CCGG site. Unlike the random cfDNA fragments profiled in conventional cfDNA assays, the deterministic locations of cfDARESOME fragments can improve the ease for determining copy numbers in cfDNA. We reasoned that the copy number of DNA within a region in cfDARESOME would be proportional to the total number of fragments containing methylation status tag divided by the unique number of CCGG sites covered. We estimated the copy number of (500 kb) tiling windows across the genome using this measurement, for cfDNA obtained from healthy subjects (n = 4) and CRC patients (n = 13) profiled with cfDARESOME, as well as matched tumor from three CRC patients (Fig. 4A). We observed that cfDNA from healthy subjects showed a uniform copy number profile as expected. On the other hand, similar analysis on DARESOME assay in CRC primary tumors yielded distinct copy number amplified and copy number deleted regions. The presence of large-scale genomic aberrations in chromosomes 7, 13, and 20 in tumors from subjects 12 and 15 is consistent with frequent copy number amplifications in these regions in CRC (42, 43). On the other hand, notably, less copy number aberrations are detected in primary tumor from subject 14; this could reflect previous observations that the frequency of copy number aberrations is very different between cancer subtypes. We observed largely similar patterns of CNVs in cfDARESOME measurements of the matching cfDNA at 500-kb resolution. Together, cfDARESOME profiling of cfDNA in 13 patients revealed interpatient differences in large-scale genomic aberrations that can be obtained from noninvasive liquid biopsy. Applying this analysis at higher resolution can even enable detection of focal amplifications containing genes important to CRC progression (fig. S9B). To date, copy number profiling of cfDNA with low-pass sequencing has shown to be useful for noninvasive cancer detection, but these methods invariably require extensive normalization with respect to GC content or against a normal sample to achieve the requisite accuracy. Because of its deterministic nature, cfDARESOME provides an alternative strategy that does not require complex normalization for determining copy number alterations in cfDNA.

Our analysis above on CNVs clearly illustrates the profound interpatient heterogeneity in genetic aberrations. The observation that certain tumors showed minimal copy number changes highlights the importance of a multiomics approach for comprehensive detection and characterization of the underlying cancer. Because of their genome-wide variations and tissue/disease specificity, 5mC profiling has been proven to be a very effective method for screening and detection of multiple cancers (35, 36). We confirmed that DNA modification profiling using cfDARESOME is accurate, as spiked-in control DNAs are tagged with high accuracy and specificity (fig. S9C). On a global scale, cfDARESOME showed that cfDNAs from CRC patients generally have lower global methylation compared to healthy subjects (Fig. 4B). This is consistent with a scenario where commonly hypomethylated tumor-derived DNAs are detected in cfDNA. Previous studies have identified specific DNA loci that are differentially methylated between CRC tumors and cfDNA from healthy plasma (44). We extracted the 5mC measurements at these loci in cfDNA (cfDARESOME) and primary tumors (DARESOME) and observed well-distinct profiles between cfDNA from healthy subjects and primary tumors (Fig. 4C), demonstrating that these differentially methylated regions (DMRs) can be observed and profiled in cfDARESOME. cfDNA from CRC patients showed a gradient of intermediate values in these DMRs, likely reflecting different fractions of tumor-derived DNA in each sample (Fig. 4C). To investigate whether quantitative tumor estimation can be performed, we applied our previous tumor fraction estimation algorithm on cfDARESOME measurements and correlated the results with tumor fraction estimated using the orthogonal genomics method (44). Our results showed excellent correlation (Pearson r = 0.912) between tumor fraction estimated from cfDARESOME 5mC measurements and genomics methods (Fig. 4D). We were able to distinguish healthy subjects (n = 4) from CRC patients (n = 11) based on the estimated tumor fractions from cfDARESOME. Hence, we demonstrate that cfDARESOME is a bisulfite-free alternative for accurate cancer detection. The benefits of multiomics cfDARESOME is clearly demonstrated in sample P14 from a CRC patient, where cancer detection in cfDNA via CNV is challenging because of the absence of CNV in the primary tumor (Fig. 4B), but concurrent 5mC profiling revealed an estimated tumor fraction of 55%.

Last, we explored the changes in cfDNA 5hmC in healthy subjects and CRC subjects measured by cfDARESOME. It has been reported that 5hmC abundance is substantially reduced in cancer cells compared to primary tissue, suggesting that loss of 5hmC could be a general feature of carcinogenesis (45). We observed significant reduction in global 5hmC levels in cfDNA from CRC patients (Fig. 4E). The difference in global 5hmC levels allowed clear distinction between CRC patients from healthy subjects in this cohort. While more tests are needed to confirm whether global 5hmC level is a robust biomarker for cancer detection, the fact that very low global 5hmC levels are observed even in cfDNA samples with relatively low tumor fractions is surprising. Some previous studies have hinted toward the changes in blood cell 5hmC levels in patients with solid tumor—a fraction of these could be present as cfDNA due to natural cellular turnover (46). If that were true, cfDNA 5hmC profiling could potentially provide additional insights into patient response to disease.

Several 5hmC profiling studies had demonstrated the presence of cancer-specific 5hmC signatures in cfDNA of cancer patients. Most of these studies use pull-down methods to identify regions that are relatively enriched for 5hmC, also known as differential hydroxymethylated regions (DhMRs). In the same vein, we identified DhMRs between healthy plasma (n = 4) and primary CRC tumors (n = 3) using cfDARESOME data. The 5hmC counts in each gene are normalized by the total 5hmC counts of the respective sample. Next, we identified genes that have significant 5hmC difference (two-tailed t test, P < 0.05) between healthy plasma and CRC tumors. We identified 194 genes that are differentially hydroxymethylated between CRC tumor and healthy plasma (125 hypohydroxymethylated genes and 69 hyperhydroxymethylated genes in tumor).

Next, we plotted the z score of each of these DhMRs across the healthy plasma and the CRC tumors, as well as cfDNA from CRC patients (Fig. 4F). As expected, healthy plasma and CRC tumors displayed clearly distinct 5hmC profiles in these genes. cfDNA from CRC patients showed intermediate 5hmC signatures. When CRC patient samples are arranged from low to high tumor fraction (from 5mC profiling estimates), the 5hmC enrichment profiles gradually change from one that resembles healthy plasma to one that is more similar to bulk tumor. To quantify this observation, we defined the “tumor-specific 5hmC score” as the sum of z score from 69 genes hyperhydroxymethylated in tumors and the “plasma-specific 5hmC score” as the sum of z score from 125 genes hypohydroxymethylated in tumors. A plot of tumor-specific 5hmC score against the estimated tumor fraction showed an excellent linear relationship (Pearson r = 0.93) (Fig. 4G), demonstrating that cfDARESOME can detect 5hmC tumor signatures from cfDNA for quantitative tumor analysis. An equivalent but opposite trend is similarly observed in the plot of plasma-specific 5hmC score against tumor fractions (Pearson r = −0.86) (fig. S9D).

DISCUSSION

Here, we described the development of the DARESOME platform and explored its adaptation to various applications. Concurrent epigenetic state profiling is achieved via a unique workflow that attaches distinct DNA tags to different modified DNA, which allowed them to be identified via next-generation sequencing. We have demonstrated the ability to detect unmodified cytosine, 5mC, and 5hmC in the CCGG context here, but the principles of DARESOME can also be generalized to other epigenetic modifications (e.g., 6 mA) or sequence context depending on the specificities of the restriction enzymes.

The key advantage of DARESOME is its ability to perform concurrent profiling of multiple DNA modification states without requiring separate assays. This approach is expected to improve accuracy, reduce bias, and simplify interpretations when integrating multiple layers of epigenomic information. A particular benefit of DARESOME is the single-tube workflow that minimizes DNA loss and unconverted DNA format that considerably improves mappability compared to bisulfite conversion methods. In a proof-of-concept experiment, we demonstrate that bulk DARESOME can reveal the unique genomic distributions of 5mC and 5hmC in mouse brain. We showed that 5mC and 5hmC deposition have opposite effects on gene regulation, and 5hmC/5mC ratio in gene bodies is highly correlated with gene expression. Last, accumulation of 5hmC with aging was observed in mouse brain. Integrated analysis of multiple DNA modification with DARESOME suggests that active conversion of 5mC into 5hmC is the key process that leads to this phenomenon.

We note that DARESOME yields epigenetic information at the single-site (e.g., CCGG) rather than single-base (every CpG) resolution. Nonetheless, important insights into the epigenetic regulation can be obtained because of the strong spatial correlation of neighboring CpG modification status (47). We have shown here that the methylation in CpG islands and 10-kb genomic windows, as well as hydroxymethylation in gene body and 10-kb genomic windows are accurately represented by the average methylation and hydroxymethylation in the CCGG context, respectively. Thus, DARESOME would find wide use for profiling epigenetic states of functional elements in the genome. Currently, the DARESOME workflow is also rather lengthy, requiring ~60 hours, mainly due to the need for long incubations to ensure that enzymatic reactions proceed to completion. There could be room for further reducing the assay time by using higher concentrations of enzymes. The DARESOME assay is also compatible with automation using robotic liquid handlers for high-throughput experiments.

The compatibility of DARESOME with low-amount samples provides the opportunity for multimodal single-cell analysis. Simultaneous profiling of multiple epigenetic marks is achieved with scDARESOME and could be valuable for many applications. With this method, we demonstrate sensitive measurement of cell type–specific global 5mC variations as well as locus-specific differences at single-cell resolution. Biased strand distribution of 5hmC observed at the chromosome level in single cells provides insights into the epigenetic heterogeneity that arises from the cell division process.

Last, we demonstrate that cfDARESOME can enable multiomics profiling of cfDNA for noninvasive cancer detection. Multiple aspects of cfDNA are typically profiled individually, but an integrative analysis can yield better insights into the underlying disease. Because of its deterministic nature, cfDARESOME enables easy and reproducible copy number estimation without complex normalization. We showed that CNVs of tumors are reflected in cfDNA through the cfDARESOME assay. Second, 5mC profiling of cfDNA has great sensitivity and utility for cancer detection. We showed that cfDARESOME detected methylation signatures of cancer cells in the blood. In conjunction with established bioinformatics protocol, highly accurate tumor fraction calling can be performed. Last, we observed the striking phenomenon of global 5hmC level reduction in cfDNA in cancer patients. Upon further investigation, we also find that 5hmC signatures of cancer cells can be quantitatively detected in cfDNA. This suggests that 5hmC could potentially be used as biomarkers of cancer upon further validation. The combined analysis of copy number, 5mC, and 5hmC profiles in a single assay using cfDARESOME would be a powerful tool for comprehensive cancer surveillance in liquid biopsy applications.

As the whole is greater than the sum of its parts, the ability to simultaneously measure all the six bases in a single assay yields valuable knowledge about the complementary roles and dynamic relationships between different DNA modifications in our epigenome. The DARESOME platform is the only platform that is capable of this function, and we demonstrated that it can be adapted for various impactful applications. We envision that DARESOME would be an essential tool for integrative analysis of the epigenome in the future.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample collections and preparation

Genomic DNA of mouse cerebellum of two adult (32 weeks) and three young [21 days postnatal (P21)] mice was a gift from S. Luo (Institute of Molecular and Cell Biology). It was extracted using a DNeasy blood and tissue kit (QIAGEN). DNA concentrations were quantified using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in Qubit 3.0 (Invitrogen).

For scDARESOME, H1-hESC cells were cultured in mTeSR medium (STEMCELL Technologies). For cell harvesting, cell culture plate was washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) once and added with ReLeSR (STEMCELL Technologies) for 30-s incubation at room temperature. ReLeSR was then aspirated, and the plate was incubated for 5 min in an incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 to ensure single-cell dissociation. Cells were suspended in mTeSR medium and washed with PBS before single-cell assay.

HepG2 cells (American Type Culture Collection, HB-8065) were cultured in low-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (1 g/liter; Gibco), 1% GlutaMAX (Gibco), 1% nonessential amino acids (Gibco), 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco). For the single-cell assay, the cells were stained with CellTrace Calcein Red-Orange AM (Life Technologies) for 10 min and washed with PBS (Gibco). They were diluted to 1 cell/μl concentration and isolated in 0.2-ml PCR tubes.

For cfDARESOME, patients were recruited at the National Cancer Centre Singapore under studies 2018/2795 and 2019/2401 approved by the SingHealth Centralised Institutional Review Board. Blood samples from healthy individuals were collected under study 2012/733/B. Plasma from both healthy volunteers and CRC patients was collected and processed by first separating plasma from whole blood in EDTA tubes within 2 hours of venipuncture via centrifugation at 10 min × 300g and 10 min × 9730g. cfDNA was extracted using the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Retrospective review of medical records was performed to collect clinicopathological details such as patient demographics, tumor staging, serum carcinoembryonic antigen, and mutational status from clinical testing where available.

DARESOME workflow

Detailed reaction mixture is provided in table S2. Most of the reagent mixtures are the same across DARESOME, scDARESOME, and cfDARESOME. For DARESOME, 1 μl of DNA and 2 μl of lysis reaction mixture were added to the tubes. The tubes were then incubated at 42°C for 2 hours, 65°C for 30 min, and 70°C for 15 min. Unmethylated CCGG sites in the genomic DNA were first digested with 2 μl of Hpa II digestion reaction mixture at 37°C for 5 hours and 65°C for 20 min. The digested unmethylated sites were ligated to U-tag adapters at 25°C for 5 hours and 65°C for 20 min with 2 μl of U-tag adapter ligation reaction mixture. To remove the excess adapters, 2 μl of thermolabile USER reaction mixture 2 was added and incubated at 37°C for 20 min, 25°C for 20 min, and 65°C for 20 min. This was followed by digestion of both methylated and hydroxymethylated CCGG sites and CATG sites with 6 μl of Msp I and Nla III digestion reaction mixture at 37°C for 5 hours and 65°C for 20 min. To completely inactivate the Msp I enzyme, 3 μl of thermolabile proteinase K reaction mixture 1 was added to the tubes and incubated at 37°C for 30 min and 55°C for 15 min. Digested DNA was then ligated with 4 μl of H-tag/N-tag adapter ligation reaction mixture at 25°C for 5 hours and 65°C for 20 min. Excess H-tag and N-tag adapters were removed with the addition of 3 μl of thermolabile USER reaction mixture 3 at 37°C for 20 min, 25°C for 20 min, and 65°C for 20 min. The single-strand ligated adapters were extended to form double-stranded fragments at 72°C for 6 min, reforming the CCGG cut site, with 25 μl of extension reaction mixture. The samples were then cleaned up using the Monarch PCR and DNA Cleanup Kit (New England Biolabs) and eluted in 8 μl of water. The hydroxymethylated CCGG sites were then glucosylated with 12 μl of glucosylation reaction mixture at 37°C for 16 hours and 65°C for 20 min. The samples were then cleaned up using the Monarch PCR and DNA Cleanup Kit and eluted in 8 μl of water. The nonglucosylated (methylated) CCGG sites were digested with 3 μl of Msp I digestion reaction mixture at 37°C for 5 hours and 65°C for 20 min. The Msp I enzyme was inactivated using 3 μl of thermolabile proteinase K reaction mixture 2 at 37°C for 30 min and 55°C for 15 min. The digested fragments were then ligated with 7 μl of M-tag adapter ligation reaction mixture at 25°C for 5 hours and 65°C for 20 min. Excess M-tag adapters were removed with the addition of 2 μl of thermolabile USER reaction mixture 3 at 37°C for 20 min, 25°C for 20 min, and 65°C for 20 min. The single-strand unligated adapters were removed with the exonuclease I reaction mixture 2 at 37°C for 30 min. Single-stranded ligated fragments were extended using 17.6 μl of preamplification reaction mixture 2 at 72°C for 6 min, followed by inactivation of exonuclease I enzyme at 85°C for 15 min. We then added 0.4 μl of 50 μM of read 1 primer and N-tag long oligonucleotide and performed 10 cycles of preamplification at 98°C for 15 s, 67°C for 30 s, and finally 72°C for 1 min. The amplified products were cleaned up with 1.6× SPRI Select beads (Beckman Coulter) and eluted in 10 μl of water. Read 1 and read 2 primers were added to the amplicons through quantitative PCR amplification in reamplification reaction mixture at 98°C for 45 s, X cycles of 98°C for 15 s, 67°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 15 s. For this reaction, 1 μl of 10-fold diluted products was used. The number of cycles (X) required was optimized using real-time monitoring. The reamplified library was purified using the Monarch PCR and DNA Cleanup Kit.

Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to quantify the library concentration, and equal concentration of all libraries was pooled. Products between 180 and 420 bp were size-selected with BluePippin 2% agarose cassette (Sage Sciences) and purified using the Monarch PCR and DNA Cleanup Kit. Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit was used to quantify the library concentration. The size distribution of the library was measured using the Bioanalyzer High Sensitivity DNA Analysis Kit (Agilent). This was followed by quantification of the library with the Kapa Library Quantification Kit (Roche) and sequenced on the HiSeq sequencing platform (Illumina).

There are slight variations in the steps and reagent mixture for scDARESOME and cfDARESOME. For scDARESOME, 1× C1 loading reagent (Fluidigm) was added to all the reagent mixtures. All steps before H-tag/N-tag adapter ligation are the same as DARESOME. However, in scDARESOME, we annealed read 2 index primer to N-tag short oligo and ligated it to digested products. This allowed us to pool single cells with cells with barcodes before purification steps. In addition, scDARESOME had an additional exonuclease reaction after H-tag/N-tag adapter ligation and USER reaction step with exonuclease I reaction mixture 1 at 37°C for 30 min. The remaining steps are similar to the DARESOME protocol.

For cfDARESOME, we introduced a few additional steps at the start of the protocol. As the cfDNAs are fragmented in nature, we excluded Nla III digestion. Hence, as the first step, we end-repaired A-tail the cfDNA with 2 μl of the end-repair A-tail reaction mixture at 20°C for 30 min and 65°C for 20 min. This was followed by ligation with cf-tag at 20°C for 2 hours and 65°C for 20 min. Excess adapters were removed with 2 μl of thermolabile USER reaction mixture 1 at 37°C for 20 min, 25°C for 20 min, and 65°C for 20 min. The samples were then cleaned up using the Monarch PCR and DNA Cleanup Kit and eluted in 10 μl of water. Single-stranded ligated fragments were extended using 10 μl of preamplification reaction mixture 2 at 72°C for 6 min, followed by purification using the Monarch PCR and DNA Cleanup Kit, and eluted in 10 μl of water. Ten microliters of phosphatase reaction mixture was then added and incubated at 20°C for 30 min and 65°C for 10 min. The samples were then cleaned up using the Monarch PCR and DNA Cleanup Kit and eluted in 6 μl of water. The remaining steps are similar to DARESOME, with the exclusion of Nla III enzyme and N-tag adapter.

Data processing

To obtain the proportion of different cytosine modifications at each assayed CCGG site, we developed a pipeline consisting of the following main steps: (i) extracting the unique molecular identifier (UMI), (ii) differentiating the different cytosine modifications based on the adapter sequence, (iii) adapter trimming, (iv) alignment to the mouse (mm10) or human (hg38)/GRCh38 genome, (v) removal of PCR duplicates, and (vi) calculating the proportion of different cytosine states at each CCGG site. The quality of the sequenced reads was analyzed using FastQC v0.11.4 (48). UMI-tools v0.5.4 was used to extract the UMI and remove PCR duplicates (49). Adapters were trimmed using Cutadapt v1.5 (50), and alignment to mm10/hg38 reference genome was done using bowtie2 v2.3.4.1 (51). Figure S10A shows a graphical description of the workflow. The code for performing DARESOME data processing is provided in code S1.

To obtain the percentage of each cytosine state at a CCGG site, the number of reads with the specific tag was divided by the total number of reads at that site. To improve confidence in the obtained cytosine state information, each assayed CCGG site was filtered to only include sites covered by more than five unique reads.

Analyzing different genomic regions

Different gene region annotations, such as CpG islands, introns, exons, and repeats, were downloaded from the UCSC table browser. Promoters were defined as the region that is 1 kb downstream and 500 bp upstream of the TSS of each gene. To calculate the mean methylation for different regions, the average methylation of the CCGG sites covered within the region was used.

Mouse cerebellum enhancer coordinates were downloaded from EnhancerAtlas (52). Proximal and distal enhancers were defined according to the proximity to TSS. Any enhancers that overlapped the promoter were defined as proximal enhancers, and those that did not were defined as distal enhancers.

Identifying genes with differential 5hmC

Differential 5hmC was calculated by performing t tests on the average gene body 5hmC level of the replicates of young mouse brain DNA versus replicates of adult mouse brain DNA. Genes with t test P value < 0.05 (Student’s t test, two-tailed, unequal variant) were considered to have differential 5hmC. The top 100 most variable (highest SD) differential 5hmC genes were chosen for GO overrepresentation analysis.

Relationship with CpG density

To investigate the relationship between cytosine modifications and the local CpG density, the number of CpGs present in ±500 bp around each CCGG site was calculated. Less than 6 CpGs were considered low CpG density (corresponding to the 25th percentile), more than 24 CpGs were considered high CpG density (corresponding to the 75th percentile), while the remaining were considered intermediate CpG density.

Integrative analysis of cytosine modifications

The 5hmC/5mC ratio metric was used to combine 5mC and 5hmC information. To calculate the 5hmC/5mC ratio for each gene, the 5hmC and 5mC percentage at each site was divided and averaged across the entire gene. Only genes with more than 10 distinct sites covered were considered. RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data for adult mouse cerebellum were obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (GSM2572262) (29) for correlation analysis between gene expression and gene body epigenetic modification. RNA-seq data for young and adult mouse brain (Sst neurons) were obtained from NCBI GEO (GSE150538) (27) for calculating gene expression change during aging.

Effects of cross-talks on 5hmC estimation

Chemical and enzymatic reactions are rarely 100% efficient. Therefore, methods that rely on these reactions could under- or overestimate certain epigenetic states. From spike-in controls, the efficiency of attaching H-tag onto 5hmC-containing fragment is ~91%. This is comparable to the efficiency of chemical methods (e.g., 87% protection for 5hmC in TAB-seq) (12). As 5hmC levels are typically low, a high number of reads are required to obtain accurate estimates of 5hmC levels. Following approaches widely adopted for 5hmC analysis, we obtained average 5hmC ratios for whole genes or genomic ranges to increase the accuracy. Furthermore, we filtered for CCGG sites with coverage >5 and require ≥10 unique CCGG sites that satisfy this requirement per gene to be considered for analysis. A total of 12,520 genes satisfied this condition in our analysis, and the median number of reads per gene was 405 (minimum, 121).

To determine which gene is significantly hydroxymethylated, we performed a one-sample z test. The null hypothesis is that the gene is not hydroxymethylated and any 5hmC reads arise from cross-talks (1.7% of 5mC and 0.1% of 5C could be read as 5hmC; fig. S1). We calculated the z statistics for each gene, defined by the following equation: z = (p − P)/σ, where p is the observed proportion of 5hmC reads in the gene, P is the 5hmC proportion from the null hypothesis (proportion of 5hmC arising from cross-talk only), and σ is the SD of the sampling distribution. Using an α value of 0.05 for one-tailed test, we reject the null hypothesis if z is >1.645. Our results showed that a vast majority (97.2%) of all genes that we considered were significantly hydroxymethylated (z > 1.645) (fig. S10B). Therefore, DARESOME measurements can provide high-confidence results for making biological inferences about epigenetic regulation.
