Aortic root motion artifacts on non-electrocardiographic-gated chest CT: A comparison of five CT scanner protocols including an ultrafast wide-detector CT system
Authors: Ah Rim Ji, Eun-Ju Kang, Kwang-Min Lee, Hee-Jin Kwon, Sang Hyeon Kim, Jong Wook Lim, Myong Jin Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to compare aortic root motion artifacts across 5 computed tomography (CT) scanner protocols on non-electrocardiographic-gated contrast-enhanced chest CT, with particular attention to a 256-detector-row wide-detector computed tomography (WDCT) scanner using an ultrafast scan mode. This single-center retrospective and prospective observational study included 321 patients (mean age: 63.9 ± 13.2 years; 167 men) who underwent contrast-enhanced chest CT on 5 CT scanners (CT1–5): CT1 (256-detector row); CT2/CT4/CT5 (64-detector row); and CT3 (320-detector row). Two radiologists scored motion-related subjective image quality at 4 aortic root anatomic sites (aortic valve, aortic sinus, coronary artery ostia, and ascending aorta) using a 3-point scale (0–2). In the prospective cohort, heart rate was recorded at scanning, and its association with subjective image quality was evaluated. Objective image quality was assessed using the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and the contrast-to-noise ratio of the ascending and descending aorta. Subjective image-quality scores differed according to anatomic site, with the ascending aorta showing the highest scores (P < .001). The 256-detector-row WDCT system with its routine ultrafast protocol (CT1) demonstrated the highest overall motion-related subjective image-quality score among the 5 CT scanner protocols (CT1–CT5: 1.60 ± 0.45, 1.31 ± 0.53, 0.52 ± 0.44, 0.23 ± 0.32, and 0.17 ± 0.22, respectively; P < .001). In the prospective cohort, heart rate was not significantly associated with subjective image quality (P = .981). Objective image quality (SNR and contrast-to-noise ratio) was highest in CT5 and lowest in CT2, whereas CT1 showed values comparable to those of CT3 and CT4 despite its ultrafast acquisition protocol. Among the 5 CT scanner protocols evaluated, the 256-detector-row WDCT protocol using an ultrafast scan mode achieved the highest motion-related subjective image quality for aortic root evaluation on non-electrocardiographic-gated chest CT without compromising objective image quality.

1. Introduction

Chest computed tomography (CT), widely performed for diverse clinical indications, can detect a broad spectrum of mediastinal and intrathoracic diseases, including pulmonary pathology. It also enables evaluation of major vascular abnormalities including the heart, aorta, and pulmonary arteries. However, because the heart and aorta move continuously even during breath-hold, motion artifacts can occur in the absence of electrocardiographic (ECG)-gating.[1,2] These artifacts may reduce interpretability and can produce pseudo-lesions such as pseudo-dissection or double-contour artifacts.[3,4] When incidental abnormalities are suspected in the aortic root or ascending aorta (AA) on conventional non-ECG-gated chest CT, diagnostic confidence may be reduced, often necessitating additional ECG-gated CT or echocardiography.

Advances in CT technology, including deep learning-based reconstruction and photon-counting detector CT have expanded opportunities to improve spatial resolution, dose efficiency, noise characteristics, and artifact reduction in thoracic and body imaging.[5–8] Nevertheless, motion artifacts around the aortic root remain a practical limitation of routine non-ECG-gated chest CT, in which ECG synchronization or dedicated cardiac motion correction is not routinely applied. The extent of aortic motion artifact varies depending on CT scanner type and acquisition protocol. High-pitch dual-source CT markedly shortens scan time and reduces aortic root motion artifacts, achieving image quality comparable to that of ECG-gated CT angiography.[9–11]

Recent wide-detector computed tomography (WDCT) systems, together with improvements in gantry rotation speed, enable ultrafast scanning with substantially wider detector beam collimation during helical acquisition, thereby shortening examination time.[12] Using an 80-mm beam collimation (128 × 0.625 mm) and a high-pitch of 1.531, chest and abdominal CT can be completed within a fraction of a second, thereby minimizing respiratory and cardiac motion. Accordingly, ultrafast acquisition may substantially reduce aortic root motion artifacts even on non-ECG-gated chest CT.

This study aimed to compare aortic root motion artifacts and image quality across 5 CT scanner protocols for non-ECG-gated chest CT, with particular attention to an ultrafast scan mode of a 256-detector-row WDCT system. We evaluated whether this protocol was associated with improved motion-related subjective image quality of the aortic root while maintaining objective image quality (SNR and contrast-to-noise ratio [CNR]). We also explored the association between heart rate (HR) at the time of scanning and motion-related subjective image quality.

2. Materials and methods

This single-center study comprised a retrospective cohort to compare image quality across scanners and a prospective cohort to assess the association between HR and image quality. This study was approved by the Dong-A University Hospital Institutional Review Board (IRB No. DAUHIRB-23-216), and informed consent was waived.

2.1. Patients

We included contrast-enhanced chest CT examinations performed for various clinical indications on 5 different CT scanners (e.g., different vendors, number of detector rows, and rotation times) using manufacturer-recommended routine clinical protocols. For the retrospective cohort, consecutive eligible contrast-enhanced chest CT examinations were selected from each scanner over a predefined 10-day period (March 22, 2023–March 31, 2023), with approximately 3 to 4 examinations selected per day until 30 examinations per scanner were included (total, n = 150). This balanced sampling strategy was used to allow scanner-group comparison rather than to estimate the real-world prevalence of aortic root motion artifacts. Examinations with markedly degraded image quality due to substantial respiratory motion or metal artifacts were excluded because these noncardiac artifacts could interfere with aortic root motion assessment. To evaluate the association between HR and image quality, we additionally enrolled a prospective cohort. Sample size estimation for the prospective cohort was based on the study by Takayanagi et al.[13] in which the image-quality score was 100 (standard deviation [SD], 27) for the latest CT and 86 (SD, 21) for conventional CT. This corresponded to an effect size of 0.8; with a type I error of 0.05 and 80% power, a minimum of 26 patients per group was required. Assuming a 10% dropout rate, at least 29 patients per group were required (total, >145 across 5 groups). Prospectively, over 4 days (January 22–25, 2024), HR was recorded at the time of scanning for 35 patients per CT scanner. Examinations with substantial respiratory motion or metal artifacts were excluded (n = 4). The final prospective cohort included 171 patients (CT1; n = 34, CT2; n = 35, CT3; n = 34, CT4; n = 34, CT5; n = 34). Finally, 321 patients were included in this study (Fig. 1).

2.2. CT image acquisition and image reconstruction

CT examinations were performed on 5 different CT scanners using the routine chest CT protocols recommended by each vendor (Table 1). All examinations were performed from the thoracic inlet to the upper abdomen, regardless of scanner type. The 5 scanners included a 256-detector-row GE Revolution Apex scanner (CT1), a 64-detector-row GE Revolution Maxima scanner (CT2), a 320-detector-row Canon Aquilion ONE scanner (CT3), a 64-detector-row Siemens SOMATOM Definition Edge scanner (CT4), and a 64-detector-row Siemens SOMATOM Definition AS + scanner (CT5). For contrast-enhanced chest CT, nonionic contrast material (Xenetix 350 [Iobitridol], 350 mg I/mL, solution for injection, Guerbet) was administered intravenously at 2.5 mL/s, followed by a 25 mL infusion of saline flush at 2 mL/s. Images were reconstructed using the routine iterative reconstruction algorithm available on each scanner at a slice thickness of 2 mm. Images were reviewed using mediastinal window settings (window width, 430 Hounsfield units [HU]; window level, 50 HU), with adjustments permitted when necessary.

2.3. Subjective image quality

Contrast-enhanced chest CT images from all 321 patients were reviewed, and 2 radiologists scored motion-related subjective image quality at 4 anatomic sites (aortic valve, aortic sinus, coronary artery ostia, and AA).

Subjective image quality was graded using a 3-point scale.[14] (Fig. 2A): 0 point (poor) for structures that appeared to have double contours or could not be distinguished at all; 1 point (intermediate) for slightly blurred margins but the contour could be identified; and 2 points (good) for clearly distinguished margins with good image quality. Concerning the coronary artery ostia, only 1 point was scored if either the right or left coronary artery ostium was clearly visible, and 2 points were scored if both ostia were clearly distinguished. Two radiologists initially scored the examinations independently. Inter-observer agreement was calculated using the initial independent scores before consensus resolution. Discrepant cases were subsequently resolved by consensus discussion to determine the final qualitative score. To assess intra-observer agreement, one radiologist repeated the measurements after an interval of >1 month.

2.4. Objective image quality

Objective image quality was assessed using the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and the CNR).[15] The SNR and CNR were calculated for the AA and descending aorta (DA). Attenuation was measured in the AA, DA, and the paraspinal muscle at the level of the right main pulmonary artery (Fig. 2B). Regions of interest (ROI) were drawn as large as possible while avoiding atheroma, calcification, and obvious motion artifacts. In paraspinal muscle, attenuation varies greatly during measurement; therefore, the average value was used by measuring several times. CT attenuation was measured in HU using ROI, and image noise was defined as the SD of attenuation within the ROI. The SNR and CNR were calculated as follows:

SNR = HUaorta/SDaorta

SNR = HUaorta/SDaorta

CNR = (HUaorta–HUmuscle)/SDaorta

CNR = (HUaorta–HUmuscle)/SDaorta

The SNR and CNR were calculated for the AA and DA for each examination.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were compared among scanner-groups using the chi-square test for categorical variables and one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, as appropriate. Subjective image quality scores were analyzed as ordinal variables using the Kruskal-Wallis test, with Bonferroni-corrected post hoc pairwise comparisons. Cliff’s delta was calculated for pairwise comparisons of CT1 with each of the other scanners. A multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed after dichotomizing subjective image quality into poor image quality (score 0) and interpretable/good image quality (scores 1 or 2). Scanner-group was included using CT5 as the reference group, and the model was adjusted for relevant patient characteristics, including age, sex, and body mass index (BMI). In the prospective cohort, associations between HR and subjective image quality were evaluated using overall and scanner-specific analyses according to predefined HR categories. HR categories were defined as <75, 75–<85, and ≥85 beats/min. Objective image quality (SNR and CNR) was analyzed using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate, with Bonferroni-corrected post hoc comparisons. Inter- and intra-observer agreement were assessed using intra-class correlation coefficients (ICCs). Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk). A two-sided P-value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

4. Discussion

Our study showed that the ultrafast 256-detector-row WDCT protocol provided the highest motion-related subjective image quality for aortic root evaluation among the 5 routine CT scanner protocols evaluated. Although CT5 showed the highest objective image quality, CT1 maintained objective image quality comparable to that of conventional scanning protocols despite its ultrafast acquisition protocol. These findings suggest that ultrafast acquisition may reduce motion-related image degradation while preserving objective image quality, thereby improving visualization of the aortic root on routine chest CT.

The aortic root is particularly susceptible to motion artifact and has traditionally required ECG-gated acquisition for reliable visualization.[16] Such artifacts can obscure or mimic aortic pathology, potentially leading to diagnostic uncertainty and unnecessary follow-up imaging.[1] Recent advances in CT technology have improved temporal resolution and scan speed, enabling clinically useful visualization of the aortic root even on non-ECG-gated scans.[9–11,14] No prior study has systematically evaluated aortic root motion artifacts using the ultrafast helical protocol of a 256-detector-row WDCT system in routine non-ECG-gated chest CT. This protocol uses an 80-mm beam collimation, a high-pitch (1.531), and a 0.28-s rotation time, enabling substantially faster helical acquisition. These features may contribute to reduced aortic root motion artifacts even without ECG synchronization.

Several studies report a typical resting adult HR of approximately 60 to 90 bpm.[17–19] At this rate, cardiac motion occurs approximately 1 to 1.5 times per second; therefore, shorter acquisition times are less susceptible to motion. High-pitch acquisition and wide-detector coverage allow rapid volumetric sampling within a short time interval. Thus, the ultrafast scan may help reduce cardiac pulsation artifacts. Although the ultrafast protocol may improve visualization of the aortic root by reducing motion artifacts on routine chest CT, ECG-gated CT angiography remains the reference standard for detailed valvular and coronary assessment.

Subjective and objective image quality represent different aspects of CT image quality. Whereas the subjective score reflects motion-related visualization of the aortic root, SNR and CNR primarily reflect noise and contrast performance. The ultrafast scan mode is primarily designed to shorten acquisition time and thereby reduce motion artifacts. Although shorter acquisition could theoretically reduce photon statistics and may lower SNR or CNR, these objective metrics are also influenced by radiation dose, tube voltage, contrast enhancement, patient body habitus, and reconstruction methods.[20]

In the present study, CT1 achieved the highest subjective motion-related image quality while maintaining objective image quality comparable to that of conventional scanning protocols despite its ultrafast acquisition protocol. In contrast, CT5 showed the highest SNR and CNR despite the lowest subjective image-quality scores, indicating that favorable noise and contrast performance does not necessarily translate into reduced motion artifacts at the aortic root. CT2 showed the lowest SNR and CNR despite a relatively high radiation dose, suggesting that objective image quality is influenced by multiple technical factors rather than radiation dose alone.

The benefit of CT1 was not uniform across anatomical sites. For the aortic sinus, coronary artery ostia, and AA on CT1, examinations scoring 0 points were rare (<5 patients), suggesting that evaluation of these sites may be feasible even without ECG-gating on routine chest CT. In contrast, although aortic valve image quality on CT1 was higher than that on the other scanners, a substantial proportion of examinations still scored 0 points, indicating that aortic valve visualization on non-ECG-gated chest CT remains limited.

Cardiac motion artifacts are caused by cardiac pulsation, making it reasonable to hypothesize that higher HR may increase motion artifacts. However, one study reported that motion artifacts were greatest in the 56 to 65 bpm group, which was the second-lowest HR category.[21] Another study reported no significant association between HR and image quality, although HR variability was associated with image quality.[22] In our prospective cohort, HR was not significantly associated with subjective image quality in the overall, scanner-specific, or HR-stratified analyses. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because subgroup sample sizes were limited and information on beat-to-beat HR variability or arrhythmia was unavailable; therefore, the HR analysis should be considered exploratory.

Current guidelines recommend ECG-gated CT angiography for precise assessment of the aortic root and AA because ECG-gating reduce pulsation artifacts and improve measurement reliability.[23] However, advances in high-end CT, including high-pitch dual-source acquisitions and wide-coverage scanners, increasingly enable clinically useful evaluations of the aortic root, even without external ECG-gating. Recent “ECG-less” coronary CT angiography examinations using virtual- or data-driven gating at reconstruction indicate that contemporary systems can effectively control cardiac motion without external leads while maintaining diagnostic image quality.[24] In addition, multicenter studies have shown that non-ECG-gated low-dose chest CT provides coronary artery calcium scoring comparable to ECG-gated reference scans, implying that modern high-end CT systems and reconstruction methods can adequately control cardiac motion in routine practice.[25,26] Taken together with our findings, these observations suggest that advances in CT technology may permit clinically useful evaluation of the aortic root during routine non-ECG-gated chest CT, although dedicated ECG-gated CT remains the reference standard when detailed assessment is required.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-institution study and the comparison included a limited number of scanner models from 3 vendors, which may restrict generalizability. In addition, although consecutive eligible examinations were selected across a predefined 10-day period to provide balanced scanner groups, the retrospective sampling strategy, scanner scheduling patterns, clinical indications, and exclusion of examinations with substantial respiratory motion or metal artifacts may have introduced selection bias or differences in case mix across scanner groups. Second, scanner vendor, detector configuration, acquisition parameters, tube voltage, radiation dose, and reconstruction methods differed simultaneously because each scanner was evaluated using its manufacturer-recommended routine clinical protocol. Therefore, the observed differences should be interpreted as differences among routine scanner-protocol combinations rather than as the isolated effect of scanner hardware, ultrafast acquisition, or radiation dose alone. Because protocol-related parameters were closely linked to scanner type, their independent effects could not be reliably separated in the present clinical dataset. Third, this study focused on image quality rather than diagnostic accuracy or clinical outcomes. We did not evaluate downstream imaging utilization, such as subsequent ECG-gated CT angiography or echocardiography, or diagnostic performance for specific findings such as aortic root pathology, valve pathology, aortic dilatation, dissection, or coronary origin abnormalities. Because most participants did not have aortic root pathology, correlation with reference standards could not be assessed. Further studies including patients with confirmed aortic root pathology are needed to determine whether improved image quality translates into improved diagnostic performance. Fourth, subjective image quality assessment is inherently reader-dependent, and the 3-point ordinal scale may be subject to ceiling effects or limited sensitivity for subtle differences in motion artifacts. Nonetheless, clear and standardized scoring criteria were established, and inter-observer agreement was excellent, supporting the reliability of the qualitative assessment. Finally, the HR analysis should be considered exploratory because HR data were available only in the prospective cohort, subgroup sample sizes were limited, and information on beat-to-beat HR variability or arrhythmia was unavailable. Accordingly, the absence of a significant HR association should not be interpreted as evidence that motion artifacts are independent of HR.

5. Conclusions

Among the 5 routine CT scanner protocols evaluated, the ultrafast 256-detector-row WDCT protocol achieved the highest motion-related subjective image quality for aortic root evaluation on non-ECG-gated chest CT while maintaining objective image quality comparable to that of conventional scanning protocols. Although it does not replace dedicated ECG-gated CT for detailed evaluation of the aortic root, coronary arteries, or cardiac valves, it may improve visualization of the aortic root on routine chest CT.
