Ophthalmic self-medication among adult ophthalmic patients attending a tertiary eye care centre in Northwest Ethiopia: a mixed-methods study assessing prevalence, associated factors and lived experience
Authors: Edom Netsanet Wondemagegnehu, Elshaday Getaneh Mengesha, Biruk Lelisa Eticha, Getenet Shumet Birhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo determine the prevalence and associated factors of ophthalmic self-medication (OSM) and explore the lived experiences of adult patients with ophthalmic problems attending the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital Tertiary Eye Care and Training Center, Northwest Ethiopia.DesignAn institution-based mixed-methods study comprising a cross-sectional quantitative component and a phenomenological qualitative component.SettingThis study was conducted at the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital Tertiary Eye Care and Training Center, Northwest Ethiopia, between 28 May and 6 August 2025.ParticipantsFor the quantitative component, 463 adult patients with ophthalmic problems were included via systematic random sampling. Data were collected using a semi-structured questionnaire and analysed with SPSS V.27. Multivariable logistic regression identified factors associated with OSM. For the qualitative component, eight participants with a history of OSM were purposively selected for in-depth interviews, and data were thematically analysed using OpenCode V.4.0.Outcome measuresPrevalence of OSM, factors associated with OSM and themes from the qualitative analysis.ResultsThe prevalence of OSM was 43.4% (95% CI 38.8 to 48.1). Significant predictors included long travel distance to health facilities (adjusted OR (AOR) = 4.49; 95% CI 2.48 to 8.16), drug availability at home (AOR=3.33; 95% CI 1.97 to 5.62), chronic systemic illness (AOR=2.96; 95% CI 1.51 to 5.80), poor knowledge about risks (AOR=3.19; 95% CI 1.95 to 5.21) and lack of health insurance (AOR=2.29; 95% CI 1.25 to 4.20). Older age was a protective factor (AOR=0.22; 95% CI 0.07 to 0.71). Qualitative findings revealed that accessibility, perceived knowledge, socio-cultural influences, symptom severity and health system barriers were key reasons for OSM.ConclusionOSM is common in Northwest Ethiopia, driven by both individual and systemic factors. Interventions should focus on awareness creation, expanding insurance coverage, regulating over-the-counter sales and improving access to eye care services.

Introduction

Self-medication, defined as the selection and use of medicines by individuals to treat self-recognised illnesses without professional supervision,1 is a common global practice. In ophthalmology, this behaviour, known as ophthalmic self-medication (OSM), is often triggered by symptoms such as ocular redness, itching, foreign body sensation and tearing.24 Although OSM may provide temporary relief for minor conditions, it carries significant risks, such as delayed diagnosis, inappropriate treatment, disease progression, steroid-induced glaucoma and cataract, antimicrobial resistance and irreversible visual impairment.47

A wide range of substances are used for OSM, including over-the-counter antibiotic eye drops or ointments,8 9 antihistamines and antiallergic agents,2 topical corticosteroids despite their well-documented risks,4 9 artificial tears2 and traditional remedies such as herbal preparations and rosewater, particularly in rural and low-income settings.8 10 Of particular concern is the unsupervised use of topical steroids, which has been strongly associated with serious adverse outcomes, including glaucoma, cataract formation and blindness.4 11 Despite these risks, ophthalmic medications remain readily accessible without prescription in many low-income and middle-income countries, where approximately 80% of medications are obtained without professional oversight.12

Globally, the prevalence of OSM varies widely, ranging from 14.9% to 73.6%.310 1214 Higher prevalence has been reported in several African and Asian settings,2 13 15 whereas lower rates have been observed in other regions.141618 Evidence shows that many individuals practising OSM have limited knowledge of medication names, indications and potential adverse effects. For instance, more than half of respondents in Saudi Arabia were unaware of the risks associated with OSM,19 and a substantial proportion of participants in Ethiopia, Eritrea and Saudi Arabia were unable to correctly identify the medications they used.3 10 19

OSM practice has been associated with multiple factors, including age,3 20 21 sex,12 22 marital status and rural residence,12 low income, lack of health insurance,3 limited education,10 23 previous ocular illness, availability of leftover medications at home, long travel distance to eye care facilities and poor knowledge or permissive attitudes toward self-medication.3 8 10 14 Reported reasons for OSM include advice from pharmacists or drug vendors,12 17 previous experience with similar symptoms,2 4 easy access to ophthalmic drugs, time constraints and recommendations from friends or family.14 19

Despite its significant clinical and public health implications, evidence on the prevalence and associated factors of OSM in Ethiopia remains limited, particularly among patients attending tertiary eye care centres.3 These centres often manage patients with advanced disease who may have previously practised OSM, making this population especially relevant. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of OSM, identify associated factors and explore the lived experiences of adult ophthalmic patients attending a tertiary eye care and training centre. The findings are expected to inform targeted interventions, guide policy decisions and promote safer ophthalmic medication practices, ultimately reducing preventable ocular morbidity and vision loss.

Methods

Study design and setting

An institution-based concurrent mixed-methods study was conducted. A cross-sectional study design was used to assess the prevalence of OSM and its associated factors, while a phenomenological qualitative approach was employed to explore patients lived experiences of OSM practices. This approach seeks to understand and describe individuals’ lived experiences of a phenomenon and the meanings they attribute to those experiences from their subjective perspectives. The qualitative inquiry was guided by an interpretivist paradigm, which assumes that individuals construct meanings based on their personal experiences and social contexts.2426 This paradigm was considered appropriate because it facilitated an in-depth exploration of participants’ perceptions, motivations and lived experiences related to OSM practices from their own perspectives.

This study was conducted at the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital Tertiary Eye Care and Training Center (UoGCSH-TECTC) from 28 May to 6 August 2025. The centre is located in Gondar City, 745 km northwest of Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. According to the information and documentation centre, the eye care centre provides care for more than 32 000 patients annually, offering a range of services including comprehensive eye examinations, automated diagnostic procedures, medical and surgical therapies, and optical dispensing through both conventional and outreach programmes.27 The centre comprises the Departments of Optometry and Ophthalmology, staffed by ophthalmologists, optometrists, ophthalmic nurses and clinical nurses, and serves as a research and training institution for eye care professionals.

Source and study population

The source population comprised all adult patients receiving ophthalmic care at the UoGCSH-TECTC, while the study population included those who attended during the data collection period.

Sampling procedures

For the quantitative component, a systematic random sampling technique was employed to select study participants. Based on service statistics from the previous 2 years, an average of 2600 adult patients (new and follow-up) receive ophthalmic care monthly across the clinics: adult outpatient department (OPD, n=600), anterior segment clinic (n=740), glaucoma clinic (n=764), retina clinic (n=350) and oculoplasty clinic (n=146). Proportional allocation was applied across the five major ophthalmic clinics to ensure representativeness, resulting in adult OPD (n=115), anterior segment clinic (n=142), glaucoma clinic (n=146), retina clinic (n=67) and oculoplasty clinic (n=28). The sampling interval (K) was calculated as 2600/498 ≈ 5. Accordingly, every fifth patient was selected after randomly choosing the first participant using a lottery method from the first five patients, followed by every Kth interval thereafter.

For the qualitative component, participants were selected using purposive sampling from the larger study population during the concurrent data collection period. Individuals were intentionally chosen based on their experience with OSM and their ability to provide rich, relevant information related to the study objectives. In addition, efforts were made to ensure variation in key characteristics such as age and sex to capture a range of perspectives. Eligible participants were approached after completion of the quantitative survey and invited to take part in in-depth interviews until data saturation was reached. The final number of interviews conducted was determined by thematic saturation, where no new themes emerged from additional interviews.

Eligibility criteria

All adult patients receiving ophthalmic care at the UoGCSH-TECTC during the data collection period were eligible for inclusion. Patients unable to provide responses due to ophthalmic emergencies or communication difficulties (hearing or speech impairment) were excluded.

This includes the use of medicines obtained without prescription, leftover drugs or shared medications. OSM was measured using a single yes/no question: “have you ever practised self-medication with eye medications in the past 1 year?” Participants who responded ‘yes’ were considered to have practised OSM.14

Data collection tools and procedures

Quantitative data were collected using a pre-tested, semi-structured questionnaire translated into the local language (Amharic), comprising five main sections (online supplemental file 1). The questionnaire was administered by four trained optometrists to assess various aspects of OSM among the participants.

Section A assessed participants’ socio-demographic and socio-economic characteristics.

The knowledge and attitude items were adapted from previously published studies on OSM and related eye health practices.3 14 The questionnaire was reviewed by the research team and pre-tested before data collection to assess clarity, comprehensibility and applicability to the study setting. Reliability of the knowledge and attitude measures was assessed during the pre-test phase using internal consistency analysis. The knowledge scale demonstrated acceptable reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.78, while the attitude scale showed good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.82. However, formal content validity assessment by an expert panel was not conducted, and the measures were not fully validated standardised instruments. Section B evaluated participants’ knowledge and awareness of OSM including its potential risks and benefits. Section C explored participants’ attitudes and perceptions toward OSM. Section D assessed OSM practices and experiences including the frequency and duration of use, medications used, conditions treated, sources of medications and information, dosage modifications and outcomes of the most recent self-treatment episode. OSM practice was assessed by asking participants, “Have you ever practised self-medication with eye medications?” with response options of ‘yes’ or ‘no’. Participants who responded ‘yes’ were classified as having practised OSM. The questionnaire was adapted from previously published studies14 and modified to suit the local context. Section E comprised 15 asset-based questions used to assess household wealth status using the Equity Tool.32

For the qualitative component, in-depth interviews were conducted by the principal investigator using a semi-structured interview guide (online supplemental file 2). The principal investigator was a trained male clinical optometrist and university lecturer with academic and clinical experience in ophthalmic care and eye health research. He also had a professional interest in OSM, the topic under investigation. Reflexivity was maintained throughout the study by acknowledging how the researcher’s professional background and prior knowledge of OSM could influence data collection and interpretation. To minimise potential bias, the interviewer received additional training in qualitative interviewing techniques prior to data collection.

No prior relationship existed between the interviewer and participants. Interviews were conducted using open-ended questions without leading prompts to encourage participants to express their views freely. Interview transcripts were read repeatedly to achieve familiarity with the data. Throughout data collection and analysis, regular reflection was undertaken to ensure that interpretations remained grounded in participants’ experiences and narratives.

Participants were approached face to face at UoGCSH-TECTC and invited to participate in the study. Interviews were conducted in a private room within the facility to ensure privacy and minimise interruptions. The interview guide explored participants’ motivations for practising OSM, their experiences with self-medication and the challenges that influenced their decision to use eye medications without consulting an eye care practitioner. Apart from the general purpose of the study explained during the informed consent process, participants were not informed of the researcher’s personal interests or motivations.

All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim in Amharic and translated into English for analysis. Field notes were taken during and after the interviews to capture contextual information and key observations. Interviews lasted approximately 20–40 min and continued until data saturation was achieved, defined as the point at which no new themes or insights emerged from subsequent interviews. No one other than the participant and interviewer was present during the interviews, and no repeat interviews were conducted. Participants did not review the interview transcripts after transcription.

Data processing and analysis

Quantitative data were entered into Kobo Collect version 2021.2.4 and exported to IBM SPSS Statistics V.27 for cleaning and analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and cross-tabulations, were used to summarise the data.

The association between OSM and explanatory variables was assessed using binary logistic regression analysis. Variables with a p<0.20 in the bivariable analysis were entered into a multivariable binary logistic regression model. Adjusted ORs (AORs) with 95% CIs were calculated to identify factors independently associated with OSM. Statistical significance was declared at a p<0.05. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, which indicated an adequate model fit.

Socio-economic status was assessed using the Equity Tool, adapted from the Demographic and Health Survey wealth index approach. Fifteen household asset variables, together with place of residence, were included in the analysis. Asset ownership was coded as binary variables (0=not owned, 1=owned), and all variables were standardised prior to analysis. Sampling adequacy was evaluated using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure, whereas Bartlett’s test of sphericity was used to assess the suitability of the data for factor analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed, and the first principal component, which explained the largest proportion of variance, was retained to construct the wealth index. Factor scores derived from this component were saved as a continuous wealth index variable and subsequently ranked into quintiles using the Rank Cases procedure in SPSS. Households were categorised into five wealth groups: poorest, poorer, middle, richer and richest. Place of residence (urban/rural) was included in the PCA to enable the construction of a unified wealth index applicable to the entire study population, consistent with the Equity Tool methodology.

For the qualitative component, audio-recorded interviews conducted in Amharic were transcribed verbatim and translated into English for analysis. The translated transcripts were imported into OpenCode version 4.03. Data were analysed using thematic analysis following a phenomenological approach. Initial codes were generated from meaningful segments of text and refined through an iterative process. Similar codes were grouped into categories, from which subthemes and overarching themes were developed. The principal investigator conducted all coding and initial analysis. Emerging codes and themes were reviewed with co-authors to enhance consistency and credibility. Quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated at the interpretation stage using a convergent mixed-methods design, allowing comparison and triangulation of statistical findings with participants’ lived experiences. The quantitative component was reported in accordance with the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist, and the qualitative component followed the COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative (COREQ) reporting guidelines.

Data quality control

The questionnaire was pre-tested on 5% of the sample at Tibebe Ghion Comprehensive Specialised Hospital. Translation and back-translation were performed to ensure linguistic consistency. Data collectors received 1 day of training, and completed questionnaires were reviewed daily for completeness and accuracy. As a result, there were no missing data, as all variables of interest were fully recorded for all participants. For the qualitative component, verbatim transcription and translation were conducted on the same day of each interview. Iterative data collection and concurrent analysis were undertaken to enhance the credibility of the findings.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Gondar, College of Medicine and Health Sciences, Ethical Review Board (Reference number: SOM 425/2025). Oral informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to data collection. Participants were informed of their voluntary participation and their right to refuse or withdraw from the study at any time. Data were collected anonymously and stored using password-protected systems to ensure confidentiality. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Oral informed consent was approved by the ethical review board due to the low literacy level of participants and the minimal-risk nature of the study.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Health service and access-related characteristics of study participants

Nearly two-thirds of participants (n=293, 63.3%) had valid health insurance. More than half (n=254, 54.9%), travelled >10 km to access eye care services. 63 (15.7%) reported having eye medication (bottles or tubes) at home. Among participants with comorbid conditions, 26 (13%) had diabetes mellitus. Regarding knowledge of the hazards of self-medication, 227 (49%) had good knowledge.

Ophthalmic self-medication among patients

The prevalence of OSM among adult patients with ophthalmic problems was 201 (43.4%) (95% CI 38.8, 48.1). Among those who practised OSM, 62.1% did not check medication expiration dates, and more than half (59.2%) were unaware of the names of the drugs they used. Regarding outcomes, the majority (76.6%) reported symptom improvement, whereas 19.9% reported no change, and 3.5% reported worsening of their condition. The most common triggering symptoms were itching and redness (table 4). The most frequently used medications were antibiotics (15.4%), artificial tears (10%), topical steroids (8.4%) and antibiotic–steroid combinations (7%).

Factors associated with ophthalmic self-medication

In bivariable logistic regression analysis, variables including travel distance to health facility, sex, residence, educational status, religion, occupation, availability of eye medication at home, past 2-year ocular history, chronic systemic disease, knowledge level, valid health insurance status, age group, marital status and attitude were identified as candidates for multivariable analysis at p<0.2.

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, travel distance to the eye care centre, availability of eye medication at home, chronic systemic disease, knowledge level and health insurance status were independently associated with OSM.

Participants who lived far from eye care centres were about 4.5 times more likely to practise OSM compared with those living nearby (AOR=4.49; 95% CI 2.48 to 8.16). Availability of eye medication at home increased the odds of OSM by more than threefold (AOR=3.33; 95% CI 1.97 to 5.62). Having a chronic systemic disease was associated with nearly threefold higher odds of OSM (AOR=2.96; 95% CI 1.51 to 5.80). Poor knowledge was also significantly associated with higher odds of OSM (AOR=3.19; 95% CI 1.95 to 5.21). Participants without valid health insurance were more than twice as likely to self-medicate (AOR=2.29; 95% CI 1.25 to 4.20). Conversely, participants aged >61 years were less likely to practise OSM compared with those aged 18–28 years (AOR=0.22; 95% CI 0.07 to 0.71) (table 5).

Lived experience of study participants regarding self-medication

A total of eight participants were included in the qualitative interviews. Their ages ranged from 18 to 63 years, with five (62.5%) males and three (37.5%) females. Most participants (75.0%, n=6) were urban residents, while the remaining two (25.0%) resided in rural areas. The sample included individuals from diverse occupational backgrounds, including a taxi driver, teacher, nurse, merchant, housewife, student, daily labourer and farmer. Overall, the demographic characteristics of the interview participants were comparable to those of the main study sample. All eligible individuals who were approached agreed to participate, and no withdrawals occurred during the study. To provide a comprehensive understanding of the experience and reasons motivating OSM among adult patients with ophthalmic problems attending UoGCSH-TECTC, the quantitative frequencies offer empirical breadth on prevalent reasons, while qualitative themes provide interpretive depth, elucidating the contextual and interrelated dynamics shaping OSM decisions. Quantitatively, the primary reasons included previous familiarity with medications (18.4%), advice from friends or relatives (17.9%), long waiting times (15.4%), perception of minor illness (12.9%), far travelling distance (10.4%), availability of leftover medications at home (10.0%), advice from pharmacists (8.0%) and time-saving considerations (7.0%). These patterns were echoed and expanded in the qualitative analysis, yielding five interconnected themes: accessibility and convenience, perceived knowledge and past experience, socio-cultural influence, perceived severity of eye problems and health system factors. The ensuing subsections integrate these findings, demonstrating how quantitative endorsements align with qualitative narratives to portray OSM as a multifaceted response to personal, social and structural influences. The analysis primarily focused on identifying the dominant patterns and experiences related to OSM. While attention was given to variations in participants’ accounts, the limited sample size meant that minor themes and deviant cases were not explored in depth.

Theme 1: accessibility and convenience

Quantitative findings indicated that accessibility and convenience were important drivers of OSM. Participants reported far travel distance to health facilities (10.4%), time-saving considerations (7.0%) and availability of eye medications at home (10.0%) as reasons for self-medication, collectively accounting for 27.4% of all reported reasons. These findings were reinforced by the qualitative data, where participants described pharmacies as more accessible and convenient than formal eye care services. Many emphasised the proximity of pharmacies to their homes, shorter waiting times and ability to obtain medications without undergoing lengthy consultation procedures. The availability of previously prescribed eye medications at home also encouraged self-medication practices. Overall, both data strands converged to demonstrate that perceived convenience and ease of access were major facilitators of OSM.

The pharmacy is just around the corner, and I can get eye drops without waiting in line at the hospital. (Participant 4)

This narrative elucidates the quantitative attribution to distance, illustrating how geographical convenience renders pharmacies a default option in resource-limited settings.

Time efficiency was similarly amplified qualitatively, intersecting with the 7.0% quantitative emphasis on time-saving and extending to frustrations with protracted healthcare processes:

Going to the hospital takes half a day. It’s easier to buy something and try it first. (Participant 7)

The hospital is always crowded. I don’t have time to wait, so I buy medicine myself. (Participant 4)

Affordability further intertwined with these elements, as participants contrasted pharmacy costs favourably against cumulative expenses of formal care:

I spend less buying medicine from the pharmacy than paying for a doctor visit every time. (Participants 2 and 4)

Theme 2: perceived knowledge and past experience

Previous familiarity with medications was the most common quantitative finding (18.4%), highlighting experiential factors as a key motivator. Qualitative findings further showed that prior successful experiences-built confidence in self-diagnosis and treatment, creating a cycle of self-reinforced self-efficacy. Participants described their ability to recognise and manage recurrent symptoms, as illustrated by one participant:

I know my eyes. If they are red or itchy, I can tell what’s wrong and treat it myself. (Participant 5)

This aligns with the quantitative prominence of familiarity, highlighting how accumulated personal knowledge reduces reliance on professional care. Reinforcement through prior successful outcomes was also recurrent, as illustrated by one participant:

Last time I had this problem, these drops helped. I just use them again. (Participants 1 and 6)

This narrative complements the quantitative findings, suggesting that past experiences create a feedback loop that perpetuates OSM by reinforcing perceived competence and reducing perceived risk.

Theme 3: socio-cultural influence

Advice from friends/relatives ranked second quantitatively (17.9%), with pharmacist recommendations adding another 8.0%, together comprising 25.9% of responses. Qualitatively, these relational inputs were embedded within broader social networks and norms, where informal endorsements carried substantial weight:

My friend said this eye drop is good, so I used it. It worked fine. (Participants 2, 4 and 7)

This elucidates the quantitative reliance on peer advice, revealing trust in social circles as a mechanism overriding formal guidance.

Cultural normalisation further contextualised these patterns:

Everyone I know buys eye drops themselves, so I thought it’s normal to do the same. (Participant 5)

Integrating with quantitative findings, this theme positions socio-cultural influence as a socially embedded facilitator, where communal practices validate and sustain individual behaviours.

Theme 4: perceived severity of eye problems

The perception of minor illness quantitatively accounted for 12.9% of reasons, reflecting a subjective triage process. Qualitative data explicitly unpacked this evaluation:

It was just a little itchiness, so I didn’t think it was worth seeing a doctor. (Participants 3 and 8)

This theme integrates seamlessly with quantitative patterns, illustrating how participants reserve OSM for non-serious symptoms, escalating to professional care only for persistent or severe issues. It underscores a cognitive framework that rationalises self-medication based on perceived urgency, bridging measurable reasons with interpretive decision-making.

Theme 5: health system factors

Long waiting times emerged quantitatively as a significant deterrent (15.4%), compounded by travelling distance (10.4%). Qualitatively, these were framed as systemic deficiencies:

The hospital is always crowded. I don’t have time to wait, so I buy medicine myself. (Participants 4 and 7)

There’s no eye clinic near my home, so I just go to the pharmacy instead. (Participant 7)

This integration highlights structural barriers as pivotal, where quantitative frustrations manifest in qualitative critiques of inefficiency and inaccessibility, positioning OSM as a necessitated adaptation rather than a preference.

As per the findings of this study, OSM emerges as the product of interdependent practical, experiential, social and systemic elements. Participants balanced convenience, cost and accessibility against perceived knowledge and social advice, while navigating health system constraints. Minor symptoms were preferentially self-managed, with formal care sought for escalated concerns. Collectively, this integrated perspective underscores OSM’s embeddedness in daily realities, informed by personal circumstances and broader contextual limitations, thereby offering actionable insights for intervention design.

Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence of OSM, its associated factors and lived experiences among adult patients receiving ophthalmic care at UoGCSH-TECTC in Gondar, Northwest Ethiopia, using a mixed-methods design. The integration of quantitative and qualitative findings provided a comprehensive understanding of OSM, supporting the development of targeted interventions to promote safe practices and reduce preventable vision loss.

The prevalence of OSM in this study was 43.4%, comparable to that in Lebanon (41.9%),33 but higher than reports from Northeast Ethiopia (28.6%),3 Eritrea (14.9%),14 Ghana (23.3%)18 and Jordan (21.0%).17 Conversely, it was lower than findings from Saudi Arabia (59.7%–62.4%),2 4 Nigeria (73.6%)13 and Tanzania (59.8%).15 These variations may be explained by differences in study settings, definitions of OSM and population characteristics. The higher prevalence compared with Northeast Ethiopia may reflect delayed care-seeking among patients with advanced disease, whereas the lower prevalence in Eritrea may be attributed to stricter regulation and greater public awareness. In contrast, higher rates in Nigeria and Tanzania may be linked to weaker regulatory control and limited access to eye care services.13 15

Steroid misuse was common, consistent with findings from Saudi Arabia (5), raising concerns about complications such as glaucoma. Minor ocular symptoms, including redness (40.2%), itching (34.8%) and foreign body sensation (18.9%), were the most common triggers for OSM, similar to reports from Jordan17 and Lebanon.33 However, unsafe practices were evident, as most participants did not check medication expiration dates (62.1%) or know drug names (59.2%), increasing the risk of adverse effects; this is higher than that reported in Eritrea,14 where only 40% reported ignoring expiration dates. Although most participants experienced symptom improvement (76.6%), a proportion reported no improvement (19.9%) or worsening symptoms (3.5%), highlighting risks such as antimicrobial resistance12 or avoidable vision loss reported in Ghana.18 22 Differences compared with some Asian studies may be explained by cultural reporting differences or variations in drug quality.8 9

Several factors were associated with higher OSM. Longer travel distance to eye care services significantly increased the likelihood of OSM, consistent with findings from Northeast Ethiopia3 and rural India,8 although the effect appears stronger in this study, possibly due to regional transport challenges. Having ophthalmic medications at home also increased OSM, likely reflecting economic constraints and medication storage practices, in line with reports from Eritrea14 and Saudi Arabia.4 Poor knowledge of OSM risks and the presence of chronic systemic illness were also associated with higher OSM, consistent with studies from Northeast Ethiopia3 and India,8 suggesting gaps in health education and integration of chronic and eye care services. Lack of valid health insurance further increased OSM, likely due to incomplete coverage.3 23 In contrast, older age was a protective factor, possibly reflecting greater caution or family support among older adults, and differing from findings in Ethiopia and India where younger age groups were more likely to practice OSM.3 20 21

Some factors, including sex, education and residence, were not significantly associated with OSM, in contrast to findings from India,12 Saudi Arabia23 and Tanzania.15 This discrepancy may be due to the relatively homogeneous nature of the tertiary care population, where clinical severity and healthcare-seeking patterns may outweigh socio-demographic differences.

Qualitative findings complemented these results by revealing multifactorial motivations for OSM. Key drivers included accessibility and convenience, reflected in long travel distance (10.4%), time-saving (7.0%) and availability of leftover medications (10.0%). Prior familiarity with treatments (18.4%), advice from peers or relatives (17.9%) and pharmacists (8.0%), socio-cultural influences and perception of minor symptoms (12.9%) also contributed to OSM. In addition, systemic barriers such as long waiting times (15.4%) were consistently identified across both quantitative and qualitative findings.

Taken together, the mixed-methods findings demonstrate convergence between quantitative factors and qualitative experiences in shaping OSM. The practice is influenced not only by individual-level decisions but also by broader social, economic and healthcare system factors.

This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional study design limits the ability to establish temporal or causal relationships between the identified associated factors and the outcome variable. Therefore, while significant associations were observed, it is not possible to determine the directionality of these relationships. Second, the study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to recall bias. Participants might not accurately remember or report past behaviours or experiences, potentially leading to misclassification of responses. These limitations may have influenced the observed estimates and should be taken into account when generalising the findings. Future longitudinal studies are recommended to better establish causal inferences and to minimise the impact of recall-related bias. Another limitation of this study is that the knowledge and attitude measures were adapted from previous studies and were not fully validated standardised instruments. In addition, formal content validity assessment was not conducted, which may have affected the accuracy of these measurements.

In conclusion, the prevalence of OSM was 43.4%. Key associated factors included long travel distance to eye care services, availability of medications at home, presence of chronic illness, poor knowledge of OSM risks and lack of valid health insurance, while older age was a protective factor. Qualitative findings further highlighted convenience, perceived knowledge, socio-cultural influences, symptom severity and systemic barriers as key drivers. Overall, OSM reflects broader gaps in healthcare accessibility, awareness and regulation. These findings underscore the need for comprehensive interventions focusing on patient education, improved access to eye care services and strengthened regulatory frameworks to reduce OSM and prevent avoidable ocular morbidity and vision loss.
