Hospital-Based Pediatric Suicide Risk Screening Outcomes Vary by Demographic and Clinical Characteristics
Authors: Lauren Gallanis, Quinn Scallon, Rachel Cafferty, Shaela Moen, Anne E Penner, K Ron-Li Liaw, Beau A Carubia, Isabel D Friesner, Pauline Gerard, Lisa Horowitz, Joel Stoddard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSuicide is a leading cause of death among youth, yet differences in screening outcomes across demographic groups remain poorly understood. This study examines associations between demographic and clinical characteristics and responses to universal suicide risk and depression screening tools in a pediatric hospital setting.MethodWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 66,225 patients 10 to 26 years of age who were screened with the Ask Suicide-Screening Questions (ASQ) and Patient Health Questionnaire—9 (PHQ-9) across emergency, urgent care, and inpatient settings. Demographic and clinical data were extracted from electronic health records. Response categories to the ASQ and PHQ-9 were analyzed against these factors with frequency and association tests.ResultsPositive ASQ screens occurred in 8.5% of patients (1.9% acute positive), with elevated rates among older youth and those assigned female at birth. Hispanic/Latino ethnicity and private insurance were associated with lower-than-expected rates of positive screens, whereas having a military insurance payor and higher Area Deprivation Index scores were associated with increased risk. Depression symptoms were more prevalent than suicidal ideation and showed similar demographic patterns.ConclusionDemographic and clinical factors are correlated with suicide and depression screening outcomes. These findings highlight opportunities to improve equity in screening practice.Key words: suicide, depression, screening, youth, demographics

Suicide is a leading cause of death for youth (10-24 years of age), representing over 20% of deaths within this age range. Suicidal ideation, suicide attempt, and death by suicide among youth have become increasingly pervasive issues, with recent studies identifying a high prevalence in both community and clinical settings.1 Among preadolescent patients, the estimated lifetime prevalence of suicidal ideation is 15%, suicide attempt is 2.6%, and suicide mortality rate is 0.79 per 1 million children.2 Given these as well as findings that most suicide decedents have contact with the health care system before their deaths, many large health care systems have adopted universal suicide risk screening in youth.3,4 Relatedly, the prevalence of depression has also increased in adolescent populations in the United States.5 With these increased rates, the American Academy of Pediatrics recommended suicide risk and depression symptom screening for all youth aged 12 years and older.6,7

To support suicide prevention in medical settings, the National Institute of Mental Health developed and validated tools and clinical pathways to identify and manage youth at risk for suicide. These pathways incorporate the Ask Suicide-Screening Questions (ASQ) tool, which includes 4 suicide risk questions, for which a “yes” response to any indicates a positive screen, and a fifth question to which a “yes” response denotes active suicidal ideation and indicates an acute positive screen. The tool was designed for efficient risk identification in medical settings in which staff have many competing priorities for patient care.8 In several validation studies, the ASQ demonstrated strong sensitivity, specificity, and negative predictive value in patients for later report of suicidal thoughts and behavior. These tools were tested in pediatric patients presenting with psychiatric and non-psychiatric chief complaints, and in outpatient and primary care clinics and pediatric emergency departments. The ASQ is concordant with scores on the Suicidal Ideation Questionnaire with high sensitivity, specificity, and negative predictive value in various medical settings (100%, 87.9%-91.2%, 100%9; 96.9%, 87.6%, 96.9%-99.7%8). The ASQ was also found to be valid in youth who were medical/surgical inpatients, with an acute positive rate of 0.5% and a nonacute positive rate of 12.8%.10

Because of its widespread use and integration into electronic health record (EHR) systems, the ASQ has been investigated in several studies using EHR data. Across these studies, most identify a positive response rate (ie, patient responds “yes” to any one of the 4 ASQ questions) of about 3% to 8% in populations of pediatric patients being evaluated in medical environments, including emergency department (ED), inpatient, and primary care settings11, 12, 13; higher positive ASQ rates are found in ED settings and among individuals presenting with psychiatric chief complaints.12,13 Notably, overall positive rates appear to have increased based on a limited number of studies on screening conducted following the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, with rates between 13.1% and 15.87%.14,15

The correlation between symptoms of depression and suicidal ideation has led some institutions to adopt the Patient Health Questionnaire—9 (PHQ-9) as a screener for both depressive symptoms and suicidality; however, studies suggest that item 9 of the PHQ-9 lacks sensitivity as a screener for identifying suicide risk compared to the ASQ.16 Health care systems that screen simultaneously and uniquely for both depressive symptoms and suicidal thoughts and behaviors aim to prevent the progression of severe depression through early identification and to mitigate the risk of suicide through appropriate-level responses to positive screens. In light of the continued children’s mental health national emergency and calls for universal screening, it is important to elevate studies that expand our understanding of how screening tools operate across different demographic and clinical variables. In particular, large pediatric health care systems with longstanding screening practices integrated into EHRs are well poised to deliver such information, although none have investigated demographic correlates of both suicide and depression screening in a large sample.

Differences in report of suicidal thoughts and behaviors and depressive symptoms appear throughout the literature. Among adolescents, natal female youth exhibit a higher prevalence of both suicidal thoughts and attempts, as well as higher rates of reported depressive symptoms.5,17,18 In contrast, death by suicide is more prevalent among natal male adolescents,19 although this difference has narrowed.20

Racial and ethnic differences in suicidality have also been identified: people of color generally report lower rates of suicidal ideation compared to White peers, although those identifying as Black and Hispanic are more likely to report suicide attempt(s) than are White individuals.21,22 These racial and ethnic disparities are further exacerbated by experiences of discrimination, which significantly increase the odds of suicidality.23 Among racial and ethnic groups, non-Hispanic American Indian/Alaska Native youth are highly overrepresented in suicide decedents, followed by non-Hispanic Black and non-Hispanic White youth.17

Socioeconomic status (SES) also plays a critical role in suicidality risk among pediatric populations. Lower SES is associated with increased risk of suicidal ideation and suicide attempt in adolescents, a relationship that is partially mediated by youths’ self-perceived social standing.24 Furthermore, residing in areas with lower economic resources increases the risk of suicide attempt, even after adjusting for individual-level SES.25 Other forms of social disadvantage further compound risk of depression and suicidality. Youth reporting adverse childhood experiences, such as parental incarceration or food insecurity, demonstrate higher rates of suicidal ideation and suicide attempt.26 Similarly, depressive symptoms decrease with increasing family income.5 Additional risk factors include family conflict or dysfunction, low self-esteem, and a personal history of maltreatment, including physical or sexual abuse. Global socioeconomic disadvantage can be measured geographically to assess its impact on suicidality. The Area Deprivation Index (ADI) is a neighborhood-level tool used in the United States to quantify socioeconomic disadvantage. It is highly correlated with other publicly available indices, such as the Childhood Opportunity Index and the Social Vulnerability Index.27,28 The ADI has been strongly associated with various health outcomes, including mortality, physical health, mental health, and overall well-being.26

Although sociodemographic differences in youth suicidality and depression have been well documented, less is known about how these factors influence responses on standardized screening tools such as the ASQ and PHQ-9, especially within large-scale, real-world EHR data. Specifically, it remains unclear whether certain sociodemographic groups are over- or underrepresented among individuals who screen positive on the ASQ relative to known population-level rates of suicidality and suicide attempts. In this study, we examined variation in both ASQ and PHQ-9 screening responses with sociodemographic characteristics among pediatric patients screened in ED, urgent care (UC), and inpatient settings at a single academic medical center.

Method

Study Design

This retrospective cohort study included patients presenting to the ED, UC, or admitted inpatient at a pediatric hospital in the Mountain West from May 1, 2022, to December 19, 2023. The study was determined to be exempt from review by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board.

Participants

We included patients 10 to 26 years of age at encounter, per the hospital’s screening policy. For multiple visits per patient, only the last encounter was considered to preserve independence of observations while maximizing clinical information. This yielded a total of 66,225 patients who were eligible for ASQ screening by policy at their last visit before the data extraction for analysis. The implementation of the PHQ-9 occurred after the ASQ and not in individuals undergoing a psychiatric evaluation in our health care system, in total 17,652 individuals. Data are understood to be clustered by service and setting, but indicators of practice setting (eg, ED, UC, inpatient) were not available.

Measures

Ask Suicide-Screening Questions

Ask Suicide-Screening Questions (ASQ)8 is a validated brief screening tool for suicide risk. It includes a set of 4 questions prompting a “yes” or “no” response regarding recent suicidal thoughts or previous suicidal behavior (ie, suicide attempt). If a patient responds “yes” to any of these questions, a fifth “acuity” question inquires whether the individual is having active thoughts of killing themself.

Patient Health Questionnaire—9

The Patient Health Questionnaire—9 (PHQ-9)29 is a 9-item tool that screens for severity of depressive symptoms. Individuals respond to questions based on how often they have experienced each symptom over the past 2 weeks, including “not at all,” “several days,” “more than half the days,” or “nearly every day,” with higher overall scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms.

Area Deprivation Index

The Area Deprivation Index (ADI)30 is a publicly available geographic mapping tool that uses census data to assign rankings to neighborhoods based on variables related to socioeconomic disadvantage, with higher values relating to greater disadvantage. Neighborhoods are ranked based on factors such as income, education, employment, and housing quality.

Demographics

Demographic variables were extracted from the EHR. Ethnicity was recorded as Hispanic or Latino, Not Hispanic or Latino, or Unknown. Race was recorded as American Indian/Alaskan Native, Asian, Black, Multi-racial, Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander, Other, Unknown, or White. Natal sex and birth date were included in patients’ EHR from first point of contact with the system.

Clinical Variables

An indicator for having any prior mental health encounter was generated from EHR records from having had at least one encounter at any level of care contact within the mental health department. An indicator was also generated by any documented mandatory report of suspected child abuse or neglect (SCAN), both current and historical instances, which are always documented in the EHR as a separate SCAN encounter.

Procedures

Suicide Risk Screening

Universal suicide risk screening was implemented across EDs, UC centers, and inpatient medical/surgical units within the hospital system in 2017, requiring screening with the ASQ for patients 10 through 26 years of age. Although the ASQ is validated for use in individuals age 8 years and older, the hospital implemented universal screening procedures based on data indicating increased risk of suicide starting at age 10 years. The ASQ was administered in EDs and UCs either verbally by nursing staff (through August 2023) or via self-report on a laminated paper screening tool (after August 2023). On inpatient medical/surgical units, the ASQ was administered verbally by nursing staff. Responses in all settings were recorded in the EHR. For patients who answered “no” to all 4 of the initial ASQ questions, screening was marked as completed without further intervention. For patients who answered “yes” to any one of the 4 initial ASQ questions but “no” to the fifth acuity question, the EHR notified the RN to alert a provider (eg, physician, physician assistant, nurse practitioner) to complete further risk assessment, often including a HEADSS (Home, Education, Activities/Employment, Drugs, Suicidality, Sex) examination, a common psychosocial “review of systems” for adolescents.31 Based on the provider’s assessment, if risk was deemed high, the provider could place a consultation for further evaluation by a licensed mental health practitioner (eg, psychiatrist, psychologist, licensed social worker, or counselor). Finally, for patients who responded “yes” to the fifth acuity question of the ASQ, providers were notified in the EHR to implement environmental safety precautions and to order a psychiatric consultation.

For analysis, ASQ responses were categorized as one of 4 outcomes: positive (any question endorsed), acute positive (question 5 was endorsed), negative, or not administered. For the purposes of this study, we combined non-acute positive and acute positive screens. Screening rates were not expected to be fully complete because of exceptions among some patients in which it was not appropriate (eg, medically unstable, developmental delay) or done by another means (ie, full crisis assessment occurring).

Depression Screening

After recognizing the success and feasibility of universal screening for suicide risk, in August 2023, the hospital elected to broaden screening efforts for patients 10 to 26 years of age to include depressive symptoms using the PHQ-2 or PHQ-9 during ED, UC, or inpatient encounters. The PHQ-2 or PHQ-9 was completed via a self-reported laminated screening tool in the ED and UC and was administered verbally in the inpatient units by nursing staff. In the ED and UC, the first 2 questions of the PHQ-2 or PHQ-9 were answered by the patient. If the PHQ-2 score resulted in a score of 2 or higher, the remaining 7 questions of the PHQ-9 were completed. Our system used a lower threshold to expand from the PHQ-2 to the PHQ-9 with the intention to capture all potential positive responses. In the inpatient units, all questions on the PHQ-9 were asked regardless of the PHQ-2 score. Responses were entered into the EHR for scoring. Patients with a score of 4 or less were considered negative and the process complete. Patients with a score between 5 and 14 were considered to have low to moderate depression and were offered written mental health resources and follow-up options provided in the after-visit summary. Patients with a score of 15 or greater were considered to have moderately severe to severe depression, resulting in 2 possible process options contingent on the ASQ score. Patients with a negative ASQ score but moderately severe to severe depression were offered an abbreviated psychiatry consultation by a licensed behavioral health clinician who briefly assessed the patient and offered insight into the best options for outpatient management and follow-up. Patients with a positive ASQ score and moderately severe to severe depression followed the processes outlined for patients with a positive ASQ. All patients with a score of 5 or greater received written mental health resources and follow-up options provided in the discharge paperwork.

Data Analysis

Frequency comparisons were made with χ2 tests and mean differences by analysis of variance. Effect sizes are represented by standard mean differences. All null hypotheses were that chance led to the observations. Post hoc analyses were by pairwise t tests in the case of means comparisons or by z tests on χ2 test residuals for representation in frequency comparisons. A demographic category is described as over-represented if observations occur at a greater frequency than would be expected by chance and as under-represented if they occur at a lesser frequency than expected. Post hoc tests were corrected for multiplicity with Holm–Bonferroni correction. All age-related analyses were conducted in raw data unit of weeks and converted to years for presentation by dividing by 52.1775.

Results

Description of the Study Population

A total of 66,225 patients were eligible for ASQ screening by policy and data available for this analysis with demographics presented in Table 1. Of these, 8.5% had a positive ASQ screen result and 1.9% also answered “yes” to the fifth acuity question. An ASQ result was entered for 79.2% of these patients. Of the 21.8% who did not have an ASQ result, 41.5% had some noted reason for a result not being entered (developmental disability preventing capacity to participate in screening [14.8% of those with no ASQ result], the patient had an alternative assessment via a psychiatric evaluation [16.5%], the patient was too medically unstable [5.4%], the patient refused the ASQ [1.5%], or there was another reason [3.4%]). A total of 12.5% had no ASQ administered during the study period for unknown reasons.

ASQ Responses by Natal Sex and Age

All characteristics assessed were associated with an ASQ screen (Table 1). Of individuals with a positive ASQ, there were twice as many natal female patients as male patients. This ratio was higher when considering “acute positives” (2.3) relative to “non-acute positives” (2.0) (ꭓ2[1] = 5.40, p = .02). Older individuals were more likely to report suicidal ideation than younger individuals, with a moderate effect (Cohen d = 0.31). Of positives, younger individuals were more likely to be acute vs non-acute, with a small effect (t[1615.7] = −4.83, p < .001, Cohen d = 0.15).

ASQ Responses by Race and Ethnicity

Of race categories, individuals who identified as Black and of unknown race were more likely to not have an ASQ screen recorded (Table 1). Those who identified as “other” were over-represented in negative ASQ results and under-represented in positive ASQ results; the opposite pattern occurred in individuals who identified as multiracial. Those who identified as White were underrepresented in having no ASQ results, explaining in part their over-representation in both negative and positive ASQ results.

Of ethnicity categories, identifying as Hispanic or Latino resulted in over-representation in negative ASQ results and under-representation in positive ASQ results. Of note, those who identified as Hispanic or Latino were also more likely to identify as other race (p < .001).

ASQ Responses by Insurance and SES

Of payor categories, those with contracted payors, which are private health insurance companies that hold contracts with the hospital system, have an over-representation of negative ASQ results and an under-representation of positive ASQ results (Table 1). Those who are in the self-pay or Medicaid categories were more likely to have no ASQ result. Finally, those with TRICARE (military) insurance were unique in being significantly over-represented in having a positive ASQ result, with 12.7% TRICARE participants reporting suicidal ideation relative to 8% of individuals with private payor contracts.

An alternative method of assessing a socioeconomic association is to examine ADI. During the data de-identification process, any 9-digit zip code was linked to a state and national ADI score. This resulted in 6,971 individuals for whom an ADI was available. Both state and national ADI scores were associated with the ASQ screen result (F2,6968 = 9.51, p < .001 and F2,6968 = 5.76, p = .003, respectively). For both scales, a positive screening result was associated with a higher deprivation score relative to either a negative result or no ASQ administered (p < .02). The greatest magnitude was for those with a positive ASQ result having a greater state ADI than those with a negative ASQ result (Cohen d = 0.17, small effect).

ASQ Responses by Clinical Characteristics

Clinical characteristics by ASQ result are displayed in Table 2. Length of stay was greater for individuals with a positive ASQ or not having an ASQ, on average >1 day. Those with a historical or current SCAN report being made were more likely to have a positive ASQ result or no ASQ recorded, the latter driven by them undergoing another means of assessing suicide risk (p < .001). Among individuals with a SCAN report, there were about twice the number ASQ positive results or no ASQ, compared to negative ASQ results.

PHQ Responses by Demographics

Moderate-to-severe symptom PHQ-9 reports were found in 21% of recorded respondents. Their demographic and clinical correlates are shown in Table 3. More severe levels of PHQ-9 symptoms were expressed with natal female patients and increasing age. Individuals who identified as white were over-represented for reporting severe symptoms, whereas those who identified as “other” race or as Hispanic/Latino ethnicity were overrepresented for reporting moderate symptoms. Significant associations between payor type and PHQ-9 severity were limited; distinct from reporting suicidal ideation, those with military-based insurance reported less moderate PHQ-9 symptoms. For those with both ADI and PHQ-9 scores (n = 1,953), there was no detectable association between these measures (F2,1950 = 2.64, p = .07).

PHQ Responses by Clinical Characteristics

With regard to clinical associations, positive and no ASQ results were associated with elevated PHQ-9 symptoms, as was a history of mental health treatment in our hospital system. Length of stay was positively associated with PHQ-9 severity (Table 3). Current or past SCAN report was positively associated with severe PHQ-9 symptom report.

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective cohort study, we examined demographic and clinical associations with responses to suicide and depression risk screeners applied in general medical settings. Rates of positive suicide risk screens in this cohort were 8.5% overall (1.9% acute positive), which is consistent with prior studies.9,12 Of the 20% who did not have ASQ results, many had reasonable rationale recorded for lack of administration (eg, already receiving full psychiatric evaluation, medical instability), suggesting that the intended patient population was appropriately screened when considering clinical exceptions. Symptoms of depression were more commonly reported than suicidal thoughts and behavior, and had similar associations with demographic and clinical features. Overall, we found that demographic characteristics of natal sex, age, race, ethnicity, insurance payor, socioeconomic disadvantage, and clinical factors were associated with rates of positive screens on the ASQ, and that natal sex, age, race, ethnicity, and clinical factors were associated with reported depressive symptom severity on the PHQ-9.

ASQ Responses by Natal Sex and Age

Both positive ASQ screens and reports of moderate and severe PHQ-9 symptoms were positively associated with age and female natal sex with moderate to large effects, similar to previous studies.11,12 Our age-related finding is consistent with a longitudinally observed increase in depressive symptoms by natal sex given physiologic (eg puberty) and psychosocial (eg peer stressors and contagion) factors.32 Our finding that female natal sex was associated with higher rates of positive screens, especially on the ASQ, is consistent with the literature noting that female individuals are more likely to report suicidal ideation and attempts, whereas male individuals are more likely to die by suicide.1,33 This also fits within the context of publicly available state-level data in Colorado on suicide decedents, in which 73.9% of suicide decedents in the years 2022 to 2023 and ages 10 to 24 years were male.34

ASQ Responses by Race and Ethnicity

Identifying as Hispanic or Latino was associated with lower report of suicidal thoughts and behavior. Given state-level data for similar age groups across the same time frame, in which 33.9% of suicide decedents identified as Hispanic or Latino vs 62.1% who identified as not Hispanic or Latino,34 this congruence suggests a possible protective effect of identifying as Hispanic or Latino. These findings present an opportunity for deeper exploration, especially geographically, as Hispanic suicide decedents in national samples have lower odds of having a documented mental health diagnosis prior to their death when compared to non-Hispanic decedents.33 Identifying as Black, being uninsured, or having Medicaid insurance were all associated with no ASQ screen administered. Recent studies have highlighted the under-identification of Black youth in having a documented mental health diagnosis prior to death by suicide33 and youth of color with ED visits being accurately coded for visits prompted by self-injurious thoughts and behaviors.35 Future work should focus on investigation of the inequities in screening and diagnosis in Black youth, especially given that Black female youth have higher rates of reported suicidal ideation and suicide attempts relative to non-Hispanic White and to male peers.36

ASQ Responses by Insurance and SES

Socioeconomic advantage was associated with lower endorsement of suicidal thoughts and behaviors on screening via both insurance proxies (ie, private insurance) and the ADI, suggesting that higher socioeconomic status is likely to provide a protective effect, except in the case of having military-based insurance. These data were, unfortunately, unavailable for the same time frame in state-level data for comparison, although a prior study found area-level disadvantage to be linked to increased attempted suicide in adolescents.25 Having military-based insurance was also associated with a positive ASQ screen with a meaningful effect. One recent study showed that military-connected youth had higher odds of suicidal ideation and plans, in comparison to youth without military connection.37

ASQ Responses by Clinical Characteristics

Although a positive ASQ screen was more likely to occur in individuals with a prior mental health contact or a SCAN report, a much larger majority of individuals with a positive ASQ screen had neither of these; this provides further support for the necessity of universal screening specific to suicide risk within medical settings. Prior studies have demonstrated that youth with a history of maltreatment are more likely to report suicidal ideation, which is consistent with our findings.38 In addition, within our system, history of abuse and neglect is assessed as part of a full crisis assessment. Thus, there may be a selection bias in which SCAN reports are elevated in youth who participate in such evaluations likely to detect them. In general, bias may also arise in those with psychosocial chief complaints, in whom screening is deferred to a more comprehensive psychiatric and crisis assessment by other means.

Existing mental health conditions are a known predictor of suicide in Medicaid-enrolled youth, although for many these concerns are unidentified and/or untreated prior to death by suicide; less than half of suicide decedents had a recorded mental health diagnosis in the 6 months preceding date of death.33,39 Compared to controls, suicide decedents had higher odds of last contact with the medical system being a physical health inpatient visit. Odds of death by suicide were reduced for those with more mental health visits in the 30 days prior to death.39 Although state-level data are not available for the full duration of our study, 2022 data on youth suicide decedents indicate that only 54.9% had a diagnosed mental health problem and that only 33.3% had ever received mental health treatment.34

This is a retrospective study that used EHR data; although this is an efficient way to capture clinical and demographic patient information, it presents inherent limitations. There were notable numbers of responses for “other” and “unknown” categories for race and ethnicity demographics; however, we are unable to further explore how individuals selecting those options identified, which challenges our understanding of response patterns related to those categorizations. As this information is often collected by institutions as a means of understanding patterns in health care use, it may be important to elevate significant findings to systems-level leaders and to advocate for expanded methods for collecting relevant demographic information. Institutional practices also allowed for nursing staff to defer administration of the ASQ when patients were already undergoing a crisis assessment, a possibility in ED and inpatient settings. Although this procedure was not followed consistently, and although many encounters included both a crisis assessment and ASQ administration, a proportion of ASQ screenings that were not completed likely would have included patients with positive or acute positive results. This report examines cross-sectional associations between ASQ responses and demographic factors at the last visit, which does not represent individuals who may have had a history of a positive ASQ screen. Future research should examine associations with any lifetime positive ASQ risk.

EHR data also limit users to what is institutionally available. Because of system-level implementation, we used the available PHQ-2 or PHQ-9 rather than the modified adolescent version, which may be less age-appropriate for younger patients and could affect depression symptom assessment in this population. The ADI was available for approximately 10% of our sample, which limits the interpretability within this study. The impact of sociodemographic advantage on responses to universal screening is important to assess through various methods, and our significant albeit small associations suggest this as an important factor to include in future studies. This consideration for future examination is highly suggested for associations between sociodemographic advantage and responses to depression screening, which were further limited in our study by small sample size.

In addition, ASQ and PHQ responses were self-reported by youth and may be affected by response bias or by privacy and confidentiality concerns that youth experience in these settings. For example, individuals may underreport suicidal thoughts or behaviors to align with perceived expectations or to avoid potential consequences of disclosure.40 Although screens were completed by patient self-report in the ED/UC, admitted patients were screened verbally by nurses; honest disclosure may vary by unmeasured aspects of the encounter affecting respondent and observer bias (eg, implicit bias). For example, a recent study examining this found a trend of higher scores on the PHQ-A from written self-report as compared to oral administration by staff41; thus, studies comparing ASQ screening modalities (verbal, paper, or electronic) are needed. Finally, this is a single center study, and results may not be generalizable to other settings.

This study used EHR data from a large pediatric hospital system to better understand associations between demographic information and responses to widely used suicide and depression screeners, and evaluated these findings in the context of state-level data on suicide decedents. Future studies will consider expanding generalizability by obtaining data beyond one hospital system, for example with use of data from state-wide hospital systems or insurance providers, as well as granularity by examining differences across settings (eg, ED, UC) within an institution. Furthermore, examinations of temporal patterns in measures and item-level responses, especially to the ASQ, might increase our understanding of suicidal thoughts and behaviors that drive demographic differences in overall screening outcomes.

The medical system continues to provide ample opportunity for screening and identification for youth at risk for suicide; however, our results suggest that there is still variability in how well this is captured for youth across different demographics. Although universal screening protocols are in place, screening rates and outcomes differ among groups, suggesting that screening may not be applied or interpreted equitably. Large health care systems may have the resources to leverage EHR data to better understand where these gaps exist, and to work toward tailoring interventions for preventing suicide following screening.
