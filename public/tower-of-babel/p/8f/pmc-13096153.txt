Effect of sex differences on the emergence of ctDNA RAS mutations in RAS wild-type colorectal cancer
Authors: Kenta Iguchi, Mamoru Uchiyama, Masahiro Asari, Koji Numata, Manabu Shiozawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn some cases of RAS wild-type metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC), mutant RAS may emerge during chemotherapy. This study aimed to identify the clinicopathological factors associated with the emergence of RAS mutations during treatment. This single-center retrospective study included 43 patients with mCRC harboring microsatellite stable RAS and BRAF wild-type mCRC who underwent RAS trace via a ctDNA assay between August 1, 2020 and April 30, 2023. The clinicopathological factors were classified as follows: pre-treatment and during treatment (when the ctDNA assay was performed). An exploratory analysis was performed to clarify the factors contributing to the emergence of RAS mutations. The RAS mutation was detected in 13 (30.2%) patients, presenting in KRAS codon 12 (46%), KRAS codon 61 (23%), NRAS codon 61 (23%), and NRAS codon 12 (8%). In a univariate analysis, RAS reversion from wild-type to mutant-type was observed to occur with a greater frequency in younger patients, females, and those with high serum carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels at ctDNA collection. Multivariate analysis demonstrated that female sex (odds ratio [OR]: 0.12, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.01–0.68, p = 0.016) and high serum CEA levels at the time of ctDNA assay during chemotherapy (OR: 1.04, 95% CI: 1.01–4.80, p = 0.004) were independent factors for RAS mutation emergence. The overall survival rates were comparable between the RAS mutation emergence and persistent RAS wild-type groups (p = 0.88). In RAS wild-type mCRC, female patients and patients with high CEA levels may benefit from precision treatment strategies that incorporate real-time genetic monitoring.Keywords: Sex, RAS gene, Circulating tumor DNA, Colorectal cancer, MutationSubject terms: Cancer, Gastroenterology

Introduction

Colorectal cancer represents a significant global health burden, being the second most common cause of cancer-related mortality1. The genetic profile of individual colorectal cancers represents the most significant determinant in the selection of therapeutic agents. In metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC), molecular-targeted drugs in combination with cytotoxic anticancer drugs are one of the key drugs, and national guidelines, such as the European Society for Medical Oncology, Japanese Society for Cancer of the Colon and Rectum, and National Comprehensive Cancer Network, recommend the use of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and anti-epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) antibody agents depending on the presence of RAS mutations2–4. In recent years, the rapid development of liquid biopsy technologies has led to the reporting of biomarker retrieval using circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) assays in the field of colorectal cancer5–7. Additionally, the dynamics of RAS gene status during treatment in mCRC have attracted significant attention. In RAS wild-type colorectal cancer, RAS mutations may emerge during treatment due to the loss of anti-EGFR antibody-sensitive clones during anti-EGFR antibody therapy, a result of intratumor heterogeneity8,9. Thus, temporal and spatial dynamics have been noted with regard to RAS mutations. In our previous study, we reported that patients with acquired RAS mutation loss in RAS-mutant colorectal cancer may have a better prognosis10. Loss of mutation during treatment for RAS-mutant colorectal cancer is termed “NeoRAS wild-type,” and its frequency has been reported to range from 9.8% to 43.5%11–14. Furthermore, mutation loss has been associated with tumor size and the presence or absence of liver metastases15.

In contrast, in the RAS dynamics of RAS wild-type mCRC, the primary clinical focus has been on the rechallenge of anti-EGFR antibody therapy16–18. The appearance of RAS mutations in tumors that were initially RAS wild-type is a widely acknowledged mechanism of acquired resistance to anti-EGFR monoclonal antibodies19,20. Conversely, in clinical practice, RAS mutations may manifest with or without the administration of anti-EGFR antibody therapy. Furthermore, the considerable heterogeneity of the clinicopathological background in mCRC represents a significant challenge, given the inherent complexity of the metastatic site, tumor volume, and chemotherapy regimen. The existing literature predominately focuses on the presence or absence of RAS mutations and the feasibility of anti-EGFR antibody therapy. However, there is a notable gap in research addressing precision treatment strategies that consider treatment courses and clinicopathologic factors in real-world clinical practice. Accordingly, the objective of the present study was to elucidate the clinical significance and clinicopathological factors associated with the emergence of RAS mutations during treatment in wild-type RAS mCRC.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Kanagawa Cancer Center in Yokohama, Japan. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kanagawa Cancer Center (approval number: 2023-85). Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for written informed consent was waived, and participants were given the opportunity to opt out. The study was registered in the Japanese Clinical Trials Registry, with registration number UMIN000053017.

The study included patients with RAS wild-type mCRC who underwent RAS trace via a ctDNA assay between August 1, 2020 and April 2023. The data pertaining to the patients, comprising demographic, clinical, and pathological information, were extracted from their medical records. The following criteria were used to select patients for inclusion in the study: (i) RAS wild-type diagnosis based on histological examination of the primary tumor, (ii) metastatic colorectal adenocarcinoma, (iii) BRAF wild-type, and (iv) microsatellite stable tumors. Patients with BRAF mutations, microsatellite instability-high tumors, and colitis-associated cancers were excluded.

RAS monitoring during standard-of-care regimens

In order to assess the RAS status prior to the commencement of treatment, tumor tissue excised either via surgical procedure or endoscopic biopsy from the primary lesion was analyzed using the MEBGEN RASKET™-B kit (Medical & Biological Laboratories Co., Tokyo, Japan). The following mutations in KRAS and NRAS were examined: (i) exon 2 (G12S, G12C, G12R, G12D, G12V, G12A, G13S, G13C, G13R, G13D, and G13V), (ii) exon 3 (A59T, A59G, Q61K, Q61E, Q61L, Q61P, Q61R, and Q61H), and (iii) exon 4 (K117N, A146T, A146P, and A146V).

Intra-treatment RAS monitoring was performed via a ctDNA RAS assay using OncoBEAM™ RAS CRC assay (Sysmex Inc., Kobe, Japan); details regarding the assay can be found in our previous study10. The timing of the ctDNA assay was determined by the treating physician in each case. Testing was performed during treatment with the standard treatment regimen or at the time of tumor progression. During the study period in Japan, the OncoBEAM™ RAS CRC assay was the only commercially available and nationally reimbursed liquid biopsy test for RAS mutation analysis. Accordingly, it represented the most feasible and standardized method for clinical ctDNA-based RAS assessment within our institutional setting.

Identification of the clinicopathological factors that contribute to the emergence of RAS mutations

An explorative data analysis was performed in order to identify clinicopathological factors that contribute to the emergence of RAS mutations in RAS wild-type colorectal cancer. The clinicopathological factors were divided into two categories: pre-treatment factors and post-treatment factors at ctDNA RAS assay after treatment initiation. We have defined the two categories of variables as follows: pre-treatment variables refer to patient-, tumor-, and disease-related characteristics assessed before systemic chemotherapy, whereas post-treatment variables refer to clinical and biochemical parameters evaluated at the exact time of the ctDNA RAS assay during treatment. The pre-treatment variables included age, sex, tumor location, histology, serum carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and cancer antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9) levels before treatment initiation, and metastatic organs at treatment initiation. The post-treatment variables included serum CEA and CA19-9 levels at ctDNA RAS assay, metastatic organs, chemotherapy regimen line, and history of anti-EGFR antibody drug use.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test and described using the median and interquartile range. The analysis of differences between categorical variables was conducted using Fisher’s exact probability test. Survival outcomes were assessed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and group data were compared using the log-rank test.

To identify clinicopathological factors that contribute to the emergence of RAS mutations in wild-type RAS colorectal cancer, factors that were significant (p < 0.05) on univariate analysis were entered and analyzed using multivariate logistic regression models. Due to the limited cohort size and the small number of event occurrences, which posed a risk of bias, we employed Firth’s penalized likelihood approach for logistic regression. Firth’s method is known to reduce bias in parameter estimates, thereby providing more robust results, particularly in datasets with small sample sizes or rare events. Statistical analyses were carried out using R software version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R, with a significance level of p < 0.05.

Plasma ctDNA RAS trace

All patients underwent a standard systemic treatment regimen, including oxaliplatin or irinotecan, with regimens continued or modified according to tumor progression. The presence or absence of molecularly targeted drugs was determined on a case-by-case basis, and anti-VEGF or anti-EGFR antibody agents were used. At the time of the ctDNA RAS assay, 86.0% of patients were in the first- and second-line of treatment, and 14.0% of patients were in the third-line or later. The median time between the start of systemic chemotherapy and the first ctDNA RAS assay was 21 months (interquartile range,11.5–37 months). Among the 13 patients in whom RAS mutations were detected, eight showed mutations in the initial ctDNA RAS assay. In the remaining five patients, although the initial ctDNA RAS assay did not identify any mutations, RAS mutations were subsequently identified in the second assay (Fig. 2). The median interval between the first and second tests was 8 months. RAS mutations appeared in KRAS codon 12 (46%), KRAS codon 61 (23%), NRAS codon 61 (23%), and NRAS codon 12 (8%) (Fig. 3).

There was no significant difference in serum CEA levels at the start of treatment between the persistent RAS wild-type (persistent RAS-WT) and RAS mutation emergence (RAS-MTe) groups. Meanwhile, serum CEA level at the time of performing the ctDNA RAS assay was significantly higher in the RAS-MTe group (4.6 ng/mL vs. 16.8ng/mL, p = 0.013). No significant differences were observed between the two groups with respect to CA19-9 levels, presence of distant metastatic organs at the commencement of treatment, and the number of distant metastatic and metastatic organs when the ctDNA RAS assay was conducted (Table 2).

Relationship between RAS mutation emergence and clinicopathological factors

In a univariate analysis of RAS mutation appearance during chemotherapy in RAS wild-type colorectal cancer, RAS reversion from wild-type to mutant-type was observed to occur with a greater frequency in younger patients, females, and patients with high serum CEA levels at ctDNA collection. Multivariate analysis demonstrated that female sex (odds ratio [OR]: 0.12, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.01–0.68, p = 0.016) and high serum CEA levels at the time of ctDNA assay during treatment (OR: 1.04, 95% CI: 1.01–4.80, p = 0.004) were independent factors for RAS-MTe (Table 3). The overall survival rates were found to be comparable between the RAS-MTe and persistent RAS-WT groups (p = 0.88) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the clinicopathological factors for the emergence of RAS mutations in patients with mCRC accompanying wild-type RAS undergoing chemotherapy. Our findings identified female sex and elevated serum CEA levels at the time of ctDNA RAS assay as independent factors. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies, which have indicated that sex-based differences in chemotherapy response, as well as tumor burden as indicated by high serum markers, may contribute to genetic changes during cancer treatment. In a clinical setting, ctDNA has the potential to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular characteristics of tumors in comparison to that by tissue biopsies21–23. This is achieved by identifying genetic alterations that are the consequence of spatial and temporal heterogeneity in real time, which in turn offers the possibility of selecting the most appropriate treatment in a well-timed manner. In ctDNA RAS assays for mCRC, the site of metastatic organs, such as liver or lung metastasis, is an important factor influencing the assay results. Meanwhile, the impact of sex on the ctDNA RAS assay remains unclear, although the differences in response rate to chemotherapy and drug sensitivity by sex have recently attracted attention.

Our finding that RAS-MTe occurred more frequently in female patients warrants biological consideration. Several mechanisms may contribute. First, sex-specific differences in drug pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics have been reported in colorectal cancer treatment, with women experiencing higher exposure and toxicity to fluoropyrimidine-based regimens24–29.Such differential drug sensitivity could impose distinct selective pressures on tumor cell populations, potentially promoting the outgrowth of resistant subclones, including RAS-mutant lineages. Second, accumulating evidence suggests that immune and microenvironmental differences—such as heightened inflammatory or immune-reactive states in females—may influence tumor evolutionary trajectories during therapy30,31. Although the precise mechanism remains speculative, our observation that emergent RAS mutations occurred more frequently in female patients suggests that sex-related biologic context may shape clonal selection during treatment. A sub-analysis of the AIO-KRK-0212 (PanaMa) study, which compared the efficacy of panitumumab (Pmab) plus FU and folic acid (FA) maintenance therapy with that of FU/FA alone, revealed some interesting findings. The addition of Pmab to maintenance therapy in patients with RAS wild-type mCRC who had undergone six cycles of FU/FA and oxaliplatin plus Pmab induction therapy resulted in a prolonged progression-free survival in male patients (hazard ratio [HR] 0.63, 95% CI 0.45–0.88). However, this effect was not observed in female patients (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.53–1.35)32. The findings of our study may provide one possible consideration when interpreting the sex-related results reported in the sub-analysis of the PanaMa study. Interestingly, we observed that acquired RAS mutation emergence during systemic chemotherapy tended to occur more frequently in female than in male patients, which could conceptually offer one potential explanation for the differing benefit of concomitant anti-EGFR therapy reported in the PanaMa study. However, any connection between our results and the PanaMa findings remains purely hypothetical, and further validation is required before drawing clinical inferences.

CEA has been established as an efficacious and economical biological tumor marker for colorectal cancer diagnostics since the 1960s33, with elevated serum CEA levels serving as an indicator of increased tumor burden34,35. In our cohort, patients with high CEA levels at the time of the ctDNA assay demonstrated a higher incidence of acquired mutations. This finding aligns with the established correlation between elevated CEA levels and the presence of larger tumors and more aggressive tumor growth. The observed correlation between high CEA levels and RAS mutation detection suggests that tumor burden may also influence the effectiveness of liquid biopsy techniques, such as ctDNA assays. Patients with higher tumor burdens are likely to release more ctDNA into circulation, improving the likelihood of detecting genetic mutations that might not be observable in patients with smaller tumors. Furthermore, the RAS-MTe group included relatively younger patients. One possible explanation is that younger patients may present with more rapidly progressive disease, resulting in a higher tumor burden at the time of ctDNA assay and potentially increasing the detectability of emerging RAS-mutant subclones. Although our data suggested a potential association between increasing CEA levels and the emergence of RAS mutations, the limited cohort size precluded a reliable ROC analysis. Even so, rising CEA levels during chemotherapy may serve as a practical indicator prompting clinicians to consider ctDNA RAS monitoring to evaluate possible acquisition of RAS mutations. A correlation between tumor burden and ctDNA has been reported; however, ctDNA assays still present several challenges in terms of cost and simplicity. Serum CEA, which is readily testable, may serve as an adjunct indicator of the emergence of RAS mutations in the bloodstream during treatment for RAS wild-type colorectal cancer. The findings of the present study may indicate the importance of regular monitoring of RAS status through the use of sensitive techniques such as ctDNA assays, particularly in female patients and those with elevated serum CEA levels. Taken together, these findings suggest that incorporating sex and serum CEA level into routine clinical assessment may help identify patients who would benefit from earlier or more frequent ctDNA RAS monitoring, thereby enabling more timely detection of emerging resistant subclones. Additionally, in contrast to prior studies in which emergent RAS mutations typically arose after exposure to anti-EGFR therapy—a setting where EGFR blockade–induced clonal selection of RAS-mutant subpopulations is known to drive treatment resistance and poor prognosis—we did not observe a significant difference in OS between the RAS-MTe and persistent RAS-WT groups. This result should be interpreted cautiously, as the small sample size and heterogeneity of treatment may have limited our ability to detect meaningful prognostic differences. Furthermore, not all emergent RAS mutations in our cohort occurred after anti-EGFR therapy, suggesting that some may reflect intrinsic tumor heterogeneity or clonal evolution independent of EGFR-directed selective pressure. These differences in biological and therapeutic context may account for the attenuated prognostic impact observed in this study.

The present study has some limitations. First, the study was based on a relatively small number of cases, and the treatment course and regimen were heterogeneous. Given the limited sample size and retrospective single-center design, the associations identified in this study should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Although female sex and elevated CEA were independently associated with RAS-MTe in our cohort, these observations require validation in larger datasets, ideally within prospective trials evaluating ctDNA-guided treatment strategies. Meanwhile, EGFR-rechallenge therapy has gained growing attention in recent trials such as the CRICKET36 and CHRONOS37 trials, and our findings may provide additional insights that could help refine patient selection for molecular-targeted therapies. Second, a major methodological limitation is the absence of baseline ctDNA assessment prior to treatment initiation. Because pretreatment plasma samples were not available, we could not determine whether RAS mutations might already have been present in circulation at diagnosis. As a result, distinguishing truly acquired RAS mutations from the expansion of pre-existing mutant subclones cannot be definitively achieved. This issue is particularly relevant in colorectal cancer, where marked spatial and temporal heterogeneity may lead to discrepancies between tumor tissue genotyping and ctDNA profiles. In addition, the OncoBEAM assay used in this study detects only RAS mutations and does not capture other genomic alterations. On the other hand, in real-world clinical practice, the disease course becomes increasingly complex as treatment lines advance—metastatic sites evolve, treatment efficacy varies, and chemotherapy regimens diversify—making repeated and comprehensive genomic profiling challenging. Although limited in scope, RAS mutations remain a clinically meaningful determinant in the selection of treatment strategies for mCRC. Despite these limitations, the findings of this study contribute to the advancement of precision oncology, including the consideration of sex-related differences in tumor biology. Recent studies38–41 have also highlighted the dynamic nature of RAS status, including the transient emergence and disappearance of RAS-mutant clones during therapy. Future studies integrating these insights may help clarify how treatment pressure interacts with the risk factors identified in our cohort.

Conclusions

The present study identified female sex and elevated serum CEA levels as independent risk factors for the development of RAS mutations in patients with RAS wild-type mCRC undergoing standard-of-care regimens. These findings indicate that biological sex differences in tumor behavior and immune response, in conjunction with increased tumor burden, may be responsible for the emergence of new RAS mutations during treatment. The observed correlation between elevated CEA levels and the emergence of RAS mutations underscores the significance of tumor heterogeneity and clonal evolution in the development of treatment resistance. Although our findings suggest the potential value of incorporating ctDNA-based genetic monitoring into personalized treatment strategies, the results should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size. Further validation in larger, prospective cohorts will be essential to clarify the biological mechanisms underlying these associations and to determine how such insights can be translated into improved therapeutic strategies for patients with mCRC.
