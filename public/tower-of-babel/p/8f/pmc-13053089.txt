A 69-year-old man presented to a dermatology clinic with a 1-day history of a painful erythematous rash. Four days before the rash appeared, he had begun Helicobacter pylori eradication therapy with amoxicillin, clarithromycin, and omeprazole. Staff at a local clinic suspected drug eruption as the reason for the rash and discontinued the antibiotic regimen. Given the marked leukocytosis (22.9 [normal 3.5 to 9.5] × 109/L), however, they prescribed intravenous azlocillin sodium (3 g, twice daily). The patient’s rash progressively worsened, with pustule formation. The patient reported no history of drug allergies and no notable medical history other than hypertension.

Two days later, the patient presented to our outpatient dermatology clinic and was admitted that same day to our service. On his admission, he reported a tingling sensation on his skin and we noted swelling in his lower limbs. His vital signs were temperature 36.7 °C, peripheral oxygen saturation [SpO2] of 97% on room air, heart rate 92 beats/min, blood pressure 141/68 mm Hg, and respiratory rate 16 breaths/min. On further examination, we observed diffuse erythema involving more than 90% body surface area with scattered, nonfollicular, pinhead-sized pustules, predominantly on the flexural areas of the lower extremities (Figure 1). We noted marked swelling of the patient’s lower extremities. Cardiac examination revealed no murmurs, and lung auscultation demonstrated an absence of wheezes or crackles. Our initial diagnosis was acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP) induced by amoxicillin and exacerbated by azlocillin. We discontinued intravenous azlocillin sodium and initiated topical corticosteroids plus subcutaneous enoxaparin sodium (4000 anti-factor Xa IU daily) for thromboprophylaxis.

Diffuse erythema on the (A) left thigh and (B) right lower leg of a 69-year-old man with acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis, with pinpoint pustules visible on the flexural surface (blue arrow).

The next day, the patient had a fever (38.4°C), with an SpO2 of 97% on room air. Laboratory investigations showed leukocytosis (18.27 [normal 3.5 to 9.5] × 109/L) with a normal eosinophil count (0.37 [normal 0.02 to 0.52] × 109/L), normal hepatic enzymes (alanine transaminase 32 [normal 15 to 40] IU/L, aspartate transaminase 39 [normal 9 to 50] IU/L), normal creatinine (66 [normal 62 to 115] μmol/L), elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (29 [normal 0 to 15] mm/h), elevated level of C-reactive protein (178.1 [normal < 5] mg/L), and normal procalcitonin level (0.3 [normal < 0.5] μg/L). We collected blood samples for culture and prescribed methylprednisolone sodium succinate at a dosage of 40 mg/d (the patient’s weight was 80 kg).

On the third day of his admission, the patient’s body temperature normalized and his lower limb edema and skin rash improved, but his facial erythema and edema were more pronounced. On the same day, the patient reported shortness of breath and dry cough. His temperature rose again to 38.8°C. His SpO2 dropped to 85% on room air and improved to 92% with supplemental oxygen therapy via nasal cannula at 5 L/min. His respiratory rate was 31 breaths/min. Subsequent arterial blood gas analysis revealed arterial pressure of oxygen (Pao2) was low (61 [normal 82 to 102] mm Hg), arterial pressure of carbon dioxide (Paco2) was low (32 [normal 35 to 46] mm Hg), pH was high (7.5 [normal 7.35 to 7.45]), oxygen saturation was normal (SpO2 95.1% [normal 91.9% to 99%]), bicarbonate was normal (24.2 [normal 22 to 27] mmol/L), and base excess was normal (1.6 [normal −3 to 3] mmol/L). Laboratory results revealed an elevated leukocyte count (25.6 × 109/L). We urgently convened multidisciplinary consultations with critical care, respiratory, and cardiology teams and proceeded with additional investigations, including echocardiography, lower extremity venous Doppler ultrasonography, and computed tomography (CT) pulmonary angiography. The left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) on echocardiography was 65%, with no venous thrombosis on lower limb venous Doppler and no evidence of thromboembolism on pulmonary CT angiography. Chest CT showed bilateral pleural effusions with adjacent compressive atelectasis, bilateral pulmonary inflammatory changes, and localized interstitial pulmonary edema (Figure 2). We prescribed 20 mg of intravenous furosemide. We did not observe improvement in the patient’s dyspnea.

(A) Computed tomography (CT) of the chest showed bilateral pleural effusions (arrow) with adjacent compressive atelectasis, bilateral pulmonary inflammatory changes, and localized interstitial pulmonary edema (left column). (B) Follow-up chest CT demonstrated improvement (right column).

On the fourth day of admission, the patient continued to exhibit fever, shortness of breath, and facial redness with swelling. No pathogens were detected by peripheral blood metagenomic next-generation sequencing. We increased methylprednisolone to 60 mg/d. The patient’s symptoms rapidly improved (Figure 3). The following day, his dyspnea and tachypnea resolved. The highest respiratory rate of the day was 25 breaths/min. His Pao2 improved to 73 mm Hg with 5 L/min of oxygen via nasal cannula. On day 13 of his admission, chest CT showed complete resolution of pulmonary inflammation (Figure 2). Before discharge, the patient no longer required oxygen therapy. We discharged him on day 14 of admission and prescribed oral prednisone (50 mg/d). We advised him to strictly avoid all β-lactam antibiotics. The corticosteroid was gradually tapered, with complete discontinuation 1 month after discharge. We recently followed up with the patient and he reported doing well, with no recurrence of the skin rash or pulmonary symptoms.

Discussion

Acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis is a severe cutaneous adverse drug reaction,1 characterized by sterile, nonfollicular, pinhead-sized pustules on an erythematous base. Neutrophil infiltration is a pathological hallmark of AGEP. The interval between drug exposure and onset of the rash ranges from 1 to 11 days. Antibiotics (particularly β-lactams, β-lactamase inhibitors, and cephalosporins); hydroxychloroquine; and antiviral, antifungal, and antineoplastic agents are the common causative drugs.2 Compared with Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS), toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) or drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), AGEP is less frequently systemic and has a better prognosis, with a mortality of less than 1%. Management of AGEP includes discontinuation of the causative drug and treatment with topical corticosteroids and, in severe cases, systemic corticosteroids.

Although systemic involvement is infrequent in AGEP, liver, kidney, and pulmonary involvement and agranulocytosis can occur. In 2 separate cohorts, the incidence of pulmonary involvement in AGEP was reported as 3%3 and 27%,4 respectively. Pulmonary involvement in AGEP occurs 3 to 7 days after onset of the rash.3,5 Most patients present with respiratory failure. Lung imaging typically shows bilateral pleural effusions. Increased neutrophil counts can be seen in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. A reduced LVEF may also occur concurrently. Pulmonary complications can also occur in other severe cutaneous adverse reactions (such as DRESS6 and SJS or TEN7) and in other dermatoses characterized by predominant neutrophilic infiltration (such as generalized pustular psoriasis).8

Our patient developed dyspnea on day 6 following onset of the rash, accompanied by recurrent fever and nonproductive cough. Arterial blood gas analysis was consistent with type I respiratory failure (hypoxemia and hypocapnia). Peripheral blood samples showed markedly elevated leukocyte and C-reactive protein levels. Chest CT demonstrated pleural effusion and pulmonary interstitial edema. After excluding pulmonary embolism, infection, and heart failure, we established the diagnosis of AGEP with pulmonary involvement. Typically, AGEP resolves spontaneously after drug withdrawal. However, our patient developed respiratory failure 3 days after stopping the drug. The concurrent return of fever, leukocytosis, and worsening facial rash indicated that the initial 0.5 mg/kg corticosteroid dose was insufficient to manage the systemic inflammation. Therefore, the dose was increased, resulting in clinical improvement.

Notably, among the 3 reported cases of AGEP with pulmonary involvement where the causative factors were clearly identified, 2 were associated with amoxicillin rechallenge.3 The third patient developed a rash, fever, and elevated leukocyte count after using cefprozil, and the antibiotic was subsequently switched to piperacillin–tazobactam and moxifloxacin.5 Therefore, avoiding re-exposure to the causative drug and preventing the misuse of antibiotics for leukocytosis and fever (which are symptoms that can be caused by the drug eruption itself) are important factors in preventing pulmonary involvement in AGEP.

In summary, we describe a patient with AGEP complicated by acute respiratory failure. Prompt identification of pulmonary involvement secondary to AGEP, while ruling out infectious causes, followed by timely escalation of systemic corticosteroid dosage, rapidly controlled both respiratory symptoms and the systemic inflammatory response.
