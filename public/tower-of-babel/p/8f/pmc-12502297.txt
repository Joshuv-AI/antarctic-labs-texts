Endothelial cell-derived apoptotic bodies modulate innate and adaptive immune responses during inflammation
Authors: Caitlin L. Vella, Pamali Fonseka, Emma J. Grant, Stephanie F. Rutter, Donia Y. Abeid, Dilara C. Ozkocak, Tien K. Nguyen, Antony Vinh, Stephanie Paone, Grant R. Drummond, Christopher G. Sobey, Stephanie Gras, Mark D. Hulett, Suresh Mathivanan, Ivan K. H. Poon, Amy A. Baxter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Endothelial cells (ECs) act as gatekeepers and signalling hubs that coordinate communication between blood vessels and surrounding tissues by regulating vascular tone, immune responses and numerous other physiological processes. During vascular inflammation commonly associated with aging, atherosclerosis, diabetes and autoimmunity, a range of biological, environmental and physical stressors can induce activation and apoptosis of ECs. Apoptotic bodies (ApoBDs) are large (~ 1–5 μm), membrane‑bound extracellular vesicles generated solely through apoptotic cell disassembly, that are increasingly recognised as mediators of intercellular communication via the transfer of bioactive molecules to target cells. Although EC apoptosis is a central feature of vascular inflammatory disorders, the formation of EC‑derived ApoBDs and their immunomodulatory roles when formed in an inflammatory environment, remains poorly defined. This study aimed to characterise the functional properties of EC‑derived ApoBDs generated under inflammatory conditions in vitro. A proteomics analysis of EC‑derived ApoBDs revealed that EC‑ApoBDs generated during inflammation (‘iApoBDs’) were enriched in inflammatory cytokines/chemokines, adhesion molecules and antigen presentation machinery compared with non-inflammatory (‘ApoBD’) controls. Functionally, iApoBDs promoted monocyte chemotaxis via the release of MCP-1, while altered expression of the adhesion molecule ICAM-1 enhanced efferocytosis by macrophages in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, iApoBDs generated from antigen-pulsed HUVECs promoted IFN‑𝛾 expression by peptide specific CD8 T cells in an in vitro model of antigen presentation. These findings demonstrate that within an inflammatory setting, apoptotic ECs can participate in continued communication with their environment via the generation of ApoBDs, thereby modulating innate and adaptive immune processes. Introduction

Endothelial cells (ECs) exist as a monolayer that lines the entire vascular network, regulating blood flow and maintaining a permeable barrier between the blood and surrounding tissue. Although EC turnover occurs slowly under steady state conditions [1]biological, environmental and physical stressors arising during vascular aging as well as pathologies such as diabetes, systemic lupus erythematosus and atherosclerosis, can significantly increase EC death including via apoptosis, often occurring in a highly inflammatory environment [2–5]. During the pathogenesis of these disorders, exposure to a range of factors in circulation such as oxidised lipids, inflammatory cytokines, reactive oxygen species and glucose, or physical factors such as turbulent blood flow and low shear stress, contribute to endothelial dysfunction, increased EC activation and apoptosis [3, 6, 7]. The resultant endothelial damage weakens vessels through promoting loss of barrier function, leading to increased immune cell and lipid infiltration into the subendothelial space, or the initiation of thrombotic events through the acquisition of procoagulant membrane properties [8, 9]. Although ECs can communicate through a complex array of membrane-associated signalling processes, secreted factors and mechanosensing, their ability to interact with their environment during cell death is not fully understood.

During apoptosis, cells undergo a tightly regulated fragmentation process resulting in the release of large membrane-bound cellular fragments known as apoptotic bodies (ApoBDs), a class of extracellular vesicle. The formation of ApoBDs, termed ‘apoptotic cell disassembly’ is characterised by three distinct steps: an initial plasma membrane blebbing phase followed by the formation of plasma membrane protrusions and, finally, the release of distinct cell fragments (‘ApoBDs’) from the parent cell [10, 11]. The biological significance of ApoBDs has been demonstrated in several recent in vivo studies in which the intercellular transfer of bioactive cargo from ApoBDs resulted in functional outcomes in recipient cells, including in the settings of viral infection, embryonic development and inflammation [12–14]. These findings highlight the concept that apoptotic cells under both physiological and pathological conditions are capable of mediating continued communication with their environment through the release and transfer of ApoBDs that may be either beneficial or detrimental to the host. As the endothelium is in constant contact with circulating and migrating immune cells, examining the formation of EC-derived ApoBDs and their interactions with surrounding tissue could extend our understanding of endothelial-immune crosstalk and how this may impact the progression of vascular diseases and aging.

In this study, we sought to investigate the functional properties of ApoBDs generated by ECs in an inflammatory environment, by employing an in vitro model of ‘inflammatory EC apoptosis’. ApoBDs generated within this model displayed an altered phenotype that was pro‑adhesive and pro‑inflammatory. Based on multiple lines of evidence, we demonstrate herein that EC‑derived ApoBDs can act as immunomodulatory vesicles with pleiotropic effects on the immune system and may serve as targets for therapeutic intervention during inflammatory vascular diseases.

Methods

Cell culture

Human umbilical vein ECs (HUVEC, Lonza) and human aortic endothelial cells (HAEC, Lonza) were cultured in base EBMTM‑2 EC growth media supplemented with EGM Bullet kit containing human epidermal growth factor (hEGF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), R3‑insulin‑like growth factor‑1 (R3‑IGF‑1), ascorbic acid, hydrocortisone, human fibroblast growth factor‑beta (Hfgf-β), heparin, fetal bovine serum (FBS, Bovogen) and gentamicin/amphotericin B (GA) (Lonza). HUVECs /HAECs were passaged every 3–4 days and cultured in 75 cm² U‑shape cell culture flasks (Corning). All experiments were performed on ECs under ten passages. THP-1 human leukemia monocytic cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) medium (Life Technologies) supplemented with 5% (vol/vol) heat inactivated FBS and J774 mouse macrophages were cultured in high glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) heat inactivated FBS. Media for both cell lines was supplemented with streptomycin (50 µg/mL) and penicillin (50 U/mL) (Life Technologies). All cell lines were cultured in media containing MycoZap (Lonza) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humidified CellXpert 170i incubator (Eppendorf). EC lines were passaged with 0.25% (weight/vol) trypsin with 0.5 mM EDTA (Thermo Fisher) and seeded 24 h before experimentation to allow them to adhere.

In vitro model of inflammatory cell death

HUVECs or HAECs were seeded onto 10–15 cm dishes 24 h prior to experimentation and allowed to adhere. To induce apoptosis, the BH3-mimetic cocktail of ABT-737 (2 µM)/S63845 (500 nM) was used, denoted ‘ABT-S’. Cells were treated with ABT-S in normal growth medium and incubated for 2 h. To induce apoptosis under inflammatory conditions, cells were pre-treated with recombinant TNF (50 ng/mL, Abcam) for 24 h prior to ABT-S treatment. Media was then removed, and cells were washed twice with 1x PBS before adding ABT-S treatment.

Animal studies

All animal experiments were approved by the La Trobe University Animal Ethics Committee (AEC21034). For plasma analysis experiments and efferocytosis experiments, wild type (WT) C57Bl/6 mice (aged between 6 and 20 weeks) were obtained from M. Hulett breeding lines (La Trobe University, Bundoora VIC Australia).

Analysis of EC-ApoBDs from whole mouse blood

WT C57Bl/6 mice aged 6 weeks or 20 weeks were humanely killed by CO2 asphyxiation and blood was collected immediately via cardiac puncture into heparinised collection tubes. Blood was diluted 1/5 with sterile 1 x PBS and centrifuged at 100 g for 20 min to separate erythrocytes. The top erythrocyte-free layer was carefully collected and centrifuged at 3,000 g for 10 min to sediment ApoBDs. Samples were stained with Vybrant FAM (‘FLICA’) Caspase‑3 marker (Thermo Fisher) as per manufacturer’s instructions, and resuspended in FACS buffer (2 mM EDTA, 10% FBS, 1x PBS). Samples were incubated with Fc Block for 15 min at 4 °C followed by antibody staining panel to determine endothelial cell-derived ApoBDs: CD45.2-APC (clone 104, Thermo Fisher), CD11b-e450 (clone M1/70, Thermo Fisher), CD41-PerCP Cy5.5 (clone MWReg30, Biolegend), CD146-PE Cy7 (clone ME-9F1, Biolegend), CD31-PE (clone 390, Biolegend) for 20 min at 4 °C and analysed by flow cytometry (BD 3-laser Canto II). Events identified as CD45.2−/CD11b−/CD41−/FSClow/CD146+/CD31+/FLICAhigh were defined as endothelial cell-derived apoptotic bodies. Spherotech AccuCount counting beads were used to determine absolute number of events per mL whole mouse blood.

Flow cytometry-based analysis of apoptotic ECs

Following the induction of apoptosis, samples were gently pipetted to lift any remaining cells and stained with A5‑FITC (1:200, BD Biosciences) and TO-PRO-3 (1 µM, Life Technologies) in 1x A5 binding buffer for 10 min at 4°C. Cell populations (i.e. viable, apoptotic, necrotic) were determined using flow cytometry gating as previously described [15]. For pan caspase‑inhibition assays, HUVECs or HAECs were seeded into a 24‑well plate at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well. Cells were pre-incubated with Q-VD-OPh (50 µM, SigmaAldrich) or DMSO for 1 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2 before induction of apoptosis with ABT-S. In adhesion molecule expression experiments, samples were stained with antibodies against ICAM-1 (clone HA58, Thermo Fisher), VCAM-1 (clone STA, Thermo Fisher), E-selectin (clone P2H3, Thermo Fisher) and P-selectin (clone PselK02.3, Thermo Fisher) following TO-PRO-3 and A5 staining. In HLA surface expression assays, samples were derived from single donor HUVECs (donor #809, Lonza) and were stained with HLA-I‑BV421 Ab (clone W6/32, BD Biosciences) following TO-PRO-3 and A5 staining. All antibodies were stained for 20 min at 4°C.

ApoBD isolation via differential centrifugation

After apoptosis induction as described above, any remaining adhered cells were lifted from dish via gentle pipetting, transferred to 14 mL polypropylene tubes and centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min. Cell pellets were discarded, supernatants were transferred to clean tubes and centrifuged at 3,000 g for 10 min. Supernatants were discarded, cell pellets were gently resuspended and washed with 10 ml 1x PBS, followed by a repeated centrifugation at 3,000 g for 10 min. Supernatants were discarded and cell pellets were collected. ApoBDs collected from cells treated with ABT-S are denoted ‘ApoBDs’ and ApoBDs collected from cells pre-treated with ABT-S and TNF are denoted ‘iApoBDs’.

Normalisation of ApoBD counts

For each preparation of ApoBDs, equal volumes of ApoBDs and iApoBDs in EGM-2 were prepared in combination with Spherotech AccuCount counting beads (20 µl/20,000 beads per sample) and analysed by flow cytometry to determine absolute number of ApoBDs per condition. Where specified, an equal number of ApoBDs/iApoBDs was then used per experiment.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy

ApoBD/iApoBD generation by HUVECs

2 × 104 HUVECs per well were seeded into a Nunc™ Lab‑Tak™ II 8‑well chamber slide in EGM-2 media (Nunc). Apoptosis was induced in cells as described above with the addition of A5-FITC (1:200, BD Biosciences) in 0.5x A5 binding buffer (BD Biosciences). Stains and drugs were added simultaneously, and endpoint images were taken using the Zeiss 900 confocal spinning disk microscope equipped with an electronically switchable illumination and detection (ESID) module (Zeiss). To observe blebbing formation, images were taken using a Zeiss Axio Observer Z1 microscope equipped with an Axiocam 506 monochrome digital camera and Zen software (Carl Zeiss Ltd). All confocal microscopy experiments were conducted at 37 °C in humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Images were processed using ZEN imaging software (Zeiss).

Quantification of ApoBDs/iApoBDs via live cell imaging

ApoBDs/iApoBDs were generated and isolated as described above. NuncTM Lab‑TakTM II 8‑well chamber slides were pre-coated in 0.01% (w/v) poly-L-lysine to allow vesicles to adhere. Vesicles were stained with A5‑FITC (1:50, BD Biosciences) and TO-PRO-3 (1 µM, Life Technologies) in 1x A5 binding buffer. 5 × 5 tile regions were imaged using the Zeiss 900 confocal spinning disk microscope. Vesicle diameters were quantified based of A5 staining, where regions of interest were exported as binary images to measure feret diameter in ImageJ (version 2.14.0).

Engulfment assay via live cell imaging

5 × 104 phagocytes per well (J774 murine macrophages) were seeded into Nunc™ Lab‑Tak™ II 8‑well chamber slide in complete DMEM media one day prior to allow adherence to slide. HUVECs were seeded two days prior at a ratio of 5:1 (EC: phagocyte). For preparation of iApoBDs, HUVECs were treated with TNF one day prior, as described above. On day of experiment, HUVECs were stained with CypHer 5E (4 mg/ml, Cytiva) in serum-free RPMI for 45 min at 37 °C, washed twice with warm PBS and treated with ABT-S followed by isolation of ApoBDs or iApoBDs as described above. Phagocytes were stained with CFSE for 20 min at 37 °C and washed twice with PBS. ApoBDs or iApoBDs were then resuspended in complete DMEM. ApoBD/iApoBD counts were normalised as described above and equal numbers were added to phagocytes. Samples were centrifuged for 5 min at 1000 g and co-incubated for 1 h, washed twice gently with PBS to remove non-engulfed particles and imaged using the Zeiss 900 confocal spinning disk microscope.

Immunoblotting

HUVECs were seeded into 15 cm plates and induced to undergo apoptosis as described above. Samples were lysed in cell lysis buffer containing 1% Nonidet P‑40, 10% glycerol, 1% TX‑100, 150mM NaCl, 20mM HEPES (pH 7.4) and protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma Aldrich) on ice for 30 min and centrifuged at 13,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove insoluble material. Cell lysates were collected and stored in ‑80 °C until needed. Protein concentration was determined using a Pierce Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was read at 562 nm on a SpectraMax M5e plate reader (Bio‑Strategy). Prior to SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis, cell lysates were incubated with dithiothreitol (DTT, Astral Scientific) and lithium dodecyl sulphate (LDS, Life Technologies) at 70 °C for 10 min, then separated on a 4–12% BisTris gel at 120 V for 5 min followed by 190 V for 30 min. Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes were activated with 100% methanol before protein transfer in NuPAGE transfer buffer (Life Technologies) at 20 V for 60 min. Following transfer, the membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk powder in 1 x PBS/0.1% Tween (0.1% PBS‑T) for 2 h at RT. Membranes were washed thrice with 0.1% PBST before being incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C diluted in 3% BSA/0.1% PBS‑T. Primary antibodies include rabbit Pannexin-1 (1:1000, clone D9M1C, Cell Signalling Technology), rabbit caspase-3 (1:1000, clone 8G10, Cell Signalling Technology), rabbit cleaved caspase-3 (1:1000, clone Asp175, Cell Signalling Technology) and mouse β-actin (1:5000, clone AC-74, Sigma Aldrich). Membranes were washed three times again with 0.1% PBS‑T and incubated 1 h at RT with a HR‑conjugated secondary antibody diluted in 3% BSA/0.1% PBST. Secondary antibodies include donkey anti-rabbit-HRP (1:5000, GE Healthcare) and sheep anti-mouse-HRP (1:5000, GE healthcare). Secondary antibodies were removed with a final three 10 min washes and detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) prime reagent (Bio-Strategy) and imaged using a G:BOX Chemi XL 1.4 system and GeneSys software (Syngene).

Cytokine antibody array

Human Cytokine Antibody Array (42 targets, Abcam) was used as per manufacturer instructions. Briefly, ECs were grown in 15 cm round tissue culture plates and ApoBDs and iApoBDs were generated and collected as described above. ApoBD/iApoBD lysates were prepared using cell lysis buffer provided by the kit. Protein concentration was determined via BCA assay and 200 µg protein per condition were loaded onto membranes, incubated and probed with HRP-antibodies. Membranes were imaged on G: Box (Syngene). Densitometry analysis was performed in ImageJ and analysed in Prism (GraphPad).

Cytometric bead array

Equal numbers of iApoBDs were incubated over 1–7 h at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Samples were centrifuged at 3,000 g for 20 min to pellet iApoBDs and supernatants were collected. Positive control supernatants were generated by lysing iApoBDs with three consecutive rounds of freeze‑thawing in liquid N2/37°C water bath. Following manufacturer’s instructions, cytokine concentrations were measured using the BD™ Cytometric Bead Array (CBA) Human Soluble Protein Master Buffer kit (cat#558264) and Human IL-6 (cat#558276), IL-8 (cat#558277) and MCP-1 (cat#558287) flex sets (BD Biosciences). Briefly, equal volumes of sample supernatant and mixed capture beads from the flex sets were incubated together in a 96‑well plate for 1 h at room temperature. Following the addition of the detection reagent, the plate was incubated for a further 2 h. The plate was pelleted, supernatant removed, and beads resuspended in wash buffer to be analysed by flow cytometry. Flow cytometry analysis was performed using FCAP Array software (version 3.0.1, BD Biosciences).

Caspase Glo 3/7 assay

1 × 104 HUVECs or HAECs were seeded per well into a black, clear bottom 96 well plate. 2 h following apoptosis induction, 50 µL of CaspaseGlo® 3/7 assay reagent (Promega, cat #G8091) was added to the wells, shaken for 5 min, and incubated at RT for 1 h. Luminescence was recorded on the SpectraMax M5e plate reader (Bio‑Strategy) and data was analysed by subtracting media only background control measurement from the samples.

Transwell migration assay

Equal numbers of ApoBDs or iApoBDs (based on a 5:1 ratio of EC starting cells to THP-1 monocytes) in 5% RPMI media were added to each lower well of a 24‑well Transwell plate (Corning), with 6.5 mm diameter and 5.0 μm pore size polycarbonate membrane inserts. 2 × 105 THP‑1 cells in normal 5% RPMI growth medium were transferred to the upper chambers of the plates and incubated across a time series at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Negative (untreated) and positive (1 µM ATP) control wells were loaded with fresh 5% RPMI media or 1 µM ATP in 5% RPMI respectively. Migrating cells were collected at 3, 6 and 9 h from the lower well and incubated with Fc Block (4 °C, 10 min), stained with CD45.2 mAb (4 °C, 20 min, clone 5B1, Miltenyi Biotec) and analysed by flow cytometry. In neutralising assays, ApoBDs were preincubated with MCP-1 Ab (25 µg/mL, clone 2H5, Thermo Fisher) or IgG Armenian hamster isotype control (25 µg/mL, clone eBio299Arm, Thermo Fisher) for 10 min at RT before being loaded into the bottom chamber.

LDH cytotoxicity assay

ApoBD lysis was measured using a lactate dehydrogenase kit (LDH, Abcam, cat #ab65393). Following manufacturer’s instructions, 10 µl of supernatant was added to the LDH reaction mix and incubated for 60 min at RT. Spectral absorbance was read using a SpectraMax M5e plate reader (Thermo Fisher) at OD 450 nm.

Static adhesion assay

Target cells (THP-1s or Jurkats) were prepared at 1 × 106/mL in low glucose DMEM supplemented with 0.5% BSA. Endothelial cells were seeded to a ratio of 10:1 (EC: target cells) and stained with CellTrace Violet. ApoBDs or iApoBDs were generated via differential centrifugation as described above. Target cells were then co-incubated with equal numbers of ApoBDs or iApoBDs at 37 °C (30 min). Prior to flow cytometry analysis, samples containing THP-1s were incubated with Fc block (4 °C, 15 min) prior to staining with CD45.2 mAb (4 °C, 20 min) and samples containing Jurkats were stained with CD3 Ab (4 °C, 20 min, clone SK7, BD Biosciences). Samples were then analysed by flow cytometry (BD Canto II) and adhesion events were based on CD45.2+/CTV+ (THP-1) or CD3+/CTV+ (Jurkat) gating.

Proteomics

For sample preparation, ApoBDs and iApoBDs were generated as described above, washed in 1 x PBS, lysed with cell lysis buffer and incubated on ice for 30 min before centrifugation at max speed to collect supernatants. Three biological replicates of each condition were collected. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay and 30 µg of protein per condition was used per sample. In-gel digestion and subsequent liquid chromatography with tandem massspectrometry (LCMS/MS) was then performed as previously described[16]. Data set was analysed by the La Trobe Proteomics Platform using Uniprot as the reference proteome database with one sequence per gene (human database March 2021 version). The common repository of adventitious peptides was used as a marker database to identify laboratory contaminants. Spectra were searched with MS Fragger 3.3 and Sequest search engines using Proteome Discoverer 2.4. Search parameters included (Trypsin (Full), fixed modification of carbamidomethyl of cysteine, variable modifications were oxidation of M, deamidation of N/Q, and N-terminal Acetylation. Precursor mass tolerance was 50 ppm and fragment tolerance was 0.05 Da. Label-free quantitative measurement of peptide precursor intensity was calculated from unique and razor peptides. intensities were normalised on total peptide amount. Peptides were validated at peptide spectral match (PS) and peptide levels according to a strict criteria (0.01 False Discovery Rate (FDR)) and a relaxed criteria (0.05 FDR). The Differential Enrichment analysis of Proteomics data (DEP) R package was used to process and complete statistical tests. Proteins were retained for analysis if detected in at least 2 out of 3 replicates for one treatment. Protein abundance values underwent variance stabilising normalisation using the vsn-package. Missing values were imputed using MinProb function with a q value of 0.01. DEP uses protein-wise linear models combined with empirical Bayes statistics for the differential enrichment analysis.

In vitro engulfment assay

Phagocytes (J774 murine macrophages) were seeded one day prior to allow adherence to tissue culture plates. Phagocytes were stained with CTV or CFSE for 20 min at 37 °C and washed twice with PBS. ApoBDs/iApoBDs pre-stained with CypHer 5E were prepared as described above and added to phagocytes. Either DMSO or Cytochalasin D (5 µM) was added to each phagocyte well and samples were co‑incubated for 1 h. Media was then removed, and wells were washed gently twice with PBS to ensure removal of non-engulfed particles. Phagocytes were suspended with Trypsin/EDTA solution and neutralised with equal volume of complete DMEM. Samples were analysed by flow cytometry (BD 3‑Laser Canto II). Engulfing populations were determined as double‑positive for CFSE/CTV and CypHer 5E staining.

In vivo engulfment assay

HUVECs were stained with CypHer 5E for 40 min at 37 °C and 5% CO2, in serum free RPMI and washed twice with 1x PBS before isolation of ApoBDs/iApoBDs. 1.5 × 105 ApoBDs/iApoBDs were injected into the intraperitoneal cavity of C57BL/6 mice. After 1 h, mice were culled via CO2 and a peritoneal lavage was performed using 5% RPMI media. Samples were passed through a 70 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min. Pellets were resuspended in FACS buffer, incubated with Fc Block for 20 min at 4 °C, then stained with CD45.2 Ab (clone 5B1, Miltenyi Biotec) and F4/80 Ab (clone T45-2342, BD Biosciences) for 20 min at 4 °C, and analysed by flow cytometry. Engulfing macrophages were identified as CD45.2+/F4/80+/CypHer 5E+ cells. For neutralising experiments, iApoBDs isolated from 1.6 × 107 HUVECs per mouse were preincubated with ICAM-1 neutralising Ab (10 µg/mL, clone HA58, Thermo Fisher) or mouse IgG1κ isotype control (clone P3.6.2.8.1, Thermo Fisher) for 10 min at 4 °C before injections. Engulfing macrophages were defined as F4/80+/CypHer 5E+ events. Due to variance in absolute values between independent experiments, isotype control samples were normalised to 100% and ICAM-1 treated samples were expressed as ‘percentage decrease’ compared to isotype controls.

Antigen presentation studies

Whole peripheral blood was obtained from volunteer donors following COVID-19 vaccination. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were seperated using Ficoll density gradient centrifugation as previously described [17, 18]harvested and stored at −80 °C until use. Donor PBMCs were HLA typed by CareDx Pty Ltd (Fremantle, WA). Research was approved by the La Trobe University Human Ethics Committee (HEC21097) and was conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki.

PBMCs from 3 x HLA-A*02:01+ donors who had received > 3 COVID-19 vaccinations, were used to generate peptide-specific CD8+ T cell lines as previously described [19]. Briefly, 1/3 of the PBMCs were pulsed with 10 µM of the known immunodominant “YLQ” peptide (YLQPRTFLL; Genscript) derived from the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 in FBS free media for 90 min. These were washed twice and added to the remaining 2/3 of PBMCs in RPMI (Gibco) supplemented with 1x Penicillin Streptomycin Glutamine (Gibco), 1x non-essential amino acids (100 x NEAA, Gibco), 2 mM L-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich), 5 mM HEPES (Sigma-Aldrich), 50 lM β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich) and foetal bovine serum (FBS, Bovogen), herein referred to as RF10 media. On day 4, 10 IU mL−1 of recombinant human IL‑2 (Peprotech) was added, then again twice weekly.

Single donor HUVECs (Lonza) were pulsed for 1 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2 with S269‑277 (YLQPRTFLL) SARS-CoV-2 spike‑derived peptide (10 µM, GenScript). Cells were washed thrice with 1x PBS to ensure the removal of free peptide before inducing apoptosis as described above. Isolated ApoBDs were resuspended in 5% RPMI media. Equal numbers of ApoBDs or iApoBDs were co‑incubated with 1 × 106 HLA-A*02:01 typed human donor T lymphocytes following a previously published protocol [20, 21]. ApoBDs/iApoBDs and T cells were co-incubated with brefeldin A (10 µg/mL, Sigma Aldrich) for 5 h (37 °C, 5% CO2). The non-specific T cell activator X500 containing 5 ng/mL phorbol myristate acetate and 500 ng/mL ionomycin was used as a positive control. Following incubation, cells were pelleted by centrifugation (300 g, 5 min) and washed with 1x PBS. Samples were stained with CD3‑APC Ab (1:100, clone SK7, BD Biosciences), CD4‑FITC Ab (1:100, clone RPAT4, BD Biosciences), CD8‑PerCPCy5.5 Ab (1:50, clone SK1, BD Biosciences), LIVE/DEADTM‑APC Cy7 (1 µL/mL, Thermo Fisher), CD14‑APC Cy7 (1:10, cloneMoP9, BD Biosciences) and CD19‑APC Cy7 (1:10, clone SJ25C1, BD Biosciences) for 30 min at 4 °C. Cells were washed with 1x PBS and fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde (PFA, SigmaAldrich) at RT for 20 min. Following fixation, samples were washed with 0.2% (weight/vol) saponin (Thermo Fisher) and stained with IFN-𝛾-BV510 (1:300 in 0.2% (weight/vol) saponin, clone B27, BD Biosciences) for 30 min at 4 °C. A final 1x PBS wash step was performed before cells were analysed by flow cytometry. Activation of T cells was determined by gating CD3+/CD8+/IFN-𝛾 events.

As a control, the proportion of CD8+ T cell activation was assessed in an intracellular cytokine staining (ICS) assay as previously described [20]. Briefly, C1R-A2 cells were peptide pulsed with 10 µM of the YLQ peptide (or no peptide control) for 60 min in FCS free media, were washed twice and added in a 1:2 C1R-A2: CD8+ T cell line ratio. Alternatively, 10 µM of the YLQ peptide (or no peptide control) was added to CD8+ T cell lines directly. An additional well with the non-specific T cell activator X500 containing 5 ng/mL phorbol myristate acetate and 500 ng/mL ionomycin was used as a positive control. CD8+ T cell lines were left to stimulate for 5 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in the presence of Golgi-Plug (1:1000, BD Biosciences), Golgi‑Stop (1:1400, BD Biosciences) and CD107a-AF488 Ab (1:200, Clone eBioH4A3, BD Biosciences). Following this, cells were washed and surface stained for 30 min at 4 °C with CD14-APCH7 Ab(1:200; Clone MϕP9, BD Biosciences) CD19-APCH7 Ab (1:200, Clone HIB19, BD Biosciences), CD3-BV480 Ab (1:100, Clone UCHT1, BD Biosciences), CD4-BV650 Ab (1:100, Clone SK3, BD Biosciences), CD8‑PerCpCy5.5 Ab (1:50, Clone SK1, BD Biosciences) and LIVE/DEAD NIR (1:1000, Molecular Probes). Cells were washed and Fix/Perm buffer (BD Biosciences) was added for 20 min at 4 °C. Cells were then washed and intracellular stained for 30 min at 4 °C with anti‑human IFN‑γ‑BV421 (1:100, Clone B27, BD Bioscience), TNF-PeCy7 Ab (1:100, Clone Mab11, BD Biosciences), MIP-1β-APC Ab (1:100, Clone D21-1351, BD Bioscience) and IL-2-PE Ab (1:100, Clone MQ1-17H12, BD Bioscience). Samples were acquired on the BD FACSymphony A3 analyser (BD Biosciences) and were analysed using Flowjo software (version 10, BD Biosciences). Samples were gated as per Figure S4.

Statistical analyses

Statistical significance was measured using various relevant statistical tests including normality testing, where appropriate, as specified within the figure legends of each figure. All analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.2.3, GraphPad Software).

Results

Endothelial cells generate apoptotic bodies under normal and inflammatory conditions

Previous studies have reported that circulating ECs are detectable in plasma at low basal levels, and that their concentration increases in inflammatory states characterised by EC death, such as SLE and atherosclerosis [5, 22, 23]. This indicates that following endothelial damage, dying ECs can be dislodged from the vessel wall and enter circulation. To determine the levels of circulating, free EC-derived ApoBDs formed under homeostatic conditions in the plasma of young (6-weeks) vs. mature (20-weeks) old C57bl/6 WT mice, we employed a flow cytometry-based approach. EC-ApoBDs were identified based on cell surface marker staining and apoptosis marker FLICA, signifying caspase 3/7 activation (Figs. 1A and S1). Using this strategy, an average basal level of 57.55 EC-ApoBDs per mL whole blood in 6-week old mice vs. 159.2 per mL in 20-week old mice (n = 18) were measured (Fig. 1B), demonstrating that in aging vs. young vessels, a higher level of free EC-ApoBDs is detectable.Fig. 1Endothelial cells generate apoptotic bodies in vitro and in vivo under homeostatic and inflammatory conditions. To further characterise the formation of EC-derived ApoBDs and determine their roles in intercellular communication, we established an in vitro model of ‘inflammatory EC apoptosis’ aimed to mimic the environment in which ECs undergo apoptosis during inflammatory vascular pathologies. Given that HUVECs are a well-characterized, readily available EC type that reliably model key aspects of endothelial function, including inflammation, we performed this model and all subsequent experiments on HUVECs. Where specified, key characterisation experiments were validated in HAECs to ensure relevance to arterial endothelium.

HUVECs were induced to undergo apoptosis via BH3-mimetic cocktail ‘ABT-S’ in the presence or absence of a pre-treatment with TNF, a key pro-inflammatory cytokine that mediates many hallmarks of vascular aging and disease, including oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and cell death [24–26]. ABT-S treatment induced a marked increase in caspase 3/7 activation, whilst no significant change in activity was detected between untreated and TNF only treated cells, or between ABT‑S treated cells in the presence or absence of TNF pre‑treatment (Fig. 1C), indicating that TNF does not impact caspase activation in HUVECs at the concentration used. Similar results were observed for HAECs (Figure S2A). Next, an established flow cytometry method based on the combined uptake of nucleic acid dye TO‑PRO‑3 and PtdSer binding protein A5 [15] was used to precisely determine the levels of apoptotic cells, necrotic cells, and ApoBDs generated by HUVECs via this model (Fig. 1D/E). Comparable levels of apoptotic cells and ApoBDs were detected in TNF and non-TNF conditions, with minimal necrosis detected. The presence of pan-caspase inhibitor Q-VD-OPh completely inhibited cell death and ApoBD formation, indicating that fragmentation was occurring in a caspase-dependent manner (Fig. 1E). A comparable distribution of apoptotic cells and ApoBDs was observed for HAECs (Figure S2B), indicating that both venous and arterial ECs undergo apoptosis and generate ApoBDs similarly under the tested conditions, although ABT-S + TNF-treated HAECs displayed a slightly higher degee of necrosis. To visualise ApoBD formation, HUVECs were treated with ABT-S in the presence and absence of TNF and monitored via live cell imaging in the presence of A5 to stain for phosphatidylserine (PtdSer) exposed on ApoBDs. By 2 h post treatment, cells under both conditions displayed typical apoptotic cell morphology including the presence of multiple A5-positive ApoBDs (Fig. 1F). Together, these data demonstrate that ECs form ApoBDs under homeostatic and inflammatory conditions in vivo. Further, that EC-derived ApoBDs generated in vitro are morphologically similar when generated under normal and inflammatory conditions.

Endothelial cell-derived apoptotic bodies generated during inflammation (‘iApoBDs’) express distinct protein cargo

The production of the inflammatory cytokine TNF by ECs and immune cells, such as through exposure to glucose, LDLs, other cytokines or mechachanical stress, contributes to endothelial activation, dysfunction and apoptosis [3, 27]. Within this inflammatory niche, ECs upregulate cytokines, chemokines and adhesion molecules that promote a loss of barrier function leading to vascular permeability and give rise to leukocyte infiltration, thrombotic events and other disruptions in tissue homeostasis. To address if these changes are passed from parental cell to their ApoBDs during apoptosis, we employed our in vitro model of inflammatory EC apoptosis to examine the cargo and functional properties of EC-derived ApoBDs generated under homeostatic and inflammatory conditions. Following cell death induction, samples were subjected to differential centrifugation as illustrated in Fig. 2A, to generate ApoBD-enriched samples. ApoBDs generated by TNF-treated cells were denoted ‘inflammatory ApoBDs (iApoBDs)’. Apoptotic samples not subject to differential centrifugation were instead centrifuged as a whole at 3,000 g and defined as ‘whole apoptotic sample (WAS)’. ApoBD purity was assessed via flow cytometry based on enrichment of forward scatter (FSC)low events in apoptotic samples subject to differential centrifugation (Fig. 2B). In WAS samples, approximately 65% of events were gated as FSClow, while approximately 95% of events were FCSlow following differential centrifugation, representing enrichment of ApoBDs/iApoBDs (Fig. 2C). No significant differences in proportion of FSClow events was observed between TNF and non-TNF conditions in WAS or ApoBD samples, indicating that TNF treatment did not impact ApoBD size, with similar results observed in HAECs (Figure S2C/D). Size distribution of ApoBDs vs. iApoBDs was further confirmed in HUVECs via live cell imaging of isolated ApoBDs (mean diameter 2.415 μm ± 0.049) and iApoBDs (mean diameter 2.523 μm ± 0.045) (Fig. 2D/E). Comparable levels of A5 staining also confirmed that TNF treatment did not significantly alter expression of PtdSer on HUVEC-derived ApoBDs (Fig. 2F) or HAEC-derived ApoBDs (Figure S2E). In addition, key apoptotic markers, caspase 3 and cleaved pannexin 1, were confirmed in HUVEC WAS fractions via immunoblot analysis. Cleaved pannexin 1 was also observed in ApoBD-enriched fractions, although caspase 3 was not abundant enough to be detected in these fractions (Fig. 2G). These data indicate that isolation of EC-derived ApoBDs under normal and inflammatory conditions via differential centrifugation results in ApoBD/iApoBD yields of approximately 95% purity that are comparable in size and apoptosis markers. To gain insights into the changes in protein cargo of EC-derived ApoBDs that could impact their intercellular communication, an untargeted proteomics analysis was performed on HUVEC-derived ApoBDs and iApoBD lysates. A total of 282 proteins were upregulated and 160 proteins downregulated in iApoBDs compared to ApoBDs (Figure S3). Of those proteins that were upregulated, 37 proteins were significantly enriched to > 1.5-fold, including several with known immunomodulatory properties (Fig. 2H). Nine proteins of interest were grouped into the following categories for further comparison between iApoBDs and ApoBDs: (i) cytokines & chemokines (IL-8, MCP-1 and CXCL1); (ii) adhesion molecules (ICAM-1 and E-Selectin); and (iii) antigen presentation machinery (TAP-1, HLA-B, HLA-F and HLA-H* (*putative)) (Fig. 2I).

iApoBDs promote chemotaxis, adhesion and efferocytosis

To further examine the cytokine and chemokine cargo of iApoBDs, a cytokine antibody array was performed on the lysates of ApoBDs and iApoBDs. Significant enrichment of several inflammatory cytokines/chemokines were noted in iApoBD including MCP-1, IL-6 and IL‑8, with a trending increase in CXCL1/CXCL1a (Fig. 3A). To determine whether cytokines/chemokines were being released from iApoBDs over time, the levels of IL-6, IL-8 and MCP-1 were measured in the supernatants of iApoBDs that were incubated over 1, 3 or 5 or 7 h, via cytometric bead array. All three cytokines/chemokines were detected in supernatants with concentrations increasing in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 3B-D). We have previously reported that the stability of ApoBDs generated in culture declines over time, with T cell- and monocyte-derived ApoBD membranes lysing over 3–6 h post formation [28]. Thus, to examine whether their release occurred simultaneously with membrane lysis, the release of the 140 kDa cytosolic protein LDH from lysates of the same iApoBDs was measured at corresponding time points (Fig. 3E), and was found to significantly correlate with cytokine/chemokine release (Fig. 3F-H), suggesting the release of cytokines from iApoBDs could be mediated through vesicle lysis.

Excessive transendothelial migration of leukocytes, initiated by chemotactic signalling from the activated endothelium is implicated throughout a wide range of pathologies including atherosclerosis [29, 30] rheumatoid arthritis [31] and systemic lupus erythematosus [32]. We next tested the ability of cytokine/chemokines contained within ApoBDs and iApoBDs to promote monocyte chemotaxis. iApoBDs promoted the migration of THP-1 monocytes in a time‑dependent manner (Fig. 3I). MCP-1 is a prominent chemokine highly expressed by ECs at atherosclerotic lesion sites that plays a key role in recruiting monocytes and is associated with plaque vulnerability [33, 34]. We therefore reasoned that MCP-1 was a likely source of chemoattraction. Indeed, monocyte migration was attenuated in the presence of an MCP-1 neutralising antibody (Fig. 3J). Interestingly, ApoBDs alone were also able to promote monocyte chemotaxis above background levels of the ‘untreated’ control condition, albeit to a lesser degree (Fig. 3I). Given the relatively high basal expression of MCP-1 in unstimulated EC-derived ApoBDs (Fig. 3A), this could have been sufficient to promote the observed migration under this condition.

Adhesion molecules including ICAM-1, VCAM-1, E-selectin and P-selectin become upregulated on the activated endothelium during vascular pathologies and collectively play critical roles in leukocyte binding, tethering, rolling and migration [35]. Our proteomics analysis revealed ICAM-1 as the most enriched protein in iApoBDs (Figure S3), whilst E-selectin was also significantly enriched. To examine the surface expression of adhesion molecules on HUVEC-derived ApoBDs and iApoBDs, a flow cytometry-based immunofluorescence approach was employed. iApoBDs displaying significantly heightened expression of all adhesion molecules tested, compared to ApoBDs (Fig. 4A-D). Comparable expression profiles were observed in HAEC-derived ApoBDs/iApoBDs (Figure S4). Functionally, HUVEC-derived iApoBDs also displayed increased adhesion to THP-1 monocytes and Jurkat T cells, as determined via a flow cytometry-based static adhesion assay (Fig. 4E-F).

ICAM-1 expression on macrophages has been reported to promote efferocytosis via mediating enhanced binding to apoptotic cells [36] although its ability to facilitate efferocytosis via expression on apoptotic cells or ApoBDs, has not been demonstrated. Considering macrophages express a key ICAM-1 receptor, lymphocyte function associated antigen-1 (LFA‑1) and since ICAM-1 displayed the highest increase in expression on iApoBDs of all adhesion molecules tested (Fig. 4D), we questioned whether iApoBDs may promote enhanced efferocytosis by macrophages via increased ICAM-1 expression. As determined by an in vitro engulfment assay, J774 murine macrophages displayed significantly elevated engulfment of iApoBDs compared to ApoBDs (Fig. 4G-H), with internalisation of CypHer 5E+ ApoBDs/iApoBDs into J774s visualised via live cell imaging (Figure S5). To test a specific role for ICAM-1 in iApoBD efferocytosis, an ICAM-1 neutralising antibody was employed, revealing a partial reduction in engulfment compared to isotype control (Fig. 4I). This observation was also confirmed in vivo using a model of intraperitoneal macrophage‑mediated efferocytosis (Fig. 4J). As shown in Fig. 4K, ~ 35% of intraperitoneal macrophages engulfed ApoBDs compared to ∼60% engulfing iApoBDs. This effect was significantly reduced in the presence of ICAM-1 neutralising antibody (Fig. 4L). These findings suggest that under inflammatory conditions, elevated levels of ICAM-1 expression on EC-derived ApoBDs may aid their clearance by macrophages.

iApoBDs participate in direct antigen presentation

In addition to adhesion molecules, activated ECs can upregulate expression of a range of proteins required for antigen presentation, including Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) class I and II molecules and co-stimulatory molecules [37, 38]. The role of ECs as nonprofessional antigen presenting cells (APCs) is most widely characterised in the context of allograft rejection, where ECs can present donor antigens to recipient immune cells [39, 40]. However, increased expression of HLA proteins and costimulatory factors on ECs at atherosclerotic lesions [41] also supports a role of ECs in adaptive immune processes during atherosclerosis development. Given the increased expression of HLA and TAP1 proteins in iApoBDs identified in our proteomics data set (Fig. 2F‑G), we hypothesized that EC-derived ApoBDs may also participate in antigen presentation. Surface expression of HLA on ApoBDs and iApoBDs derived from single-donor HUVECs was determined by flow cytometry, with significantly increased expression of surface HLA‑I detected after TNF stimulation (Fig. 5A-B). To demonstrate the ability of ApoBDs and iApoBDs to elicit CD8+ T cell activation via direct antigen presentation, an in vitro proofofconcept model of antigen presentation was established using peptide-specific CD8+ T cell lines (Fig. 5C). PBMCs from COVID-19 vaccinated HLA-A*02:01+ individuals (n = 3) were peptide pulsed with the known immunodominant YLQ peptide derived from the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 [42] to establish peptide-specific CD8+ T cell lines[19]. Co‑incubation of donor CD8+ T cells with ApoBDs or iApoBDs generated from spike-derived ‘YLQ’ peptide-pulsed HUVECs promoted IFN‑γ production in all three donor T cell lines tested, with a more pronounced response seen in the iApoBD condition (Fig. 5D‑E; full gating strategy shown in Figure S6). The proportion of CD8+ T cell responses was consistent with a control intracellular cytokine staining (ICS) assay (Figure S7). Discussion

Within the vasculature, inflammation is a major driver of disrupted tissue homeostasis and is frequently associated with EC death, particularly through apoptosis. This study investigated how inflamed, dying ECs communicate with immune cells through the release and trafficking of ApoBDs, shedding light on the potential role of this EV subtype in driving the progression of inflammatory vascular diseases. Our model of inflammatory EC apoptosis demonstrated that EC-derived iApoBDs displayed increased expression of adhesion molecules and enhanced adhesion to monocytes and T cells. They also released inflammatory cytokines and chemokines via membrane lysis and promoted MCP‑1‑dependent chemotaxis of monocytes. This work expands on the concept that, once released from apoptotic cells, ApoBDs, typically deemed as immunologically silent, may instead become propagators of inflammatory signalling via the trafficking of bioactive cargo from their parental cells. These findings are supported by previous work that investigated the ability of dying ECs to mediate immune signalling via their ApoBDs. In this former study, HUVEC-derived ApoBDs containing active IL-1α could induce IL-8 and MCP-1 production in healthy HUVECs in vitro and promote neutrophil recruitment in vivo [7]. Within the activated endothelium, how newly formed ApoBDs communicate with their environment, that is, by interacting locally versus being released into circulation, is unknown. Enhanced adhesive properties or other membrane modifications on ApoBDs could enable their retention at the site of cell death via binding to neighbouring ECs [43, 44]. Over time, liberated cytokines and chemokines could promote further activation and immune cell recruitment. In the context of atherosclerosis development, nascent ApoBDs could bind to immune cells migrating across the endothelial wall and be delivered into developing plaque sites to release inflammatory cargo within the intima, contributing to plaque growth.

Similarly, how ECs are cleared following cell death within the vasculature is poorly understood. While apoptotic ECs and ApoBDs entering circulation could be engulfed by tissue resident phagocytes at distal organs such as the liver [45] local efferocytosis of ECs or ApoBDs in the endothelium by macrophages or by neighbouring, healthy ECs, may also occur. A small number of previous studies have suggested a role for adhesion molecules in facilitating apoptotic cell clearance, both via expression on apoptotic cells and phagocytes. ICAM‑3 expression on dying leukocytes cells was found to act as an eat-me signal via tethering to phagocytes [46]; In a separate study, ICAM-1 expression on inflammatory macrophages promoted uptake of apoptotic cells in vitro and in vivo [36]. In the present study, TNF-mediated expression of ICAM-1 on EC-derived ApoBDs enhanced their clearance by macrophages, demonstrating a previously unknown role for ICAM-1-mediated efferocytosis via its expression on the apoptotic cell. As the removal of apoptotic cells via efferocytosis classically limits inflammation and promotes homeostasis [47]the enhanced effect of inflammation on macrophage efferocytosis of ApoBDs in our study is paradoxical. Impaired efferocytosis is observed frequently in advanced plaques where increased TNF signalling can upregulate don’t-eat-me signal CD47 on apoptotic cells [48] and may also lead to modification or downregulation of engulfment receptors on phagocytes [49, 50]. On one hand, our findings suggest that elevated ICAM-1 expression could counter some of these impaired signals at lesion sites. On the other hand, how engulfing phagocytes response to the uptake of inflammatory cargo is not well defined; whether internalisation could have deleterious rather than reparative effects on the local environment, remains to be determined. Further investigation is required to delineate the significance of ICAM-1 as an apoptotic ‘eat-me’ signal during inflammation.

ECs have demonstrated roles in antigen presentation, best described in the context of allograft rejection [51] and also in settings such as malaria and cytomegalovirus infections [52–54]. Within inflamed vessels, CD4+ and CD8+ T cell recruitment and activation contributes to persistent inflammation and atherosclerotic plaque growth [55, 56]. ECs can interact directly with T cells within the endothelium [57] and have been shown to express antigen presentation machinery including HLA molecules and costimulatory factors ICOS, CD40 and CD40L [37, 58, 59]. However, whether EC-derived ApoBDs could perform antigen presentation has not been previously proposed. By establishing a novel proof-of-concept method to assess CD8+ T cell activation by ApoBDs, we demonstrated for the first time that EC-derived ApoBDs can activate CD8+ T cells via direct antigen presentation. This observation highlights the novel concept that continued T cell activation can occur beyond the death of an antigen presenting cell via its ApoBDs, and that antigen presentation contributes to the immunomodulatory repertoire of EC-derived ApoBDs during vascular inflammation.

A number of limitations in this study highlight the need to further interrogate the biological significance of ApoBD-mediated immune signalling by ECs in vivo. Our in vitro model of inflammatory EC apoptosis aimed to mimic an inflammatory environment through pharmacologically promoting intrinsic apoptosis and using TNF as a source of EC activation. However, a range of other inflammatory stimuli including glucose, oxLDL and other cytokines that can cause EC activation and apoptosis [3] could promote the formation of ApoBDs that express alternative protein cargo with unique bioactive properties. Likewise, how the inflammatory environment alters local immune cells that interact with EC-derived EVs was not addressed in this study. In addition, while the use of venous ECs allowed for the investigation of ApoBDs in a model of vascular inflammation, they do not fully recapitulate arterial-specific characteristics—such as exposure to disturbed flow and pro-atherogenic molecular signatures—which could influence the formation and molecular cargo of EC-derived ApoBDs. While our model did not address all possible complexities of the vascular inflammatory niche in which EC-ApoBDs may be generated in vivo, it provided a generalised view of how inflammation can alter the cargo of dying EC-derived ApoBDs. Furthermore, although this study solely investigated how apoptotic ECs communicate during cell death, ECs can undergo several other forms of death including pyroptosis [60, 61] necroptosis [62] and ferroptosis [63]. How the protein cargo of EVs derived from other forms of dying cells could impact their communication with immune cells, warrants further investigation. Finally, advances in in vivo imaging techniques such as intravital microscopy [64] have the potential to further expand our understanding of ApoBD-mediated immune signaling within vascular pathologies, in real time.

Together, the findings of this study demonstrate how an inflammatory environment can impact the protein cargo of EC-derived ApoBDs, increasing their capacity to modulate both innate and adaptive immune processes. These observations provide insights into how dying ECs may propagate inflammatory signalling via the generation of ApoBDs and suggest the formation of EC-ApoBDs could serve as a therapeutic target to limit inflammation.
