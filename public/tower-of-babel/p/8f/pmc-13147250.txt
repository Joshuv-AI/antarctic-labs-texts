Cardiogenic shock is not a single entity: therapeutic implications
Authors: Marco Marini, Francesca Coraducci, Matilda Shkoza, Roberto Manfredi, Leonardo Belfioretti, Luca Angelini, Giulia Pongetti, Maria Vittoria Matassini, Ilaria Battistoni, Matteo Francioni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiogenic shock (CS) is a life-threatening syndrome characterized by systemic tissue hypoperfusion due to inadequate cardiac output. Despite advances in cardiovascular care, mortality remains high, particularly during the first hours and within the first month after onset, underscoring the need for early recognition and tailored therapeutic strategies. The marked heterogeneity of CS has prompted the development of phenotyping approaches encompassing clinical, haemodynamic, aetiological, and emo-metabolic dimensions. Clinically, patients may present as cold and congested, cold and dry, warm and congested, or warm and dry. From an aetiological perspective, CS may be ischaemic, related to de novo heart failure, acute decompensation of chronic heart failure, secondary, or mixed. Mixed shock—characterized by the coexistence of pump failure and inappropriate systemic vasodilation—is associated with higher mortality and rapid clinical deterioration. Advanced phenotyping strategies, including machine learning-based approaches, have identified additional CS subgroups with distinct prognostic profiles and therapeutic requirements. The Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions classification provides a practical framework for severity stratification and longitudinal monitoring, emphasizing the importance of frequent reassessment, particularly in early-stage patients at risk of rapid progression. Management of CS integrates pharmacological therapy—including inotropes and vasopressors—with mechanical circulatory support, tailored to the patient’s phenotype and haemodynamic profile. Optimal care of cardiogenic shock relies on accurate phenotyping, dynamic risk assessment, and personalized therapeutic strategies to improve outcomes while minimizing complications.Keywords: Cardiogenic shock, Phenotype, Advanced treatment

What is cardiogenic shock?

Cardiogenic shock (CS) is a clinical syndrome characterized by systemic tissue hypoperfusion due to inadequate cardiac output, resulting in a life-threatening condition.1 Although the conceptual description of CS identifies a well-recognized clinical scenario, there are no universally accepted diagnostic criteria. Indeed, the definition of CS varies across randomized clinical trials and position papers issued by scientific societies (Table 1).

Why cardiogenic shock?

Cardiogenic shock is associated with a mortality rate ranging from 30% to 60%, with deaths predominantly occurring within the first hours and during the first month (70–80%).1 Despite significant scientific advances, mortality remains unacceptably high. Data from the Cath-PCI registry demonstrate that CS-related mortality has not improved over time; moreover, in patients with ischaemic CS, mortality has further increased, highlighting a clear disconnect between scientific progress and actual clinical outcomes.2

These findings reflect the limitations observed to date: clinical trials attempting to identify effective treatments for CS have often yielded inconclusive results, largely because they enrolled heterogeneous patient populations. Consequently, recognizing and phenotyping the different forms of CS is essential to enable the implementation of targeted and effective therapeutic strategies.

Timing of intervention

Management of cardiogenic shock must be rapid and focused, aiming to promptly identify the patient’s phenotype in order to initiate effective and personalized treatment strategies. The concept of the ‘golden hour’ is therefore central to CS management.3 To integrate clinical phenotyping with therapeutic decision-making, we propose the FAST protocol (Figure 1).

Phenotyping

Clinical phenotyping

Phenotyping of cardiogenic shock may be aetiological, clinical, haemodynamic, or emo-metabolic. The most historically established approach is clinical phenotyping,1 which identifies four patient subtypes:

Cold and wet: patients with reduced cardiac output and elevated pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP), representing approximately two-thirds of patients with ischaemic CS.

Haemodynamic–aetiological phenotyping

Alongside clinical phenotyping, aetiological classification allows identification of distinct CS subtypes: ischaemic CS, de novo heart failure–related CS, acute decompensation of chronic heart failure (ADHF), secondary CS, and mixed shock.

Twenty to twenty-five years ago, CS was predominantly secondary to acute myocardial infarction (AMI), accounting for approximately 80% of all cases. In recent decades, however, the prevalence of ischaemic CS has declined in favour of CS related to heart failure. A recent analysis from the Critical Care Cardiology Trials Network (CCCTN), using the Shock Academy Research Consortium (SHARC) definition, reported that 27% of CS cases were of ischaemic origin, whereas 59% were related to heart failure (72% ADHF and 28% de novo).4

Patients with de novo heart failure–related CS have fewer comorbidities but exhibit higher 30-day mortality compared with those with CS due to ADHF. Furthermore, CCCTN data show that patients with ADHF-related CS and ischaemic CS more frequently present with cardiac arrest at onset, whereas patients with CS overlapping with non-cardiogenic shock components—the so-called mixed shock—have a worse prognosis and more severe clinical presentations.

Mixed shock represents a condition in which cardiogenic shock, where an increase in systemic vascular resistance would be expected to compensate for reduced cardiac output, is complicated by inappropriate systemic vasodilation. Mixed shock was introduced as a distinct phenotype by the American Heart Association in 20175 and later by SHARC in 2023.6 According to CCCTN data, approximately 20% of patients admitted to intensive care units for shock actually present with mixed shock. Moreover, mixed shock may complicate nearly 25% of cardiogenic shock cases after a mean of only 5 days, conferring significantly higher mortality compared with pure cardiogenic shock.7

Mixed shock therefore represents a clinical turning point characterized by a steeper mortality trajectory. In this setting, haemodynamic deterioration can no longer be explained solely by pump failure but requires timely recognition of the development of a systemic inflammatory response and progressive vasoplegia.

Emo-metabolic phenotyping and new perspectives

In addition to the aetiological classification of CS, more recent models have been proposed that integrate haemodynamic assessment with metabolic profiling, some of which have been derived using machine learning (ML) analyses. Among the most relevant is the model proposed by Zweck et al., which analysed patients with CS from international cohorts and identified three distinct CS phenotypes: non-congested, cardio-renal, and cardio-metabolic.

The non-congested phenotype is characterized by lower central venous pressure (CVP) and higher arterial blood pressure. The cardio-renal phenotype includes a higher prevalence of elderly, multi-morbid patients with worse renal function, reflected by elevated serum creatinine levels. The cardio-metabolic phenotype is characterized by higher arterial lactate levels, signs of multi-organ involvement, and increased CVP due to RV involvement. The three phenotypes showed progressively higher mortality rates of 21%, 45%, and 55%, respectively. Notably, despite these marked differences in mortality, patients across the three groups were treated in a similar manner, underscoring the lack of phenotype-guided therapeutic strategies.8

A second Italian study applied ML techniques to a cohort of patients with CS and identified five phenotypes: phenotype I, non-dilated left ventricle (LV); phenotype II, dilated LV; phenotype III, RV dysfunction; phenotype IV, advanced age with mild ventricular dysfunction; and phenotype V, hypotension with severe renal failure. Phenotype classification was readily achievable through bedside clinical and echocardiographic assessment and was associated with progressively increasing mortality (hazard ratio 1.29; 95% CI 1.07–1.57).9

These findings further confirm that patients with cardiogenic shock are not a homogeneous population and that accurate phenotyping enables more precise prognostic stratification and the potential for truly personalized, phenotype-guided therapy.

The SCAI classification not only stratifies patient prognosis—with mortality increasing in parallel with worsening SCAI stage—but also serves as a dynamic tool for monitoring disease trajectory and should be reassessed whenever the patient’s clinical condition changes. Data from the Cardiogenic Shock Working Group (CSWG) registry demonstrate that patients initially classified as stage D who progress to stage E experience an in-hospital mortality of 53%, while patients initially in stage B who deteriorate to stage E reach mortality rates as high as 75%. Furthermore, according to CSWG data, approximately 90% of patients initially in stage B progress to a more advanced stage within the first 52 h, highlighting the critical importance of timely and repeated reassessment. These findings also emphasize that patients in SCAI stage B should not be underestimated.11

Invasive and non-invasive haemodynamic monitoring

Invasive haemodynamic monitoring is performed using the Swan–Ganz (SG) pulmonary artery catheter. In the Evaluation Study of Congestive Heart Failure and Pulmonary Artery Catheterization Effectiveness (ESCAPE) trial, the use of the SG catheter did not demonstrate an improvement in event-free survival (Binanay et al., 2005). However, retrospective registry data have shown that SG-guided monitoring is associated with reduced mortality in cardiogenic shock, particularly in patients with AMI and those receiving mechanical circulatory support.

Non-invasive monitoring using echocardiography allows estimation of multiple haemodynamic parameters, including cardiac output (CO), pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP), pulmonary artery pulsatility index (PAPi), pulmonary and systemic vascular resistances, CVP, and cardiac power.

A prospective Italian study demonstrated good agreement between invasive and non-invasive haemodynamic assessments for CVP, CO, and pulmonary artery pressure (correlation coefficient R > 0.8), with a more modest correlation for PAPi (R = 0.67).12

Furthermore, analysis of the left ventricular pressure–volume (P–V) relationship throughout the cardiac cycle allows derivation of two parameters used to assess ventriculo–arterial coupling (VAC): end-systolic elastance (Ees), a surrogate of left ventricular contractility, and arterial elastance (Ea), a surrogate of arterial load. Even without invasive reconstruction of the P–V loop, echocardiography enables estimation of the Ees/Ea ratio. A ratio close to 1 reflects a condition of maximal cardiac efficiency.13

Conversely, if a patient exhibits VAC < 1 due to low peripheral resistance (Ea < 2) with preserved Ees, vasopressor therapy is likely to be beneficial. If, instead, VAC > 1 is driven by elevated Ea, the patient may benefit from inodilator therapy. Ventriculo–arterial coupling can also be assessed for the right ventricle (RV) using PAPi as a surrogate of RV Ees. A normal PAPi associated with low pulmonary Ea indicates a physiological state; a reduced PAPi in the presence of low pulmonary Ea suggests isolated RV dysfunction; and a reduced PAPi combined with elevated pulmonary Ea indicates biventricular dysfunction with an unfavourable prognosis.

Epinephrine has been evaluated in several clinical trials, the most notable being the OPTIMA CC trial (Epinephrine Versus Norepinephrine for Cardiogenic Shock After Acute Myocardial Infarction),14 which was terminated prematurely due to a higher incidence of refractory shock in the epinephrine arm.

With regard to dopamine, a subanalysis of the SOAP III trial in patients with cardiogenic shock reported an increase in heart rate and myocardial oxygen consumption.15

Among alternative drug classes, phosphodiesterase inhibitors, such as milrinone and enoximone, may be used. The DOREMI trial (Dobutamine Compared with Milrinone in the Treatment of Cardiogenic Shock) randomized patients with cardiogenic shock to dobutamine vs. milrinone and found no significant difference in the primary composite endpoint.16

Among calcium sensitizers, levosimendan deserves mention. Several studies have investigated levosimendan; in the LIDO study, patients with cardiogenic shock due to acute decompensated heart failure were randomized to levosimendan or dobutamine, with levosimendan proving more effective in achieving the primary haemodynamic endpoint of increased cardiac output.17 Owing to its long-acting metabolite, levosimendan has also been shown to reduce hospitalizations in patients with advanced heart failure treated with intermittent infusions, as demonstrated in the LION-HEART trial.18

Among less frequently used agents, vasopressin may be beneficial in mixed shock or in cases of refractory hypotension associated with right ventricular dysfunction, as its vasoconstrictive effects spare the pulmonary circulation.

Finally, methylene blue merits consideration in selected cases of mixed shock. However, the supporting evidence remains limited, and its use may be associated with serious adverse effects, including serotonin syndrome when administered concomitantly with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors.

Mechanical circulatory support

Mechanical circulatory support devices differ in their mechanism of action, vascular access requirements, and the degree of haemodynamic support provided.

The intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) was evaluated in the IABP-SHOCK II trial, which assessed its use in patients with ischaemic cardiogenic shock and found no mortality benefit. However, the study had several limitations, including a high crossover rate between treatment arms and variable timing of device insertion, with 85% of placements occurring after percutaneous coronary intervention.19

More recently, the Altshock-2 trial (Study on Early Intra-aortic Balloon Pump Placement in Acute Decompensated Heart Failure Complicated by Cardiogenic Shock) randomized patients with cardiogenic shock due to heart failure to early IABP implantation vs. standard therapy, with the primary endpoint of 60-day survival or successful bridging to heart transplantation. The trial yielded neutral results. However, patients were highly selected and predominantly exhibited a low-output syndrome rather than overt cardiogenic shock, as reflected by relatively low mean arterial lactate levels (1.8 mmol/L). Indeed, 75% of patients in the non-IABP group survived to 60 days or were successfully transplanted.20

Escalating the level of mechanical support, Impella® is a percutaneous microaxial flow pump with an inlet positioned in the left ventricle and an outlet in the ascending aorta.

Recently, the multicentre DanGer Shock trial (Microaxial Flow Pump or Standard Care in Infarct-Related Cardiogenic Shock) reported highly relevant results. This study randomized patients with acute myocardial infarction complicated by CS—defined by laboratory evidence of peripheral hypoperfusion, hypotension, and left ventricular ejection fraction < 45%—to standard care alone or standard care plus implantation of an Impella CP device.21

The trial demonstrated a significant reduction in 180-day mortality of 13% in the Impella group. The study population was predominantly male (79%), with a mean age of 79 years, and patients were randomized early (mean time from symptom onset of 4 h). Approximately 84% of enrolments occurred in the cardiac catheterization laboratory. Most patients were classified as SCAI stage C (55%), with the remaining 45% in stages D or E. The mean left ventricular ejection fraction was 25%, and baseline arterial lactate levels averaged 4.5 mmol/L. Most infarctions were anterior or extensive anterior myocardial infarctions, with 74% involving the left main coronary artery or the left anterior descending artery as the culprit lesion.

In addition to reduced mortality, subanalyses showed that Impella implantation in the DanGer Shock trial was associated with more rapid lactate clearance (approximately 12 h earlier than standard care), reduced requirements for inotropes and vasopressors, and improved mean arterial pressure.22 However, despite the favourable impact on the primary endpoint, the Impella group experienced a higher incidence of adverse events, including major bleeding, limb ischaemia, or haemolysis, occurring in 24% of cases compared with 6.2% in the standard-care group.

These findings suggest that the substantial benefit of mechanical circulatory support with Impella should be reserved for carefully selected patients, in order to maximize survival benefit while minimizing complications.

Conclusions

In summary, cardiogenic shock should not be considered a single, monolithic entity, but rather a complex and heterogeneous spectrum of clinical, aetiological, haemodynamic, and emo-metabolic phenotypes. The ability to accurately phenotype each patient is fundamental to selecting the most appropriate therapeutic strategy. Only through such stratification can advanced therapies be allocated to patients who are most likely to derive meaningful benefit, while simultaneously minimizing treatment-related risks.
