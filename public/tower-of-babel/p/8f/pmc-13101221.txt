Are 2D MRI slices equally important in microvascular invasion prediction: a study based on multiple instance learning with attention
Authors: Yingying Liu, Yafang Dou, Wenning Yuan, Shiman Wu, Zhenwei Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMicrovascular invasion (MVI) is a critical prognostic indicator of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Although MRI-based prediction of MVI has drawn great attention, many previous studies focused on either 3D volumetric regions of tumors or the slices of the largest tumors size in the axial plane. This study aimed to investigate the predictive value of 2D MRI slices on MVI, based on a multiple instance learning (MIL) method with an attention block to estimate the slice-wise importance.MethodsA total of 174 patients (training cohort: 121 patients; testing cohort: 53 patients) were evaluated in this retrospective study. MRI scans of arterial phase, venous phase and delayed phase, as well as sixteen clinical characteristics were collected. A transformer-based model was utilized to extract features from the original and dilated tumor regions (with 5 mm and 10 mm margins). A MIL based method was developed to predict MVI, with an attention block to estimate the slice-wise weights. Eighteen phase-specific and six combined-phase models were trained and tested. Three hybrid models were constructed from the best-performed models. Area under the curve (AUC), precision, accuracy, F1 were utilized to evaluate model performance. The hybrid model using soft voting strategy achieved the highest test AUC (0.80, 95%CI: 0.69, 0.92) in this study. CKA analysis of one patient’s data showed that the top 5 MRI slices of the highest weights have stronger correlation with MVI than others (0.87 versus 0.75).ConclusionThe group of 2D MRI slices demonstrated predictive value for MVI prediction using a MIL method. The learned weights of MRI slices from the attention block indicate their potential relevance with MVI. These findings may help to identify the most relevant MRI slices for MVI prediction, which may be utilized in MVI diagnosis in future applications.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02282-7.

Background

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the third leading cause of cancer deaths [1, 2], which has over 50% recurrence rate after initial treatments [3–5], and lower than 20% five-years survival rate [6, 7]. Previous studies have demonstrated that microvascular invasion (MVI) is a pivotal predictor of prognosis in HCC patients who had curative treatments [8, 9]. It is identified by the presence of clusters of tumor cells within blood vessels lined with endothelial cells [10, 11], which indicates aggressive behavior and increased heterogeneity in HCC [12]. Current assessment of MVI relies on histopathological analysis, which is invasive, prone to sampling bias and time-consuming. Therefore, the development of a non-invasive and convenient technique for MVI assessment is critical for clinical applications.

Researchers have identified correlations between clinical, radiological characteristics and MVI, for example, serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels, tumor size, and portal and hepatic vein invasion. However, a uniform standard has not yet been established [13]. In recent studies, image features have been extensively studied for MVI prediction. Based on 3D volumetric regions of interest (VOI), both classical radiomics features [13–17] and deep learning-based image features [12, 18, 19] using pretrained deep learning architectures have been studied. Although 3D VOIs may provide comprehensive information, it is challenging for radiologists to visually interpret in clinical workflow. A prior study selected the slices of the largest tumor size in the axial plane and utilized a deep neural networks (DNNs)-based classifier to predict MVI [20]. Another study resampled VOIs into the same size and built DNN classifier to predict MVI with attention mechanism [21]. However, given the inherent heterogeneity of tumors, the selected slices or resampled VOIs may not contain visual indicators of MVI. Although 2D region of interests (ROIs) from MRI slices is less comprehensive than 3D VOIs, it retains the potential to capture tumor heterogeneity and provide valuable information for MVI assessment, while offering greater clinical practicality for future workflow integration. However, it has not been well studied in MVI assessment.

Multiple instance learning (MIL) was initially developed for drug activity prediction [22], where each sample is represented by a bag of features. It aligns well with numerous real-world problems [23], such as histopathological analysis [24–27]. In this study, a MIL-based predictive model was employed to investigate the association between the group of 2D MRI slices and MVI, with an integrated attention block to learn weights of different slices. Centered kernel alignment (CKA) was utilized to access the value of the learned weights by computing the correlation between features of MRI and histopathological images. Our study aimed to investigate the predictive value of 2D MRI slices on MVI, and determine their relevance to MVI using machine learning techniques, which may potentially assist clinical diagnosis and research of MVI.

Materials and methods

The work is based on a retrospective study, which was approved by the institutional medical ethics review board of Huashan hospital (KY2025-718). The necessity of written informed consent was waived due to retrospective study design. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The step-by-step workflow of the study is illustrated in Fig. 1, and the details are described as follows.

Patient criteria

Patients with pathologically-confirmed HCC and also underwent preoperative MRI examination were identified over the period between December 2015 to December 2024, through a comprehensive search of the medical records. Patients’ data with the following conditions were excluded from the study: (1) MRI images of arterial (A), venous (V), and delayed (D) phases are not all present; (2) tumor size was diagnosed as less than 1 mm; (3) Any of the A, V, D phases of MRI was with poor quality due to the presence of artifacts, distortion or noise. In the final analysis, 174 patients (mean age, 57.1 ± 11.6 years, 148 men) were identified and randomly divided into a training cohort and a testing cohort at a ratio of 7:3 (stratified by MVI label: positive or negative). The flowchart of the patient enrollment is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Clinical characteristics and preprocessing

A total of 16 clinical characteristics were extracted from the medical archives of our institution, including: age, gender, tumor size, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), albumin (ALB), direct bilirubin (DBIL), prothrombin time (PT), alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), AFP lens culinaris agglutinin-reactive fraction (AFP-L3), protein induced by vitamin K absence or antagonist-II (PIVKA-II), ASAP score, GALAD score, Hepatitis b surface antigen (HBsAg), Hepatitis b viral DNA load (HBV-DNA). Histopathological confirmed MVI label was also collected.

Imputation method was applied to address the problem of missing values. Features with more than 10 missing values were excluded, while those with less than 10 missing values were imputed: numerical features were replaced with the mean value of the cohort, and categorical features were imputed using the most frequent value of their respective dimension. After imputation, beside MVI label, 9 clinical characteristics were kept for MVI prediction, include ‘Age’, ‘Sex’, ‘size’, ‘AFP’, ‘PT’, ‘ALB’, ‘TBIL’, ‘ALT’ and ‘AST’. The missing ratio of each clinical characteristics is illustrated in Table S2 of the supplementary materials.

The age and size of the subjects were normalized based on the researcher’s domain knowledge. All age values were scaled to values between 0 and 1, with the range defined as 0 to 120 years. The range encompasses the majority range of human lifespan. The tumor sizes were transformed into values ranging from 0 to 1, with the minimum size set to 1 centimeter and the maximum size set to 30 centimeters. The range corresponds to the majority of clinically observed tumor size. Laboratory variables were converted to binary values based on clinically adopted criteria: ‘AFP < 7’, ‘PT (10.8–13.5)’, ‘ALB (40–50)’, ‘TBIL < 26’, ‘ALT (9–50)’, ‘AST (15–40)’. Specifically, values in the range were converted to 1, otherwise 0.

MRI image acquisition and preprocessing

MRI examinations were performed on 3.0-Tesla scanners (uMR 790, United Imaging Healthcare; Discovery MR750, GE Healthcare; Prisma, Siemens Healthcare). The description of scanner parameters and phases are illustrated in Table S1 of the Supplementary Material.

Because enhanced phases can show the blood supply of tumor, which has higher diagnostic value for MVI prediction, and are observed with highest image resolution, therefore, MRI images of A, V, and D phases were used for analysis in this study. MRI images were preprocessed before feature extraction. Using Python SimpleITK package (version 2.4.0), N4 Bias Field Correction was utilized to minimize intensity inhomogeneity [28]. Pixels with MRI intensity values above the 99th percentile or below the 1st percentile were removed. The remaining intensities were normalized to a [0, 1] range using max-min normalization.

Tumor segmentation and dilation

The tumor was manually delineated by two radiologists, both with more than 10 years of MRI imaging experience, using ITK-SNAP (University of Pennsylvania Image Computing and Science Laboratory, version 3.8.0-beta) [29]. One radiologist performed the segmentation and the second radiologist reviewed and confirmed the annotated areas. To capture peritumoral features, each of the manually annotated tumor region was expanded by 5 mm and 10 mm respectively using isotropic 3D dilation, to generate dilated tumor regions. The dilation was performed without applying a liver mask or accounting for vessel-liver boundaries and adjacent organs, to simplify implementation and enhance practical usability.

Deep-learning based feature extraction and feature bag construction

Using pretrained transformer-based deep learning models as feature extractors has demonstrated effective performance across a range of computer vision tasks. In this study, we employed a pretrained vision transformer (ViT) [30] implemented by Hugging Face [31] as the backbone feature extractor to extract discriminative image features. For each 2D slice, a 192-dimensional feature vector was extracted independently from each of the original and dilated tumor regions. In the MIL methods, samples are treated as “bags” of instances, and only the bags are assigned with predefined labels, while the labels of instances are unknown. In this study, each tumor was treated as a bag composed of features extracted from multiple 2D ROIs. Specifically, for each tumor, three phase-specific bags were constructed from features of A, V, D phases respectively. And a combined-phase bag that including all features of A, V, D phases were also constructed.

MVI prediction model based on MIL method

In this study, we focused on investigating the association between the complete set of 2D MRI slices and MVI. Given that each tumor comprised multiple MRI slices and the heterogeneous nature of tumor, the importance of the MRI slices in MVI prediction may not be uniform. In order to address this issue, a MIL framework with an attention block was developed to model the association between multiple image features and MVI.

As demonstrated in previous section, deep learning-derived features were independently extracted from each 2D ROI and aggregated to form bag-level representations. Based on a previous work combing MIL and attention mechanism [32], an attention block was developed to learn to weight the relative importance of individual 2D ROIs during feature aggregation, which demonstrate the importance of difference slices and facilitate model interpretability. These attention weights were subsequently employed to evaluate the contribution of different slices to MVI prediction. The MIL based neural network structure is illustrated in Fig. S1 of the Supplementary Material.

For each of the original tumor region, and dilated tumor regions (with 5 mm, 10 mm peritumoral extension), phase-specific MIL models were built from each of the A, V, and D phase-specific bags, and combined-phase MIL models were built from the combined-phase bags. Preprocessed clinical characteristics is optional in model construction, which can be concatenated with unified features by passing feature bags through a series of dense layers. This yielded eighteen phase-specific models and six combined-phase models, half with clinical characteristics, and half without. Beside the MIL models, three hybrid models were also developed by combing the above MIL models using hard voting, soft voting and lasso methods, respectively. Additional details of the three methods are provided in Supplementary Material (Section S3).

During training, data augmentation was implemented by adding zero-mean Gaussian noise (σ = 0.01) to the training samples, to increase the quantity of training data and enhance the robustness of the model. Hyperparameters were optimized using Optuna framework [33] based on the training cohort with 3-fold cross validation. The random state was setup to 42 across the model training and testing process. A baseline model trained on clinical characteristics with logistic regression (LR) was also constructed. The model was optimized with 1000 iterations, and the same data augmentation method was employed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Python’s SciPy (version 1.13.1) and scikit-learn (version 1.5.1) packages. Categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test, while continuous variables were compared using Welch’s t-test. The receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) was plotted and area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) was employed to evaluate MIL model’s performance, along with accuracy, precision, and F1-score.

Performance evaluation of prediction models

Performance of hybrid models

DeLong test was utilized to compare the top five best-performed models and the three hybrid models (see Table 4). The soft voting method demonstrated superior discriminative performance compare to the majority of best-performed MIL models, with statistically significant differences (p < 0.05). Additionally, both hard voting and lasso also exhibited significant differences in predictive performance to most of the best-performed MIL models. These findings suggests that the hybrid methods integrating multiple sources of information may provide meaningful contributions to prediction. It can also be noted that no significant differences were observed between AVD_10_Yes and the three hybrid models. These results reveal that utilizing information from all the three phases in combination with clinical variables may lead to comparable prediction performance.

Correlation between MRI slices and histopathology WSI

To evaluate the learned weights, we searched our database and retrieved the histopathological whole slide image of one patient in Fig. 3 (the 5th patient of (a)), whose prediction probability is 0.89. MVI ROI was manually labelled by an experienced pathologist and cropped into titles using QuPath [34]. Deep learning-based features were extracted from each title using the same pretrained ViT model. Then Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) analysis [35], a method to evaluate correlation of deep learning-based features, was applied to compute correlation between features of the top five ranked MRI slices (Group 1) and features of the histopathological titles, the other slices (Group 2) and the features of the histopathological titles, respectively. As CKA requires equal number of samples, we sampled Group 1 and Group 2 multiple times and computed their CKA score respectively. As demonstrated in Fig. 4, Group 1 were more strongly associated with the histopathological titles, than Group 2 (Average value 0.87 verses 0.75). This demonstrates that slices with higher weights have higher CKA similarity score with MVI.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the predictive value of 2D MRI slices on MVI, and to discover whether the 2D MRI slices are equally important in MVI prediction. A total of twenty-four phase-specific and combined-phase MIL models was trained and tested, as well as three hybrid models from the top performed MIL models. By utilizing an attention block with MIL method, each of the 2D MRI slices was assigned with a learned weight, which was further evaluated using CKA analysis.

Our results showed that certain phase-specific and combined-phase models demonstrated better predictive value than others, while the hybrid models achieve the best performance. CKA analysis showed that highly-weighted MRI slices have better correlation with MVI in histopathological images, which demonstrate the value of the learned weights.

Phase-specific, combined-phase and hybrid models

According to Table 2, phase-specific models of arterial phase demonstrated superior performance than others, and the ones without peritumoral extension achieved the best performance among all of phase-specific and phase-combined models. This is consistent with the clinical observation of HCC, where tumor is often highlighted during arterial phase due to large blood supply. It can be observed from Fig. 3 that large size, non-smooth tumor margin, tumor capsule with enhancement, peritumoral arterial phase enhancement, inhomogeneous intensity is more likely to present in MVI positive group. These features often related with aggressive biological behavior of HCC, which may be potential indicators of MVI. However, these features can also be observed in some patients with false prediction, which need further investigation. The hybrid model using soft voting method achieved the best performance across all models (see Table 3). Besides, combined-phase model with 10 mm peritumoral extension also showed good predictive performance (see Table 2).

In the phase-specific models, the two models with no-peritumoral extension from the arterial phase outperformed all other models, including those incorporating peritumoral information. The reason behind this finding may be that while peritumoral extension may introduce additional information, it may also introduce noise, particularly when the expanded regions extend beyond the liver parenchyma into adjacent anatomical structures. In the combined-phase models, the AVD phase model with peritumoral information also demonstrated strong predictive performance. This suggests that peritumoral information becomes more meaningful when all three phases are considered together. During the integration of patterns across A, V and D phases, the noise might be reduced due to the consensus of information across multiple phases. This demonstrated that combining multiple phases may improve the predictive performance, which is consistent with clinical practice, where characteristic changes across dynamic contrast phases are routinely used as important diagnostic clues for liver lesions.

While DeLong test demonstrated significance between specific model pairs, overlapping CIs were observed across models. This may mainly due to the limited sample size of our cohort, which may impact the robustness of the evaluation. In future work, we will collect data from multiple centers to enhance robustness of our findings and to rigorously evaluate performance differences across diverse clinical setting with external validation.

Tumor dilation with peritumoral extension

It can be observed that including the peritumoral regions may not maintain or improve prediction performance. This may be attributed to the unconstrained peritumoral expansion approach. Although the approach was utilized to simplify implementation and enhance practical usability, the expanded regions may extend beyond the liver parenchyma into adjacent anatomical structures. Although the deep learning based ViT is capable of capturing relationships across all image patches of the annotated tumor region with inherent self-attention mechanism, which enable a global understanding of the annotated region even when it extends beyond the liver boundary. However, the inclusion of non-liver tissues may introduce additional noise into the features, which potentially impact the model’s performance. In future works, we will consider incorporating anatomical constraints, such as liver segmentation masks, to improve the prediction performance and clinical interpretability.

Performance with and without clinical characteristics

It can also be noticed that adding clinical characteristics has little impact on the performance. This may be because of the constrained preprocessing strategies of clinical characteristics. Constrained by sample size, we employed a conservative threshold of 10 missing values for variable exclusion to ensure data quality, which may lead to unnecessary removal of potentially informative features. Additionally, while mean imputation for numerical features and mode imputation for categorical features were chosen for their simplicity and computational efficiency, these methods may distort data distributions, which could affect both model performance and clinical interpretability. Furthermore, instead of data-driven methods like min-max normalization, the fixed theoretical ranges (0–120 years for age and 1–30 cm for tumor size) were employed for scaling, in order to maintain integrity and prevent data leakage across training and test splits. Although this ensures consistent scaling, these fixed ranges may not accurately reflect our population’s true distribution, potentially underestimating the predictive influence of these variables. Similar to many previous studies, continuous laboratory variables were binarized using established clinical cutoff values to enhance interpretability and reduce model complexity. However, this may diminish the predictive contribution of clinical characteristics due to potential information loss. Therefore, it is important to note that the conclusions regarding clinical variables are not definitive due to the limited sample size and the pragmatic preprocessing choices previously outlined. The findings related to the contributions of clinical variables are exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Future study will involve large, multi-site cohorts and refined preprocessing techniques to reach definitive conclusions.

MVI prediction with weighted MRI slices

Compared with previous VOI-based methods [12–19, 21], the MIL-based method is more applicable in extending to clinical application of preoperative radiological evaluation. Slices of high weights from attention block demonstrated higher CKA similarity score with MVI ROI in the histopathological image, which indicates their potential diagnostic importance. The highly-weighted slices may potentially be utilized to assist MVI diagnosis in clinical settings. Compared with the method using the slices of the largest tumor size in the axial plane [20], the MIL-based method retains more information by keeping all slices in prediction process, which is important in MVI prediction. The reason is that if MVI indicator exists in 2D MRI slices, it may not necessarily be present on the slices of the largest tumor size in the axial plane.

Limitations

There’re some limitations of this study. Firstly, the study cohort is limited. Due to the inclusive criteria and single site limitation, our final cohort is not large. To enhance the dataset size, augmentation method was utilized in the current study. However, due to the limited cohort size, several pragmatic choices were made during the clinical data preprocessing stage, including the determination of missing-data threshold, the utilization of fixed scaling ranges, and the dichotomization of continuous laboratory values. These choices were implemented to avoid overfitting. However, it is important to note that they may reduce the granularity and predictive value of clinical data, thereby contribute to the limited added value of clinical features in the models. In our future study, data from multi-sites will be included to create a larger cohort. And more robust approaches, including less conservative variable retention thresholds, multiple imputation or model-based techniques for missing data, and continuous clinical variables will be explored to improve predictive accuracy and generalizability. The second limitation is the discriminative capability of the deep-learning based feature may be limited. Although the pretrained ViT has been widely adopted as the feature extractor in many researches due to its success in capturing discriminative image features, it may not be able to catch specific information in medical images. The reason is that it was trained based on general image datasets. Our future study will explore transfer learning-based methods to extract more expressive features. The third limitation is that the dynamics across A, V, D phases over time were not integrated. The dynamics often leveraged in clinical diagnosis workflow, which may contain valuable information for MVI prediction. In our future study, advanced modelling method will be explored to include dynamics when combing different phases. The last limitation is that the impact of different contrast agents on the extracted features and models has not been investigated. In this study, multiple agents including Gadopentetate dimeglumine (Gd-DTPA) and Gadobutrol (Gd-BT-DO3A) were employed over the 2015–2024 period, and greater than 80% patients were injected with Gd-DTPA. We could not stratify the contrast agents in detail due to the difficult data collection associated with long-period time span. Although the contrast agents we used were all extracellular agents produced based on Gadolinium (Gd), and followed the same scanning method, using multiple contrast agents over time without a stratified analysis or harmonization, has the potential to engender a source of non-biological variability in intensity-based features. This may restrict the generalizability of the findings across institutions and protocols. To further investigate the effects on image-derived features and model performance, future work will involve a more detailed stratification of agents.

Conclusions

In summary, we adopted an MIL based method with an attention block to investigate the association between the group of 2D MRI slices and MVI using deep learning-based features. Phase-specific, combined-phase and hybrid models all showed certain predictive value. While hybrid model obtained the best performance, arterial phase demonstrated superior predictive value than other phases. The learned weights from attention block provides slice-wise importance for MVI prediction, which demonstrates the potential for computer-aided slice prioritization that may be used in future clinical workflow to assist radiologists for MVI diagnosis. The proposed method achieves an AUC comparable to existing benchmarks, although not exceeding them, its advantage is in terms of workflow-oriented clinical value. In the future, after establishing the generalizability and reliability of the proposed method with intensive evaluation across large-scale, multi-center cohorts, it may serve as a complement to existing tumor-specific factors, such as size or multiplicity, by prioritizing slices for fast preoperative assessment.
