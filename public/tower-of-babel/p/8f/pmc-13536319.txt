Epigallocatechin gallate in dentistry: Therapeutic applications, biomaterial integration, and translational challenges
Authors: Chloe Jui-Yun Chang, Fang-Yu Fan, Yung-Li Wang, Thi Thuy Tien Vo, Yang-Che Wu, Yu-Chia Chang, I-Ta Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), a major bioactive catechin in green tea, has attracted increasing attention in dentistry because of its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, matrix-modulating, and regenerative properties. This narrative review summarizes current evidence on the biological mechanisms, dental applications, and translational challenges of EGCG in oral healthcare. EGCG can modulate oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling pathways, including nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2/heme oxygenase-1 (Nrf2/HO-1), and NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3)-related responses, and has activity against oral disease-associated microorganisms and biofilms. In periodontal therapy, EGCG has been investigated as an adjunct to non-surgical periodontal treatment and as a local therapeutic agent through gingival patches, injectable hydrogels, and other delivery systems. In adhesive and restorative dentistry, EGCG may improve resin-dentin interface stability through matrix metalloproteinase inhibition, dentin biomodification, and antimicrobial effects against cariogenic biofilms. In regenerative dentistry, EGCG has been reported to promote osteogenic and odontogenic differentiation, regulate immune responses, support angiogenesis, and enhance bone-related repair when incorporated into scaffolds, membranes, hydrogels, or bioactive coatings. However, most evidence remains derived from in vitro and preclinical studies, and clinical translation is limited by low bioavailability, chemical instability, dosage variability, delivery efficiency, material compatibility, and insufficient long-term clinical evidence. Further studies using standardized protocols, optimized delivery systems, long-term safety assessment, clinically relevant oral models, comparative biomaterial testing, mechanism- based outcome measures, and well-designed randomized clinical trials are required to determine whether EGCG can become a reliable adjunctive strategy for periodontal, restorative, and regenerative oral healthcare.

Introduction

Green tea is a widely consumed beverage, and its biological effects are mainly attributed to its polyphenolic catechins. Among these catechins, epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) is one of the major bioactive components of green tea and has been investigated for its structural and chemical properties.1 In the oral health field, green tea and its catechins have been discussed as natural compounds with potential benefits for dental caries, periodontal inflammation, oral infection control, and oral cancer prevention.2 EGCG has attracted considerable attention as a multifunctional bioactive compound; previous reviews have described its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer-related mechanisms, while additional evidence supports its anti-infective activity against bacteria, fungi, and viruses.3,4 These activities are relevant to dentistry because many oral diseases are closely associated with oral disease-related microorganisms and biofilm formation, including cariogenic and periodontopathic microbes.5

In dentistry, EGCG has been explored across several major application areas, including periodontal therapy, adhesive and restorative dentistry, and regenerative dentistry. In periodontal research, EGCG has been evaluated as an adjunctive agent for non-surgical periodontal treatment and as a bioactive molecule with potential host-modulatory and regenerative relevance.6–8 In adhesive and restorative dentistry, EGCG has been investigated for its potential to improve dentin-adhesive interface stability, including strategies involving dimethyl sulfoxide wetbonding and resin-dentin bonding preservation.9 These findings support the feasibility of using EGCG not only as an antimicrobial or anti-inflammatory compound, but also as a bioactive molecule incorporated into selected dental material strategies.

Despite these promising applications, the clinical translation of EGCG remains limited by several challenges. EGCG has relatively low bioavailability and is affected by stability-related factors, which may restrict its biological performance under physiological conditions and during incorporation into dental materials.3,10 Therefore, current research has increasingly focused on stabilizing systems, improved formulations, and delivery strategies to enhance EGCG stability, activity, and therapeutic applicability.10 In dental research, these issues are particularly relevant to local oral microbial control, periodontal therapy, dentin-adhesive applications, and regenerative approaches.5,7–9 Based on these considerations, this review provides an updated synthesis of the biological properties and dental applications of EGCG, with emphasis on its roles in periodontal therapy, adhesive and restorative dentistry, and regenerative applications. It also discusses major translational challenges, including stability, delivery, dosage, biosafety, material integration, and the gap between preclinical findings and clinical evidence.

Biological properties and molecular mechanisms of epigallocatechin gallate

EGCG exerts a wide range of biological effects that are mediated through its ability to modulate oxidative stress responses, inflammatory signaling, host–microbe interactions, and extracellular matrix-related tissue responses. These properties are closely related to its polyphenolic structure and its ability to interact with multiple molecular targets involved in cellular regulation.1,3 One of the most extensively studied biological properties of EGCG is its antioxidant activity. EGCG can directly scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) and regulate redox-sensitive cellular responses.3,7,11 In periodontal disease models, EGCG has also been reported to reduce oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and alveolar bone loss, supporting its relevance to oxidative stress-associated oral tissue damage.7

EGCG also exhibits anti-inflammatory effects through the regulation of key intracellular signaling pathways. EGCG has been reported to inhibit lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathways in macrophages, with a corresponding reduction in pro-inflammatory gene expression.12 In experimental periodontitis, EGCG has further been reported to modulate the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)/heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1)/NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3)/NF-κB p65 signaling axis, which may contribute to the attenuation of oxidative stress, inflammation, and alveolar bone loss.7 In addition to its effects on host responses, EGCG demonstrates antimicrobial activity against a wide range of microorganisms. EGCG has been reported to exert anti-infective effects against bacteria, fungi, and viruses, whereas green tea catechins have also been associated with antimicrobial activity through effects on microbial membranes, microbial enzymes, adhesion, and biofilm-related processes.5,13 These antimicrobial properties are particularly relevant in the oral cavity, where biofilm-associated pathogens play a central role in disease progression.5

Another important mechanism of EGCG is its ability to modulate extracellular matrix-related degradation. In adhesive dentistry, EGCG has been investigated as a dentin biomodifier, and its combination with dimethyl sulfoxide wet-bonding has been reported to improve dentin-adhesive interface stability.9 This property is particularly significant in restorative dentistry, as collagen degradation contributes to the deterioration of resin-dentin bonding over time. EGCG has also been reported to influence cellular proliferation, differentiation, and tissue regeneration. In human periodontal ligament cells, EGCG promotes cell proliferation and osteogenic differentiation, suggesting a possible role in periodontal tissue repair and regeneration.8 In addition, studies using periodontitis models have demonstrated that EGCG can reduce oxidative stress, suppress inflammation, and limit alveolar bone loss, further supporting its potential role in modulating periodontal bone metabolism.7 Collectively, these biological properties indicate that EGCG functions as a multifunctional bioactive molecule capable of targeting oxidative stress, inflammation, microbial infection, and extracellular matrix-related tissue responses. These combined effects provide a mechanistic basis for its potential applications in periodontal therapy, restorative dentistry, and regenerative approaches.

Epigallocatechin gallate in periodontal therapy

EGCG has been investigated as a potential adjunctive agent in periodontal therapy because of its anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antioxidant, and host-modulatory activities. This rationale is supported by evidence that EGCG and green tea extracts can affect oral disease-associated microorganisms, including cariogenic and periodontopathic bacteria.5 EGCG may attenuate periodontal inflammation by modulating inflammatory signaling and oxidative stress-related responses. Previous reviews have described EGCG as a bioactive compound capable of interacting with multiple molecular targets involved in inflammation, oxidative stress, and cellular regulation.3 In addition, EGCG has been reported to exert anti-infective effects against bacteria, fungi, and viruses, supporting its potential relevance to infection-associated inflammatory conditions.4

In addition to its general biological activities, EGCG exhibits antimicrobial activity against oral pathogens associated with periodontal disease. Green tea extract and EGCG have been reviewed for their inhibitory effects on oral disease-associated microbes, including periodontopathic microorganisms.5 These antimicrobial properties are particularly relevant in periodontal therapy, where microbial biofilms contribute to disease progression and where control of biofilm-associated pathogens is an important component of periodontal management.5 However, the antimicrobial activity of EGCG may vary depending on microbial species, concentration, exposure conditions, and the surrounding oral environment.4,5

EGCG also plays a potential role in modulating oxidative stress, inflammation, and bone-related responses in periodontal tissues. In a rat periodontitis model, EGCG reduced oxidative stress and inflammatory responses and decreased alveolar bone loss through modulation of the Nrf2/HO-1/NLRP3/NF-κB p65 signaling pathway, supporting its protective effects on periodontal structures through host modulation.7 More recent in vitro evidence further showed that EGCG reduced LPS-induced oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in human periodontal ligament fibroblasts through the Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1)/Nrf2/HO-1 pathway and histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6)-related regulation, supporting its relevance to periodontal inflammation and oxidative stress control.14 In human periodontal ligament cells, EGCG has also been shown to promote proliferation and osteogenic differentiation, indicating its possible relevance to periodontal tissue repair and regeneration.8

Clinically, green tea catechins and EGCG-based preparations have been evaluated as adjuncts to nonsurgical periodontal therapy using different regimens and delivery approaches. Earlier clinical evidence suggested that local delivery of green tea catechin may improve periodontal status, although the findings were derived from a pilot clinical study and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.15 Chava and Vedula evaluated a thermo-reversible green tea catechin gel as a local adjunct in chronic periodontitis and reported improvements in periodontal parameters during a short observation period.16 Rattanasuwan et al. also reported that locally delivered green tea gel provided additional benefits in reducing bleeding on probing and gingival inflammation when used together with non-surgical periodontal therapy.17 In contrast, a more recent randomized clinical trial using EGCG as a coolant during scaling and root planing showed only limited additional improvement in periodontal indices, with statistical significance mainly observed for the bleeding index.6 These variable findings may be related to differences in sample size, follow-up duration, EGCG dose, formulation, local retention, application route, and baseline periodontal conditions. Therefore, although available clinical studies support the potential adjunctive value of EGCG or green tea catechin preparations in periodontal therapy, the current evidence remains heterogeneous and insufficient to confirm a predictable clinical benefit. Larger randomized controlled trials with standardized EGCG concentrations, delivery protocols, retention assessment, and longer follow-up periods are required to clarify its clinical efficacy in periodontal treatment. These findings suggest that EGCG may provide adjunctive benefits in periodontal therapy, but its clinical efficacy may depend on treatment protocol, concentration, exposure time, and local availability.6 To enhance its therapeutic applicability, formulation-related issues should also be considered. In the clinical periodontal setting, EGCG has been applied as a coolant during scaling and root planing.6 More broadly, recent periodontal literature has emphasized that natural bioactive compounds relevant to periodontal therapy, including EGCG, still face challenges related to bioavailability, oral cavity stability, and the lack of large-scale clinical trials; therefore, advanced delivery systems such as nanoparticles and hydrogels have been proposed to improve bioavailability and sustained release.18 In addition to its use as a coolant during scaling and root planing, local delivery systems have been explored to improve EGCG availability in periodontal tissues. Mucoadhesive gingival patches loaded with EGCG have been reported to modulate inflammatory cytokines by reducing interleukin-6 (IL-6) and increasing IL-10 expression, suggesting their potential for localized anti-inflammatory periodontal therapy. 19 EGCG-Cu injectable hydrogels have also shown antibacterial, immunomodulatory, and osteogenic effects in periodontitis models, supporting the value of localized sustained-release platforms for periodontal applications.20 Despite these advances, the clinical application of EGCG in periodontal therapy remains limited by several factors. Variability in study design, treatment protocol, EGCG concentration, exposure time, local availability, and formulation stability may contribute to discrepancies between experimental findings and clinical outcomes.6,18 Therefore, further research is needed to establish standardized protocols and optimize EGCG formulation and delivery strategies before its full potential as a bioactive adjunct in peri-odontal treatment can be confirmed.

Epigallocatechin gallate in adhesive and restorative dentistry

EGCG has attracted attention in adhesive and restorative dentistry because of its potential to improve resin-dentin interface stability and to provide antimicrobial activity against cariogenic microorganisms. The long-term degradation of dentin bonding is closely associated with endogenous matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which can degrade exposed collagen fibrils within the hybrid layer and contribute to the loss of bond stability over time.21 EGCG has been investigated as a dentin biomodifier and MMP-inhibitory agent for improving the durability of resin-dentin bonding. The combination of EGCG with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) wet-bonding has been reported to improve dentin-adhesive interface stability, supporting its potential role in preserving resin-dentin bonding.9 In addition, dentin biomodification strategies using natural bioactive compounds have been proposed to reinforce the dentin matrix by modifying collagen structure and improving resistance to degradation.22

Experimental studies have further shown that EGCG can be incorporated into dental adhesive systems. EGCG-incorporated dental adhesives have been reported to inhibit the growth of Streptococcus mutans while maintaining resin-dentin bond durability, suggesting that EGCG may provide both antibacterial and bonding-related benefits in adhesive dentistry.23 More recent material-based studies have also explored EGCG-encapsulated halloysite nanotubes in dental adhesives, showing sustained EGCG release and preserved MMP-9 inhibitory activity, which may help maintain dentin-resin interface integrity over time.24 Beyond its effects on bonding stability, EGCG exhibits antimicrobial activity against cariogenic microorganisms. EGCG has been shown to inhibit Streptococcus mutans biofilm formation by suppressing gtf gene expression, which interferes with glucan synthesis and early biofilm development.25 EGCG has also been reported to suppress cariogenic virulence factors of Streptococcus mutans, including acid production and acid tolerance, suggesting its relevance to reducing the cariogenic potential of dental biofilms.26 These properties may be beneficial for restorative dentistry because secondary caries at restoration margins is strongly associated with cariogenic biofilm activity.

EGCG may also be useful in dentin substrates affected by erosive or abrasive challenges. In eroded and abraded dentin, EGCG has been evaluated as a dentin biomodifier and was reported to improve bond strength on both sound and eroded-abraded dentin substrates.27 This finding suggests that EGCG may help enhance adhesive performance in compromised dentin conditions, although the quality of the adhesive interface may still be influenced by substrate status and aging time.27 Despite these promising findings, the clinical translation of EGCG in adhesive and restorative dentistry remains limited. Most available evidence is derived from in vitro studies, and the effects of EGCG may vary according to concentration, application time, solvent system, adhesive formulation, dentin substrate condition, and aging protocol.9,23,27 In addition, the incorporation of EGCG into adhesive formulations requires careful optimization to maintain material properties and curing efficiency. Encapsulation strategies, such as EGCG-loaded halloysite nanotubes, have therefore been explored to control release, preserve MMP-inhibitory activity, and maintain adhesive curing efficiency.24,28

In addition, recent work on methacrylated EGCG has suggested that chemical modification may improve the compatibility of EGCG with adhesive systems. EGCG-methacrylate monomers have been reported to reduce gelatinolytic activity within the hybrid layer while maintaining immediate and long-term bond strength, indicating a possible strategy for integrating EGCG bioactivity into resin-based materials.29 However, the performance of EGCG-containing adhesive systems remains concentration-dependent, as excessive loading may compromise physicochemical properties or bonding performance.28,29 Overall, EGCG has potential value in adhesive and restorative dentistry through its effects on MMP inhibition, dentin matrix biomodification, resin-dentin interface stabilization, and antimicrobial activity against cariogenic biofilms. However, further long-term clinical studies are still needed to determine whether these experimental benefits can be translated into durable improvements in restorative outcomes.

Epigallocatechin gallate in regenerative dentistry

EGCG has emerged as a promising bioactive compound in regenerative dentistry because of its broad biological activities and ability to interact with multiple molecular targets involved in inflammation, oxidative stress, and cellular regulation.3 In regenerative dentistry, these properties are relevant because tissue repair in the oral environment, particularly in periodontal, dentin-pulp, and bone defects, requires coordinated regulation of stem/progenitor cell activity, extracellular matrix remodeling, immune responses, and local microenvironmental conditions.8,20,30,31 EGCG has been shown to promote osteogenic differentiation and cellular proliferation, which are critical processes in tissue regeneration. In human periodontal ligament cells, EGCG enhances proliferation, osteogenic differentiation, and mineralization potential, suggesting its possible role in periodontal tissue repair.8 In stem cells from the apical papilla, EGCG promotes osteo-/odontogenic differentiation through activation of the bone morphogenetic protein (BMP)-Smad signaling pathway, supporting its relevance to dentin regeneration and regenerative endodontic applications.30

In addition to its effects on cell differentiation, EGCG may help create a more favorable regenerative microenvironment by reducing oxidative stress and inflammation. In a rat periodontitis model, EGCG reduced oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and alveolar bone loss through modulation of the Nrf2/HO-1/NLRP3/NF-κB p65 signaling pathway.7 These findings suggest that EGCG may contribute to periodontal tissue repair not only by supporting osteogenic responses, but also by attenuating inflammation-associated tissue destruction. Recent studies have also highlighted the potential role of EGCG in immunomodulation. EGCG-modified collagen membranes used in guided bone regeneration have been reported to promote new bone formation in vivo by recruiting M2 macrophages, enhancing growth factor expression, and increasing osteogenic differentiation-related markers.31 More recently, an injectable EGCG-Cu hydrogel for periodontitis was shown to inhibit Porphyromonas gingivalis, suppress M1 macrophage polarization, promote M2 polarization, and facilitate periodontal tissue regeneration, supporting the potential value of EGCG-based immunomodulatory biomaterials.20

In addition to its cellular and immunomodulatory effects, EGCG has been incorporated into biomaterial-based delivery systems to enhance its regenerative performance. EGCG-modified gelatin sponges have been shown to enhance bone formation in critical-sized rat calvarial defects, suggesting that EGCG-functionalized scaffolds may support bone tissue engineering applications.32 EGCG release from three-dimensional printed tricalcium phosphate scaffolds has also been reported to enhance in vitro osteogenic activity, anti-osteoclastogenic activity, and endothelial tube formation, indicating that scaffold-based EGCG delivery may provide multiple biological benefits for bone defect repair.33 Beyond single-function scaffolds, EGCG has increasingly been explored in multifunctional regenerative platforms that combine antibacterial activity, osteogenic stimulation, immunomodulation, angiogenesis, and controlled release. EGCG combined with curcumin on hydroxyapatite-coated titanium has been reported to enhance osteoblast growth, antibacterial activity, and chemopreventive performance, supporting the potential of combinational bioactive coatings for complex bone and implant-related applications.34 In addition, EGCG-modified bioactive core–shell fibers have been shown to improve antioxidant activity, angiogenesis, and bone regeneration, suggesting that EGCG-functionalized fibrous membranes may help improve the regenerative microenvironment.35

Despite these promising findings, several challenges remain in translating EGCG-based regenerative strategies into clinical applications. EGCG stability, release kinetics, dosage, biomaterial interaction, scaffold design, and the lack of long-term clinical data remain important considerations in the development of regenerative systems.10,20,33 Therefore, further studies are needed to standardize EGCG delivery platforms, clarify optimal concentrations, and determine whether the experimental benefits observed in periodontal, dentin-pulp, and bone regeneration models can be translated into predictable clinical outcomes. Overall, EGCG represents a multifunctional bioactive molecule with potential value in regenerative dentistry. Its combined effects on osteogenic/odontogenic differentiation, inflammation control, immunomodulation, and biomaterial integration provide a strong rationale for further development of EGCG-based regenerative strategies.

Translational challenges and limitations

Despite the promising biological and therapeutic potential of EGCG, several challenges remain that limit its translation into routine clinical applications in dentistry. These limitations are primarily related to its physicochemical properties, bioavailability, dose standardization, delivery efficiency, material compatibility, and inconsistencies across experimental and clinical studies. One of the major challenges associated with EGCG is its relatively low bioavailability and chemical instability. EGCG is susceptible to oxidation, degradation, and structural transformation under physiological conditions, which may reduce its biological activity and therapeutic effectiveness.3,10 These limitations are particularly relevant in the oral environment, where fluctuations in pH, oxygen exposure, enzymatic activity, and salivary components may further affect its stability and local persistence.10,18

Another critical issue is the lack of standardized delivery systems and application protocols. The therapeutic effects of EGCG are highly dependent on concentration, exposure time, formulation, and mode of delivery.6,10 Variability in these parameters across experimental and clinical studies may contribute to inconsistent findings, particularly when comparing in vitro, in vivo, and clinical outcomes.6,7 For example, although EGCG has been evaluated as an adjunct to scaling and root planing, clinical findings suggest that its additional benefit may depend on treatment protocol, application method, and local retention.6,36 In addition, differences in experimental models and study designs contribute to the variability in reported outcomes. Many studies investigating EGCG have been conducted under controlled laboratory or animal conditions that do not fully reproduce the complexity of the oral environment. In a rat periodontitis model, EGCG reduced oxidative stress, inflammation, and alveolar bone loss, supporting its biological activity in vivo.7 However, such experimental findings may not directly predict long-term clinical effectiveness because human periodontal lesions, salivary flow, microbial ecology, patient-related factors, and treatment compliance are more complex than controlled model systems.6,7,36

The integration of EGCG into dental materials and biomaterial-based systems also presents several challenges. While encapsulation systems, nanoparticle-based delivery, hydrogels, and scaffold-based approaches have been proposed to enhance EGCG stability and controlled release, these strategies require further optimization to ensure consistent release kinetics, biological activity, material performance, and biocompatibility.10,20,36 For example, EGCG-encapsulated halloysite nanotubes have been investigated in dental adhesive systems to provide sustained release and preserve MMP-9 inhibitory activity, but the formulation must still be optimized to maintain adhesive performance and curing efficiency.24,28 Similarly, injectable EGCG-Cu hydrogels have shown promise for localized periodontal therapy, but further validation is needed before such systems can be translated into routine clinical use.20

Safety and dosage considerations represent additional limitations. Although EGCG is generally regarded as a bioactive natural compound, its effects are concentration-dependent, and excessive or poorly controlled exposure may cause cytotoxicity or unintended cellular responses. 3,10 Therefore, determining optimal dosage ranges, exposure duration, release profiles, and long-term safety remains essential for clinical translation. Dose standardization remains another major obstacle for clinical translation. Across studies, EGCG concentrations have been reported using heterogeneous units, including μM, μg/mL, mg/mL, and mg/kg, making direct comparison difficult. 6,7,14,27 Moreover, EGCG may exert concentration-dependent effects, with insufficient doses failing to maintain biological activity and excessive doses potentially reducing cell viability or altering material properties.3,10,28 Delivery systems further complicate dose interpretation because the administered concentration may differ from the effective local concentration released over time.10,20,24 Therefore, future studies should report EGCG dose, release kinetics, exposure duration, and biological response in a more standardized manner.10,18 Overall, the molecular mechanisms, major dental applications, and translational challenges of EGCG are schematically summarized in Fig. 1 and further organized with representative evidence in Table 1. Future perspectives

Future research on EGCG in dentistry should prioritize the translation of its multifunctional biological properties into clinically applicable strategies. Successful clinical implementation will depend on advances in delivery technologies, standardized protocols, material optimization, and a clearer understanding of its mechanisms in oral tissues and microbial environments. A key future direction is the development of advanced delivery systems to improve the stability, bioavailability, and local retention of EGCG. Because EGCG is affected by environmental conditions and formulation-related factors, stabilizing systems and controlled-release platforms are important for maintaining its biological activity.10 Recent studies have explored injectable EGCG-Cu hydrogels and three-dimensional printed calcium phosphate scaffolds to achieve localized EGCG delivery for periodontal and bone-related applications.20,33 In addition, EGCG-loaded injectable hydrogels have recently been explored for extraction socket healing, suggesting a potential future direction for oral wound repair and post-extraction regenerative applications.37 Optimization of release kinetics, material compatibility, biological activity, and local retention will be essential before these platforms can be translated into predictable clinical use.10,18,29,37

Another important area is the integration of EGCG into bioactive dental materials. EGCG-encapsulated halloysite nanotubes have been incorporated into dental adhesives to provide sustained EGCG release and preserve MMP-9 inhibitory activity.24 Further studies have also evaluated the curing efficiency of EGCG-encapsulated halloysite nanotube-modified adhesives, indicating that material formulation must be carefully optimized to maintain both biological function and adhesive performance.28 In restorative dentistry, EGCG-based mineralizing systems, such as EGCG-amorphous calcium phosphate nanocomposites, may also provide future opportunities for caries prevention and demineralized enamel restoration.38 Future studies should therefore assess long-term bonding durability, antibacterial effects, curing behavior, remineralization performance, and mechanical properties under clinically relevant oral conditions.

In addition, growing evidence suggests that EGCG may influence oral host–microbe interactions and immune regulation. EGCG and green tea extracts have been reviewed for their activity against oral disease-associated organisms, including cariogenic and periodontopathic microorganisms. 5 A more recent review also summarized the effects of green tea extract and EGCG on oral diseases, further supporting their relevance to oral health research.39 Future microbiome-oriented studies should move beyond single-pathogen models and evaluate how EGCG affects multispecies biofilms, microbial ecology, and oral dysbiosis under conditions that better reflect the clinical oral environment. The immunomodulatory effects of EGCG also represent a promising research direction. An injectable hydrogel incorporating EGCG-Cu complexes has been reported to provide synergistic antibacterial, immunomodulatory, and osteogenic effects in periodontitis, including effects relevant to macrophage polarization and periodontal tissue regeneration.20 EGCG-loaded injectable hydrogels have also shown potential in modulating oxidative stress, inflammation, and bone-healing responses in extraction socket models.37 These findings suggest that EGCG-based strategies may be developed not only for antimicrobial or antioxidant purposes, but also for regulating host immune responses during tissue repair and regeneration.

Importantly, future research should focus on establishing standardized experimental and clinical protocols. Variability in EGCG concentration, formulation, application method, exposure time, and outcome measurements remains a major limitation when comparing results across studies.6,10,18 Although EGCG has been evaluated clinically as an adjunct to scaling and root planing, available clinical evidence remains limited, with small sample sizes and relatively short follow-up periods.6 Therefore, future clinical studies should include larger populations, longer follow-up periods, standardized treatment protocols, and well-defined periodontal, restorative, wound-healing, and regenerative outcome measures.6,18 Overall, the future development of EGCG in dentistry will depend on the integration of advanced delivery systems, biomaterial innovation, microbiome research, immunomodulatory mechanisms, and robust clinical evidence. These directions will be essential for transforming EGCG from a promising natural compound into a clinically reliable therapeutic strategy.

Conclusion

EGCG is a multifunctional bioactive compound with potential applications in periodontal therapy, adhesive and restorative dentistry, and regenerative dentistry. Its anti-oxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, matrix-modulating, and immunomodulatory properties provide a biological basis for controlling oral inflammation, stabilizing resin-dentin interfaces, and supporting tissue repair. However, current evidence is still largely derived from in vitro and preclinical studies, and clinical translation remains limited by challenges related to bioavailability, chemical stability, dosage standardization, delivery efficiency, material compatibility, and variability in clinical protocols. Future research should focus on standardized experimental designs, optimized delivery systems, long-term safety evaluation, and well-designed clinical trials to determine whether EGCG can be developed into a reliable adjunctive strategy for oral healthcare.
