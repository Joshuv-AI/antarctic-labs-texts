Genome-wide impact of codon usage bias on translation optimization in Drosophila melanogaster
Authors: Xinkai Wu, Mengze Xu, Jian-Rong Yang, Jian Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Accuracy and efficiency are fundamental to mRNA translation. Codon usage bias is widespread across species. Despite the long-standing association between optimized codon usage and improved translation, our understanding of its evolutionary basis and functional effects remains limited. Drosophila is widely used to study codon usage bias, but genome-scale experimental data are scarce. Using high-resolution mass spectrometry data from Drosophila melanogaster, we show that optimal codons have lower translation errors than nonoptimal codons after accounting for these biases. Genomic-scale analysis of ribosome profiling data shows that optimal codons are translated more rapidly than nonoptimal codons. Although we find no long-term selection favoring synonymous mutations in D. melanogaster after diverging from D. simulans, we identify signatures of positive selection driving codon optimization in the D. melanogaster population. These findings expand our understanding of the functional consequences of codon optimization and serve as a foundation for future investigations.

Introduction

The translation of messenger RNA (mRNA) into proteins is a fundamental process in all domains of life. However, translation is not completely error-free. A common type of translational error is amino acid misincorporation, occurring at a rate of approximately 10−3 to 10−4 per codon site1,2. Despite their contribution to environmental adaptability3–5, translation errors are generally detrimental to organisms as they reduce the functional activities of proteins and can lead to toxic protein misfolding and misinteraction6–12. Consequently, it is not surprising that translational fidelity has been shown to be optimized through strong natural selection13–20.

The optimization of translational fidelity, however, comes with its costs. Early studies have demonstrated a trade-off between accuracy and efficiency in mRNA translation21–23. Increasing fidelity at the expense of efficiency may not necessarily be advantageous, as maintaining translational efficiency is crucial for ensuring the availability of limited resources like ribosomes and tRNAs. This is especially significant for rapidly dividing cells24,25. Notably, when comparing the translational efficiency of the same codon in different genomic contexts, an increase in accuracy at the cost of efficiency can only be observed at genomic sites where amino acid misincorporations are highly detrimental to the cell26. Mechanistically, the trade-off between accuracy and efficiency is underpinned by factors such as Magnesium concentration23 and mRNA secondary structure26.

However, beyond the aforementioned site-specific regulation, the orchestration of translational accuracy and efficiency encompasses a multitude of mechanisms, creating a more intricate relationship when viewed from various angles. For instance, the interplay between accuracy and efficiency across distinct types of codons is substantially influenced by tRNA selection during translational elongation15. Firstly, codons associated with more abundant aminoacyl-tRNAs (aa-tRNAs) are believed to be translated more efficiently than codons associated with less abundant aa-tRNAs17,27,28. For instance, Ribo-Seq data in yeast substantiates that ribosome occupancy of codons is inversely proportional to their corresponding tRNA densities, suggesting that codons with a greater abundance of corresponding tRNA in the cell exhibit faster translation elongation rates29,30. Secondly, during translation elongation, a cognate aa-tRNA is favored for peptidyl transfer, whereas a noncognate aa-tRNA tends to dissociate from the ribosome in a proofreading step before peptidyl transfer31,32. This raises the question of whether the accuracy-efficiency trade-off, which is observed among different genomic sites of the same type of codon, also applies to the accuracy-efficiency relationship across different codon types. To answer this question, comprehensive empirical data that provide matched estimations of accuracy and efficiency on a genome-wide scale are required. Recent technological advancements have facilitated this pursuit. On the one hand, ribosome profiling (Ribo-Seq) has emerged as a powerful tool for quantifying translational elongation rates by examining ribosome aggregation at specific genomic positions33–37. On the other hand, advances in mass spectrometry analytical pipelines have enabled the systematic identification of translation errors at the proteome level, achieved by disentangling erroneously translated peptides from their correctly translated counterparts20,38.

Apart from its functional genomic implications, the earlier mentioned question carries significant evolutionary implications, particularly in terms of the molecular mechanisms that underlie natural selection for synonymous codon usage bias (CUB). CUB refers to the phenomenon observed among codons that encode the same amino acid, where certain optimal or preferred codons are more frequently used than nonoptimal or rare codons. CUB is prevalent across organisms39 and is influenced by mutation40–43, drift44, selection12,39,45–47, or their combinations48,49. Previous studies based on simple organisms such as bacteria and yeasts have shown that CUB significantly impacts the efficiency and accuracy of mRNA translation, potentially contributing to organismal fitness and adaptation15,16,18,19,46,50–54. Despite this, the interplay between translation efficiency and accuracy at the individual codon level still poses a challenging aspect to fully understand and characterize, particularly when considering complex multicellular organisms.

Drosophila has been extensively utilized as a model to investigate CUB, predominantly in the realms of population genetics and evolutionary biology, with the underlying assumption of a link between CUB and translational optimization (e.g., refs. 55–57). For instance, prior studies have demonstrated that evolutionarily conserved amino acid residues tend to favor preferred codons, implying that conserved residues necessitate heightened translation accuracy in Drosophila14. Notably, preferred codons in Drosophila frequently terminate with G or C, possibly due to selection for codon bias58. Synonymous sites generally experience weak selective constraints in Drosophila and other species59,60. Analyzing fixed versus polymorphic synonymous mutations in five genes of D. melanogaster and D. simulans has revealed a preference for mutations from nonoptimal to optimal codons, while the reverse is disfavored in the lineage leading to D. simulans13. However, this analysis has also indicated a reduction in selection pressure on synonymous mutations in the lineage of D. melanogaster after its divergence from the most recent common ancestor with D. simulans. The observed difference in these two lineages likely arose due to a smaller effective population size of D. melanogaster compared to D. simulans13,61,62, a change in the selective coefficient on the synonymous sites due to changing ecological conditions63, a shift in mutational bias towards A/T alleles in the D. melanogaster lineage64–67, or a combination of these factors.

While theoretical studies have long suggested that natural selection for translation optimization leads to increased codon bias levels47,68, experimental evidence confirming the impact of codon optimization on translation accuracy or speed in Drosophila remains limited. Notable experiments have shown that introducing less preferred synonymous codons into the Adh gene reduces ADH protein levels and ethanol tolerance in D. melanogaster69,70. In addition, in vitro translation experiments employing luciferase reporter genes have underscored codon optimization’s ability to enhance ribosome translation elongation rates in D. melanogaster71. Despite these individual experiments, there remains a significant gap in our understanding due to the absence of comprehensive genome-scale experimental data exploring the influence of codon optimization on translation in Drosophila. This is particularly notable given the widespread use of Drosophila in CUB studies. Therefore, further research is urgently needed to fill this critical knowledge gap comprehensively.

Notably, reports have focused on a limited number of genes that indicate relaxed selection on CUB in the lineage leading to D. melanogaster, attributed to a reduction in population size in this species13. Therefore, it is essential to provide further evidence to determine whether the widely accepted idea of natural selection for codon optimization47,68 applies to D. melanogaster and how this relates to the evolutionary drivers of CUB and their functional consequences.

This study aimed to address these questions by evaluating translational efficiency and accuracy at the genome-wide level in D. melanogaster. We analyzed high-resolution mass spectrometry datasets72 to measure translation errors and utilized Ribo-Seq data that we previously generated73 to assess elongation rates at the codon level in D. melanogaster. Our findings provide genome-wide evidence that codon optimization increases both translation accuracy and efficiency in this organism. In addition, we revisited the evolutionary forces influencing the evolution of synonymous mutations using population genomic data from D. melanogaster. Our study serves to bridge an existing knowledge gap and contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of how natural selection influences codon usage in the translational process during evolution.

Results

Detection of amino acid misincorporation using mass spectrometry data

Codon-anticodon mispairing is a key source of amino acid misincorporation, leading to translation errors (Fig. 1a). To detect and analyze individual misincorporation events, we thoroughly examined the previously published high-resolution mass spectrometry datasets comprising 17 different samples collected from 15 representative time points during D. melanogaster development72. Each of these samples consisted of four biological replicates (see “Methods”). Since the mass spectrometry data were generated using the Oregon-R strain72 and the reference genome was based on the ISO-1 strain of D. melanogaster74, we first identified single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the Oregon-R strain75. Then, we incorporated these SNPs into the ISO-1 reference genome to create the Oregon-R reference coding sequences and mitigate SNP effects on translation error detection. We used MaxQuant76 to distinguish between base peptides (those without errors or amino acid substitutions, BP) and dependent peptides (those with residual modifications or substitutions, DP). We identified a total of 1705 distinct codon positions showing amino acid differences between base and dependent peptides, excluding two possible adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I) RNA editing sites in D. melanogaster77, as A-to-I RNA editing can post-transcriptionally alter the protein sequence78,79. Out of these positions, 993 were found in one sample, 265 in two samples, 117 in three samples, and 330 in at least four samples (Supplementary Fig. 2). Given the distribution of these codon positions across samples, we needed to determine if the observed translation errors occurred randomly. To gauge the anticipated number of translation errors detected across multiple libraries under random conditions, we performed simulations considering all the codon positions with the detected intensity of their residing peptides in a sample. We assumed the occurrences of errors for one amino acid incorporated followed a Poisson distribution and randomly assigned errors to each codon position in the genome based on the observed intensity of the peptides covering that position in a library. We conducted the simulation for each library and summarized the number of codon positions that showed translation errors in 1, 2, or more samples. Of note, our simulations accounted for the differences in the expression profiles and coverage of each codon position across samples.

Compared to the observed numbers, the simulations showed a significant decrease in the expected number of codon positions with amino acid differences in at least two out of 68 samples (41.76% vs. 1.02% for observed versus simulated, respectively; Supplementary Fig. 3). This suggests that most of the observed amino acid differences detected in multiple libraries are likely caused by hotspots of translation errors or other confounding factors, rather than random occurrences. Although the Oregon-R strain has undergone additional inbreeding and selection since its establishment in 192580, which has reduced genetic variation within the strain, it is not genetically identical across all individuals75. Many mutations may have accumulated over time, with some of them potentially segregating at low frequencies within this strain. Upon further examination of the genome sequencing data of the Oregon-R strain75, we identified 25 sites possibly caused by very rare SNPs that were not detected in our initial SNP curation when preparing the reference library for MaxQuant analysis. These sites included two amino acid differences detected in one library and 23 amino acid differences present in at least two libraries (“Methods”). To conservatively identify translation error sites, we only considered the 991 codon positions that showed amino acid substitutions present in a single sample (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Data 1), with 14.57 ± 1.01 (mean ± s.e.) codon positions showing translation errors in a sample (Fig. 1b).

The 991 codon positions that exhibited putative translation errors are distributed across 711 genes, which are significantly enriched in ribosomal proteins (Supplementary Fig. 5). This observation could be attributed to the high expression levels of these genes, resulting in a greater likelihood of detecting errors within them. The number of sites with translation errors varies across libraries; however, we did not find a significant correlation between the number of error sites and the total number of codon positions covered in a library (Supplementary Fig. 6). Interestingly, we observed an overall decrease in the number of errors as development progresses (Spearman’s rho = − 0.425, P = 0.0003, Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 7). Notably, remarkably higher numbers of sites with translational errors were found in larvae (83–85 h) and adult males compared to other stages. Upon removing these two stages, the decreasing trend between the number of sites with translational errors and developmental progression became even more pronounced (Spearman’s rho = − 0.558, P = 3.616 × 10−6, Supplementary Fig. 8). The biology behind the relationship warrants further studies.

Two types of errors can occur due to codon-anticodon mismatches. Near-cognate errors (NeCEs) happen when a tRNA binds to a codon differing by a single nucleotide from the correct one. Noncognate errors (NoCEs) occur when a tRNA binds to a codon differing by two or more nucleotides from its complementary anticodon. For each translation error event, we inferred possible mistranslation from the original codon to the possible destination codons (codons that encode the misincorporated amino acid) based on codon usage frequencies in D. melanogaster transcriptomes, categorizing them as NeCEs or NoCEs (Methods, see Fig. 1c for illustration). We estimated that 211.3 (the decimal reflects one out of multiple possible mismatch types in a detected misincorporation) of the 991 translation errors (21.3%) were NeCEs, while 779.7 (78.7%) were NoCEs (Fig. 1d). In contrast, our simulation, based on the assumption that codon mistranslation probabilities are proportional to the product of their usage and the usage of the destination codons, suggested that 13.8% of errors were NeCEs and 86.2% were NoCEs, indicating a significant difference (P = 5.73 × 10⁻6, one-sided Fisher’s exact test). These results confirm that NeCEs are more likely than NoCEs to occur20, presumably due to the higher complementarity between the tRNA and the codon, facilitating incorrect binding.

Among the 211.3 inferred NeCEs, 111.0 occurred in the first codon position, 66.0 in the second, and 34.3 in the third. Comparing observed NeCE mismatch distributions with simulated ones revealed similar mismatches in the third codon position (P = 0.49, two-sided Fisher’s exact test; Fig. 1e). However, observed mismatches were significantly enriched in the first codon position (P = 0.035, one-sided Fisher’s exact test; Fig. 1e), suggesting that first-position mismatches significantly contribute to translation errors. Significantly fewer mismatches than expected were observed in the second position (P = 0.007, one-sided Fisher’s exact test), aligning with structural insights that ribosomal decoding stability largely depends on the base pair at the second codon-anticodon position81.

Notably, G:G mismatches between codons and anticodons were the most prevalent, constituting approximately 24.7% (52.2/211.3) of NeCE instances. Specifically, 30.6, 9.0, and 12.6 of these G:G mismatches were inferred in the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd codon position, respectively (Fig. 1f). The presence of two stable hydrogen bonds in G:G pairing82 suggests its high tolerance as a codon-anticodon mismatch in translation elongation, making it a significant source of translation errors.

Optimal codons are translated more accurately in D. melanogaster

The translation error rates have been shown to vary among codons20,54,83,84. To test whether optimal codons are translated more accurately in Drosophila, we calculated the relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU)85 values for each specific codon. Unlike the traditional RSCU process that uses a small number of highly expressed genes to calculate codon usage frequency, we took into account the gene expression levels from various developmental stages of D. melanogaster in our analysis, which allowed us to determine a more representative average usage frequency for codons in the transcriptomes (“Methods”). The codons with RSCU ≥ 1 were classified as optimal, whereas the rest were considered nonoptimal (Supplementary Data 2). For each of the 61 codon types, we followed the method from a previous study54 and calculated the translation error rate as the ratio of the total intensity of mistranslated peptides (DP) to that of all peptides (BP + DP) mapped to that codon in a mass spectrometry library (Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Data 3). This estimation reflects the fraction of misincorporated amino acids out of the total amino acids encoded by a codon type in the proteome.

One potential bias in investigating the impact of codon optimization on translation error is that, due to the low translation error rate (10−3 to 10−4 per codon site1,2), sampling imbalance might generate varying standard errors for the translation error estimates of different codons, causing some rare codons to display no detected translation errors in the mass spectrometry data. To illustrate, we noticed that when not accounting for the imbalance from codon usage frequency in the analysis, optimal codons, which are typically more frequently used, showed more translation errors than nonoptimal ones, which are less frequently used (P = 2.23 × 10⁻11, one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test; Fig. 2a). Notably, calculations of codon occurrences in all peptides detected by mass spectrometry confirmed that optimal codons occurred significantly more frequently than nonoptimal codons in the mass spectrometry libraries (P = 3.82 × 10⁻13, one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test; Fig. 2b). Furthermore, in a library, the intensities of all peptides (BP + DP) covering genomic positions with translation errors were significantly higher than those without errors (P = 3.82 × 10⁻13, one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test; Fig. 2c), and codon types that exhibited translation errors tended to have significantly higher peptide intensity than those without errors (P = 1.26 × 10⁻11, one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test; Fig. 2d). In addition, optimal codons tend to have significantly higher peptide intensities than non-optimal codons in a library (P = 3.82 × 10⁻13, one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test; Fig. 2e). We simulated error detection across all codon types using a uniform error rate of 2.94 × 10⁻5 errors per amino acid, which was determined by attributing an error rate of zero to codons with no detected error event in a library and computing the mean error rate for all codon types (“Methods”). As expected, many rare codons did not exhibit translation errors when their corresponding peptides have only low signal intensity in the mass spectrometry data. However, increasing mass spectrometry throughput led to more error detections (Fig. 2f), supporting the notion that translation errors are more likely detected for more frequently used codons. In conclusion, our simulations suggest that the infrequent usage of rare or nonoptimal codons, along with low translation error rates, can significantly bias the estimation of translation error rates. We employed three strategies to reduce bias in comparing translation error rates. First, we controlled for sample size imbalance between optimal and non-optimal codons. We balanced the sample size by fixing non-optimal codons and down-sampling optimal codons to ensure the total intensity of both was nearly the same (within 0.5%). Then, we focused on the sampled optimal codons and calculated the translation error rate as the ratio of the total intensity of mistranslated peptides to the total peptide intensity. We repeated this process 1000 times for each library and calculated the median translation error rate for optimal codons. In the second strategy, we omitted codons without error events from each library, resulting in an average error rate of (9.30 ± 1.20) × 10−5 errors per amino acid, which is close to the range of previously estimated error rates in bacteria (between 10−4 and 10−3 errors per amino acid)1,2. We employed a linear mixed model to analyze the relationship between codon translation error rates and RSCU, with the experimental replicates as random effects. The results showed a significant negative correlation between the translation error rate (log10) and the RSCU value (slope coefficient = −0.326, P = 7.91 × 10−18; Fig. 3b), reinforcing the notion that optimal codons exhibit lower translation error rates than nonoptimal codons. Figure 3c illustrates this anti-correlation with an example from pupae.

In the third method, following a previous study54, we considered only amino acids where all synonymous codons showed translation errors in a sample. We calculated the relative translation error rate (RMR) by dividing a codon’s error rate by the average error rate of its synonymous codons (see “Methods”). We found a significantly negative correlation between RMR and RSCU when pooling data from all samples together (slope coefficient = − 0.870, P = 1.78 × 10−4, Fig. 3d).

Collectively, these findings suggest that although the estimation of translation error rates is often influenced by imbalances in codon occurrences, optimal codons generally exhibit lower translation errors than nonoptimal codons after accounting for these imbalances.

Optimal codons are translated faster than nonoptimal codons in D. melanogaster

Previous studies in D. melanogaster have shown that optimal codons are translated faster than nonoptimal codons, as evidenced by reduced ADH protein levels when unpreferred synonymous codons were introduced into the Adh gene69,70 and increased translation elongation rates in codon-optimized luciferase reporter genes71. Given the variable CUB among genes, we investigated whether this pattern could be detected using Ribo-Seq data reflecting in vivo conditions. To measure the impact of codon optimization on translation elongation, we analyzed mRNA-Seq and ribosome profiling data that we had previously generated across various developmental stages and tissues of D. melanogaster73. This dataset included ten samples, encompassing 0–2, 2–6, 6–12, and 12–24 h embryos; larvae, pupae, female and male bodies; and female and male heads. While these samples do not directly correspond to the samples used in the current mass spectrometry-based analysis of translation errors, they provide a comprehensive representation of gene expression and translation efficiency across different developmental stages and tissues in D. melanogaster. We mapped the ribosome-protected fragments (RPFs) to the reference genome, inferred the A-site location on each RPF, and calculated the ribosome density on each codon position in the coding region (CDS) of a gene. In parallel, we calculated the mRNA density on each codon position using matched mRNA-Seq data for each sample. For each codon position, we calculated the mRNA-calibrated ribosomal coverage (mRC) by contrasting the RPF with mRNA coverage. To account for variations in translation efficiency between genes, we calculated the gene-normalized mRC (gmRC) value for each codon position as the ratio of mRC to the average mRC value within that gene (Fig. 4a). We excluded the first 15 and last five codons of a CDS from the analysis, as they are known to produce aberrant ribosome blockage due to translation start or termination86. As Ribo-Seq can only capture a stationary snapshot of the translation processes, it is unsurprising that 20–33% of the codons exhibited no RPF detection in the expressed genes in a sample (Supplementary Fig. 10). These zero-RPF coverage codons could be under-sequenced, untranslated, or rapidly translated. For each of the ten samples, the optimal codons displayed significantly lower gmRC values than the nonoptimal ones, suggesting faster translation. This conclusion held whether zero-RPF codons were excluded (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 11) or retained (Supplementary Fig. 12).

We expected that optimal codons would be substantially enriched in the bottom 1% of codons exhibiting the lowest gmRC values (see Fig. 4c for 0–2 h embryos; excluding codon positions without RPF coverage). Indeed, in all ten samples, optimal codons were significantly enriched among these rapidly elongating codons (Fig. 4d). To assess whether nonoptimal codons were enriched at stalling sites, we selected the top 1% of codons with the highest gmRC values as stalling sites within a sample. In all the ten samples, the nonoptimal codons were significantly enriched in these slowly elongating codons (Fig. 4e). To further control for potential biases caused by gene-specific factors that might influence the relationship between codon optimization and gmRC values, for each gene, we calculated ΔgmRC, the median gmRC value of nonoptimal codons minus that of optimal codons for that gene. We found that ΔgmRC is consistently greater than zero in all samples, regardless of whether zero-RPF codons were excluded (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 13) or included (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 14). In summary, our data provide genome-wide evidence that in Drosophila tissues, optimal codons are translated more rapidly than nonoptimal ones, a pattern observed even after further controlling for possible gene-specific effects.

Detecting positive selection on the synonymous mutations that change codon optimality in D. melanogaster

To detect the signature of positive selection on synonymous changes in the lineage of D. melanogaster at the genome-wide level, we carried out the asymptotic McDonald-Kreitman test87,88. As a neutral baseline for comparison, we focused on the 8–30 nt region of short introns (≤65 nt), which are considered neutral in Drosophila89–91 (“Methods”). We used D. yakuba as an outgroup to polarize the fixed synonymous mutations in D. melanogaster after it diverged from D. simulans approximately 5.4 million years ago92. We employed the polymorphic data from the Drosophila Genetic Reference Panel 2 (DGRP2) database of D. melanogaster93,94 in the asymptotic MK test. We categorized fixed and polymorphic synonymous mutations into two groups: optimization (increasing RSCU) and deoptimization (decreasing RSCU), estimating the fraction of synonymous substitutions driven to fixation by positive selection (α) for each mutation category. Our findings revealed an α of −0.018 (95% confidence interval (CI): −0.068 to 0.032) for codon optimization substitutions and 0.013 (95% CI: −0.039 to 0.065) for codon deoptimization substitutions (Fig. 5a, b). As expected95–100, when categorizing D. melanogaster genes into three groups based on their median expression levels (see “Methods”), we observed substantial differences in the prevalence of optimal codons. The proportions of optimal codons within the highly (n = 5357 genes), moderately (n = 3575), and lowly (n = 4923) expressed gene categories were 0.605, 0.565, and 0.540, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 15). For highly expressed genes, our results were somewhat equivocal. When we employed all short introns as a neutral control, we observed α values of 0.105 (0.052 to 0.158) for codon optimization and 0.117 (0.067 to 0.167) for codon deoptimization. However, when using only the short introns from these highly expressed genes as a neutral control, α values were 0.008 (− 0.076 to 0.092) for codon optimization and 0.021 (−0.067 to 0.108) for codon deoptimization (Fig. 5a, b). Conversely, we found no positive selection on synonymous mutations in moderately and lowly expressed genes (Fig. 5a, b). Taken together, we did not find conclusive evidence that natural selection has favored synonymous mutations during the long-term evolution of the D. melanogaster lineage following its divergence from D. simulans.

The absence of positive selection on synonymous mutations in the D. melanogaster lineage could be attributed to various factors, including a smaller effective population size in D. melanogaster compared to D. simulans13,61,62, changes in selection intensity due to shifting ecological conditions63, and a stronger mutational bias from C/G towards A/T alleles in the D. melanogaster lineage64–67. Temporal fluctuations or declines in these factors, coupled with their intricate interactions, could have potentially masked the signals of positive selection on synonymous mutations or violated the assumption in the asymptotic MK test.

In contrast, a comparison of different classes of synonymous mutations in the D. melanogaster population may offer an alternative approach to detect signals of positive selection. This approach is less susceptible to factors that could have fluctuated or continued to evolve during long-term evolution. However, conflicting results have been obtained from previous studies on this issue. While some studies provided evidence for selection favoring preferred codons59,61,101, others did not find support for this conclusion63,102,103. To address this discrepancy, we analyzed the frequencies of codon-optimizing and deoptimizing mutations using the larger sample size provided by the DGRP2 dataset. Our results revealed that codon-optimization mutations exhibit significantly higher derived allele synonymous frequencies compared to codon-deoptimization mutations (Fig. 5c). Furthermore, codon-optimizing mutations showed significantly higher derived allele frequencies compared to mutations within short introns, while codon-deoptimizing mutations showed significantly lower derived allele frequencies than the mutations within short introns (Fig. 5c). These trends persisted when analyzing genes based on different expression categories (Fig. 5c). In summary, these findings support the hypothesis that codon-optimizing mutations are favored by natural selection, while codon-deoptimization mutations are subject to negative selection in D. melanogaster populations.

Discussion

Both translational accuracy and speed are crucial for efficient cellular protein synthesis. Decades of CUB studies in Drosophila, mainly from the angles of population genetics and evolutionary biology (e.g., refs. 13,14,47,55,56,104,105), have bolstered the notion that natural selection drives biased synonymous codon usage to enhance protein synthesis accuracy and/or speed14,47,68. For instance, it was previously shown that preferred codons were significantly more prevalent at conserved amino acid codons than at non-conserved ones in 38 genes between D. melanogaster and other species14. This pattern was also evident when comparing the genomes of D. melanogaster with closely related species, D. simulans and D. yakuba, or the most inferred most recent common ancestor of D. melanogaster and D. simulans (Supplementary Fig. 16 and 17). Although these findings support the notion that conserved amino acid residues necessitate more accurate translation by using optimal codons14, there is a noticeable gap in empirical data directly validating the hypothesis that codon optimization boosts protein translation accuracy. Similarly, few pieces of evidence confirm that codon optimization increases translation elongation speed in Drosophila, except for some case studies, like the Adh gene69,70, and in vitro translation experiments using the luciferase reporter gene71. In this genome-wide study, we offer direct evidence that codon optimization enhances translation accuracy and elongation speed in D. melanogaster. Our population genomics data analysis further underscores the concept that natural selection prefers codon-optimizing mutations and negatively selects against codon-deoptimizing mutations in the population of D. melanogaster. These insights serve as a bridge, linking functional results with the evolutionary underpinnings of CUB in this model species.

This study identified translational errors by analyzing high-resolution mass spectrometry datasets containing samples from 15 developmental time points in D. melanogaster72. Notably, owing to the low translation error rate (ranging from 10−4 to 10−3 per codon site1,2), imbalanced sampling can result in some rare codons (typically unpreferred) not displaying any detected translation errors in the mass spectrometry data. This has the potential to skew estimates of translation error rates significantly and underscores the need for caution when comparing error rates across codons. To address this bias, we employed three strategies, all of which supported the conclusion that optimal codons in D. melanogaster exhibit significantly lower translation error rates than their nonoptimal counterparts after correcting for imbalances. Notably, a recent study in E. coli has also presented evidence of higher translational accuracy associated with optimal codons compared to nonoptimal ones54. These findings suggest that codon optimization might represent a conserved strategy across evolution for enhancing translation accuracy in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes.

In this study, we focused only on translation errors present in a single sample, which might have led to the omission of some genuine errors due to translation error hotspots. To address this concern, we also analyzed the 1350 amino acid substitutions that occurred in no more than three samples (after removing the rare SNPs) and still observed an inverse relationship between the translation error rate and the RSCU value of a codon type (Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19). These results support the robustness of our conclusion that optimal codons are translated more accurately than non-optimal ones. It is important to note that our current methodology relies on the intensity differences between dependent and base peptides as a proxy for error rates. However, we recognize that variations in ionization efficiency are known limitations within the field and can impact the quantitative accuracy of our results. To further improve the precision of peptide quantification in proteomic studies, the development and implementation of methods to correct ionization efficiency variations would be a significant advancement.

Initially regarded as neutral44,106, synonymous mutations are now recognized to be subject to either weak12,39,45,47 or strong purifying selection46,104,105. Despite the large effective population size of D. melanogaster (estimated at around 106), which should theoretically boost the efficiency of natural selection107, neither previous studies13,61–63 nor this study have found definitive proof of positive selection on synonymous mutations in the lineage of D. melanogaster after diverging from D. simulans. In contrast, significant signals of positive selection have been detected on the synonymous mutations in the D. simulans lineage13,61,108. Notably, a more pronounced mutational bias from C/G toward A/T alleles was inferred in the D. melanogaster lineage compared to the D. simulans lineage64–67,109. Given that translationally favored codons consistently end with G or C in Drosophila58, this mutation bias is anticipated to diminish the prevalence of optimal codons in D. melanogaster. Indeed, our examination of codon occurrences in the genomes of D. melanogaster, D. simulans, and D. yakuba revealed that the prevalence of optimal codons was significantly lower in the D. melanogaster genome than in the other two species, or even when compared to the inferred sequences of the most recent common ancestor of D. melanogaster and D. simulans (Supplementary Fig. 20). The driving force responsible for the shift in mutation bias toward A/T in D. melanogaster lineage remains unclear. It is possible that this alteration in mutational bias, in conjunction with changes in effective population size and shifting ecological conditions in D. melanogaster after its divergence from D. simulans, obscures or diminishes the effectiveness of positive selection on synonymous mutations during evolution in the D. melanogaster lineage13,61–63. However, by comparing derived allele frequencies within D. melanogaster populations, we have provided support for the hypothesis that natural selection favors codon-optimizing mutations, while codon-deoptimization mutations are prone to negative selection. It’s important to note that not all codon-deoptimizing mutations are necessarily deleterious in D. melanogaster, as some may prove advantageous in specific genomic contexts110. In summary, our study effectively bridges the gap between the functional consequences of CUB and the underlying evolutionary driving forces.

Previous studies have revealed the existence of a general trade-off between translation accuracy and speed21,22,111. Such a trade-off has recently been deciphered for the same type of codon occurring at different genomic positions, driven by factors such as magnesium concentration23 and RNA secondary structure26. Our findings, however, propose a different scenario, where optimal codons are both more accurate and faster in translation compared to nonoptimal codons. To further demonstrate this point, for each of the ten samples with both mRNA-Seq and Ribo-Seq data available, we divided codon positions into two categories based on their gmRC values: the ones above the median were arbitrarily categorized as slow sites and ones below the median are categorized as fast sites. We then collected all the error sites detected in the mass spectrometry samples of similar developmental stages, merged them, and counted the number of occurrences of error sites in slow and fast sites. The data showed that error sites were significantly higher in the slow sites category than the fast ones, regardless of whether we considered all codon types or individually considered optimal and nonoptimal codon types (Supplementary Fig. 21). Overall, we suggest that, when examining distinct codons encoding the same amino acid, the accuracy-speed trade-off might not apply. The role of tRNA abundance is pivotal in the translation decoding process and the competition among tRNA molecules for codon binding impacts translation efficiency and accuracy. Typically, optimal codons are decoded by more prevalent tRNAs, while nonoptimal codons are decoded by rarer tRNAs112–118. It’s plausible that natural selection has influenced tRNA abundance within cells, though further research is necessary to validate this hypothesis.

In addition to regulating translation accuracy and efficiency, synonymous changes may affect precursor mRNA splicing119, alter mRNA secondary structure120, control translation initiation121, and influence co-translational protein folding by modulating local translation elongation rates117. Beyond the harmful effects observed in yeast46 and associations with human diseases122,123, it’s becoming increasingly clear that synonymous mutations may contribute to adaptive evolution more frequently than previously assumed, as seen in microbes124. Our study sheds light on the driving forces behind natural selection on synonymous mutations related to codon optimization and deoptimization. This underscores the significance of gaining a comprehensive understanding of CUB, which in turn enriches our comprehension of the translation process, its influence on cellular functions, and the evolutionary factors shaping codon adaptation.

Methods

Identifying translation error events using mass spectrometry data

We downloaded mass spectrometry datasets of 17 distinct samples from 15 representative time points of the Oregon-R strain of D. melanogaster from the Proteomics Identifications Database (PRIDE)125 with the identifier PXD00569172. These samples encompassed four stages of embryos (0–2, 4–6, 10–12, and 18–20 h old), four stages of larvae (L1, L2, early L3, and L3 crawling larvae), five stages of pupae (P1 to P5), virgin males and females at 4 h after hatching, and one-week-old adult male and female flies. The genome sequencing data of the Oregon-R strain was downloaded from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession SRP03523775. We aligned the next-generation sequencing (NGS) reads to the ISO-1 reference genome (FlyBase r6.45 http://www.flybase.org) using Spliced Transcripts Alignment to a Reference (STAR) 2.7.10a126. We identified the single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with FreeBayes 1.3.5127 (using parameters --use-best-n-alleles 4 -m 20 -q 20 -F 0.1 -C 5 --pooled-continuous) and retained the identified SNPs with mapping quality >10.

Based on the Oregon-R strain’s genome sequencing results, we generated reference protein sequences for mass spectrometry data processing by combining SNP-incorporated sequences with corresponding reference sequences together. MaxQuant 2.1.0.076 was used to identify the base and dependent peptides at a 1% False Discovery Rate (FDR). The translation error event candidates were detected using previously published procedures20 applying the Dependent Peptide Search algorithm within MaxQuant software. Specifically, for every amino acid substitution, we only retained dependent peptide identifications with a difference between the observed mass shift and the AA substitution’s mass shift of no more than 0.005 Da, while the observed mass shift was localized on the substitution’s original AA with a probability no less than 0.95. False Discovery Rate (FDR) was controlled at 1%, and substitutions explainable by AA modifications were removed before detection, as previously described at a 1% FDR.

Simulating translation error events across multiple samples

To estimate the expected number of translation errors across multiple libraries under random conditions, we performed simulations considering the detected intensity of peptides at each codon position in a sample. Assuming a total of 106 amino acids per proteome, we converted peptide intensities at each codon position into amino acid amounts (summing base and dependent peptides, if available; otherwise, only base peptides were considered). We assumed error occurrences for one incorporated amino acid followed a Poisson distribution with a mean of 2.94 × 10−5 per amino acid. Simulations were conducted for each library, summarizing the number of codon positions showing translation errors in 1, 2, or more samples. After 200 simulations, we calculated the median, 0.025, and 0.975 quantiles of sites detected with errors in 1, 2, 3, and more samples, normalizing single-hit sites to our MS-detected sites. Of note, our method well accounted for differences in expression profiles and coverage across samples.

Filtering candidate translation error events

Potential interference from RNA editing sites was excluded from the identified translation error sites. Specifically, translation error events on the 217th residue (lysine) of Prm and the 293rd residue (serine) of Adk2 were excluded due to their overlap with known editing sites77. To further eliminate undetected SNPs that have a very low frequency that escapes our FreeBayes detection, we used BCFtools 1.20128 “mpileup” and “call” functions to examine the possible reference and alternative alleles for each codon position that showed putative translation error. For reserved single-hit sites, two SNP-related sites with both reference and alternative depth >1 were also excluded. Finally, 991 translation error events were preserved in the downstream analysis.

The simulation was conducted with M values ranging from 5 × 104 to 1 × 107, with an interval of 5 × 104. The simulation was independently repeated 200 times for each M value, and the median and 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of numbers of the codon types with at least one translation error event were summarized.

Categorizing translation errors based on codon usage and nucleotide differences

For each identified translation error event, we inferred the potential mismatch occurrences between the original and destination codons (Fig. 1c). To account for the concentrations of aminoacyl-tRNAs (aa-tRNAs), we used codon usage (corresponding to the aa-tRNA in correct codon-anticodon pairing) as a proxy and assigned translation errors proportionally to the usage of possible destination codons. Controlling for occurrence imbalance between optimal and nonoptimal codons in comparing translation error rates

For each mass spectrometry library, we balanced the sample size by fixing non-optimal codons and down-sampling optimal codons to ensure the total intensity of both was nearly the same (within 0.5%). Then, we focused on the sampled optimal codons and calculated the translation error rate as the ratio of the total intensity of mistranslated peptides to the total peptide intensity. We repeated this process 1000 times for each library and calculated the median translation error rate for optimal codons.

Linear mixed analysis

The linear mixed models were fitted using the “mixedlm” function from the Python package Statsmodels (pypi.org/project/statsmodels; v.0.14.0) to examine the relationship between translation error and codon usage (RSCU).

Calculation of ribosome density distribution

We reanalyzed the mRNA-Seq and Ribo-Seq data we had previously generated for different stages of the ISO-1 strain of D. melanogaster (Sequence Read Archive (SRA) accession number SRP067542)73 [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/?term=SRP067542]. After trimming the 3’-adapter using Cutadapt 1.18131 and eliminating tRNA- and rRNA-related reads using bowtie2 2.3.4.3, raw reads were aligned to the reference genome of D. melanogaster using STAR 2.7.10a126. The corresponding sites of the ribosome A-site on different length (25–35 nt) reads were estimated using plastid 0.5.1132, and the A-site occupancy values corresponding to each site on the transcript with the longest CDS in the gene were counted. Because regions at the beginning and end of the CDS can cause abnormal ribosome blockage due to translation initiation or termination, we omitted the first 15 and last 5 codons of the CDS. We eliminated genes with insufficient RPF coverage (RPF coverage rate <0.5) and short coding regions (<150 codons).

We calculated the mRNA density on each codon position using matched mRNA-Seq data for each sample. For each codon position, we calculated the mRNA-calibrated ribosomal coverage (mRC) by contrasting the RPF with mRNA coverage. To account for variations in translation efficiency between genes, we calculated the gene-normalized mRC (gmRC) value for each codon position as the ratio of mRC to the average mRC value within that gene. Only codon positions supported by at least one mRNA-Seq read were considered in the analysis.

Inference of the common ancestral sequences of D. melanogaster and D. simulans

FASTA alignments of 26 insects with D. melanogaster for CDS regions were downloaded from the UCSC Genome Browser (genome.ucsc.edu). Using D. yakuba as an outgroup, the common ancestral CDS sequences of D. melanogaster and D. simulans were inferred. The determination of ancestral sequences for D. simulans and D. melanogaster was conducted employing the maximum parsimony method. This method minimizes the number of inferred mutations, thereby discerning evolutionary outcomes within the sequence. The specific inference methodology is depicted in Supplementary Fig. 16. In summary, for orthologous sites where D. simulans and D. melanogaster exhibit identical nucleotides, the ancestral state is conservatively assigned as that of D. melanogaster. Conversely, in instances of nucleotide disparity between D. simulans and D. melanogaster, the nucleotide concordant with that of D. yakuba is retained as the inferred ancestral sequence. If the nucleotides at a certain position were not the same among all three species, the ancestral sequence of that position was labeled as unclear, and the codons associated with that position were excluded in subsequent calculations.

McDonald-Kreitman test for synonymous mutations in the D. melanogaster populations

The genome sequence alignments between D. melanogaster, D. simulans, and D. yakuba were retrieved from the UCSC Genome Browser (genome.ucsc.edu). We inferred the ancestral states of the synonymous mutations that are polymorphic in the Drosophila Genetic Reference Panel 2 (DGRP2) database93,94 of D. melanogaster by comparing the nucleotides at the orthologous sites of D. melanogaster, D. simulans, and D. yakuba. The synonymous mutations were separated into two categories, optimization, and deoptimization, based on whether the codon RSCU value increased or decreased after mutation. Mutations in the 8–30 nt region of the short introns (≤65 nt) were parsed as previously described, by examining whether they were polymorphic in the database or fixed in the D. melanogaster lineage73. After correcting for multiple substitutions of fixed sites between D. melanogaster and D. simulans using Kimura’s 2-Parameterm model133, we calculated the α values of synonymous mutations using the AsymptoticMK method87,88. Mutations with minor allele frequency <0.05 were excluded in the AsymptoticMK tests.

We classified D. melanogaster genes into three groups based on their median expression levels across samples: highly expressed (5357 genes), moderately expressed (3575 genes), and lowly expressed (4923 genes). We calculated the α values of synonymous mutations in each class of genes using the same neutral controls in the AsymptoticMK tests.
