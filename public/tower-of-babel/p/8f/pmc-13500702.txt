Quantitative LC-MS/MS profiling reveals aberrant chondroitin sulfate in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Yongmei Xu, Haoran Wang, Zhangjie Wang, Jianxiang Zhang, Eduardo Stancanelli, Qin Xu, Yiru Hou, Chunyu Wang, Peng Zhang, Marla Gearing, Jian Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChondroitin sulfate (CS) is an essential sulfated glycan in the brain, but standard LC-MS/MS disaccharide analysis provides only limited quantitative accuracy for detecting CS structural changes under physiological and pathophysiological conditions. Here, we incorporated eight distinct 13C-labeled CS disaccharide calibrants into the analytical workflow. Using this enhanced approach, we identified structural alterations in both sulfation patterns and total CS abundance in pre-clinical and clinical Alzheimer’s disease (AD) brain samples compared with controls. Analysis of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from AD patients further revealed elevated levels of the CS-E disaccharide and reduced levels of hyaluronic acid. Functionally, we found that synthetic CS-E 19-mer—but not other synthetic CS 19-mer subtypes—impaired neuronal growth, underscoring the need to pinpoint specific CS structures that contribute to neurodegeneration. Because CS abnormalities are detectable in the pre-clinical AD brain, our findings raise the possibility that CS glycans could serve as early biomarkers for AD.Subject terms: Glycobiology, Polysaccharides

Introduction

Chondroitin sulfate (CS) is a member of the glycosaminoglycan (GAG) family and is found on the cell surface and within the extracellular space. CS plays diverse biological roles and has been implicated in cancer metastasis1, parasitic infections2,3, and the inhibition of neuronal growth following spinal cord injury4–6. Structurally, CS is a sulfated polysaccharide composed of repeating disaccharide units of glucuronic acid (GlcA) and N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) linked through a β(1 → 3) glycosidic bond. Sulfation can occur at multiple positions: the 4-OH and 6-OH groups of GalNAc, as well as the 2-OH group of GlcA. These modifications give rise to distinct CS subtypes. The specific sulfation patterns are critical determinants of CS interactions with protein effectors and consequently influence diverse biological functions7. For example, 6-O-sulfated CS (CS-C) facilitates infection by Borrelia burgdorferi, the causative agent of Lyme disease8, while 4-O-sulfated CS (CS-A) contributes to Plasmodium falciparum invasion in malaria2. More recently, CS-A has been shown to regulate hippocampal perineuronal nets and modulate social memory9. Beyond sulfation patterns, the total abundance of CS glycans in tissues also plays an important regulatory role in biological processes. Thus, elucidating the structure–activity relationships of CS and accurately quantifying its levels in biological samples are central goals in CS research.

Emerging evidence demonstrates important physiological and pathophysiological roles for CS in regulating brain development and function10. CS has been shown to inhibit axon regeneration after spinal cord injury in the adult central nervous system11. Enzymatic removal of CS from injury sites using chondroitinase ABC (ChABC) stimulates axonal regeneration and restores neuronal function. CS also regulates cortical plasticity during a defined developmental window known as the critical period5. Specifically, enrichment of 4-O-sulfated CS (CS-A), but not 6-O-sulfated CS (CS-C), leads to the termination of the critical period for ocular dominance plasticity. CS influences cortical plasticity in part by altering the density and structure of the perineuronal net (PNN), an extracellular matrix structure rich in CS and hyaluronic acid (HA)12. More recently, the Hsieh-Wilson group and collaborators reported that 4-O-sulfated CS regulates the density of PNNs surrounding hippocampal neurons9. Beyond roles in plasticity, CS also modulates immune cell function and can impair the resolution of inflammation13. CS polysaccharides inhibit the transition of pro-inflammatory immune cells to a pro-repair phenotype in rodent models of spinal cord injury, thereby prolonging inflammation and contributing to irreversible tissue damage. Genetic defects in GalNAc-4-sulfatase—an enzyme required for CS degradation in lysosomes—cause CS accumulation in neuronal cells, leading to lysosomal dysfunction14. Reduced expression of this enzyme has also been associated with altered Alzheimer’s disease (AD)–related parameters in the hippocampus and cortex of mice, suggesting that GalNAc-4-sulfatase may contribute to AD progression14. Notably, neuronal inflammation, lysosomal dysfunction, and impaired neuroplasticity are hallmarks of many neurodegenerative disorders, including AD. To better understand the complex roles of CS in the nervous system and in neurological diseases, we have developed a sensitive and quantitative method for CS analysis.

Disaccharide analysis is a widely used strategy for characterizing chondroitin sulfate (CS) isolated from biological samples. In this approach, CS polysaccharides are enzymatically depolymerized into sulfated disaccharides using ChABC and subsequently derivatized with 2-aminoacridone (AMAC). The resulting AMAC-labeled disaccharides are then separated and quantified by liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)15. Although LC-MS/MS provides high analytical sensitivity, the consistency of quantitative data from clinical samples is often limited by the absence of appropriate internal standards. Incorporation of 13C-labeled disaccharide calibrants would substantially improve data accuracy and reproducibility. While 13C-labeled CS disaccharides have been used previously—for example, in the analysis of CS from fibrolamellar carcinoma cells16—only four of the eight possible 13C-labeled species were available due to synthetic limitations. Consequently, that study enabled only partial quantification of CS disaccharides, restricting the capability of the LC-MS/MS method for comprehensive structural analysis.

In the present study, we developed an enzymatic strategy to synthesize all eight commonly occurring CS disaccharide calibrants with 13C-labels. These calibrants were incorporated into a standardized LC-MS/MS workflow to enhance the quantitative accuracy of CS disaccharide analysis. Using this improved method, we characterized CS structures in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) patient samples, including brain tissue, plasma, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Our analysis revealed structural alterations in CS within brain tissues from both preclinical AD and confirmed AD cases. In CSF samples from AD patients, we observed a decrease in HA levels accompanied by an increase in CS-E disaccharide levels. Together, these findings demonstrate that the new LC-MS/MS platform provides a robust and quantitative approach for detecting CS structural changes relevant to brain function. Moreover, the distinct glycan alterations identified in AD patient samples suggest potential for developing glycan-based biomarkers to support AD diagnosis.

Results

Enzymatic synthesis of 13C-labeled CS disaccharide calibrants

CS polysaccharides isolated from mammalian tissues can be fully depolymerized into eight distinct disaccharide units following ChABC digestion prior to compositional analysis (Fig. 1). To prepare 13C-labeled calibrants corresponding to each of these disaccharides, we employed a three-step enzymatic synthesis strategy: (1) construction of CS backbone oligosaccharides containing 13C-labeled GlcA units; (2) selective sulfation of these backbones; and (3) enzymatic digestion of the modified oligosaccharides into disaccharides. This workflow used one glycosyltransferase, four CS sulfotransferases, and one polysaccharide lyase (Supplementary Table S1). Backbone synthesis was initiated using GlcA-pNP (para-nitrophenyl glucuronide) as the primer and the glycosyltransferase KfoC from E. coli K4 to assemble 13C-labeled CS oligosaccharides (Fig. 1)17. 13C-labeled GlcA residues were incorporated using a 13C-labeled UDP-GlcA donor substrate16. This reaction generated a heterogeneous mixture of CS backbones ranging from 10- to 30-mers, which are suitable substrates for subsequent sulfotransferase modification steps. Distinct sulfation patterns were introduced by treating the backbone oligosaccharides with defined combinations of CS sulfotransferases, yielding sulfated CS oligosaccharides with targeted structures. These intermediates were then digested with ChABC to produce the corresponding 13C-labeled disaccharides. For example, CS-0S disaccharide (Procedure 1, Fig. 1) was generated by directly digesting the unsulfated backbone without any sulfotransferase treatment. In contrast, synthesis of the CS-TriS disaccharide (Procedure 8, Fig. 1) required a sequence of three sulfotransferase reactions to introduce sulfate groups at the appropriate positions prior to ChABC digestion. During CS-TriS preparation, small quantities of non-sulfated or partially sulfated disaccharides were co-produced following ChABC digestion. These impurities were removed by anion-exchange chromatography, yielding pure 13C-labeled CS-TriS suitable for use as an internal calibrant.

The synthetic schemes for the preparation of 13C-labeled CS disaccharide calibrants.

These backbone oligosaccharides were then processed through eight distinct enzymatic schemes, designated 1 through 8 in the figure, to produce each of the eight individual 13C-labeled CS disaccharides. The generic structure of CS disaccharides is illustrated in the lower left corner of the figure, and the shorthand symbols used throughout are defined in the adjacent box. Enzymes used in the synthesis include chondroitinase ABC (ABCase), CS 6-O-sulfotransferase (CS 6-OST), CS 4-O-sulfotransferase (CS 4-OST), uronyl 2-O-sulfotransferase (UST), and GalNAc4S 6-OST (4-O-sulfo-N-acetylgalactosamine 6-O-sulfotransferase). The specific reactions catalyzed by each enzyme are detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

The purities of the synthesized disaccharides were assessed by high-resolution anion-exchange HPLC, and their structures were confirmed by high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) (Supplementary Table S2). Several of the purified disaccharides eluted as two distinct peaks in the HPLC chromatograms, which is expected because they exist as both α- and β-anomeric isomers (Supplementary Fig. S1). Because the 13C-labeled disaccharides were intended for use as internal calibrants in quantitative LC-MS/MS analysis, it was critical to determine the extent of unlabeled (12C) disaccharide contamination in each preparation. HRMS analysis demonstrated that the proportion of unlabeled disaccharides was below 2% (Supplementary Figs. S2 and S3), indicating a high level of isotopic purity in the synthesized calibrants. Together, the HRMS and HPLC results confirmed that the 13C-labeled disaccharides were suitable for use as internal standards for CS disaccharide quantification. The LC-MS/MS method was further validated using commercially available CS-A and CS-C polysaccharides, as detailed in the Experimental Procedures in the Supplementary Information and illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S4.

Neurite outgrowth of primary neurons is inhibited by a specific sulfated CS subpopulation

CS polysaccharides isolated from biological sources consist of mixed subpopulations with distinct sulfation patterns. To evaluate how these subpopulations influence neuronal growth, we examined five structurally defined oligosaccharides: dekaparin, CS-0S 19-mer, CS-A 19-mer, CS-C 19-mer, and CS-E 19-mer. Dekaparin is a highly sulfated heparan sulfate 12-mer containing N-sulfoglucosamine (GlcNS) in place of the GalNAc present in CS 19-mers. The CS-0S 19-mer is a nonsulfated nonadecasaccharide, whereas CS-A, CS-C, and CS-E 19-mers contain 4-O-sulfation, 6-O-sulfation, and 4,6-O-disulfation, respectively (Fig. 2). Using E17 mouse cortical neurons, we found that only the CS-E 19-mer inhibited neurite outgrowth. The other oligosaccharides showed no inhibitory activity. Although the CS-C 19-mer reduced axon length by approximately 30%, this effect did not reach statistical significance threshold (P = 0.08). Brown and colleagues previously reported that CS-E polysaccharides—comprising mixed CS populations enriched in the CS-4S6S subpopulation—suppress axon growth in chick neurons6. Our findings are consistent with this earlier observation.

Analysis of CS and hyaluronic acid (HA) from mice brains

We employed this technique to analyze CS and HA isolated from mouse brains across three age groups: the 1.5-M group, consisting of 1.5-month-old WT and 5xFAD mice (n = 5 per genotype; WT group included two females and three males; 5xFAD group included four males and one female); the 3-M group, including WT and 5xFAD mice aged 3–3.5 months (n = 4 per genotype; WT group included three females and one male; 5xFAD group included three females and one male); and the 7-M group, consisting of 6.5–8-month-old WT mice (n = 6, five females and one male) and 6.5–9-month-old 5xFAD mice (n = 7, two males and five females) (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5). The 5xFAD mouse is a widely used Alzheimer’s disease (AD) model that overexpresses human amyloid precursor protein and presenilin-1 carrying familial AD mutations18. Previous studies have shown that 5xFAD mice begin to accumulate Aβ plaques at approximately 2 months of age and develop AD-like phenotypes by 4–6 months19–21. Based on this timeline, our goal was to follow CS structural changes during AD progression; therefore, we analyzed brain tissues from 1.5- to 7-month-old animals. We included HA in the analysis because HA coexists with CS polysaccharides in the brain and is also susceptible to cleavage by ChABC, producing an HA disaccharide (ΔUAβ(1 → 3)GlcNAc) that shares the same molecular mass as the CS-0S disaccharide (ΔUAβ(1 → 3)GalNAc). We previously demonstrated that HA and CS-0S disaccharides can be resolved by liquid chromatography22, and we incorporated a 13C-labeled HA disaccharide internal standard in the LC-MS/MS workflow22. Unlike CS, depolymerized HA yields only a single disaccharide species; therefore, the detected HA disaccharide directly reflects the total HA content. Each analysis thus allowed identification of nine disaccharides in total: eight CS disaccharides and one HA disaccharide. In our study, CS-2S disaccharide was not detected in mouse brain CS.

We found that the total amounts of CS and HA were unchanged between WT and 5xFAD mice across all three age groups (Fig. 3A, B and Supplementary Tables S3 and S8). When comparing individual CS disaccharide levels between WT and 5xFAD mice within the same age group, no significant differences were observed (Fig. 3C–E). In contrast, several age-dependent differences were detected among WT mice (Fig. 3C–E). Therefore, our analyses focused on age-related changes in CS disaccharide composition in WT animals. A prominent example is the age-dependent decrease in CS-6S levels, which declined from 6.5 ± 0.7% in the 1.5-M group to 4.8 ± 0.7% in the 3-M group and further to 3.3 ± 0.4% in the 7-M group (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Table S10). This pattern is consistent with previous reports showing dynamic regulation of 6-O-sulfation during brain maturation in young mice5,23. We also observed a small but statistically significant increase in CS-4S disaccharide levels in the 3-M group (89.0 ± 0.8%) compared with the 1.5-M group (86.2 ± 1.6%) (Fig.3C and Supplementary Table S8). Notably, no additional increase in CS-4S was detected between the 3-M and 7-M groups (Fig.3C), indicating that changes in 4-O-sulfation occur primarily during early postnatal development. Furthermore, the ratio of CS-4S to CS-6S increased steadily with age, reflecting a shift toward higher 4-O-sulfation as mice mature. Taken together, these results indicate that aging is associated with structural remodeling of CS glycans at the disaccharide level.

Analysis of CS and HA from brain of AD patients

A total of 33 human brain samples were analyzed, consisting of 10 normal control individuals, 8 individuals with preclinical AD (Pre-AD), and 15 individuals with AD. Donor information is provided in Supplementary Table S11. Our analysis focused on the total amounts of CS and HA in each patient group, as well as the composition of individual CS disaccharides (Table 1). Notably, CS levels increased from 440 ±210 ng/mg (n = 10) in the control group to 840 ± ng/mg (n = 8) in the Pre-AD group and 750 ± 220ng/mg (n = 15) in the AD group. These results correspond to a 91% increase in CS in Pre-AD compared with controls and a 70% increase in AD compared with controls (P = 0.0013). In contrast, no statistically significant differences in HA levels were observed among the three groups (Fig.4A).

Significant differences in individual CS disaccharide levels were observed among the patient groups. CS-4S disaccharide increased from 54 ± 11% in controls to 68 ± 10% in the Pre-AD group (P < 0.01) and 65 ± 8% in the AD group (P < 0.05) (Table 1). Conversely, CS-0S disaccharide decreased from 22 ± 6% in controls to 14 ± 3% (P < 0.01) and 15 ± 4% (P < 0.01) in the Pre-AD and AD groups, respectively. Although CS-TriS disaccharide levels were reduced in the Pre-AD group, no reduction was detected in the AD group; given its overall low abundance, the change in CS-TriS is unlikely to contribute meaningfully to AD progression. The ratio of CS-4S to CS-0S disaccharides was significantly higher in both the Pre-AD and AD groups compared with controls (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4B and Table 1). The amounts of individual disaccharides from the CS of three patient groups were also measured. Three CS disaccharides, including CS-4S, CS-4S6S, and CS-2S4S, exhibited significant increases in the Pre-AD and AD groups (Supplementary Tables S13–S24).

Structural analysis of CS and HA from plasma and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from AD patients

To further evaluate whether our method can be applied to clinically accessible specimens, we analyzed plasma and CSF samples from AD patients (n = 5) and age-matched controls (n = 5). Donor information is provided in Supplementary Table S12. We observed a significant reduction in HA levels in CSF from the AD group (480 ± 150 ng/mL) compared with controls (960 ± 130 ng/mL; P = 0.0013), whereas no significant difference in plasma HA levels was detected (Table 2 and Fig.5). Additionally, the percentage of CS-4S6S disaccharide in CSF was significantly elevated in AD patients, increasing to 2.4 ± 0.7% from 1.3 ± 0.2% in controls (P = 0.011) (Fig. 5E). Consistent with this finding, the absolute amount of CS-4S6S disaccharide was also increased in the brains of AD patients (Supplementary Tables S13–S24), suggesting a parallel rise in CS-E–related structures in both brain and CSF. No differences were observed between AD and control groups in CSF levels of other CS disaccharides, including CS-6S, CS-4S, and CS-2S4S (Fig. 5F), nor in the ratios of CS-4S/CS-6S or CS-4S/CS-0S (Fig. 5G, H). Together, these results indicate that most CS structural alterations detected in AD brain tissue are not reflected in CSF, with the notable exception of CS-4S6S. This selective concordance raises the possibility that movement of CS glycans from brain to CSF may depend on their sulfation patterns.

The CSF and plasma samples analyzed in this study were obtained from the same individuals, allowing a direct comparison of CS structures between these two biological fluids on a per-patient basis. Based on disaccharide composition, we did not observe structural similarity between CS isolated from CSF and that from plasma within the same individual (Supplementary Table S27). Moreover, substantial inter-individual variability was apparent among patients within the same clinical group. For example, the CS-4S disaccharide accounted for 82% of total CS disaccharides in the CSF of control donor 2, whereas in control donor 1 it accounted for only 46% (Supplementary Table S27). This considerable variability in disaccharide profiles highlights the necessity of using 13C-labeled internal calibrants to ensure accurate quantification of CS structures. It also suggests that CS-4S disaccharide levels in CSF are not suitable as a reliable biomarker for AD diagnosis.

Discussion

In this manuscript, we applied an improved LC-MS/MS method to perform structural analysis of CS from tissues and biological fluids obtained from AD patients. A key innovation of this method is the incorporation of 13C-labeled, authentic CS disaccharides as internal standards, which enables robust quantitative measurement. The use of 13C-labeled internal standards also minimizes variability arising from differences in ionization efficiency during mass spectrometry, a common source of analytical inconsistency. Although isotopically labeled peptides and small molecules are routinely used as internal standards in quantitative LC-MS workflows, such an approach was previously not feasible for CS disaccharide analysis due to the unavailability of 13C-labeled CS calibrants. Here, we used an enzymatic synthesis strategy to generate a complete set of eight 13C-labeled CS disaccharides with high chemical and isotopic purity. Notably, fucosylated CS and CS containing 3-O-sulfated GlcA have been reported in CS isolated from marine organisms, but these structures have not been detected in CS from humans or other mammals24. Thus, the absence of 13C-labeled calibrants for these rare disaccharides does not limit the applicability of our method for CS analysis in human and mouse samples.

The roles of CS in regulating neuronal growth have been extensively investigated in models of spinal cord injury. The prevailing hypothesis proposes that CS inhibits axonal regeneration by interfering with receptor protein tyrosine phosphatase sigma (PTPσ) signaling25. Specifically, CS chains competitively block PTPσ oligomerization, thereby disrupting the formation of phosphotyrosine-rich microdomains. This disruption impairs autophagic flux and promotes the formation of dystrophic growth cones, ultimately hindering axonal extension26. Despite these inhibitory effects, CS also exerts beneficial functions in the acute phase following spinal cord injury. The injury-induced synthesis and accumulation of CS have been shown to suppress tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) release from microglia and phagocytes—at least in part through CD44-mediated signaling—and to stimulate the secretion of insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) and matrix metalloproteinases. These responses collectively contribute to tissue remodeling and repair at the injury site27. Sawrup and colleagues reported that CS polysaccharides can also direct neuronal outgrowth28, highlighting the complex and context-dependent roles of CS in regulating neurite growth.

Using synthetic, structurally defined oligosaccharides, we demonstrated that both the sulfation level and the underlying saccharide backbone are essential determinants of the ability of CS to inhibit neurite growth. Among the four CS 19-mers tested, the CS-E 19-mer exhibited the highest sulfation level, with 1.68 sulfo groups per disaccharide unit, and therefore carries the greatest negative charge density under physiological pH (Fig.2). However, its sulfation level is still lower than that of dekaparin, a highly sulfated heparan sulfate 12-mer that contains 2.83 sulfo groups per disaccharide unit. The absence of inhibitory activity from dekaparin indicates that neurite growth inhibition is not solely driven by overall negative charge density or sulfation level. Instead, our results underscore the importance of both sulfation pattern and glycan backbone identity in determining biological activity. These findings further justify the detailed structural analysis of CS from brain and other tissues to elucidate how aberrant CS structures may contribute to AD pathogenesis.

We identified a significant increase in the total amount of CS in brains from Pre-AD and AD individuals relative to controls. The pathological implications of elevated CS levels in AD remain unclear. Given prior evidence that CS can exert pro-inflammatory roles in rodent models of spinal cord injury12, it is plausible that chronically increased CS levels in Pre-AD and AD brains may contribute to sustained neuroinflammation29. We also observed alterations in specific CS disaccharides, particularly CS-0S and CS-4S, between control and disease groups. Notably, a reduction in CS-0S disaccharide in AD brain relative to controls was also reported by Alonge and colleagues30. However, the same study did not detect a statistically significant increase in CS-4S levels in AD samples (P = 0.23)30. CS-4S has been described as a “molecular brake” that restricts neuroplasticity9, and reduced neuroplasticity is a well-recognized contributing factor in AD pathogenesis31. Thus, elevated CS-4S levels in Pre-AD and AD brains may accelerate neurodegenerative processes by limiting neuronal growth and remodeling.

Although there was no statistically significant difference in age between the control and AD groups included in this study, a significant age difference was detected in the pre-AD group when comparing to control and AD groups collectively (P = 0.0301) (Supplementary Table S3). Therefore, the contribution of age to the changes in CS and HA observed in the Pre-AD samples cannot be fully ruled out.

From our structural analysis of CS in mice, we did not detect significant differences between 5xFAD and WT mice, despite observing clear age-dependent changes in CS composition in the WT groups. Two factors may explain the lack of detectable differences between WT and 5xFAD mice: (1) the relatively small sample size may limit statistical power, and (2) age-related changes in CS structure within WT mice may mask disease-associated alterations that emerge during AD progression in the 5xFAD model. Further studies profiling CS structures from human brain samples across a broader range of ages—as well as from specific brain regions—will be important to delineate the relationship between CS structural remodeling and AD pathology.

The biosynthetic pathways governing CS production are highly conserved between humans and mice, as reflected by the shared classes of sulfotransferases and the identical numbers of isoform enzymes in both species32. Despite this conservation, we did not detect structural alterations in CS from wild-type or 5xFAD mouse brains, suggesting that additional regulatory factors may underline the divergent CS biosynthetic responses observed between the mouse and human brain during Alzheimer’s disease progression. Future studies aimed at manipulating CS structures in 5xFAD mice to more closely resemble those found in AD patient brains will provide an important opportunity to elucidate the functional roles of CS in modulating disease progression.

Our findings regarding CS levels in human brains differ in several respects from earlier reports31,33,34. These discrepancies are likely attributable to differences in analytical methodologies and sample preparation. For example, Huynh et al. quantified CS polysaccharides using the colorimetric 1,9-dimethylmethylene blue (DMMB) assay and reported no difference in CS abundance between AD and control groups31. In contrast, our study employed a highly specific and quantitative LC-MS/MS–based approach, which directly detects individual CS disaccharides and is therefore less susceptible to interference from other glycosaminoglycans. Additionally, Huynh et al. analyzed hippocampal tissue31, whereas our measurements were performed on frontal cortex samples, raising the possibility of region-specific differences in CS abundance. Other studies33,34 relied on formalin-fixed tissue, while we analyzed frozen brain specimens. We did not detect an increase in HA levels in AD brain samples relative to controls, a result that differs from earlier observations35. Methodological differences between studies—such as tissue handling, extraction conditions, and the specificity of analytical techniques—likely contribute to the divergent findings for both CS and HA.

One unmet need in management of AD is to offer early diagnosis for the disease. For this purpose, the analysis of CS may offer new biomarker targets. Our findings revealed that the changes in CS in the brain were already detectable in the Pre-AD group, suggesting that the disruption of CS biosynthesis in the brain happens prior to the development of cognitive changes in AD. To this purpose, we measured the levels of CS and HA from patients’ plasma and CSF, biospecimens that can be collected readily in clinics. Two components, including HA polysaccharide and CS-4S6S disaccharide, displayed significant changes in the analysis of CSF. The CS-4S6S disaccharide displayed an 85% increase in the AD group. The CS-4S6S is the characteristic domain found in the CS-E polysaccharide that is known to be enriched in the CS from the brain6. Results from our analysis also found that the amount of CS-4S6S disaccharide is elevated in both Pre-AD and AD brains (Supplementary Table S13). Interestingly, CS-E oligosaccharides, i.e., CS-E 19-mer, displayed the strongest inhibition of neuronal axion growth among four tested oligosaccharides as demonstrated by the in vitro experiment (Fig. 2). Our data support the hypothesis that CS-4S6S disaccharide is a biomarker for early AD diagnosis for future study.

The amount of HA from the brain, CSF, and plasma exhibits very little similarity. For example, we did not observe a change in the level of HA in AD brain tissues compared with the control group; however, our data clearly showed that there is a significant decrease in the level of HA in CSF from AD patients. Lower concentration of HA in CSF among AD patients is possibly attributed to the impairment of the translocation of HA from the brain to CSF36,37. We note that a previous publication for the analysis of HA from CSF showed no difference in AD and control groups38; however, the method used for the analysis of HA in the previous study differed from the method used by us.

In conclusion, CS is a key sulfated glycan within the perineuronal net, where it performs essential functions in regulating brain physiology and neural plasticity. Accurate and sensitive methods for analyzing CS structures are critical for advancing our understanding of its roles in brain development and disease. In this work, we developed an improved LC-MS/MS–based platform for CS analysis that incorporates highly purified 13C-labeled CS disaccharides as internal standards, substantially enhancing both quantitative accuracy and analytical reliability. Using this method, we identified significant structural changes in CS—and in the related glycan HA—in brain tissue and CSF samples from Pre-AD, AD, and control patients. Although some of our findings differ from previous reports, LC-MS/MS is widely regarded as the most specific and sensitive approach for analyzing CS and related glycosaminoglycans, and our work underscores the value of applying this technology to human samples. Our next step will be to apply this approach to individual subregions of AD patient brains, enabling higher-resolution mapping of CS structural remodeling across disease-relevant neuroanatomical circuits. Such analyses will fully leverage the accuracy and sensitivity of our method and may provide deeper mechanistic insight into how CS contributes to AD progression. The current study offers new evidence supporting a role for CS alterations in AD pathogenesis and highlights the potential utility of glycan-based biomarkers for early detection. Although the patient sample size in this study is relatively small and warrants further validation, our findings provide a solid proof of concept and a strong foundation for future, larger-scale investigations.

Preparation of recombinant enzymes for the enzymatic synthesis of oligosaccharides

KfoC from the E. coli K4 strain and chondroitinase ABC lyase I (ChABC) from Proteus vulgaris were expressed in E. coli as previously described39,40. Briefly, the expression plasmids were transformed into E. coli competent cells (Invitrogen). The E. coli cells were grown in LB medium and incubated at 37 °C until the OD600 reached 0.6–0.8. Then, a final concentration of 0.2 mM isopropylthiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added to induce the expression of the proteins. The cultures were kept at 22 °C and 200 rpm overnight. The E. coli cells were harvested by centrifuge with 6000 rpm for 10 min, then

resuspended in 25 mM Tris, 30 mM imidazole, and 500 mM NaCl, pH 7.5. Sonication

was used to lysis the cell, and subsequent centrifuge was at 14,000 rpm for 30 min.

The supernatant was subjected to a nickel agarose column (GE Healthcare). Buffer A

(25 mM Tris, 60 mM imidazole, and 500 mM NaCl, pH 7.5) was used to wash the

column, and buffer B (25 mM Tris, 250 mM imidazole, 500 mM NaCl, pH 7.5) was used

to elute the proteins. All the proteins were kept in 20% glycerol at −80 °C. CS 4-OST, CS 6-OST, UST, and GalNAc4S6-OST were expressed in insect cells using the baculovirus expression system and purified by heparin–Sepharose chromatography, as previously described39,41,42.

To express CS 4-OST, CS 6-OST, GalNAc4S6-OST, and UST in insect cells using the baculovirus expression system, a critical parameter was minimizing contamination by viral chondroitinase produced by the baculovirus itself43. Contamination with viral chondroitinase leads to degradation of chondroitin substrates during enzymatic synthesis, resulting in markedly reduced production yields. Expression conditions were therefore optimized to suppress viral chondroitinase expression while maintaining high levels of CS sulfotransferase production. The four sulfotransferases were expressed in Sf9 insect cells (Invitrogen) cultured in Sf-900™ III SFM medium (Life Technologies). Recombinant baculovirus infection was performed when the Sf9 cells reached a density of 2.0 × 10⁶ to 8.0 × 10⁶ cells/mL. Cultures were incubated at 28 °C with shaking for 3–4 days, after which the cells were harvested by centrifugation. PMSF, Triton X-100, and glycerol were added to the collected supernatant to final concentrations of 1 mM, 0.1%, and 2%, respectively. The treated supernatant was then applied to a heparin-Toyopearl column (Tosoh Bioscience). The column was washed with buffer containing 20 mM MOPS, 100 mM NaCl, 2% glycerol, and 0.1% reduced Triton X-100 (Sigma), pH 7.0, until the UV absorbance at 280 nm returned to baseline. Bound proteins were eluted using a linear gradient of 0.3 M to 0.9 M NaCl in 20 mM MOPS, 2% glycerol, and 0.1% reduced Triton X-100, pH 7.0, at a flow rate of 4 mL/min over 2 h. Fractions were assayed for both chondroitin sulfotransferase activity and viral chondroitinase activity; any fractions exhibiting viral chondroitinase activity were discarded.

To quantify viral chondroitinase expression, a non-sulfated chondroitin backbone oligosaccharide was used as a substrate, allowing simultaneous monitoring of chondroitinase and sulfotransferase activities by HPLC. Briefly, a CS-0S 9-mer (10 μg; (GlcA–GalNAc)\₄–GlcA-pNP) was incubated with insect cell culture media (20 μL) in a buffer containing 0.5 mM 3′-phosphoadenosine-5′-phosphosulfate (PAPS) and 50 mM MES, pH 6.5, in a total reaction volume of 100 μL at 37 °C for 1 h. The reaction products were analyzed by ProPac HPLC at 310 nm. Sulfated CS-9-mer, intact CS-0S 9-mer, and degraded CS-0S 9-mer eluted at distinct retention times, enabling quantification of viral chondroitinase and CS sulfotransferase activity based on the relative peak areas at 310 nm.

This analytical method was used to guide the expression of CS 4-OST, CS 6-OST, UST, and GalNAc4S6-OST, with substrate selection tailored to each enzyme. CS-0S 9-mer was used for expressing CS 4-OST and CS 6-OST; CS-6S 9-mer ((GlcA–GalNAc6S)\₄–GlcA-pNP) was used for UST expression; and CS-4S 9-mer ((GlcA–GalNAc4S)\₄–GlcA-pNP) was used for GalNAc4S6-OST expression.

Synthesis of 13C-labeled CS oligosaccharides and 13C-labeled CS disaccharide calibrants

The 13C-labeled non-sulfated CS backbone oligosaccharides were synthesized by polymerization using GlcA-pNP, UDP-GalNAc, and UDP-[13C]GlcA at a molar ratio of 1:10:8 in the presence of KfoC. The reaction mixture was incubated at 37 °C overnight. LC–MS analysis revealed that the non-reducing end of the synthesized oligosaccharides was a GalNAc residue, and the products ranged from 10 to 30 residues in length. These CS backbone oligosaccharides were subsequently subjected to sulfotransferase modifications as illustrated in Fig.1. For the CS 4-OST and CS 6-OST reactions, 600 mg of CS backbone oligosaccharides were incubated with 4 mM PAPS, 50 mM MES (pH6.5), 2 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 20 mM CaCl₂, and either 200 mL CS4-OST (1.3 mg/mL) or CS6-OST (1.6 mg/mL) in a total volume of 1.5 L at 37 °C overnight. For UST reactions, 30 mg of CS backbone oligosaccharides were used to generate 2-O-sulfated CS oligosaccharides; 30 mg of CS-4-O-sulfated oligosaccharides were used to synthesize 2,4-O-sulfated products; and 30 mg of CS-6-O-sulfated oligosaccharides were used to produce 2,6-O-sulfated products. Each reaction contained 4 mM PAPS, 50 mM MES (pH6.5), 2 mM DTT, 20 mM CaCl₂, and 16 mL UST (2.1 mg/mL) in a total volume of 150 mL and was incubated at 37 °C overnight.

Approximately 20 mg of CS-4-O-sulfated oligosaccharides were used to produce 4,6-O-sulfated CS saccharides, and 20 mg of 2,4-O-sulfated oligosaccharides were used to synthesize tri-sulfated CS oligosaccharides. For GalNAc4S6-OST reactions, CS oligosaccharides were incubated with 4 mM PAPS, 50 mM MES (pH 6.5), 2 mM reduced glutathione, 20 mM CaCl₂, and 16 mL GalNAc4S6-OST (1.8 mg/mL) in a total volume of 100 mL at 37 °C overnight. All reactions were monitored by ProPac-HPLC and high-resolution LC–MS.

After each synthetic step, the backbone oligosaccharides were purified using a C18-HPLC column as previously described41. All sulfated oligosaccharide products were further purified on Q-Sepharose columns. The columns were washed with 0.3 M NaCl in 20 mM NaOAc–HAcO buffer (pH 5) to remove residual PAPS, and the bound oligosaccharides were eluted with 2.0 M NaCl in the same buffer. Different sizes of Q-Sepharose columns (GE Healthcare) were selected based on the binding affinity of each product and the scale of the reaction.

13C-labeled CS disaccharide calibrants were generated by ChABC digestion of 13C-labeled CS oligosaccharides. For example, a reaction mixture containing ChABC (20 mg) and the corresponding oligosaccharides (200 mg) in a total volume of 20 mL of buffer (20 mM Tris, 500 mM NaCl, pH 7.5) was incubated at 37 °C overnight. Reaction completion was monitored using ProPac-HPLC (Thermo) and LC-MS. All resulting disaccharides were purified by preparative ProPac-HPLC. Buffer A consisted of 20 mM NaOAc–HAcO (pH5.0), and buffer B consisted of 2 M NaCl in 20 mM NaOAc–HAcO (pH5.0). CS-0S disaccharide was eluted with buffer A for 10 min, followed by a linear gradient of 0–1% buffer B over 20 min. Mono-sulfated disaccharides were eluted with buffer A for 5 min, followed by a 6–8% buffer B gradient over 60 min. Disulfated disaccharides were eluted with buffer A for 5 min, then a linear gradient of 35–60% buffer B over 40 min. Trisulfated disaccharides were eluted with buffer A for 5 min, followed by a 30–45% buffer B gradient over 40 min and a 45–65% buffer B gradient over 50 min.

Purity analysis of CS disaccharides using HPLC

All the disaccharides was eluted with buffer A for 3 min, then a linear gradient of 0–1% buffer B over 20 min, then a linear gradient of 8–15% buffer B over 40 min, then a linear gradient of 40–60% buffer B over 40 min, and a linear gradient of 60–100% buffer B over 10 min. Buffer A contained 3 mM NaH2PO4, Buffer B contained 1.5 M NaCl and 3 mM NaH2PO4. Seven of the eight disaccharides showed doublet peaks in the HPLC analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1). Such HPLC profiles for pure CS disaccharides are not uncommon, as these compounds typically exist as mixtures of α- and β-anomeric isomers. Similar observations were reported in our previous publication16 and are also documented in product datasheets for commercially available CS disaccharides (Chondroitin/Dermatan Sulphate Disaccharides | Iduron).

Validation of the LC-MS/MS method

Method validation experiments were performed using two commercially available polysaccharides: CS-A from bovine trachea (Sigma, C9819-25G) and CS-C (Carbosynth, YC31458). Among the eight CS disaccharides included in our analytical panel, the levels of CS-TriS, CS-2S4S, and CS-2S were each <0.3% (if present at all) in both CS-A and CS-C polysaccharides. Consequently, the quantitative validation focused on the remaining five disaccharides, and the total CS-A or CS-C content was calculated as the sum of these five measurable components.

Across the range of 150–1200 ng, excellent linearity was obtained for all five detected disaccharides as well as for the total polysaccharide concentration in both CS-A and CS-C samples (Supplementary Fig. 4). As expected, CS-A showed high abundance of the CS-4S disaccharide, and CS-C consisted predominantly of CS-6S disaccharide. However, our measurements revealed that the CS-A preparation contained 42 ± 2% CS-4S and 53 ± 2% CS-6S. The unexpectedly high level of CS-6S in the CS-A sample indicates that this commercial preparation is likely a mixture of CS-A and CS-C polysaccharides. The CS-C polysaccharide preparation contained 82 ± 1% CS-6S and 8 ± 1% CS-4S. Additionally, a substantial amount of CS-2S6S disaccharide (9 ± 1%)—a structural motif characteristic of CS-D—was present in the CS-C sample.

The purpose of analyzing the two commercially available CS polysaccharides was to validate the accuracy of our disaccharide-based quantification method. Our results showed that the measured amounts of the two CS polysaccharide isomers were consistent with the manufacturers’ reported compositions (Supplementary Fig. 4). In addition, substantial CS-6S disaccharide contamination in commercial CS-A preparations has been reported previously28, consistent with our observation of elevated CS-6S levels in the CS-A sample analyzed here.

Expression and purification of ChABC

Recombinant chondroitin sulfate ABC lyase I (ChABC) from Proteus vulgaris was expressed in E. coli and purified using Ni-Sepharose columns as previously described40. Briefly, the E. coli cells harboring ChABC expression plasmid were grown in LB media at 37 °C in a shaker incubator at the speed of 225 rpm. The temperature was reduced to 22 °C when the cell density reached to optical density (OD) of 595 nm to 0.6. IPTG was added to the culture to the final concentration of 0.2 M, and the culture was incubated in the shaker incubator at 22 °C for additional 15–18 h. The bacterial cells were pelleted using centrifuge at 10,000 × g for 10 min. The bacterial cells were resuspended in a buffer containing 25 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 30 mM imidazole, and 500 mM NaCl, and were broken using sonication. The bacterial lysate was subjected to a Ni-Sepharose column. The enzyme was eluted with a buffer containing 25 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 300 mM imidazole, and 500 mM NaCl. The purified recombinant enzyme migrated as a single band at approximately 120 kDa on SDS–PAGE, with a purity greater than 95% as assessed by Coomassie Blue staining.

ChABC activity was measured using chondroitin sulfate-A sodium salt (Sigma, YC31458) as the substrate. Reaction mixtures contained 25 mM Tris–AcO (pH8.0), 100 mM NaCl, 0.2% BSA, 30 mg CS-A substrate, and 6–12 ng of enzyme in a total volume of 100 mL. Reactions were incubated at 32 °C for 1 h. The absorbance at 232 nm—reflecting the release of ΔUA-GalNAc±4S and ΔUA-GalNAc±6S disaccharides—was monitored at 10, 20, 30, and 60 min. The time interval showing the best linearity was used to calculate enzymatic activity.

One unit (U) of ChABC activity was defined as the amount of enzyme generating 1 μmol of ΔUA-GalNAc±4S and ΔUA-GalNAc±6S per minute at 32 °C. At 232 nm, 1OD unit corresponds to approximately 0.44 mM of a mixture of these disaccharide products. Under these conditions, the ChABC preparations typically exhibited specific activities greater than 80 U/mg protein.

Procedures for the analysis of CS from tissues

Frozen tissues were excised and homogenized in tissue homogenization buffer consisting of 0.2 M 2-(4-morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid (MES), 0.05 M sodium phosphate, and 1 mM EDTA (pH 6). The homogenized tissues were defatted by sequential suspension and vortexing in three chloroform/methanol mixtures (2:1, v/v; 1:1, v/v; 1:2, v/v)15. The defatted tissues were then dried and weighed to determine tissue dry mass. Dried tissues were subsequently digested with PronaseE (Sigma) at 55°C for 24 h. Following digestion, the sample was applied to a 200-µL DEAE-Sepharose column (GE Healthcare). The resin was washed with 1.5 mL of buffer A (20 mM Tris, 50 mM NaCl, pH 7.5) and eluted with 1.5 mL of buffer B (20 mM Tris, 1 M NaCl, pH 7.4). The eluted fraction was desalted using 3 kDa MWCO centrifugal filters (Waters).

The retentate was then subjected to ChABC digestion. The digestion solution contained 0.27 mg/mL ChABC in 7.5 mM sodium acetate (pH 7.0), 0.15 mM calcium acetate, and 7.5 µg/mL bovine serum albumin in a total volume of 100 µL. The reaction mixture was incubated at 37°C overnight. After digestion, a known amount of 13C-labeled calibrants (50 ng of each of the eight disaccharides) was added to the mixture. The resulting filtrates were lyophilized and subjected to AMAC (2-aminoacridone) derivatization.

AMAC derivatization was performed by adding 5 µL of 0.1 M AMAC prepared in a dimethyl sulfoxide/acetic acid mixture (17:3, v/v). Samples were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 15 min, followed by addition of 5 µL aqueous NaBH₃CN. The mixture was incubated at 45 °C for 2 h, after which the derivatized samples were collected and stored at room temperature for LC–MS/MS analysis.

LC-MS/MS analysis- Analysis of AMAC labeled CS disaccharides was performed using an LC–MS system consisting of a Vanquish Flex UHPLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to a TSQ Fortis triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were separated on a Poroshell 120 EC C18 column (2.7 µm, 50 × 4.6 mm; Waters) maintained at 45 °C using a 20 min gradient. The mobile phases were buffer A (50 mM ammonium acetate) and buffer B (acetonitrile) delivered at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min under the following conditions: 0–10 min, 5–45% B; 10–10.2 min, 45–100% B; 10.2–15 min, 100% B; 15–15.2 min, 100–5% B; 15.2–20 min, 5% B.

Ion source parameters were set as follows: spray voltage, −4.0 kV; sheath gas, 45Arb; auxiliary gas, 15Arb; ion transfer tube temperature, 320 °C; and vaporizer temperature, 350 °C. The triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer was operated in multiple-reaction monitoring (MRM) mode for quantitative detection of AMAC-labeled CS disaccharides. Transition parameters used to identify CS disaccharides isoforms include 12C-CS-2S4S6S (405.736 → 365.708, 493.917), 13C-CS-2S4S6S (408.686 → 368.708, 493.917), 12C-CS-2S4S (732.15 → 396.125, 652.208), 13C-CS-2S4S(738.3 → 96.929, 658.28), 12C-CS-2S6S (732.214 → 138.143, 652.167), 13C-CS-2S6S(738.3 → 658.292), 12C-CS-4S6S (732.2 → 536.083, 652.292), 13C-CS-4S6S(738.3 → 538.083, 658.292), 12C-CS-2S (652.3 → 96.917, 156.75), 13C-CS-2S(658.279 → 96.929, 163.054), 12C-CS-4S (652.279 → 396.065, 536.083), 13C-CS-4S(658.343 → 396.22, 538.095), 12C-CS-6S (652.3 → 396.167, 572.208), 13C-CS-6S(658.386 → 396.095, 578.28), 12C-CS-0S (572.2 → 396.1), 13C-CS-0S(578.2 → 396.1), 12C-HA (572.2 → 396.1), and 13C-HA(578.2 → 396.1). The data processing was performed using Quan Browser software.

Preparation of mice brain samples

5xFAD mice (034848-JAX) were housed and bred in the Animal Resource Center of Case Western Reserve University School of Medicine on a 12 h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. The procedures in studies were conducted in accordance with National Institutes of Health regulations and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of Case Western Reserve University. Mice were age-matched within the new experiment. Specifically, the 1.5-month group includes five WT mice (two females and three males) and five 5xFAD mice (four males and one female); the 3-month-old group includes four WT (three females and one male), and four 5xFAD mice (three females and one male); and the 7–month-old group contains six WT (7–8-month-old, five females and one male) and seven 5xFAD in mice (6.5–9-month-old, two males and five females). The age and sex for each individual mouse used in the study are listed in Supplementary Table S4 and S5. All mice were group-housed, and WT and 5xFAD littermates were housed in the same cages. No animals were single housed. Housing conditions (cage type, bedding, enrichment, diet, light cycle, and husbandry) were identical across genotypes. 5xFAD mice and their age-matched littermates were anesthetized with isoflurane and subsequently decapitated. The cortexes and hippocampi were rapidly dissected out, immediately frozen on dry ice, and stored at −80 °C freezer until disaccharide analysis.

Patient samples from neurodegenerative diseases

All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed. Frozen postmortem frontal cortex samples as well as plasma and CSF samples were provided by the Neuropathology Core brain bank at the Goizueta Alzheimer’s Disease Center at Emory University (Emory University IRB approved # AM4_IRB00045782, where IRB refers to Institutional Review Board). Use of the samples was permitted after informed consent had been obtained from all participants.

The neuropathologic diagnosis of neurodegenerative diseases was established following current diagnostic criteria44. Control subjects consisted of individuals with no known history of neurological disease and no significant neurodegenerative pathology at autopsy. The detailed information for patients is shown in Supplementary Table S3. During the analysis of two brain samples from pre-AD group, the signal from 13C-labeled CS-6S disaccharide calibrant was lost accidentally during the analysis, resulting in the inability to calculate the disaccharide composition from two samples (case numbers: OS01-09 and OS03-394), therefore, the data for these two samples were excluded from the analysis.

To isolate CS from plasma, 100 μL of donor patient plasma was treated with 1 mL of methanol to precipitate proteins. The resulting precipitate was dried and subsequently digested with Pronase E (Sigma) at 55 °C for 24 h to release glycans. To isolate CS from CSF, 100 μL of donor CSF samples were directly digested with Pronase E at 55 °C for 24 h without prior precipitation. Following protein digestion, all samples were subjected to identical downstream procedures, including DEAE purification. CS isolated from plasma and CSF was then digested using ChABC, followed by LC–MS/MS analysis, as described for solid tissue samples.

Inhibition of neurite outgrowth by CS 19-mers and dekaparin

Glass coverslips were coated with 100 µg/mL poly-D-lysine (Invitrogen) for 24 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂, followed by coating with either 10 µg/mL laminin (Invitrogen) and 10 µg/mL dekaparin, or 10 µg/mL CS 19-mers, or vehicle control for an additional 24 h. E17 C57BL/6 J mouse embryos were anesthetized via hypothermia and subsequently sacrificed by decapitation on ice following UNC IACUC protocol 22–197. Primary cortical neurons were isolated from pooled 8–10 embryos of both sexes from the same litters. The resulting tissue was minced into ~1 mm3 fragments and digested with 0.05% trypsin-EDTA (Invitrogen) for 20 min at 37 °C, followed by treatment with 0.25 mg/mL DNase I (Sigma) for another 5 min. The DNase I should be added after the removal of trypsin-EDTA and in the presence of Mg2+ to ensure its activity. The resulting cell suspension was passed through a 40 µm strainer to obtain single cells, which were then seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/cm² onto coverslips pre-coated with the substrates described above. Dissociated neurons were cultured in Neurobasal Plus medium (Invitrogen) supplemented with 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Invitrogen) for 48 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂.

Immunofluorescence and neurite outgrowth analyses were performed as follows. Neurons were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100. Cells were then incubated with anti-βIII tubulin primary antibody (1:100, Thermo, MA532035), followed by an AF594-conjugated secondary antibody (1:250, Thermo, A21207). Neurite outgrowth was quantified using ImageJ (NIH). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA on data from at least three independent experiments, followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test.

Synthesis of CS 19-mers and dekaparin

The synthesis and structural characterization of the four CS 19-mers have been described previously39,45. The synthesis and characterization of dekaparin were reported earlier45. The purity of each CS 19-mer and dekaparin preparation exceeded 90% as confirmed by HPLC, and their structures were verified by high-resolution mass spectrometry39,41,45. The synthesis of non-sulfate CS 19-mer, starting from GlcA-pNP, elongate to 19 mer, involving both reactions of the addition of GalNAc and GlcA residue. Briefly, GlcA-pNP (3 mM) was dissolved in buffer containing Tris 25 mM, pH 7.2, MnCl2 5 mM, Kfoc 100 µg/ml, and UDP-GalNAc 4 mM, then the reaction was incubated at 37 °C overnight. A C18-column (3 × 15 cm, or 120 g, Biotage) was used for purification with gradient elution method (0–100% methanol in H2O, 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid, 5 ml/min). Then GalNAc-GlcA-pNP (3 mM) was dissolved in buffer containing Tris 25 mM, pH 7.2, MnCl2 5 mM, Kfoc 100 µg/ml, and UDP-GlcA 4.5 mM, then the reaction was incubated at 37 °C overnight. The reaction was purified by C18 column as described above. Repeating the elongation steps to obtain non-sulfate CS 19-mer. Non-sulfate CS 19-mer was modified by CS 4-OST or CS 6-OST to obtain CS-A-19mer and CS-C-19mer. The reaction was contained non-sulfate CS 19-mer 100 mg, MOPS 50 mM pH 6.5, CaCl2 10 mM, DTT (DL-Dithiothreitol) 2 mM, PAPS 0.4 mmol, 0.2 ml/ml enzyme in total 100 ml and incubated at 37 °C for overnight. CS-A-19mer was further modified by GalNAc4S6-OST to obtain CS-E-19mer. The reaction was contained CS-A-19mer 50 mg, MOPS 50 mM pH 6.5, CaCl2 10 mM, GSH (L-glutathione) 2 mM, PAPS 0.3 mmol, GalNAc4S6-OST 0.3 mg/ml in total 50 ml, and was incubate at 37 °C for overnight.

At every synthesis step, the products were monitored by Shimadzu HPLC equipped with a Propac- column (9 × 250 mm, Thermo). The molecular weights of the intermediates from each step were confirmed by ESI-MS. All the sulfated products were purified using Q-Sepharose fast flow column (GE Life Science) eluted with a linear gradient 0–100% 2 M NaCl in 20 mM NaAcO-HAcO, pH 5.0, for 3–5 h. Different sizes of Q-Sepharose columns were chosen based on the binding affinity of the product and the reaction scale.

The synthesis of Dekaparin was completed according to the chemoenzymatic method published previously45. Briefly, heparosan synthase-2 (PmHS2) from Pasteurella multocida was used to elongate the monosaccharide, GlcA-pNP, to the appropriate size backbone. The backbone was then subjected to the modification by N-sulfotransferase (NST), C5-epimerase (C5-epi), 2-O-sulfotransferase (2-OST), 6-O-sulfotransferase (6-OST), 3-O-sulfotransferase isoform 1 (3-OST-1). Five major steps were involved in the overall synthesis, including Step elongation to add GlcNTFA or GlcA), Step detrifluoroacetylation and N-sulfation, Step 2-O-sulfation/epimerization, Step 6-O-sulfation, and Step 3-O-sulfation by 3-OST-1.

Steps 1 and 2, elongation was to elongate the oligosaccharide backbone to the desired size, involving the addition of GlcNTFA (N-trifluoroacetyl glucosamine) residue or GlcA. Briefly, as for Step 1, the addition of GlcNTFA, for example, GlcA-pNP (3.2 mM), was dissolved in buffer containing Tris (25 mM, pH 7.2), MnCl2 (5 mM), pmHS2 (60 μg/ml), and UDP-GlcNTFA (4.5 mM), then was incubated at 37 °C overnight. A C18-column (3 × 15 cm, or 120 g, Biotage) was used for purification with gradient elution method (0–100% methanol in H2O, 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid, 5 ml/min). As for the addition of GlcA residue, for example, GlcNTFA-GlcA-pNP (3.2 mM) was dissolved in a buffer containing Tris (25 mM, pH 7.2), MnCl2 (5 mM), pmHS2 (30 µg/ml), and UDP-GlcA (1.5 mM), then incubated at 30 °C overnight. The total reaction volume was 3 L. A C18 column was used for purification as described above. Repeating the sept1 and 2 three times, (GlcNTFA-GlcA-)3-pNP was obtained.

Step 3, detrifluoroacetylation and N-sulfation, aimed to convert GlcNTFA residue to GlcNS residue, involving both detrifluoroacetylation and N-sulfation. The detrifluoroacetylation of the oligosaccharide was conducted in 0.1 M LiOH (pH >12.0) under an ice bath for 0.5 h. The product formation was monitored by electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS). After the reaction was completed, the pH was immediately adjusted to 7.0 using hydrochloric acid (1 M). Then the detrifluoroacetylated oligosaccharide, for example, 1.3 mM, GlcNH2-GlcA- GlcNH2-GlcA-GlcNH2-GlcA-pNP) was N-sulfated in a solution containing 2-(N-morpholino) ethanesulfonic acid (MES, 50 mM), pH 7.0, and N-sulfotransferase (32 μg/ml) and PAPS (2 molar equivalent of free amino groups) at 37 °C overnight in a reaction volume of 5.6 L.

Step 4, 2-O-sulfation/epimerization, was to convert a GlcA residue to an IdoA2S residue using both C5-epi and 2-OST. For example, the oligosaccharide GlcNS-GlcA-GlcNS-GlcA-GlcNS-GlcA-pNP (1.2 mM) was incubated in a solution containing Tris (25 mM) buffer (pH 7.5) and C5-epi (3 μg/ml), 2-OST (6.5 μg/ml), and PAPS (1.8 mM) at 37 °C overnight in a reaction volume of 4.9 L. Repeating step 1-4 three times. GlcNS-GlcA-GlcNS-(IdoA2S-GlcNS)4-GlcA-pNP was obtained.

Step 5, 6-O-sulfation step was to introduce 6-O-sulfation using both 6-OST-3. The oligosaccharide intermediate GlcNS-GlcA-GlcNS-(IdoA2S-GlcNS)4-GlcA-pNP (0.5 mM) was incubated in a buffer containing MES (50 mM, pH 7.0), 6-OST-3 (100 μg/ml), and PAPS (3 molar equivalent of 6-hydroxyl groups) at 37 °C overnight in 1 L.

Step 6, 3-O-sulfation step, was to introduce 3-O-sulfation by 3-OST-1. The oligosaccharide GlcNS6S-GlcA-GlcNS6S-(IdoA2S-GlcNS6S)4-GlcA-pNP (0.5 mM) was incubated in a solution containing MES (50 mM) buffer (pH 7.0), 3-OST-1 (20 μg/ml), and PAPS (0.675 mM) in 0.9 L at 37 °C for 2 h. After purification, Dekaparin, GlcNS6S-GlcA-GlcNS6S3S-(IdoA2S-GlcNS6S)4-GlcA-pNP was obtained. At every synthesis step, the products were monitored by Shimadzu HPLC equipped with a Propac- column (9X250mm, Thermo). The MWs of the intermediates from each step were confirmed by ESI-MS. All the sulfated products were purified using Q-Sepharose fast flow column (GE Life Science) eluted with a linear gradient 0–100% 2 M NaCl in 20 mM NaOAc-HAcO, pH 5.0, for 3–6 h. Different sizes of Q-Sepharose columns were chosen based on the binding affinity of the product and the reaction scale.

Endotoxin removing and test

CS 19-mer and dekaparin solutions (~500 mg, ~10–20 mg/mL) were filtered using a 50 mL centrifugal filtration unit (Amicon Ultra-15, Ultracel-100 kDa; Merck Millipore) at 4000 rpm (~4000 × g) for 10 min. The filtration was followed by four sequential washes, each performed by refilling the sample chamber with 30 mL of water and centrifuging under the same conditions. The absorbance (O.D.) of the final wash was monitored to ensure recovery yield. The filtered retentate was collected and dried.

Endotoxin levels were measured using the Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) assay (Associates of Cape Cod Inc.). Briefly, 0.1 mL of reconstituted Pyrotell reagent (sensitivity: 0.03EU/mL) was added to 0.1 mL of each oligosaccharide sample prepared at various concentrations (96, 48, 24, 12, 6, 3, and 1.5 µg/mL) in 10 × 75 mm depyrogenated glass reaction tubes (Associates of Cape Cod Inc.). After thorough mixing, samples were incubated in a dry-block incubator at 37 °C for 60 ± 2 min. Tubes were then removed and inverted 180°. The formation of a stable gel clot indicated the presence of endotoxin at levels greater than or equal to the assay sensitivity. The endotoxin concentration for all oligosaccharide preparations was determined to be <5EU/mg.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical significance between experimental and control groups was analyzed by two-tailed unpaired Student t test, between multiple groups by one-way ANOVA followed by Fisher’s LSD or Tukey’s multiple comparison test.
