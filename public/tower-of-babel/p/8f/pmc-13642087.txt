Transmissive Terahertz Tunable and Multifunctional Polarization Manipulation With Ge/VO2 Hybrid Metasurface
Authors: Xingxing Fan, Qiang Bian, Qingguo Du, Song Sun, Qin Fu, Zhengying Li, Fei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTo meet the growing demand for transmissive tunable multifunctional polarization manipulation devices, a tunable multifunctional polarization‐manipulating metasurface (TMPMM) based on germanium dielectric pillars and VO2 grating is proposed. When the VO2 grating is in the insulating state, efficient cross‐polarization conversion in the frequency range of 3.88–5.5 THz is achieved, with a maximum conversion efficiency exceeding 0.98. Within the frequency ranges of 2.31–2.61 THz and 6.42–6.68 THz, the linearly polarized (LP) wave is efficiently converted to a circularly polarized (CP) wave. When the VO2 grating is in the metallic state, the function of asymmetric transmission (AT) is realized, where the TMPMM can be utilized as a broadband isolator and half‐wave plate for an x‐polarized wave and can also be utilized as a narrowband isolator and polarization conversion switch for a y‐polarized wave. Under the forward incidence of CP waves, the TMPMM selectively transmits left‐ and right‐circularly polarized waves near 2.25 THz and 6.37 THz, respectively, achieving significant spin‐dependent polarization selection. Additionally, through simple temperature tuning, the proposed TMPMM enables dynamic control of AT, circular dichroism, and ellipticity. All these multifunctional and tunable properties are realized via temperature variation without altering the structural geometry parameters.Keywords: asymmetric transmission, multifunctional polarization manipulation, spin‐dependent, tunable

Introduction

Owing to the unique properties of good penetration, low photon energy, and rich spectral information, terahertz (THz) waves have been widely employed in many applications such as wireless communication, biomedical imaging, nondestructive testing, and material spectral analysis [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Flexible manipulation of the polarization state of a THz wave plays a crucial role for realizing the above‐mentioned applications. However, conventional polarization manipulation based on birefringence or the Faraday effect normally suffers from inherent limitations, including narrow operational bandwidth, bulky size, and high insertion losses, which severely hinder the development of polarization manipulation devices toward miniaturization, integration, and high performance [6, 7]. To overcome these limitations, metasurface‐based polarization manipulation devices have attracted attention from researchers, which can enable efficient control of polarization states by designing anisotropic meta‐atoms with enhanced light–matter interactions [8, 9, 10, 11].

To date, polarization manipulation devices based on metasurfaces with different materials or structures have been widely studied [12, 13, 14]. Among these, the metal‐insulator‐metal (MIM) configuration serves as a common one [15, 16, 17]. By carefully engineering the thickness of the dielectric layer to compensate for the intrinsic dispersion generated by the resonance of the metal structure, broadband and dispersion‐free polarization conversion can be achieved in reflection mode [18]. However, reflective metasurfaces suffer from inherent issues such as feed blockage and reliance on additional beam‐steering components, making them difficult to integrate into optical paths as conveniently as transmissive devices [19, 20, 21, 22]. Therefore, the development of transmissive polarization‐control metasurfaces that combine high performance with multifunctionality holds significant application prospects [23]. Because of the inherently high reflectivity of metallic materials in the THz wave [24], polarization converters composed solely of metallic resonators are hard to achieve high conversion efficiency with transmission efficiencies exceeding 0.9 over a broad wavelength range [25, 26, 27]. This limitation severely restricts the polarization conversion performance and working bandwidth of optoelectronic devices when applied in imaging and communication applications [28, 29]. To improve the transmission efficiency, researchers have proposed structures integrating orthogonal metal gratings on the top and bottom of metallic resonators [30, 31, 32, 33]. These designs leverage Fabry–Perot‐like cavity resonances to increase cross‐polarization conversion efficiency [34]. However, this approach relies primarily on the polarization‐selective properties of the front and back gratings, limiting polarization control to linearly polarized (LP) waves. To overcome the limitation and enable efficient manipulation of multi‐polarization states, dielectric metasurfaces composed of structures with a high aspect ratio and materials with a high refractive index have emerged as promising alternatives [35, 36, 37]. Dielectric metasurfaces can excite displacement current resonances, induce strong internal reflection at the interface between the nanostructures and the surrounding low‐refractive‐index media, and effectively trap energy in the form of standing waves while avoiding ohmic losses, thereby establishing the physical foundation for efficient multi‐polarization‐state control [38]. Recently, dielectric metasurfaces have been successfully applied to various functional polarization devices such as half‐wave plates (HWPs) [39], quarter‐wave plates (QWPs) [40], polarization splitters [41], and polarization switches [42]. However, with fixed geometric parameters, most dielectric metasurfaces can achieve only a single function and lack dynamic‐control capabilities. Consequently, enabling a dielectric metasurface to possess high‐efficiency tunable and multifunctional polarization manipulation without altering the unit structure remains a challenge in this research field.

In this work, we propose a terahertz tunable multifunctional polarization‐manipulating metasurface (TMPMM), which consists of cascaded germanium (Ge) dielectric pillars and VO2 grating. The designed metasurface can support multiple functional operations, including HWP, QWP, asymmetric transmission (AT), polarization conversion switching, and spin‐dependent polarization selectivity. Specifically, at 300 K, VO2 is in the insulating state, and the proposed metasurface efficiently converts an LP wave into its orthogonal counterpart over a broadband range of 3.88 THz–5.5 THz, with transmittances exceeding 0.98 near 4.8 THz and a polarization conversion ratio (PCR) reaching 0.999, demonstrating a near‐perfect function as HWP. Within dual frequency bands of 2.31 THz–2.61 THz and 6.42 THz–6.68 THz, the proposed metasurface efficiently transforms an LP wave into a circularly polarized (CP) wave, with an ellipticity above 0.9, realizing a high‐performance QWP. When the temperature is raised to 340 K, VO2 is in the metallic state, and the proposed metasurface shows a significant AT response across 3.68 THz–5.87 THz (AT>0.8), with a maximum AT of 0.958, making it highly suitable for optical isolators and polarization switches. Under CP forward illumination, the structure selectively transmits a left‐circularly polarized (LCP) wave near 2.25 THz and a right‐circularly polarized (RCP) wave near 6.37 THz, exhibiting circular dichroism (CD) values as high as 0.917 and −0.83, respectively. These results indicate that the structure exhibits significant spin‐dependent polarization selectivity, offering new insights into constructing robust CP/LP wave generators. Through Jones matrix analysis and multipole decomposition, the underlying physical mechanisms of polarization manipulation are systematically discussed. Furthermore, with only temperature tuning, the metasurface enables dynamic multidimensional control over AT, CD, and ellipticity, including flexible switching between symmetric and asymmetric transmission, transition from nonchiral to strongly chiral responses, and continuous modulation between CP and LP waves. The proposed TMPMM not only integrates diverse polarization‐manipulation capabilities but also enables dynamic reconfiguration, paving the way for further exploration in polarization optics and chiral photonics.

Structure Design and Methods

The schematic diagram of the proposed TMPMM is illustrated in Figure 1. Each unit cell comprises a bilayer structure, with the upper layer consisting of five Ge dielectric pillars placed on a SiO2 substrate with an VO2 grating embedded in the substrate. In the terahertz regime, the refractive index of Ge is 3.96 with low loss [43]. The refractive index of the SiO2 substrate is 1.45 [44]. The relative permittivity of VO2 can be described by the Drude model [6].

Figure 1b shows a top view of the top layer of the unit cell in the x‐y plane, where all Ge dielectric pillars are aligned along the u‐axis at an angle of ξ=45° relative to the x‐axis. The top layer remains in‐plane C2v symmetry, enabling efficient conversion of an incident LP wave into its orthogonally polarized wave or a CP wave. Figure 1c presents a top view of the bottom layer within one unit, where the VO2 grating is aligned with the x‐axis. Dynamic polarization manipulation is achieved by switching the conductivity state of VO2 (insulating or metallic state) through temperature modulation. Unlike previously reported front‐back bilayer grating configurations [30, 31, 32, 33, 34], here, only one layer of grating is employed, which not only offers a simpler design but also enables modulation of the CP wave, providing an additional degree of freedom that enhances flexibility for spin‐dependent manipulation. The modeling and simulation in this work are performed using the finite element method [7]. Perfectly matched layers are applied as absorbing boundary conditions in the z‐direction, whereas periodic boundary conditions are employed along the x‐ and y‐directions.

Results and Discussion

Multifunctional Polarization Converter

At room temperature (300 K), VO2 is in the insulating state with σVO2=20 S/m [6]. Figure 2a,b shows the simulated co‐ and cross‐polarized transmission spectra of the TMPMM under forward and backward incidence of LP waves, respectively. The results indicate that under both forward and backward excitation, the TMPMM can efficiently convert x‐ and y‐polarized waves into waves with orthogonal polarization states over a broad frequency band. One may notice that there are slight differences in the co‐ and cross‐polarized transmittances. This mainly originates from the different modulation effects exerted by the high‐contrast VO2 grating on x‐ and y‐polarized waves [47]; see Supporting Information S1: Figure S1a in Note S1. For forward x‐ and y‐polarized incident waves, the cross‐polarized transmittances exceed 0.8 in the ranges of 3.58 THz–5.77 THz and 3.6 THz–5.66 THz, respectively, reaching maxima of 0.988 at 4.8 THz and 0.98 at 4.9 THz, which demonstrate that the proposed metasurface is a near‐perfect HWP in the working frequency bands. For the HWP function under CP waves, incidents are provided in Supporting Information S1: Note S2. When the x‐ and y‐polarized waves are incident from the backward direction, they are first modulated by the VO2 grating and subsequently converted into the orthogonal polarization state by the Ge dielectric pillars. Consequently, the cross‐polarized transmittances for backward‐incident x‐ and y‐polarized waves are interchanged compared to those of the forward case, whereas the co‐polarized transmittance remains the same. As a result, the co‐ and cross‐polarized curves intersect at different points for different incident polarizations or different propagation directions, as indicated by the blue and red arrows in Figure 2a,b, where the insets show enlarged views of the gray‐shaded regions. Because CP waves can be regarded as the coherent superposition of x‐ and y‐polarized waves, this asymmetric response further leads to polarization‐ and direction‐dependent conversion from LP to CP waves, as shown in Figure 2c,d. In the low‐frequency region around 2.45 THz, forward‐incident x‐ and y‐polarized waves are efficiently converted into LCP and RCP waves, respectively, with maximum conversion efficiencies reaching 0.928 and 0.927. Simultaneously, the corresponding orthogonal components are both below 0.005, realizing near‐perfect QWP performance. For backward incidence, the x‐ and y‐polarized waves are efficiently converted to RCP and LCP waves, respectively, with the chirality inversion of CP waves originating from the change in the observation direction [48]. The resulting RCP and LCP waves exhibit maximum transmittances of 0.917 and 0.937, respectively, with orthogonal components as low as 0.0031 and 0.0037, also exhibiting a near‐perfect function as a QWP. In the high‐frequency region around 6.55 THz, forward‐incident x‐ and y‐polarized waves can still be efficiently converted into RCP and LCP waves, but the corresponding orthogonal components are relatively larger (minimum values of 0.0147 and 0.062, respectively), which limits the ellipticity of the transmitted waves. In contrast, for backward‐incident x‐ and y‐polarized waves, high‐efficiency CP conversion is achieved, while the orthogonal components are effectively suppressed below 0.005, with minima reaching 4×10−4 and 7×10−4, respectively. Therefore, in the low‐frequency region, the proposed TMPMM achieves near‐perfect CP conversion for both forward‐incident and backward‐incident LP waves. In the high‐frequency region, to attain high‐quality CP conversion, backward illumination of the TMPMM is preferred.

In the high‐frequency region, the forward‐incident ellipticities ηxf and −ηyf reach maxima of 0.836 at 6.59 THz and 0.893 at 6.51 THz, respectively, whereas the ellipticities of backward‐incident waves, −ηxb and ηyb, exceed 0.9 within the ranges of 6.37 THz–6.68 THz and 6.42 THz–6.75 THz, respectively, with maxima of 0.975 at 6.52 THz and 0.966 at 6.57 THz. Hence, in the high‐frequency region, the TMPMM with backward incidence can be adopted to effectively achieve broadband CP conversion for both x‐ and y‐polarized incident waves. In summary, at room temperature, the proposed TMPMM simultaneously achieves the functionalities of high‐efficiency broadband HWP and QWP.

The underlying physical mechanisms of polarization conversion can be further verified through multipole decomposition and field‐distribution analysis. The contributions of the electric dipole (ED), magnetic dipole (MD), electric toroidal dipole (ETD), magnetic toroidal dipole (MTD), electric quadrupole (EQ), and magnetic quadrupole (MQ) to far‐field radiation are calculated based on the induced current density [49]. Figure 5a,d shows the multipole decomposition results for forward‐incident u‐ and v‐polarized waves, respectively. Scattering power analysis indicates that, at 4.8 THz, where high‐efficiency orthogonal polarization conversion is achieved, the response for a u‐polarized incident wave is primarily governed by the MTD mode, whereas the v‐polarized incidence is jointly controlled by the MTD and ETD modes. The corresponding electric and magnetic field distributions on the right verify these observations, where the red arrows and white arrows represent current vectors and magnetic vectors, respectively. For u‐polarized incidence, the electric field is primarily localized around the dielectric pillars, forming three current loops on the u‐z plane with alternating rotational directions, as indicated by the red circles in Figure 5b. This indicates that three MD modes are excited along the v‐axis. Further analysis of the magnetic field distribution in Figure 5c shows that these three MD modes couple with each other to generate two counter‐rotating magnetic loops and excite the MTD1 mode along the v‐axis, as illustrated schematically in Figure 5g. For v‐polarized incidence, the electric field similarly localizes around the dielectric pillars, forming two current loops with opposite rotations in the v‐z plane, marked by red circles in Figure 5e. This indicates that two opposing MD modes are excited along the v‐axis, thereby forming the ETD2 mode along the v‐axis. Furthermore, the magnetic field distribution in Figure 5f reveals two counter‐rotating magnetic loops on the u‐z plane, exciting an MTD3 mode along the u‐axis. Consequently, the transmission amplitude and phase with u‐polarized incidence are mainly modulated by the MTD1 mode, whereas those with v‐polarized incidence are jointly regulated by the ETD2 and MTD3 modes, as depicted in Figure 5i. Because the field intensity excited by v‐polarized waves in the TMPMM is significantly lower than that for u‐polarized waves, the corresponding variations in amplitude and phase are relatively weaker, as shown in Figure 4. The conversion from LP to CP can also be interpreted through scattering power analysis. At 2.45 THz, with u‐polarized incidence, the response is dominated by the MD1 and ED2 modes [see inset in Figure 5a], whereas with v‐polarized incidence, the response mainly excites the ED3 mode. Broadband Asymmetric Transmission and Polarization Conversion Switching

When the temperature increases to 340 K, VO2 transforms into its metallic state with σVO2=2×105 S/m [6]. In this state, the VO2 grating embedded in the substrate functions as a polarizer, providing high transmission for the y‐polarized wave and high reflection for the x‐polarized wave across a broad frequency band [34]. Detailed transmission and reflection spectra are provided in Supporting Information S1: Figure S1b in Note S1. Because of forward‐incident LP waves being first efficiently converted into their orthogonal polarization by the Ge dielectric pillars and then interacting with the VO2 grating, their transmission behavior differs remarkably from the case of backward incidence, where the wave impinges directly on the VO2 grating. The contrast is shown in Figure 6. Figure 6a,b presents the co‐ and cross‐polarized transmission spectra under forward and backward incidence of LP waves, respectively. Firstly, for the x‐polarized wave under forward incidence, the co‐polarized component vanishes across the entire frequency band of 2 THz−7 THz, whereas the cross‐polarized component exceeds 0.8 in the range of 3.7 THz–5.86 THz, with a maximum of 0.963 at 5 THz, demonstrating near‐perfect polarization conversion, as shown by the black and blue curves in Figure 6a. Under backward incidence, both of the transmittances of co‐ and cross‐polarized waves remain close to zero throughout the 2‐THz–7‐THz band, appearing as two flat lines, as shown by the black and blue curves in Figure 6b. Clearly, the transmission of x‐polarized waves exhibits significant asymmetry between the two propagation directions. The AT parameter for the x‐polarized wave Δlinx, calculated from Equation (7a), is plotted and indicated as the blue curve in Figure 6c. The results show that Δlinx>0.8 across the broadband of 3.7 THz–5.86 THz (gray‐shaded region), with a maximum of 0.958 at 5 THz. Clearly, at higher temperatures, the proposed TMPMM can function as a high‐performance broadband optical isolator, efficiently transmitting the forward‐incident x‐polarized wave while almost completely blocking the backward‐incident counterpart.

Next, consider the case of y‐polarized incidence. Under forward incidence, the cross‐polarized transmittance remains nearly zero across the entire 2 THz–7 THz frequency band, whereas the co‐polarized transmittance approaches zero near 5 THz and increases as the frequency deviates from this frequency, as shown by the orange and red dashed curves in Figure 6a. This phenomenon arises because the Ge dielectric pillars efficiently convert the incident y‐polarized wave into an x‐polarized wave over a broadband frequency range centered at 4.8 THz, resulting in the x‐polarized wave being reflected back by the VO2 grating. As the frequency shifts outside the conversion band, the conversion efficiency gradually decreases, allowing the unconverted y‐polarized component to transmit through the TMPMM. Under backward incidence, the polarization modulation effect of the TMPMM primarily depends on the germanium dielectric pillars because the VO2 grating is highly transparent to the y‐polarized wave. Consequently, the co‐polarized transmittance approaches zero around 5 THz, whereas the cross‐polarized transmittance reaches the maximum, as indicated by the red and orange dashed curves in Figure 6b. The AT parameter for the y‐polarized wave Δliny, calculated from Equation (7b), is plotted as the red curve in Figure 6c. It can be seen that, over the broadband of 3.7 THz–5.86 THz (gray‐shaded region), Δliny<−0.8, with a minimum of −0.958 at 5 THz, which is the opposite of Δlinx. Hence, for a y‐polarized wave, the proposed TMPMM achieves broadband high AT. Notably, around 5 THz, it only efficiently transmits a backward‐incident y‐polarized wave while almost completely blocking the forward‐incident counterpart, demonstrating its potential application as a narrowband optical isolator.

For the backward‐incident y‐polarized wave, PCRxb>0.9 across the 4.45‐THz–5.59‐THz range, demonstrating that the TMPMM can efficiently convert the backward‐incident y‐polarized wave into an x‐polarized wave within the spectral region.

Moreover, over the entire 2 THz–7 THz frequency band, ∣tyxf∣≫∣txxf∣, indicating that the forward‐incident x‐polarized wave is efficiently converted into a y‐polarized wave, whereas the backward‐incident x‐polarized wave is almost completely blocked. This confirms that the structure can serve as a high‐performance broadband optical isolator. The frequency of 4.95 THz obtained here is consistent with 5 THz, as shown in Figure 6; the slight deviation of 0.05 THz arises from approximations in the amplitudes and phases. A detailed analysis of the mechanisms of the AT for the y‐polarized wave is provided in Supporting Information S1: Note S5. The corresponding electric field distributions are shown in Supporting Information S1: Note S6.

Spin‐Dependent Polarization Selection

As discussed in Section 3.1, the Ge dielectric pillars function as QWPs in both the low‐frequency and high‐frequency regions. When CP waves of opposite polarizations are incident from the forward direction, they are transformed into orthogonal LP waves in the low‐frequency and high‐frequency regions, respectively. Additionally, at 340 K, the VO2 grating functions as a linear polarization selector. Obviously, in both the low‐frequency and high‐frequency regions, CP waves with different polarizations are efficiently transmitted or reflected after interacting with the TMPMM, achieving spin‐dependent polarization selection. To validate this analysis, the simulated transmission and reflection spectra for RCP and LCP waves under forward incidence are shown in Figure 9a,b, respectively. In the low‐frequency region near 2.25 THz, for an incident RCP wave, the peak reflectance for a co‐polarized wave reaches 0.836, whereas the cross‐polarized reflectance, co‐polarized transmittance, and cross‐polarized transmittance all remain below 0.014. This indicates that the incident RCP wave is efficiently reflected while preserving its helicity. For the incident LCP wave, the co‐ and cross‐polarized reflectances are only 0.043 and 0.01, respectively, whereas the co‐ and cross‐polarized transmittances are 0.459 and 0.471, respectively. This indicates that the incident LCP wave is efficiently transmitted and converted into an LP wave, as illustrated by the orange curve in Figure 9c. In the high‐frequency region near 6.37 THz, for an incident RCP wave, the reflectances are low (0.083 for co‐polarized and 0.032 for cross‐polarized), whereas both transmittances of co‐ and cross‐polarized waves are approximately equal to 0.427. This indicates that the incident RCP wave is efficiently transmitted and converted into an LP wave, as shown by the blue curve in Figure 9c. For an incident LCP wave, the co‐polarized reflectance reaches 0.798, whereas the corresponding cross‐polarized reflectance, co‐polarized transmittance, and cross‐polarized transmittance are all below 0.033, indicating that the incident LCP wave is efficiently reflected while preserving its helicity. The proposed TMPMM selectively reflects the RCP wave in the low‐frequency region and the LCP wave in the high‐frequency region, exhibiting remarkable spin‐dependent polarization selection. Notably, because LP waves of any polarization state can be expressed as a linear superposition of RCP and LCP components, the TMPMM efficiently reflects RCP waves (while preserving their chirality) and exhibits near‐zero reflectivity for LCP waves at approximately 2.25 THz. Therefore, at this frequency, the structure can effectively convert LP waves of arbitrary polarization into RCP waves. In transmission, the TMPMM efficiently transmits LCP waves and converts them into y‐polarized waves, while exhibiting near‐zero transmittance for RCP waves at approximately 2.25 THz. Meanwhile, benefiting from the polarization‐selective characteristics of the grating, RCP waves incident at other frequencies are also converted into y‐polarized waves in transmission. Therefore, over a broad frequency range around this frequency, the structure can convert LP waves of arbitrary polarization into near‐perfect y‐polarized waves. A detailed discussion is provided in Supporting Information S1: Note S7. This conversion mechanism, based on spin‐dependent polarization selection, differs distinctly from previous reports that require specific incident polarization directions [50, 51, 52]. The findings here offer a novel approach for constructing robust CP/LP wave generators with low sensitivity to polarization angle. Furthermore, through structural optimization, the chiral response can be further enhanced, as discussed in detail in Supporting Information S1: Note S8.

Various Dynamic Controls of AT, CD and Ellipticity With Conductivity Changes

Next, systematic investigation of the three tunable properties enabled by the conductivity variation of VO2 (corresponding to variations in temperature) for device functionality is performed, including dynamic control of AT, CD, and ellipticity. All these tunable properties confirm that the proposed TMPMM serves as an effective platform for dynamic polarization manipulation.

Dynamic Control of AT

Figure 10a shows the variation trend of the AT parameters with different conductivities of VO2. It can be observed that, within the frequency range of 3.7 THz–5.86 THz, as σVO2 increases from 20 S/m to 2×105 S/m, the magnitude of the AT parameter effectively rises from near zero to above 0.8. This demonstrates that the AT efficiency can be flexibly controlled by tuning the conductivity. Specifically, at 5 THz, the AT parameter increases from 0.001 to 0.958, yielding a modulation depth as high as 0.957, which highlights the significant advantage of the proposed structure for achieving high‐contrast AT switching. It is worth emphasizing that, although previous studies have realized dynamic tuning of the AT parameter by integrating bilayer metallic gratings with tunable materials, all of these approaches typically come at the cost of sacrificing forward transmittance [30, 53]. In particular, when the AT parameter in those structures is tuned to zero, both forward‐ and backward‐incident waves are reflected, and transmission is strongly suppressed, limiting the utilization efficiency of the system's energy. For comparison, Figure 10b presents the cross‐polarized transmittance of the TMPMM under different σVO2. It is observed that, as σVO2 increases, the overall transmittance first decreases and then increases, reaching its minimum at σVO2=5×103 S/m. Nevertheless, around 5 THz, the cross‐polarized transmittance remains above 0.8 for all the examined conductivities, as indicated by the black dashed line in Figure 10b. Accordingly, the proposed TMPMM not only enables dynamic control of AT but also maintains high‐efficiency transmission, a feature that is crucial for practical applications such as communication and imaging [28, 29].

Dynamic Control of CD

Dynamic control of the chiral response holds significant potential for applications such as polarization manipulation, sensing, and imaging [54]. However, modifying chiral effects often faces challenges because the chiral response mainly relies on the structural parameters of the metasurface, which are difficult to adjust after fabrication [55, 56]. To address this issue, this work leverages the tunable conductivity of VO2 in the proposed TMPMM and presents an effective method for achieving dynamic control of CD. The dynamically tunable performance of CDT is illustrated in Figure 11. Figure 11a shows the CDT spectra at different σVO2, whereas Figure 11b presents the corresponding transmittance difference of the VO2 grating for x‐ and y‐polarized waves. When σVO2=20 S/m, the VO2 grating exhibits nearly identical transmission modulation for x‐ and y‐polarized waves across the frequency range of 0.3 THz–6.26 THz, with a transmittance difference below 0.05 (black curve in Figure 11b). Consequently, the transmittance with RCP and LCP incidence is mostly the same, resulting in an overall nonchiral response. At frequencies above 6.26 THz, the transmittance of the VO2 grating for the y‐polarized wave gradually exceeds that for the x‐polarized wave. At 6.8 THz, because the incident RCP wave is mainly converted into a y‐polarized wave by the Ge dielectric pillars, whereas the LCP wave is primarily converted into an x‐polarized wave, the transmittance difference of −0.144 between the two polarizations leads to a corresponding difference in the transmitted RCP and LCP waves, generating a weak CDT response of −0.129, as shown by the black curve in Figure 11a. As σVO2 gradually increases, the transmittance difference between x‐ and y‐polarized waves in the VO2 grating exhibits an increasing trend, effectively enhancing the CDT response. When σVO2 increases from 20 S/m to 2×105 S/m, at 2.27 THz and 6.36 THz, CDT is tuned from 0.021 and −0.07 to 0.917 and −0.83, respectively, with corresponding maximum modulation depths ΔCDT reaching 0.896 and −0.76. Ultimately, by tuning the VO2 conductivity, CDT can be transformed from a nearly zero nonchiral response to a strong chiral response exceeding 0.7 across the dual broadband ranges of 0.86 THz–3.7 THz and 6.03 THz–6.83 THz.

Dynamic Control of Ellipticity

In addition to enabling dynamic control of AT and CD, the proposed TMPMM structure also offers continuous control over the polarization state of the transmitted waves, allowing on‐demand output of CP, elliptically polarized (EP), or LP waves. Figure 12a,b shows the ellipticity η of the transmitted wave as a function of σVO2 for x‐ and y‐polarized incidence, respectively. The results demonstrate that, within the frequency range of 2.31 THz–2.58 THz, as σVO2 increases from 20 S/m to 2×105 S/m, the η of the forward‐incident x‐polarized wave can be effectively tuned from above 0.9 to below 0.1, whereas that for forward‐incident y‐polarized waves shifts from below −0.9 to above −0.1. This demonstrates a continuous transition from RCP/LCP waves toward LP waves, respectively. Figure 12c,d presents the polarization azimuth angle φ (where φ=tan−1S2/S1/2 [27]) of the transmitted wave as a function of σVO2 for forward‐incident x‐ and y‐polarized waves, respectively. It can be observed that the drastic changes in φ primarily occur within the range of small σVO2, whereas φ approaches 90° for both incidence cases as σVO2 further increases. The drastic changes in φ in the low‐conductivity range occur because the transmitted waves in this range approximate ideal CP waves (particularly under y‐polarized incidence), and because φ of an ideal CP wave cannot be defined, even minor variations in electric field amplitude and phase can lead to significant changes in φ. For this reason, in Figure 12d, φ variations corresponding to η≈1 are partially omitted to prevent the excessively wide range of variation from obscuring the trend of φ in other regions. When σVO2 is large, the polarization‐selective properties of the grating allow only the transmission of the y‐polarized component, causing φ to gradually approach 90°. To quantitatively describe the evolution of the polarization states, Figure 12e,f presents the amplitudes of η and φ at 2.45 THz under different σVO2, respectively. When σVO2=20 S/m, the ellipticities after x‐ and y‐polarized incident waves reach as high as −0.925 and 0.996, respectively, indicating that the incident waves are efficiently converted into LCP and RCP waves. When σVO2=2×105 S/m, the corresponding ellipticities decrease to as low as −0.052 and 0.075, respectively, demonstrating that the transmitted waves have evolved into LP waves. Therefore, the continuous transition of η from near 1/−1 to around 0 in Figure 12e fully demonstrates the capability of the TMPMM to achieve complete polarization control. Regarding the polarization azimuth angle φ, opposite trends are observed for x‐ and y‐polarized incident waves, as shown by the blue and red curves in Figure 12f. As σVO2 increases, the φ for x‐polarized incidence first increases and then decreases to approximately 90°, whereas φ for y‐polarized incidence first decreases and then increases to around 90°. Figure 12g further plots the evolution of the polarization state of the transmitted wave on the Poincaré sphere. It can be seen that as σVO2 increases, the polarization trajectories for x‐ and y‐polarized incident waves converge from the two poles of the sphere toward the equator, respectively, intuitively revealing the transition of the transmitted wave from CP to LP, which is in excellent agreement with the previous analysis. Figure 12h displays representative polarization states at 2.45 THz for different σVO2. When σVO2=20 S/m, σVO2=2×104 S/m, and σVO2=2×105 S/m, the incident x‐polarized wave can be converted into LCP, LEP, and y‐polarized waves, respectively, whereas the incident y‐polarized wave can be converted into RCP, REP, and y‐polarized waves, respectively. Thus, by dynamically tuning the conductivity of VO2, the proposed TMPMM provides an effective route toward active and flexible polarization‐state manipulation.

Potential Fabrication Methods of the TMPMM

The proposed metasurface structure can be fabricated using the process flow illustrated in Figure 13. The main steps are outlined as follows. The fabrication starts with a SiO2 handle layer approximately 300 μm thick. First, a VO2 thin film is deposited onto the SiO2 handle layer via magnetron sputtering, and the VO2 grating structure is formed through electron‐beam lithography and a lift‐off process [58, 59]. Subsequently, a 15‐μm‐thick SiO2 layer is deposited via plasma‐enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD) [60], and a Ge film is deposited onto the SiO2 layer using high‐density plasma chemical vapor deposition (HDPCVD) [61, 62]. A chromium (Cr) film is then deposited on the Ge surface as a hard mask layer using the electron‐beam (E‐beam) deposition technique [36]. The Cr film is subsequently coated with photoresist. The structural features of the central dielectric pillars are exposed by E‐beam lithography (to facilitate observation of the multidielectric pillar characteristics, cross‐sectional views in the v‐z plane are used hereafter, where v represents the short‐axis direction of the Ge dielectric pillar). The nanopattern is transferred onto the SiO2 mask using a lift‐off process. The pattern is then transferred to the Cr hard mask layer using an inductively coupled plasma‐reactive ion etching (ICP‐RIE) system [36]. The sample with the patterned Cr hard mask layer is etched by ICP to achieve the desired height difference between the central Ge pillar and the side pillars. A Cr film is then reapplied as a hard mask layer, followed by coating the Cr film with photoresist. The structural features of all pillars are exposed by E‐beam lithography. The nanopattern is transferred onto the SiO2 mask using a lift‐off process, and the pattern is subsequently transferred to the Cr hard mask layer using ICP‐RIE. The Cr hard mask layer is then etched. The sample is etched by ICP to fabricate all Ge pillars. The patterned Cr hard mask is removed using a Cr etchant, yielding the top Ge dielectric pillars. Finally, the SiO2 in the handle layer is etched by ICP to reduce the thickness beneath the corresponding region of the top Ge pillars to 30 μm. Meanwhile, the SiO2 handle layer in the nonstructural region is retained to serve as a supporting structure [63], obtaining the final sample.

Conclusion

In summary, a TMPMM based on cascaded Ge dielectric pillars and a VO2 grating is proposed. At room temperature (300 K), the structure achieves efficient cross‐polarization conversion within a broad bandwidth of 3.88 THz–5.5 THz for both forward and backward incidence of LP waves, with an overall orthogonal polarization transmittance above 0.86 and PCRs exceeding 0.9, serving as an efficient broadband HWP. Within the frequency ranges of 2.31 THz–2.61 THz and 6.42 THz–6.68 THz, a backward‐incident x‐polarized (y‐polarized) wave is efficiently converted into RCP (LCP) and LCP (RCP) waves, respectively, with the ellipticity consistently remaining above 0.9. The maximum conversion efficiencies reach 0.917 (0.937) and 0.87 (0.954), demonstrating functionality as a high‐performance QWP. At high temperatures (340 K), the VO2 grating exhibits high transmission for x‐polarized waves and high reflection for y‐polarized waves, thereby generating a significant AT effect for forward‐ and backward‐incident LP waves. This effect enables broadband isolators and an HWP for x‐polarized waves, as well as narrowband isolators and polarization conversion optical switches for y‐polarized waves. When forward‐incident RCP and LCP waves are considered, the structure selectively transmits LCP and RCP waves near 2.25 THz and 6.37 THz, respectively, demonstrating remarkable spin‐dependent polarization selectivity. This provides a novel approach for constructing robust CP/LP wave generators. Through Jones matrix analysis and multipole decomposition, the physical mechanisms underlying these polarization controls are systematically elucidated. Furthermore, by leveraging the temperature‐controlled conductivity of VO2, the proposed TMPMM enables dynamic control of AT, CD, and ellipticity, allowing for flexible switching between symmetric and asymmetric transmission, transitions from nonchiral to strongly chiral responses, and continuous modulation between CP and LP waves. Our work provides a valuable design strategy for realizing multifunctional, multiband, highly efficient, and dynamically tunable polarization manipulation devices, which is expected to advance the development of polarization devices and chiral functional devices in the field of integrated photonics.
