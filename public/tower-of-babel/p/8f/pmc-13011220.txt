Not just a mark: Seatbelt sign as a clue to hidden abdominal wall avulsion
Authors: Arpád Panyko, Martin Dubovský, Marián Vidiščák, Marianna Hajská
Published: 2026

A 20-year-old male with no prior medical history was involved in a high-speed (cca 80 km/h) head-on collision while seated in the middle back seat, restrained by a 2-point seatbelt. On arrival, he was hemodynamically stable (BP 125/80 mmHg, HR 90/min) but complained of lower abdominal pain and dyspnea (SpO₂ 92%). Examination revealed tenderness, ecchymosis, and a visible bulge over the lower abdomen and pelvis (Fig. 1). Laboratory findings included WBC 43 × 109/L, S-Myoglobin 2570 μg/L, US-Ery 83, and US-Leu 44. Contrast-enhanced CT showed a complete disruption of the lower anterior abdominal wall with herniation of small bowel loops and free intra-abdominal fluid (Fig. 2; Table 1). The injury involved full-thickness rupture of the rectus abdominis, external, internal oblique, and transversus abdominis muscles bilaterally. Shortly after, his condition deteriorated with increasing pain, hypotension, and tachycardia (HR 130/min). Exploratory laparotomy revealed over 1.5 L of hemoperitoneum, mesenteric avulsions of the small bowel and caecum, serosal tears, and perforation—necessitating right hemicolectomy. Additional lacerations were identified in the sigmoid and upper rectal mesocolon. The abdominal wall defect was repaired layer by layer using continuous non-absorbable sutures. Postoperative CT showed no residual herniation or complications. Recovery was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 16.

Traumatic abdominal wall hernia (TAWH) is defined as a muscular and fascial disruption without skin penetration after blunt trauma and represents about 1% of such cases. Motor vehicle collisions cause most TAWHs (60–70%), with nearly half associated with seatbelt use [1], [2]. The hernias usually occur in weak lower abdominal areas, often lumbar. Seatbelts, especially when improperly positioned across the abdomen rather than the bony pelvis, can cause a sudden rise in intra-abdominal pressure and shearing of muscles from their pelvic attachments. Concomitant intra-abdominal injuries occur in 60–90% of TAWHs. CT is the diagnostic gold standard, as more than half of TAWHs are missed on physical examination. CT-based grading systems aid in assessing severity [2]. Management should be individualized, considering defect size, hernia contents, and associated visceral injuries. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for TAWH in seatbelt-related trauma and the necessity of prompt CT evaluation to detect hidden injuries. While seatbelts remain lifesaving, correct positioning is essential to prevent severe abdominal wall and visceral trauma.
