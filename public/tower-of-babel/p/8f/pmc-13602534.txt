A non-classical necroptosis pathway mediated by caspases
Authors: Qingyue Wang, Hang Xu, Xin Ding, Li Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNecroptosis is a regulated form of cell death mediated by the RIPK1/RIPK3−MLKL pathway. In humans, caspase (CASP) 8 negatively regulates the necroptotic pathway by inactivating RIPK1 and RIPK3. In teleosts and other non-mammalian vertebrates, the role of caspase in necroptosis remains to be explored. By using large yellow croaker Larimichthys crocea as a representative teleost species, this work investigated caspase-mediated regulation on teleost necroptosis.MethodsNecroptosis was investigated with microscopy and biochemical assays. Caspase cleavages were analyzed via immunoblotting. Protein-protein interactions were examined by co-immunoprecipitation. Evolutionary conservations were assessed through sequence alignment and WebLogo analysis.ResultsThe three core necroptotic machinery components (RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL) were identified from L. crocea and named LcRIPK1, LcRIPK3, and LcMLKL, respectively. Cellular transfection studies showed that LcRIPK3 complexed with LcRIPK1 and recruited LcMLKL. The recruited LcMLKL was subsequently activated by LcRIPK3. LcMLKL possessed two conserved phosphorylation sites essential to LcMLKL activation, and mimetic phosphorylation of these sites induced strong necroptosis. The activities of LcRIPK1, LcRIPK3, and LcMLKL were all subjected to caspase regulation. L. crocea CASP (LcCASP) 3 and 6 inactivated LcRIPK1 by preferentially cleaving at 366VEVD369, while LcCASP1/3/6 inactivated LcRIPK3 by preferentially cleaving at 377CDVD380. In contrast, LcCASP1/3/7 activated LcMLKL by cleaving LcMLKL at 133DAVD136 to generate a constitutively active necroptosis executor, thus bypassing the RIPK1/3 signaling. A survey across Actinopterygii, Amphibia, Aves, and Mammalia showed that the standard caspase cleavage site (p4-xxxD-p1) was broadly conserved in vertebrate RIPK1/3, but the critical P4 residue varied in teleosts and exhibited lineage-specific conservation in mammals and amphibians. The DxxD motif was universally present in teleost, mammalian, and avian MLKL, while the NxxD motif was highly conserved in amphibian MLKL. These results suggested that caspase-mediated necroptosis regulation may be a common feature shared by most vertebrates.ConclusionsThis study revealed a non-canonical necroptotic pathway mediated by integrated caspase regulation networks in teleosts and provided a strong hypothesis for the existence of a conserved CASPs−MLKL pathway in other vertebrate lineages. These findings added new insights into the complex regulation mechanisms of necroptosis in Vertebrata.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-026-03035-3.

Introduction

Programmed cell death (PCD), including necroptosis and apoptosis, plays critical roles in host defense and tissue homeostasis [1–4]. While apoptosis is immunologically silent, necroptosis causes plasma membrane rupture and the release of intracellular contents known as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), thereby triggering potent inflammatory and immune responses [5, 6]. The process of necroptosis is predominantly characterized in mammals. It is initiated by receptors such as tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 (TNFR1) when the apoptotic signaling is blocked, leading to the activation of receptor-interacting protein kinase (RIPK) 1. RIPK1 then interacts with RIPK3, via a RIP homotypic interaction motif (RHIM) present in both of these molecules, and activates RIPK3 [7–10]. The RIPK1−RIPK3 complex thus formed, termed necrosome, and RIPK3 phosphorylates MLKL (mixed lineage kinase domain like protein), which is the terminal effector of necroptosis [11, 12]. The phosphorylation event induces a critical conformational change in MLKL, leading to the exposure of its N-terminal four-helical bundle (4HB) domain [13–15]. The exposed 4HB domain drives MLKL homo-oligomerization, and the oligomers translocate to and bind the phosphatidylinositol phosphates (PIPs) on the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane [16–19]. Ultimately, membrane-embedded MLKL oligomers form pores in the membrane, resulting in ion dysregulation, osmotic imbalance, cellular swelling, plasma membrane rupture, and the release of DAMPs that characterize necroptotic cell death [20–24].

CASP is a conserved family of cysteine-aspartic proteases that act as master regulators of PCD [25–29]. Structurally and functionally, caspases are categorized into three groups: initiator caspases (CASP2, 8, 9, and 10), effector caspases (CASP3, 6, and 7), and inflammatory caspases (CASP1, 4, 5 and 12) [30]. These enzymes recognize and hydrolyze substrates at a specific tetrapeptide site (denoted P4-P3-P2-P1) with a highly conserved aspartate (D) residue at the P1 position [31–33]. The recognition preference of the caspase is largely determined by the residue occupying the P4 position. For instance, CASP3 and CASP7 exhibit a specificity for the DxxD motif, while CASP8 preferentially cleaves at the LxxD motif [33]. Several studies have demonstrated that caspases regulate not only apoptosis but also necroptosis [28, 34]. In humans, the initiator CASP8 serves as the primary inhibitory switch of necroptosis by cleaving both RIPK1 and RIPK3 at specific aspartate residues [35, 36]. The cleavages disable the interaction between RIPK1 and RIPK3, thereby preventing the assembly of the necrosome complex (RIPK1-RIPK3) essential for recruiting and activating MLKL [37–39]. When the CASP8 activity is compromised, the assembly of RIPK1 and RIPK3 is allowed, and necroptosis can be initiated [29, 40, 41]. In mice, CASP8 deficiency leads to embryonic lethality, as unrestrained necroptosis disrupts the integrity of the yolk sac vasculature [42, 43]. In addition to RIPK, MLKL was also reported to be cleaved by caspases in certain cellular contexts [44]. However, whether caspase cleavage affects MLKL activity remains an unresolved question.

Comparing to mammals, studies on non-mammalian necroptosis are limited. Researches on necroptosis in non-mammalian vertebrates have been largely confined to identifying and cataloguing the core molecular components of the necroptotic pathway [45]. Teleost fish, which roughly comprise half of all extant vertebrate species, represent a highly diverse and evolutionarily significant group within the vertebrate lineage [46, 47]. Recent studies showed that genes encoding a complete set of necroptotic molecules (RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL) were present in most teleost species, and exceptions were observed with zebrafish (Danio rerio) and pufferfish (Takifugu rubripes), which lacked MLKL [45, 48]. Another study showed that in Japanese flounder (Paralichthys olivaceus), MLKL-mediated necroptosis was activated via RIPK1/3-dependent phosphorylation, indicating a functional conservation that mirrors the established mechanism in mammals [49]. To date, the regulation of necroptosis, particularly that mediated by caspase, remains poorly understood in fish as well as other non-mammalian vertebrates.

Large yellow croaker Larimichthys crocea has been used in the study of teleost biology in various aspects [50, 51]. It is also an important aquaculture species in Asia. In recent years, L. crocea culture has been affected by infectious diseases that lead to substantial annual economic losses through increased mortality and reduced production [52–55]. Understanding the necroptotic pathway may facilitate the development of disease-control strategies to improve fish production. In this work, we used large yellow croaker as a representative fish to study the role of caspase in teleost necroptosis. We examined the ability of L. crocea MLKL (LcMLKL) to mediate necroptosis, the activation mechanism of LcMLKL by L. crocea RIPK1/3 (LcRIPK1/3), and the effects of seven L. crocea CASPs (LcCASPs) on the activity of LcRIPK1/3 and LcMLKL. We also examined the possibility of conserved caspase regulation of necroptosis in Vertebrata. The results of our study deepened the understanding of the regulatory mechanism of necroptosis in teleosts as well as other vertebrates. In addition, these results also provided a theoretical basis to develop technologies that apply necroptosis regulations to the aquaculture industry.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and transfection

HEK293T cells (purchased from American Type Culture Collection) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Renfrewshire, UK) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The cells were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling, confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination, and used between passages 3 and 15 after thawing. For transfection, HEK293T cells were cultured overnight in 96-, 24-, or 6-well plates (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and then transfected with 100 ng/well (96-well), 500 ng/well (24-well), or 1000 ng/well (6-well) of plasmids expressing the indicated proteins using PolyJet™ Reagent (SignaGen Laboratories, Ijamsville, MD, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Sequence collection and conservation analysis

Sequences were collected as previously described [56]. In brief, reference sequences of RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL were obtained from the NCBI databank and used as queries to search against the specialized databases via BLASTP. For the analysis of conserved caspase cleavage sites in vertebrate RIPK1, RIPK3 and MLKL, the corresponding sequences (Supplemental Table S1) were collected from NCBI, aligned using the MEGA program, and analyzed via WebLogo3 (https://weblogo.threeplusone.com/) [57]. Three-dimensional models of LcRIPK1, LcRIPK3 and LcMLKL were generated with AlphaFold and visualized using Mol* Viewer (https://molstar.org/viewer/) [58].

Gene cloning and mutagenesis

Total RNA was isolated from L. crocea tissues using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme Biotech Co. Ltd, Nanjing, China). cDNA was synthesized with the Revert Aid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The coding sequences (CDSs) of RIPK3 and MLKL were amplified by PCR. The CDS of RIPK1 was synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Site-directed mutagenesis was performed with the Fast Mutagenesis System (Transgen Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The primers used are listed in the Supplemental Table S2.

Immunoblot

Immunoblot was performed as described previously [59]. Briefly, protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Cytiva, MA, USA). After blocking with 5% skim milk for 1 h, the membranes were incubated with appropriate primary antibodies. The membranes were then treated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h, and the proteins were visualized with Sparkjade ECL plus (Sparkjade Biotechnology Co. Ltd, Shandong, China). The primary antibodies used were as follows: Flag-tag (Abclonal, Wuhan, China) (1:3000 dilution), Myc-tag (Abclonal, Wuhan, China) (1:3000 dilution), and β-actin (Abclonal, Wuhan, China) (1:5000 dilution). The secondary antibody was goat anti-mouse IgG-HRP antibody (Abclonal, Wuhan, China) (1:4000 dilution). The antibodies used in the immunoblotting of Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay were as follows: mouse anti-Flag HRP antibody (Abclonal, Wuhan, China) (1:3000 dilution) and mouse anti-Myc HRP antibody (Abclonal, Wuhan, China) (1:3000 dilution). The final concentrations of the antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Microscopy

Microscopy was performed as described previously [59]. Briefly, HEK293T cells were plated in 24-well plate and observed after transfection at about 60% confluency. To detect cell death, the cells were treated with 1 µM Sytox Green (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 10 min after transfection. The bright-field and fluorescent views of the cells were recorded using a Nikon ECLIPSE Tis inverted microscope (Nikon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). For microscopic analysis, approximately 1,000 cells were recorded per experiment, and representative fields of view were selected for presentation. All images were acquired using the same objective (20 x, NA0.45) and the same gain (1.0 x), and were then exported using ZEN Microscopy Software.

Caspase cleavage analysis

To determine caspase cleavage of LcRIPK1, LcRIPK3, and LcMLKL, recombinant LcCASP1/2/3/6/7/8/9 were purified as described previously [60]. HEK293T cells were transfected separately with the plasmids expressing Myc-tagged LcRIPK1, LcMLKL, LcRIPK3, or the mutants of these proteins. After 24 h transfection, the cells were collected by centrifugation and lysed with Cell lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). After centrifugation at 15,000 g for 5 min, the cleared supernatants were incubated with 2U of LcCASP1/2/3/6/7/8/9 at 26 °C in the reaction buffer (50 mM HEPES [pH 7.5], 3 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 0.005% [v/v] Tween 20, and 10 mM DTT) for 1–2 h. One unit (U) of caspase activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to cleave one nmol ρNA-based substrate per hour at 26 ℃. Caspase cleavages were analyzed by immunoblotting as described above.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay

Approximately 40,000 HEK293T cells per well were seeded in a 96‑well plate for each experimental condition. LDH release from HEK293T cells was determined using the CytoTox96® LDH release kit (Promega, Leiden, Netherlands) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Co-IP was performed as described previously [61]. Briefly, cells cultured in 6-well plates were transfected with the specified plasmids and incubated for 24 h. The cells then were lysed, and the cell lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-Flag or anti-Myc affinity gels (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The gels were subsequently washed ten times with PBS, and the proteins bound to the affinity gels were analyzed by immunoblotting.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with the GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (www.graphpad.com/) software. One‑way ANOVA was used for between‑group comparisons after confirming its assumptions: normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, homogeneity of variances was evaluated using the Brown–Forsythe test. Tukey’s HSD test was applied for multiple comparisons. Data are shown as the means ± SD, and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 and indicated with asterisks (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; *** P < 0.001).

Results

Identification and characterization of L. crocea RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL

LcRIPK1, LcRIPK3, and LcMLKL were identified from large yellow croaker L. crocea. LcRIPK1 and LcRIPK3 contain 681 and 507 amino acid residues, respectively, and exhibit an N-terminal protein kinase C (PKC) domain and a RIP homotypic interaction motif (RHIM) (Fig. 1A and B). LcRIPK1 has an additional death domain (DD) at its C-terminal region (Fig. 1A, B). LcMLKL contains 467 amino acid residues and exhibits an N-terminal four-helix bundle (4HB) domain and a C-terminal pseudokinase domain (PsKD) (Fig. 1C). When ectopically expressed in HEK293T cells, none of these proteins induced significant release of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) or rendered the cells susceptible to Sytox Green staining (Fig. 1D, E). In contrast, expression of a truncated LcMLKL containing the 4HB domain alone (LcMLKL-4HB) caused necroptotic cell death, characterized by massive LDH release, positive Sytox Green staining, and evident membrane ballooning (Fig. 1F, G). These results suggested LcMLKL being an executor of necroptosis.

LcMLKL activation requires LcRIPK1/3 and phosphorylation at specific residues

To determine the activation mechanism of LcMLKL, Co-IP was first performed to examine potential interactions among LcRIPK3, LcMLKL, and LcRIPK1. The results showed that LcRIPK3 could not only bind LcMLKL and LcRIPK1 separately, but also bind LcMLKL and LcRIPK1 concurrently, implying the formation of a ternary complex (Fig. 2A-C). Co-expression of LcMLKL with LcRIPK1 did not elicit cell death, whereas co-expression of LcMLKL with LcRIPK3 triggered robust necroptosis, accompanied by a significant increase in LDH release (Fig. 2D, E). It is known that MLKL activation requires phosphorylation at specific residues. Sequence alignment of LcMLKL with human MLKL (HsMLKL) and flounder P. olivaceus MLKL (PoMLKL) revealed two putative phosphorylation sites in LcMLKL, i.e., T350 and S351, that align with the conserved phosphorylation residues in HsMLKL (T357 and S358) and PoMLKL (T360 and S361) (Fig. 3A). To investigate the functional importance of T350 and S351, three LcMLKL mutants with T350A, S351A, or T350A plus S351A (T350A/S351A) mutation were each created. When the mutants were each co-expressed with LcRIPK3 in HEK293T cells, only the T350A/S351A mutant lost the ability to induce necroptosis (Fig. 3B, C). To further examine the importance of T350 and S351 phosphorylation, three LcMLKL variants with the phosphomimetic mutation of T350D, S351D, or T350D plus S351D (T350D/S351D) were each created. Compared with LcMLKL, both the T350D and, especially, the S351D mutants caused significant LDH release and marked necroptosis (Fig. 3D, E). Moreover, the T350D/S351D mutant triggered an even stronger necroptotic response than that triggered by the S351D mutant (Fig. 3D, E).

LcCASP3/6 cleave LcRIPK1 and disable LcRIPK1 to interact with LcRIPK3

In mammals, CASP8 inhibits necroptosis by cleaving RIPK1 and RIPK3. To investigate whether caspase-mediated regulation of RIPK existed in fish, we screened seven L. crocea caspases (LcCASP1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 8, and 9) for their ability to cleave LcRIPK1. The results showed that only LcCASP3 and LcCASP6 cleaved LcRIPK1, and the degree of these cleavages increased with the dose of the enzymes (Fig. 4A-C). Based on the molecular weights of the cleaved fragments and the conserved mammalian caspase cleavage motif (p4-xxxD-p1), five residues (D353, D356, D369, D384, and D402) were selected for mutational analysis to ascertain the cleavage sites. Alanine substitution was performed with each of these five residues, and the resulting five LcRIPK1 mutants were then treated with LcCASP3. Compared with the wild type, only the D369A mutant exhibited an altered cleavage pattern: it produced two N-terminal fragments that were both larger than the single fragment produced by the wild type (Fig. 4D). This result suggested that LcCASP3 preferentially cleaved at 366VEVD369 and could also cleave at least two downstream sites. To identify these secondary sites, LcRIPK1 mutants with D369A plus D402A (D369A/D402A), D369A plus D423A (D369A/D423A), and D369A plus D402A plus D423A (D369A/D402A/D423A) mutations were each created. Both the D369A/D402A and the D369A/D423A mutants were still cleaved by LcCASP3, but producing only one N‑terminal fragment. In contrast, the triple mutant was completely resistant to LcCASP3 (Fig. 4E). These results indicated that, in the absence of 366VEVD369, LcCASP3 cleaved with no apparent preference at two secondary sites, 399NQPD402 and 420CTTD423. When treated with LcCASP6, the D369A mutant produced a NT fragment larger than that produced by the wild type, while the D369A/D423A mutant was completely resistant to cleavage (Fig. 4F, G), suggesting that LcCASP6 cleaved LcRIPK1 preferentially at 366VEVD369 and, when this primary site is unavailable, secondarily at 420CTTD423. For convenience, the 369-, 402-, and 423-residue NT fragments generated by LcCASP3/6 cleavage were designated LcRIPK1-NT369, -NT402, and -NT423, respectively (Fig. 4H). To examine whether LcCASP3/6-mediated cleavage affected LcRIPK1−LcRIPK3 interaction, Co-IP assays were performed. The results showed that LcRIPK1-NT369 was incapable of binding to LcRIPK3 (Fig. 4I).

LcCASP1/3/6 cleave LcRIPK3 and impair LcRIPK3 interaction with LcRIPK1

The susceptibility of LcRIPK3 to LcCASPs cleavage was determined in a manner similar as above. The results showed that LcRIPK3 was specifically cleaved by LcCASP1, 3, and 6 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5A, B). Based on the sizes of the cleaved fragments and the conserved caspase cleavage motif (xxxD), the D378 and D380 residues were selected as candidate cleavage sites. To ascertain whether these sites were indeed recognized by LcCASP1/3/6, two LcRIPK3 mutants bearing D378A and D380A substitutions, respectively, were generated. Proteolytic analysis revealed that the D380A mutant, but not the D378A mutant, exhibited complete resistance to LcCASP1, 3, and 6 (Fig. 5C), indicating that the three caspases cleaved LcRIPK3 by recognizing the same 377CDVD380 motif. LcCASP1/3/6 cleavage produced the same 380-residue NT fragment, which was designated LcRIPK3-NT380 (Fig. 5D). Co-IP assays showed that compared with LcRIPK3, LcRIPK3-NT380 exhibited markedly reduced ability to interact with LcRIPK1 (Fig. 5E).

LcCASP1/3/7 cleave LcMLKL and activate necroptosis.

In addition to LcRIPK1/3, LcMLKL was also examined for the potential regulation by LcCASPs. The results showed that LcCASP1/3/7 specifically cleaved LcMLKL in a manner that was dependent on the dose of the caspases (Fig. 6A, B). To ascertain the cleavage sites in LcMLKL, two LcMLKL mutants (D133A and D136A) were generated. Proteolytic analysis revealed that both mutants were completely resistant to LcCASP1, 3, and 7, implying that all of the three caspases cleaved LcMLKL at 133DAVD136 (Fig. 6C, D). For convenience, the 136-residue NT fragment produced by LcCASP1/3/7 cleavage was named LcMLKL-NT136. When expressed in HEK293T cells, LcMLKL-NT136 triggered marked necroptosis, characterized by significant LDH release, cell swelling, and positive Sytox Green staining (Fig. 6E, F).

Caspase cleavage sites are well conserved in RIPK1/3 and MLKL in vertebrates

To examine the potential existence of caspase-mediated regulation of necroptosis in other fish, we analyzed the sequences of RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL for conserved caspase recognition motifs across 30 teleost species. The results showed that the standard caspase cleavage site (p4-xxxD-p1) is broadly present in RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL orthologs, in particular MLKL, in which the DxxD motif is completely conserved in teleost species (Fig. 7A-C). For RIPK1/3, the P4 residue varies among species: RIPK1 typically features IxxD or LxxD, while RIPK3 commonly exhibits CxxD. The analysis in fish was then extended to other vertebrate classes known to possess RIPK1/3 and MLKL, i.e., Mammalia, Aves, and Amphibia. A consistent LxxD motif was found in the RIPK1 of all these classes (Fig. 7D). Since RIPK3 appears to be lost in Aves [62], the search for caspase cleavage site in RIPK3 was performed with Mammalia and Amphibia. The result showed that, unlike that observed in teleost RIPK3, the P4 residue is strictly conserved in the RIPK3 of mammals (TxxD) and amphibians (NxxD) (Fig. 7E). For MLKL, the DxxD motif, which is universally present in teleosts as shown above, is completely conserved in mammals and birds, while the NxxD motif is highly conserved in amphibians (Fig. 7F).

Discussion

In teleosts, the activation and execution of necroptosis have been reported only in Japanese flounder P. olivaceus, in which, MLKL phosphorylation by RIPK1/3 initiated the necroptotic pathway [49]. The broader landscape of necroptotic signaling in teleost remains largely unexplored. In this study, we identified LcRIPK1, LcRIPK3, and LcMLKL in large yellow croaker L. crocea, and found that LcMLKL was activated by LcRIPK3. Similar observations have been reported in mammals, where RIPK3 alone can activate MLKL under certain conditions (e.g., upon ZBP-1‑mediated activation). Nevertheless, the canonical mode of MLKL activation in mammals is mediated by the necrosome complex consisting of RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL, in which RIPK1 serves as an upstream activator of RIPK3 [10, 63, 64]. Likewise, in our study, LcRIPK3 interaction with LcRIPK1 was observed, and the recruitment of MLKL to form the LcRIPK1−LcRIPK3−LcMLKL triplex was detected, suggesting that, analogous to mammalian MLKL, LcMLKL was activated by the LcRIPK1/3 complex [11]. In humans, RIPK3 activates MLKL by phosphorylating residues T357 and S358 [65]. Similarly, in flounder P. olivaceus, RIPK3 phosphorylates MLKL at T360/S361 to trigger necroptosis [49]. In large yellow croaker L. crocea, we found that the corresponding phosphorylation residues (T350 and S351) were conserved in LcMLKL. Mutation of these sites abolished the ability of LcMLKL to be activated by LcRIPK3 and consequently disabled LcMLKL-mediated necroptosis. These results suggest that, like mammalian and flounder P. olivaceus MLKL, LcMLKL may be activated via phosphorylation by LcRIPK3 at the conserved Thr and Ser residues. In support of this conclusion, phosphomimetic mutations of T350D and S351D were found to confer constitutive activity on LcMLKL. Taken together, these findings suggest that RIPK3-mediated phosphorylation for MLKL activation is possibly a conserved mechanism in teleosts.

In mammals, necroptosis is regulated by CASP8 to maintain the balance among cell death modalities [66]. Human CASP8 inhibits necroptosis by proteolytically cleaving RIPK1 and RIPK3 at 321LQLD324 and 325TEMD328, respectively, thereby allowing the cells to undergo apoptosis [36, 67]. Whether CASP8 or other caspases exert a similar regulatory function in non-mammalian vertebrates, such as teleosts, remains unknown. In this study, we found that LcCASP8 failed to cleave RIPK1/3. Instead, LcRIPK1 was cleaved by LcCASP3/6, predominately at 366VEVD369, to yield LcRIPK1-NT369 that lacked the RHIM domain. Likewise, LcRIPK3 was cleaved by LcCASP1/3/6 at a specific site, 377CDVD380, to produce a truncated protein without the RHIM domain. As a result, the cleaved LcRIPK1 and LcRIPK3 lost the ability to partner with LcRIPK3 and LcRIPK1, respectively, thus preventing necroptosis via the RIPK1/3−MLKL pathway. These results suggest that the RHIM-mediated RIPK1−RIPK3 interaction is likely conserved from teleost to mammals. The primary caspase cleavage sites in LcRIPK1 and LcRIPK3, i.e., VEVD and CDVD, respectively, differ at the P4 position from the CASP8 cleavage motifs in human RIPK1 and RIPK3, which are LQLD and TMED, respectively [36, 68]. This difference explains the observation that it was LcCASP1/3/6, rather than LcCASP8, that executed the cleavage of LcRIPK1 and LcRIPK3. It is interesting that, in addition to cleaving at the primary sites, LcCASP3/6 also cleaved LcRIPK1 at two other sites. Since both of these additional cleavages remove the RHIM domain, the two truncated LcRIPK1 thus generated are probably functionally disabled in the same manner as that of LcRIPK1-NT369. These redundant cleavages may represent a strategy to ensure inactivation of RIPK1 and blockage of RIPK1-mediated responses.

A survey of the classes of Vertebrata known to possess RIPK1/3 and/or MLKL sequences showed that the canonical caspase cleavage site was broadly conserved in vertebrate RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL. In mammals, birds, and amphibians, the caspase cleavage site in RIPK1 is highly conserved as an LxxD motif. Given that human RIPK1 is cleaved by CASP8 at the LQLD motif [68], this conservation suggests a CASP8-mediated regulatory mechanism across these vertebrate groups. The caspase cleavage site in RIPK3 was also conserved, but with lineage-specific characteristics: TxxD in mammals and NxxD in amphibians. This distinction at the P4 position suggests the involvement of different executing caspases in mammals and amphibians. In teleosts, the P4 residue in the p4-xxxD-p1 motif of both RIPK1 and RIPK3 varies considerably in different species, which raises the possibility that the RIPK1/3 cleavage in fish may be executed by diverse caspase members.

A previous report showed that in human multiple myeloma cells, MLKL was cleaved by caspases at Asp140 [44]. Whether this event impacted MLKL activity remained unresolved. In this study, we found that LcMLKL was cleaved by LcCASP1/3/7 at the 133DAVD136 motif, yielding an N-terminal fragment (LcMLKL-NT136) with an intact 4HB domain. LcMLKL-NT136 was found to be constitutively active and induce necroptotic cell death without the need of necrosome. This result, on the one hand, is consistent with the established role of the 4HB domain in triggering oligomerization and cell death [23], and on the other hand, indicates an alternative pathway for MLKL activation in fish that bypasses the requirement for RIPK1/RIPK3-mediated phosphorylation. In line with this result, the same LcMLKL-activating caspases, i.e., LcCASP1/3, also cleaved and inactivated LcRIPK1/3. This may be a mechanism to prevent the unnecessary RIPK1/3 action on LcMLKL, or a protective mechanism for necroptosis induction in the case where the activities of RIPK1/3, which are known to be involved in processes other than necroptosis, have to be blocked. With respect to the working mechanism of LcMLKL-NT136, we at this stage do not know whether it acts like the canonical MLKL, i.e., inducing cell death by membrane translocation and pore formation. In humans, the key structural elements and amino acid residues responsible for MLKL-mediated membrane binding and perforation are located within the 4HB domain [17, 69–72]. Since LcMLKL-NT136 retains an intact 4HB domain, it may possibly induce necroptosis via a mechanism similar to that of canonical MLKL. Future studies are needed to examine this hypothesis. It is worth noting that a highly conserved DxxD cleavage motif is present in the MLKL orthologues across mammals, birds, and teleosts, suggesting the existence of caspase-mediated MLKL activation in these vertebrate groups. Since the DxxD motif is the canonical substrate for CASP3/7, CASP3/7 may be the actual necroptosis driver in these cases. This is supported by the evidence showing CASP3 cleavage of human MLKL at the DQQD site under certain contexts [44]. In contrast, the cleavage motif in amphibian MLKL is NxxD, suggesting a possible difference in the executing caspase.

It should be noted that all functional validation experiments in this study were performed with the human HEK293T cell line, which is widely used for functional studies of exogenous proteins due to its high transfection efficiency and robust protein expression capacity. Nevertheless, this choice carries clear limitations. Species specific factors (such as specific proteins and chemical compounds) and cellular processes (such as distinct post-translational modifications) may alter the patterns of protein-protein interaction and cleavage specificity. Future studies should aim to replicate key experiments using teleost-derived cell lines or in vivo knockin/knockout fish models, with approaches such as pharmacological inhibition, biomarker detection, and genetic rescue, to assess the physiological relevance and generalizability of the present findings.

Conclusions

This study elucidates the activation and regulation mechanism of MLKL-mediated necroptosis in teleosts using large yellow croaker as a representative (Fig. 8). MLKL can be activated via either the classical RIPK1/3−MLKL signaling pathway, or the alternative CASPs−MLKL pathway. In the latter case, CASPs concurrently inactivate RIPK1/3 and activate MLKL by liberating the necroptotic 4HB domain. The bioinformatic analyses and experimental data from L. crocea provide a strong hypothesis for a conserved CASPs−MLKL pathway that now warrants experimental validation in other vertebrate lineages. These findings shed new light on the diversity and complexity of necroptosis regulation in vertebrates.
