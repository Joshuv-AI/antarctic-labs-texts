Gut-brain axis modulation by fecal microbiota transplantation improves dual-organ injury after cerebral ischemia-reperfusion via Caspase-8 dependent inhibition of necroptosis
Authors: Zhangfeng Shen, Dongdong Xu, Ke Wang, Yuhao Chen, Qian Dou, Shengyu Jin, Bing Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe pathological features of cerebral ischemia-reperfusion (CIR) include necroptosis activation. This study investigated how healthy fecal microbiota transplantation (H-FMT) improves CIR and intestinal barrier damage. Rats subjected to middle cerebral artery occlusion and reperfusion (MCAO/R) were treated with H-FMT and/or a Cysteine-aspartic acid protease-8 (Caspase-8) inhibitor. Survival and body weight were monitored throughout the experiment. Neurological function, tissue damage, inflammatory cytokines, and Caspase-8/Receptor-interacting protein kinase 1 (RIPK1)-Receptor-interacting protein kinase 3 (RIPK3)-Mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL) expression were assessed. Ultrastructural changes were examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), p-RIPK1/p-RIPK3 expression by immunohistochemistry (IHC), and gut microbiota by 16 S sequencing. H-FMT significantly ameliorated neurological deficits and intestinal barrier disruption, reduced infarct volume and neuronal loss, and attenuated mitochondrial damage. These effects were accompanied by reduced apoptosis and inflammation, increased Caspase-8 activation, and suppressed RIPK1-RIPK3-MLKL phosphorylation. IHC confirmed reduced p-RIPK1/p-RIPK3 signals after H-FMT. 16 S sequencing revealed that H-FMT restored microbial diversity, reduced pathogenic Proteobacteria, and enriched beneficial Lactobacillus, which positively correlated with Caspase-8 activation. Our findings suggest that H-FMT alleviates CIR injury by activating Caspase-8 and suppressing necroptosis. Introduction

Although recanalization therapies like thrombolysis and endovascular intervention have improved clinical outcomes in ischemic stroke (cerebral infarction) (Wu et al. 2020), the subsequent cerebral ischemia-reperfusion (CIR) injury often leads to secondary brain damage (He et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2023; Zeng et al. 2022). CIR represents a complex cascade of events—including energy dysregulation, excitotoxicity, calcium overload, and oxidative stress—that interact synergistically to trigger cell death (Gong et al. 2017). Given the incomplete understanding of CIR pathogenesis and the scarcity of effective treatments, unraveling its mechanisms and identifying new therapeutic avenues remain pressing challenges.

Necroptosis, a programmed form of necrosis, is a major cell death mode in ischemic injury, regulated by membrane receptors and intracellular signaling (Li et al. 2023). Upon activation of death receptors like TNFR or Toll-like receptors, key adaptor proteins (FADD, TRADD, TRIF) engage Receptor-interacting protein kinase 1 (RIPK1) and Cysteine-dependent aspartate-specific protease-8/10 (Caspase-8/10). This leads to assembly of the RIPK1-Receptor-interacting protein kinase3 (RIPK3)-Mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL) complex (necrosome), which drives plasma membrane rupture and necroptosis via ion influx and cellular swelling (Yawoot et al. 2023). Inhibition of necroptosis (e.g., by Necrostatin-1) alleviates blood-brain barrier disruption in CIR models, partly by blocking microglia-derived TNF-α–mediated endothelial necroptosis (Yu et al. 2023). RIPK3 and MLKL also participate in neuroimmune crosstalk (Bertheloot et al. 2021), and necroptosis coincides with apoptosis and pyroptosis in ischemic models (Yan et al. 2022). Notably, necroptosis inhibition reduces neuronal apoptosis, indicating pathway interplay in CIR (Chen et al. 2019). Caspase-8 plays a dual role: initiating apoptosis and suppressing RIPK3-MLKL–dependent necroptosis (Li et al. 2022); its inhibition triggers necroptosis (Oliva-Martin et al. 2016). We hypothesize that Caspase-8 activation may protect against CIR by blocking necrosome assembly, though underlying mechanisms remain unclear.

The advancement of microbiome technologies has expanded the brain-gut axis to include microbiota (Mayer et al. 2022). Under homeostasis, gut-brain immune signaling is blocked by intestinal and blood-brain barriers (Pellegrini et al. 2023), but dysbiosis can trigger central nervous system disorders. For example, young microbiota promotes protective cytokine production in ischemic mice (Mou et al. 2022), while fecal transplant from ischemic mice disrupts brain connectivity in germ-free recipients (Spychala et al. 2018). Probiotics also alleviate necroptosis in stressed mice (Wang et al. 2021a), and pathogens like Salmonella inhibit Caspase-8 to promote necroptosis (Dojo Soeandy et al. 2021). Although healthy microbiota colonization mitigates ischemia-reperfusion injury (Xu et al. 2025), the mechanisms remain unclear.

Although several studies have shown that fecal microbiota transplantation protects against cerebral ischemia and that necroptosis participates in this process, none of them have asked whether Caspase-8 acts as a link between gut microbiota and injury in both brain and intestine at the same time. In this study, we show for the first time that healthy fecal microbiota transplantation (H-FMT) can reverse the worsening effect caused by Caspase-8 inhibition. We also found that changes in the gut microbiota-especially an increase in Lactobacillus-are associated with Caspase-8 activation. This connection has not been reported before. Currently, the mechanism by which a healthy gut microbiota alleviates CIR injury via Caspase-8-mediated regulation of necroptosis remains poorly understood. Therefore, this study investigated how H-FMT improves CIR and intestinal barrier damage, combining in vivo modeling with 16 S rDNA sequencing to elucidate the underlying molecular pathways, thus providing a basis for microbiota-targeted therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Animals

A total of 48 male Sprague-Dawley rats (Specific Pathogen-Free [SPF] grade; 230–250 g) were purchased from Shanghai SLAKE Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (License No.: SCXK(Shanghai) 2022-0004) and housed in the barrier facility of Hunter Biolnsight Co., Ltd. (License No.: SYXK(Zhejiang) 2024-0003).

The animals were maintained under standard conditions with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, an ambient temperature of 22–24 °C, and relative humidity of 40%−60%. They were provided with a standard laboratory diet and water ad libitum, with feed replenished daily. The ventilation system ensured 15–20 air changes per hour.

Antibiotic-cleared rat construction

After a one-week acclimation, rats received a daily oral gavage of an antibiotic cocktail (vancomycin, 50 mg/kg, V820413; neomycin sulfate, 100 mg/kg, N814740; metronidazole, 100 mg/kg, HY-B0318; ampicillin, 100 mg/kg, A800200) for 10 days to ablate gut microbiota and create a pseudo-germ-free state, following an established protocol (Zhang et al. 2024). All antibiotics were procured from Shanghai Maclin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., except for metronidazole, which was purchased from Shanghai Haoyuan Bio-Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd.

Construction of middle cerebral artery occlusion and reperfusion (MCAO/R) rat models

Following the establishment of the pseudo-germ-free state, rats were anesthetized (1.5–2% isoflurane) and subjected to transient MCAO. For specific procedures, refer to the research published by (Xu et al. 2023). Briefly, a neck incision was made to expose the right carotid arteries. A 4 − 0 nylon suture was advanced from the external carotid artery (ECA) into the internal carotid artery (ICA) to a depth of 17–20 mm, occluding the MCA for 90 min at room temperature. Reperfusion commenced upon suture withdrawal. The wound was then sutured and disinfected. Sham controls underwent the same surgical exposure without arterial occlusion or reperfusion.

Sterile fecal suspension preparation

Fecal microbiota suspension was prepared from three healthy SPF male SD rats for subsequent transplantation. Briefly, fresh feces were diluted 1:5 (w/v) in sterile PBS and filtered through gauze. After sequential centrifugation (100 × g for 5 min, then 6,500 × g for 5 min), the pellet was resuspended in 20% glycerol-PBS at 0.5 g/mL, following a published protocol (Wang et al. 2021a). The aliquots were stored at −80 °C.

Drug intervention and grouping

Forty-five rats were allocated into five groups (n = 20): Sham, MCAO/R, MCAO/R + H-FMT, MCAO/R + Z-IETD-FMK, and MCAO/R + Z-IETD-FMK + H-FMT. Animals were randomly assigned to groups based on body weight to ensure baseline comparability. All interventions began 24 h post-modeling and continued for 14 days. The Sham and MCAO/R groups received 1 mL sterile PBS enemas daily. The MCAO/R + H-FMT group received 1 mL healthy fecal supernatant enemas daily. The MCAO/R + Z-IETD-FMK group received daily Z-IETD-FMK (Caspase-8 inhibitor, 1.5 mg (Lentini et al. 2023), i.v., in 0.4% DMSO-aCSF; 218773; Sigma) plus 1 mL sterile PBS enemas. The MCAO/R + Z-IETD-FMK + H-FMT group received daily Z-IETD-FMK (1.5 mg, i.v.) plus 1 mL H-FMT enemas. During the experiment, the survival status of the rats in each group was observed and recorded daily. Body weight was measured at baseline (Day 1), on postoperative Day 1 (Day 11), Day 7 (Day 18), and Day 14 (Day 25).

Sample collection

Brain and colon tissues were collected and processed according to standard protocols. For histological assessment, fresh brain tissue was frozen for Nissl staining to preserve neuronal architecture; paraffin-embedded sections of brain and colon tissue were used for TUNEL and immunofluorescence staining to assess apoptosis and necrotic apoptosis. For protein analysis, tissue homogenates were collected for ELISA assays (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α) and Western blot analysis (Caspase-8, RIPK1, p-RIPK1, RIPK3, p-RIPK3, MLKL, p-MLKL, IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α). For ultrastructural observation, small samples of cerebral cortex tissue were fixed and examined using transmission electron microscopy. For immunohistochemical analysis, additional paraffin-embedded brain sections were used to detect the expression of p-RIPK1 and p-RIPK3. Furthermore, colonic contents were collected from rats in the sham-operated group, the MCAO/R group and the MCAO/R + H-FMT group; these were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent 16 S rDNA sequencing to analyse the composition of the gut microbiota.

Longa neurological score

Neurological function was assessed using the Longa score in rats at baseline (prior to any surgery), during the acute phase of cerebral ischemia-reperfusion (within 24 h post-surgery) and the recovery phase (following completion of gut microbiota intervention). The Longa scoring criteria are referenced in Table S1.

FITC-dextran 4 detection

Following an overnight fast, rats were administered FITC-dextran 4 (50 mg/mL in saline; Y262484; Beyotime) by oral gavage at 10 µL/g body weight. Blood was collected from the orbital sinus 3 h post-administration, and serum fluorescence (Ex/Em: 490/525 nm) was measured to quantify intestinal permeability.

TTC staining

Following perfusion and weighing, brains were sliced into six 1–2 mm thick coronal sections. The slices were stained with 1% TTC (A610558-0025; Sangon Biotech) at 37 °C for 15 min, fixed with 4% PFA, and then digitally imaged. The red (viable) and white (infarcted) areas were measured using image analysis software. The percentage infarct area was calculated as (Total Infarct Area/Total Brain Area) × 100%.

Nissl staining

Fresh brain tissues were processed for conventional Nissl staining. Hydrated sections were stained with 1% toluidine blue (6586/4/5; Hefei Qiansheng Biological Co., Ltd.) at 50 °C, followed by dehydration, clearing in xylene (10023418; Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.), and mounting with neutral resin (10004160; Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.). The number of viable neurons in the hippocampal CA1 region was quantified in a blinded manner according to the following protocol. For each animal, three non-overlapping, consecutive coronal sections encompassing the dorsal hippocampus were selected. Under a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ci-L) with a 40× objective lens, three fields of view (approximately 0.1 mm² each) within the CA1 stratum pyramidale were randomly selected per section. A neuron was considered viable and counted if it met all the following criteria: (1) exhibited a clear, round, or oval cell body with a distinct nucleus; (2) showed abundant purple-blue Nissl substance in the cytoplasm; and (3) lacked signs of pyknosis (shrunken, dark nucleus) or karyorrhexis (nuclear fragmentation). The counts from the three fields (3 sections × 1 fields) per animal were averaged to represent the neuronal density for that subject. Data are presented as the mean number of viable neurons per field for each experimental group.

Apoptosis level assessment

Apoptosis in brain and colon tissues was evaluated using TUNEL staining and immunofluorescence for phosphorylated Mixed Lineage Kinase domain-Like protein (p-MLKL).

TUNEL staining: Deparaffinized tissue sections were subjected to antigen retrieval by incubation with Proteinase K working solution (B030; Anxi Xinle) for 30 min at 37 °C. After permeabilization for 20 min, the sections were stained using the TUNEL Apoptosis Detection Kit (C1090; Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunofluorescence staining: For p-MLKL detection, sections underwent routine hydration followed by antigen retrieval with 3% H₂O₂ (73113760; Sinopharm Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd.). After blocking with BSA (B2064; Sigma), the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a rabbit anti-p-MLKL primary antibody (AF7419; Affinity; RRID: AB_2843859) diluted 1:200. The following day, the sections were treated with a secondary antibody (ab150079; Abcam; RRID: AB_2722623) diluted 1:500. Finally, the sections were mounted with an antifade mounting medium containing DAPI (ab104139; Abcam) to counterstain nuclei.

All stained sections were visualized, and images were captured using a Nikon Eclipse Ci-L fluorescence microscope. Image acquisition and quantification were performed in a blinded manner. For each animal, three non-consecutive sections were analyzed. In the brain, images were captured from one random field within the cortical penumbra per section; in the colon, images were taken from three fields covering the crypt mucosa per section. Imaging used a Nikon Eclipse Ci-L microscope with a 20× objective and fixed exposure times. Using ImageJ, TUNEL-positive (red nuclear) and p-MLKL-positive (green cytoplasmic/membranous) cells were counted, and normalized to the total DAPI-positive nuclei in the same field. Data are presented as the mean percentage of positive cells per animal, derived from three fields (3 sections × 1 fields).

ELISA

Tissue samples (brain and colon) were homogenized in ice-cold PBS after rinsing and mincing. The homogenates were centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 5–10 min, and the supernatants were collected. Cytokine levels (IL-6 [RX2D302196], IL-1β [RX302869R], and TNF-α [RX302869R]) were measured using specific ELISA kits (Ruixin Biotechnology) following the manufacturer’s protocol and established methods (Gong et al. 2023) and (Jia et al. 2023).

WB assay

Total protein was extracted from the brain tissue using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (P6730, P1260; Solarbio). The homogenates were centrifuged to remove debris. The protein concentration of the supernatant was determined with a BCA protein assay kit (P0012S; Beyotime), and all samples were normalized to an equal concentration with lysis buffer. Subsequently, the proteins were denatured. glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as an internal control to ensure equal protein loading across samples.

Protein was separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (10600023; GE Healthcare) via the wet transfer method. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and then incubated overnight with specific primary antibodies. After washing, the membranes were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescent substrate (PE0010 SolarBio), and the signal intensity was quantified with ImageJ software. The primary and secondary antibodies used are listed in Table S2.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Brain tissue was fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (111-30-8; Alfa Aesar) for 2–4 h and 1% osmium tetroxide for 1–2 h, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100092683; Sinopharm Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd.) and acetone (67-64-1; Shanghai Lingfeng), embedded in resin, and polymerized overnight at 70 °C. Ultrathin Sects. (50–70 nm) were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate (MD911411; MDL) and examined under a transmission electron microscope (H7650; Hitachi) at 2,500 − 30,000× magnification.

Immunohistochemical staining

Paraffin-embedded brain tissue sections were deparaffinized with xylene (10023418; Sinopharm), rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100092683; Sinopharm), and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval in Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0; 648310; E9884; Sigma). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with H₂O₂ (73113760; Sinopharm), followed by blocking with BSA (B2064; Sigma). Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against phosphorylated RIPK1 (p-RIPK1) (PAE640Ra01; Wuhan Yunklon; 1:1000) and phosphorylated RIPK3 (p-RIPK3) (AF3894; Affinity; 1:150), then with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (ab97080; Abcam; 1:5000) for 30 min at 37 °C. Immunoreactivity was visualized using DAB, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Stained sections were examined under a light microscope.

16S rDNA microbial diversity analysis

After extracting fecal DNA (HiPure Stool DNA Kit), the 16 S rDNA gene V3-V4 region was amplified (primers 338 F/806R) and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform. The resulting sequences were quality-filtered, and OTUs were clustered at 97% similarity using VSEARCH (v1.9.6) and taxonomically classified using the SILVA 138 database. Alpha diversity (Chao1, ACE, Shannon) and beta diversity (PCoA based on Bray-Curtis distance, ANOSIM) were analyzed. Functional predictions and differential taxa at the genus level were identified via LEfSe (LDA score > 3.0, p < 0.05).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD and were analyzed with SPSS 20.0. For normally distributed data, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test (equal variance) or Dunnett’s T3 test (unequal variance) was used for group comparisons. The non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis H test was applied otherwise. Body weight data were analyzed by two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (group × time) with Bonferroni’s post-hoc test, and survival data were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method with log-rank test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Effect sizes for key pairwise comparisons were calculated using Cohen’s d, defined as (M₁ − M₂)/SDpooled, where SDpooled = . According to Cohen’s conventions, d = 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 were considered small, medium, and large effect sizes, respectively. Given the limited sample size (n = 3 per group for tissue-based assays), all primary outcome data were also analyzed using the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis H test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test. A difference was considered statistically significant only when both parametric (ANOVA) and non-parametric tests yielded p < 0.05.

Results

H-FMT reversed brain and intestinal barrier damage exacerbated by Caspase-8 inhibition after MCAO/R

In a rat model of MCAO/R, we evaluated the effects of a Caspase-8 inhibitor (Z-IETD-FMK) and H-FMT on cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury. The experimental procedure is detailed in Fig. 1A. As shown in Fig. 1B, all rats in the Sham group survived during the 14-day experimental period (survival rate: 100%). The survival rate in the MCAO/R group was 60%, which increased to 75% following H-FMT treatment. In contrast, the survival rate was lowest in the Caspase-8 inhibitor Z-IETD-FMK group (50%), but rose to 70% following H-FMT treatment. Changes in body weight are shown in Fig. 1C. At baseline (Day 1), there were no significant differences in body weight among the groups (p > 0.05). On Day 11, body weights in all model groups were significantly lower than those in the Sham group (p < 0.01). The H-FMT treatment group had significantly higher body weights than the MCAO/R group on Days 18 and 25 (p < 0.05), indicating that H-FMT effectively mitigates weight loss caused by cerebral ischemia. In contrast, the Z-IETD-FMK group exhibited the most pronounced weight loss, while body weight improved following the combination of H-FMT and Z-IETD-FMK. A two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance revealed that the treatment factor (p < 0.01), the time factor (p < 0.001), and the interaction between the two (p < 0.05) were all statistically significant. All animals were healthy and showed no signs of neurological impairment (Longa score = 0; Fig. 1D) prior to MCAO/R surgeries. And, neurological deficits, quantified by Longa scores, were significantly increased 24 h post-MCAO/R compared to the sham group (p < 0.01; Fig. 1E). H-FMT monotherapy effectively ameliorated these deficits (p < 0.01). In contrast, treatment with the Caspase-8 inhibitor alone exacerbated neurological impairment, resulting in even higher Longa scores than the MCAO/R group. Notably, this detrimental effect was reversed by the co-administration of H-FMT (p < 0.01; Fig. 1F). In parallel, MCAO/R-induced intestinal barrier disruption, reflected by elevated serum FITC-dextran levels (p < 0.01), was significantly attenuated by H-FMT treatment (p < 0.05; Fig. 1G).

H-FMT attenuated necroptosis in brain and colon tissues exacerbated by Caspase-8 inhibition after MCAO/R

We next assessed the impact of H-FMT on apoptosis and the expression of p-MLKL, a key marker of necroptosis, in the context of MCAO/R injury. The MCAO/R group exhibited a pronounced increase in TUNEL-positive cells in the brain cortex and colon, which was significantly mitigated by H-FMT (Fig. 3A-B). Strikingly, immunofluorescence results revealed that Z-IETD-FMK alone, by inhibiting Caspase-8, further increased necroptosis (as evidenced by significantly elevated p-MLKL fluorescence expression levels). However, this effect was reversed when administered in combination with H-FMT. Similarly, the upregulation of p-MLKL expression in MCAO/R rats was suppressed by H-FMT but enhanced by Z-IETD-FMK (Fig. 3C-D). The combination of H-FMT and Z-IETD-FMK ultimately reduced p-MLKL levels compared to the inhibitor-only group, mirroring the apoptotic data. All quantitative comparisons were statistically significant (P < 0.05).

H-FMT alleviated MCAO/R-induced neuroinflammation and colitis exacerbated by Caspase-8 inhibition

As shown in Fig. 4A-B, the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) were significantly elevated in both brain and colon tissues of MCAO/R rats compared to the Sham group (p < 0.01). Notably, treatment with the Caspase-8 inhibitor Z-IETD-FMK further augmented this inflammatory response (p < 0.01). In contrast, H-FMT intervention effectively counteracted the inflammation, significantly reducing the levels of all three cytokines in the MCAO/R + H-FMT group compared to the MCAO/R group, and in the MCAO/R + Z-IETD-FMK + H-FMT group compared to the MCAO/R + Z-IETD-FMK group (all p < 0.01).

H-FMT reduced the protein expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and preserves mitochondrial ultrastructure

To further quantify inflammatory signaling at the protein level and assess subcellular injury, we performed additional Western blot and TEM analyses. Western blot results confirmed that the protein levels of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α were significantly increased in both brain and colon tissues after MCAO/R, and these increases were markedly suppressed by H-FMT treatment (all p < 0.05; Fig. S1A-D), corroborating our ELISA findings. Furthermore, TEM analysis of the cerebral cortex revealed that MCAO/R induced severe ultrastructural damage, most notably mitochondrial swelling and cristae disintegration. H-FMT intervention effectively preserved mitochondrial morphology (Fig. S1E), indicating protection at the organelle level.

H-FMT modulated the Caspase-8/RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL signaling axis in MCAO/R rats

Molecular analysis revealed that MCAO/R significantly activated the necroptosis pathway, as evidenced by elevated phosphorylation of RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL, concomitant with reduced Caspase-8 expression in brain tissue compared to the sham group (all p < 0.05; Fig. 5A-B). H-FMT intervention effectively reversed these changes, significantly suppressing the phosphorylation cascade and upregulating Caspase-8 (p < 0.01). Conversely, administration of the Caspase-8 inhibitor Z-IETD-FMK further activated necroptosis signaling (all p < 0.05). Crucially, this aggravation was rescued by H-FMT co-administration, which significantly downregulated the phosphorylation of RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL while restoring Caspase-8 levels (all p < 0.01).

H-FMT suppressed the activation of RIPK1/RIPK3 in the ischemic brain parenchyma

To spatially localize the activation of the necroptosis pathway, we performed immunohistochemical staining for p-RIPK1 and p-RIPK3 on brain sections. As shown in Fig. S2A-C, robust p-RIPK1 and p-RIPK3 immunoreactivity was observed in the peri-infarct region of the MCAO/R group, indicative of active necroptosis signaling. H-FMT treatment markedly reduced this p-RIPK1 and p-RIPK3 signal. In contrast, administration of the Caspase-8 inhibitor Z-IETD-FMK intensified p-RIPK1 and p-RIPK3 staining, an effect that was again mitigated by co-treatment with H-FMT (all p < 0.05). These in situ findings corroborate our Western blot data and visually demonstrate that H-FMT intervention dampens the activation of a key necrosome component within the vulnerable brain tissue.

H-FMT modulated gut microbial α- and β-diversity in MCAO/R rats

To identify gut microbes associated with MCAO/R, we performed 16 S rDNA sequencing on fecal samples from Sham, MCAO/R (MCAOR), and MCAO/R + H-FMT (H-FMT) groups. The analysis demonstrated that H-FMT robustly restored the gut microbial diversity and composition disrupted by MCAO/R. α-diversity results showed that MCAO/R significantly reduced microbial richness (Chao1, ACE) and diversity (Shannon), whereas H-FMT treatment significantly restored all indices (all p < 0.01; Fig. 6A-C). PCoA analysis revealed a clear separation of microbial community structures among the three groups (Sham, MCAO/R, H-FMT) along principal coordinates (PCo1 22.6%, PCo2 17.7%), indicating a significant shift in overall composition following H-FMT intervention (Fig. 6D). ANOSIM analysis confirmed a significant structural difference in the microbiota between groups, with a high R value of 0.767 and a significance of p = 0.001, thereby confirming that inter-group variation was substantial and non-random (Fig. 6E).

At the phylum level, communities were dominated by Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Actinobacteriota, and Proteobacteria. MCAO/R induced a dysbiotic state characterized by an increase in Proteobacteria and a decrease in Bacteroidota. H-FMT intervention partially restored this balance, notably reducing the abundance of Proteobacteria.

To explore the potential functional link between the altered microbiota and the central molecular mechanism, we performed correlation analyses at the individual animal level. We found that the relative abundance of Lactobacillus was significantly positively correlated with the activation level of Caspase-8 in the brain, whereas the abundance of Bacteroides was negatively correlated with Caspase-8 activation (Fig. S3). These correlations suggest that the H-FMT-induced microbial shift is associated with the upregulation of this key neuroprotective enzyme.

Discussion

Growing evidence implicates cellular necroptosis in the pathological damage following CIR (Yan et al. 2022), while the introduction of a healthy gut microbiota offers protection against this injury (Feng et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2023). Caspase-8, a key initiator of death receptor-mediated apoptosis (Li et al. 2022), may exert a pivotal role at the intersection of these processes. To test this hypothesis and explore the mechanisms involved, we utilized a MCAO/R rat model, treating the animals with a Caspase-8 inhibitor and H-FMT.

Evaluations of neurological and intestinal barrier function showed that oral H-FMT significantly counteracted MCAO/R-induced damage. In contrast, Caspase-8 inhibition post-MCAO/R worsened these injuries, confirming the aggravation of disease progression by Caspase-8 activation, which aligns with findings from (Huang et al. 2025). Crucially, the addition of H-FMT controlled and reversed the exacerbating effects of Caspase-8 inhibition. These data suggest that the ameliorative effect of H-FMT on CIR injury may operate via a mechanism linked to Caspase-8 activity. Consistent with these functional assessments, pathological examination of brain tissues (via TTC and Nissl staining) provided direct morphological evidence supporting the afore-mentioned findings.

Consistent with previous reports, MCAO/R injury is associated with elevated apoptosis (Shi et al. 2022), underscoring the therapeutic potential of its inhibition. TUNEL assays on brain and colon tissues revealed that the caspase-8 inhibitor significantly increased apoptosis, whereas H-FMT effectively suppressed it. This suggests that the ameliorative effect of H-FMT is linked to the attenuation of apoptotic cell death. To further explore the involvement of necroptosis, we assessed the expression of p-MLKL, a key marker of this pathway (Wang et al. 2021b). As anticipated, the level of p-MLKL was markedly elevated in the MCAO/R and MCAO/R + Z-IETD-FMK groups, with the latter showing more pronounced expression, confirming that caspase-8 inhibition exacerbates MCAO/R injury. Crucially, H-FMT co-administration significantly downregulated p-MLKL expression in both contexts. Collectively, these data indicate that H-FMT confers protection by inhibiting necroptosis, and that this effect may be mechanistically dependent on Caspase-8 activation.

Notably, CIR injury is known to rapidly induce a surge of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Beyond its role as a potent inflammatory mediator, TNF-α can initiate the necroptosis pathway (Chen et al. 2019). Consistent with this, our analysis of inflammatory cytokines in brain and colon tissues revealed elevated levels, corroborating the TUNEL and immunofluorescence findings which demonstrated robust activation of overall cell death in the model, effectively suppressed by H-FMT. It should be noted that TUNEL staining detects DNA fragmentation, a common terminal event in multiple modes of cell death including apoptosis and necroptosis. Therefore, the increased TUNEL signal upon Caspase-8 inhibition likely reflects an augmentation of overall cell death, potentially skewed towards necroptosis as suggested by the concurrent rise in p-MLKL. Mechanistically, upon binding to its receptor, TNF-α triggers the activation of RIPK1 and RIPK3 (Newton 2015), culminating in MLKL phosphorylation and the execution of necroptosis (Shi et al. 2023)—a cascade fully aligned with our molecular data and IHC data. Therefore, it is plausible that H-FMT alleviates MCAO/R-induced injuries by promoting Caspase-8 activity, which interrupts the necroptosis cascade.

A hallmark of MCAO/R-induced dysbiosis was the marked expansion of Proteobacteria, a phylum harboring opportunistic pathobionts, widely recognized as an indicator of gut barrier compromise, inflammation, and ecological deterioration (Cen et al. 2025; Choi et al. 2025; Fu et al. 2021). H-FMT intervention was associated with a reduction in this pathogenic expansion alongside an increased abundance of Lactobacillus, a genus often linked to enhanced intestinal barrier function through mechanisms such as mucin secretion and tight junction protein expression (Huang et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2022). At a finer taxonomic resolution, H-FMT also correlated with reduced levels of Bacteroides, a taxonomic group previously identified as a post-stroke dysbiosis marker (Zhang et al. 2021), suggesting a potential shift away from a pro-inflammatory microbial profile. LEfSe analysis further indicated that H-FMT was linked to a restructuring of gut microbiota composition. The sham group showed enrichment of taxa such as Clostridia and Prevotellaceae, which are often associated with short-chain fatty acid production and the support of barrier integrity and immune homeostasis (Hays et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2024). In contrast, the MCAO/R group displayed an increase in Gammaproteobacteria, consistent with a state of dysbiosis and inflammation. The H-FMT-associated signature, characterized by Lactobacillus and related taxa, coincided with a reduction in opportunistic pathogens and a partial restoration of microbial community balance. Furthermore, our correlation analysis revealed that the abundance of beneficial Lactobacillus was positively associated with Caspase-8 activation in the brain, while the pathobiont Bacteroides was negatively associated, providing statistical evidence for a microbiota-Caspase-8 link. Collectively, these descriptive microbiota changes are consistent with a model in which H-FMT may alleviate systemic and neuroinflammation by promoting a microbial profile associated with gut health, thereby potentially interrupting a detrimental gut–brain axis cycle and contributing to the mitigation of MCAO/R-induced brain injury.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the sample size for tissue-based assays (Western blot, histology, TEM, etc.) was n = 3 per group, and no a priori power analysis was performed before the experiments. This is a significant methodological limitation. Although non-parametric validation (Kruskal-Wallis) confirmed the main findings and effect sizes were very large, the small sample size limits statistical power and generalizability. Therefore, our conclusions should be considered hypothesis-generating and preliminary rather than definitive. Validation in larger independent cohorts with pre-specified sample size estimation is required. Second, we did not monitor cerebral blood flow (e.g., by laser Doppler) to confirm successful MCA occlusion. Although all MCAO/R animals exhibited immediate neurological deficits (Longa score ≥ 2) and consistent infarct patterns on TTC staining, the lack of direct CBF verification is a methodological limitation. Third, we did not perform immunohistochemical staining for microglial (Iba-1) or astrocytic (GFAP) activation. Instead, we quantified pro-inflammatory cytokine levels (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α) as functional readouts of neuroinflammation, which were significantly reduced by H-FMT. Future studies should include cellular characterization of inflammatory responses in the brain. Fourth, the gut microbiota analysis was performed only in the Sham, MCAO/R, and H-FMT groups. The inclusion of the caspase-8 inhibitor-treated groups would have allowed a more direct interrogation of the proposed FMT–caspase-8 link. Fifth, although different tissue processing methods were used for different histological stains (each optimized for its specific purpose), all inter-group comparisons for a given assay were performed under identical conditions, ensuring the validity of the observed differences. Beyond these methodological considerations, our findings also highlight several unresolved mechanistic questions. The protective effects of H-FMT demonstrated here remain correlative; a definitive causal relationship requires validation in future studies using, for example, gnotobiotic models or FMT in caspase-8-deficient animals. And, while our data (reduced p-RIPK1, p-RIPK3, and p-MLKL) strongly suggest an inhibition of the necroptosis pathway, we lack direct biochemical evidence for the disruption of RIPK1-RIPK3 necrosome complex formation, which would further solidify this conclusion. Furthermore, the upstream mechanisms by which H-FMT activates Caspase-8 are not defined, and our 16 S rDNA sequencing data, while revealing compositional shifts, lack functional insights into the microbial metabolites or signals involved. Additionally, the therapeutic effect was not recapitulated using a defined bacterial consortium, and the temporal dynamics of microbiota-host interactions after MCAO/R were not explored. Notwithstanding these limitations, the large and consistent effect sizes observed across multiple, independent endpoints strengthen the primary conclusions of this proof-of-concept study. It successfully lays the groundwork for the novel hypothesis that targeting the gut microbiota can protect against CIR injury via modulation of the Caspase-8/necroptosis pathway, thereby providing a firm basis for the future investigations outlined above.

While the synchronized modulation of Caspase-8 and the RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL axis strongly implicates this pathway, direct evidence for Caspase-8’s indispensable role and necrosome complex formation awaits further investigation using genetic or more direct biochemical approaches.

In summary, this hypothesis-generating study provides preliminary evidence that H-FMT alleviates CIR injury and associated intestinal barrier damage, possibly by activating Caspase-8 and suppressing necroptosis via the RIPK1-RIPK3-MLKL pathway. The protection was accompanied by beneficial gut microbiota remodeling, particularly enrichment of Lactobacillus. However, given the small sample size and lack of a priori power analysis, these findings require validation in larger independent cohorts. Nevertheless, this work links gut microbiota changes to cell death regulation and supports the gut-brain axis as a potential therapeutic target for CIR injury.
