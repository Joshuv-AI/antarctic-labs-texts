Upper mantle low-velocity layer tied to volatile-charged carbonate melts
Authors: Yongsheng Huang, Guoji Wu, Xuran Liang, Kaiwei Tao, Yu Wang, Michihiko Nakamura, Yuan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recent geophysical observations indicate the presence of small-volume, carbonate-rich melts in Earth’s deep interior. However, the mechanisms by which such minute melt fractions migrate through largely solid rocks and generate large-scale geophysical anomalies have remained unclear. Here, our high-pressure experiments demonstrate that, in the presence of H2O and NaCl, carbonate-rich melts act as super-spreaders, completely wetting and coating the surfaces of surrounding mantle minerals. This perfect wetting enables even trace amounts of melt (0.02 to 0.08 vol%) to form fully interconnected networks. Such connectivity facilitates efficient melt migration and chemical exchange, driving global material recycling between the surface and planetary interior.

INTRODUCTION

The distribution and migration of carbonate melts in the upper mantle play a critical role in regulating global material cycles and geophysical responses (1–9). Carbonates are primarily transported into the Earth’s interior through the subduction of oceanic lithosphere (10–20). The addition of carbonates markedly lowers the solidus of both mantle and slab lithologies in the upper mantle and transition zone, thereby promoting the generation of carbonate-rich melts (4, 21–24). Evidence for such melts includes carbonate melt inclusions in mantle-derived diamonds, CO2-rich petit-spot volcanism, isotopic anomalies, and numerical modeling results (19, 22, 25–34). Unlike silicate melts, carbonate melts exhibit exceptionally low viscosity, high mobility, elevated electrical conductivity, and remarkable capacities to dissolve volatiles, metallic, and incompatible elements (8, 9, 35–44). These properties make carbonate melts highly efficient metasomatic agents within the mantle (1, 2, 8). Therefore, elucidating their distribution and migration mechanisms is essential for understanding the deep carbon cycle, mantle metasomatism, and the geodynamics of the upper mantle.

The dihedral angle [θ, where 2cos(θ/2) = γss/γsf, γss and γsf denote the solid-solid grain boundary energy and the solid-fluid interfacial energy per unit area, respectively] between olivine and melt exerts a primary control on melt distribution and migration (45). When θ < 60°, the melt forms an interconnected network that enables pervasive flow, whereas θ > 60° results in melt isolation at grain boundaries/edges, forming melt pockets (46–48), particularly at low melt fractions. The critical melt fraction required to form interconnected networks increases systematically with increasing θ (49). Geophysical observations in the upper mantle—such as high electrical conductivity and low seismic velocities (50–59)—could provide crucial constraints on the presence, distribution, migration, and connectivity of carbonate melts (4, 59–64). These large-scale geophysical anomalies are fundamentally controlled by grain-scale melt geometry. Notably, Yoshino et al. (65) experimentally demonstrated complete wetting between olivine and hydrous silicate melt under upper mantle conditions. Building on this, Han et al. (59) and Xu et al. (64) interpreted seismic anomalies at the base of the upper mantle as evidence that carbonate melts with θ ≈ 0° (complete wetting) form interconnected melt networks along grain boundaries, even at extremely low melt fractions (<0.1 vol %). However, this interpretation contrasts sharply with extensive experimental data showing consistently higher θ values (25° to 30°) for various olivine-carbonate melt systems under mantle conditions: 28° for olivine-dolomite melt at 3 GPa and 1290°C (66), 25° to 30° for olivine-Na2CO3 melt at 1 GPa and 1300°C (67), and ~30° for olivine-magnesite melt at 3.5 to 20 GPa and 1550°C (68). Such large θ, combined with the crystallographic anisotropy of olivine, imply that melt fractions exceeding 2.0 vol % are required to achieve interconnected melt networks—and much higher melt fractions for complete wetting (69). This fundamental discrepancy between geophysical interpretations and experimental constraints highlights a critical uncertainty in our understanding of melt connectivity and the origin of geophysical anomalies at the base of the upper mantle. A comprehensive reassessment of θ between olivine and carbonate-rich melts is therefore imperative.

The θ in solid-liquid systems is governed by multiple factors, including pressure-temperature (P-T) conditions, liquid composition, mineral assemblage, and crystallographic orientation (65, 69–72). Petrological and geochemical evidence confirms that H2O, NaCl, and SiO2-Al2O3 are key components of mantle carbonate melts (23, 73–83). For instance, superdeep diamonds contain highly saline fluids and halite inclusions (82, 83), and melt inclusions from intraplate volcanism show high CO2 (2.0 wt %) and Cl [1100 parts per million (ppm)] contents (84). In particular, H2O and NaCl have been shown to promote carbonatitic melt formation under deep mantle conditions (85). Carbonate melts exhibit remarkable solvent properties. At 0.1 GPa and 900°C, they can dissolve ~10 wt % H2O—two to three times more than silicate melts under comparable conditions (38); at 3 GPa and 1375°C, they incorporate at least 5.0 wt % SiO2, achieving complete carbonate-silicate melt miscibility above 1400°C (86). The presence of volatiles substantially alters melt/fluid properties and their interactions with minerals: (i) H2O reduces θ to near 0° in olivine-basaltic melt systems under high pressure conditions (65), contrasting with anhydrous systems (θ ≈ 30° to 50°) (87–89); (ii) The addition of H2O enhances the electrical conductivity of carbonate melts (90), indicating a structural evolution toward supercritical fluid behavior (91); (iii) NaCl lowers θ in olivine-fluid systems (69, 71, 72). In addition, SiO2-Al2O3 may reduce θ by diminishing chemical and structure contrasts between carbonate melts and silicate minerals. Overall, these findings indicate that volatile and silicate components may critically influence melt geometry and connectivity. Systematic experimental investigations are therefore required to quantify how H2O, NaCl, and SiO2-Al2O3 collectively affect θ in olivine-carbonate melt systems—an essential step toward accurately constraining the distribution and migration of natural carbonate melts in Earth’s upper mantle.

In this study, we experimentally investigated the effects of P-T conditions, H2O, NaCl, and SiO2-Al2O3 on θ between olivine and carbonate melt under conditions of 1 to 13 GPa and 1100° to 1400°C, using both piston-cylinder and multi-anvil apparatuses. Our results elucidate grain-scale melt connectivity and reveal the mechanism of θ reduction. By integrating our experimental results with previous studies, we calculated bulk electrical conductivity, derived seismic wave velocities, and quantified the volume fraction of volatile-bearing carbonate melt required to explain geophysical anomalies at the base of the upper mantle.

RESULTS

Dihedral angle between olivine and volatile-charged carbonate melt

The experimental run products consisted of mineral aggregates and intergranular fluids or melts (Fig. 1 and table S1). In the olivine-CaCO3±SiO2-Al2O3 systems at temperatures below 1300°C, no melt was detected. Instead, CaCO3 decomposed into CaO and CO2, producing large θ between olivine and CO2. When volatiles (H2O±NaCl) were added to these systems at the same temperatures (<1300°C), the solidus was notably lowered, resulting in partial melting. Partial melting consistently occurred in the systems when temperatures exceeded 1300°C. In all experiments, olivine reacted with carbonate (±SiO2-Al2O3) to form minor pyroxene along grain boundaries. Small-volume (<1 vol %) Fe-rich phases—interstitial magnetite and (Fe,Mg)CO3 solid solution—were observed at 1 to 3 GPa and 6 to 13 GPa, respectively (fig. S1). These observations are consistent with the stability field of the (Fe,Mg)CO3 solid solution (92, 93). The absence of reduced carbon phases (e.g., graphite, diamond, and carbide) indicates relatively oxidized conditions in our experiments, approximately between the CCO (graphite-CO-CO2) and FMQ (fayalite-magnetite-quartz) buffers (43). Olivine grain sizes in run products increased with temperature, reaching ~200 μm at 1400°C due to Ostwald ripening and coalescence of adjacent grains with similar crystallographic orientations (71). Olivine-melt interfaces showed both curved and faceted morphologies. Curvature reflects energy minimization through constant mean curvature (49, 89), whereas flat facets indicate crystallographically controlled minimum interfacial energy (69, 94). Interstitial melt distribution in olivine aggregates depended strongly on P-T conditions and melt composition. Below 1300°C in the olivine-CaCO3 systems, CO2-filled pores remained isolated at grain corners/boundaries. In contrast, when carbonate melt was present, melt distribution along grain boundaries varied from partial to complete, depending on θ.

Textural equilibrium, defined as the balance of interfacial tensions at triple junctions, was evidenced by (i) numerous olivine triple junctions displaying angles of ~120° angles in run products (Fig. 1, A1 to A5) (69, 71, 72, 95, 96); (ii) cumulative frequency curves of apparent θ match the predicted curves for equilibrated textures (97); (iii) our experimental durations (48 to 72 hours) exceeded those required for textural equilibration [e.g., 12 hours for grain size of 10 μm at 727°C (98)]. In addition, olivine grains exhibited homogeneous compositions without obvious zoning (table S2). As olivine is the dominant mineral in the upper mantle, the connectivity of interstitial melts is primarily controlled by the θ between olivine and melt, although pyroxene grains are also wetted by melt (Fig. 1A5). We therefore measured the olivine-melt-olivine θ to quantify melt connectivity.

The cumulative frequency and frequency distribution histograms from this study are provided in figs. S2 to S4. A sharp increase in cumulative frequency occurs near the median θ, consistent with theoretical predictions for an isotropic system with a single true θ (figs. S2 and S3). However, deviations between the measured data and the theoretical curve appear in some systems, particularly at high apparent θ values. These deviations likely arise from very large θ values associated with low-angle grain boundaries, where adjacent grains exhibit minimal misorientation (69). Despite these outliers, the median θ robustly represents the true θ in the system as the distribution of θ values is strongly concentrated around this value.

Figure 2 shows the P-T dependence of the median θ in olivine-CaCO3±H2O±NaCl±SiO2-Al2O3 systems. Overall, θ decreases systematically with increasing P-T conditions. In olivine-CO2 systems, θ > 60°, characteristic of dry grain boundaries, whereas olivine-melt systems exhibit θ < 60°, indicating partial to complete wetting. The addition of H2O±NaCl substantially reduces θ, whereas SiO2-Al2O3 has a negligible effect (Fig. 3). On the basis of wetting behavior, three regimes can be defined: (i) dry boundaries (θ > 60°), (ii) partial wetting (10° ≤ θ ≤ 60°), and (iii) complete wetting (θ < 10°), with the latter approaching θ ≈ 0° in H2O-NaCl–bearing systems at 1 to 13 GPa and 1200° to 1400°C. Microstructural observations of fully wetted grain boundaries confirm complete wetting under these conditions.

At 3 GPa and 1300°C, we examined the effects of water content, salinity, and carbonate composition [CaCO3, (Mg,Ca)CO3, and MgCO3] on θ in the olivine-carbonate-H2O-NaCl system (Fig. 3). Increasing water content systematically reduced θ: The water-free system yielded θ ≈ 30°, which decreased to 10° at >10 wt % H2O and approached 0° at >20 wt % H2O (Fig. 3D). Similarly, adding NaCl sharply lowered θ, with complete wetting (θ = 0°) occurring at only 0.25 wt % NaCl in the melt (Fig. 3E). In contrast, variations in carbonate composition produced no measurable change in θ (Fig. 3F), indicating their negligible influence on olivine-carbonate melt wetting behavior.

The wetness results show compositional, pressure, and angle dependencies (fig. S5, A to C). At 1300°C, wetness remains nearly constant (~60%) across 1 to 3 GPa in the olivine-carbonate melt system. Although SiO2-Al2O3 has negligible effect, both H2O and NaCl notably enhance wetness. At 1400°C, systems containing CaCO3-H2O-NaCl-SiO2-Al2O3 exhibit almost complete wetting (~100%) throughout 6 to 13 GPa. The compiled data reveal a linear correlation between wetness and θ; wetness increases from ~60 to ~100% as θ decreases from ~30° to 0° (fig. S5C). This relationship is consistent with our observation that grain boundaries become fully wetted by volatile-bearing carbonate melts at θ ≈ 0°.

DISCUSSION

Comparison with previous works

We compared our partial melting temperatures with previously determined solidi for dry and wet carbonated peridotite systems (2, 99–102). The solidus temperatures in dry systems are consistently higher than those in wet systems. For example, Weidendorfer et al. (100) reported dry solidus temperatures of 1225° to 1250°C at 1 GPa and 1275° to 1300°C at 1.5 GPa for the magnesian calcite–olivine–clinopyroxene system. In contrast, adding 3.5 wt % H2O lowers the solidus to 1025° to 1050°C at 1 GPa in the same system. Similarly, H2O-CO2–bearing fertile peridotite melts at 925° to 1060°C under 1.4 to 2.8 GPa (103), whereas carbonated phlogopite peridotite melts at 1050° to 1150°C under 3 to 6 GPa (79). Our results are consistent with these observations: No partial melting occurred below 1300°C in dry systems, whereas H2O-bearing systems exhibited melting at lower temperatures.

The θ measured in this study align with those reported in previous works. In both olivine-carbonate and olivine-carbonate-SiO2-Al2O3 systems below 1300°C, we observed large θ between olivine and CO2. This agrees with Watson and Brenan (46) and Huang et al. (72), who reported that θ between olivine and H2O-CO2 fluids increases with increasing CO2 content. This behavior arises because CO2, as a nonpolar molecule, lacks surface activity and thus hinders olivine surface wetting. For the carbonate melt-bearing systems, our measured θ match prior results across broad P-T ranges (fig. S5D) [e.g., 1 to 20 GPa, 1300° to 1550°C (66–68)]. This consistency implies that P-T exerts negligible influence on θ between olivine and dry carbonate melt.

Origin of the volatile effect on the dihedral angle

The observed decrease in θ with changing melt composition suggests reduced interfacial energy between olivine and melt in volatile-bearing systems, assuming constant grain boundary energy between olivine grains. Theoretically, interfacial energy between solid and liquid phases decreases with increasing solubility of the solid component in the liquid phase, as documented in binary eutectic systems including silicate + fluid/melt, binary alloys, and organic systems (104). Yoshino et al. (65) demonstrated that in an olivine-hydrous basalt melt system, θ decreases to 0° with increasing pressure up to ~7 GPa due to enhanced olivine solubility in the liquid. NaCl also reduces the θ in olivine-fluid systems by enhancing olivine solubility (71, 72, 105). Consequently, silicate solubility in carbonate melt plays a decisive role in reducing interfacial energy and θ. To investigate this relationship, we chemically analyzed interstitial melts in run products (table S3). Elemental mapping reveals complex multicomponent melt compositions (figs. S6 and S7), containing not only Ca, Na, Cl, and/or Al but also significant Mg and Si. In addition, Fe is concentrated in magnetite at 1 to 3 GPa and in (Fe,Mg)CO3 solid solution at 6 to 13 GPa—minor phases (<1 vol %) predominantly distributed along grain boundaries.

We particularly focused on the SiO2 + MgO + FeO (SMFO) concentration in carbonate-rich melts, as these components readily adsorb onto Mg-rich olivine (Fo91) surfaces, reducing interfacial energy (fig. S5E). Our results at 1 to 3 GPa and 1300°C show that, (i) in olivine-carbonate melt systems, the SMFO content reaches ~10 wt %; (ii) with the addition of H2O, the SMFO content increases to 20 wt %, rising further to ~50 wt % in H2O-NaCl–bearing systems; and (iii) in olivine-CaCO3-H2O-NaCl-SiO2-Al2O3 systems, SMFO stabilizes at ~46 wt %. These findings agree with previous studies: Dasgupta et al. (106) reported ~22 wt % SMFO dissolution in carbonated eclogite partial melts at 3 GPa and 1300°C, whereas Weidendorfer et al. (100) observed SiO2 + MgO contents up to 46.2 wt % in H2O-rich CaO-MgO-SiO2-CO2 systems. The SMFO enrichment likely reflects enhanced olivine solubility in volatile-bearing melts, facilitated by both high H2O solubility in carbonate melts (38) and carbonate-H2O interactions that promote supercritical aqueous-like properties (91). Xie et al. (107) recently demonstrated that hydrous melts near the 410-km discontinuity can contain 20 wt % H2O, 12.8 wt % CaO, 42.3 wt % (Mg,Fe)O, 0.5 wt % Al2O3, and 25.2 wt % SiO2, which shows a high SMFO of 67.5 wt %. Accordingly, volatile-rich carbonate melts exhibit significantly higher olivine solubility, as confirmed by our interstitial melt chemistry. This solute adsorption at mineral-melt interfaces reduces interfacial energy, ultimately decreasing the θ.

With increasing silicate solubility and volatile content (H2O±NaCl) in carbonate melts, the melt evolves toward complex carbonate-rich melts. Although our study lacks in situ melt-state observations, precluding definitive determination of melt miscibility/immiscibility, we can infer the likely behavior and state of melts in our system based on previous works. Silicate-carbonate immiscibility is well documented in carbonated nephelinite systems (108, 109), whereas miscibility dominates at T > 1400°C in carbonated eclogite systems (106). The immiscibility gap diminishes at high P-T conditions due to enhanced CO2 solubility in silicate melts (106). H2O solubility in carbonate melts reaches ≥15 wt % at 0.25 GPa and increases with P-T conditions (38). Melt miscibility in our system can also be inferred from the wetting behavior along olivine grain boundaries as carbonate melt, silicate melt, and aqueous fluid exhibit distinct wetting capacities (reflected by different θ values) under identical P-T conditions; complete uniform wetting indicates a homogeneous liquid phase, whereas heterogeneous wetting properties would suggest immiscible liquids. Our results demonstrate complete olivine wetting in CaCO3-H2O-NaCl (>12 wt % H2O) systems at 3 GPa and 1300°C, consistent with the wetting properties of the miscible carbonate-rich melts, with this behavior persisting at 1 GPa and 1200°C with 40 wt % H2O, indicating potential volatile-induced miscibility at relatively low P-T conditions. Recent studies support this interpretation: Supercritical H2O-CO2-NaCl liquids (>60 wt % solutes) form at 7.8 GPa and 1090°C (110), CaO-MgO-SiO2-CO2-H2O systems show continuous evolution to Mg-rich silico-carbonatites from siliceous Ca-rich carbonate melts (100), and carbonate structurally integrates into silicate melts at high pressure (111). Volatile-rich carbonate melts exhibit properties favoring miscibility—low viscosity (35), low density (36), high mobility (8), and exceptional dissolving capacity—that collectively promote efficient silicate wetting and pervasive mantle infiltration, strongly supporting miscible melt formation under elevated P-T conditions. The miscible melt/supercritical liquid can overcome olivine’s crystallographic anisotropy, achieving complete wetting of olivine grain boundaries.

Low-fraction carbonate-rich melt with complete grain-boundary wetting

The spatial distribution (i.e., θ) and volume fraction of intergranular melts in mantle rocks critically control bulk geophysical properties, including electrical conductivity and seismic wave velocity. Conversely, melt fractions can be estimated from θ using simplified models combined with geophysical observations. Under high P-T conditions, H2O-rich silicate melt completely wets olivine grains [θ ~ 0° (65)], a process that may generate geophysical anomalies beneath arc volcanoes via flux melting (112–115). In contrast, partial melting of carbonated slab and mantle rocks produces carbonate-rich melts at depths corresponding to the upper mantle’s base and the transition zone (4, 21, 23, 24, 85, 116). These melts, characterized by extremely low viscosity, high mobility, low seismic velocity, and elevated electrical conductivity (117), significantly facilitate the deep carbon cycle (84, 118–120). For instance, DeVitre et al. (84) revealed high CO2 contents in global intraplate volcanism, with olivine-hosted melt inclusions from Fogo volcano containing 2.8 wt % H2O, 2.0 wt % CO2, and 1100 ppm Cl. Consequently, volatile-rich carbonate melt is the most plausible explanation for large-scale geophysical anomalies at the base of the upper mantle.

To predict bulk electrical conductivity in olivine-carbonate melt systems, we used models with simplified fluid/melt geometries (Supplementary Text I): (i) the tube model (121), which assumes rectangular tube networks and yields relatively low conductivity increases with melt fraction; (ii) the Hashin-Shtrikman upper-bound (HS+) (122) and cube (123) models, both assuming grain coating of high-conductivity melt layers and producing similar conductivity trends; and (iii) Archie’s law (124) and modified Archie’s law (125) models, where bulk conductivity depends on solid-phase connectivity parameterized by the cementation exponent (m). For calculations, we used conductivities of 1.65 × 10-3 S/m for H2O-bearing (137 to 167 ppm) olivine aggregates (95) and 100 S/m for carbonate melts (90, 126). Model results are presented in Fig. 4 (A and B) and fig. S8. Given experimental confirmation of complete grain-boundary wetting by carbonate-rich melts, bulk conductivity is dominated by well interconnected melt networks. We therefore applied HS+, cube, Archie’s, and modified Archie’s models to estimate melt fractions explaining high-conductivity anomalies (i.e., 0.02 to 0.05 S/m) at the base of the upper mantle (127). For the Archie-type models, the cementation exponents (m = 0.1 to 1.8) were tested (fig. S8), with m = 0.5 and 1.0 shown in Fig. 4 (A and B), corresponding to high wetness (>90% grain-boundary wetting) and low solid connectivity in our experiments. We compared conductivity data from anhydrous/hydrous olivine studies (128–131) and our models with upper-mantle anomalies at 200 to 400 km in depth (50, 56, 57, 132); the results show these anomalies (0.02 to 0.05 S/m) are best explained by 0.02 to 0.08 vol % volatile-bearing carbonate melt, not hydrous olivine (Fig. 4, A and B).

Even small melt fractions substantially affect rock density and seismic velocity (59). In texturally equilibrated, partially molten mantle rocks where interfacial energy is minimized, melt geometry—characterized by constant θ and aspect ratios—interacts with melt fraction to control compressional and shear wave velocities (Vp and Vs) through bulk and shear modulus variations (133). Applying Takei’s (133) methodology (Supplementary Text II), we calculated Vp and Vs across θ values (~1° to 100°) and melt fractions (0.001 to 100 vol %) for a peridotite-carbonate melt system at 3 to 13 GPa (Fig. 4, C to F, and figs. S9 and S10). Our results demonstrate that (i) both Vp and Vs increase with larger θ (60° to 100°) but decrease sharply below 10° (Fig. 4C), with melt fraction effects becoming negligible at very low angles; (ii) for fixed θ, velocities decrease markedly when melt fractions exceed 10 vol % (Fig. 4D).

The combined effects of θ and melt fraction on seismic velocities demonstrate that, (i) at melt fractions exceeding 50 vol %, even large θ (>60°) causes notable reductions in Vp and Vs; (2) at small θ (<10°), even minimal melt fractions (0.001 to 0.1 vol %) substantially decrease both velocities (Fig. 4, E and F). These relationships explain how a completely wetted layer above the mantle transition zone can simultaneously enhance bulk conductivity and reduce seismic velocities with only trace amounts (0.02 to 0.08 vol %) of volatile-bearing carbonate melt. Our estimated melt fractions are significantly smaller than or comparable to previous estimates for the upper mantle base [0.7 vol % (134); 0.8 ± 0.5 vol % (135); ~0.05 vol % (64); 0.3 vol % (61)]. Although Yang and Faccenda (136) attributed low-velocity layers above the transition zone to 0.2 to 0.3 wt % H2O in northeast Asia, the thermal conditions and carbonated peridotite solidus instead favor volatile-rich carbonate melts as the dominant phase (21, 24, 137–142). Global seismic tomography reveals a low-velocity layer at ~390 km in depth in the upper mantle (fig. S11) (143–146), likely resulting from partial melting of carbonated slab and/or mantle rocks. Receiver function analyses indicate an average 3 to 4% reduction in Vp within this layer (147). Although such velocity reductions typically imply significant melt fractions, our findings suggest that low θ could also explain the observed anomalies. Our calculations demonstrate that θ of 20° to 30° is consistent with the Vp and Vs reductions detected in the low-velocity layer. In contrast, global seismic observations have not detected the pronounced velocity reduction expected for a 0° melt-rock θ. This discrepancy may arise from two main factors: (i) Seismic imaging resolution is fundamentally wavelength limited, operating at the kilometer scale—far larger than the nano- to microscale of melt-grain wetting properties, and (ii) mantle heterogeneity in both horizontal and vertical dimensions, accompanied by variations in mineral assemblages and wetting properties (148, 149), may obscure the effects of very low θ. Thus, low θ (~0°) obtained in our study offers a viable explanation for the low-velocity layer atop the mantle transition zone. Future efforts to determine the origin of this layer and estimate melt fractions should integrate high-resolution seismological and magnetotelluric observations with laboratory-based measurements.

Our calculations of electrical conductivity and seismic wave velocity are based on pure carbonate melt due to a lack of data for H2O-rich and silicate-rich compositions. These added components could significantly alter the properties, especially at high P-T conditions where silicate solubility increases (65, 150, 151). Under such conditions, carbonate melts, volatiles, and silicate components might become fully miscible, potentially forming a homogeneous supercritical liquid (91, 100, 106, 110, 111). The electrical conductivity of this multicomponent liquid may equal or exceed that of pure carbonate melt, and its complete wetting of rock grains could cause significant seismic wave attenuation. Quantifying these effects on deep mantle rocks requires future in situ measurements.

Regarding melt mobility, CO2-rich melts exhibit lower viscosity and less polymerized structures than basaltic melts, with mobility decreasing as melts evolve toward SiO2-rich compositions (40–42, 44). The viscosity of carbonate melt decreases with increasing temperature but shows negligible pressure dependence (42). In contrast, the presence of volatiles notably reduces viscosity; the addition of 10 wt % H2O doubles the transport efficiency of carbonate-rich melts, indicating high mobility of volatile-charged carbonate melts within mantle rocks (36, 152). The buoyant volatile-bearing carbonate melts form interconnected permeable networks in the upper mantle, facilitating efficient migration while enhancing both global mass transport and metasomatic processes through following key mechanisms (Fig. 5): (i) driving the deep carbon cycle, evidenced by geochemical signatures including diamond melt inclusions, CO2-rich petit-spot volcanism, and isotopic anomalies (19, 22, 25–28, 84, 153–155); (ii) chemically modifying mantle composition through slab-derived components (Mg, Ca, Al, and Si), fertilizing the mantle rocks (156–161); and (iii) extracting and transporting trace/rare earth elements through interaction with mantle rocks, contributing to their enrichment (6, 162–169). Consequently, despite their ultralow fraction (0.02 to 0.08 vol %), volatile-charged carbonate melts effectively percolate within mantle rocks, exerting disproportionate control over global-scale mass transfer and mantle metasomatism.

Low-volume fraction volatile-bearing carbonate melts at the base of the upper mantle.

(A) Material transport in subduction zones. As oceanic slab subducts, it carries materials into the mantle where devolatilization and partial melting occur at various depths, enabling material recycling to the upper mantle and Earth’s surface. Our experimental demonstration of complete olivine wetting (A1) shows that anomalously high electrical conductivity and reduced seismic velocities layer [30 to 100 km in thickness (58)] at the upper mantle base could be explained by minimal volatile-rich carbonate melt fractions. These interconnected melts modify physical properties through their unique rheological and electromagnetic characteristics.

This study experimentally demonstrates that volatile-bearing carbonate melts completely wet olivine grain boundaries. Our modeling shows that such melts can account for observed geophysical anomalies—including high electrical conductivity and low seismic velocities—at the base of the upper mantle, even at ultralow melt fractions (0.02 to 0.08 vol %). These results provide invaluable insights into the distribution, migration, metasomatic effects, and global recycling of volatile-rich carbonate melts in the upper mantle.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental procedures

High P-T experiments were conducted to investigate the effects of H2O, NaCl, and SiO2-Al2O3 on θ between olivine and carbonate melt at 1 to 13 GPa and 1100° to 1400°C. San Carlos olivine (Fo91Fa9) powder with grain sizes of 38 to 53 μm was used as the starting material. Reagent-grade calcite served as the carbonate source for most experiments, with dolomite and magnesite also being used to examine the effect of carbonate species on θ between olivine and carbonate melt. Deionized distilled water provided the sources of pure H2O. The NaCl solution was prepared by dissolving reagent-grade NaCl (99.99% purity) in deionized distilled water at room temperature (~25°C) and atmospheric pressure. Reagent-grade SiO2 and Al2O3 powders were mixed in a 1:1 ratio to obtain SiO2-Al2O3. H2O, NaCl solution, and SiO2-Al2O3 were then combined to create the target system.

High P-T experiments were conducted using a piston-cylinder apparatus and a 2500-ton multi-anvil press at the Guangzhou Institute of Geochemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences. For pressures of 1 to 3 GPa, end-loaded piston-cylinder apparatus with piston diameters of 3/4- and 1/2-inch cells were used for experiments at 1 and 2–3 GPa, respectively, along with a standard talc-Pyrex assembly. For pressures of 4 to 13 GPa, experiments were performed using a multi-anvil apparatus. The olivine powder, combined with an ~5.0 wt % carbonate-rich mixture, was loaded into an end-welded noble metal capsule that was sealed by arc welding. The Au80Pd20 alloy capsule (2.0 mm in outer diameter and 1.7 mm in inner diameter) was used for experiments at 1100° to 1400°C. During the experiment, the temperature was monitored and controlled by an S-type (Pt-Pt90Rh10) thermocouple for piston-cylinder experiments and a C-type (W95Re5-W74Re26) for multi-anvil experiments. Detailed procedures for the high P-T experiments are described by previous studies (170–172). The run duration was 48 to 72 hours, depending on the target temperature. All experiments were quenched by switching off the electrical power supplied to the heaters. Details of the starting materials, experimental conditions, and results are summarized in table S1.

Dihedral angle measurement

The well-polished cross sections of the run charges were observed by using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (JEOL, JSM-7100F) with an accelerating voltage of 15 keV at Tohoku University. We observed the mineral phases and microstructures in the run products. The fluid/melt triple junctions were used for quantitative analysis. More than 200 backscattered electron (BSE) or secondary electron (SE) images of 1280 × 960 pixels were obtained for each run product with high magnifications and resolution, depending on the pore size. The apparent θ values were precisely measured at fluid/melt triple junctions on the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images using the Image J software (v.1.52p, National Institutes of Health). The method used for angle measurements by Huang et al. (69, 71, 72). The measured median θ from a two-dimensional (2D) section most likely equals the true value of the population of the 3D angle (173). In this study, we measured more than 100 apparent angles based on the acquired SEM images with high magnification (up to ×150,000) and resolution for each sample to determine the median θ, which is comparable with the value from transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (174). The statistical error of the median value was estimated to be ±1.5°, as discussed by Huang et al. (72). The details of θ selection and measurement are described by Huang et al. (69, 71, 72). We carefully distinguished between cracks formed during quenching/polishing and original grain-boundary wetting features. To elucidate the correlation between θ and wetness (ψ), we calculated ψ for our experimental products using the following equation (175, 176): ψ = LSL/(LSL + 2LSS), where LSL and LSS are the lengths of solid-liquid boundaries and the length of solid-solid boundaries, respectively.

Analytical method

Mineral compositions were analyzed with an electron probe microanalyzer (EPMA; JEOL JXA-8230) equipped with five wavelength-dispersive spectrometers at the Laboratory of Microscopy and Microanalysis, Wuhan Microbeam Analysis Technology Co. Ltd. The samples were first coated with a thin conductive carbon film before analysis. The precautions suggested by Zhang et al. (177) were used to minimize the difference in carbon film thickness between samples and obtain an ~20 nm approximately uniform coating. Details of EPMA methods are described by Yang et al. (178). Operating conditions for quantitative wavelength-dispersive spectroscopy analyses involved an accelerating voltage of 15 kV, a beam current of 20 nA, and a 1-μm spot size to minimize sample damage and reduce the x-ray intensity fluctuations (179). Data were corrected online using a ZAF (atomic number, absorption, and fluorescence) correction procedure. The peak counting time was 10 s for Ca, K, Na, Mg, Si, Al, Cl, and Fe and 20 s for Ti and Mn. The background counting time was 1/2 of the peak counting time on the high-energy and low-energy background positions. The following standards were used: diopside (Ca), rutile (Ti), microcline (K), jadeite (Na), olivine (Si and Mg), pyrope (Al), almandine (Fe), rhodonite (Mn), and scapolite (Cl). For melt composition analysis, the CO2 content was determined using different approaches depending on the system composition. In dry systems (olivine + CaCO3), the CO2 content was estimated from the electron microprobe analytical shortfall, assuming CO2 to be the only unanalyzed component (180, 181). In H2O-bearing systems, CO2 was calculated on the basis of a 1:1 molar CaO:CO2 ratio, disregarding potential Ca incorporation into limited pyroxene formation; the H2O content was derived from the difference between 100% and the analytical total of other components.
