What do aged care leaders need to meet quality and safety challenges?
Authors: Siri Wiig, Johanna Westbrook, Jeffrey Braithwaite, Louise A. Ellis, Hilda Bø Lyng
Published: 2026

Introduction

Leadership is a puzzle, and many pieces need to fit together to ensure quality and safe care services. Leaders in aged care, such as nursing homes, residential aged care homes, and homecare services, face significant challenges with increasing demands from aging populations, labor shortages, financial constraints, and increased complexity [1, 2]. Aged care organizations can lack the capacity to cope with these challenges and leaders struggle with reducing risk. A consequence is that rates of adverse events are persistently high [3]. Innovative solutions and new leadership skills are needed to handle the capacity-demand gaps, while at the same time delivering quality care. We argue that part of the solution is for aged care services to focus on their leaders, identify what their main leadership challenges are, and support them with tools, training and competence development so they can promote quality and safety for both workers and patients. Leaders in aged care may span from frontline leaders, head of departments, to leaders of larger organizations and all the way up to policy and ministry of health level. In this editorial we focus on frontline leaders and head of departments and organizations like a nursing home or a homecare unit. In the following, we first describe key leadership challenges from the literature and then discuss what is needed to be an effective quality and safety leader in aged care.

Why is leadership so hard?

Previous research has identified that leaders are challenged in their quality and safety work related to structure, culture, contextual settings, and external pressures [2]. In this regard, it is often the accumulated effects of many small, everyday problems that add risks for both patients and staff [4]. Leaders struggle to retain and ensure continuity of staff. Older people receiving care value building relations with care staff who understand their needs [2]. High staff turnover increases the risk of poor-quality care.

Further challenges relate to team composition and competence, allocating limited resources, and compliance to increasing governance and regulatory demands. Leaders have constrained time to meet and talk with their staff and work on relations [2, 5]. The conceptualization of quality and risk are often anchored in old school thinking where quality is understood as following procedures and compliance with standards in a linear way [6]. The real world shows huge variation in the delivery of what is considered as care quality. Staff frequently need to adapt to standards and are sometimes compelled to follow undocumented workarounds and adaptations due to emerging patient needs, outdated standards, innovative solutions, or just because it eases the work processes and benefit both service users and work efficiency [2, 7]. Leaders may remain uninformed about workarounds as staff anticipate that this information will be judged negatively by their leaders. Constant firefighting tends to leave leaders with a focus on short-term perspectives such as micro-managing staff rosters, or replacing staff because of high turnover, leaving little time for more strategic and visionary efforts.

Leaders are responsible for compliance with a huge variety of regulations to ensure staff and patient safety and the available training and requirements vary substantially [5]. Moreover, leaders are tasked with the responsibility of implementing change processes decided externally for their organization or unit. This may be changes happening due to new regulatory frameworks and responsibilities, policy decisions, new IT-infrastructures, changes in internal organizing and decisions made by higher level leaders within organizations without proper involvement from frontline and department leaders. Hence, they can experience quality systems imposed on them as being incompatible with their other systems and they may not have access to management tools to support change or be able to weigh the risk implications of the changes imposed on them [2]. If there are too many quality improvement interventions that are received too frequently, implementation will be hampered, leading to sustainability problems that will burden leaders and staff [8]. Such a situation is likely to lead to goal conflicts and continuous tradeoffs – sometimes between staff safety and patient safety. Leaders are responsible for compliance with a huge variety of regulations to ensure staff and patient safety and the available training and requirements vary substantially [5].

Challenges perceived by leaders trying to run services may not always appear substantial, but when aggregated, can be overwhelming – especially for frontline and department leaders who often have not been offered proper training and competence enhancement and support prior to or when entering their leader positions. This leaves them with a large responsibility according to the law, but with a lack of support to handle it [5]. What characterizes the challenges, and their solutions, is that they often rely upon a wide variety of skills in human factors, risk management, human resources, logistics, regulations, strategy, operational work, professional judgement, and decision making. All these influence quality and safety but have traditionally been underrepresented in professional training programs, such as medical and nursing schools [9] - the professional background for most aged care leaders. Although attention some of these competence components have improved in different countries and study programs, there is still a need to strengthen it as quality and safety challenges in aged care become increasingly complex to lead [5, 9].

What is needed?

Working on quality and safety may be perceived by leaders as competing with other tasks, and not integral to, but additional to, their work. What then is needed to be an effective quality and safety leader? Unfortunately, there is no easy or generic solution. Measures must be contextualized and related to country and setting specific needs. We will need measures at different levels—individual, organizational, and systemic—to enhance quality and safety in aged care. Measure will relate to structural, cultural and relational needs. This includes not only leadership competencies but also structural supports such as adequate staffing, regulatory alignment, and a culture that prioritizes safety and learning and relational work. Addressing these multi-level factors would provide a more comprehensive understanding of what enables sustainable quality and safety improvement and effective leadership in aged care settings.

In a structural perspective, we argue that aged care organizations and policy makers need to acknowledge the complexity in leadership tasks and responsibilities, and support leaders to develop a more strategic and more systematic approach to promote quality and safety. Such an approach needs to consider the structural and legal elements of aged care leaders’ role and function. Leaders must be equipped with the authority, resources and support to initiate and follow up on improvement efforts, and to work strategically with both short-term and long-term goals. Leaders need in-depth knowledge and skills discussed above as the foundation for enacting their role and formal responsibilities [5]. They also require access to timely, useable data for continuous improvement, such as those from electronic health record systems, allowing them to monitor care effectively and to identify areas requiring attention [10].

However, even this is not enough. Structural elements must be supported by increased attention to the cultural, relational, and more soft elements of leadership [6]. In a cultural and a relational perspective, this means leaders need time to be present in the work environment, and to round, meet, reflect, and get to know their staff to understand their needs and attend to their wellbeing, at the same time as optimizing team compositions and safety for patients and increase their situational awareness These are crucial for quality and safety, but rarely acknowledged in practice or as the core components of training. Furthermore, an emergent area challenging leaders’ skills arises from the increasing complexity of aged care contexts and the need for adaptive capacity. Healthcare professionals and leaders must continuously adapt their services to user needs [1]. Hence, promoting a culture of openness about workarounds, adaptations, and the importance of flexing and tailoring care is required. Effective leaders need to be exposed to new evidence and skills to recognize that many adaptations are important sources of safety, but also that adaptations can pose additional risks. Leaders need skills and a vocabulary to engage staff in reflections about their work, to openly discuss work practices, and formulate responsive action to reduce risk. Such elements are partly covered in masters-level study programs in health leadership or human factors. The problem is that only a few of the many leaders hold such degrees, hence the problem must be solved via additional routes of capacity building. The latter needs larger systemic health educational reforms from policy level with actions to better incorporate these elements professional education programs. It furthermore most likely needs to incorporate requirements for relevant quality and safety courses when people enter leader positions, equal to what is for example required for leaders in Norway in occupational health and working environment where a 40 h course is mandatory [5].

Conclusions

If we are to do more than simply pay lip service to the ideal of continuous improvement, leaders must possess a variety of leadership skills to effectively lead quality and safety. One cannot expect nurses or doctors to be good leaders without proper training, support, and skills. Policy makers and aged care organizations need to put leaders and leadership squarely on the agenda. Leaders need evidence-based tools, improved sources of data to monitor care and the effectiveness of interventions, and training programs to support them to advance leadership skills individually and collectively. There is a long way to go, but action is imperative and the time is now.
