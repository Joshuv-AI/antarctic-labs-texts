Small vesicles, big potential: A review of innovative exosome delivery and molecular mechanisms in preclinical myocardial infarction models
Authors: Chayanisa Phutiyothin, Kovit Pattanapanyasat, Sarawut Kumphune
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExosomes have emerged as promising therapeutic carriers, with over 40 000 scientific publications reflecting their exponential growth in biomedical research. Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of mortality worldwide. Among these, myocardial infarction (MI) represents the most prevalent and devastating form, contributing significantly to global morbidity and mortality. Reducing the mortality and long‐term complications associated with MI is therefore both necessary and urgent. Exosomes, naturally occurring extracellular vesicles, offer a promising cell‐free alternative for drug delivery and cardiac repair and regeneration after ischemic injury due to their ability to transport bioactive proteins, lipids, and RNA. This narrative review summarizes recent advancements in exosome‐based therapeutics for ischemic heart disease, focusing on the efficacy of various animal models. Various approaches are being studied to optimize exosome‐based therapies, including methods to enhance their stability, targeting ability, and bioavailability in the cardiovascular system, highlighting the potential of exosomes not only as drug delivery vehicles but also as regenerative mediators capable of promoting myocardial repair and reducing MI. With continued advances in exosome technology and a deeper understanding of their biological functions, there is growing optimism that these vesicles could pave the way for more effective and less invasive treatments for cardiovascular diseases in the near future.

INTRODUCTION

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for nearly 18 million fatalities annually, which represents approximately 31% of all global deaths. 1 These conditions pose a significant public health challenge, with their prevalence continuing to increase further due to aging populations and lifestyle changes. CVDs are predicted to be the major cause of death among the population around the world in 2030. 2 Ischemic heart disease (IHD), the most common type of CVDs, has become one of the major causes of death, not only in developed countries, but also in Thailand. 2 The survivors of MI can subsequently suffer from reparative changes (cardiac remodeling), which include ventricular dilatation and hypertrophy. This is associated with a significant increase in the risk of heart failure and arrhythmia. Therefore, any strategies that prevent or lower the severity of myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury, as well as cardiac hypertrophy, can save patients' lives and improve their quality of life. 3

Current therapeutic strategies for MI primarily include percutaneous angioplasty, stenting, and coronary artery bypass grafting. 4 Although many efforts have been made to improve patient outcomes, effective therapies remain elusive. For patients with end‐stage MI, heart transplantation remains the only available treatment option. However, this approach is significantly constrained by high healthcare costs, often resulting in wait times of at least 2 years. In some cases, cardiac assist devices 5 are used as a “bridge to transplant,” but they do not cure the disease. 6 These challenges highlight the urgent need for alternative therapies capable of not only improving outcomes but also addressing cardiovascular damage directly.

Recent clinical interest has increasingly focused on extracellular vehicles (EVs), particularly exosomes, which have emerged as potential biomarkers and therapeutic agents. 7 Exosomes have demonstrated significant promise as drug delivery vehicles, and their involvement in the pathogenesis of a variety of diseases has prompted further investigation into their therapeutic potential. 8 The analysis of the Content Addressable Storage (CAS) Content Collection, the most extensive human‐curated repository of published scientific knowledge, has revealed intriguing insights into the publication trends concerning exosomes. Currently, the CAS Content Collection contains over 40 000 scientific publications, including journal articles and patents, related to exosomes and EVs, demonstrating a steady and exponential growth trend over time. 9 The EVs have demonstrated potential not only in CVDs but also in oncology and neurodegenerative disorders, such as Alzheimer's disease and ischemic stroke, highlighting their potential as a novel therapeutic approach in treating complex, chronic conditions. Despite this promise, the development of new therapies remains challenging due to the complexity of CVDs.

Although animal models are essential for elucidating the molecular pathways of CVD, the “translational chasm” between preclinical success and clinical efficacy endures, influenced by intrinsic interspecies variances and systemic physiological subtleties. 10 The current review article interrogates the frontier of exosome‐based therapeutics for myocardial infarction (MI) by focusing on recent advancements in exosomes within experimental animal models of MI. The differences in exosome origin, separation technique, dose, and administration approach that significantly affect the therapeutic outcomes are summarized. Furthermore, the novel techniques that enhance the efficacy of exosomes and their retention inside the heart are highlighted. This review identifies key approaches to providing an overview for guiding future research toward more repeatable, mechanistically robust, and translatable exosome‐based cardiac treatments. LITERATURE REVIEW METHODOLOGY

The review collects data from MEDLINE (via PubMed) by searching 10‐year publications from 2014 to 2025 regarding “The data in this review was collected from animal models and exosomes in cardiovascular.” The selection parameters included original studies identified among 238 articles pertinent to animal models and exosomes in cardiovascular research, with 31 articles ultimately included in this review article. The following exclusion criteria were applied:

For the article type, review articles, meta‐analyses, editorials, book chapters, and conference abstracts were excluded to prevent data duplication. The studies utilizing in vitro or in vivo models without an MI model were excluded. The studies that focus primarily on CVDs other than MI or I/R injury (e.g., atherosclerosis without infarction, primary pulmonary hypertension, or isolated genetic cardiomyopathies) were excluded.

EXTRACELLULAR VESICLES

EVs are a generic term that refers to secreted vesicles. Current criteria for classifying EVs rely on size (ranging from 30 to 5000 nm), density, cellular origin, function, and molecular cargo. 11 EVs consist of a variety of subtypes based on different types of biogenesis and membranous vesicles: exosomes (endocytic origin with the size of 30–150 nm in diameter), microparticles or MPs (plasma membrane budding with the size of 150–1000 nm in diameter), and apoptotic bodies (membrane blebbing with the size of 800–5000 nm in diameter). 12 The biogenesis of MPs seems to originate directly from the plasma membrane. The fission and subsequent release of MP occur after budding starts at a specific site on the cell membrane. Molecular rearrangement of the membrane is owed to the alteration of protein and lipid composition and intracellular Ca2+ levels. Changes in Ca2+ levels lead to the recruitment and activation of Ca2+‐dependent enzymes, including floppase, scramblase, and calpain, which modify the membrane lipid composition. 13 Phosphatidylserine (PS) externalization to the outer leaflet appears to be one of the main features of MPs. 14 In addition, lipid raft domains, as well as proteins that are involved in maintaining cell shape, like actin, also participate in MP biogenesis. At the molecular level, a member of the small GTPase family, RhoA, in combination with Rho‐associated coiled‐containing protein kinase and the LIM kinase, serves as the MP‐releasing regulator. ARF6 is also a crucial protein in MP production and release, and the ARF6‐regulated endosomal complex is responsible for the selective sorting of molecular cargo into MPs. 15 , 16 In contrast to MPs, exosomes are the smallest EVs produced within the endosome, in addition to direct budding from the plasma membrane. Exosome formation occurs within intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) formed by inward budding of cellular compartments known as multivesicular bodies (MVBs). 17 MVB can both integrate with lysosomes to be biologically degraded or re‐enter and unite with the plasma membrane. 18 The process of EV cargo sorting during the internal budding of the membrane that leads to ILV formation is a crucial step for exosome bioinformation. 19 The endosomal‐sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT), comprising several ESCRT subunits together with associated proteins such as ALG‐2‐interacting protein X (ALIX) and tumor susceptibility gene 101 protein (TSG101), as well as protein chaperones (heat‐shock proteins), is responsible for accumulating and sorting biomolecules directed into ILVs. 11 , 16 Once exosomes are formed, ILVs together with MVBs fuse with the plasma membrane or lysosome before release into the extracellular space as exosomes with the aid of the soluble NSF‐attachment protein receptor (SNARE) complex and the Ca2+‐regulated vesicle‐associated membrane protein. 16 , 20

Collectively, exosomes are intercellular communication mediators and potential therapeutic agents for MI due to their unique biogenesis and properties. ISOLATION OF EXOSOMES

The EV isolation method, shown in Table 1, involves several methods that have therapeutic efficacy for use in MI models, as different techniques vary in yield, purity, and suitability for applications. Because the exosome isolation techniques have been reviewed in several articles, in this article, we will briefly focus on the current methods that have been used in cardiovascular exosome isolation, including ultracentrifugation (UC) and polyethylene glycol (PEG) precipitation.

UC, the gold standard for exosome isolation, separates EVs based on size and density using high centrifugal force. 21 Although UC is time‐consuming and requires specialized equipment, it effectively removes soluble proteins and other contaminants from the supernatant to provide high‐purity vesicles, which are essential for mechanistic studies, such as analysis of the miR‐21‐5p/YAP1 axis, where vesicle‐specific effects must be reliably distinguished. 22 Some research studies used shock wave therapy (SWT) before UC on cells, particularly human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), to enhance exosome release with specific bioactive cargo, such as miR‐19a‐3p. 23 Ultrafiltration (UF) uses semi‐permeable membranes to separate vesicles based on size. 24 In MI research, it has been successfully used to isolate exosomes from lung spheroid cells (LSCs) for inhalation therapy for spray application. 25

PEG precipitation is a chemical technique that isolates exosomes by decreasing their solubility, leading to vesicle aggregation that can be separated through low‐speed centrifugation. 26 PEG is an inexpensive, easy‐to‐perform, high‐yielding method that has been successfully used in MI studies. 27 The commercial exosome isolation kits in MI studies, such as the ExoQuick‐TC, Ribo, and Invitrogen, are high‐yielding and easy to perform but low in specificity compared to other methods, as polymer‐mediated precipitation can lead to the co‐isolation of non‐vesicular proteins. 28 However, exosomes isolated with a commercial kit have exhibited therapeutic effectiveness in experimental MI models, especially in applications that require a sufficient therapeutic dosage, such as microneedle patches 29 and shear‐thinning gels. 30

EXOSOMES AS THERAPEUTICS IN MI

The in vivo models of MI employ exosomes that are derived from a variety of cellular sources and biological fluids, each of which possesses unique therapeutic and diagnostic properties 31 (Table 2 and Figure 2). The molecular cargo of exosomes and, as a result, their functional impact on cardiac repair, inflammatory modulation, and disease progression are significantly influenced by their cellular origin. 32 Among their bioactive components, microRNAs (miRs) are critical regulators that facilitate intercellular communication, orchestrating processes such as tissue regeneration, angiogenesis, and inflammation resolution. 33 Exosomes function as potent biological mediators that can reprogram recipient cells and influence the myocardial microenvironment after ischemic injury via each of these mechanisms. Comparative overview of exosome sources in myocardial infarction therapy.

Exosomes from stem cells

Stem cell–derived exosomes are a novel regenerative therapy for MI. Exosome therapy is a safe, cell‐free approach for myocardial repair post‐infarction, preserving the regenerative advantages of stem cells without the risks of immune incompatibility or tumorigenesis. Recent research highlights their potential in enhancing therapies for IHD. 34

Mesenchymal stem cells

MSCs‐derived exosomes are the most widely studied in MI in vivo models. They deliver reparative microRNAs and proteins, which reduce cardiac apoptosis, promote angiogenesis, and improve heart function after MI or ischemic injury. Ji and Wang, 22 exosomal miR‐21‐5p in post‐MI cardiomyocyte responses, including inflammation, apoptosis, and fibrosis, may signal via YAP1 suppression, leading to novel treatment strategies that enhance outcomes after MI. In parallel, transplanted MSCs reduce autophagic flux in infarcted hearts through the exosomal transfer of miR‐125b. Exosomal miR‐125b‐5p is the main anti‐autophagic and cytoprotective component of the MSCs‐exosome cargo, functioning by interfering with the p53/Bnip3 signaling pathway. This is the first demonstration that MSC transplantation during MI is attributable to the suppression of ischemia‐induced autophagic flux. 35 Furthermore, exosomal miR‐25‐3p derived from MSCs reduced MI by targeting pro‐apoptotic proteins and EZH2. miR‐25‐3p directly binds with the 3′UTR of pro‐apoptotic genes, resulting in their suppression, decreased cardiac apoptosis, and improved cardiomyocyte viability. miR‐25‐3p inhibits the expression of the epigenetic repressor EZH2. This eliminates the repressive epigenetic mark H3K27me3 from two cardioprotective genes, including endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), which is cardioprotective, and SOCS3 (anti‐inflammatory), therefore restoring their expression and resulting in the reduction of inflammation. 36

Bone marrow‐derived mesenchymal stem cells

Bone marrow‐derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) are a prominent focus in research for MI treatment due to their unique biological properties, such as low immunogenicity, and exhibit a strong ability to differentiate into cardiomyocyte‐like cells, which can contribute to the repair and regeneration of damaged heart muscle. 37 , 38 , 39 These characteristics make BMSCs a promising candidate for therapies aimed at improving cardiac function and promoting vascularization in MI patients. 40 However, despite these advantages, cell‐based therapies have low cell survival and the risk of immunological rejection. Exosomes are a cell‐free alternative, with stability and reduced immunogenicity. 41 In the Li et al. 42 study, the exosomes extracted from BMSC have a high level of miR‐29c, which reduces the MI area and excessive autophagy by targeting the PTEN gene and activating the AKT/mTOR pathway, thus protecting the myocardial I/R injury.

Adipose‐derived stem cells

Wang et al., 43 exosomes derived from adipose‐derived stem cells (ADSCs) and genetically modified to overexpress miR‐146a have enhanced cardioprotective effects against acute AMI. The overexpression of miR‐146a inhibits EGR1, a transcription factor that is rapidly increased in response to stress and promotes inflammatory and fibrotic signaling.

Umbilical cord‐derived mesenchymal stem cells

Yuan et al. 29 found that exosomes derived from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (HUCMSCs) are engineered to deliver antifibrotic microRNA cargo, especially miR‐29b mimics, to reduce post‐infarction fibrosis. The dual‐engineering approach of cargo modification by electroporation with advanced delivery methods, such as microneedle patches, enhances the stability, targeting, and therapeutic efficacy of exosomes. This approach effectively limits cardiac fibroblast function and extracellular matrix deposition.

Other stem cells

Li et al. 25 demonstrate that exosomal miR‐100 derived from lung spheroid cells (LSCs) is delivered to endothelial cells (ECs) in the ischemic heart, where it downregulates CD36, a critical fatty acid transporter. This suppression promotes cardiomyocyte proliferation and contractile recovery by shifting cardiac metabolism from excessive fatty acid oxidation to glucose utilization. Thus, the miR‐100/CD36 axis is a promising therapeutic target for metabolic reprogramming and cardiac repair, with the potential to provide anti‐thrombotic benefits by reducing platelet adhesion to ECs.

Exosomes from immune cells

Macrophage‐derived exosomes play a dual role: M1 exosomes are pro‐inflammatory and exacerbate CVD, whereas M2 exosomes are anti‐inflammatory and promote tissue repair. 44 Research has rarely investigated the functions of exosomes derived from macrophages in MI/R injury, although they contain a substantial portion of the circulating microvesicles in blood. 45 For instance, exosomes carrying miR‐155 produced from macrophages inhibit fibroblast proliferation and enhance fibroblast inflammation by targeting Sos1 after heart damage. 43 Sos1, a key regulator of proliferation, significantly enhances the initial proliferation and collagen deposition by cardiac fibroblasts (CFs), essential for stabilizing the infarct and preventing heart rupture, a primary contributor to early mortality. 46 The strategy employed is to use a miR‐155 inhibitor (antagomir) to block the harmful signaling pathway, instead of developing advantageous miRs. 43 Conversely, Dai et al. 47 found that exosomes produced from M2 macrophages transport microRNA‐148a to reduce cardiac ischemia/reperfusion damage by suppressing TXNIP and the TLR4/NF‐KB/NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway, a major cascade for inflammation and cell death.

Exosomes from vascular cells

Exosomes derived from vascular cells are essential for maintaining myocardial homeostasis and promoting intercellular communication during injury and repair. In IHD, these vesicles function as crucial regulators of angiogenesis, fibrosis, and metabolic adaptation by delivering bioactive molecules, such as microRNAs and proteins. 48 The study of Gollmann‐Tepeköylü et al. 23 demonstrates that exosomes released from endothelial cells (ECs) in response to shock wave therapy (SWT) are enriched with miR‐19a‐3p, which promotes angiogenesis by inhibiting the anti‐angiogenic factor TSP‐1.

Exosomes from blood and plasma

Exosomes in acute myocardial infarction and recovery

Circulating exosomes obtained from blood or plasma are a highly significant source of EVs for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes in IHD. In experimental and clinical research, plasma‐derived exosomes have emerged as prospective sources for biomarkers in the early detection and prognosis of cardiac damage, as well as functional mediators that can transport cardioprotective signals to recipient cells. 49 Wang et al. 50 found that in individuals recovering from AMI, plasma exosomes obtained during the initial recovery phase demonstrate a significant downregulation of the cardioprotective miR‐342‐3p. Functionally, miR‐342‐3p has dual regulatory effects by suppressing apoptosis through the inhibition of SOX6 and inhibiting excessive autophagy via the control of TFEB. The significant decrease in miR‐342‐3p after AMI indicates compromised intrinsic cardioprotective signaling and may indicate reduced capacity for myocardial recovery. Thus, circulating exosomal miR‐342‐3p has potential as a sensitive biomarker for evaluating cardiac recovery capability and risk stratification in patients during post‐infarction recovery. 50 Similarly, Minghua et al. 51 discovered that the protective effect of remote ischemic preconditioning (RIPC), characterized by non‐lethal ischemia applied to a distant organ (the limb), is facilitated by circulating plasma exosomes enriched with miR‐24. miR‐24 and its target Bim are confirmed as essential molecular targets for the development of therapeutic interventions against I/R injury. In parallel, Liu et al. 52 reported that miR‐17‐3p, found in peripheral blood exosomes, alleviates programmed necrosis in cardiac ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) damage by regulating TIMP3 (tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases 3) expression, resulting in improved cardiac function and decreased infarct size in vivo. 52 These studies collectively highlight the various functions of circulating exosomal miRNAs in modulating apoptosis, autophagy, and necrosis, thereby emphasizing their potential as both diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic agents in cardiac repair.

Exosomes in peri‐operative and post‐surgical remodeling

Although most studies have focused on circulating exosomes in AMI and ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury, circulating exosomes provide critical insights into the complex inflammatory and remodeling processes triggered by surgical interventions, such as cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and valve repair. 53 In the peri‐operative setting, plasma exosome levels in elderly patients undergoing on‐pump cardiac surgery, particularly coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) or heart valve surgery, were found to reach the peak value 3 h after aortic unclamping, paralleling the release of cardiac troponin I. 54 These peri‐operative exosomes showed a distinct protein signature dependent on the specific surgical procedure: exosomes from heart valve surgery patients were enriched with antioxidant catalase and the chaperone sacsin, whereas CABG‐associated exosomes contained increased levels of the anti‐inflammatory Serpin A3 and glutathione peroxidase 3 (GPX3). These vesicles showed acute cardioprotective effects by inhibiting cardiomyocyte apoptosis in hypoxia/re‐oxygenation models, indicating an endogenous adaptive response to surgical stress and ischemia/reperfusion injury. 54

In the late post‐operative period, circulating exosomes function as prognostic markers for structural recovery and reverse remodeling. In patients undergoing mitral valve replacement for primary mitral regurgitation, increased plasma exosome levels 6 months after surgery were significantly correlated with enhanced left ventricular reverse remodeling (LV Rev‐Rem). 55 Mechanistically, these late‐phase exosomes showed a significant enrichment of miR‐21‐5p in comparison to patients with incomplete remodeling. Exosomes enriched with miR‐21‐5p were demonstrated to reduce angiotensin II‐induced cardiomyocyte hypertrophy in vitro and restore the expression of the calcium‐handling gene SERCA2a, thereby preventing adverse remodeling and preserving cardiac function. These findings collectively highlight the importance of plasma exosomes as indicators of acute ischemic injury and as active mediators of peri‐operative protection and long‐term myocardial plasticity. 55

Pharmacological and nutritional modulation of EV biology

The therapeutic application of exosomes in MI is not only by their delivery but also by their capacity to regulate their biogenesis, cargo loading, and targeting through pharmacological and nutritional interventions. A recent study has demonstrated that cardiovascular drugs and specific dietary compounds can stimulate parent cells to release vesicles with enhanced regenerative properties.

Pharmacological agents

Small‐molecule drugs used in CVD can influence both the quantity and quality of EV release. 56 For example, ticagrelor, a P2Y12 platelet inhibitor commonly used in acute coronary syndromes, has been shown to regulate the release of exosomes from human cardiac progenitor cells (hCPCs). Casieri et al. 57 demonstrated that at clinically relevant doses (1 μM), ticagrelor enhances the release of exosomes from hCPCs. Ticagrelor‐induced exosomes were enriched with the anti‐apoptotic chaperone HSP70. When applied to cardiomyocytes, these exosomes attenuated hypoxia‐induced apoptosis and upregulated the pro‐survival ERK42/44 signaling pathway, an effect not seen with EVs from untreated cells. 57 Similarly, other metabolic modulators, anti‐inflammatory agents (e.g., statins, 58 glucocorticoids 59 ), and epigenetic drugs (e.g., HDAC inhibitors 60 ) have been reported to affect EV output and cargo loading in various cell types. These effects may reflect alterations in intracellular signaling pathways that converge on the ESCRT machinery or influence EV packaging of specific proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. 58 , 59 , 60 , 61 Cardiovascular patients are frequently exposed to such agents; a deeper understanding of drug‐EV interactions is essential for accurate interpretation of preclinical MI models and for the development of clinically translatable exosome‐based therapies.

Nutritional factors

Beyond traditional drugs, nutritional factors play a crucial role in reshaping exosomal composition and delivery target. Papini et al. 62 reported that sulforaphane (SFN), a bioactive isothiocyanate found in cruciferous vegetables, can change the secretome of fibroblasts, which are the most common type of non‐myocyte cells in the heart. These cells usually release exosomes that don't protect the heart very well. Low‐dose SFN treatment induces the release of SFN‐F‐EXOs (sulforaphane‐induced fibroblast exosomes) characterized by a specific protein signature, including increased levels of HSP90 and the chaperone sacsin. Significantly, SFN‐priming enhances the cardiomyocyte‐targeting efficiency of these vesicles. When administered in vivo, these nutritionally modulated exosomes significantly reduce infarct size and attenuate adverse cardiac remodeling by inhibiting cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and restoring histone acetylation balance (e.g., increasing AcH4 levels). 62 These findings highlight a transition to “secretome engineering,” where food and pharmaceuticals are utilized to enhance the endogenous production of therapeutic EVs, providing a highly applicable approach for improving cardiac healing in MI models.

ROUTES OF EXOSOME ADMINISTRATION IN PRECLINICAL MODELS OF MYOCARDIAL INFARCTION

Exosomes can be administered to the heart through several delivery routes (Table 4). In small‐animal models of MI, local delivery via intramyocardial (IM) injection is generally preferred over systemic administration, as it allows for the direct deposition of exosomes at the injury site, resulting in improved therapeutic efficacy and enhanced cardiac retention. 63 IM injection has been utilized in several investigations, with considerable differences in both the injection site and time. The border zone of the infarcted heart is the primary target area, as it has a substantial population of viable cardiomyocytes that can react to exosome‐mediated cardioprotection. 64 The quantity of injection sites varies, often between two and five, to provide a broader distribution of exosomes throughout the ischemic myocardium. 42 , 65 , 66 , 67 , 68 A single IM injection of exosomes is the most common experimental approach. This injection is administered either immediately post‐ MI or before reperfusion to mitigate acute myocardial injury and early inflammatory responses. 69

Nevertheless, rapid dispersion and poor preservation of exosomes within the cardiac tissue are noteworthy disadvantages of a single IM injection. To improve local retention and prolong the therapeutic advantages of exosomes in the infarcted myocardium, recent research has implemented hydrogel‐ or scaffold‐based technologies as slow‐release carriers. 30 , 70 Conversely, systemic administration requires higher dosages to attain equivalent therapeutic results. Previous studies demonstrate that unmodified exosomes administered intravenously (IV) are rapidly cleared by primary organs, notably the liver, lungs, and spleen, 71 due to off‐target biodistribution and absorption by the reticuloendothelial system. 72 Recent techniques, including surface modification, exosome engineering, and biomaterial‐assisted delivery, have been developed to solve these challenges by enhancing heart targeting, prolonging circulation duration, and improving treatment effects in systemic administration models. 73 Numerous studies have successfully employed intravenously (IV) systemic injection of exosomes engineered to generate cardiac‐homing peptides or specific ligands that target damaged myocardium, resulting in improved localization and enhanced cardioprotective effects. 43 , 52 , 74 , 75 Monocyte membrane‐coated MSC‐derived exosomes 74 facilitate targeted administration by utilizing the natural recruitment of monocytes to the infarcted myocardium following an MI. 76 Moreover, biomaterial‐assisted delivery systems, including hydrogels, 30 sprays, 77 porous scaffolds, 70 and cardiac patches, 29 have emerged as effective strategies to improve exosome bioavailability. These methods provide prolonged release, enhanced local retention, protection against enzymatic degradation, and rapid clearance; therefore, they enhance the therapeutic efficacy of exosome‐based treatments for MI.

Inhalable therapy

To overcome the limitations of traditional delivery methods, such as intramyocardial (IM) injection and intravenous (IV) injection, the inhalation therapy can be delivered using exosomes with a non‐invasive and repeatable dosage. 78 Li et al. 25 effectively administered lung spheroid cell‐derived exosomes (LSC‐Exos) by nebulization, marking the first report of inhalation‐mediated exosome treatment for myocardial repair. This method enables exosomes to traverse the alveolar–capillary barrier, enter the circulatory system, and inherently travel to the damaged cardiac tissue. Moreover, repeated inhalation over seven consecutive days produced strong cardioprotective effects in both mouse and swine models, without causing any detectable toxicity, inflammation, or dysfunction in major organs such as the lungs, liver, or kidneys. This innovative approach broadens the possibilities for cardiac repair and represents an important step toward safe, patient‐friendly, and clinically applicable exosome‐based treatments for ischemic heart disease. 25

Microneedle patches

Intramyocardial injection of exosomes can enhance cardiac regeneration by facilitating cardiomyocyte proliferation and viability. 79 Nonetheless, the short half‐life and poor retention of exosomes in the heart typically lead to constrained therapeutic efficacy when directly injected for the treatment of MI. Exosomes are often eliminated and become undetectable within 3 h after injection. 80 Using biomaterials as carriers can help deliver exosomes directly to the infarcted area, improving their retention and overcoming this challenge. MN patches, which consist of small needles, have become known as a painless and efficient way to deliver a variety of transdermal medications. 81 , 82 These patches have been extensively utilized for treating both localized and systemic diseases through the administration of pharmaceuticals and bioactive compounds. 83 In the MI model, the implantation of MN patches into the infarcted heart significantly enhances the retention of exosomes inside the compromised myocardium, therefore mitigating inflammation, reducing infarct size, preventing fibrosis, and improving cardiac function. This method shows that MN patches can be used to deliver exosomes that contain anti‐fibrotic agents to prevent cardiac fibrosis. This could be a new way to treat myocardial infarction in the clinic. 83 MN patches have conventionally been utilized to provide therapeutic drugs or exosomes directly to the heart, enhancing local retention and enabling controlled release. 84 , 85 , 86 , 87 Despite demonstrating promising outcomes in preclinical research, the practical use of these patches has limitations due to the highly invasive open‐chest surgery, which involves thoracotomy and suturing to the cardiac surface. 88 , 89

Porous scaffold

One of the major limitations of direct exosome injection is their short half‐life and rapid clearance from the body, which significantly reduces their therapeutic efficacy. 90 Thus, repeated administrations are often required to maintain a therapeutic concentration of EVs at the target site. 78 To overcome this challenge, a porous biomaterial scaffold is a local, long‐term bioreactor capable of continuously generating and releasing functional exosomes over extended periods from weeks to months, thereby minimizing the need for frequent dosing. A particularly promising design is a biodegradable and biocompatible microtube array membrane that is composed of poly(lactic‐co‐glycolic acid) (PLGA) and poly(l‐lactic acid) (PLLA). 91 This scaffold features surface pores of approximately 800 nm, small enough to limit host cell infiltration, yet sufficiently large to permit the controlled diffusion of secreted EVs and exosomes. 70 This innovative platform not only improves the local retention and sustained release of therapeutic vesicles but also replicates a native microenvironment, thereby fostering cell viability and continuous exosome secretion. This could significantly advance the clinical translation of EV‐based cardiac therapies. 70

Shear‐thinning gel

The shear‐thinning gel (STG) is a practical and innovative method for enhancing the immune‐compatibility and storage stability of exosome‐based therapies for MI. 30 The primary objective of this strategy is to facilitate the transition of exosome therapy to a clinical solution that is readily available for storage, distribution, and administration to patients without the necessity for individualized preparation or the risk of immune rejection. EVs stored at −80°C for 2 months retained their structural integrity, protein cargo including angiogenic growth factors, exosomal surface indicators such as CD9, CD63, and CD81, and total nucleic acid content. 30 , 92 Moreover, the application of allogeneic extracellular vesicles, pooled from several donors, has shown little immune activation to cardiac tissue, proving their non‐immunogenic and biocompatible characteristics. 93 , 94 These data indicate that exosomes can be synthesized into safe, ready‐to‐use biologics appropriate for emergency or large‐scale therapeutic usage. Regarding effectiveness, both fresh and cryopreserved EVs administered via the STG exhibited similar cardioprotective effects at 4 weeks post‐MI, including improved contractility, enhanced angiogenesis, and maintenance of scar thickness compared to control groups. 95 , 96 The STG is an injectable hydrogel characterized by shear‐thinning properties; it transforms into a liquid state under applied force (such as during injection) and swiftly reverts to a stable gel upon removal of the force. 97 This unique characteristic enables precise, less invasive administration while ensuring targeted and prolonged release of exosomes inside the infarcted myocardium. 95 , 96 The STG overcomes the challenges of poor retention and quick clearance and provides a therapeutically applicable approach to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of exosome treatment in heart regeneration. 30 , 95 , 96 In contrast to conventional IM injections or cardiac patches, 83 which are either invasive or limited by poor delivery control, the STG system effectively combines the benefits of injectability, sustained release, and biocompatibility, representing a notable advancement toward scalable, patient‐friendly exosome therapies for IHD.

Spray

Invasive techniques may induce surgical injuries, inflammation, and extended recovery periods, restricting their feasibility for clinical use. A minimally invasive exosome spray technique has recently been developed to overcome these challenges. 88 , 89 Exosome spray is a double‐lumen syringe that consists of a fibrinogen carrier that carries the exosomes to the heart, and thrombin reacts with the fibrinogen to turn the liquid spray into a sticky gel patch on the heart surface. To perform minimally invasive regenerative therapy, small incisions are made, and the thoracoscope‐guided exosome spray delivery is performed. In comparison to direct intramyocardial injection, exosome spray has been demonstrated to enhance exosome retention on the myocardium after 24 h, resulting in lower inflammatory responses and less surgical stress in swine models of MI, as well as reduced neutrophil and white blood cell counts. This innovative method provides a more clinically practicable, safer, and simpler method of delivering exosomes for cardiac repair, indicating an optimistic advancement in the minimally invasive regenerative therapy. 77

CLINICAL TRANSLATION OF EV THERAPIES: CURRENT STATUS, TOXICITY, AND IMMUNOGENICITY

Initial generations of cell‐based treatments, such as cardiac stem cells and cardiosphere‐derived cells, successfully entered phase I/II clinical trials for ischemic cardiomyopathy; however, their clinical efficacy has often been variable due to poor cell retention and survival. 99 , 100 The therapeutic advantages of these cells are mostly provided by paracrine pathways; the research is shifting toward cell‐free extracellular vesicle and exosome treatments. 66 Although large‐scale phase I/II clinical trials specifically investigating purified exosome formulations for MI remain in the preliminary phases of clinical translation, lung spheroid cell‐derived exosomes (LSC‐Exos), which show significant cardioprotective and regenerative properties, have obtained Investigational New Drug (IND) approval from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to enter phase I clinical trials for severe respiratory conditions (IND18218; IND19942). 25 This can pave the way for prospective IND applications targeted at treating acute cardiac injury. Furthermore, preliminary clinical data from other fields, such as oncology, have already shown that the systemic delivery of exosomes in humans is practical and safe. 101

The safety profile of exosomes in terms of systemic toxicity and immunological rejection has been a key factor for the shift from cell‐based to exosome‐based therapeutics. 102 In contrast to live stem cell transplantation, cell‐free exosomes overcome major safety challenges, such as the risks of uncontrolled cellular proliferation, tumorigenesis, and pro‐arrhythmias. 74 Furthermore, stem cell‐derived exosomes are naturally immune‐tolerant and have low intrinsic immunogenicity, which is an essential advantage for their clinical use as “off‐the‐shelf” allogeneic therapies. 29 , 68 EVs derived from progenitor cells typically lack major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II molecules, protecting them from complement‐mediated lysis and cytotoxic T‐cell elimination. 30 Therefore, the exosomes pooled from many allogeneic donors have shown biocompatibility. The localized administration of allogeneic EVs through hydrogels into injured myocardium results in minimal inflammatory cell infiltration and no significant adverse immunological responses or cellular mortality. 30

Regarding systemic toxicity, numerous biosafety evaluations across various administration methods have repeatedly validated their safety. In both rodent and large animal (swine) models of MI, the repeated administration of exosomes (e.g., through inhalation or epicardial spray) did not produce any detectable systemic inflammation, abnormal elevation of white blood cells, or toxic dysfunction in major clearance organs, including the liver, lungs, kidneys, and spleen. 25 , 77 The lack of MHC class II expression and systemic toxicity provides exosome‐based therapeutics exceptionally safe, scalable, and non‐immunogenic choices for future human cardiovascular studies.

CHALLENGES AND STRATEGIES IN CLINICAL TRANSLATION

Isolation purity, preparation heterogeneity, and nanoparticle tracking analysis pitfalls

Despite EV‐based therapeutics providing cardioprotective effects in preclinical studies, their translation into clinical settings remains challenging. A major limitation of current EV research is the purity of isolation. For instance, although commercial polymer‐based precipitation kits (e.g., ExoQuick, Invitrogen) are frequently used due to their high yield and ease of use, they show low specificity and are prone to co‐precipitating non‐vesicular proteins. Thus, therapeutic effects reported in studies utilizing low‐purity methods cannot be definitively attributed to EVs alone, as co‐isolated cytokines, growth factors, or protein complexes may affect the observed myocardial repair. 28

Furthermore, the heterogeneity of exosome preparations provides a challenge to uniformity. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) is the most widely used for quantifying EV concentration; however, NTA measures all light‐scattering nanoparticles within a specific size range but is unable to differentiate between EVs and non‐vesicular contaminants. Consequently, these preparations frequently contain several undesirable substances detectable by NTA, such as protein aggregates and lipoproteins, which may significantly affect EV concentration measurements and lead to incorrect results. To verify therapeutic claims, further research must adhere to MISEV criteria, combining NTA with morphological (e.g., electron microscopy) and biochemical characterizations to confirm that the detected cardioprotection is specific to EVs. 105

Batch‐to‐batch variability

The limited production scale results in batch‐to‐batch variation, providing major regulatory and manufacturing challenges for clinical translation. 24 The molecular cargo of exosomes (e.g., miRNAs, proteins) is highly dynamic and is profoundly influenced by the cellular origin and the specific microenvironment of the parent cells. Variables such as cell passage number, donor age and health, seeding density, and the specific composition of the culture medium (including EV‐depleted FBS) can all alter EV yield and bioactivity. 106 The clinical reliability of “off‐the‐shelf” EV therapies will remain limited by batch‐to‐batch variability until scalable and reproducible production methods, such as immortalized clonal cell lines, are developed. 107

Animal models and chronic post‐MI remodeling

A significant barrier to therapeutic translation is relying on animal models that fail to accurately recapitulate human cardiovascular pathophysiology. The majority of research investigates the effectiveness of EVs in small rodent models using acute ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) or permanent coronary ligation, often with short‐term follow‐ups of 28 days. Although these models help understand acute anti‐apoptotic and anti‐inflammatory pathways, they do not adequately mimic the chronic, progressive characteristics of severe left ventricular remodeling and heart failure observed in actual myocardial infarction survivors. 55 Moreover, the majority of pre‐clinical models use young, healthy animals, excluding the essential systemic setting of human patients who often have comorbidities such as advanced age, atherosclerosis, and diabetes, which are known to influence both endogenous EV function and the myocardial response to treatment. 54 , 108 To bridge the translational gap, further studies must evaluate EV therapy in large animal models (e.g., pig models) with long‐term (about 6 months) functional follow‐ups to verify if acute EV administration may effectively arrest or reverse chronic fibrotic remodeling and end‐stage heart failure. 55

CONCLUSIONS AND OUTLOOK

This review highlights the potential of EVs, particularly exosomes, as innovative, cell‐free therapeutics for IHD. Preclinical studies show that exosomes derived from different sources, such as stem cells and mesenchymal stem cells, provide significant cardioprotective effects. Advancements in delivery technology, including shear‐thinning hydrogels, microneedle patches, and inhalation, have solved the challenges of rapid clearance and poor myocardial retention related to conventional administration methods.

Despite these translational advancements, clinical usage remains limited by a continuing standardization gap. A major limitation is the lack of standardized high‐purity isolation and characterization techniques. High‐yield precipitation techniques often co‐isolate non‐vesicular proteins and lipoproteins, challenging mechanistic attribution and leading to significant batch‐to‐batch variability. In parallel, most preclinical studies use young, healthy rodent models with a short follow‐up period, which inadequately reflect the chronic post‐myocardial infarction remodeling process and the complex inflammation of elderly patients and metabolic comorbidities.

To bridge these gaps, future research must prioritize surface engineering strategies, such as the incorporation of cardiac‐homing peptides, to enhance organ‐specific tropism following systemic delivery. Additionally, validation is required in large‐animal models (e.g., swine) with human‐like cardiac anatomy, incorporating chronic comorbidities and prolonged functional and safety assessments. Furthermore, scalable and reproducible manufacturing platforms are essential for regulatory compliance and must adhere to the MISEV guidelines to ensure consistent dosing and characterization before performing clinical trials, especially for “off‐the‐shelf” exosome‐based cardiovascular therapeutics.
