Knowledge, practices, perceived challenges, and factors associated with menstrual hygiene among adolescent school girls in southern Sri Lanka
Authors: D M Y W K Dissanayaka, W M P A F Balapitiya, S M T D Sundarapperuma, P N Kariyawasam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMenstrual hygiene is essential for adolescent girls, as it greatly affects their capacity to engage in academic and extracurricular activities. Adolescent girls often encounter various challenges in maintaining proper menstrual hygiene, making it essential to explore these challenges to design effective future interventions. Therefore, this study was conducted to assess the knowledge, practices, perceived challenges, and factors associated with menstrual hygiene among adolescent school girls in Galle Educational Zone in southern Sri Lanka.MethodsThis study employed a mixed-methods design, utilizing a sequential explanatory strategy, comprising a quantitative descriptive component followed by an exploratory descriptive qualitative component. The study was conducted with the participation of 389 girls in the Galle educational division using the multi-stage cluster sampling method in 2024. A validated, modified, self-administered questionnaire was used to collect data. Descriptive statistics, Chi-square test, independent sample t-test, one-way ANOVA, and multiple linear regression were used based on the type of variable. For the qualitative component, in-depth interviews were conducted with 15 purposively selected school girls. The interviews were transcribed verbatim, and the data were coded systematically to identify recurring patterns and themes. The ethical approval was obtained from the ethics review committee Faculty of Allied Health Sciences, University of Ruhuna.ResultsAmong the participants, only 32.7% (n = 125) had satisfactory knowledge, and only 17.3% (n = 66) had a satisfactory level of practice on menstrual hygiene. Knowledge on menstruation was associated with menstrual hygiene practices (p = 0.015), information received from the media (p = 0.010), experience on menstruation (p = 0.025), mother’s level of education (p < 0.001) and father’s level of education (p < 0.001). Hygiene practices on menstruation were associated with the type of family (p = 0.023). In the qualitative component, under the perceived challenges, four sub-themes emerged: comfort and dignity, socio-cultural taboos, physical constraints, and insufficient resources.ConclusionThe participants' overall knowledge on menstruation and menstrual hygiene was inadequate. Key factors influencing menstrual hygiene included parents' level of education, family structure, and prior experience with menstruation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28255-7.Keywords: Hygiene practices, Knowledge, Menstruation, Perceived challenges

Background

Menstruation is a natural physiological process involving the monthly vaginal discharge of blood and other body fluids. The menstrual cycle typically lasts about 28 days, varying among individuals, and spans from menarche to menopause, excluding pregnancy [1]. Effective menstrual hygiene management (MHM) is essential for the health and well-being of adolescent girls. According to UNICEF [2], MHM includes the use of clean menstrual management materials, regular washing of the body with soap and water, and access to appropriate facilities for the safe disposal of used materials. Similarly, the WHO/UNICEF Joint Program [3] emphasized that women and adolescent girls should have access to clean menstrual materials, privacy for changing, safe disposal facilities, and accurate knowledge to manage menstruation with dignity and without fear or discomfort.

Globally, poor MHM remains a significant challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Studies estimate that at least 500 million women and girls lack access to adequate menstrual hygiene facilities [2]. In such contexts, inadequate MHM contributes to school absenteeism, with girls missing up to 20–25% of school days annually due to menstruation-related challenges [4]. These challenges are compounded by cultural taboos, stigma, and misinformation surrounding menstruation, which negatively impact the physical and psychological well-being of adolescent girls [5]. Poor MHM has also been linked to adverse health outcomes, including reproductive tract infections (RTIs) and urinary tract infections (UTIs) [6].

A previous study identified that the mean age at menarche among Sri Lankan girls is 11.2 years, with over 90% attaining menarche by the age of 14 [7]. Accordingly, girls aged 14–15 years are particularly vulnerable to menstruation-related challenges, as this period represents a critical transitional stage of adolescence. Girls aged 14–15 years often face numerous challenges during this age, particularly after menarche, bringing significant physical, emotional, and social adjustments. A significant proportion of adolescent girls only learn about menstruation after menarche, often through inadequate and inaccurate sources, leading to poor knowledge and hygiene practices [8]. Moreover, a lack of family support, accidental soiling of clothing, myths about menstruation, the high cost of menstrual products, and inadequate water and sanitation facilities at schools and houses further hinder effective MHM. In Sri Lanka, menstruation remains a socially sensitive topic due to deeply rooted cultural and religious beliefs. These challenges disproportionately affect school girls and contribute to persistent gender inequities in education, participation in extracurricular activities, and health outcomes [9].

Several studies have been conducted in the Sri Lankan context on menstrual hygiene. A study conducted among adolescent girls in Kalutara District identified inadequate knowledge regarding menstruation and menstrual hygiene practices [10]. In contrast, a study conducted in the Northern Province in 2024 to assess menstrual hygiene practices reported satisfactory hygiene behaviors, including daily bathing and cleansing of the genital area before sleep [11]. Similarly, a study conducted in Kegalle District identified inadequate facilities for sanitary napkin disposal and limited access to washing facilities as major barriers to effective menstrual hygiene management [12]. However, research focusing on the Southern Province, particularly the Galle educational division, remains limited. As a major urban center in southern Sri Lanka with considerable ethnic and religious diversity, Galle presents a unique context in which cultural and socioeconomic factors may influence MHM. Sri Lanka’s ongoing economic crisis has adversely impacted access to essential menstrual hygiene products. Rising prices of menstrual absorbents and limitations in school-based facilities disproportionately affect economically vulnerable populations, further intensifying MHM challenges. These issues highlight the need for localized research to identify context-specific gaps to guide targeted interventions. Previous studies indicate that menstrual MHM is influenced by factors such as ethnicity, religion, parental education, family structure, and socioeconomic status [13, 14]. Yet, qualitative insights into the challenges faced by adolescent girls in managing menstruation remain limited. Exploring these challenges is crucial for designing effective, context-specific interventions that address both practical and psychosocial barriers.

This study was conducted to assess the knowledge, practices, and factors associated with menstrual hygiene among adolescent school girls in Galle Educational Zone in southern Sri Lanka. Furthermore, to support the quantitative study findings, a qualitative component was conducted to explore the perceived challenges faced by adolescent girls in managing menstrual hygiene. The findings of this study will provide valuable insights to guide the development of targeted awareness programs, improve menstrual health support systems in schools, and foster supportive family and community environments. Such efforts are essential to enhance reproductive health, prevent complications, and improve the participation of adolescent girls in social, educational, and extracurricular activities.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study adopted a mixed-methods design with a sequential explanatory approach. Initially, a descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted, followed by an exploratory qualitative phase to complement the quantitative findings and to further investigate challenges related to menstrual hygiene management. During the interpretation phase, qualitative themes were used to explain the key findings observed in the quantitative data.

This study was conducted in Galle, the main city in Southern Sri Lanka, which is rich in religious and cultural diversity. The Galle Educational Division includes 31 schools, comprising mixed and girls’ schools with culturally and religiously diverse female adolescent populations.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sample size was calculated using the formula [15], based on a knowledge proportion of 63.9% from a previous study [10]. An additional 10% was included to account for potential non-response, resulting in a minimum required sample size of 389. Based on the practices, the calculated sample size was 240. Therefore, we have taken the largest sample size for this study. We used the stratified multi-stage cluster sampling method with probability proportionate to size. First, all government schools in the Galle Educational Division were identified and stratified by school type according to the Sri Lankan school categorization: Schools were stratified by educational level (primary and secondary), administrative category (national and provincial), and gender composition (girls’ and mixed schools) to ensure adequate representation. Government schools were subsequently selected from each stratum. Within each selected school, the number of students to be sampled was determined using probability proportional to the school size. Subsequently, students were randomly selected from each school until the required sample size was achieved.

The inclusion criteria were female students aged 14–15 years enrolled in government schools who could read and write in Sinhala or English. Exclusion criteria included students who had not attained puberty, were absent during the data collection period, had disabilities, or had significant pain at the time of data collection (moderate or severe pain that interferes with data collection) or other notable physical health conditions.

For the qualitative component, in-depth interviews were conducted with 15 girls from purposively selected schools until data saturation was achieved. The same inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied as in the quantitative component. Participants were selected using purposive sampling and included girls aged 14–15 years, with the aim of ensuring representation across diverse cultural, ethnic, religious, and socio-economic backgrounds.

Study variables and data collection tools

The primary outcome variables of the study were menstrual hygiene knowledge and menstrual hygiene practices among adolescent school girls. Independent variables included socio-demographic and contextual factors such as ethnicity, religion, type of family, type of school, mother’s education level, prior experience, and awareness of menstruation. These variables were collected using a structured questionnaire. To assess the knowledge and practices, data were collected using a modified, previously validated, self-administered questionnaire, which was developed and checked for reliability using the Cronbach’s alpha of 0.78 and internal consistency and inter-rater reliability using the Kappa coefficient of 0.77. Content validity and pre-testing were done by the initial developer, covering socio-demographic details, general knowledge of menstruation, menstrual hygiene knowledge, and hygiene practices [10] [Additional file 1]. Knowledge was categorised as “satisfactory” with a cut-off score of ≥ 50%, while practices were assessed using five key indicators, with a total score of ≥ 3 (60%) considered satisfactory according to the previous study done in Kalutara district, Sri Lanka [10].

Before administration, the data collection tool was pre-tested with 20 girls from the same age group, recruited from a school not included in the main study. The clarity and feasibility of the questionnaire were assessed during the pre-testing.

Qualitative data were gathered through in-depth interviews to explore students’ experiences and challenges related to menstrual hygiene. An interview guide, developed based on relevant literature, included five open-ended questions with follow-up probes. To ensure dependability, the guide was reviewed by an experienced qualitative researcher. Fifteen participants were purposively selected to ensure maximum variation in terms of ethnic, religious, and socioeconomic backgrounds, thereby enhancing the transferability of findings. Face-to-face, in-depth interviews were conducted individually in private settings, each lasting approximately 20–30 min. Data collection continued until data saturation was achieved, as described by Saunders et al. [16]. At the end of each interview, the main points expressed by the participant were reviewed and summarized to enhance credibility. The responses of the participants were audio-recorded with consent and transcribed verbatim for analysis. To ensure dependability and confirmability, transcripts were reviewed multiple times, and coding was performed systematically. Additionally, peer debriefing with a qualitative research expert was conducted to validate emerging themes and interpretations.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee, Faculty of Allied Health Sciences, University of Ruhuna (ERC reference No: 2023.11.339), and written informed consent was obtained from the parents (legal guardians), and written assent was obtained from the girls individually before data collection.

Data analysis

The collected quantitative data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, as appropriate. Inferential statistical analyses were conducted to examine associations between menstrual hygiene knowledge and practices and selected socio-demographic and contextual factors. The independent samples t-test and one-way ANOVA were used to compare mean differences between groups for continuous variables, while the Chi-square test was applied to assess associations between categorical variables. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify the independent predictors of knowledge on menstrual hygiene among schoolgirls. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Qualitative data were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using thematic analysis, following the six-step framework proposed by Clarke and Braun [17]. This process involved familiarization with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the final report. The verbatim transcription ensured the authenticity of participants’ responses, while the systematic approach facilitated the identification of key themes and patterns.

Results

Descriptive cross-sectional study

Socio-demographic characteristics of the participants

Out of 389 eligible school girls, 382 (98.2%) responded to the survey. The sample comprised 14-year-old (50.3%) and 15-year-old (49.7%) school girls. Most participants were of Sinhalese ethnicity (90.3%, n = 345), followed by Muslim (8.1%, n = 31) and Tamil (1.6%, n = 6). Regarding religious affiliation, 83.5% (n = 319) identified as Buddhist, 8.1% (n = 31) as Muslim, 7.1% (n = 27) as Christian/Catholic, and 1.3% (n = 5) as Hindu. Regarding parental education, 29.8% of mothers had completed the General Certificate of Education Ordinary Level (GCE O/L), while 27.7% of fathers had completed the General Certificate of Education Advanced Level (GCE A/L). The majority of participants belonged to nuclear families (76.7%).

Knowledge on menstruation

Most participants (74.6%, n = 285) had been menstruating for more than one year, and 68.1% reported having prior awareness of menstruation. Knowledge about menstruation was assessed and categorized as either “satisfactory” (≥ 50% score) or “unsatisfactory.” Overall, 32.7% (n = 125) of participants demonstrated satisfactory knowledge, with a mean knowledge score of 24.77 (± 7.48) (Table 1). Notably, 96.1% of participants stated that they had received information regarding menstrual hygiene from various sources, with mothers being the primary source (56.5%), while science teachers accounted for 12.8%.

Menstrual hygiene practices

Menstrual hygiene was assessed based on five essential practices. Participants who demonstrated at least three satisfactory practices were categorized as having “satisfactory practices.” According to this criterion, only 17.3% (n = 66) of participants had satisfactory hygiene practices. The majority, 82.7% (n = 316), exhibited unsatisfactory practices. The mean score for menstrual hygiene practices was 1.64 (± 0.93). Regarding the type of sanitary napkins used, 48.7% (n = 186) used commercial sanitary napkins, 0.8% (n = 3) used only cloth sanitary napkins, and 50.5% (n = 193) used both.

Among participants, 28.8% (n = 110) changed their sanitary napkins three times per day during the first two days of menstruation, while 37.4% changed them twice per day from the third day onward. Cloth napkins were cleaned with soap and water by 28.8%, and 36.4% disposed of them by burning. The majority (62.0%, n = 237) disposed of commercial sanitary napkins through burning. Notably, 38.7% (n = 148) bathed only after the first three days of menstruation, and 86.9% washed their genitalia with soap and water during each change of sanitary napkins.

Among participants (n = 382), 17.5% (n = 67) maintained a diary to track their menstrual cycle, while 82.5% (n = 315) did not. Additionally, 69.1% (n = 264) were able to predict their next menstruation, whereas 30.9% (n = 118) could not. Additional details on menstrual hygiene practices are provided in Table 2.

A multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify the factors associated with menstrual hygiene knowledge among schoolgirls. The overall model was statistically significant, F (11, 370) = 4.68, p < 0.001, indicating that the set of predictors collectively contributed to the prediction of knowledge scores. The model accounted for approximately 12% of the variance in menstrual hygiene knowledge (R² = 0.12), reflecting a modest effect. Among the predictors, mother’s occupation (B = 3.16, p < 0.001), father’s education (B = 0.73, p = 0.04), and father’s occupation (B = 6.11, p = 0.003) were significantly associated with knowledge scores. Specifically, girls whose mothers were unemployed or whose fathers had lower levels of education or were unemployed tended to have lower knowledge scores. In contrast, no significant predictors were identified by multiple regression analysis in relation to menstrual hygiene practices.

Perceived influence of menstruation on academic and extracurricular activities

Menstruation was reported to affect academic performance in 16.5% of participants (n = 63), while 22.0% (n = 84) reported experiencing discomfort, particularly when seated next to boys. Extracurricular activities were affected in 46.1% of students, suggesting a substantial impact on participation during menstruation (Table 4).

Results of qualitative phenomenological component

Perceived challenges on menstrual hygiene

This section presents the findings of the exploratory descriptive qualitative study exploring the challenges of maintaining menstrual hygiene among schoolgirls in Galle, Sri Lanka. Four sub-themes emerged from the in-depth interviews under the major theme of “challenges”: (1) Comfort and dignity, (2) Socio-cultural taboos, (3) Physical constraints, and (4) Insufficient resources (Fig. 1).

Provides a graphical representation summarizing the identified themes related to the challenges of maintaining menstrual hygiene

Sub-theme 1: comfort and dignity

Most participants emphasized the importance of maintaining genital hygiene during menstruation to ensure future health. Girls frequently changed their sanitary napkins, especially during the first two to three days of menstruation, with many reporting two to three changes per day. However, they faced several challenges while maintaining menstrual hygiene:

“I usually change my pads at least three times a day, especially during the first few days when the flow is heavy. However, at school, it’s difficult to change pads because I have to walk far from the classroom to reach the toilet. So most of the time, I end up not changing them during school hours.” (G1)

Some girls extended hygiene practices to include changing their underwear as well, citing the need for comfort and cleanliness:

“I not only change my pads but also change my underwear three times per day because it makes me feel clean and comfortable.” (G2)

Additionally, most participants reported washing their genital area regularly during pad changes and some of them mentioned that they carry a sanitary kit as well:

“I clean my genital area often, especially when changing pads. I bring a small bag to school with everything I need, and my mother packs it for me during my period.” (G6)

Although sanitary napkins are readily available in Sri Lanka, some girls used cloth pieces at home due to financial constraints:

“Clothes are okay to use when we are at home, but washing and drying them is a problem. They’re not as comfortable as sanitary napkins. When going to school, I use disposable napkins.” (G4)

Moreover, some girls expressed dissatisfaction with cloth use, citing discomfort, leakage, and the burden of maintenance:

“I never use cloth pads because it’s difficult to keep them in place, and they can leak.” (G9)

“If we use clothes, they need to be washed and dried before reuse. It’s time-consuming, so I prefer using pads.” (G3, G4)

Sub-theme 2: socio-cultural taboos

Cultural norms and taboos significantly influenced menstrual practices. Most participants viewed menstruation as a private matter, often confiding only in their mothers:

“I only tell my mother when I have my period; no one else knows. I feel shy to talk about it with others, even my friends. It’s not something we talk about openly. I feel embarrassed to mention it to anyone else.” (G6)

Mothers were often the main source of support, assisting with sanitary products, meals, and relieving girls of household duties:

“My mother brings pads for me, feeds me when I can’t get up, and doesn’t ask me to do any work. She tells me to rest and takes care of everything during my period. But my father never brings pads. He only brings other household items, and I don’t feel like I can ask him to bring them for me. I feel uncomfortable.” (G10)

Pain management practices varied. Some girls used medications such as paracetamol, while others preferred non-pharmacological methods. Some refuse to take medications for pain:

“Sometimes I feel a lot of pain during my period. Even if I take paracetamol, it doesn’t always work. I lie in bed with a pillow under my stomach to ease the pain. Sometimes my mother gives me herbal drinks like coriander water or ginger tea, and I use a warm cloth or hot water bottle on my lower abdomen. These help a little, but the pain still makes it hard to do anything”. (G15)

Cultural beliefs discouraged the use of medication for menstrual pain in some families:

“Mother says paracetamol is not good, it will stop my menses. So I have to bear the pain.” (G6)

Feelings of embarrassment and fear of being teased were common when blood stains were visible, particularly in co-educational settings:

“It’s not easy to wash blood stains on the uniform at school because there are boys around. I feel very shy and uncomfortable. I try to hide the stains as best as I can, but I’m always worried someone will notice and laugh or gossip.” (G8)

“Once, when I was trying to hide a pad in my bag, a boy saw me and said, ‘What are you trying to hide? Did you bring something bad to school?’ I felt so embarrassed, I didn’t know what to say. It made me scared to carry pads openly after that.” (G14).

Bathing practices were also shaped by cultural beliefs, with some girls avoiding full baths in the first few days of menstruation:

“I don’t take a full bath during the first three days of my period. My mother says bathing will stop the blood flow and give me a headache. So I just use a little water quickly to freshen up, but I avoid washing my whole body. I feel uncomfortable, but I’m scared to do anything that might worsen the pain” (G11)

Sub-theme 3: physical constraints

Physical symptoms such as abdominal pain, leg pain, tiredness, and lethargy were frequently reported. These discomforts hindered girls’ ability to attend school or participate in daily activities:

“I have severe lower abdominal pain and feel really tired. I remember one time I came to school even though I was in pain, and the teachers noticed I wasn’t well. They ended up sending me home because I couldn’t even sit properly or focus on the lessons.” (G6)

Physical activity was often restricted during menstruation, with girls avoiding sports or dancing due to discomfort and fear of leakage:

“I don’t engage in sports during my period because wearing pads is uncomfortable. I’m scared it might move or leak if I run or jump. Sometimes I even feel like I need to change in the middle of the activity, and there’s no proper place to do that at school.” (G10)

“I cancel all my classes and just sleep all day until the pain goes away. I can’t study or do anything when it’s that bad. It makes me feel like I’m losing important time, but I really can’t help it.” (G7)

Sub-theme 4: insufficient resources

Participants reported inadequate physical and human resources, especially in school settings. The lack of changing facilities, water supply, and disposal bins made menstrual management difficult:

“It is not possible to change pads in school; most of the time there is no water supply, and also no dustbins to put the pads.” (G9)

As a result, some girls refrained from changing pads at school, trying to avoid staining their uniforms:

“I never change pads in school. I try my best to protect my uniform from blood stains.” (G5)

To cope, girls adopted personal strategies such as lifting uniforms when sitting or wearing dark-colored underwear:

“I lift my uniform before sitting so it doesn’t get blood stains.” (G8)

“I wear black-colored underpants so stains don’t show.” (G4)

In emergencies, girls borrowed sanitary napkins from friends or purchased them from the school canteen:

“I usually bring them to school. If I forget, I borrow from a friend or buy from the canteen.” (G12)

However, even at home, proper disposal remained a challenge for many. Without access to sanitary bins or disposal systems, girls bury used pads as an accepted practice.

“I wrap the used pads and collect them in a plastic bag. Once I finish my menses, I burn them.” (G14)

The qualitative findings provided a deeper understanding of the patterns observed in the quantitative phase. While the quantitative data indicated inadequate overall knowledge and practices related to menstrual hygiene, highlighting the influence of parental education, family structure, and prior menstrual experience, the qualitative themes revealed specific underlying challenges that contributed to these outcomes. For instance, unsatisfactory genital hygiene practices and limited resources explained the observed low levels of hygienic behavior. Additionally, socio-cultural taboos affect some of the MHM practices, such as avoiding bathing for the first three days and tolerating menstrual pain without using analgesics. Further, physical constraints, such as school infrastructure limitations and a lack of privacy, further clarified why students struggled to manage menstruation effectively, regardless of basic awareness.

Discussion

This study revealed that knowledge about menstruation and menstrual hygiene practices was inadequate among 14-15-year-old schoolgirls in the Galle Educational Division. Only 32.7% of students demonstrated a satisfactory level of knowledge, which aligns with findings from a study conducted in the Kalutara district, Sri Lanka, in 2022, where cultural and demographic similarities likely contributed to comparable outcomes [10]. Another study done in India reported similar findings, indicating that adolescent girls have low knowledge regarding menstruation, which is consistent with the findings of the present study [18].

When considering the overall study design, the present study adopted a mixed-methods approach using a sequential explanatory design to gain deeper insights into the findings of the quantitative component. This approach enabled further exploration and explanation of the results obtained from the initial descriptive cross-sectional phase. In contrast, most previously conducted studies in Sri Lanka have primarily utilized descriptive cross-sectional designs, which provide a limited scope of data and lack in-depth explanation of underlying factors and contextual influences [8, 10, 11].

Overall, previous studies both locally and internationally have predominantly employed descriptive cross-sectional designs using structured, self-administered questionnaires for quantitative data collection [18, 19]. While some studies have incorporated mixed-method approaches, qualitative components have commonly relied on focus group discussions. In contrast, the present study utilized a sequential explanatory mixed-method design, integrating quantitative findings with in-depth interviews, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding and deeper insight into the study findings.

Knowledge and hygiene practices of menstruation

The study found that pre-menarche awareness was relatively high (68.1%), primarily due to mothers acting as the main source of information, which aligns with findings in Kalutara [10] and Nepal [19]. However, only 13.4% of students reported receiving menstruation-related information from teachers, highlighting a gap in school-based education, similar to findings in Indian studies [20]. While knowledge levels showed improvement compared to earlier studies in Kalutara (25%). Hygiene practices, such as changing sanitary napkins at least four times a day during the first two days (29.5%), were poor than the study done in Northern Province, Sri Lanka which shows 35.1% of participants changed pads every few hours [11]. However, daily bathing during menstruation (24.6%) was found to be lower than rates reported in Northern Province, Sri Lanka [11], India [20] and Nepal [19], although it was higher than findings from earlier Nepalese studies [21]. Cleaning the genital area with soap and water during each change was a relatively positive finding, showing better adherence compared to studies conducted in other countries [19, 20]. Nonetheless, inadequate hygiene practices such as infrequent bathing and minimal diary-keeping (17.5%) indicate areas requiring improvement.

Perceived influence on academics and extracurricular activities

Menstrual challenges significantly affected academic performance and extracurricular participation, with 46.1% of participants reporting an impact on their academic and extracurricular activities. This proportion was lower than that reported in comparable studies conducted in India [20].

Factors associated with menstrual hygiene

Our findings indicate a significant association between exposure to media and higher levels of menstrual knowledge among female adolescents.

This study found that higher parental education, especially the father’s level of education, was significantly associated with better menstrual hygiene knowledge and practices among adolescent girls. Educated parents are more likely to share accurate information and encourage healthy practices. Evident from qualitative explorations of this study, fathers do not play a notable role in the menstruation and related issues of girls. However, father’s level of education may serve as a proxy indicator of the family’s socioeconomic status in the Sri Lankan context, particularly in households where mothers are not formally employed. The paternal knowledge and socioeconomic status may enhance access to health-related information, support better hygiene practices, and increase the affordability of sanitary products, thereby contributing to improved living standards. Moreover, the type of family structure also emerged as a factor influencing menstrual practices. Adolescents from extended families were more likely to adhere to traditional restrictions, possibly due to the influence of elders who may uphold conservative views on menstruation. In contrast, those from nuclear families showed slightly more liberal practices. A previous study conducted in another district of Sri Lanka similarly found that higher maternal education was associated with better menstrual hygiene practices, while adolescents living in extended family settings tended to have poorer menstrual hygiene, likely due to the influence of traditional norms and restrictions [10]. Further, a study conducted in Nepal also identified that parental education and the size of the family are associated with menstrual hygiene, showing similarities in the South Asian region [21]. This suggests that intergenerational norms and family dynamics may significantly influence menstrual behavior.

The linear regression model explained 12% variance in menstrual hygiene knowledge. The independent variables studied had a modest explanatory effect. This suggests that other unmeasured factors, such as peer influence, school health education, media exposure, cultural beliefs, and access to menstrual hygiene resources, might also contribute to menstrual hygiene knowledge and practices. The father’s education level was more closely associated with knowledge and practice of menstrual hygiene in Sri Lankan context. However, fathers are usually less involved in menstruation-related matters in the current socio-cultural context. Therefore, this association may be an indirect reflection of the socioeconomic status of the household rather than the direct involvement of the father. As many mothers are housewives in Sri Lanka, higher paternal education may be indicative of higher income, better living conditions, better access to health information, and better ability to afford sanitary products, all of which may have a positive influence on menstrual hygiene practices.

Challenges faced when maintaining menstrual hygiene

Physical pain, including lower abdominal pain, tiredness, and lethargy, emerged as significant challenges for participants. These discomforts negatively impacted their ability to concentrate on academics and participate in daily activities. These findings are consistent with studies from Uganda [22] and India [23]. A considerable number of participants refused to take medication for pain relief, opting instead to endure the discomfort, a pattern also observed in Uganda [24].

Socio-cultural beliefs played a notable role in shaping menstrual practices. Many girls avoided activities such as sports and dancing during menstruation due to concerns about increased flow and discomfort, reflecting common myths and taboos observed in other studies. Bathing practices were influenced by cultural beliefs, with many participants avoiding baths during the first three days of menstruation. This practice is comparable with findings from Nepal, where bathing after three to five days is common [25].

A lack of proper resources, including inadequate water supply, garbage disposal facilities, and privacy in school bathrooms, was a major challenge. Many students avoided changing and discarding sanitary pads at school, instead bringing used pads home for disposal. The challenges identified by the qualitative component further elaborate on the findings of the quantitative component, where students showed poor practices while maintaining menstrual hygiene. These findings align with studies from Zambia [24] and Uganda [22]. However, the availability of sanitary napkins in school canteens and peer support for emergency needs were positive findings unique to this study. Poor disposal methods, such as burying or burning used sanitary pads, highlighted inadequate waste management both at home and in schools. Unlike some international studies that identified flushing as a disposal method, this practice was not reported in this study.

Qualitative insights explained the poor menstrual hygiene practices identified in the survey, revealing how cultural taboos, inadequate hygiene infrastructure, and limited resources contribute to the challenges faced by schoolgirls, especially those from less-educated or socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds. Moreover, qualitative findings complemented the quantitative results by providing deeper insight into the suboptimal menstrual hygiene practices observed among schoolgirls. Cultural beliefs and social taboos emerged as significant determinants of MHM, particularly misconceptions regarding bathing during the initial days of menstruation and the perceived negative effects of analgesic use on menstrual flow. Furthermore, mothers were the primary source of menstrual information for most schoolgirls; however, the information received was often inadequate. Maternal knowledge was significantly associated with girls’ knowledge of MHM. These findings underscore the need for strengthened school health programmes that incorporate comprehensive menstrual health education. Integrating evidence-based MHM education into school health services, delivered by trained teachers and supported by healthcare professionals, is essential to address misconceptions, promote healthy practices, and improve the overall well-being of adolescent girls.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Strengths

By integrating both quantitative and qualitative approaches, this study provides a more comprehensive understanding of menstrual hygiene knowledge and practices among schoolgirls. As Sinhala is the medium of instruction in most schools, with a limited number offering English-medium classes, participants from diverse ethnic and religious backgrounds were able to comprehend and respond in either Sinhala or English. This inclusivity enhances the representativeness of the findings and improves their applicability to a broader population within the Galle Educational Division.

Limitations

The cross-sectional design means data were collected at one point in time, which may not capture changes or long-term patterns. The sample size calculation was based only on the knowledge proportion reported in a previous study. The cluster effect was not incorporated into this calculation, as the primary objective was to determine the initial sample size. However, to minimize the potential impact of this limitation, clusters were selected to ensure internal diversity, for example, including students from varying socioeconomic backgrounds within each school. Cultural and religious backgrounds may have influenced some participants’ willingness to disclose personal or sensitive experiences, potentially resulting in incomplete or less detailed responses. As the study relied on self-reported data collected through interviews, there is a risk of recall bias and social desirability response bias, which may have affected the accuracy of the information provided. In addition, the study sample may not fully represent all adolescents within the region, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings.

Although the qualitative component yielded valuable contextual insights, it does not permit causal inferences or broad generalizations. Also, in this study, only experiences were explored as this is a novel study area in Sri Lanka. Moreover, responses could have been affected by social desirability response bias, particularly in relation to hygiene-related practices.

Conclusion

This study reveals significant gaps in menstrual health knowledge and hygiene practices among adolescent girls in the Galle district, with only one-third exhibiting satisfactory knowledge and just one-fifth practicing adequate hygiene. Key factors influencing menstrual hygiene included parents’ level of education, family structure, and prior experience with menstruation. However, factors such as ethnicity, religion, age, and pre-menarche awareness were not significantly associated with menstrual hygiene practices, highlighting the multifaceted nature of the issue. Further, this study reveals how cultural beliefs, stigma, and resource limitations impact adolescent girls’ menstrual experiences, causing discomfort, especially in mixed-gender settings.

Recommendations

The findings highlight the need for well-structured awareness programs to enhance knowledge and practices related to menstrual hygiene, as well as for implementing interventions that address the challenges faced by adolescent girls. Such efforts are essential to minimize the impact of menstruation on education and participation in extracurricular activities. Addressing cultural factors is also important in designing effective menstrual health interventions, which should not only focus on adolescents but also engage caregivers and family members to challenge entrenched taboos and foster a more supportive environment.
