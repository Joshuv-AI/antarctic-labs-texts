Enhancing explainability of random survival forests in predicting stent patency risk for malignant colonic obstruction

Introduction

The application of self-expandable metallic stents (SEMS) has become a widely recognized palliative treatment for patients with malignant colonic obstruction, offering significant advantages such as reduced early complications, improved quality of life, and shorter hospital stays compared to surgical interventions [1, 2]. SEMS also allows for the avoidance of emergency surgery, which is often associated with higher mortality and morbidity​ [3]​. However, despite these advantages, SEMS placement is associated with late complications, particularly stent occlusion, which can lead to severe, even fatal, outcomes [4–6]. Investigations of stent obstruction in upper gastrointestinal disease demonstrate comparable predictive factors, reinforcing the clinical value of applying such models across the spectrum of malignant gastrointestinal strictures [7]. Previous studies in gastroenterology have reported variable outcomes following SEMS placement, with predictors of re-obstruction including tumor length, location, and prior chemotherapy exposure [8, 9]. Incorporating such evidence provides a clinical foundation for applying advanced predictive models to better identify patients at risk of stent failure.

Predictive models have been developed to forecast stent patency, but their interpretability often remains limited. Traditional models such as the Cox proportional hazards (CPH) model provide clear insights into individual risk factors but may fall short in capturing complex interactions among variables. In contrast, machine learning approaches like the Random Survival Forest (RSF) algorithm excel at handling non-linear relationships and high-dimensional data, potentially offering superior predictive performance ​ [10]. However, the opacity of these models—often referred to as the “black box” problem—can hinder their adoption in clinical practice due to a lack of transparency and interpretability [11].

To overcome the challenge of limited interpretability, enhancing the explainability of RSF models is crucial. In the context of machine learning, explainability refers to the extent to which a model’s predictions can be understood and trusted by human users [12]. This involves not only identifying and ranking the importance of input features but also providing a clear rationale for how these features influence the model’s predictions [13]. Several methods have been developed to explain machine learning models, ranging from intrinsic measures like Variable Importance (VIMP) and permutation importance to more advanced techniques such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) [14, 15]​​. Improving RSF interpretability provides clinicians with transparent tools to predict stent patency and optimize patient care.

In this study, we sought to enhance the explainability of the RSF algorithm in predicting stent patency risk among patients with primary malignant colonic obstruction. We applied the RSF model to clinical prognostic data and assessed its performance relative to the CPH model. To interpret these models, we employed a combination of global and local explanation methods, providing a comprehensive understanding of the underlying predictive factors [16].

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective study was conducted at our medical center, focusing on patients with primary malignant colonic obstruction who underwent self-expandable metallic stent (SEMS) placement between September 2014 and October 2023. The study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and predictive factors for stent patency in this patient population. All patients initially underwent SEMS placement with palliative intent for malignant colonic obstruction. Some patients subsequently proceeded to surgical resection after successful stent placement, either as part of a bridge-to-surgery strategy or due to improved clinical condition. For the purposes of this study, both groups were included, as the primary outcome of interest was stent patency rather than long-term oncologic survival. As depicted in Fig. 1, the initial cohort consisted of 354 patients who were considered for inclusion. After applying exclusion criteria, as detailed in Fig. 1, a total of 109 patients with confirmed primary malignant colonic obstruction treated with SEMS as a palliative intervention were included in the final study cohort. The study cohort was randomly divided into a training cohort (n = 77, 70%) and a validation cohort (n = 32, 30%) to develop and validate the prognostic models.

Workflow of the study. This flowchart outlines the study design and the workflow used to develop and validate predictive models for stent patency in patients with malignant colorectal obstruction

The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of our medical center, and due to the retrospective nature of this analysis, the requirement for individual written informed consent was waived. All patient data were anonymized to ensure privacy and confidentiality. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, adhering to ethical standards and safeguarding patient rights.

Data collection

Clinical, laboratory, and procedural data were collected retrospectively from patient medical records. The variables collected included: Demographic parameters: Age, gender, cardiovascular health, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels, and white blood cell (WBC) counts. Therapeutic variables: Treatment history, chemotherapy use pre- and post-SEMS procedure. Tumor characteristics: Tumor location, stage, degree of obstruction (tumor-related luminal narrowing before SEMS placement), maximum diameter of obstructed intestinal tubes (Dmax), and presence of peritoneal metastasis. Interventional practices: Stent expansion rate at day 1 and balloon dilation after SEMS placement.

The primary endpoint was stent patency, defined as the duration from stent placement to the recurrence of symptoms due to stent blockage or migration, confirmed by endoscopy or radiography. Stent patency duration was considered equal to survival time if no symptoms occurred. Technical success was defined as the ability of the self-expanding stent to cover narrow sites over 2 cm at both ends after release. Clinical success was defined as the relief of obstructive symptoms within 48 h post-SEMS procedure.

Feature selection and final model variables

To identify the most critical predictors for stent patency, we combined the results from the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (Lasso) regression feature selection and the RSF variable importance.

Lasso regression identified several significant features with non-zero coefficients at the optimal λ value determined by cross-validation. The RSF algorithm provided a ranking of the top 10 important variables based on their contribution to the model’s prediction accuracy. We compared the top features from both methods and selected the intersection of these features to finalize our model. These variables were selected based on their consistent importance across both feature selection methods.

CPH and RSF models

To develop predictive models, we trained the CPH and RSF models using the final selected variables on the training cohort. The RSF model, a type of ensemble learning technique, is specifically suited for survival analysis as it can handle right-censored data (cases where the exact survival time is unknown) and capture complex interactions among predictors. This approach allows for a nuanced prediction of outcomes by considering multiple factors simultaneously.

The performance of both models was validated on an independent validation cohort. To assess the models’ predictive ability, we calculated Harrell’s concordance index (C-index), the integrated Cumulative/Dynamic Area Under the Curve (C/D AUC), and the integrated Brier score. The C-index is a commonly used metric in survival analysis that indicates the model’s overall ability to rank patients correctly by their predicted survival times. Higher values indicate better predictive accuracy. To evaluate model performance over different time intervals, we used the integrated C/D AUC, which extends the traditional area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve to survival analysis. This metric helps us understand the model’s predictive efficiency across the entire follow-up period, accounting for cases where patients may not have experienced an event (censored data) [17, 18].

The Brier score measures how well predicted survival probabilities match actual outcomes. Lower values indicate better accuracy. The integrated Brier score summarizes model performance over the entire follow-up period. Additionally, we have included a summary table outlining the main steps in our analysis, highlighting the clinical relevance of each metric to enhance accessibility for clinicians who may not specialize in statistics.

Explainability analysis

To improve the interpretability of the RSF model, we assessed four feature importance metrics: VIMP, Permutation Importance, SurvSHAP(t), and SurvLIME.

VIMP, an intrinsic metric provided by the RSF algorithm, quantifies the contribution of each variable to the model’s predictive accuracy, with higher VIMP values indicating more significant roles in the model’s decisions. Permutation Importance was determined by randomly shuffling the values of each feature and observing the corresponding decrease in model performance, measured by metrics such as the Brier score and the integrated C/D AUC. This approach identifies critical features by the extent to which their permutation degrades the model’s predictive power. SHAP values offer a unified measure of feature importance by evaluating each feature’s contribution across all possible combinations of features. LIME, adapted for survival analysis as SurvLIME, was used to understand the local importance of variables for specific predictions. Both SHAP and LIME have been extended to address survival tasks, incorporating time and event variables into their computations, resulting in SurvSHAP [19] and SurvLIME [20]. SurvLIME also provided a comparative visual analysis between the CPH and RSF models, highlighting how individual features influence survival predictions.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies with corresponding percentages. To compare characteristics between the training and validation cohorts, Student t test was employed for normally distributed continuous variables, and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical data. All p-values were calculated using two-sided tests, with a significance level set at 5%. Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1, with the glmnet [21], survival [22], survex [23], and randomForestSRC [24] packages (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; http://www.R-project.org).

Predictive features selection

To develop a robust prognostic signature for predicting stent patency, we employed a combination of RSF and Lasso regression techniques in the training cohort. These methods were utilized to identify the most predictive variables from a comprehensive set of clinical and laboratory features.

Lasso regression was applied to the same dataset to further refine the selection of features. Lasso, known for its ability to handle high-dimensional data and enforce sparsity in the model, identified 11 key features by minimizing the prediction error (Fig. 2A, Figure S1). The RSF model was used to rank the importance of features based on their contribution to the prediction of stent patency. The top-ranked features identified by the RSF algorithm included variables (Fig. 2B). To ensure robustness and reduce the risk of overfitting, we took the intersection of the top features identified by both RSF and Lasso. This intersection included four key variables: Diabetes, CA199, Pre-Chemotherapy and Length of obstruction. The Venn diagram (Fig. 2C) illustrates the overlap between the features selected by RSF and Lasso, with the final prognostic signature comprising the intersecting variables.

Feature selection via Lasso and RSF methods. A Lasso coefficient profiles of the 11 candidate risk factors in the training cohort. B Top 10 risk factors selected using RSF model. C Venn diagram of final selected four feature consistently by both methods

To further mitigate overfitting, we conducted 1,000-fold cross-validation and feature selection on the entire cohort of 109 patients. The results (Figure S2) were consistent with those from the training cohort (Fig. 2), repeatedly highlighting Diabetes, CA199, Pre-chemotherapy, and Length of obstruction as the most stable predictors. This finding supports the stability of our feature selection process despite the modest sample size.

Comparison of CPH and RSF models performance

Using the final four selected features (Diabetes, CA199, Pre-Chemotherapy and Length of obstruction), we developed and compared CPH and RSF prognostic models to predict the risk of stent patency in patients with malignant colonic obstruction using key metrics, including the C-index, integrated C/D AUC, and Brier score. The model was trained using the training cohort and validated on an independent validation cohort shown in the Fig. 3.

The RSF model achieved a higher C-index in the validation cohort (0.743 vs. 0.681 for the CPH model; Fig. 3A), demonstrating superior ability to rank patients by stent occlusion risk over time. The integrated C/D AUC also favored RSF (0.766 vs. 0.744; Fig. 3B), indicating more accurate predictions across all time points. Furthermore, the RSF model had a lower Brier score (0.092 vs. 0.108; Fig. 3C), reflecting better overall accuracy and fewer prediction errors, particularly in later follow-up.

Global and local explanations

We assessed the global explanations of the RSF and CPH models using (VIMP scores and Partial Dependence Plots (PDPs). VIMP, shown in Fig. 4, quantified each variable’s contribution to predictive performance, with Length of Obstruction, Pre-Chemotherapy, and Diabetes consistently emerging as significant predictors of stent patency in both models, though with varying levels of importance. The partial dependence plots (Figure S3-S4) illustrated how individual predictors influenced survival probabilities. Longer obstruction length and higher CA199 levels were consistently associated with reduced stent patency, whereas pre-chemotherapy status showed a positive effect. These relationships were more clearly captured by the RSF model, which demonstrated non-linear effects not evident in the CPH model.

The RSF model shows greater variability and higher importance for certain features compared to the CPH model. B The time-dependent feature importance measured by the loss in one-minus C/D AUC after feature permutation

For local explanations, we employed SurvSHAP(t), SurvLIME, and ceteris paribus profiles to interpret individual predictions (No.12 patient from validation cohort) by analyzing how specific features influenced survival outcomes. As depicted in Fig. 5A, SurvSHAP(t) provided time-dependent insights, showing that in the RSF model, factors like Length of Obstruction and Pre-Chemotherapy status had varying impacts over time, whereas the CPH model maintained a more consistent influence from features such as Diabetes and CA199 levels. That highlights the RSF model’s ability to capture complex, time-dependent interactions among variables, whereas the CPH model’s feature influence remains more consistent. Figure 5B provides the SurvLIME analysis for the CPH model, similar to RSF (Fig. 5C): Diabetes again shows a strong positive contribution to survival probability, Length of Obstruction displays a more complex interaction, with a noticeable negative impact, and CA199 has a smaller, yet still negative influence, while Pre-Chemotherapy status appears to have a slight positive effect. SurvLIME (Fig. 5B and C) underscored the RSF model’s flexibility and superior capability in handling non-linear and time-varying relationships among features.

Ceteris paribus profiles (Figure S5-S6) further illustrated how changes in individual features (No.12 patient from validation cohort) affect survival predictions. The RSF model demonstrated greater sensitivity to variations in features like CA199 levels and Length of Obstruction, reflecting its non-linear modeling approach. Compared with the CPH model, the RSF model showed greater responsiveness to such variations, reflecting its ability to capture complex, non-linear effects and provide more individualized predictions.

Discussion

In this study, we enhanced the explainability and predictive accuracy of the RSF algorithm for predicting stent patency in malignant colonic obstruction. The RSF model outperformed the traditional CPH model, as evidenced by higher C-index and integrated C/D AUC values, offering more accurate risk stratification for stent occlusion. Additionally, the RSF model’s lower Brier score underscores its superior ability to capture complex, non-linear interactions, addressing the “black box” challenge often associated with machine learning models through the application of both global and local explanation techniques, thereby improving its clinical relevance [13, 25].

Our study identified Diabetes, CA199, Pre-Chemotherapy status, and Length of Obstruction as key predictors of stent patency in patients with malignant colonic obstruction. Our findings align with prior gastroenterology literature reporting that factors such as tumor burden, chemotherapy, and metabolic conditions influence SEMS outcomes [26, 27]. By integrating these known predictors into an explainable AI framework, our study expands their clinical applicability, offering a more individualized risk assessment for re-obstruction. Notably, pre-chemotherapy status emerged as a significant factor, highlighting the importance of distinguishing between pre- and post-chemotherapy phases. Pre-chemotherapy was associated with improved stent patency, possibly due to its role in reducing tumor burden before stent placement, consistent with trends observed in previous literature [4, 28]. CA199 levels were also identified as critical, with higher levels indicating a greater risk of stent occlusion. This aligns with other studies linking tumor markers like CA199 and CEA to colorectal cancer outcomes [29]. The length of obstruction was another strong predictor, reinforcing the impact of tumor burden on stent efficacy [30]​. Finally, the association of diabetes with stent patency, though less frequently reported [31], suggests that metabolic conditions may influence outcomes through effects on wound healing and inflammation.

Overall, our findings demonstrate that the RSF model provides a robust tool for predicting stent patency by accounting for complex interactions among multiple variables, thereby supporting a more personalized approach to treatment planning. Additionally, the model has identified key factors influencing stent patency, allowing clinicians to assess the risk of stent reobstruction prior to SEMS placement—a crucial consideration. Although the latest ESGE guidelines recommend stent placement as the preferred treatment for palliative care patients [2], the risk of late complications, particularly stent reobstruction, remains high [6]. For patients who experience reobstruction shortly after placement, this outcome can impose significant physical and psychological strain. Therefore, stenting may not be suitable for all patients, and a preoperative assessment of reobstruction risk could help ensure that SEMS treatment is directed toward those most likely to benefit. Our model not only enables the analysis and prediction of stent patency duration but also helps identify palliative patients who may achieve greater benefit from SEMS placement. While reobstruction can often be managed with a second stent to extend patency time, this approach can also increase treatment risks and costs. Thus, considering the anticipated initial patency duration before opting for SEMS placement is important for optimizing patient outcomes.

The RSF model provides an interpretable framework to identify patient-specific risk factors, enabling tailored discussions about reintervention and follow-up needs. The ability of the RSF model to account for complex interactions and time-dependent effects may enhance personalized predictions, aiding clinicians in preparing patients and caregivers more effectively for potential outcomes. Clinically, recognizing patients at higher risk of early stent failure has practical implications. Those with diabetes or elevated CA199 may require closer surveillance and earlier planning for reintervention, while such risk factors could also inform stent selection or adjunctive therapy. By enabling individualized risk stratification, our model supports more proactive and tailored management of malignant colonic obstruction.

Our findings demonstrate that the RSF model is a robust tool for predicting stent patency by capturing complex interactions among multiple variables, thereby supporting a more personalized approach to treatment planning. However, several limitations should be noted. First, our study was retrospective, single-center, and limited to a Chinese cohort. These factors may restrict generalizability, as variations in stent type, procedural techniques, and access to palliative chemotherapy across institutions and regions could influence outcomes. Future multicenter studies involving more diverse populations are required to validate the model and confirm its external applicability. Second, in patients who died with a patent stent, patency duration was equated with survival time. This definition may overestimate patency by confounding shorter survival with apparently longer stent function, particularly in high-mortality subgroups. Although this approach is common in retrospective studies, it should be interpreted cautiously, and future analyses should consider sensitivity approaches such as censoring at death to refine patency estimates. Third, although stent diameter has been shown to influence patency in prior studies, all patients in our cohort received SEMS with a uniform 25 mm diameter. As a result, this factor could not be evaluated in our analysis. In contrast, stent length exhibited variability and was included as a predictor. This limitation highlights the need for future multicenter studies with heterogeneous stent specifications to better assess the impact of diameter on outcomes. To address concerns about overfitting in a relatively small cohort, we performed additional internal validation. Cross-validation and feature selection across the full dataset consistently identified the same four key predictors, supporting the robustness of our findings. While Diabetes and CA199 did not differ significantly between groups in baseline comparisons, their frequent selection across bootstrap resampling analyses indicates predictive value beyond univariate associations. These results reduce concern about model instability, though validation in larger, multicenter cohorts remains essential.

Other limitations include the potential confounding influence of unaccounted changes in palliative chemotherapy regimens and the limited statistical power in certain subgroups, such as diabetic patients. The observed association between diabetes and stent patency may therefore be coincidental or influenced by other comorbidities. Additionally, the high proportion of censored cases due to mortality and loss to follow-up may complicate long-term outcome interpretation. Expanding future models to incorporate additional biomarkers and perioperative imaging data could further enhance predictive accuracy and clinical utility.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the RSF algorithm, augmented by comprehensive feature importance analyses and advanced interpretability techniques, presents a robust framework for predicting stent patency risk in patients with malignant colonic obstruction. While this model shows promise in assessing risk, further validation through larger, multi-center studies is essential to confirm its efficacy and facilitate reliable clinical adoption.
