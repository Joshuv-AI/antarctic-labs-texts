Plant viruses exploit insect salivary GAPDH to modulate plant defenses
Authors: Xin Wang, Haibo Wu, Zhongkai Yu, Jing Wu, Chengcong Lu, Taiyun Wei, Qian Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Salivary proteins of insect herbivores can suppress plant defenses, but the roles of many remain elusive. One such protein is glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) from the saliva of the Recilia dorsalis (RdGAPDH) leafhopper, which is known to transmit rice gall dwarf virus (RGDV). Here we show that RdGAPDH was loaded into exosomes and released from salivary glands into the rice phloem through an exosomal pathway as R. dorsalis fed. In infected salivary glands of R. dorsalis, the virus upregulated the accumulation and subsequent release of exosomal RdGAPDH into the phloem. Once released, RdGAPDH consumed H2O2 in rice plants owing to its –SH groups reacting with H2O2. This reduction in H2O2 of rice plant facilitated R. dorsalis feeding and consequently promoted RGDV transmission. However, overoxidation of RdGAPDH could cause potential irreversible cytotoxicity to rice plants. In response, rice launched emergency defense by utilizing glutathione to S-glutathionylate the oxidization products of RdGAPDH. This process counteracts the potential cellular damage from RdGAPDH overoxidation, helping plant to maintain a normal phenotype. Additionally, salivary GAPDHs from other hemipterans vectors similarly suppressed H2O2 burst in plants. We propose a strategy by which plant viruses exploit insect salivary proteins to modulate plant defenses, thus enabling sustainable insect feeding and facilitating viral transmission.

Introduction

Most plant arboviruses of agricultural importance are transmitted by hemipterans, such as aphids, planthoppers, leafhoppers, and whiteflies1,2. Viral transmission among plant hosts by insect vectors involves the conflict between insect attack and plant defense that occurs during the insect feeding. Insect feeding activates early defense responses of plants, including reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts3. To induce a broad defense response, early signaling steps connect hormonal signaling networks, like jasmonic acid (JA) signaling and salicylic acid (SA) signaling, which lead to the production of secondary metabolites that enhance plant resistance to insects3,4. To evade the defense response of plant hosts, insects employ various strategies, including the release of salivary effectors, like Fer1 of whitefly and DNase II of small brown planthopper5–8. Some viruses benefit from these strategies to evade or modify plant defenses, ultimately promoting viral transmission and pathogenicity among plant hosts9–12. For instance, geminivirus can induce salivary effector Bsp9 of whitefly vector to suppress immune response of plant host and enhance the insect adaptation for viral transmission9. The modulation of plant defense by virus in insect can be shown as altering insect feeding behavior, affecting host choice preference, prolonging insect lifespan, and increasing insect fecundity, all of which contribute to virus transmission9,13,14. It is believed that plant viruses in insect can exploit several salivary effectors to suppress plant defense against insects, thereby facilitating insect feeding and viral transmission.

Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) has pleiotropic functions beyond its glycolytic role in energy metabolism15. For instance, GAPDH participates in intercellular communication through it being loaded into exosomes16–20. GAPDH is also a good H2O2 sensor owing to its highly reactive cysteine residues with sulfhydryl (−SH) groups, which are sensitive to oxidation by H2O2. In several in vitro biochemical assays, excessive H2O2 has been shown to lead to the oxidation of −SH groups of animal GAPDH, resulting in the formation of sulfenic (–SOH), sulfinic (–SO2H), and sulfonic (–SO3H) acids and even intermolecular disulfide-bonded aggregates in a dimer or even in a polymer form21,22. These disulfide-bonded GAPDH can lead to the formation of insoluble amyloid-like aggregates, which promote cell death at lesion sites in cultured mammalian cells23–26. This oxidation impairs the dehydrogenase activity of GAPDH22. However, this irreversible oxidation can be prevented by the presence of glutathione (GSH) in cells, leading to the formation of S-glutathionylated GAPDH (GAPDH-S-SG) and elimination of the toxicity of disulfide-bonded aggregates of GAPDH21,23,26,27. Animal and plant GAPDHs exhibit similarities in various aspects, such as their sensitivity to H2O2 and their ability to be rescued through glutathionylation28–30. GAPDH is abundant in the saliva of aphids, leafhoppers, planthoppers, whiteflies and psyllids31,32. Despite this previous research, the specific function of salivary GAPDH in these insects remains unknown.

Rice gall dwarf virus (RGDV) is a member of the Phytoreovirus genus in the Reoviridae family33. This virus causes phloem-limited infection in rice hosts and is transmitted by the leafhopper Recilia dorsalis (Hemiptera: Cicadellidae) in a persistent-propagative manner34. The latent period of RGDV in R. dorsalis is ~12–14 days. During infection in salivary gland, RGDV reduces the secretion of salivary calcium-binding proteins to rice plants, thus regulating viral transmission by R. dorsalis13. RGDV also exploits R. dorsalis GAPDH (RdGAPDH) to initiate autophagy in the alimentary canals, which promotes persistent viral infection in R. dorsalis 35. These findings highlight the close association between RGDV and RdGAPDH during viral infection. Previous proteomic data indicate the presence of RdGAPDH in saliva of R. dorsalis. It is hypothesized that RGDV likely takes advantage of salivary RdGAPDH during R. dorsalis feeding to facilitate viral transmission.

In the present work, we show that RGDV exploits salivary RdGAPDH to suppress H2O2 burst in rice host, thereby facilitating RGDV transmission. RdGAPDH suppresses H2O2 accumulation through the consumption of H2O2 during its −SH groups oxidation. However, the overoxidation of RdGAPDH generated disulfide-bonded RdGAPDH dimers, which was potentially cytotoxic. To launch emergency defense to potential cellular damage from overoxidation of RdGAPDH, rice utilizes GSH to S-glutathionylate the oxidization products of RdGAPDH. Salivary GAPDHs from other hemipteran vectors can also suppress H2O2 burst in their respective plant hosts. This suggests a broader phenomenon of H2O2 manipulation by salivary GAPDHs occurs among hemipteran vectors during feeding that facilitates plant pathogen transmission.

Results

RGDV induces release of exosomal RdGAPDH from salivary glands to rice phloem

The release of RdGAPDH to rice plants through R. dorsalis feeding was first investigated. Western blot assays showed that the antibody against RdGAPDH specifically detected RdGAPDH in saliva collected through membrane feeding, as well as in rice plants exposed to RGDV-free R. dorsalis (Fig. 1a). These findings were consistent with our unpublished proteomic data on R. dorsalis saliva, suggesting that RdGAPDH is indeed released along with saliva into rice plants. Notably, this exposure to RGDV-free R. dorsalis did not change the accumulation of rice GAPDH (OsGAPDH), which was specifically detected using the OsGAPDH antibody (Fig. 1a). Next, the manner of RdGAPDH release from salivary glands was examined. The salivary gland cells of leafhoppers contain apical plasmalemma-lined cavities, which store substantial volumes of saliva (Fig. 1b-I). Immunofluorescence assays demonstrated that RdGAPDH was visible as punctate structures within the cytoplasm of salivary cells and cavities of RGDV-free R. dorsalis (Fig. 1b-II). Similar to mammalian exosomal GAPDH, RdGAPDH also partially co-localized with RdRab27a, an exosomal membrane marker, within the cytoplasm of salivary cells and cavities (Fig. 1b, c). These findings suggest that RdGAPDH may be loaded into exosomes that are released into the saliva of RGDV-free R. dorsalis. Partial colocalization of RdGAPDH with RdRab27a in the salivary glands reasonably indicated that some RdGAPDH proteins are enclosed in exosomes, and some also participates in other biological processes like glycolytic pathway, regulation of mRNA stability, autophagy and so on36. Furthermore, western blot assays specifically revealed the presence of RdGAPDH and RdRab27a in the saliva collected from RGDV-free R. dorsalis and in rice leaves exposed to RGDV-free R. dorsalis (Fig. 1a). Immunofluorescence assays also demonstrated the accumulation of RdGAPDH and RdRab27a in the phloem of rice leaves that were exposed to RGDV-free R. dorsalis(Fig. 1d). These findings indicate that RdGAPDH is released into the saliva, likely through exosomes, and ultimately into the rice phloem.

Then RdRab27a gene was knocked down using dsRNA targeting RdRab27a (dsRdRab27a). Both RT-qPCR and western blot assays demonstrated that knockdown of RdRab27a reduced the accumulation of RdRab27a and RdGAPDH in salivary glands, as well as their subsequent release into rice plants (Fig. 1e, f). Additionally, when R. dorsalis was microinjected with GW4869, an inhibitor of exosome biogenesis, the accumulation of RdRab27a and RdGAPDH in the salivary glands was evidently blocked, thus preventing their release into rice seedlings (Fig. 1f). These results indicated that RdGAPDH is loaded into exosomes within the salivary glands and subsequently released into the rice phloem along with saliva.

Next, the effect of RGDV infection on the accumulation and release of salivary RdGAPDH was investigated. Western blot assays showed that RGDV infection increased the expression of RdGAPDH in the salivary glands as well as the release of RdGAPDH into both saliva and rice plants (Fig. 1g). These findings are consistent with previous studies that have shown RGDV infection induces RdGAPDH expression in alimentary canals35. Western blot assays also indicated that RGDV infection increased accumulation of RdRab27a in salivary glands, along with the release of RdRab27a into both saliva and rice plants (Fig. 1g). This suggested that RGDV infection increases the production of exosomes in salivary glands. Immunofluorescence assays showed that RGDV infection led to an increase in the colocalization of RdGAPDH with RdRab27a in the cavities and cytoplasm of salivary glands in RGDV-infected R. dorsalis, compared to RGDV-free R. dorsalis (Fig. 1b, c). It was indicated that RGDV induces more RdGAPDH-containing exosomes in salivary glands. Immunofluorescence assays conducted on rice leaves exposed to RGDV-infected R. dorsalis showed the colocalization of RdGAPDH with P8, which is the outer capsid protein of RGDV and thus indicates the presence of virions, and the colocalization of RdGAPDH with RdRab27a in the phloem (Fig. 1d). Taken together, these results indicate that RGDV infection enhances RdGAPDH accumulation in salivary glands and its release to rice plants through exosomes.

RdGAPDH suppresses H2O2 burst in rice plants

To investigate the role of salivary RdGAPDH in rice plants, we first tested rice plant defense to R. dorsalis after a 12-h exposure. It was found that exposure to R. dorsalis significantly increased the levels of JA, SA, and H2O2 in rice plants (Supplementary Fig. 1b, c). Additionally, the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), a metabolite of H2O2, as well as the activity of peroxidase (POD), an important H2O2-metabolism-related enzyme, were significantly elevated in rice plants exposed to RGDV-free R. dorsalis (Supplementary Fig. 1c). These data suggest that rice defense against R. dorsalis, after a 12-h exposure, involves both H2O2 burst and H2O2 metabolism responses. To further confirm the H2O2 burst in rice plants was caused by exposure to R. dorsalis, the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (H2DCFDA) was utilized to treat rice leaves that were exposed to R. dorsalis for 12 h. The visualization results demonstrated the accumulation of abundant H2O2 at the feeding sites (Supplementary Fig. 1d), further confirming the H2O2 burst in rice plants was caused by exposure to R. dorsalis.

Next, rice defense against R. dorsalis in which RdGAPDH was knocked down using dsRNA targeting RdGAPDH (dsRdGAPDH) was examined. The reduced release of RdGAPDH into rice plants did not have a significant effect on the levels of JA or SA in rice plants, as determined from their exposure to dsRdGAPDH-treated R. dorsalis for 12 h (Fig. 2a–c). However, the reduced release of RdGAPDH into rice plants significantly increased the levels of H2O2 in rice plants exposed to dsRdGAPDH-treated R. dorsalis for 12 h (Fig. 2d). Additionally, both the activity of POD and the level of MDA in rice plants were also significantly increased (Fig. 2d). H2DCFDA staining assays demonstrated a higher accumulation of H2O2 at the feeding sites of rice leaves exposed to dsRdGAPDH-treated R. dorsalis compared to the dsGFP treatment (Fig. 2e). H2DCFDA staining assays also showed the increased number of feeding sites caused by exposure to dsRdGAPDH-treated R. dorsalis (Fig. 2f). It is suggested more frequent probing by these R. dorsalis. Thus, the feeding behavior of dsRdGAPDH-treated R. dorsalis was analyzed using electrical penetration graph (EPG) assays, which detect different stages produced by R. dorsalis during feeding37. These stages contain the stylet probing between mesophyll cells (waveform A), continuous salivation as the stylet penetrates the epidermal tissue and reaches the phloem (waveform S), active ingestion before reaching the phloem (waveform C), passive prolonged ingestion from the phloem (waveform E), an obstacle signal caused by mechanical puncture obstruction, e.g., involving mesophyll cells and sieve tubes (waveform F), the insertion of the stylet into leaf tissue (waveform R) (Supplementary Fig. 2). The EPG assay revealed that the mean duration of stylet probing between mesophyll cells and salivation caused by dsGAPDH treatments was significantly longer compared to the dsGFP-treated control (Fig. 2g). Meanwhile, the mean duration of active ingestion caused by dsGAPDH treatments was significantly shorter than that of the dsGFP-treated control (Fig. 2g). The number of probes under dsGAPDH treatments was significantly higher than that under the dsGFP-treated control (Fig. 2g). These data suggested more difficulties in R. dorsalis feeding under dsGAPDH treatment. These results were consistent with the finding that knocking down RdGAPDH expression of R. dorsalis induced a higher level of H2O2 burst in rice plants (Fig. 2d–f). Taken together, these results indicate RdGAPDH serves as an effector that suppresses H2O2 burst in the rice host, thus facilitating R. dorsalis feeding.

RGDV exploits RdGAPDH effector for transmission to rice plant

Next, we examined the response of the rice host to RGDV-infected R. dorsalis. Previous studies showed that exposure to RGDV-infected R. dorsalis did not cause significant changes in the JA or SA level of rice plants compared to exposure to RGDV-free R. dorsalis13. Therefore, the H2O2 burst of rice plants responding to RGDV-infected R. dorsalis was investigated. Exposure to RGDV-infected R. dorsalis for 12 h significantly increased the levels of both H2O2 and MDA as well as POD activity in rice plants (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Thus, exposure to RGDV-infected R. dorsalis increased the H2O2-related metabolism of rice plants. Immunofluorescence assays conducted on feeding site sections revealed the accumulation of abundant H2O2, RGDV P8, and RdGAPDH in the rice phloem (Fig. 3a). Enhanced H2O2 burst and RdGAPDH accumulation were found at the feeding sites of RGDV-infected R. dorsalis compared to those of RGDV-free R. dorsalis. These findings suggest that rice plants exhibit defense through an H2O2 burst when exposed to RGDV-infected R. dorsalis. H2DCFDA staining assays demonstrated that the number of feeding sites of RGDV-infected R. dorsalis was higher than that of RGDV-free R. dorsalis (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Next, the effect of knocking down RdGAPDH expression on RGDV release was determined. RT-qPCR and western blot assays demonstrated that the knockdown of RdGAPDH significantly reduced P8 accumulation in salivary glands and its release into rice plants (Fig. 3b, c). Exposure to dsRdGAPDH-treated RGDV-infected R. dorsalis resulted in higher levels of H2O2 and MDA, as well as increased POD activity in rice plants compared to dsGFP-treated RGDV-infected R. dorsalis (Fig. 3d). H2DCFDA staining assays displayed greater number of feeding sites of dsRdGAPDH-treated RGDV-infected R. dorsalis in comparison to the dsGFP control (Fig. 3e). These results indicate that RGDV upregulates the RdGAPDH effector to suppress rice plant defense against insects. The greater number of feeding sites under exposure to dsRdGAPDH-treated RGDV-infected R. dorsalis also suggested more frequent probing. Indeed, EPG assays showed increases in the number of probes, duration of probing, and salivation, but a short duration of active ingestion of dsGAPDH-treated RGDV-infected R. dorsalis (Fig. 3f). These results indicated an improvement of difficulty in the feeding of dsGAPDH-treated RGDV-infected R. dorsalis. Therefore, RdGAPDH induced by RGDV facilitates R. dorsalis feeding.

The transmission profile of RGDV by surviving R. dorsalis revealed that the daily transmission rate of alive dsRdGAPDH-treated RGDV-infected R. dorsalis was significantly lower compared to the dsGFP control (Fig. 3g). These data confirm that salivary RdGAPDH facilitates RGDV transmission from R. dorsalis to rice plants because RdGAPDH promotes R. dorsalis feeding. Additionally, the daily survival rate of dsRdGAPDH-treated RGDV-infected R. dorsalis was significantly lower than that of the dsGFP control (Fig. 3h), suggesting that the reduced salivary RdGAPDH decreased the survival of R. dorsalis partly through the suppression of feeding, consequently hindering RGDV transmission by R. dorsalis. These data indicated that RdGAPDH acts as a salivary effector that enhances R. dorsalis feeding, thus promoting RGDV transmission.

–SH groups of cysteine residues in RdGAPDH are active sites of H2O2 oxidation in vivo and in vitro

It is known that excessive H2O2 can oxidize the –SH groups of GAPDH in biochemical assays in vitro, resulting in the formation of –SOH, –SO2H, and –SO3H derivatives of GAPDH, as well as intermolecular disulfide-bonded aggregates of GAPDH (Fig. 5a)22,24,25. Such oxidation can cause GAPDH to lose its dehydrogenase activity22. To understand how RdGAPDH suppresses H2O2 accumulation in rice plants, the protein properties and amino acid sequence of RdGAPDH were analyzed. The RdGAPDH protein consists of 333 amino acids and contains conserved domains of the GAPDH-I superfamily, GapA superfamily, and G3PDH_Arsen superfamily (Supplementary Fig. 5a). The oxidation of RdGAPDH in rice plants exposed to RGDV-free or -infected R. dorsalis for 12 h was analyzed. The dehydrogenase activity of GAPDH in RGDV-infected R. dorsalis was higher than that in RGDV-free R. dorsalis (Supplementary Fig. 5b). The total dehydrogenase activity of GAPDH and the level of –SH groups in rice plants exposed to RGDV-infected R. dorsalis was higher than those is plants exposed to RGDV-free R. dorsalis (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Because GAPDH–SOH possesses acylphosphatase activity, which is proportional to NAD+ content21,22, the generation of GAPDH–SOH was examined through assaying NAD+ content. The acylphosphatase activity of GAPDH–SOH in rice plants exposed to RGDV-infected R. dorsalis was also significantly increased compared to those exposed to RGDV-free R. dorsalis (Supplementary Fig. 5b). This increased dehydrogenase activity, –SH groups and acylphosphatase activity respectively indicated that exposure to RGDV-infected R. dorsalis improved the total GAPDH activity, GAPDH content and oxidation of GAPDH in rice.

Further analysis showed that the dehydrogenase activity of GAPDH, –SH group content, and acylphosphatase activity of GAPDH–SOH in rice plants exposed to dsRdGAPDH-treated RGDV-free or -infected R. dorsalis were significantly lower than those exposed to dsGFP-treated R. dorsalis (Fig. 5b). Additionally, rice plants exposed to dsRNA-treated RGDV-infected R. dorsalis exhibited higher levels of dehydrogenase activity of GAPDH, –SH group content, and acylphosphatase activity of GAPDH–SOH compared to dsRNA-treated RGDV-free R. dorsalis (Fig. 5b). These findings indicate that salivary RdGAPDH is oxidized in rice plants at the –SH groups of its cysteine residues and that RGDV-induced salivary RdGAPDH is subject to stronger oxidation in rice plants. RdGAPDH-OE plants possessed significantly higher dehydrogenase activity of GAPDH, –SH group content, and acylphosphatase activity of GAPDH–SOH compared to the WT plants (Fig. 5c). The exposure of RdGAPDH-OE plants to both RGDV-free and RGDV-infected R. dorsalis also exhibited significantly higher dehydrogenase activity of GAPDH, –SH group content, and acylphosphatase activity of GAPDH–SOH compared to the WT plants (Fig. 5c). These higher dehydrogenase activity of GAPDH and –SH group content respectively indicated high activity and presence of RdGAPDH in rice plants. The data of acylphosphatase activity once again supported the finding that RdGAPDH in rice plants can be oxidized, likely at the –SH groups of its cysteine residues.

Then, the process of RdGAPDH being oxidized by H2O2 was examined in vitro. Prokaryotically expressed RdGAPDH was treated with a series of concentrations of H2O2. As the concentration of H2O2 was increased, the dehydrogenase activity of GAPDH and the –SH content decreased, while the acylphosphatase activity of GAPDH–SOH increased (Fig. 5d). These results indicate that the oxidation of –SH groups causes RdGAPDH to lose its dehydrogenase activity and generate RdGAPDH–SOH. Furthermore, western blot assays demonstrated that as H2O2 concentration increased, the production of intermolecular disulfide-bonded RdGAPDH dimers (68 kDa) increased, while the accumulation of RdGAPDH (36 kDa) decreased (Fig. 5e). These findings reveal that prokaryotically expressed RdGAPDH can be irreversibly overoxidized by excessive H2O2 in vitro. This overoxidation results in RdGAPDH losing its dehydrogenase activity and even forming intermolecular disulfide-bonded dimers.

Next, we investigated whether the oxidation of RdGAPDH by H2O2 occurred at the –SH groups of active-site cysteine residues. To explore this, a His tag-fused RdGAPDH mutant mutated at its active sites, i.e., codons of amino acids 149 and 153, was constructed and referred to as RdGAPDHΔ. After treating RdGAPDH and RdGAPDHΔ with 500 μM H2O2, H2O2 induced generation of disulfide-bonded RdGAPDH dimers but not RdGAPDHΔ (Fig. 5f). These findings suggest that the –SH groups of Cys149 and Cys153 are the key active sites involved in RdGAPDH oxidation in vitro. Then, the stem bases of rice seedlings were separately injected with prokaryotically expressed RdGAPDH and RdGAPDHΔ in equal amounts. After injection, RdGAPDH treatment caused significantly lower levels of H2O2 in rice seedlings but higher levels of acylphosphatase activity of GAPDH–SOH and –SH groups, compared to the RdGAPDHΔ treatment (Fig. 5g). The difference in H2O2 level between His-RdGAPDH and His-RdGAPDHΔ was explained by the consumption of H2O2 owing to the reaction of -SH groups with H2O2. This result suggests that heterologously expressed RdGAPDH, when injected into rice seedlings, suppressed H2O2 by reacting with it. Taken together, these results indicate that the –SH groups at Cys149 and Cys153 are the key active sites of RdGAPDH oxidation by H2O2 in vivo.

Next, we investigated whether prolonged exposure of rice seedlings to R. dorsalis could lead to the formation of disulfide-bonded RdGAPDH dimers. Approximately 50 R. dorsalis leafhoppers were allowed to feed on a single rice seedling at the two-leaf stage for 12, 24, or 48 h. Western blot assays with RdGAPDH antibody detected RdGAPDH in the rice seedlings exposed for 12 h (Fig. 5h). However, after a 24- or 48-h exposure, assays with RdGAPDH antibody could not detect RdGAPDH (36 kDa), but instead detected disulfide-bonded RdGAPDH dimers (Fig. 5h). Additionally, the yield of these disulfide-bonded RdGAPDH dimers (68 kDa) was higher after the 48-h exposure compared to the 24-h exposure (Fig. 5h). These findings indicated that the overoxidation of RdGAPDH by rice H2O2 was enhanced by prolonged exposure duration. Rice seedlings after the exposure 12, 24, and 48 h appeared withered and wilted (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Fig. 6a). Notably, the prolonged exposure duration enhanced these phenotypes (Supplementary Fig. 6a), and even after being separated from R. dorsalis for an additional 48 h, the rice plants after the 48-h exposure had difficulty in recovering. Trypan blue staining showed a significantly higher number of dead cells in rice leaves after the exposure for 12, 24, and 48 h compared to rice leaves without exposure, and the number of death cells was increased from 12-h to 48-h exposure (Fig. 5j). The H2O2 concentration in exposed rice leaves was significantly increased in comparison to rice leaves without exposure (Supplementary Fig. 6b). However, the concentration of induced H2O2 gradually decreased from 12-h to 48-h exposure (Supplementary Fig. 6), indicating a decrease in H2O2-related plant defense against insects. This suggests that the cell death in the withered and wilted rice plants was likely caused by the disulfide-bonded RdGAPDH dimers, but unlikely by the accumulation of H2O2. Given the parallels to studies on animal GAPDH aggregates promoting cell death23–26, it was concluded that disulfide-bonded RdGAPDH dimers likely cause the cytotoxicity in the withered and wilted rice plants. These results also shed light on one of the reasons why prolonged exposure to leafhoppers causes irreversible damage to rice plants at the protein and cellular levels.

Rice plants exploit GSH to eliminate damage caused by R. dorsalis

However, exposure to an appropriate number of leafhoppers does not cause withered and wilting phenotypes in rice plants in field settings, where rice plants instead maintain a normal phenotype. We hypothesized that the GSH in rice plants competes with H2O2 to S-glutathionylate RdGAPDH–SOH, leading to the production of S-glutathionylated RdGAPDH (RdGAPDH–SSG) (Fig. 6a), ultimately alleviating the overoxidation of RdGAPDH and eliminating potential cytotoxicity. To test this hypothesis, we analyzed the S-glutathionylation of RdGAPDH–SOH in rice plants exposed to RGDV-free or -infected R. dorsalis for 12 h. The levels of GSH and –SSG groups in rice plants exposed to RGDV-infected R. dorsalis were higher than those in plants exposed to RGDV-free R. dorsalis (Fig. 6b). Moreover, the levels of GSH and –SSG groups in rice plants exposed to dsRdGAPDH-treated RGDV-free or -infected R. dorsalis were significantly lower than those in plants exposed to dsGFP-treated R. dorsalis (Fig. 6c). RdGAPDH-OE plants without exposure to R. dorsalis had significantly higher levels of both GSH and -SSG groups compared to WT plants (Fig. 6d). Furthermore, the exposure of RdGAPDH-OE plants to RGDV-free or -infected R. dorsalis also resulted in significantly higher levels of GSH and –SSG groups compared to WT plants (Fig. 6d). These results indicate that active S-glutathionylation of RdGAPDH–SOH by GSH is likely involved in alleviating the overoxidation of RdGAPDH. Then, competition of GSH with H2O2 to S-glutathionylate RdGAPDH–SOH was analyzed in vitro. Under increased concentrations of GSH in the presence of H2O2 and prokaryotically expressed RdGAPDH, the dehydrogenase activity of GAPDH, acylphosphatase activity of GAPDH-SOH, and levels of H2O2 and –SH groups were significantly decreased, while the level of –SSG groups increased (Fig. 6e). Western blot assays also revealed that higher concentrations of GSH reduced the production of disulfide-bonded RdGAPDH dimers (Fig. 6f). These findings indicated that after the formation of GAPDH–SOH, GSH intervened in the overoxidation of RdGAPDH by S-glutathionylating RdGAPDH–SOH, ultimately inhibiting the accumulation of disulfide-bonded RdGAPDH dimers. Additionally, in the presence of GSH and prokaryotically expressed RdGAPDH at a constant initial concentration, increased concentrations of H2O2 gradually reduced the level of GSH (Fig. 6g). And a significant increase in –SSG group yield was not observed, indicating that as the concentration of H2O2 increased, GSH could not continue to S-glutathionylate RdGAPDH-SOH. Based on these findings and the results shown in Fig. 5d, it can be inferred that limited GSH levels cannot fully S-glutathionylate RdGAPDH–SOH and that the overoxidation or S-glutathionylation of RdGAPDH is dependent on the levels of H2O2 or GSH. Furthermore, rice seedlings injected with prokaryotically expressed RdGAPDH exhibited a significantly higher level of GSH compared to those injected with prokaryotically expressed RdGAPDHΔ (Fig. 6h). These results once again indicated that the oxidation of RdGAPDH can induce GSH accumulation.

To further investigate the capability of GSH in S-glutathionylating RdGAPDH-SOH in vivo, we treated rice seedlings with DL-buthionine-sulfoximine (BSO), an inhibitor of GSH biosynthesis38. The BSO-treated rice seedlings were then exposed to RGDV-free leafhoppers. The levels of GSH and –SSG groups were significantly reduced under BSO treatment, while the acylphosphatase activity of GAPDH–SOH was significantly increased (Fig. 6i). Western blot analysis showed the presence of RdGAPDH in the H2O control and both RdGAPDH and disulfide-bonded RdGAPDH dimers in the BSO-treated seedlings (Fig. 6j). However, the accumulation of RdGAPDH was lower in the BSO-treated group compared to the H2O control (Fig. 6j). These data indicate that the inhibition of GSH biosynthesis promotes the overoxidation of RdGAPDH. Taken together, these results demonstrate that GSH in rice plants competes with H2O2 to alleviate the RdGAPDH overoxidation and potential cytotoxicity. These findings also reveal the delicate balance between insect attack mediated by effectors and plant defense through the reversal of insect-induced damage.

Salivary GAPDHs of other hemipteran vectors suppress H2O2 burst in plants

To determine whether salivary GAPDH from other hemipterans can also function as effectors suppressing H2O2 burst, we investigated several species of insect vectors that transmit plant pathogens of agricultural importance. We examined the leafhopper (Nephotettix cincticeps), which transmits rice dwarf virus (RDV), the white-backed planthopper (Sogatella furcifera), which transmits southern rice black-streaked dwarf virus, the brown planthopper (Nilaparvata lugens), which transmits rice ragged stunt virus, and the Asian citrus psyllid (Diaphorina citri), which transmits Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus (CLas), the causal agent of Huanglongbing in citrus.

When rice plants were exposed to N. cincticeps in which GAPDH of N. cincticeps (NcGAPDH) was knocked down, there was a significant increase in the level of H2O2 in the plants (Supplementary Fig. 7a). EPG assays detected different waveforms produced by N. cincticeps during feeding, including waveforms indicating non-probing, stylet penetration into the host, path, and salivation, and the presence of the stylet in the phloem and xylem tissues14,39. Compared to knockdown of GFP, knockdown of NcGAPDH in RDV-free N. cincticeps significantly prolonged the duration of non-probing, but significantly shortened the duration of ingestion from phloem and xylem bundle tissues. However, knockdown of NcGAPDH had no significant effect on stylet penetration or salivation (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Similarly, knockdown of NcGAPDH in RDV-infected N. cincticeps also resulted in a significant prolongation of the non-probing, stylet penetration, and salivation durations, but significantly shortened the duration of ingestion from phloem and xylem bundle tissues (Supplementary Fig. 7b). These findings indicate that the knockdown of NcGAPDH in RDV-free or RDV-infected N. cincticeps led to increased difficulty in their feeding process.

We also found that plant hosts exposed to S. furcifera, N. lugens, or D. citri, in which GAPDH had also been knocked down, exhibited higher levels of H2O2 compared to the dsGFP treatments (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Therefore, it can be concluded that the salivary GAPDHs of these hemipterans act as effectors suppressing H2O2 production in rice plants and facilitating insect feeding.

Discussion

This study focused on the first 12 h of rice plant exposure to virus-infected insects, which is the early stage of viral transmission by insects. During this period, RGDV begins to establish infection in plant hosts, but does not completely initiate replication and spread; therefore, over this time interval, most of the plant defenses were aimed at resisting insects rather than the virus specifically.

Hemipterans release abundant salivary components into plant tissues, including effectors that enhance insect feeding performance by suppressing plant defense and elicitors that intervene insect feeding performance by activating plant defenses7,13,40–44. These elicitors are recognized by the plant host, leading to the accumulation of ROS, induction of plant cell death, upregulated expression of defense-related genes, callose deposition, and so on45,46. Other salivary effectors can inhibit these elicitor-induced plant defenses. It has been concluded that arbovirus infection in the insect vector can affect the production of salivary components, thereby modulating host defense during insect feeding12. The infection of RGDV in R. dorsalis caused difficulties in feeding by increasing rice H2O2 burst (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S3), which does not conflict with the function of salivary RdGAPDH in suppressing plant H2O2. Viral infection is thought to promote the production and release of various salivary effectors and elicitors12,13. Thus, this intensified plant defense against virus-infected insects was comprehensive and not equivalent to the individual effect of RdGAPDH on plant defense (Fig. 2). Additionally, dsRdGAPDH-treated RGDV-free or -infected R. dorsalis exhibited prolonged durations of salivation (Figs. 2g and 3f). One possible explanation is that when RdGAPDH was knocked down, insects had to secrete and release other effectors to suppress H2O2 burst, thus prolonging the salivation duration. Therefore, the individual effect of RdGAPDH on plant defense did not conflict with the integrated effect of RGDV-infected insects on plant defense.

The infestation of plants by insects is considered as an arm race between insects and plants47. Insects release abundant salivary elicitors, which induce plant defense molecules, including H2O2, and abundant salivary effectors, which suppress plant defenses to insects, e.g., RdGAPDH. Based on present knowledge of salivary proteins, it is difficult to quantitatively evaluate salivary effectors and plant defense molecules. Therefore, it is hard to estimate the number of RdGAPDH molecules that is released to rice plants, while the amount of H2O2 molecules that was quenched by RdGAPDH was quantified (Fig. 2d). However, it has been demonstrated that RGDV infection upregulates salivary RdGAPDH, promoting its release into rice phloem (Fig. 1g). In rice phloem, the upregulation of RdGAPDH further enhances the suppression of H2O2 burst, ultimately promoting viral transmission (Fig. 4). Salivary GAPDH from other hemipteran vectors can similarly suppress H2O2 burst (Supplementary Fig. 7), suggesting that salivary GAPDH generally facilitates insect feeding and transmission of plant pathogens. This study, for the first time, also details the process of heterogeneous GAPDH oxidation by plants.

In vitro biochemical assays have shown that excessive H2O2 results in the overoxidation of animal GAPDH and the formation of insoluble disulfide-bonded GAPDH aggregates, which can promote cell death in cultured mammalian cells21–26. In this study, prolonged exposure to R. dorsalis caused irreversible withering and wilting of rice plants (Fig. 5i). This excessive stress from R. dorsalis also reduced rice defense to insects (Supplementary Fig. 6), while promoted the formation of disulfide-bonded RdGAPDH dimers and increased the number of dead cells in rice plants (Fig. 5h, j). Therefore, it is concluded that disulfide-bonded RdGAPDH dimers is likely cytotoxic and causes the cell death in the withered and wilted rice plants.

The present results suggest that homeostasis between H2O2 and GSH is disrupted by RdGAPDH during R. dorsalis feeding. RdGAPDH was not overoxidized by excessive H2O2 of rice hosts because induced GSH alleviated oxidation via converting RdGAPDH–SOH into S-glutathionylated RdGAPDH (Fig. 6). This process reduces the potential damage to plant cells caused by R. dorsalis feeding. This regulation of oxidation reflects an ongoing battle between insect attacks and plant defenses. In contrast, prolonged exposure to R. dorsalis led to overoxidation of RdGAPDH, cell death of rice plants and a notable decrease in rice resistance to insects (Fig. 5h, i, Supplementary Fig. 6). These findings reveal the mechanism of appropriate exposure to insects inducing a balance between insect attacks through effectors and plant defense by preventing such damage.

In the presence of GAPDH in rice plants, OsGAPDH is thought to maintain a balance with H2O2 in the absence of R. dorsalis feeding. The limited effect of R. dorsalis feeding on OsGAPDH accumulation (Fig. 1a) suggested that most OsGAPDH is unlikely to consume the H2O2 burst triggered by R. dorsalis feeding. GAPDH is a crucial glycolytic enzyme involved in energy metabolism in the cytoplasm and functions in various non-glycolytic processes15. If most OsGAPDH is involved in the consumption of H2O2 triggered by R. dorsalis feeding, the normal cell activity, and even the development of rice plants would be damaged. Therefore, among piercing-sucking insects, exploiting the salivary GAPDH of the insect itself to suppress H2O2 produced during feeding is a sustainable feeding strategy for maintaining the normal metabolism of plant hosts.

The ROS produced by plant respiratory burst oxidase homologs (RBOHs) play a crucial role in plant stress responses, e.g., defense against attacks from pathogens or chewing herbivore-elicited responses48,49. However, it remains unknown whether they also mediate responses against piercing-sucking insects. It would be meaningful to explore whether the feeding of piercing-sucking insects, like leafhoppers and planthoppers, induces ROS produced by RBOHs and whether RBOHs play a role in plant virus transmission by insects.

To understand how GSH S-glutathionylates the oxidized RdGAPDH, the association among H2O2, RdGAPDH, and GSH was examined in vitro (Fig. 6). A gradually excessive reacting dose of GSH was added to the reaction system of RdGAPDH and H2O2 and interfered in the overoxidation of RdGAPDH (Fig. 6e). It was unlikely for H2O2 to directly oxidize excessive GSH in the presence of GAPDH. First, GAPDH is more sensitive to H2O2 than GSH, because the rate constants of H2O2 reacting with GSH and GAPDH, respectively, are 0.87 and 500 (M−1 s−1)50. When the amount of GAPDH equals that of GSH, GAPDH will be preferentially oxidized. Second, if the level of H2O2 is excessive, H2O2 may oxidize GSH. However, in this reaction system (Fig. 6e), the reacting dose of GSH was gradually excessive, indicating that most GSH molecules S-glutathionylated GAPDH–SOH. Third, it is reported the absence of a chemical reaction between GSH and H2O2 under the presence of GAPDH21. As shown in Fig. 6g, as the H2O2 level increased, the S-glutathionylation of GAPDH tended to become saturated, as the excessive H2O2 possibly oxidized GSH, leading to GSH becoming undetectable.

It has been reported that the persistence of S-glutathionylated state of GAPDH from Arabidopsis thaliana tends to change the GAPDH folding and form insoluble aggregates28,29. Figure 6f showed that disulfide-bonded RdGAPDH dimers were decreased, but RdGAPDH were decreased even more. This suggests that the portion of prokaryotically expressed RdGAPDH that does not show up in the gel may forms insoluble S-glutathionylated RdGAPDH aggregates in vitro, which is precipitated before loading on the gel. However, it is undetermined whether S-glutathionylated RdGAPDH forms insoluble aggregates in rice plants. It is also known that plant glutaredoxins and GSH may remove S-glutathionylation and regenerate reduced GAPDH (GAPDH-SH), which then would once again be ready to scavenge H2O228. This S-glutathionylation/deglutathionylation cycle30 enables GAPDH to quench H2O2 like POD. It is possible for OsGAPDH to participate in the glutathionylation/deglutathionylation cycle to maintain the OsGAPDH level and biological functions. However, it remains unclear if insect GAPDHs also go through the glutathionylation/deglutathionylation cycle in plants, similar to plant GAPDH, because it is unknown whether plants can tolerate heterologous insect GAPDH to be recycled and persistently existing within plants.

Exosomes, commonly present in the saliva, are small membrane-bound vesicles secreted by various types of cells into the extracellular space51. Exosomes play a crucial role in intercellular material and information exchange and delivery, including delivery of GAPDH52–54. As such, exosomes serve as important vehicles for intercellular communication, facilitating the transmission of antiviral signals to inhibit viral infection or aiding in the delivery of viral materials to evade the host immune response during viral infection55,56. Plant exosomes have also been shown to carry virus-induced small RNAs that can inhibit pathogen infection52. Recent reports have highlighted the role of insect vector exosomes in transmitting plant viruses from salivary glands to plant hosts16,57. In our study, we show that leafhopper exosomes can deliver salivary RdGAPDH to plant hosts. RGDV utilizes this mechanism by inducing the production of more exosomes to load an increased amount of RdGAPDH effectors into the rice host (Fig. 1). These findings reveal that plant viruses not only utilize exosomes for the transport of virions but also exploit them as vehicles for delivering biological factors beneficial for viral transmission. These insights uncover the role of exosomes as effective carriers for the cross-kingdom transfer of materials, mediating virus–insect–plant interactions for viral transmission.

In light of our findings, we propose a model for how RGDV induces RdGAPDH to consume H2O2 in rice plants for viral transmission (Fig. 7). In this model, salivary RdGAPDH is loaded into exosomal vesicles and released from the salivary glands into the plant phloem during R. dorsalis feeding. Because R. dorsalis feeding induces H2O2 burst in the rice phloem, the released salivary RdGAPDH decrease the H2O2 level owing to its –SH group reacting with H2O2, facilitating R. dorsalis feeding. When RdGAPDH becomes overoxidized, it forms dimers through disulfide bonds, which are likely cytotoxic and promote plant cell death. To alleviate such RdGAPDH overoxidation, rice cells launch emergency defense via exploiting GSH to S-glutathionylate RdGAPDH–SOH, resulting in the formation of GAPDH–SSG and eliminating the cytotoxicity caused by RdGAPDH overoxidation. When RGDV infects the salivary glands of R. dorsalis, it induces the production of more salivary RdGAPDH, which is loaded into additional exosomes and released into the rice phloem. The induced RdGAPDH effectively decreases H2O2 levels, thereby suppressing rice resistance to R. dorsalis feeding and facilitating viral transmission. The induction of GSH in the rice host leads to increased S-glutathionylation of RdGAPDH. The induced S-glutathionylation of RdGAPDH also reduces oxidative stress-induced aggregation of disulfide-bonded RdGAPDH and potential cytotoxicity, ultimately maintaining balance among the virus, insect, and plant. This model provides insight into the intricate interactions among insect effectors, plant defenses, and viruses, highlighting the mechanisms that maintain the delicate balance among viruses, insect attack mediated by effectors, and plant defense through the reversal of insect-induced damage. Methods

Ethics statement

This research complies with all relevant ethical regulations of Science and Technology Ethics Committee of Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University.

Insects, viruses, and antibodies

RGDV-free R. dorsalis leafhoppers were initially obtained from Fujian Province in southern China and subsequently propagated for several generations at 25 ± 3 °C in a greenhouse at Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University, Fuzhou city, China. All rice plants used in this study were Oryza sativa ssp. japonica variety Nipponbare. The rice plants were cultivated in a greenhouse maintained at 28–32 °C, with a relative humidity of 60 ± 5% and natural sunlight. RGDV-infected rice plants were collected from Fujian Province and propagated through transmission by R. dorsalis under greenhouse conditions.

Virus-free N. cincticeps, S. furcifera, and N. lugens as well as CLas-free D. citri were also propagated for several generations at 25 ± 3 °C in a greenhouse at Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University. RDV-infected rice plants were originally collected from Fujian Province and propagated through transmission by N. cincticeps under greenhouse conditions.

Polyclonal rabbit antibodies against RdRab27a, RdGAPDH, OsGAPDH, and RGDV P8 peptides were prepared by Genscript Biotech Corporation in Nanjing, China. The process was approved by the Science Technology Department of Jiangsu Province, China. Mouse monoclonal antibodies against the 6×His tag were obtained from Transgene Biotech (cat. HT501). Goat anti-rabbit IgG-peroxidase or goat anti-mouse IgG-peroxidase was used as a secondary antibody (Sangon, cat. D110058 or D110087, respectively). The actin dye phalloidin-Alexa Fluor 647 carboxylic acid was acquired from Thermo Fisher Scientific (cat. A22287).

Western blots for target proteins in saliva and rice plants

Approximately 15 R. dorsalis adults were used for saliva collection over a period of 2 days in each membrane feeding group and 15 groups were carried out. To collect the saliva, a sterile feeding diet of 5% sucrose solution (40 μL) was placed between Parafilm membranes (Neenah, cat.PM-996) that covered one end of a small, dark pipe-like cage (~2.5 cm in diameter by 4 cm in height). The leafhoppers were starved for 2 h and then confined in membrane feeding cage, with the diet placed under light to attract R. dorsalis to the diet. After 48 h, saliva samples were tested for the presence of target proteins using western blot assays. Three biological replicates were performed. The feeding diet without exposure to R. dorsalis served as the blank.

To examine the release of target proteins to rice plants, 20 leafhoppers were starved for 2 h and then allowed to feed on a specific region (~1.5 cm in length by 0.3 cm in width) of a rice seedling at the two-leaf stage. This feeding occurred in a pipe-like cage for 12 h. Subsequently, the tested regions were collected for protein extraction in equal quantities. Equal amounts of proteins from each treatment were loaded for western blot assays. At least three biological replicates were performed to ensure the reliability of the results. Quantitation of western blots was conducted using ImageJ_v1.8.0_Java8 software, and the ratio values were obtained from the blot analyzed in relevant figure. Uncropped and unprocessed scans of blots were present in the Source Data file and as a Supplementary figure in the Supplementary Information.

Immunofluorescence staining for target proteins in salivary glands and rice sections

The salivary glands of R. dorsalis were dissected and then fixed in 4% (v:v) paraformaldehyde. Subsequently, they were permeabilized in 0.4% Triton X-10016. The samples were then incubated with RdRab27a-specific IgG conjugated to FITC (RdRab27a-FITC), RdGAPDH-specific IgG conjugated to rhodamine (RdGAPDH-rhodamine), or actin dyes (phalloidin-Alexa Fluor 647 carboxylic acid) for immunostaining. Immunostained samples were analyzed using a TCS SP5 laser confocal microscope from Leica Microsystems.

To detect target proteins released into rice plants, 20 R. dorsalis adults were starved for 2 h and then allowed to feed on a specific region of a rice seedling at the two-leaf stage within a pipe-like cage for 12 h. The feeding areas of the tested rice seedlings were embedded in O.C.T. Compound (Sakura, cat. 4583) and then sectioned using a Shandon Cryotome FSE (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The sections were fixed in 4% (v:v) paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.4% Triton X-100, and subsequently immunolabeled with RdRab27a-specific IgG conjugated to rhodamine (RdRab27a-rhodamine), RdGAPDH-specific IgG conjugated to FITC, or Alexa Fluor 647 (RdGAPDH-FITC or RdGAPDH-647), RdGAPDH-rhodamine, RGDV P8-specific IgG conjugated to rhodamine (P8-rhodamine), or H2DCFDA (Merck, cat. D6883). The sections were then processed for immunofluorescence microscopy16. Rice leaves not exposed to R. dorsalis were treated similarly.

To establish immunofluorescence microscopy parameters, digital images (1024 × 1024 pixels) were captured using specific excitation/emission wavelengths for FITC (495/517 nm), rhodamine (551/573 nm), and Alexa Fluor 647 (652/658 nm). These images were obtained employing a 63× oil-immersion objective. For consistency, samples within the same experimental group were evaluated under the same immunofluorescence microscopy parameters to standardize background levels and were captured for images in a single section.

Effect of knocking down RdRab27a or GW4869 treatment on RdGAPDH release from salivary glands into rice plants

A T7 RNA polymerase promoter with the sequence 5′-ATTCTCTAGAAGCTTAATACGACTCACTATAGGG-3′ was added to the forward and reverse primers for the RdRab27a and GFP genes at the 5′ terminus to amplify regions of ~494 bp and 475 bp, respectively. Subsequently, a T7 RiboMAX Express RNAi System (Promega Biotech, cat. P1700) was utilized for in vitro synthesis of dsRNAs targeting RdRab27a (dsRab27a) and GFP (dsGFP).

RGDV-free third instar nymphs were microinjected with dsRdRab27a (~200 ng/insect) or GW4869 at a final concentration of 15 μg/ml at the intersegmental region of the thorax using a Nanoject II Auto-Nanoliter Injector (Spring). Treatments with dsGFP and DMSO, respectively, served as the controls of dsRdRab27a and GW4869. These microinjected leafhoppers were then transferred to healthy rice seedlings for recovery.

At 4 days post microinjection, 30 dsRNA-treated R. dorsalis adults were dissected and analyzed using RT-qPCR assays. Total RNA samples were extracted from the salivary glands of 30 dsGFP-treated, dsRdRab27a-treated, or dsRdGAPDH-treated leafhoppers for first-strand cDNA synthesis using M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase (Promega). Then, qPCR was performed using 2× Real Star Green Fast Mixture (with ROX II) (Genstar, cat. A303). The EF-1α transcript of R. dorsalis served as the internal reference for the normalization of gene expression levels, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCT method. The experiments were replicated at least three times, with a pool of 30 leafhoppers used for each replicate.

At 4 days post microinjection, 20 dsRNA-treated R. dorsalis adults were starved for 2 h and then allowed to feed on a specific region (~1.5 cm in length by 0.3 cm in width) of a rice seedling at the two-leaf stage within a pipe-like cage for 12 h. Thereafter, the salivary glands of these leafhoppers were dissected. Salivary glands and plant samples were tested for the presence of target proteins using western blot assays. RdRab27a- and RdGAPDH-specific IgG were used as primary antibodies, while goat anti-rabbit IgG-peroxidase served as the secondary antibody (Sangon Biotech, cat. D110058). Three independent biological replicates were conducted and analyzed to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the results.

Effect of RGDV infection of leafhoppers on RdGAPDH release into rice plants

RGDV-free second instar nymphs were allowed to feed on RGDV-infected rice plants for 3 days. At 14 days padp, the salivary glands of 30 RGDV-infected and -free leafhoppers were dissected and analyzed using RT-qPCR and western blot assays.

Approximately 15 RGDV-infected and -free R. dorsalis leafhoppers were starved for 2 h and then confined for saliva collection for 2 days in each group. Fifteen groups were carried out, as mentioned above. Saliva samples were tested for the presence of target proteins using western blot assays. At least three independent biological replicates were performed to ensure reliable results.

To examine the release of target proteins into rice plants, 20 RGDV-infected or -free leafhoppers were starved for 2 h and then allowed to feed on a specific region (~1.5 cm in length by 0.3 cm in width) of a rice seedling at the two-leaf stage within a pipe-like cage for 12 h, as mentioned above. Equally quantities of the tested plants were then collected for protein extraction. Equal amounts of proteins from each treatment were loaded for western blot assays. Three independent biological replicates were conducted and analyzed for accuracy.

Analyses of rice JA and SA induced by RGDV-free R. dorsalis feeding

Twenty RGDV-free leafhoppers were starved for 2 h and then allowed to feed on a specific region (1.5 cm in length by 0.3 cm in width) of a rice seedling at the two-leaf stage within a pipe-like cage for 12 h, as described above. Then the regions of fresh rice leaves were ground in liquid nitrogen. Approximately 50 (±0.5) mg of sample powder were mixed with 900 μl of ethyl acetate and 100 μl of 10 ng/ml [2H4]-SA, 10 ng ml [2H6]-JA, and 10 ng ml [2H4]-SA as internal standards58. Then the samples were vortexed followed by ultrasonic extraction at 4 °C for 20 min. The supernatant after centrifugation was evaporated to dryness under a cold trap concentrator and then redissolved with 200 μl of precooled 70% (v/v) methanol. Then the samples were vortexed followed by ultrasonic extraction at 4 °C for 8 min. The resulting supernatant after centrifugation was filtered using 0.22 μm PVDF filter (Millipore) and analyzed for the content of JA and SA using a UPLC-XEVO TQ-S MS triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Waters, USA) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) thermostatted at 40 °C. Water containing 5 mM ammonium formate (phase A) and methanol (phase B) were served as mobile phases for separation. The chromatographic gradient was as follows: 0–3 min (95–80% A), 3–5 min (80–55% A), 5–9 min (55–0% A), and 9–12 min (0–95% A). The flow rate was 0.3 ml/min. The mass spectrometer was operated in ESI-mode with the following settings: capillary voltage: 1.00 kV; desolvation gas temperature: 400 °C; source temperature: 150 °C; cone gas flow: 150 L/h; desolvation gas flow: 800 L/h58. Collision energy and cone voltage for JA and SA were individually optimized with MRM for quantification using authentic standards. The precursor and product ions for JA were 209.3 and 59.3, respectively. The precursor and product ions for SA were 137.1 and 65.2, respectively. JA and SA contents were analyzed using masslynx4.1 (Waters) software and quantified based on the ratio between their ion intensities and the respective internal standards.

These tests were conducted at the Horticultural Biology and Metabolomics Center of Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University. More than three independent biological replicates were performed and analyzed to ensure accuracy and reliability of the results.

Analyses of rice H2O2 and related metabolite levels induced by RGDV-free R. dorsalis feeding

Twenty RGDV-free leafhoppers were starved for 2 h and then allowed to feed on a specific region (~1.5 cm in length by 0.3 cm in width) of a rice seedling at the two-leaf stage within a pipe-like cage for 12 h. The H2O2 content of ~10 mg of the tested rice seedlings was determined using a Hydrogen Peroxide Assay Kit (Beyotime, cat. S0038). Leaf tissues were homogenized with 100–200 μL of lysis solution on ice. The supernatant was collected for absorbance measurement at 560 nm after centrifugation at 12,000 × g and 4 °C. A standard curve was established using standard substances with a series of known H2O2 content samples and tested for absorbance at 240 nm. The absorbance of the samples and standard substances was measured using the SPARK 10M Microplate spectrophotometer (TECAN). Three independent biological replicates were assayed.

The POD activity of the tested rice plants was determined using a Peroxidase assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, cat. A084-3-1). Approximately 0.05-g leaf tissue samples were ground in liquid nitrogen and then homogenized with 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.2–7.4) on ice. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 2500 × g for 8 min at 4 °C. Reagents from the kit were added successively according to the user manual. The absorbance of the final sample was measured at 420 nm using a spectrophotometer. Three independent biological replicates were assayed.

The MDA content of the tested rice plants was determined using a Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, cat. A003-1-2). Approximately 0.05-g leaf tissue samples were ground in liquid nitrogen and then homogenized with 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.2–7.4) on ice. The samples were successively incubated with reagents from the kit, vortexed, and heated in a water bath at 95 °C. The supernatant was collected after cooling the sample and centrifugation at 3500 × g for 10 min. The absorbance of the final sample was measured at 532 nm using a spectrophotometer. Three independent biological replicates were assayed.

Visualization of H2O2 location and accumulation

Thirty RGDV-free R. dorsalis leafhoppers were starved for 2 h and then allowed to feed on a specific region (~1.5 cm in length by 0.3 cm in width) of a rice seedling at the two-leaf stage within a pipe-like cage for 12 h. The feeding areas of the tested rice seedlings were immersed in 10 μM H2DCFDA (Merck, D6883), which is a fluorescent dye precursor for H2O2. The seedlings were then vacuumized at 60 kPa for 10 min and incubated for 1–2 h in the dark. Thereafter, the samples were washed three times with sterile water in the dark, each time for 10–30 min. Finally, the samples were examined using the TCS SP5 laser confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems). Three independent biological replicates were assayed.

Effect of knocking down RdGAPDH on rice resistance to R. dorsalis

The T7 RNA polymerase promoter was added to the forward and reverse primers for the RdGAPDH gene at the 5′ terminus to amplify a region of about 463 bp. The T7 RiboMAX Express RNAi System was used to synthesize dsRNAs targeting RdGAPDH (dsRdGAPDH) in vitro. RGDV-free third instar nymphs were then microinjected with either dsRdGAPDH or dsGFP, following the procedure described above.

At 4 days post microinjection, 20 dsRNA-treated R. dorsalis leafhoppers were starved for 2 h and then allowed to feed on a specific region (~1.5 cm in length by 0.3 cm in width) of a rice seedling at the two-leaf stage within a pipe-like cage for 12 h. The salivary glands of these leafhoppers were then dissected. The salivary glands and tested regions of plants were examined to determine the presence of RdGAPDH by RT-qPCR or western blot assays. The tested regions of the plants were also analyzed for their contents of JA, SA, H2O2, and MDA as well as their POD activity. In addition, the tested region exposed to 30 dsRNA-treated leafhoppers was treated with H2DCFDA for visualization of H2O2 location and accumulation. Three independent biological replicates were analyzed to ensure accuracy and reliability of the results.

Effect of knocking down RdGAPDH on leafhopper feeding behavior

To examine the effect of RdGAPDH knockdown on penetration behavior of R. dorsalis at 4 days post microinjection, dsRNA-treated R. dorsalis adults were starved for 2 h. Then, the Giga-8 direct current EPG assay was utilized37.

Before the EPG assay, rice seedlings were transplanted into plastic pots filled with turf soil one day prior. At 4 days post microinjection, each treated leafhopper was anesthetized using CO2, and a gold wire (diameter, 20 μm; length, 10–15 cm) was attached to the dorsal thorax using a water-soluble silver glue. Each wired R. dorsalis was connected to the EPG probe, which was further connected to an amplifier and placed on the rice plant stem. Additionally, a copper wire (diameter, 2 mm; length, 10 cm) connected to another amplifier served as the plant electrode and was inserted vertically into the pot soil.

The EPG signals were digitized using a converter (DI710-UL; Dataq) and recorded using Stylet++a software (Wageningen University, Wageningen, Netherlands). Each leafhopper was continuously recorded for 5 h, and 10 valid biologically independent replicates were recorded to ensure reliable results. The EPG recordings were conducted in a quiet room at a temperature of 25–28 °C and a relative humidity of 70 ± 5%.

R. dorsalis produced A, S, C, E, F, and R waveforms during feeding37. These waveforms respectively indicate the stylet probing (waveform A), continuous salivation (waveform S), active ingestion (waveform C), passive prolonged ingestion (waveform E), obstacle signal (waveform F), and stylet insertion (waveform R) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Analyses of rice resistance to RGDV-infected R. dorsalis

RGDV-free second instar nymphs were initially allowed to feed on RGDV-infected rice plants for 3 days and were then transferred to healthy rice seedlings. Our tests typically confirmed that more than 80% of the leafhoppers were infected with RGDV by the end of this process.

Following a latent period of 14 days, 20 RGDV-infected R. dorsalis leafhoppers were starved for 2 h and then allowed to feed on a specific region (~1.5 cm in length by 0.3 cm in width) of a rice seedling at the two-leaf stage within a pipe-like cage for 12 h. The tested regions of the plants were also analyzed for their contents of JA, SA, H2O2, and MDA as well as their POD activity. The feeding areas were collected to prepare frozen sections, which were subsequently immunolabeled with RdGAPDH-specific IgG conjugated to Alexa Fluor 647 (GAPDH-647), P8-rhodamine, and H2DCFDA. These sections were then processed for immunofluorescence microscopy. Three independent biological replicates were analyzed to ensure accuracy and reliability of the results.

Effect of knocking down RdGAPDH of RGDV-infected R. dorsalis on rice resistance and viral transmission

RGDV-free second-instar nymphs were initially allowed to feed on diseased rice plants for 3 days. At 9 days padp, when viruses had initially infected salivary glands59, ~100 RGDV-infected and -free leafhoppers were microinjected with dsGAPDH or dsGFP (~200 ng/insect).

At 14 days padp, salivary glands of 10 and 20 dsGFP- or dsGAPDH-treated RGDV-infected leafhoppers were dissected and analyzed using RT-qPCR and western blot assays, respectively. Twenty RGDV-infected leafhoppers treated with dsRNA were starved for 2 h and then allowed to feed on a specific region (~1.5 cm in length by 0.3 cm in width) of a rice seedling at the two-leaf stage within a pipe-like cage for 12 h. The tested regions of the plants were also examined for their contents of JA, SA, H2O2, and MDA as well as their POD activity. The tested region exposed to 30 dsRNA-treated leafhoppers was treated with H2DCFDA for visualization of H2O2 location and accumulation. At least three independent biological replicates were conducted.

At 14 days padp, the feeding behavior of dsRNA-treated RGDV-infected leafhoppers was assessed by EPG assays. Each insect was continuously recorded for 5 h, and 10 valid biologically independent replicates were recorded to ensure reliable results.

To investigate the survival profile of dsRNA-treated RGDV-infected leafhoppers and the profile of RGDV transmission by a group of surviving dsRNA-treated RGDV-infected R. dorsalis, 100 leafhoppers at 12 days padp were allowed to individually feed on a healthy rice seedling in a glass tube for 24 h. The survival rates of R. dorsalis were recorded, and the surviving R. dorsalis were then transferred each day to new healthy rice seedlings for 7 days. All of the tested rice seedlings were planted in an insect-proof greenhouse for 60 days. RT-PCR was performed to determine the presence/absence of the RGDV P8 gene in the tested plants, indicating whether RGDV was transmitted to the plants by surviving R. dorsalis. The transmission rate for each day was calculated as the percentage of RT-PCR-positive plants out of the total number of plants exposed to surviving R. dorsalis. Three independent biological replicates were analyzed.

Generation and insect resistance of transgenic rice

O. sativa ssp. japonica, variety Nipponbare was transformed with 35S:RdGAPDH constructs using Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation at Biorun Bio-technology (Wuhan, China). The overexpression lines were characterized using western blot assays. T1 transgenic lines that consistently expressed RdGAPDH were selected for further analyses.

At the tillering stage, RdGAPDH-OE and WT plants were exposed to 20 RGDV-infected or -free leafhoppers. The leafhoppers were starved for 2 h prior to exposure. The exposed region of the leaves was ~1.5 cm in length by 0.3 cm in width, and the exposure lasted for 12 h. The tested regions were analyzed for their contents of JA, SA, H2O2, and MDA, POD activity, and presence of target proteins. Additionally, each tested region exposed to 30 RGDV-infected or -free leafhoppers for 12 h was treated with H2DCFDA for visualization of H2O2 location and accumulation. At least three independent biological replicates were conducted.

The feeding behavior of RGDV-infected and -free leafhoppers on RdGAPDH-OE and WT plants at the tillering stage was assessed by EPG assays. Each insect was continuously recorded for 5 h, and 10 valid biologically independent replicates were recorded to ensure reliable results.

To test the transmission rate, RGDV-infected leafhoppers at 13 days padp were starved for 2 h prior to exposure. Then 30 of these leafhoppers were allowed to individually feed on RdGAPDH-OE or WT plants in a glass tube for 24 h, then transferred to healthy rice seedlings. The tested rice seedlings were planted for 60 days, as mentioned above. RT-PCR was performed to determine the presence/absence of the RGDV P8 gene in the tested plants. The transmission rate was calculated as the percentage of RT-PCR-positive plants out of the total number of plants exposed to R. dorsalis. Three independent biological replicates were analyzed.

The phenotypes of rice plants observed in this study are consistent with the expected effects of RdGAPDH overexpression. Without independent transgenic lines or empty vector controls, these effects may also be influenced by other factors.

GAPDH activity and RdGAPDH oxidation by H2O2 in rice plants

Twenty dsRNA-treated RGDV-infected or -free leafhoppers at 14 days padp were starved for 2 h. They were then allowed to feed on a WT or RdGAPDH-OE rice seedlings at the two-leaf stage for 12 h. The exposed region of the leaves was ~1.5 cm in length by 0.3 cm in width, within a pipe-like cage.

For analysis, ~0.05-g samples of the tested regions of the plants were examined for their dehydrogenase activity of GAPDH using the GAPDH Activity Assay Kit (Merck, cat. MAK277). The reaction mixture was incubated with the GAPDH activity assay buffer for 2 h at room temperature. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 450 nm. The dehydrogenase ability of GAPDH activity (milliunits/mg protein) of the sample was determined according to the instructions provided with the kit. At least three independent biological replicates were conducted.

Similarly, the content of –SH groups in ~0.05-g samples of the tested regions of the plants was examined using the Total Sulfhydryl Group Content Assay Kit (Boxbio, cat. AKAO010M). The reaction mixture was incubated with reagents from the kit, following the user manual. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 412 nm. The content of sulfhydryl groups (μmol/mL) of the sample was determined according to the kit instructions. At least three independent biological replicates were conducted.

Furthermore, 0.05-g samples of the tested regions of the plants were examined for the acylphosphatase activity of GAPDH–SOH. This activity was determined by the accumulation of NAD+ caused by the coupling of the acylphosphatase reaction to the dehydrogenase reaction in the presence of a small concentration of phosphate that was insufficient for the dehydrogenase activity21,22. The accumulation of NAD+ was tested using the NAD+/NADH Assay Kit with WST-8 (Beyotime, cat. S0175). The reaction mixture was incubated with reagents from the kit, following the user manual. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 450 nm. The content of NAD+ (pmol) of each sample was determined as described in the kit instructions. At least three independent biological replicates were conducted.

RdGAPDH sensitivity to H2O2 in vitro

The full-length ORF of RdGAPDH was cloned into the pEASY-Blunt E1 Expression Vector (Transgen Biotech, cat. CE111-01) to create plasmids expressing a His fusion protein referred to as His-RdGAPDH. These recombinant proteins were then expressed in Escherichia coli strain BL21, which was maintained in our laboratory at the Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University. Lysates containing the total protein isolate at a concentration of ~8 mg/mL were collected for further analysis; the lysates were used to determine the activity of GAPDH and its sensitivity to H2O2 in vitro. Approximately 50 μL of the lysates was incubated with 50 μL of H2O2 at a series of concentrations (0, 1, 500, and 1000 μM) for 10 min. Following this incubation, the product was analyzed to evaluate the dehydrogenase activity of GAPDH, the content of –SH groups, and the acylphosphatase activity of GAPDH–SOH. These analyses were conducted using specific kits, as mentioned above. Additionally, western blot assays were conducted to further examine the product.

Oxidation of cysteine residues of RdGAPDH

To generate RdGAPDHΔ, the codons for cysteine residues at amino acid positions 149 and 153 were mutated into codons for serine residues. The full-length ORF of RdGAPDHΔ was then cloned into the pEASY-Blunt E1 Expression Vector to create plasmids expressing the His fusion protein, referred to as His-RdGAPDHΔ. As described above, recombinant His-RdGAPDHΔ proteins were expressed in E. coli strain BL21.

A total 50-μL volume of lysate, containing His-RdGAPDH or His-RdGAPDHΔ (at a concentration of ~8 mg/mL), were incubated with 50 μL of H2O2 at a concentration of 500 μM for 10 min. The resulting reaction mixture was then examined using western blots to identify the products. The mouse monoclonal antibody against the 6×His tag was used as the primary antibody, while goat anti-mouse IgG-peroxidase was used as the secondary antibody.

Furthermore, ~200-μL volumes of lysates from BL21 E. coli expressing His-RdGAPDH and His-RdGAPDHΔ were injected into the stem base of rice seedlings at the two-leaf stage (at ~1 cm above the stem base). The procedure of injecting rice seedlings has been described by ref. 60. In brief, the lysates were injected into the seedlings 1 cm above the stem base using a 0.45 mm × 15 mm RWLB needle attached to a 1-mL disposable syringe. Gentle pressure was applied to the syringe until a drop of lysate emerged at the leaf apex. When resistance to injection was felt by the researcher, the injection was inferred to be proper. The syringes and needles were changed for each treatment. After 10 min, ~0.05-g samples of the tested rice seedlings were examined for their contents of H2O2 and –SH groups as well as their acylphosphatase activity of GAPDH–SOH. This analysis was conducted on three independent biological replicates to ensure reliable results.

Trypan blue staining

Fifty R. dorsalis leafhoppers were starved for 2 h and then allowed to feed on a rice seedling at the two-leaf stage for 12, 24 and 48 h. These leaves were then successively cut off, placed in a 10 mL tube, and immersed in trypan blue solution. These tested leaves were boiled for 3–5 min, then stained overnight. Thereafter, the leaves were transferred to 95% ethanol to decolorize at 42 °C for 18 h. Blue spots on the leaves were recorded and photographed.

S-glutathionylation of RdGAPDH–SOH by GSH oxidation

Twenty dsRNAs-treated RGDV-infected and -free R. dorsalis leafhoppers at 14 days padp were starved for 2 h. They were then allowed to feed on a WT or RdGAPDH-OE rice seedling at the two-leaf stage for 12 h. The exposed region of the leaves was ~1.5 cm in length by 0.3 cm in width, within a pipe-like cage. Approximately 0.05 g of the tested regions of plants were examined to determine the content of GSH using a Reduced Glutathione (GSH) Content Assay Kit (Boxbio, cat. AKPR008M). The reaction mixture was incubated with the reagents provided in the kit, following the user manual. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 412 nm with a spectrophotometer. The GSH content (μg/mg protein) of the sample was determined according to the kit instructions.

Because protein-glutathione mixed disulfides can be quantified by the reduction of the disulfide bond to liberate GSH61, an Oxidized Glutathione (GSSG) Content Assay Kit (Boxbio, cat. AKPR009M) with the same testing principle was used to determine the molar quantity of –SSG. The reaction mixture was incubated with reagents in the kit according to the user manual. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 412 nm. The molar quantity of –SSG (nmol/g protein) in the sample was determined based on the kit instructions. These experiments were conducted in three independent biological replicates.

The ability of GSH to prevent RdGAPDH oxidation was tested as follows. The mixture of 50 μL of BL12 E. coli lysates (~8 mg/mL) expressing His-RdGAPDH and 50 μL of H2O2 (1000 μM) was incubated with 50 μL of GSH at various concentrations (0, 1.6, 3.2, and 4.8 mM) for 10 min. The resulting products were analyzed for the dehydrogenase activity of GAPDH, the content of –SH groups, the acylphosphatase activity of GAPDH–SOH, and the presence of H2O2 using the respective kits mentioned above. Additionally, western blot assays were conducted on the products.

To examine the competition of H2O2 in oxidating RdGAPDH in the presence of GSH, the mixture of 50 μL lysates (~8 mg/mL) expressing His-RdGAPDH and 50 μL GSH (~4.8 mM) was incubated with 50 μL H2O2 at concentrations of 0, 1, 500, and 1000 μM for 10 min. The resulting products were analyzed for the content of GSH using the corresponding kits as mentioned before.

Effect of GSH inhibitor on RdGAPDH S-glutathionylation by GSH

Approximately 200 μL of BSO (Merck, cat. 5072-26-4) in a concentration of 50 mg/mL or H2O was injected into the stem base of a rice seedling at the two-leaf stage (at ~10 cm high). At 10 min post injection, ~0.05-g samples of these seedlings were examined for their GSH content. Additionally, at 10 min post injection, 20 RGDV-free R. dorsalis leafhoppers, which had been starved for 2 h, were confined in a pipe-like cage and allowed to feed on a specific region (~1.5 cm in length by 0.3 cm in width) of either a BSO- or H2O-treated rice seedling for 10 min. Approximately 0.05-g samples of tested regions of the plants were analyzed for the acylphosphatase activity of GAPDH–SOH and the presence of disulfide-bonded RdGAPDH dimers in western blot assays.

Effect of knocking down GAPDH of other hemipteran vectors on H2O2 burst of plants

The T7 RNA polymerase promoter was added to the forward and reverse primers for the GAPDH genes of N. cincticeps, S. furcifera, N. lugens, and D. citri at the 5′ termini to amplify regions of about 646, 695, 632, and 641 bp, respectively. Subsequently, the T7 RiboMAX Express RNAi System was used for in vitro synthesis of dsRNAs targeting NcGAPDH (dsNcGAPDH), SfGAPDH (dsSfGAPDH), NlGAPDH (dsNlGAPDH), and DcGAPDH (dsDcGAPDH), corresponding to N. cincticeps, S. furcifera, N. lugens, and D. citri, respectively.

RDV-free fourth instar nymphs of N. cincticeps were microinjected with either dsNcGAPDH or dsGFP (~200 ng/insect), as described above. These microinjected leafhoppers were then transferred to healthy rice seedlings for recovery. At 2 days post microinjection, 20 dsRNA-treated N. cincticeps leafhoppers, which had been starved for 2 h, were allowed to feed on a specific region (~1.5 cm in length by 0.3 cm in width) of a rice seedling at the two-leaf stage in a pipe-like cage for 12 h. Approximately 0.05-g samples of tested regions of the rice plants were also examined for their H2O2 content. At 3 days post microinjection, N. cincticeps leafhoppers were tested with RT-qPCR assays to determine the efficiency of RNAi. These experiments were performed on at least three independent biological replicates. At 4 days post injection, EPG assays were conducted to analyze the feeding behavior of N. cincticeps. Each leafhopper was continuously recorded for 5 h, and at least 18 valid biologically independent replicates were recorded and analyzed.

Fourth instar nymphs of S. furcifera and N. lugens ingested corresponding dsSfGAPDH and dsNlGAPDH, respectively, through membrane feeding. A sterile feeding diet containing 80 μL of dsRNA (0.05 μg/μL) and 5% sucrose was enclosed between Parafilm membranes, covering one end of a small pipe-like cage (~2.5 cm in diameter by 4 cm in height). Planthoppers that had been starved for 2 h were confined in these pipe-like cages and allowed to feed on the diet. At 2 days post dsRNA ingestion, 20 dsRNA-treated planthoppers were allowed to feed on a specific region (~1.5 cm in length by 0.3 cm in width) of a rice seedling at the two-leaf stage in a pipe-like cage for 12 h. Approximately 0.05-g samples of tested regions of the plants were also examined for their H2O2 content. At 3 days post dsRNA ingestion, planthoppers were tested with RT-qPCR assays to determine the efficiency of RNAi. Three independent biological replicates were conducted and analyzed.

D. citri adults were microinjected with either dsDcGAPDH or dsGFP (~200 ng/insect). These microinjected psyllids were then transferred to healthy orange jessamine (Murraya Koenig ex Linn.) plants for recovery. At 4 days post microinjection, 20 dsRNA-treated psyllids that had been starved for 2 h were confined in pipe-like cages and allowed to feed on one leaf of an orange jessamine plant for 12 h. Approximately 0.01-g samples of tested regions of the plants were also examined for their H2O2 content. At 5 days post microinjection, D. citri psyllids were tested with RT-qPCR assays to determine the efficiency of RNAi. Three independent biological replicates were conducted and analyzed.

Statistical analyses

Two-tailed Student’s t-tests (for comparisons between two groups) were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software) to analyze the quantitative data from RT-qPCR assays, the number of colocalization puncta identified by immunofluorescence microscopy, levels of H2O2, MDA, –SH groups, GSH, –SSG groups, POD activity, GAPDH activity, acylphosphatase activity, number of feeding sites, transmission rate, survival rate, grain length, and width. Data were also graphed in GraphPad Prism 9.

Multi-variate analysis was conducted using SPSS program version 21 to assess the difference of H2O2 level among rice plants that were not exposed and exposed to R. dorsalis leafhoppers for 12, 24 and 48 h. Multiple comparisons of the means were conducted using a one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test analysis. Any p-values less than 0.05 were taken statistically significant.

The data from the EPG assays were also entered and analyzed using SPSS program version 21. Univariate analysis was used to assess the difference of feeding behavior between the dsGFP- and dsRdGAPDH-treated R. dorsalis leafhoppers that were either RGDV-free or -infected, as well as between the dsGFP- and dsSfGAPDH-treated S. furcifera planthoppers, dsGFP- and dsNlGAPDH-treated N. lugens planthoppers, dsGFP- and dsDcGAPDH-treated D. citri psyllids. Comparisons between two means were conducted using Two-tailed Student’s t-tests. Multi-variate analysis was used to assess the difference of feeding behavior among RGDV-free and -infected R. dorsalis leafhoppers feeding on WT and RdGAPDH-OE plants, as well as among dsGFP- and dsNcGAPDH-treated N. cincticeps leafhoppers that were either RDV-infected or RDV-free. Multiple comparisons of the means were conducted using a one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s HSD test analysis.
