Serum amyloid A and risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in chronic kidney disease: a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis
Authors: Li Li, Hongli Liu, Qinglin Zhang, Hao Jin, Hui Tao, Rong Zhu, Zhongwei Zhou
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundsThe available literature on the correlation between serum amyloid A (SAA) and prognosis of chronic kidney disease (CKD) are limited, and the findings from existing studies are inconclusive. This meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the available evidence regarding the link between SAA and risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in CKD patients. Additionally, we aimed to investigate the potential dose-response relationships, provided that adequate data is accessible.MethodsPubmed and Embase were searched for related literature (last update: 12 July 2023). The pooled effect estimates were calculated using random- or fixed-effects models depending on heterogeneity among studies.ResultsThis meta-analysis incorporated 8 studies encompassing 2331 CKD patients. The findings revealed an 85% increase in all-cause mortality risk [hazard risk (HR) 1.85, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.29–2.65] and a 39% increase in cardiovascular mortality risk (HR 1.07, 95% CI 1.07–1.80) when comparing the highest tertile of baseline SAA levels to the lowest tertile. Furthermore, a positive linear relationship between SAA and all-cause mortality risk was observed (Pnon-linearity = 0.959), with a 17.7% increase in risk for each 10 mg/L SAA increase (HR 1.177, 95% CI 1.055–1.313). Similarly, a linear relationship between SAA and cardiovascular mortality risk was identified (Pnon-linearity = 0.477) with a 19.3% increase in risk for each 10 mg/L SAA increase (HR 1.193, 95% CI 1.025–1.388).ConclusionsThis meta-analysis provided evidence that SAA levels are positively and linearly associated with risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality among CKD patients.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) has emerged as a global health concern, affecting approximately 10% of the general population [1]. Individuals with CKD, particularly those undergoing dialysis, face an elevated susceptibility to significant cardiovascular events and mortality [2]. Numerous factors, such as endothelial dysfunction, vascular calcification, oxidative stress and inflammation, may contribute to the unfavorable prognosis associated with this disease [3]. Currently, inflammation is recognized as a pivotal factor in the development and advancement of CKD, ultimately leading to mortality [4].

Serum amyloid A (SAA) is a prominent acute-phase proteins found in various mammals, including humans [5]. Hepatocytes are the primary source of SAA synthesis, and its levels in circulation increase significantly in response to inflammatory stimuli or endothelial injury [6]. Experimental investigations on apo E-deficient mice have demonstrated that the introduction of specific concentrations of SAA can lead to early renal damage, characterized by renal fibrosis and severe cardiovascular disease [7]. In a recent immunohistochemical assay study, widespread distribution of SAA protein deposition was observed in the glomeruli and tubulointerstitium of both human subjects and mouse models with diabetic kidney disease [8]. Multiple studies have indicated that inflammation plays a crucial role in major cardiovascular events and mortality among CKD patients [4,9,10]. Therefore, SAA, functioning as an inflammatory marker, holds promise as a prognostic biomarker candidate with a biologically plausible mechanism associated with CKD. However, the investigation into the relationship between SAA and CKD prognosis has been limited in recent decades, potentially due to the instability and complexity of SAA measurement techniques compared with commonly used biomarkers like C-reactive protein (CRP), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [11,12]. Furthermore, the existing studies on the prognostic value of SAA for CKD have yielded inconsistent results. Therefore, this meta-analysis was conducted to investigate the relationship between SAA and the risk of cardiovascular and all-cause death in CKD patients, with a specific focus on exploring dose-response relationships.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted following PRISMA guidelines [13]. The PRISMA checklist is provided in Supplementary Material ‘PRISMA_2020_checklist’. The protocol has been registered on INPLASY (registration number: INPLASY202230040).

Search strategy

Electronic databases including PubMed and EMBASE were searched (last update: July 12 2023). The search was performed using the following subject words and free words: (‘serum amyloid A’ OR ‘SAA’) AND (‘chronic kidney disease’ OR ‘end-stage renal disease’ OR ‘peritoneal dialysis’ OR ‘hemodialysis’) AND (‘mortality’ OR ‘death’). In Supplemental Appendix, the detailed retrieval strategy is described. Manual searching was also performed to find out additional eligible studies from the references in related papers.

Selection criteria

Studies were considered eligible if they: (1) were prospective cohort studies, nested case-control studies or post hoc analyses of clinical trials; (2) included individuals with CKD regardless of dialysis; (3) evaluated the relationship of SAA with all-cause and/or cardiovascular mortality; (4) reported a multivariable-adjusted hazard ratio (HR), relative risk (RR), or odds ratio (OR), and their corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI); (5) had a minimum follow-up time of 1 year; (6) were full-text articles written in English.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two authors (Q.Z. and H.T.) conducted a systematic review by independently selecting relevant studies, and extracting data, assessing the methodological quality of the studies. Any disagreement between the authors was resolved by a third author (H.L.). The extracted data included information on first author’s name, publication year, country of study origin, types of patients, sample size (including the percentage of male participants), SAA detection methods, mean age, SAA comparison, event number, adjusted risk estimates, follow-up time, and the adjusted cofounders in final model. If multiple models were reported, we prioritized the most adjusted models for data extraction. The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [14], which assessed three domains: selection, comparability, and outcome, with scores ranging from 0 to 9 points. Studies scoring ≥ 7 points would be considered high quality, 4–6 moderate quality, and ≤3 low quality. It was assessed by Kappa statistics whether the two authors agreed on data extraction and quality assessment. The Kappa coefficient was 0.92 for the data extraction and 0.86 for the methodological quality.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata15.0 (Stata Corp LP, College Station, TX, USA). If studies presented OR or RR, they were deemed to be roughly equivalent to HR. Due to the non-normal distribution of SAA in the majority of the included studies, effect estimates were reported using various approaches, including comparing the top versus bottom halve, third or quartile of SAA distribution, or per unit increase in SAA or log (SAA). To facilitate a direct comparison of study-specific effect estimates, these associations were transformed into HR for the highest versus lowest third of the baseline SAA levels by employing established methodologies [15,16]. Briefly, we first assumed a log-normal distribution of baseline SAA levels and a log-linear association between SAA and mortality risk. Then, the unified scale was estimated as a scaling factor of 2.18 divided by 1.59 times the log HR for a comparison of the upper and lower halves, and 2.18 divided by 2.54 times for the top and bottom quartiles. For those studies that presented per unit change in SAA or log (SAA), they were first converted to 1-standard deviation (SD) change, followed by 2.18 as a scaling factor. Heterogeneity among the pooled studies was assessed through the application of the chi-squared and I2 tests. A statistical significance level of p < .10 was used for the chi-squared test, while a value greater than 50% was considered as an indication of significant heterogeneity. In cases where heterogeneity was found to be significant (I2 > 50% or p < .10), the random-effect model was adopted; otherwise, the fixed-effects model was selected. We performed subgroup analyses to explore heterogeneity sources and gather additional insights. Sensitivity analyses were performed by systematically removing one study at a time to ensure the stability of the overall pooled HR. Publication bias was assessed by visually inspecting funnel plots as well as Egger’s test.

Additionally, dose-response analyses were performed on select studies that furnished sufficient information. To achieve this, studies would have been eligible for inclusion if they: (1) reported a minimum of three categories of SAA levels; (2) provided the range or median/mean exposure for each category of SAA level; (3) disclosed the number of cases or subjects in each SAA category. In instances where the highest SAA categories were unbounded, we assumed the width of the interval to be equivalent to that of the adjacent interval. The generalized least-squares trend (GLST) model, as proposed by Berlin et al. [17] and Orsini et al. [18], was employed to evaluate the potential linear and non-linear dose-response associations between SAA and the risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. This procedure was applied by modeling SAA levels using a restricted cubic spline model with three knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the distribution. If a linear relationship was observed (Pnon−linearity > 0.05) [18], we pooled the study-specific dose-response HR (95% CI) per 10 mg/L increase in SAA levels.

A p-value < .05 was considered statistically significant, unless otherwise indicated.

Results

Literature search

We identified 167 records from the two electronic databases including PubMed and Embase. After excluding duplicates (n = 64), the remaining 103 articles were assessed based on the titles and abstracts. Following the evaluation, 88 studies were excluded, leaving 15 publications eligible for full-text analysis. Among these, 7 publications were further excluded due to non-English publications and no outcomes of interest. Finally, 8 published studies [19–26] were deemed to be eligible and were included in the final analysis (Figure 1).

The flow chart of the study selection process.

Characteristics of selected studies

The baseline characteristics of the selected studies are summarized in Table 1. Eight studies, including two in America, three in Serbia, one in Germany, one in China, and one in Austria, were published between 1999 and 2016 and evaluated a total of 2331 patients with CKD. Six studies enrolled hemodialysis patients, and one study included non-dialysis CKD and one peritoneal dialysis patients. The number of included individuals ranged 87 to 1152, and the proportion of male patients ranged from 44.3% to 68.6%. The mean age of participants at baseline ranged from 55.1 to 66.3 years. The median follow-up period lasted 2.0–4.8 years. According to the NOS, three studies were deemed of high quality, and the other five were rated as moderate quality.

Risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality with the highest versus lowest SAA tertile

The included studies all reported on the relationship between SAA levels and all-cause death. The risk estimates were converted into a comparison of highest versus lowest third of SAA concentrations. The overall pooled analysis indicated a significant association between SAA levels and all-cause mortality (HR 1.85, 95% CI 1.29–2.65, p = .001) (Figure 2A). However, there was significant heterogeneity among the studies (I2 = 62.9.2%, p = .009), so a random-effects model was used.

Forest plot of all-cause mortality (A) and cardiovascular mortality (B) for the highest versus lowest tertile of circulating SAA levels. HR: hazard risk; CI: confidence interval.

Additionally, three studies [19,23,24] examined the relationship between SAA levels and the risk of cardiovascular mortality. The pooled analysis also found a significant association in this context (HR 1.39, 95% CI 1.07–1.80, p = .013) (Figure 2B). Given the lack of significant heterogeneity, a fixed-effects model was adopted (I2 = 16.8%, p = .301).

Dose-response relationship of SAA with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality risks

Three studies [19,24,26] that yielded adequate data were incorporated into the dose-response analysis pertaining to the relationship between SAA and all-cause mortality. The findings revealed a positive linear relationship between the two variables (Pnon-linearity = 0.959; Figure 3A). The linear model showed that the risk was increased by 17.7% with each 10 mg/L increase in SAA levels (HR = 1.177, 95% CI: 1.055–1.313, p = .004).

Dose-response association of SAA with risk of all-cause mortality (A) and cardiovascular mortality (B).

Two studies [19, 24] were considered eligible for inclusion in the dose-response analysis concerning the relationship between SAA and cardiovascular mortality. Similarly, a positive linear relationship between these factors was observed (Pnon-linearity = 0.477; Figure 3B). According to this model, each 10 mg/L elevation in SAA levels was linked to a 19.3% heightened risk of cardiovascular death (HR = 1.193, 95% CI: 1.025–1.388, p = .022).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted to investigate the relationship between SAA and all-cause mortality in studies that compared the highest SAA tertile to the lowest tertile. Stratification was performed based on study location, study design, patient types, sample size, SAA detection methods, mean patient age, follow-up time, statistical adjustment for hypertension or blood pressure parameters, hs-CRP or CRP, or diabetes. As shown in Table 2, stratified analyses indicated no significant associations between SAA and all-cause death in subgroups of Chinese patients (p = .602), peritoneal dialysis patients (p = .179), post hoc analysis (p = .110), sample size ≥200 (p = .081), and immunonephelometry methods (p = .055). Similar findings were observed in all the other stratifications, consistent with the overall pooled estimate. Although heterogeneity was reduced or disappeared in some subgroups, it remained statistically significant in the majority of subgroups.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the impact of individual studies on the overall pooled estimate of all-cause mortality risk when comparing the highest and lowest tertile of SAA levels. The results of this analysis indicated that no single study significantly influenced the overall pooled estimate (Figure 4). However, when employing visual funnel plot inspection and Eggers test to detect potential publication bias, an asymmetrical funnel plot was observed (Figure 5) and the p value of Egger’s test was found to be .015, suggesting the potential existence of publication bias.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this meta-analysis represents the initial examination of the association between SAA levels and the risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality CKD in individuals with CKD. Our findings indicate that individuals in the top tertile of SAA levels had an 85% higher risk of all-cause death and a 39% higher risk of cardiovascular mortality compared to those in the bottom tertile among CKD patients. Furthermore, our analysis reveals a positive linear relationship between SAA levels and both mortality outcomes, with a 17.7% increase in all-cause mortality, and 19.3% increase in cardiovascular mortality per 10 mg/L SAA increment. These findings suggest that higher levels of SAA serve as a significant predictor for both all-cause and cardiovascular death in CKD patients, with the strength of these associations being dependent on the dosage.

CKD patients frequently experience a range of comorbidities, including inflammation, diabetes, and hypertension [27]. It is noteworthy that these comorbidities interact with one another, contributing to the development of CKD [28]. In the present study, we performed a subgroup analysis that involved stratifying the participants based on whether these comorbidities were adjusted for. Our analysis revealed that the association between SAA and the risk of total death remained statistically significant in the subgroups where adjustments were made for diabetes, hypertension, blood pressure parameters, hs-CRP, or CRP. The findings suggest that the observed risk-relationship between SAA and mortality may be independent of diabetes, hypertension, or other inflammatory markers, such as CRP. Among the 8 studies included in this meta-analysis, 6 investigated the relationship between both SAA and CRP levels and the risk of all-cause or cardiovascular mortality [19–23,25]. One study indicated that SAA, rather than CRP, exhibited a significant correlation with all-cause mortality [22]. Two studies reported that both SAA and CRP were significantly and positively correlated with all-cause or cardiovascular death [19,25]. However, two studies demonstrated that increased CRP levels were associated with all-cause or cardiovascular mortality, while SAA levels were not [20,23]. Interestingly, another study found no significant association between either SAA or CRP levels and the total mortality risk [21]. A prior meta-analysis has also demonstrated that elevated circulating CPR is an independent prognosticator for both all-cause and cardiovascular death in CKD patients [29]. SAA and CRP are positive acute phase proteins in humans, exhibiting significantly increased serum concentration during systemic inflammation. However, in comparison to CRP, SAA exhibits a broader range of variation and can surge by up to 1000-fold, reaching peak circulating concentrations exceeding 1000 mg/L during infection [30]. Over the past few decades, several studies have been conducted to assess the comparative prognostic efficacy of CRP and SAA. Some of these studies have indicated that SAA may serve as a more reliable indicator of disease severity than CRP in specific conditions such as sepsis, acute pancreatitis, and COVID-19 [31–33]. A recent investigation demonstrated that SAA outperformed CRP in terms of prognostic accuracy for predicting 90-day mortality, overall survival and unfavorable outcomes in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage [34]. Similarly, a retrospective cohort study revealed that preoperative SAA levels exhibited superior predictive performance compared to CRP in determining overall survival among patients with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [35]. These data suggests that SAA may serve as a more reliable prognostic indicator for various diseases, although our analysis did not reveal clear superiority of SAA over CRP.

Cardiovascular disease is the primary cause of mortality among patients with CKD [1,2]. This meta-analysis included three studies that provided data on the total number of deaths as well as deaths specifically attributed to cardiovascular disease [19,23,24]. Our findings indicate that nearly half of the deceased CKD patients had succumbed to cardiovascular disease. It is widely recognized that atherosclerosis serves as the fundamental pathological basis for cardiovascular disease [36]. Furthermore, accumulating evidence suggests that SAA not only serves as a potential biomarker for atherosclerosis but also plays an important role in its pathogenesis [7,36,37]. The expression of SAA was observed in macrophages, smooth muscle cells, and foam cells within atherosclerotic plaques, and it possesses the ability to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, which plays a crucial role in the development of atherosclerosis [38]. Furthermore, the inflammatory process significantly alters the distribution of SAA and the structure of high-density lipoprotein (HDL), leading to a reduction in the antiatherosclerotic effect of HDL [39]. Notably, the abnormal proliferation of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMC) is a distinctive characteristic of atherosclerosis, and SAA is implicated in inducing the transformation of contractile VSMC into synthetic forms through the p38 MAPK signaling pathway [40]. Therefore, SAA should be regarded as more than just a serum biomarker of atherosclerosis; it actively participates in the development process of atherosclerosis, which in turn plays a substantial role in the progression mechanism of CKD.

There are some potential limitations in the present meta-analysis. First, out of the 8 studies included, only one study recruited a sample size of more than 1000 individuals with CKD, while the remaining 7 cohorts had relatively small patient numbers. Consequently, the limited sample size in some studies may have affected their statistical power. As a result, the overall sample size in our pooled analysis was also insufficiently large, which could potentially diminish the persuasiveness of the pooled results, particularly in relation to cardiovascular mortality and subgroup analyses. Second, although the ELISA assay was employed in all the studies focusing on the dose-response relationship to ascertain SAA levels [19,24,26], it is important to note that reference range values for SAA may vary across different laboratories and producers. Furthermore, the limited sample size in this analysis may also restrict the statistical power of the dose-response relationship, which was similar to the first limitation. Consequently, caution should be exercised when interpreting the dose-response relationship between SAA and mortality risks. Third, despite the inclusion of studies that accounted for numerous confounding variables, it is important to note that the adjusted models varied among the studies, potentially introducing biased risk due to unadjusted factors Notably, only one study [26] among the included ones considered renal function as a confounder. Furthermore, the conversion of effect estimates into tertile analysis in certain studies may have also introduced bias. Fourth, only a single study involving non-dialysis CKD patients was incorporated into this meta-analysis, although this study did demonstrate a significant and positive correlation between SAA levels and an increased risk of all-cause mortality. However, caution must be exercised when generalizing our findings to encompass all CKD patients. Fifth, there was a relatively large heterogeneity in the meta-analysis concerning all-cause death. Despite observing a reduction or elimination of heterogeneity in certain subgroups following stratified analysis, it persisted in the majority of subgroups. Therefore, our subgroup analyses were unable to comprehensively elucidate the origin of heterogeneity. Finally, the presence of an asymmetric funnel plot and the results of Egger’s test indicate the possibility of publication bias. However, it is crucial to acknowledge that the asymmetric shape can also be attributed to the between-study heterogeneity, and publication bias is not the sole source of this asymmetry [41].

Conclusion

The present meta-analysis suggests that elevated circulating SAA is correlated with increased risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in individuals with CKD. Moreover, a 10 mg/L increase in SAA concentrations appears to contribute to a nearly 20% increase in the risk of mortality from CKD. To validate these findings, future prospective studies with large sample sizes are necessary.
