A roadmap to key traits of invasive Drosophilidae
Authors: Gwenaëlle Deconninck, Bréa Raynaud‐Berton, Méghan Boulembert, Olivier Chabrerie, Aude Couty, Franck Dedeine, Patrice Eslin, Vincent Foray, Romain Georges, Patricia Gibert, Laurence Mouton, Sylvain Pincebourde, David Renault, Christelle Suppo, Hervé Colinet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBiological invasions have intensified in recent decades, mostly driven by international trade and travel, raising significant concerns, particularly regarding insect pests. Once non‐native species establish, they can disrupt natural ecosystem stability, undermine agroecosystem sustainability and cause substantial economic losses. The urgency to anticipate these socio‐economic impacts has accelerated research into the traits and processes that predispose certain species to invasion success. Our review examines the factors contributing to invasion success, using the well‐documented case of Drosophilidae as a model taxon given the extensive literature on this family. The invasion of Drosophila suzukii is the most well studied, yet it represents just one example among several Drosophilidae successful invasions, including those from the genera Drosophila, Scaptomyza and Zaprionus. Their traits and adaptive capacities have enabled them to overcome environmental barriers, facilitating their global spread and establishment. We first explore the selective forces acting on pioneer individuals and their ability to establish reproducing populations. We then analyse the roles of abiotic factors (through phenotypic plasticity, tolerance to thermal and water stress, synergies between multiple stressors) and biotic factors (through the exploitation of novel trophic niches, resistance to competition and predation, symbiont‐mediated assistance) in shaping invasion success. A nuanced understanding of how these constraints interact is essential for predicting and managing the proliferation of invasive Drosophilidae and other non‐native species. We propose that successful invasive species do not necessarily excel in one single trait but rather perform adequately across multiple traits and processes. In this review, we found support in the literature for 14 key traits and processes of Drosophilidae biology that facilitate a species' ability to become invasive and provide future perspectives to address critical knowledge gaps, paving the way towards a comprehensive understanding of invasion success.

INTRODUCTION

Numbers of biological invasions have escalated in recent decades with no sign of abatement (Seebens et al., 2017). Hereafter, an invasive species is defined as a species that (i) is non‐native to a specified geographic area, (ii) was introduced by humans (intentionally or unintentionally) or has expanded its geographic range as an indirect consequence of human activities (e.g. climate change) and (iii) has successfully established and spread in its introduced area (Iannone et al., 2020). Potentially, non‐native species (also often referred to as exotic, non‐indigenous or alien) can deeply impact the stability of natural ecosystems and the sustainability of agroecosystems, resulting in severe economic losses (Diagne et al., 2021; Renault et al., 2022). The average annual cost of invasive species worldwide was estimated at ~US$165 billion in 2020 (Renault et al., 2022). Without prompt and urgent action, the number and impacts of biological invasions will continue to rise, degrading natural capital and impeding sustainable development (Henry et al., 2023).

Insects are among the most successful invaders (Bonnamour, Gippet & Bertelsmeier, 2021) and include many pest and disease agent species (Neupane, Larsen & Ries, 2024). Given the increasing impacts caused by insect invasion worldwide, comprehensive knowledge of the mechanisms underlying successful invasions is crucial. In Europe, insect invasions rank second after plants (Roy et al., 2020), with first records of 7,918 and 16,348 non‐native species, respectively (Bonnamour et al., 2021). Many of these insects originate from other continents (Roques et al., 2009) and their invasion routes are often related to the globalisation of human‐mediated transport routes (Levine & D'Antonio, 2003).

This review aims to identify to what extent deep fundamental knowledge of a species is required to reveal the key factors and their interactions facilitating its invasion success. We focus on the Drosophilidae, an insect family that comprises >3,900 species (Merritt, Courtney & Keiper, 2009), of which 18 non‐native Drosophilidae have been reported in Europe (Skuhravá, Martinez & Roques, 2010) and 57 worldwide (Turner et al., 2024). From these species, we selected 36 species that are considered invasive and not only established (with more than one record in the introduced country or island) (see online Supporting Information, Table S1). These species, including representatives of the genera Drosophila, Scaptomyza and Zaprionus, can differ markedly in their behavioural, ecophysiological, trophic and seasonal ecology, implying that invasion success or failure is not linked to one single key feature but rather to a combination of traits and their interplay with the environmental conditions of the recipient ecosystems. Eleven species are currently considered cosmopolitan due to the global scale of their establishment and some, such as Drosophila suzukii (Mastumura, 1931) and Zaprionus indianus (Gupta, 1970), are defined as fruit pests (Walsh et al., 2011; Asplen et al., 2015; Panel et al., 2022). Drosophilidae species are ideal models for studying the biology of invasion due to a combination of key advantages: (i) a well‐resolved taxonomy and phylogeny, enabling accurate inference of invasion routes and demographic history (Markow & O'Grady, 2006); (ii) extensive genetic, ecological, and physiological data are available for many species, facilitating integrative research (Merritt et al., 2009); (iii) a long history of use in experimental biology, supporting robust mechanistic approaches (Markow & O'Grady, 2006); (iv) a broad range of invasion outcomes, from non‐invasive to globally invasive species like D. suzukii (Asplen et al., 2015); (v) ease of breeding and maintenance under controlled conditions, while also being amenable to field studies (Feder, Blair & Figueras, 1997); (vi) a short life cycle, allowing for the study of multiple generations and rapid evolutionary processes (Gibert et al., 2016); and (vii) remarkable adaptability, as demonstrated by latitudinal clines in invaded areas, further enhancing their suitability for studying invasion processes from genes to phenotypes (Huey & Pascual, 2009). These characteristics make them particularly well suited for integrative studies on the many factors driving biological invasions. The considerable literature on Drosophilidae can also serve as a valuable resource, offering insights into the factors contributing to their invasion success.

In this review, we leverage this knowledge to explore comprehensively the genetic, physiological and ecological determinants of invasion success of Drosophilidae species, with the aim of identifying broader principles applicable to invasive species dynamics. We examine the global abiotic and biotic constraints that invading Drosophilidae can encounter during the invasion process. First, we consider the routes of invasion and the selective forces operating during the invasion continuum. Second, we discuss the role of abiotic factors, such as temperature, desiccation and the potential synergy between stressors, for invasion success in new areas. Third, we discuss the role of biotic factors, including trophic niche, competition, predation, facilitation and symbionts in shaping invasion success. A nuanced understanding of how these constraints interact is essential for predicting and, ultimately, managing the proliferation of invasive Drosophilidae and other non‐native species arriving in new environments. This review provides a road map of the various processes that must be integrated to achieve a comprehensive understanding of successful biological invasions. Finally, we consider perspectives for addressing crucial knowledge gaps and opening new research avenues in the light of information collated in this review.

DROSOPHILIDAE ROUTES OF INVASION AND SELECTIVE FORCES ALONG THE INTRODUCTION–NATURALISATION–INVASION CONTINUUM

The invasion process of any non‐native species beyond its native range consists of three main stages: (i) the movement of individuals from their native range to a new area; (ii) the foundation of a reproducing population by pioneer individuals; and (iii) the further spread of the introduced population to different locations, as well as possible development of local adaptations to different habitats within the invaded area (Daly et al., 2023). Each of these steps is critical in a successful invasion. Here, we summarise general aspects of these invasion steps before focusing on how they relate to Drosophilidae invasion traits (see Fig. 1).

Transportation and introduction pathways

The anthropogenic dimension in biological invasions is unquestionable, with the geographic expansion of many non‐native species tracking major routes of human travel and trade [‘hitch‐hiking’ (Verdasca et al., 2021; Lanner et al., 2022)]. Transportation of fruit and plants by passengers is an important introduction pathway for non‐native insects, including Diptera (Liebhold et al., 2006; Pace et al., 2022) and the propagation success of Drosophilidae species depends strongly on their association with human activities. While some species, such as Drosophila melanogaster (Meigen, 1830) and D. simulans (Sturtevant, 1919), have dispersed naturally over a long period (Lachaise & Silvain, 2004), their close association with humans, such as in agrosystems or urban structures (David & Tsacas, 1981; Keller, 2007; Ulmer et al., 2024), have favoured unintentional transportation through trade and have significantly accelerated their global spread (David & Capy, 1988). In particular, the transportation of infested commercial fruit is recognised as a significant factor in the spread of the invasive Chymomyza amoena (Loew, 1862), C. procnemis (Williston, 1896), D. suzukii, and Z. indianus (Okada 1976; Burla & Bächli, 1992; Band, 1994; Cini et al., 2014; Cini, Ioriatti & Anfora, 2012; Bragard et al., 2022; Table S1). Similarly, transport of eucalyptus contributed to the dispersal of D. flavohirta (Malloch, 1924) from Australia to Africa and the US (McEvey, Aulard & Raliosa‐Randrianasolo, 1989; Grimaldi et al., 2015).

Drosophila melanogaster and D. simulans also demonstrate remarkable ability to disperse in different climatic regions without human intervention. Their ability to thrive in both tropical and temperate regions points to a particular sequence of selection regimes across the invasion process. The existence of a distinct Far Eastern lineage of D. melanogaster with unique morphological and physiological traits suggests a range expansion that may have begun long before the last glacial period (David & Capy, 1988). Considerable variation exists in natural dispersal rates and range shifts, both within and among species. An illustrative example is that of Drosophila subobscura (Collin, 1936), which exhibited rapid north–south dispersal of ~850 km/year in the Americas during the first decade after introduction. By contrast, its east–west dispersal was only 85 km/year. This provides a classic example of accelerated dispersal in favourable environments (Williamson, 2009). The combination of natural dispersal coupled with unintentional anthropogenic assistance has probably fuelled the successful global invasion of these Drosophilidae (David & Capy, 1988; Tidon, Leite & Leão, 2003).

The establishment of reproducing populations

A non‐native species can only persist in its new range if pioneer individuals are able to form a reproducing population that will ensure the production of future generations. Establishing a viable population with a small number of individuals can be challenging (Lockwood, Cassey & Blackburn, 2009) as a strong reduction in population size (i.e. a demographic bottleneck) resulting from the introduction process can have important deleterious consequences and increase the risk of extinction (Charlesworth & Willis, 2009; Blackburn, Lockwood & Cassey, 2015; Kołodziejczyk et al., 2025).

Small populations, such as those of recent introductions, can suffer from low‐density Allee effects, with individual fitness decreasing due to a reduction of positive interactions between conspecifics (Taylor & Hastings, 2005; Liebhold & Tobin, 2008; Tobin, Berec & Liebhold, 2011). For instance, in allogamous sexual species, individuals may not find mates when the population density is too low (Gascoigne et al., 2009). While the influence of mate‐finding failure on the Allee effect has been studied in some invasive species (e.g. the gypsy moth, Lymantria dispar; Contarini et al., 2009), its effect on invasive Drosophilidae remains unknown (Fauvergue, 2013). The low density of an introduced species compared to native species can also increase the risk of reproductive interference, with fitness reduction as a cascading consequence (Kyogoku & Wheatcroft, 2020). For instance, satyrisation was found to occur experimentally between D. melanogaster males and D. suzukii females, reducing the fitness of D. suzukii (Cerasti et al., 2023).

Traits related to survival (e.g. foraging efficiency) may also be affected when population density is low (Berec, Angulo & Courchamp, 2007). For instance, in D. melanogaster, increased adult densities on apple substrates prior to larval development resulted in higher larval survival and larger emerging flies, likely due to reduced fungal growth on the substrate (Wertheim et al., 2002). However, this effect is species and substrate dependent as it was not observed in D. subobscura on Sorbus fruit (Hoffmeister & Rohlfs, 2001).

Inbreeding and its negative consequences on individual fitness (i.e. inbreeding depression) associated with the maintenance of deleterious alleles through genetic drift (i.e. drift load) are well‐known phenomena that can severely affect small populations and lead to their extinction (Bijlsma, Bundgaard & Boerema, 2000; Charlesworth & Willis, 2009; but see Kołodziejczyk et al., 2025). In this regard, introduced drosophilids might have an advantage, as widespread representatives, including Drosophila hydei (Sturtevant, 1921), D. melanogaster, D. repleta (Wollaston, 1858) and D. simulans, suffer less from inbreeding depression than do species that are (warm) climate specialists when population size declines (Bechsgaard et al., 2013). Phenotypic plasticity may also counter the bottleneck effect by increasing the probability that a genotype remains adapted to novel conditions (Estoup et al., 2016).

Genetic diversity and adaptive capacities of non‐native populations during the expansion phase

The fact that many species have successfully invaded new areas and adapt rapidly to new ecological conditions, despite demographic bottlenecks that potentially reduce their genetic diversity, has long been considered a paradox (Allendorf & Lundquist, 2003). More recently, however, the paradoxical nature of successful invasion has been debated (Estoup et al., 2016; Schrieber & Lachmuth, 2017) as the loss of genetic diversity may not be as substantial as previously thought, or may be more pronounced for neutral genetic markers than for ecologically relevant quantitative traits (Reed & Frankman, 2007). In some cases, even if introduced populations do show lower genetic diversity compared to their source populations, they may not face any significant adaptive challenge in the invaded habitat (Reed & Frankman, 2007). Bottlenecks of intermediate intensity can also purge the deleterious alleles that cause inbreeding depression in the introduced population (Estoup et al., 2016).

In D. suzukii and D. subobscura, reconstruction of invasion routes has highlighted the occurrence of moderate to severe bottlenecks during their invasions (Pascual et al., 2007; Adrion et al., 2014; Fraimout et al., 2017). However, these studies also showed that the genetic diversity of introduced populations is only slightly reduced compared to the original populations (Pascual et al., 2007; Adrion et al., 2014). This could be a result of the large effective size and genetic diversity of the original populations, which allows the retention of a substantial amount of genetic variation even with a small number of founding individuals (Pascual et al., 2007; Adrion et al., 2014). Additionally, in Drosophilidae, introduction events rarely occur only once (Yassin et al., 2009a; Lavrinienko et al., 2017), and admixture between introduced populations (Fraimout et al., 2017) likely contributes to maintaining genetic diversity and adaptive capacity in these populations. This scenario of multiple introduction pathways and admixture maintaining adaptive capacity is particularly well documented in D. suzukii (Fraimout et al., 2017; Lavrinienko et al., 2017; Camus et al., 2025). The high rate of population growth of non‐native species can also mitigate the impact of bottlenecks on genetic variability (Nei, Maruyama & Chakraborty, 1975).

Ultimately, the genetic paradox of invasive species assumes that the mutation rate is low and constant during the invasion process (Estoup et al., 2016). However, numerous examples indicate that the activity of transposable elements (TEs) can be significantly increased in response to environmental disturbances, generating genetic variation and facilitating adaptation (Oliver & Greene, 2009). The role of TEs in the adaptive capacity of invasive species represents an important area for investigation (Stapley, Santure & Dennis, 2015). For instance, in D. melanogaster, several TEs were positively selected during the colonisation of the North American continent (González et al., 2008), and one of these TEs (pogo) was associated with increased resistance to a natural xenobiotic and a synthetic insecticide (Mateo, Ullastres & González, 2014). Similarly, a recent study revealed 15 potentially adaptive TE insertions in D. suzukii, six of which were associated with its recent invasion (Mérel et al., 2021).

Finally, beyond specific mechanisms generating or maintaining diversity, comparative genomics suggests that invasion success may rely on a shared ‘genetic toolkit’ across species. El‐Sabrout et al. (2025) recently demonstrated genomic parallelism in two distant tropical drosophilid lineages simultaneously invading a desert environment. They found correlated differentiation in genes, particularly those associated with thermal adaptation (acclimation and thermosensation) and circadian rhythms. These findings imply that, despite distinct evolutionary histories, a limited set of common genes and functions may be pivotal in facilitating the establishment of drosophilids in novel environments. These genomic footprints of climatic adaptation underscore the decisive role of environmental filters (see Fig. 1).

ROLE OF ABIOTIC FACTORS IN NICHE COMPATIBILITY

When an invading species expands its distribution, it encounters new environments that are more or less aligned with its requirements, i.e. its ecological niche. The abiotic environment in the invaded area can thus limit the establishment and further spread of the non‐native species. Adaptation to such stressful conditions is a key factor influencing invasion success (Sexton, Mckay & Sala, 2002; Lee, 2003; Piiroinen, Lyytinen & Lindström, 2013). Temperature and humidity commonly exert strong selective pressure on non‐native populations in the recipient ecosystems, resulting in the evolution of tolerance traits (El‐Sabrout et al., 2025). For example, because D. subobscura populations were regularly exposed to stressful abiotic conditions when invading low‐latitude regions of North America, they faced strong selective pressure that led to better tolerance to desiccation and starvation (Gilchrist et al., 2008). Depending on the species, other abiotic factors such as available oxygen concentration may also provide selective pressure on introduced Drosophilidae populations (Pincebourde & Casas, 2016). Environmental matching is often among the main factors explaining invasion/establishment of non‐native species across taxa (Daly et al., 2023). By assessing how abiotic stressors shape the ecological niches of invading Drosophilidae, we can better anticipate their establishment and spread in new environments, thereby providing information to improve strategies for conservation and ecosystem management.

Thermal stress and plasticity

Temperature is a key environmental factor that drives all physiological processes by acting on biochemical rates, with consequences from cell to community levels (Colinet et al., 2015; Ma, Ma & Pincebourde, 2021). Temperature dictates growth, reproduction and survival, and is often the primary environmental factor determining the geographical distributions of insects (Kimura, 1988; Kellerman et al., 2009; Hoffmann & Sgró, 2011), including Drosophila species at regional (Deconninck et al., 2024b) and global (David & Tsacas, 1981; Ulmer et al., 2022) scales. It has been suggested that populations originating from more thermally variable environments (e.g. temperate species) are more likely to become invasive than those from more stable areas (e.g. the tropics) because they can endure thermal stresses better (Lee & Gelembiuk, 2008). However, while species native to temperate environments may have theoretical invasion advantages (Kellerman et al., 2009), the successful establishment of non‐native drosophilids in diverse climatic zones indicates that their great adaptability and plastic responses to temperature variation and stress are crucial to their invasion success. For instance, while members of the genus Zaprionus of tropical origin were generally assumed to be unable to colonise temperate regions (Araripe et al., 2004), recent records of establishment in Europe (Raspi, Grassi & Benelli, 2014; Georges, Yassin & Colinet, 2024) suggest that even representatives of Afrotropical origin can survive the harsh winter conditions experienced in their exotic range. A climatic niche shift may have taken place enabling population adaptation to colder climates (da Mata et al., 2010). Such observations may also indicate that environmental conditions in southern Europe are now suitable for the establishment of new species from tropical regions, and these regions will become even more favourable with climate change.

The ability to tolerate thermal stress through plastic responses (i.e. by acclimation) and/or to adapt to abiotic stressors is critical in determining the range limits of invasive drosophilid populations (Gilchrist et al., 2008). Enhanced tolerance to temperature extremes (both cold and heat) can be acquired through plastic responses such as rapid cold or heat hardening (in the short term) and more gradual acclimation responses (Colinet & Hoffmann, 2012). Drosophilids are notorious for exhibiting remarkable capacities for acclimation to thermal stresses through plastic thermal responses (Gibert & Huey, 2001; Hoffmann, Sørensen & Loeschcke, 2003b; Ayrinhac et al., 2004; Enriquez & Colinet, 2019), highlighting the pivotal role of acclimation in facilitating invasion success. Drosophila kikkawai (Burla, 1954) is a non‐pest species whose ability to increase tolerance significantly to colder and drier conditions through plasticity facilitated its invasion (Parkash, Lambhod & Pathak, 2021). Acclimatory responses differ between the cold and warm ends of the temperature gradient (Colinet et al., 2013). Studies indicated that Drosophilidae exhibit greater thermal plasticity and acclimation ability to cold temperatures compared to heat. This difference in thermal plasticity is attributed to physiological and phylogenetic constraints that make heat acclimation less effective than cold acclimation (Overgaard et al., 2011; Kellermann et al., 2012a,b; Colinet et al., 2013), a limitation that might be crucial for predicting the future distribution and managing the impact of these invasive species.

In Drosophila nasuta (Lamb, 1914), an Asian species that invaded Brazil about ten years ago, larger wing sizes were observed in cold environments within just a few years post‐invasion (Santos, Garcia & Montes, 2024). Similar morphological changes – specifically, increased wing size in colder regions – have been documented in other invasive drosophilids, including D. suzukii, D. subobscura, and Z. indianus (Gilchrist & Huey, 2004; Yassin, David & Bitner‐Mathé, 2009b; Fraimout et al., 2018), suggesting that cold temperatures in new environments may drive wing size increase as part of a rapid evolutionary adaptation. Large‐winged insects are more effective at absorbing heat, making this trait advantageous in colder regions (Douglas, 1981). Additionally, research on various Drosophila species has shown improved flying ability in cold conditions associated with increased wing area and length (Frazier et al., 2008; Fraimout et al., 2018; Huisamen et al., 2022). Hence, this pattern of wing size variation in response to colder climates appears to be a common adaptive response among non‐native drosophilids, likely enhancing their survival and facilitating their expansion in cooler regions.

In addition to physiological and morphological plasticity in response to thermal stress, behavioural plasticity can also play an important role in invasion success. Drosophilids inhabiting different climatic environments can have species‐specific behavioural responses to temperature as part of their life‐history strategies (Ito & Awasaki, 2022). For instance, optimal temperature for daily locomotor activity and sleep of different species approximately matches the temperatures they frequently encounter in their habitats (Ito & Awasaki, 2022). Non‐native species can shift their daily time of activity when they progress into novel thermal environments as evidenced by invasive Zaprionus populations which exhibit rapid genetic differentiation regulating sleep and circadian rhythms in invaded areas (El‐Sabrout et al., 2025). In invasive populations of D. subobscura, thermoregulatory behaviours (e.g. seasonal shift in activity time) enable compensation for seasonal and latitudinal variation in climate (Huey & Pascual, 2009). This behavioural flexibility was not sufficient, however, to prevent a decrease in body temperature in cooler seasons and at higher latitudes (Huey & Pascual, 2009). Furthermore, thermoregulatory behaviours may confer the ability to find and exploit microclimatic niches in the invaded area that provide conditions similar to those of the native area, despite the overall shift in larger scale regional climate conditions. This aspect of microclimatic niche similarity between native and invasive ranges may explain why invasive ants display a smaller (macro)climatic niche shift than non‐invasive alien ants (Bates, Ollier & Bertelsmeier, 2020). While this hypothesis remains to be investigated for invasive Drosophilidae, several key traits linked to behavioural thermoregulation (e.g. thermal preference) have been assessed in various fruit fly species. Overall, Drosophilidae are capable of selecting a relatively large thermal range (Dillon et al., 2009; Kinzner et al., 2019; MacLean et al., 2019) indicating that a portion of the population is likely to encounter and satisfy microclimatic requirements.

Winter and overwintering

Overwintering is a crucial phase of the life cycle for many insect species and this phase can act as an important bottleneck for invading populations. For instance, D. suzukii can overwinter either as winter‐acclimated dormant adults (in reproductive quiescence) or as juvenile stages (pupae survive winter effectively when buried in the soil; Kaçar et al., 2016), developing gradually during winter (Raynaud‐Berton et al., 2024). The use of mixed strategies for overwintering may contribute to this species' invasion success in several ways. Overwintering as both dormant adults and juvenile stages may reduce the threat from different environmental conditions (Raynaud‐Berton et al., 2024). For instance, if winter conditions are milder, the juvenile stages may have a better chance of surviving and emerging when conditions become favourable in spring, while adults may become active and exhaust their energy stores before suitable breeding sites become available. In colder winters, overwintering females of D. suzukii and D. melanogaster retain their reproductive capacity, enabling them to relaunch populations in spring if they successfully survive the winter (Hoffmann et al., 2003a; Panel et al., 2020; Cloutier, Guay & Champagne‐Cauchon, 2022). The ability to overwinter at different developmental stages suggests that D. suzukii is poised to take advantage opportunistically of any early warm spells as the winter comes to an end, facilitating rapid population increase when conditions become suitable for reproduction (Deconninck et al., 2024a, 2025a). Such adaptability and plasticity have been suggested to have played a crucial role in the rapid range expansion of this species and its ability to thrive in diverse environments (Little, Chapman & Hillier, 2020).

Drosophilids can display a variety of overwintering strategies. For instance, in six Eurasian species of the D. obscura group, two overwinter without diapause [D. subobscura, D. guanche (Monclus, 1977)], two in pupal diapause [D. alpina (Burla, 1948), D. subsilvestris (Hardy & Kaneshiro, 1968)] and two in reproductive diapause [D. bifasciata (Pomini, 1940), D. obscura (Fallen, 1823)] (Goto, 1999). Species with diapause exhibited higher cold resistance and their distribution extended further into colder northern areas. Again, having more than one effective strategy for surviving winter can contribute to invasion success, as it allows the exploitation of different ecological niches to survive diverse and changing environmental conditions. The diversity of overwintering strategies across species may also suggest rapid expansion at an early phase of adaptation to temperate climates (Goto, 1999). It has been suggested that the D. subobscura subgroup exhibits an ancestral trait in climatic adaptations (lack of diapause), similar to tropical species in the D. melanogaster group, which also lack a true diapause mechanism (Saunders, Henrich & Gilbert, 1989), although it is also possible that the D. subobscura subgroup lost the diapause mechanism during its colonisation of temperate regions. Kimura (1988) reported a case in Drosophila triauraria (Bock & Wheeler, 1972) where subtropical populations of this species were assumed to have lost winter reproductive dormancy following secondary colonisation of subtropical areas. Clear shifts in dormancy incidence also exist in D. melanogaster and they vary predictably with latitude (Schmidt et al., 2005). Hence, it seems that plasticity in overwintering strategies, including an increased incidence of dormancy in cold‐invaded areas or its loss in milder invaded areas, may be an integral life‐history component of invasive drosophilid populations.

Summer and heat stress

Heat stress may act as a critical limiting factor influencing the ability of Drosophilidae to establish and sustain populations in new and potentially hostile climates. Like any ectotherm, drosophilids have specific thermal limits beyond which their survival, development and reproduction are severely compromised (Hoffmann, 2010; Hangartner et al., 2015; MacLean et al., 2019; Colinet & Kustre, 2025). Population growth and successful range expansion can be affected by summer temperatures and thus heat tolerance (Xue & Ma, 2020; Alruiz et al., 2022). The capacity to tolerate heat stress may be advantageous for non‐native species with the potential for rapid expansion in subtropical and tropical regions, as predicted for D. suzukii in the African and Australian subtropics (dos Santos et al., 2017; Ørsted & Ørsted, 2019). Heat stress during summer thus poses a significant challenge to the invasion success of drosophilids by directly affecting their physiological functions and reproductive fitness (Green, Moore & Sial, 2019; van Heerwaarden & Sgrò, 2021; Grandela et al., 2024). High summer temperatures can affect populations directly by inducing cellular damage and reducing metabolic efficiency (Krebs & Loeschcke, 1994; Sørensen, Loeschcke & Kristensen, 2012), and indirectly by impairing gamete production and embryo development (Araripe et al., 2004; van Heerwaarden & Sgro, 2021; Grandela et al., 2024; Colinet, Lehnhoff & Raynaud‐Berton, 2025).

The negative effects of summer heat stress can, however, act in a more subtle way, with damage and mortality accumulating exponentially over time as the population experiences repeated heat events, as shown in D. melanogaster populations (Rezende et al., 2020). Little information is currently available to indicate whether invasive populations or species possess greater tolerance to summer heat or adaptive mechanisms compared with native relatives. The thermal tolerance breadth of some invasive and widely distributed species [e.g. D. busckii (Coquillett, 1901), D. hydei, D. immigrans (Sturtevant, 1921), D. melanogaster, D. repleta, D. subobscura] is high compared to other non‐invasive species (MacLean et al., 2019) and the temperatures that induce sterility are also higher in widespread invasive species [D. buzzatti (Patterson & Wheeler, 1942), D. hydei, D. melanogaster] compared to tropical species (van Heerwaarden & Sgrò, 2021). However, because the upper thermal limits of Drosophilidae are highly phylogenetically conserved (Kellerman et al., 2012b) and because there is no difference in plasticity for thermal traits between widely distributed and tropical species (Overgaard et al., 2011), it is unlikely that invasive species possess inherently greater thermal tolerance or adaptive mechanisms compared to native species. To exploit new environments effectively, invasive species may counteract the effect of heat stress and their physiological limits by plasticity in behaviour, as mentioned in Section III.1 (see also Ulmer et al., 2024; Destierdt et al., 2026), however, data comparing the behavioural plasticity of native and invasive Drosophilidae are needed.

Water availability

Theoretically, invasion success should be favoured for species that can tolerate greater variability in water availability, particularly those tolerant to drier locations as drought is more limiting than humidity (Schneider, Rebetez & Rasmann, 2022). The success of various drosophilids in exploiting novel drier areas can be partly attributed to their high physiological and morphological plasticity and adaptability to varying environmental conditions, including desiccation (Little et al., 2020).

Various invasive Drosophilidae are commonly found in environments with fluctuating water availability (Karan et al., 1998), as is the case for the near‐globally distributed D. suzukii, which can tolerate a decrease in atmospheric humidity to 20% before survival and reproduction are impaired (Tochen et al., 2016). The tropical invasive drosophilid Z. indianus, which now occurs from humid subtropical to cold semi‐arid climates (Bragard et al., 2022), can increase (> twofold) its tolerance to various stresses including desiccation (Karan et al., 1998). Its populations, as well as those of Z. tuberculatus, exhibit greater desiccation resistance compared to native African populations in arid environments (El‐Sabrout et al., 2025). Likewise, the tropical dimorphic species Drosophila ananassae (Doleschall, 1858) has successfully invaded cold and dry montane localities of the Western Himalaya in recent years, due to the greater desiccation tolerance of the dark morph (Chahal, Kataria & Parkash, 2013; Parkash et al., 2014). The dark and pale morphs differ in their physiological capacity for water conservation under experimental conditions as well as desiccation acclimation potential, consistent with their distributions in dry or wet habitats (Chahal et al., 2013; Parkash et al., 2014). Dark phenotypes of D. suzukii also show greater desiccation tolerance than paler ones (Fanning et al., 2019).

Drosophilidae may display other adaptive traits limiting direct exposure to desiccation stress, even in dry climates. Their larval stages often develop within fruit or decaying organic materials, which buffer direct exposure to atmospheric conditions (Fekete et al., 2016). Larvae of D. melanogaster perceive and react to humidity, selecting damp substrates at pre‐pupation stages and drier ones to pupate (Godoy‐Herrera et al., 1989). In practice, the measurement of relative humidity at such fine spatial scales remains technically challenging. Adult stages can also actively search for and select wetter microenvironments, at the landscape scale by seeking water elements (ponds, rivers) and at the microclimate scale by seeking microhabitats such as leaf litter cavities (Maceda‐Veiga et al., 2021). In spring and summer, the proportion of D. suzukii increases along riversides and litter cover in urban areas and dead wood debris are major ecological indicators of its presence (Ulmer et al., 2024). Further research on the behaviour of flies in relation to humidity gradients is required to shed light on their ability to buffer the negative impacts of desiccation stress on their fitness.

Synergies between multiple stressors in heterogeneous habitats

Temperature and humidity are probably the two most important drivers of physiological and ecological rates and their interactions are well documented in ectotherms in general, including invasive insects (Schneider et al., 2022). High temperatures contribute to increased evaporation and lower water availability, leading to desiccation stress, with small organisms such as insects being particularly vulnerable. High temperature is, thus, often associated with dry conditions, in turn resulting in multi‐stress conditions that can synergistically affect insect physiology and behaviour (Hoffmann et al., 2003b). In D. suzukii, laboratory data showed that exposure to relative humidity below 20% prevents flies from surviving and reproducing (Tochen et al., 2016). However, such low‐humidity conditions are very unlikely to be encountered in natural situations in most temperate and tropical regions. Even in the driest field habitats, shaded microhabitats may provide refuges to enable persistence in otherwise unsuitable areas (Tochen et al., 2016). Desiccating conditions in combination with thermal stress are often associated with reduced survival probability compared to thermal stress alone, as noted in both D. melanogaster (Bubliy et al., 2012) and D. suzukii (Enriquez & Colinet, 2017). However, desiccation stress does not seem to interact with low temperature to affect survival (Enriquez & Colinet, 2017), probably because the vapour pressure deficit remains limited at low temperatures (Sinclair, Saruhashi & Terblanche, 2024). Exposure to wind could also act in synergy with both temperature and humidity, further increasing the stress exposure of invasive Drosophilidae. Supporting this, wind, temperature and rainfall were reported to be the main abiotic forces driving D. melanogaster environmental adaptation (Bogaerts‐Márquez et al., 2021).

Other abiotic factors such as oxygen availability can both play a role directly and interact with temperature and humidity to impact invading species (Galic et al., 2018; Sarà et al., 2018). Physiological plasticity should be a key aspect of tolerance to multiple stressors. The tropical Z. indianus has a substantial capability to increase its tolerance to various stresses including desiccation, heat exposure and starvation after hardening/acclimation and also shows cross tolerance between these stressors (Kalra, Tamang & Parkash, 2017). Such flexibility in abiotic stress tolerance likely accounts for its invasion success (Kalra et al., 2017). In general, overall plasticity to multiple stressors does not result simply from additive plasticity to individual environmental factors. For instance, the non‐linearity of the physical interaction between temperature and water vapour pressure deficit (the driving force of evapotranspiration) implies that the plastic response to warm and dry environments is a complex mixture of physiological responses to both variables (Sinclair et al., 2024).

The larval stages of Drosophilidae develop in microenvironments (e.g. fruit, decomposing materials) that can display contrasting abiotic conditions. Living plant tissues influence the thermal, hydric and gaseous conditions experienced throughout larval development (Pincebourde & Casas, 2016). Drosophilids developing within unripe fruit can be subjected to conditions that are warmer than ambient external conditions (Saudreau et al., 2009). For instance, fallen fruit on the ground expose the larvae to the soil microclimate (Deconninck et al., 2024b). The soil microclimate often buffers atmospheric extremes when in the shade (Geiger, Aron & Todhunter, 2003), particularly when soil moisture levels are high (Greiser et al., 2024) or, conversely, amplifies ambient conditions when exposed to solar radiation (Feder et al., 1997). These contrasting thermal conditions can interact with the gaseous environment within the fruit to generate strong physiological impacts on larvae. For example, low oxygen levels within fruit tissues can lower the physiological threshold to high‐temperature tolerance, thereby causing thermal death at temperatures lower than those expected from the critical temperature alone (the so‐called oxygen and capacity limitation of thermal tolerance hypothesis), although this effect can be observed in terrestrial insects only at very low oxygen levels (well below 2 kPa; Verberk et al., 2016). Unfortunately, there is limited knowledge of the temperature and gas composition patterns experienced within such fruit resources, and it is therefore unclear whether and to what extent invasive drosophilids are better adapted to such peculiar micro‐environments.

Finally, drosophilids live and move across mosaics of micro‐environments that can present flies with a wide range of abiotic conditions. Their behaviour is key to selecting the most appropriate micro‐environment both for their own performance (survival, fecundity) and the success of their progeny (developmental rate, survival) [see Destierdt et al. (2026) for an example in D. suzukii]. These behavioural abilities must be integrated with spatial and temporal mapping of abiotic conditions at relatively fine scales, as illustrated for temperature by Pincebourde & Woods (2020). Such integrative approaches remain to be applied to other variables such as water vapour partial pressure and oxygen levels, for example, and behavioural choice strategies at these fine scales remain to be investigated for most Drosophilidae.

ROLE OF BIOTIC FACTORS IN RESOURCE USE AND NICHE ACCESSIBILITY

Besides facing new combinations of abiotic conditions, non‐native Drosophilidae need to access the resources of recipient ecosystems (e.g. fruit) and adapt to new biotic interactions and local resistances to establish successfully. Here we consider different traits through the lens of biotic interactions: (i) adaptation to new trophic conditions, (ii) competition, (iii) predation, (iv) facilitation and (v) symbiosis (see Fig. 1).

Adaptation to new trophic conditions

Invasion success in Drosophilidae is largely influenced by trophic niche width and the ability to feed on novel food sources (Poyet et al., 2015). An invading species may utilise a distinct host range in the invaded area compared with that of its native range. For instance, the success of D. melanogaster was, in part, a result of transitioning from a natural to a synanthropic habitat, switching its trophic niche from Musaceae fruit to human food crops and waste (Lachaise & Silvain, 2004). Proliferation and spread can occur only if trophic preferences and requirements (the trophic dimension of the ‘species invasiveness’), match with the quality, quantity and availability of resources (the trophic dimension of ‘ecosystem invasibility’) (Alpert, Bone & Holzapfel, 2000; Richardson & Pyšek, 2006). The trophic dimension of drosophilids invasiveness is, thus, based on a set of life‐history traits related to their physiology, morphology and behaviour that enable them to use the resources of the recipient ecosystem.

Polyphagy can be considered as a form of pre‐adaptation leading towards the evolution of a cosmopolitan invader, allowing a species to benefit from a diverse range of substrates/hosts, regardless of the geographic area invaded (Lachaise et al., 1988; Mack, 2003). Most drosophilids display high levels of polyphagy (Table S1). Invasive populations of D. melanogaster, D. simulans, Z. indianus and Z. tuberculatus (Malloch, 1932) are notably abundant in South America due, in part, to their ability to exploit a diverse range of plant species (Bragard et al., 2022; Ribeiro, Proença & Tidon, 2023; Viana et al., 2024). Urban commercial fruit and vegetable markets, characterised by consistent and cosmopolitan resource availability, favour generalist/polyphagous drosophilids such as D. melanogaster, D. mercatorum and D. suzukii (Widmann & Bächli, 2022: Ulmer et al., 2024). These species can readily locate suitable breeding sites, leading to the development and maintenance of larger populations in urban settings (Ribeiro et al., 2023).

Advantages of polyphagy include reduced foraging time to locate a resource, greater success of location within and between years, nutrient complementation, attenuation of the toxic effects of some substrates, and higher fecundity (Zhang et al., 2014; Poyet et al., 2015). There are also costs or disadvantages including variation of the nutritional composition/quality among resources, and intergenerational impacts on offspring fitness (e.g. development rate, survival) (Janz, 2003; Poyet et al., 2015). Drosophila suzukii, like other polyphagous insects, can also make oviposition mistakes by ovipositing on host plants that are unsuitable or suboptimal for the development of its larvae, such as ‘trap plants’ (Ulmer et al., 2020; Fragnière, Bacher & Kehrli, 2024). Rapid evolution and adaptation of D. suzukii to such plants emerged among recent invasive populations that adapted to the firethorn, Pyracantha coccinea, from which adults emerged (Fellin et al., 2023); the fruit were unsuitable for larval development in previous studies (Ulmer et al., 2020; Fragnière et al., 2024).

Few drosophilids are strictly monophagous or show narrow specialisation to a specific substrate. Most specialist species are not invasive (Barker & Starmer, 1982; Lachaise et al., 1988), as exemplified by D. nigrospiracula (Patterson & Wheeler, 1942), D. mettleri (Heed, 1977), D. mojavensis (Patterson, 1940), D. pachea (Patterson & Wheeler, 1942), a group of desert‐adapted species, or D. sechellia (Tsacas & Bachli, 1981) and D. mauritiana (Tsacas & David, 1974), insular species. Being a specialist can be advantageous for invasive species if the resource used is widely commercialised or cultivated, as insect invaders often follow plant invasion (Bonnamour et al., 2023). Cacoxenus perspicax (Knab, 1914) and D. flavohirta are two examples of specialist Drosophilidae that became invasive and likely benefit from feeding on mealybugs associated with sugarcane for the former (Tsacas & Desmier de Chenon, 1976), and eucalyptus flowers for the latter (McEvey et al., 1989).

In their invaded range, drosophilids must perform in the key steps summarised by Kogan (1977) as host habitat finding, host finding, host recognition and acceptance, and host suitability. To access their resources, some species developed specific organs, generally associated with specific behaviour (Cavey et al., 2023). For instance, D. suzukii and Scaptomyza flava (Fallen, 1823) evolved a serrated ovipositor allowing them to insert eggs into harder substrates that are inaccessible to other species: unripe fruit for the former (Atallah et al., 2014) and fresh leaves for the latter (Peláez et al., 2022). These convergent adaptations allow these fly species to be the first to access and benefit from the resource, before native species (Rombaut et al., 2017), avoiding most competitors (Clemente et al., 2018). These two examples echo the empty niche hypothesis (Stachowicz & Tilman, 2005) which is often used to explain invasion success. In regions invaded by non‐native species, particular ecological niches (in the current context, trophic) may be unoccupied by native species due to their different evolutionary history. Thus, the establishment success of non‐native populations strongly relies on niche differentiation from native populations. This explains the higher performance and competition success of the invasive D. suzukii over the native D. subobscura in unripe fruit (Poyet et al., 2014; Deconninck et al., 2024a) and the reverse in rotting fruit (Deconninck et al., 2024b).

Most plants evolved defences against insects, including through the production of toxic compounds (Bruce, 2015; Hilker & Fatouros, 2015). Some polyphagous insect species are able to overcome plant defences in their invasive range and develop in fruit or other plant substrates that are unsuitable for (or not used by) native species. Drosophila suzukii provides a striking example of this ability, being able to develop in fruit that are highly toxic to most fauna, such as those of Atropa belladonna (Poyet et al., 2015) or Viscum album (Deconninck et al., 2024a). It is also able to use the secondary metabolites contained in A. belladonna berries and pass them trans‐generationally to its offspring as a means of increasing resistance to parasitoid attacks (Poyet et al., 2017). These differences in quality of trophic resources can explain why D. suzukii vary greatly in their attractiveness to or protection against parasitoids and thus their host plants influence the level of parasitisation of the fly (Viciriuc et al., 2025) and are directly linked to its invasion success. Similarly, the invasive D. busckii shows tolerance towards high concentrations of dimethyldisulphide, a neurotoxic compound widespread in nature (emitted by bacteria, fungi, plants and animals) that is highly toxic to most drosophilids (Mahadevan et al., 2025). Even if such biochemical strategies can be used by species that are not invasive (e.g. D. sechellia, restricted to the Seychelles, infest fruit of Morinda citrifolia that are toxic to other drosophilids), they are clearly advantageous and can make an important contribution to invasion success.

These beneficial traits of invasive drosophilids (detoxification capacity, serrated ovipositor, detection of immature fruit) already existed in their native range (Atallah et al., 2014), where the species are in equilibrium with the combination of functional traits characterising the local communities with which they coevolved. However, in their invaded range, these traits are original (i.e. not present in the newly colonised area) and become new ways to exploit resources (‘novel weapon hypothesis’; Callaway & Ridenour, 2004) that can disturb community equilibrium (Callaway et al., 2008). Such adaptations that provide advantages to invasive over native species were originally recognised and are well known in plants (Callaway & Aschehoug, 2000), but much less so in insects (see example in Vilcinskas et al., 2013).

Overcome inter‐ and intraspecific competition

Interspecific and intraspecific competition are widespread among insects, particularly for trophic resources and reproduction sites. Interspecific competition plays a role in invasion success by shaping the distribution and abundance of phytophagous insects (Denno, McClure & Ott, 1995; Kaplan & Denno, 2007), including invasive drosophilids (Bühlmann & Gossner, 2022). While the role of interspecific competition has been extensively studied in the context of plant invasions (Levine, Adler, & Yelenik, 2004; Valladares et al., 2015; Aschehoug et al., 2016), its relative importance compared to other ecological factors remains debated in insects (Facon et al., 2021). Co‐occurring species can use different strategies when exposed to intra‐ or interspecific competition and their responses (e.g. life‐history parameters) may help to explain the different distribution patterns observed on local and global scales (de Paiva Mendonça, Haddi & Godoy, 2023). In addition, although climate warming is likely to intensify competition in both established and novel communities, longer growing seasons, more severe environmental stress and increased climatic variability may buffer against competition intensification (Lancaster, Morrison & Fitt, 2017). A better understanding of competitive interactions between invasive and native species, as well as between the invasive species themselves, can improve the prediction of their distributions at various scales and the development of pest‐control strategies.

In the context of biological invasions, limited attention has been paid to the role of competition compared to those of other biotic and abiotic factors such as the existence of ecological niches not exploited by native species (empty niche hypothesis; Herbold & Moyle, 1986) or the lack of natural enemies in the invaded area [enemy release hypothesis (ERH) (Williamson & Fitter, 1996; Keane & Crawley, 2002)]. It is generally predicted that communities are more likely to be invaded when large amounts of unused resources are available (Davis, Grime & Thompson, 2000), when introduced species have better acquisition efficiency for a given resource than do native species, or when, at comparable acquisition efficiency, the non‐native species has lower requirements for this resource than the native species (Shea & Chesson, 2002). Accessing the resource before other species, such as allowed by the serrated ovipositor of D. suzukii and S. flava, also reduces competition with other drosophilids. However, while the serrated ovipositor of species like D. suzukii and S. flava may facilitate their ability to exploit new ecological niches and contribute to their invasion success, this morphological adaptation alone does not automatically underlie invasiveness, as evidenced by species like D. subpulchrella (Takamori & Watabe, 2006) and D. biarmipes (Malloch, 1924), which possess similar ovipositors but are not invasive (Atallah et al., 2014).

Food substrates such as fruit are ephemeral resources, creating the conditions for intra‐ and interspecific competition (Rohlfs & Hoffmeister, 2004; Butterworth, Benbow & Barton, 2023). The investment in strategies related to competition reduced the performance of various drosophilid life‐history traits (e.g. survival, growth, fecundity) in experimental situations with limited access to resources (Henry & Colinet, 2018; Henry, Tarapacki & Colinet, 2020). As a consequence, spatial and temporal partitioning in the use of a given resource occur, allowing escape from or reduction of competition between members of the drosophilid community, including between invasive and native species (Deconninck et al., 2024b). Direct demonstration of the role of competitive ability in the success of invasions is rare. In Brazil, invasive Drosophilidae including Z. indianus, Drosophila malerkotliana (Parshad & Paika, 1964) and D. nasuta outcompeted native species, having higher abundance and using more diverse resources (dos Santos Leal Martins, Garcia & Montes, 2025). Zaprionus indianus is also by far the most prevalent species (>80% in collected fruit in Brazil) and interspecific competition bioassays with increasing levels of inter‐ and intraspecific competition between Z. indianus and D. suzukii suggested better competitive ability in Z. indianus, consistent with its prevalence in the field (de Paiva Mendonça et al., 2023). In laboratory trials with ripe strawberries, damage caused by D. suzukii facilitated infestations by Z. indianus (Bernardi et al., 2017) and another study reported that competition with Z. indianus could limit D. suzukii numbers (Shrader, Burrack & Pfeiffer, 2020).

Intraspecific competition is likely to take place in the field as females usually oviposit several eggs in the same substrate. Drosophila suzukii can oviposit up to 50 eggs in individual blueberries in experimental conditions (Kienzle et al., 2020) and up to 88 eggs in 10 ml of blackberry puree (Olazcuaga et al., 2019), even if in the field the number of eggs per fruit is lower (e.g. ~5 eggs in raspberries; Wallingford et al., 2016). The resulting larval density resulted in fitness costs (e.g. slower development, reduced survival and fecundity) in D. suzukii, D. melanogaster and Z. indianus (Henry et al., 2020; de Paiva Mendonça et al., 2023). Overcrowding larvae of D. suzukii pupate further away from their host fruit compared to singly developing individuals, reducing intraspecific competition as detached pupation is associated with survival and fitness gains (Bezerra Da Silva et al., 2019). In two other invasive species, D. ananassae and D. nasuta, experiments on larval crowding over several generations showed that the evolution of competitive ability was achieved through a combination of a shorter larval stage with more rapid growth to the minimum critical size for pupation, greater food conversion efficiency, increased pupation depth and greater urea/ammonia tolerance (Nagarajan et al., 2016). By contrast, in D. melanogaster, the evolution of competitive ability results from a balance between mutually antagonistic traits such as increased larval feeding and foraging behaviour, greater tolerance to nitrogenous wastes and reduced food conversion efficiency (Nagarajan et al., 2016). Together, such results suggest that increased competitive ability can be attained through the evolution of different suites of traits.

Avoid and resist natural enemies

The biotic resistance of the recipient ecosystem can significantly influence the ultimate outcome of invasions (Daly et al., 2023). A non‐native drosophilid arriving in a novel area will encounter new local communities and interact with species that may hinder its long‐term establishment in the recipient ecosystem. Natural enemies of drosophilids can be generalist or specialist and target specific or multiple life stages (i.e. eggs, larvae, pupae and/or adults) (Symondson, Sunderland & Greenstone, 2002). Native predators and parasitoids are among the most important contributors to biotic resistance towards drosophilid invasions (Gibert et al., 2010; Schmidt et al., 2019; Ulmer et al., 2021), but interactions with other entomopathogenic organisms (e.g. pathogenic fungi, bacteria, nematodes, viruses) can also influence invasion success (Woltz et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2020). These regulating taxa are grouped under the term ‘natural enemies’ (Keane & Crawley, 2002). They can affect invasive drosophilids through a consumptive effect by which a predator affects the prey population by directly killing the individuals, and/or by a non‐consumptive effect, by which the presence of the predator or enemy influences performance traits such as reproduction or oviposition activity (Ulmer et al., 2021). The ERH suggests that the establishment of a new invading species can be facilitated by a lack of natural competitors and enemies (Williamson & Fitter, 1996; Keane & Crawley, 2002) or that, even if potentially appropriate native species are present, they are generally naïve and unable to recognise/detect and then predate/parasitise the new species. The ERH is one of many hypotheses used to explain the abundance and/or impact of invading species [see Colautti et al. (2004) for review]. Once again, D. suzukii provides a pertinent example of how the ERH affects invasion success: in its native range, it is susceptible to at least 19 larval parasitoids, while in its invaded area it is susceptible to far fewer (around six; Wang et al., 2020).

Strategies to counter predation and parasitism include avoidance, camouflage, physical resistance and protective biochemical responses. One or more of these strategies are used by different drosophilids, in both native and invaded ranges. The avoidance strategy involves the use of behaviours induced by the presence of natural enemies and depends on the type of predator (de la Flor et al., 2017). The visual or olfactory detection of predators induces innate defensive behaviours and increase the flies' exploratory activity (de la Flor et al., 2017). Larval and adult D. melanogaster avoid sites ‘smelling of’ Leptopilina wasps, their primary parasitoid enemy (Ebrahim et al., 2015). Based on specific volatile organic compound cues emitted by the wasps, the fly develops a dedicated circuit (i.e. olfactory sensory neuron, OSN) for its detection. Polyphagy and the use of fruit of a wide range of colours can also be incorporated into an enemy avoidance strategy by circumventing those attracted by a specific range of colours (Vargas et al., 1991). In D. suzukii, polyphagy allows it to develop in a set of host fruits that are not or less targeted by parasitoids (Viciriuc et al., 2025).

Drosophilids have developed other strategies to reduce predation impacts. The relatively thick and hard cuticle (about 20 μm) of pupae is hypothesised to protect them from predator attacks (Borne et al., 2021). Pupal adhesion, used by the invasive D. suzukii and D. simulans, reduces predation by 30% in experimental conditions (Borne et al., 2021). The depth of D. suzukii pupation in fruit or soil is also likely to influence their predation and parasitism rate (Kanzawa 1939; Woltz & Lee, 2017). Encapsulation, an immune response to large parasites such as endoparasitoids can also reduce predation (Carton & Nappi, 2001). The parasitoid community of frugivorous Drosophilidae in France primarily comprises five species, three larval and two pupal parasitoids (Fleury et al., 2009). In the D. suzukii invasion range, only the generalist pupal parasitoids develop successfully (Chabert et al., 2012). The low parasitic efficiency of native larval parasitoids of the wasp genus Leptopilina is associated with a high capacity for immune resistance in D. suzukii, with the number of circulating haemocytes higher than ever recorded in larvae of the D. melanogaster group (Poyet et al., 2013). Other defensive strategies include the activation of specific biochemical pathways that either directly lead to the death of parasitoids or reduce their impacts. For instance, D. melanogaster and D. suzukii modify their oviposition behaviour in the presence of parasitoids, preferentially ovipositing in fruit that either contain alkaloids or alcohol (Milan, Kacsoh & Schlenke, 2012; Kacsoh et al., 2013; Poyet et al., 2017), which is facilitated by their high tolerance to these compounds (Fogleman, 2000). Globally, however, the role of the immune system in the success of invasion has been poorly investigated, and commonalities in immune responses among invasive drosophilids warrant further study. Recently, the tritrophic system of plants–Drosophilidae–parasitoids is experiencing profound modifications due to the introduction of oligophageous parasitoid species used in biocontrol and the spontaneous spread of the non‐native polyphagous parasitoid Leptopilina japonica across the globe (Viciriuc et al., 2025). These parasitoids are transforming the assembly rules between native and invasive species within Drosophila communities. With the arrival of L. japonica, D. suzukii may encounter a new obstacle to the proliferation of its populations for the first time in its invasion history (Viciriuc et al., 2025), potentially impacting the balance between native and non‐native Drosophila.

Taking advantage of facilitation

There is growing research interest in understanding the impact of positive interactions between species on the structure of communities (Stachowicz, 2001; Fridley et al., 2007; Cavieres, 2021; Koffel, Daufresne & Klausmeier, 2021). Facilitation is defined as a positive interaction between two species that results in an increase in the density or biomass of at least one of the species (Rodriguez, 2006). A large body of work demonstrated that the presence of non‐native species can facilitate infestations of native species, with sometimes wide‐ranging impacts (Rodriguez, 2006). Within the Drosophilidae community, the introduction of D. suzukii could promote other fruit flies by increasing the amount of oviposition substrate available. Through this species' ability to pierce the skin of ripening fruit, an opening is created that allows access to the flesh for other species of fruit flies, such as D. subobscura or Z. indianus (Poyet et al., 2014; Bernardi et al., 2017). The opening also allows pathogenic microorganisms to access the fruit, causing premature decay (Walsh et al., 2011) and creating resources for fruit fly species that develop preferentially in rotten fruits (Poyet et al., 2014). For example, D. suzukii facilitates infestation by D. melanogaster in grapes (Rombaut et al., 2017).

Conversely, the question of whether and how native species can facilitate non‐native species has received less attention (Fridley et al., 2007; Gribben et al., 2020). The facilitation of invasive Drosophilidae by native species can occur at different trophic levels, via the host plant or via the activity of other insects (including other drosophilids) or microorganisms. Invasive species can benefit from the presence of favourable host plant species (native or non‐native) already present in their introduced ranges (Mally et al., 2024). The mechanisms by which plant invasion can facilitate insect invasion have been reviewed in Bertelsmeier et al. (2024). Horticulture of non‐native plants in urban parks and gardens contribute to the establishment of non‐native insects that are pre‐adapted to these plants (Bertelsmeier et al., 2024). Drosophila suzukii infests Aucuba japonica (Thunb.), Mahonia japonica (Thunb.) and Elaeagnus ×submacrophylla (Servett), three exotic plants native to Asia that are widely planted in urban areas in its invasive range (Poyet et al., 2015; Deconninck et al., 2025a; Larges et al., 2026). Other non‐native plants can also facilitate invasive drosophilids. For instance, Prunus serotina, a tree native to North America and invasive in Europe, provides a suitable reservoir for D. suzukii and D. subobscura (Poyet et al., 2014).

Facilitation can also occur when an invasive drosophilid benefits from a native or other invasive insect species. For example, C. amoena oviposits in galleries made by larvae of the moth Cydia pomonella (Linnaeus, 1958) in apples (Deconninck et al., 2024b). This invasive drosophilid also benefits from seed‐boring insect parasites that damage and modify the fruit of several broad‐leaved tree species, providing opportunities for the flies to oviposit (Band, Bächli & Band, 2005). Similarly, it has been observed that both Z. indianus and Z. tuberculatus benefit from figs already infested by the invasive Mediterranean fruit fly, Ceratitis capitata (Bragard et al., 2022; Georges et al., 2024). The presence of C. capitata larvae may facilitate the invasion of Zaprionus spp. by breaking down the fruit tissue, making it more suitable for oviposition and larval development. This interaction allows Zaprionus spp. to exploit a niche created by the native flies, thereby enhancing its ability to establish and spread in new environments. Zaprionus indianus also benefits from fruit infested by D. suzukii (Bernardi et al., 2017), producing more offspring (de Paiva Mendonça et al., 2023).

Finally, yeasts can act as facilitators for non‐native species. For example, the yeast Hanseniaspora uvarum, which proliferates on both unripe and ripe raspberries, creates favourable conditions for D. suzukii development, suppressing an antagonist grey mould (Botrytis cinerea) and producing less ethanol (Chakraborty et al., 2022). Consequently, D. suzukii females lay more eggs on raspberries that host H. uvarum (Chakraborty et al., 2022).

A little help from symbionts

Microbial symbionts are pervasive in insects and encompass a diverse array of bacteria, fungi and viruses that colonise the insect cuticle, gut and cells (Douglas, 2015). These symbionts often influence various aspects of the insect phenotype, ranging from physiology to behaviour (reviewed by Feldhaar, 2011), thereby holding a central position in shaping the ecology and evolutionary trajectory of their hosts. Recent studies have increasingly emphasised the roles of these microbes in enhancing the invasion success of their insect hosts (Lu, Hulcr & Sun, 2016; Liu et al., 2022). Throughout the introduction–naturalisation–invasion process, microbial symbionts can play crucial roles, influencing propagule pressure, resource acquisition, resistance to pathogens, tolerance to abiotic stressors and the competitive capacity of insects. These effects may stem from symbionts already present in the native population or arise from others acquired during the invasion process via horizontal transfer from native insects or directly from the environment (Wooding et al., 2013). Conversely, the diversity of the microbiome can be negatively impacted by the invasion process, particularly through the occurrence of bottlenecks, which may result in reduced fitness of non‐native populations (Ørsted et al., 2022b).

Some symbionts have evolved mechanisms to manipulate the reproduction of their host to favour their own transmission. The best‐known example is the bacterial symbiont Wolbachia, which has been described in various invasive Drosophila species including D. suzukii, D. simulans and D. immigrans. Wolbachia can spread by providing a fitness advantage to infected host individuals compared to uninfected ones (Kriesner & Hoffmann, 2018). For instance, in D. simulans and D. suzukii, Wolbachia infection is associated with increased fecundity and/or offspring survival (Mazzetto, Gonella & Alma, 2015; Cao, Jiang & Hoffmann, 2019; Deconninck et al., 2024c; Destierdt et al., 2026). Such symbiont‐induced effects could increase the propagule pressure of their host and, consequently, the likelihood of invasion events.

There are many examples within drosophilids of diverse bacterial symbionts enhancing resistance to natural enemies such as viruses, fungi and parasitoids. Here, again, Wolbachia provides a well‐known example and has been associated with resistance to viruses in D. melanogaster, D. simulans and and D. suzukii (Hedges et al., 2008; Teixeira, Ferreira & Ashburner, 2008; Martinez et al., 2014; Cattel et al., 2016b; Cogni et al., 2021) and to entomopathogenic fungi in D. melanogaster (Panteleev et al., 2007). Another bacteria, Spiroplasma, is associated with parasitoid resistance in D. hydei (Xie, Vilchez & Mateos, 2010).

The acquisition of symbionts can also increase tolerance to abiotic stresses which may, in turn, enhance the invasive potential of species. The gut microbiota is involved in heat tolerance in D. subobscura (Jaramillo & Castañeda, 2021) and cold tolerance in D. melanogaster [Henry & Colinet, 2018; see Corbin et al. (2017) for review]. Symbionts can also be beneficial to their host under nutrient‐deficient conditions. For example, under iron stress, D. melanogaster females infected with Wolbachia have a higher fecundity than uninfected females (Brownlie et al., 2009). Recent studies have shown that the gut microbiota can affect food choice and foraging decisions under laboratory conditions in D. melanogaster and can allow fruit flies to tolerate unfavourable nutritional situations (Yamada et al., 2015; Leitão‐Gonçalves et al., 2017; Henriques et al., 2020). Similarly, Wolbachia impacts oviposition site selection in D. suzukii, leading to a more even distribution of eggs (Destierdt et al., 2026). Thus, symbiotic microbiota could impact the ability of their host flies to adapt to various available food sources and, more generally, to diverse abiotic and biotic conditions.

The microbiota associated with invasive species can also affect their competitive interactions with native species. For example, in the case of the invasive ladybird Harmonia axyridis, an associated microsporidium is benign but is a lethal pathogen for native ladybird species (Vilcinskas et al., 2013). To date, such effects have not been documented in invasive drosophilids, but this is worthy of research, given that many invasive species share the same or overlapping trophic niches with native counterparts.

Despite the multiple potential impacts that microbiota may have on invasion success, no symbiont has yet been directly linked to the invasion of any drosophilid. Some studies have noted differences in the microbiota associated with invasive and native populations. For example, Martinez‐Sañudo et al. (2018) reported alterations in the microbiota of D. suzukii in introduced populations compared to native ones. However, the precise roles of microbiota in their host's adaptation to a new environment remain a challenge to disentangle from the influence of the environment in shaping the microbiota.

FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

Many invasive Drosophilidae species have an impact not only on agricultural systems but also on natural ecosystems through new interactions that can disrupt ecosystem functioning (Poyet et al., 2014; Tonina et al., 2018; Bühlmann & Gossner, 2022). A better understanding of the traits underlying the success of these invasions is required to explain and predict the influence of these invasive species on biodiversity and socio‐ecosystems (Poyet et al., 2015; Bühlmann & Gossner, 2022; Greenleaf et al., 2023). Among the traits or processes highlighted in this review that facilitate the ability of a non‐native Drosophilidae species to become invasive, we can highlight: (1) multiple introduction events; (2) association with human activities; (3) coping with inbreeding depression by maintaining high genetic diversity and counteracting the Allee effect; (4) benefiting from the activity of TEs; (5) adaptability to thermal stress; (6) mixed overwintering strategies; (7) adaptability to water deprivation; (8) broad trophic niche; (9) the ability (pre‐adaptation) to exploit empty niches; (10) high behavioural plasticity; (11) mitigation of competition; (12) release of natural enemies in the invaded area and resistance/avoidance capacity to newly introduced parasitoids; (13) benefiting from the presence of other species; and (14) benefiting from microbial symbionts (see Fig. 1). Having all of these traits is not necessary for a successful invasion but possessing most of them enhances the chances of success.

We emphasise two categories of perspectives for filling crucial knowledge gaps and opening new research avenues in the light of the information collated in this review. First, key knowledge is still missing or incomplete, even for basic traits such as the ability to tolerate heat and cold, limiting our capacity to decipher invasion success in Drosophilidae. This lack of knowledge could be a consequence of biased research towards crop pests, such as D. suzukii. Here, we recommend the following actions.(1)Acquire knowledge on the traits that improve survival and reproduction during the transportation routes. Transportation via human means can take time (e.g. several months for a species travelling in a container on a cargo ship), allowing several generations and/or acclimation to novel conditions, providing that the conditions are adequate, thereby increasing the propagule size. However, the abiotic conditions within containers transported via marine or terrestrial routes are rarely recorded (Marquez et al., 2012).(2)Develop knowledge of stress tolerance traits across invasive and non‐invasive Drosophilidae, using comparative approaches applying comparable methodologies to alleviate the challenge of comparing traits that have been measured differently in different studies (Ørsted, Jørgensen & Overgaard, 2022a). We further highlight that, although physiological traits related to temperature have received research attention, we still lack information on important areas such as behavioural thermoregulation [but see Deconninck et al. (2025b), for an example using D. melanogaster] or the capacity for behavioural adjustments including shifts in daily activity rhythms or in the ability to detect and exploit microclimates. In addition, we lack information and the technical ability to measure the precise microclimatic conditions experienced by larvae within their food resources.(3)Investigate the physiological and ecological correlations involved in the immune responses of invasive versus non‐invasive species. The role of the immune system in the success of invasions has been poorly investigated to date. Complex interactions between immune responses and physiological tolerance to stressors including heat and cold are known (Ferguson & Sinclair, 2020). Comparative analyses of these interactions between native and invasive species will offer novel insights into the determinants of invasion success.(4)Resolve the puzzle of mutualistic symbionts. While numerous examples illustrate the extent to which symbionts provide various benefits across drosophilids, key knowledge gaps exist in how these symbionts vary in natural populations and their contribution to invasion success. A key limitation here is that the prevalence of most symbionts in native and invading populations remains to be quantified (Cattel et al., 2016a).

Second, the outcomes of our review pave the way for new research avenues. There is a clear need for the use of integrative approaches in understanding the success of invasions. Such integrative approaches should connect the genetic and genomic processes along the invasion route, the physiological and behavioural determinants of tolerance/resistance to multiple stressors, the roles of neurobiology and sensory ecology, chemical ecology and the biotic drivers of population establishment and development (see Fig. 1). Nonetheless, we also recognise the sheer complexity of the mechanisms that can contribute to invasion success. Neurobiology and sensory ecology of species is expected to play a role in distribution spread (Szabo, Damas‐Moreira & Whiting, 2020) such as in hematophagous bugs for example (Lazzari, Pereira & Lorenzo, 2013; de la Vega & Schilman, 2017). Trait databases for insects need to be developed, including the identification of invasion potential. There are ongoing initiatives [e.g. Carabids.org (Homburg et al., 2013); GlobalAnts (Parr et al., 2017); AnimalTraits (Herberstein et al., 2022)], but functional and multi‐trait‐based approaches to the distribution and ecology of Drosophilidae are rare (but see Kellermann et al., 2012a; Mendes et al., 2021; Parratt et al., 2021) and do not target invasive species. Moreover, most focus only on the gut microbiome of Drosophila species (Kang & Douglas, 2020) or are restricted to a single or low numbers of traits such as wing size or colour (Ulmer et al., 2024).

CONCLUSIONS

(1)This review highlights the diversity of factors shared by invasive Drosophilidae, including physiological traits and invasion strategies that are likely to be found in many invasive species. Tephritidae species, such as Bactrocera dorsalis and C. capitata, are among the top 20 worst invasive species (Zhao et al., 2023) and share key traits that we have identified in invasive Drosophilidae, such as habitat generalism, high fecundity, rapid development, and adaptability to various environments (Papadopoulos et al., 2024). Identifying traits that are shared in invasive species across families may contribute to a better understanding of the broader patterns of invasive species biology.(2)Investigating ongoing biological invasions of drosophilids sheds light on how pre‐introductory genetic predispositions in the native area, post‐introductory adaptations and/or phenotypic plasticity can facilitate the establishment of new colonisers.(3)A fundamental question, which is often difficult to answer when it comes to invasion success, is why have some species of drosophilids become invasive, while others have remained confined to their native ranges? We believe that this review highlights key traits associated with this transition, offering critical insights into the factors that drive certain species to expand beyond their native boundaries, and hopefully illustrates the need and potential for comparative analyses to test these hypotheses.(4)These findings could pave the way for a deeper understanding of the mechanisms behind biological invasions and help predict or manage future invasions more effectively.
