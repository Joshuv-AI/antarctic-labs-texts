Inappropriate Appeal Denials for Head and Neck Cancer Surgery in Medicare Advantage Plans
Authors: Jeffrey R Bellinger, Cameron B Fattahi, Ericka L Erickson, Nolan B Seim, Vinay K Rathi, Lauren E Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedicare Advantage (MA) plans use prior authorization to manage utilization, with denied services appealable through a five‐level process. Independent review entities (IREs) adjudicate second‐level appeals, determining whether initial plan denials were appropriate. This study assessed whether MA plan denials for head and neck cancer surgery are overturned by IREs at higher rates than denials for other services. We analyzed IRE determinations from January 2020 to December 2024, categorizing appeals as head and neck cancer surgery, surgical oncology, all surgery, or all healthcare services. Among 94 head and neck cancer surgery appeals, 11 denials (11.7%) were favorable/partially favorable. This rate was similar to surgical oncology (9.3%; P = .45) but significantly higher than all surgical services (5.6%; P < .001) and all healthcare services (4.5%; P < .001). The most common denial rationale was inadequate documentation. MA plan denials for head and neck oncologic surgery are overturned by IREs at higher rates compared to general healthcare services, which may represent a barrier to time‐sensitive cancer care.Keywords: appeal, denial, head and neck cancer, Medicare Advantage, prior authorization

The Medicare Modernization Act of 2003 established the current Medicare Advantage (MA) program, 1 allowing beneficiaries to obtain Medicare benefits through private plans. Enrollment has grown significantly; by 2024, MA membership exceeded traditional Medicare. 2 , 3 Unlike fee‐for‐service Medicare, MA plans receive risk‐adjusted capitation. County‐level benchmarks are set annually, plans submit bids, and the bid‐benchmark difference constitutes a rebate that plans partially retain. 1 The Trump administration has recently prioritized audits of these rebates, which frequently reveal significant overpayments to MA plans. 4

When plans deny coverage, beneficiaries may appeal. There are 5 levels of appeals in MA plans, the first of which is reconsideration from the plan. If upheld, the case automatically goes to the second level which includes a review from an independent review entity (IRE) for adjudication under Medicare criteria. 5 IRE outcomes are “favorable” (denial inappropriate), “partially favorable” (denial partially inappropriate), or “unfavorable” (denial appropriate) (Supplemental Figure S1, available online).

Patients with head and neck cancer frequently present with advanced disease, making prompt care imperative. Insurance delays can worsen survival and tumor‐related symptoms, compounding disability and widening disparities. 6 , 7 We examined whether MA plans inappropriately deny appeals for head and neck cancer surgery relative to other surgical oncology procedures, all surgery, and all healthcare services.

Methods

This study used only publicly available data and thus did not require institutional review board approval. We extracted Appeals Decision Search records from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) website between January 2020 and December 2024. 8 Surgical appeals were filtered by the category “surgery.” Using pilot testing, we refined a 46‐term MeSH‐ and lay‐derived list of keyword stems entered sequentially and deduplicated by appeal decision identifier (Supplemental Figure S2, available online).

IRE decisions were queried as: head and neck cancer surgery (category “surgery,” condition “neoplasm,” plus keyword filter), surgical oncology (category “surgery” + “neoplasm”), all surgery services, and all healthcare (surgical and medical) services. We then focused on head and neck surgery appeals. Given disease heterogeneity, an otolaryngology provider confirmed inclusion and assigned subsites (Table 1). Favorable and partially favorable determinations were pooled; the primary outcome was unfavorable rulings because it directly affects star ratings and plan reimbursement. 9

Denial rationales for head and neck surgery were classified from decision narratives by keyword‐based script into nine predefined themes (eg, “documentation/coding not supported,” “coverage exclusion,” etc.). CPT codes were examined to identify frequently denied procedures and outcomes. Analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Chicago). Results

From 2020 to 2024 the IRE adjudicated 761,880 appeals of coverage denials across all healthcare services; 727,286 (95.5%) were upheld as unfavorable. Surgical cases accounted for 52,101 appeals, demonstrating a slightly lower, though substantial, unfavorable rate of 94.4% (P < .001 versus all services). Within surgical oncology, 1533 appeals were filed, of which 1390 (90.7%) were unfavorable (P < .001 versus all surgery). There were 94 head and neck cancer appeals, of which 83 (88.3%) were upheld, a rate comparable to the surgical oncology cohort (P = .45). By subsite, oral cavity generated the most appeals (n = 31), followed by thyroid/parathyroid (n = 17), and cutaneous (n = 16). Nonmucosal subsites demonstrated a higher inappropriate denial rate than mucosal subsites (15.7% vs 7.0%; P = .19; Table 1).

The volume of surgical oncology appeals increased from 171 in 2020 to 426 in 2024, while the proportion of unfavorable appeals remained stable at about 90% (Figure 1A). Head and neck surgical appeals averaged about 20 per year, with yearly variability in unfavorable rates but no discernible temporal trend (Figure 1B).

Among head and neck cancer surgery appeals, the most common plan‐stated theme (n = 33) was “documentation/coding not supported,” followed by “cost‐sharing (copay/deductible; n = 15 claims)” and “coverage exclusion/benefit not covered” (n = 13 claims) (Table 2 and Supplemental Table S1, available online). Ten CPT codes were denied more than twice (Table 2). CPT 64716 (neuroplasty) was the most frequently denied (n = 5), followed by cervical lymphadenectomy codes (38724 n = 4; 38720 n = 3).

Discussion

MA beneficiaries requiring head and neck oncologic surgery experienced an inappropriate denial rate of 11.7% (ie, favorable/partially favorable IRE determinations), exceeding the rate for all surgical services (5.6%) and health care services (4.5%). CMS records represent appealed items/services (often CPT‐level), including diagnostic procedures, and may reflect partial episode denials rather than complete denial of definitive surgery. Limitations include that the CMS database reflects a subset of IRE decisions and may under‐capture MA denials/appeals, sparse rationales that could permit misclassification, and small head and neck volume (6.1% of surgical oncology). 7 Findings are associated and may be influenced by unmeasured factors (case complexity, documentation quality).

Appeals increased over the study period, potentially tracking prior authorization expansion, which requires providers to demonstrate medical necessity before reimbursement. Whether this practice reduces low‐value care remains debated 10 amid concerns about increased administrative burden. 1 , 11 , 12 As MA enrollment has grown, authorization volumes followed: nearly 50 million prior authorization determinations were issued in 2023, up from 37 million in 2021. 13 Only 11.7% of denied requests were appealed in MA in 2023, of which over 80% of appeals were partially or fully overturned, 13 suggesting both deterrent effects of appeals processes and coverage denial incentives.

Potential solutions include enhanced CMS reporting requirements for plan‐level transparency 14 and re‐weighting quality measures to penalize services with high rates of inappropriate denials. 15 Given “documentation/coding not supported” was the most frequent denial rationale in our cohort, 12 , 15 clinicians may improve approvals by explicitly linking operative indications to Medicare coverage criteria, anticipating common objections, and ensuring multidisciplinary recommendations are clearly documented. Recent CMS prior authorization reforms (CMS‐0057‐F), with compliance beginning in 2026, may shorten decision timelines and expand submission requirements, 11 , 13 , 14 , 16 reducing inappropriate denials and delays in care. 6 , 7
