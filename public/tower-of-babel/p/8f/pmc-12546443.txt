Sodium and Potassium Excretion in CKD: What is the Impact of Differences Observed Among Patients of African Descent?

Historically, nutritional recommendations for patients with kidney diseases have been immutably based on a low sodium intake to help in controlling blood pressure (BP) and on a reduction of potassium intake in advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD) stages, to prevent the development of cardiac complications such as arrhythmias and sudden death. In the past 2 decades, however, the restriction of potassium intake has been challenged with the promotion of a more liberal approach favoring a normal potassium intake in CKD.1 The main reason for this paradigm shift is the increasing evidence that a low potassium excretion, as a surrogate for dietary potassium intake, is associated with an increased risk of developing hypertension, a greater risk of mortality, and an increased risk of cardiovascular events such as stroke and a more rapid progression of CKD in the general population and in patients with hypertension.2,3 Thereafter, studies have reported clinical benefits of a high dietary potassium intake on kidney function decline and subsequent cardiovascular events in patients with CKD. These data have been gathered from several large surveys in Europe,4 Korea,5 Japan,6 and United States.7 However, except in the Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort Study,7 in which Black and non-Black patients from the United States were enrolled, no study has investigated the impact of sodium and potassium excretions on BP and renal parameters in patients of African ancestries living on the African continent. Because salt substitution using potassium-enriched salt has been found to decrease cardiovascular events and death in older Chinese hypertensive patients,8 information from African countries would be of great interest to support the new World Health Organization recommendations widely promoting a higher potassium intake in the general population with some caution in patients with advanced CKD.

The lack of African data is now filled by the recent publication of Ilori et al.9 in KI Reports. In this study, authors compared the associations between urinary sodium and potassium excretion (UNa 24 h and UK 24 h) with BP and proteinuria in individuals with CKD from Western Africa and from the United States. To this purpose, they used the data from 2 CKD cohorts with participants of African ancestry: the Diet, CKD, and Apolipoprotein L1 (DCA) nephropathy study, an ancillary study to the Human Hereditary and Health in Africa (H3Africa) Kidney Disease Network Study in sub-Saharan Africa and the Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort (CRIC) in the United States containing Black and non-Black patients. The results of these comparisons provided somewhat surprising and puzzling results. The first finding is a significantly lower urinary sodium (130 vs. 154 mmol/24 h) and potassium excretion (34 vs. 52 mmol/24 h) in African participants when compared with CRIC participants, with a significant leftward shift of the distribution curve of UNa 24 h and UK 24 h in DCA participants.

Looking at the association between urinary sodium and potassium excretion and BP, no association of UNa 24 h and UK 24 h with systolic BP or diastolic BP was found in DCA subjects. In contrast, some significant associations were observed in Black patients in CRIC; for example, between UNa 24 h and systolic BP. Similarly, some associations were found with diastolic BP in CRIC but not in DCA participants. Thus, in Black CRIC participants, higher UK 24 h excretion was significantly associated with a lower diastolic BP. Regarding the associations between urinary protein excretion and urinary sodium and potassium excretion, UNa 24 h was significantly associated with higher proteinuria in all 3 groups. In the Black and White CRIC participants, higher quartiles of UK 24 h were significantly associated with an increased 24-hour urine protein excretion. A similar trend was observed in DCA participants but it was not significant.

Taken together, these results suggest that there are some significant differences in the associations between sodium and potassium excretion and BP among Black patients with CKD whether they live in the United States or in Africa. One obvious question is: are these differences real? As mentioned by the authors, the study has some important limitations. First, the power of the study may be insufficient as far as the DCA population is concerned. Second, there were major differences in the characteristics of the 2 populations. Indeed, the DCA population was younger, had a lower prevalence of hypertension (∼ 65% vs. ∼ 90%), smoking, alcohol use, diabetes history, but a higher estimated glomerular filtration rate and a higher 24-hour urine protein than Black participants in CRIC. Third, there might have been major socioeconomic and environmental differences between DCA and Black CRIC participants as suggested by a higher body mass index in CRIC versus DCA (32 vs. 26 kg/m2, P < 0.001). Thus, the comparison between DCA and CRIC participants might not be optimal, and data would need to be confirmed using better matched populations. Nevertheless, these findings would tend to emphasize the fact that race cannot be characterized by simple morphological and biological parameters and that there might be major differences within the Black populations depending on the socioeconomic environment.

Ilori et al.’s9 observations raise an interesting question regarding the future management of this CKD population characterized by the association of a low sodium or low potassium intake. This combination is rather unusual because one is generally confronted with the situation of a high sodium or low potassium intake.S1 In this latter situation, potassium supplementation has been found to provide clinical benefits; but would potassium supplementation be beneficial in patients on a lower sodium intake? Today, we do not have any clear evidence to support potassium supplementation in patients on a lower salt diet in terms of cardiovascular and kidney protection. Therefore, there is a need to perform a prospective study to answer that question. However, one should be careful to increase potassium intake without increasing sodium intake, a risk which is increasing in many middle- and low-income countries because of a global “Westernization” of food.
