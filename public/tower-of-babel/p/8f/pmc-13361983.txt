Predictors of Acute Kidney Injury and In-Hospital Mortality in Patients With RT-PCR Confirmed Lassa Fever in Southwest Nigeria
Authors: Olalekan E Ojo, Muzamil O Hassan, Olubukola A Ojo, Adetumi A Subulade, Isaac Ihinmikaye, Oyebimpe O Oyegunle, Olufunke B Gbenga-Ayeni, Olufemi A Ayodeji, Liasu A Ahmed, Fatiu A Arogundade, Rasheed A Balogun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundData on the epidemiology of acute kidney injury (AKI) in adults hospitalized with Lassa fever is limited. We investigated the rates and predictors of AKI and in-hospital mortality at a National Treatment Centre in Southwest Nigeria.MethodsThis retrospective study included 482 adults with RT-PCR-confirmed Lassa fever admitted to the Federal Medical Centre, Owo, Ondo State, Nigeria, from January 2019 to December 2024. Predictors of AKI and in-hospital mortality were identified by multivariable logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards regression analyses.ResultsAcute kidney injury prevalence was 21% (95% CI, 17.4%–24.9%). Overall, 48 patients (10.1%) died: 33 (32.7%) with AKI and 15 (4.0%) without AKI (OR 11.647; 95% CI, 6.001–22.604; P < .001). Independent predictors of AKI included male sex (P < .001), bleeding abnormalities (P = .044), diabetes (P = .029), elevated white blood cell count (P = .005), increased neutrophil count (P = .003), and elevated aspartate transaminase (P = .017). Predictors of in-hospital mortality were AKI (P = .033), oliguria (P = .045), mean arterial blood pressure (P = .001), hematuria (P = .015), and elevated monocyte count (P = .022). Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated significantly poorer survival in patients with AKI versus those without (mean survival 19.0 vs 29.7 days; P ≤ .001).ConclusionsAcute kidney injury complicates approximately one-fifth of RT-PCR-confirmed Lassa fever hospitalizations and is associated with an 11-fold increase in mortality. Male sex, bleeding, diabetes, leukocytosis, and elevated aspartate transaminase independently predict AKI, while oliguria, hematuria, blood pressure, and monocyte count predict death.Keywords: acute kidney injury, Lassa fever, mortality, predictors

Acute kidney injury (AKI), a rapid decline in kidney function, characterized by a serum creatinine rise of ≥0.3 mg/dL in 48 hours, a ≥ 1.5 times increase from baseline in 7 days, or urinary output <0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours [1], affects over 13 million people globally, causing nearly 2 million deaths, with about 80% of cases in low- and middle-income countries [2–4].

Acute kidney injury is a common complication of Lassa fever, affecting 28% of patients in a Nigerian study [5, 6]. Mortality rates among hospitalized adults with Lassa fever who experience AKI increase from 24% to 60%, with 30% requiring hemodialysis [5, 6]. Acute kidney injury poses a significant challenge in developing countries like Nigeria due to its high incidence, limited resources for severe cases, and late patient presentation, which is exacerbated by the complications associated with Lassa fever, a viral hemorrhagic fever [7].

First identified in Nigeria in 1969 [8], Lassa fever has been responsible for recurrent outbreaks in Nigeria [9] and several other West African countries where it is endemic, notably in Sierra Leone [10], Togo [11], the Republic of Benin [12], and Liberia [13], exhibiting a high case fatality rate [14]. Additionally, cases have been documented in Europe [15] and the United States [16], primarily linked to imports from West Africa. Lassa fever is caused by the Lassa virus, an arenavirus primarily hosted by the multimammate rat, Mastomys natalensis. The virus spreads through contact with the blood, urine, or feces of infected rats, as well as through the bodily fluids of those who are infected [17].

Lassa Fever can range from asymptomatic to severe, life-threatening multisystem diseases. Early symptoms often resemble other febrile illnesses, including fatigue, fever, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, muscle and chest pain, and hearing loss, complicating early diagnosis and delaying treatment. Acute kidney injury frequently manifests as part of a multisystem syndrome characterized by the impairment and dysfunction of various systems, including the central nervous system, pulmonary system, cardiovascular system, hematopoietic system, gastrointestinal system, and liver [18, 19].

A study by Asogun et al [20] indicated that fatal cases exhibited consistently elevated blood urea nitrogen and serum creatinine levels compared to survivors, with AKI being a common complication of severe systemic disease. Despite the endemic nature of Lassa fever and its high mortality rate, the specific factors contributing to AKI in Lassa fever infections remain unclear. It is possible that additional elements, beyond the virus itself, could exacerbate mortality linked to Lassa fever and AKI. Understanding these factors could enhance our ability to predict and manage AKI more effectively. This study aimed to retrospectively investigate the occurrence of AKI, and the factors linked to AKI and mortality in patients with reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) confirmed Lassa Fever Infection in Southwest Nigeria.

METHODS

Participants and Setting

The study involved all consecutive patients diagnosed with Lassa fever who were admitted to the Lassa fever ward at the Federal Medical Centre, Owo (FMCO) in Ondo State, Nigeria, from January 2019 to December 2024. Confirmation of Lassa fever was achieved using the Quantitative PCR machine, Model Rotor-Gene Q, S/N RO516117, Qiagen Hilden, Germany. The FMCO Research Ethics Committee approved the study with the approval number FMC OWO/HREC/2025/100. The study involved all adult patients with RT-PCR confirmed Lassa fever aged 18 and older who were managed at the isolation ward of the infectious unit of the hospital. Patients were excluded if they were suspected of having Lassa fever but tested negative for the Lassa polymerase chain reaction (PCR), lacked baseline creatinine data, had pre-existing end-stage renal disease, had incomplete data, were already on maintenance dialysis, or were pregnant. The research adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational studies.

Data Collection

Data regarding age, sex, monthly income, clinical signs and symptoms, mean arterial blood pressure, lab tests including serum creatinine, urea, electrolytes, complete blood count, urinalysis, comorbidities like diabetes, and outcomes such as survival, death, transfer, or discharge against medical advice were recorded. During the initial enrollment, participants self-reported their demographic information. This included recording their highest level of educational attainment. Furthermore, trained personnel recorded the date of symptom onset (defined as the first day the patient reported experiencing symptoms related to their illness) and the date of hospital admission for each patient. Complete blood count samples were analyzed using the automated hematology analyzer from Sysmex Corporation, Japan, while creatinine, urea, plasma electrolytes, albumin, aspartate aminotransferase, and alanine aminotransferase were measured with the Biochemistry analyzer Piccolo, BioSan SIA, Latvia. Urinalysis was performed using Macherey-Nagel GmbH & Co. KG, Düren, Germany.

The RealStar Lassa Virus Reverse Transcriptase Polymerase Chain Reaction Kit 2.0 assay

Confirmatory testing for Lassa virus (LASV) is performed at the FMCO Infection Control and Research Laboratory using the commercial RealStar Lassa Virus RT-PCR Kit 2.0 (Altona Diagnostics GmbH, Hamburg, Germany). This molecular assay provides qualitative detection of LASV RNA and cycle threshold (Ct) values to evaluate disease severity.

Assay Workflow and Design

The assay employs a dual-target, RT-PCR system specifically engineered to accommodate the high genetic diversity of LASV strains across West Africa.

Quality Assurance

An exogenous, heterologous internal control is incorporated into the reaction mix to monitor nucleic acid extraction efficiency, sample preparation integrity, and to identify the presence of any RT-PCR inhibitors.

Diagnosis and Staging of Acute Kidney Injury

The diagnosis and staging of acute kidney injury (AKI) were established using the kidney disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines [1]. Acute kidney injury is characterized by any of the following conditions: a rise in serum creatinine (SCr) of at least 0.3 mg/dl (or 26.5 μmol/L) within a 24-hour period, or an increase in SCr to 1.5 times the baseline level, which is either known or assumed to have occurred within the last 7 days. Stage 1 AKI is identified by a SCr increase of 1.5 to 1.9 times the baseline, or by an increase of 0.3 mg/dl (or 26.5 μmol/L), or a urine output of less than 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 to 12 hours. Stage 2 is characterized by a SCr increase of 2.0 to 2.9 times the baseline or a urine output of less than 0.5 mL/kg/h over 12 hours. Stage 3 is defined as an increase of 3.0 times the baseline or a SCr level of 4.0 mg/dl (or 353.6 μmol/L) or lower, a decrease in estimated glomerular filtration rate to below 35 mL/min/1.73m2, a urine output of less than 0.3 mL/kg/h for 24 hours or more, or anuria lasting 12 hours or more. However, due to the lack of accurate urine output data for many patients upon admission, we were limited to using the criterion of elevated SCr.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of interest were the incidence of AKI and in-hospital mortality. These endpoints were determined throughout the duration of the hospital stay. Information regarding patient survival, instances of death, and cases of transfer or discharge against medical advice were obtained from hospital discharge records and electronic health records. Survival time was defined as the duration from the date of hospital admission to the date of death or the end of the follow-up period. In-hospital mortality was defined as all-cause mortality that occurred during the hospital stay.

Statistical Analysis

Patients’ characteristics and risk factors were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, while continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range. Chi-square tests were employed to assess associations among selected categorical variables, and either independent sample t-tests or Mann Whitney tests were utilized to explore relationships between continuous variables, as appropriate. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the associations of these factors with AKI, with associations quantified by odds ratios (ORs). The Kaplan–Meier survival curve was applied to compare in-hospital mortality rates between patients with AKI and those without. Hospital admission day was designated as day 0. The relative hazard of in-hospital mortality was assessed using Cox proportional hazards regression, with models adjusted for previously mentioned factors. Data analyses were executed using SAS version 9.4 (Cary, NC) and SPSS version 21 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY), with a P value of less than .05 deemed statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics of Lassa Fever Patients

From January 2019 to December 2024, 15,346 samples were received at the laboratory as suspected cases of Lassa fever. Among these, 14 845 tested negative, while 501 tested positive for Lassa RT-PCR. Out of the 501 confirmed cases, 482 (96.2%) were included in the study (see Figure 1). The cohort consisted of 241 males (50%) and 241 females (50%), with a mean age of 38.4 ± 17.8 years. Most of the participants, 410 in total (85%), had at least completed secondary school. Of these, 281 participants (58.3%) had obtained tertiary degrees, while 129 (26.8%) had finished secondary education. The median duration from the onset of symptoms to admission was 7 days (IQR 4–9).

Prevalence of Acute Kidney Injury

A total of 101 patients, representing a prevalence rate of 21% (95% CI, 17.4%–24.9%) of the population, developed AKI at different stages as defined by the KDIGO criteria. Specifically, 41 patients (40.6%) were classified as stage 1, 17 patients (16.8%) as stage 2, and 43 patients (42.6%) as stage 3, with the latter requiring immediate hemodialysis. Tables 1 and 2 present a comparison of the sociodemographic, clinical, and laboratory parameters between patients with AKI and those without AKI. Acute kidney injury was more common in male patients, with 69 cases documented in males compared to 32 in females. Patients with AKI exhibited markedly elevated MABP, peak SCr, hepatic transaminases, and total white blood cell count. Additionally, there was a greater incidence of proteinuria, hematuria, and bleeding abnormalities, alongside significantly reduced PCV and albumin levels. However, the variations in mean platelet count, lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio, and neutrophil-to-monocyte ratio did not reach statistical significance. Of the 482 patients in the cohort, 47(9.8%) received hemodialysis during their hospital stay. The indications for hemodialysis included uremia and fluid overload.

Table 3 presents the findings from univariate and multivariable logistic regression analysis aimed at identifying factors associated with AKI. The adjusted odds (OR) of developing AKI were significantly higher among male patients with Lassa fever [OR, 5.108 (2.202–11.849); P < .001], those exhibiting bleeding abnormalities (OR, 6.492 [1.049–40.179]; P = .044), individuals with diabetes (OR, 4.286 [1.157–15.870]; P = .029), and patients with elevated total white blood cell counts (OR, 1.105 [1.031–1.185]; P = .005), high neutrophil% (OR, 1.064 [1.021–1.109]; P = .003), and elevated AST levels (OR, 1.002 [1.000–1.004]; P = .017).

In-Hospital Mortality

Out of 482 participants, 5 were discharged against medical advice and 1 was transferred to another facility, resulting in 476 participants who were treated at FMCO. Among these 476 participants, 48 (10.1%) died, including 33 (32.7%) from a group of 101 patients diagnosed with AKI, and 15 (4.0%) from a cohort of 375 patients without AKI. A significant correlation exists between AKI and mortality (OR, 11.647 [6.001–22.604]; P < .001). The mean survival time until death was 27 days (IQR 16.7–37.3). Out of the 48 fatalities, 42 took place within the first 7 days of hospital admission, 3 occurred between 8 and 19 days, and 3 happened more than 20 days after admission. No significant statistical association was found between duration of symptoms before presentation to the hospital and outcome (P = .292). Likewise, post hoc analysis did not show any association between education status and outcomes.

The Kaplan–Meier Survival Curve (Figure 2) showed that patients with AKI had significantly poorer survival rates compared to those without AKI, with mean survival duration of 19.0 days versus 29.7 days (P ≤ .001). Table 4 presents the findings from the Cox proportional hazard regression analysis, identifying risk factors linked to in-hospital mortality in patients undergoing treatment for Lassa fever. The adjusted analysis revealed that oliguria (HR, 11.257 [1.052–120.501]; P = .045), MABP (HR, 1.125 [1.048–1.206]; P = .001), the presence of AKI (HR, 17.377 [1.261–239.370]`; P = .033), hematuria (HR, 9.767 [1.551–61.504]; P = .015), and monocyte count (HR, 1.099 [1.014–1.192]; P = .022) were all independently associated with an increased risk of mortality.

DISCUSSION

We evaluated the prevalence and risk factors of AKI and mortality among patients with RT-PCR confirmed Lassa fever treated at a National Treatment Center in Southwest Nigeria. The findings revealed a significant AKI rate of 21% in these patients, with male sex, bleeding disorders, a history of diabetes, increased total white blood cell counts, elevated neutrophil counts, and high AST levels identified as predictors. The in-hospital mortality rate was 10.1%, and the predictors of mortality were AKI, oliguria, blood pressure, hematuria, and monocyte count.

We found that the prevalence of AKI was 21% (95% CI, 17.4%–24.9%), aligning closely with the 28% and 28.5% rates reported by Okokhere et al [5] in South-south Nigeria and in a systematic review by Merson et al [21], respectively. However, this figure is notably higher than the 13.5% prevalence found in a study by Ajayi et al [6] conducted at the same treatment center in Owo, Southwest Nigeria. The higher prevalence in our study may be due to our stricter inclusion criteria, which excluded patients without baseline creatinine values, those with pre-existing end-stage renal disease, and individuals with incomplete data. These exclusions helped ensure more accurate AKI diagnosis and staging. The observed variation in prevalence across different studies underscores the importance of standardized diagnostic criteria and comprehensive data collection in assessing AKI among patients with Lassa fever.

Our study identified several significant predictors of AKI. Male sex emerged as the strongest predictor, with males having over 5 times the odds of developing AKI compared to females (OR 5.108, P < .001), consistent with previous reports of higher AKI rates in males across clinical contexts; possible mechanisms include differences in body composition, sex hormones, and immune responses to viral infection. Bleeding abnormalities were strongly associated with AKI (OR 6.492, P = .044), likely reflecting hemorrhagic-mediated renal injury through hypovolemia, disseminated intravascular coagulation, or direct viral effects on the renal vasculature. Pre-existing diabetes independently predicted AKI (OR 4.286, P = .029), consistent with known heightened AKI susceptibility in this population due to chronic kidney disease, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired immunity [22]. Elevated leukocyte and neutrophil counts (OR 1.105 and 1.064, respectively) implicate systemic inflammation in AKI pathogenesis, while elevated AST (OR 1.002, P = .017) underscores the multi-organ nature of severe Lassa fever, where hepatic and renal injury frequently coexist. While socioeconomic factors clarify the initial acquisition of Lassa fever, they fail to predict mortality in severe cases. Biological and clinical markers—such as renal and hepatic function panels—are superior predictors of fatality and AKI because they reflect the immediate, systemic impact of the virus. Consequently, our study found no significant association between educational status and clinical outcomes in Lassa fever patients. Our study found an in-hospital mortality rate of 10.1%, with significantly higher mortality among patients with AKI (32.7%) compared to those without (4.0%), conferring an 11-fold increase in mortality risk. Acute kidney injury was the strongest independent predictor of death (adjusted HR 17.377, P = .033), with oliguria as an additional marker of severe renal injury and reduced perfusion (HR 11.257, P = .045). Higher MABP also predicted mortality (HR 1.125, P = .001), likely reflecting catecholamine-mediated compensatory vasoconstriction in the context of critical illness rather than hypertensive injury per se. Hematuria (HR 9.767, P = .015) may signal glomerular or tubular damage, while elevated monocyte counts (HR 1.099, P = .022) suggest a role for innate immune dysregulation in fatal outcomes.

Our findings have important clinical implications. The identification of specific predictors of AKI and mortality can help clinicians stratify risk and implement targeted interventions. Patients who are male, have diabetes, or present with bleeding, elevated inflammatory markers, or liver dysfunction should be monitored closely for AKI. Early recognition and aggressive supportive care, including fluid management and consideration for renal replacement therapy, may improve outcomes.

Our observed in-hospital mortality rate of 10.1% is notably lower than the 30%–69% case fatality rates reported from Lassa fever treatment centers in Sierra Leone and Liberia [10, 13, 23] and lower than mortality rates reported during previous Nigerian outbreaks [24-27]. Several factors may account for this difference. First, the FMCO is a dedicated National Lassa fever Treatment Centre with established isolation infrastructure, trained clinical personnel, and standardized management protocols, which may confer a survival advantage over settings with less specialized capacity. Second, ribavirin, a nucleoside analog antiviral with demonstrated efficacy when administered early in the course of Lassa fever, is part of the standard of care at FMCO and is administered to all confirmed cases, consistent with WHO and Nigerian national treatment guidelines. Third, the genetic diversity of LASV strains across West Africa is well recognized; Nigerian strains (predominantly lineages II and III) may differ in pathogenicity compared to strains circulating in Sierra Leone and Liberia (lineage IV), potentially contributing to differences in clinical severity and mortality. Finally, the exclusion of patients with incomplete data in our cohort may have introduced a survival bias, as sicker patients who died early were more likely to have incomplete records. Regarding longitudinal AKI progression, the retrospective nature of this study precluded systematic tracking of AKI stage transitions over time. Among the 47 patients (9.8%) who received hemodialysis, the indication was uremia or fluid overload, reflecting Stage 3 AKI; however, granular data on the trajectory from lower AKI stages to Stage 3, and on the impact of dialysis initiation timing on outcomes, were not available. Future prospective studies should systematically capture AKI progression and the effect of renal replacement therapy on survival in this population.

This study has several limitations. The retrospective design and single-center setting may limit generalizability. The lack of complete urine output data meant we could not fully apply KDIGO criteria. Additionally, we did not have data on viral load, which may influence disease severity and AKI risk. Although the RealStar Lassa Virus RT-PCR Kit 2.0 provides cycle threshold (Ct) values, we did not systematically use Ct values for semi-quantitative viral load stratification (eg, high, medium, or low viremia) in the current analysis; future studies should prospectively incorporate Ct-based viral load categorization to explore associations with AKI severity and clinical outcomes. Additionally, because our center uses a conventional real-time RT-PCR platform rather than more sensitive next-generation methods, patients with low-level viremia may have been missed, potentially introducing a sample bias toward more severe disease. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the reported AKI prevalence and mortality rates. Future prospective, multi-center studies with comprehensive data collection, including viral load measurements and novel biomarkers of kidney injury, would provide deeper insights into AKI pathogenesis in Lassa fever [28, 29].

CONCLUSION

Acute kidney injury is common in patients hospitalized with Lassa fever, affecting 21% of our cohort and conferring a markedly increased risk of mortality. Male sex, bleeding abnormalities, diabetes, elevated leukocyte and neutrophil counts, and elevated AST predict AKI development. Acute kidney injury, oliguria, elevated blood pressure, hematuria, and elevated monocyte counts independently predict in-hospital mortality. These findings underscore the importance of early recognition and management of AKI in patients with Lassa fever and highlight the need for enhanced renal care capacity in endemic regions.
