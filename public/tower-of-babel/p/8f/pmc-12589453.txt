Effects of cold storage on the growth and development of Trichopria drosophilae (Hymenoptera: Diapriidae)
Authors: Qinyuan Zhang, Xuxiang Liu, Yuhao Wu, Run Zhang, Yongbang Yang, Qingwen Fan, Shi Chen, Bo Wang, Qinge Ji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrosophila suzukii (Diptera: Drosophilidae) is a polyphagous pest native to Asia that can cause serious economic damage. In this study, the effects of low-temperature storage on growth and development of Trichopria drosophilae were studied by using the methods of cold storage pupae to evaluate the cold storage tolerance and quality assessment of T. drosophilae, providing a theoretical basis for solving the mass production, storage and concentrated release of T. drosophilae. The results showed that the emergence rate of the 4th instar larvae decreased with extension of storage time from 10 to 20 d at 8 °C, 10 °C and 12 °C. The emergence rate remained as high as 79.24% after storage for 15 d at 16 °C. At 14 °C and 16 °C, the emergence rate of the 1-day-old pupae was not significantly different from that of control (77.27%). The above results indicated that the 4th instar and 1-day-old pupae of T. drosophilae were more suitable for long-term storage at 14 °C and 16 °C. Under the above experimental conditions, T. drosophila quality after cold storage for 10, 15 and 20 d was continuously investigated. The results showed that parasitism rates of different stages after treatment were significantly affected by storage temperature and time. Long-term cold storage could reduce the emergence rate and sex ratio of F1 generation. The emergence time of the 1-day-old pupae was gradually shortened with extension of storage time at 16 °C. The starvation-resistant lifespan of T. drosophilae after cold storage was significantly longer than that of control. This study results will further clarify the effects of cold temperature storage on growth and development of T. drosophilae.Keywords: Natural enemy, Spotted wing drosophila, Low-temperature storage, Biological control, Shelf lifeSubject terms: Developmental biology, Physiology

Introduction

Drosophila suzukii (Diptera: Drosophilidae), also known as blueberry and cherry fruit fly, has a short generation time and high fertility, which can lead to rapid population growth and significant economic losses of commercial crops1. Female adults have large, hard, sharp and serrated ovipositors that penetrate the epidermis of berry fruits, helping D. suzukii to infest mature or sub-mature fruits before these are harvested, and therefore, they are considered economically destructive pests2. Drosophila suzukii has a wide host range, and can harm thin-skinned berries, such as raspberries, grapes, blueberries, strawberries, cherries, and other fruits, such as apples, persimmons, apricots and plums3,4. Since Tan5 first reported D. suzukii in China, it has been found in more than 20 provinces6. Over the past decade, D. suzukii has become one of the most studied fruit pests in China.

Biological control, especially through the artificial mass and release of natural enemies, can effectively suppress pest populations7. Studies have shown that many species of hymenopteran parasitoids have a wide range of hosts, and they can attack a variety of fruit flies worldwide8. At present, the known natural enemies of D. suzukii include predators, such as Chrysoperla carnea (Neuroptera: Chrysopidae) and Atheta coriaria (Coleoptera: Staphylinidae), and parasitoids9. The parasitoids are mainly from Braconidae, Figitidae, Pteromalidae and Diapriidae. Among them, Trichopria drosophilae (Hymenoptera: Diapriidae) is the most effective parasitoid, which can parasitise the pupae of many fruit fly species10. Trichopria drosophilae is a dominant parasitoid in the pupal stage of D. suzukii11. Large-scale breeding and commercial production of parasitoids have long been considered crucial worldwide measures of biological control12. Unlike chemical pesticides, most parasitoids used in biological control have a relatively short shelf life due to the conditions and time constraints of production13,14. Cold storage has been proven to be one of the effective ways to extend the shelf life of parasitoids, allowing flexible and efficient mass production, such as storing living bodies when they are not needed and accumulating or storing sufficient numbers for simultaneous release into the field during critical periods of pest outbreaks by prolonging adult survival and delaying emergence15. Insect cold storage is widely used in biological control programmes by providing a stable and adequate supply of natural enemy sources and reducing the economic input to maintain populations during periods of weak market demand16.

Insect cold tolerance is a malleable trait that is usually influenced by a range of endogenous and exogenous factors before, during and even after cold storage17–19. Studies have shown that various factors have vital synergistic or antagonistic effects on insect survival and development under short or long-term cold storage conditions20. In the process of cold storage, temperature is one of the most abiotic factors affecting the survival rate of insects21. In general, the lower the storage temperature is, the higher is the insect mortality22. In cold storage, a temperature of 5 °C or below may not be suitable for parasitoids23. Because the temperature selection for storage should be based on metabolic rate reduction or development need and maintain a relative balance with the cumulative risk of chilling injury, which can play a role in extending the shelf life of insects24. Therefore, to extend shelf life, parasitoids are generally stored at 5–15 °C25. Because different species and temperatures have different optimal insect states for cold storage, it is crucial to determine the optimal development stage when planning cold storage of parasitoids26. The growth and development of insects are usually affected by temperature, and studies have shown that long-term exposure to cold of hosts and parasitoids may lead to lethal or sub-lethal phenomena27. At the same time, low temperatures can also cause insects to deplete their energy reserves after prolonged starvation28. Therefore, the result of cold storage is the cryopreservation effect, starvation, or a combination of both. Parasitoids, like other insects, have a trade-off between survival and reproduction, usually acquiring and converting energy resources obtained from the environment to maintain their own homeostasis and produce offspring29. Lipid reserves play a central role in this trade-off of resource allocation, especially for parasitoids that cannot synthesise new lipids30. The depletion of energy reserves, especially lipids, during cold storage is expected to translate into health costs for survival or reproduction31. In other words, the effects of cold storage may not be immediately noticeable but may carry over to another stage, with negative effects on insect development, sex ratio, reproductive ability, longevity or flight ability32.

Breeding a large number of T. drosophilae for large-scale release in a short period is a wonderful dream that is difficult to realise because D. suzukii easily produces a strong immune response to T. drosophilae, resulting in a slow reproduction process. It is a very encouraging fact that our previous studies have demonstrated that the use of D. melanogaster of the same species group, which has a small size, fast reproduction, short growth cycle, and easy breeding, has great application potential as an alternative host for large-scale breeding of T. drosophilae33. Cold storage is widely used in biological control because it can provide a stable and sufficient insect source supply for large-scale breeding and reducing the economic investment in maintaining the population during the period of weak market demand34. The study of the cold storage conditions of T. drosophilae can provide a scientific basis for solving the problem of mass breeding and released parasitoids, which have quite significance in research and application. Although there have been several studies on the cold storage conditions of T. drosophilae, these studies were based on objects parasitised by D. suzukii pupae. There were some problems in the study, such as very low storage temperature and long storage time. Therefore, this study investigated the effects of cold storage on the quality of T. drosophilae to explore the practical storage conditions suitable for the mass reproduction of parasitoids. This study will further clarify the cold storage effects on the growth and development of T. drosophilae. The results provide a basic theoretical guide for the application of cold storage technology to prolong the shelf life of natural enemy resources.

Discussion

To store natural enemy insects for a long time and maintain a high emergence rate, low-temperature storage is a relatively simple and common method35. In summary, studying the cold storage technology of T. drosophilae is necessary to determine the most suitable insect state, storage temperature and time18. According to the actual application and the existing experimental basis, firstly, in this study, the emergence rates of the 4th instar and 1-, 2- and 3-day-old pupae were studied after storage for 10, 15 and 20 d at 8 °C, 10 °C, 12 °C, 14 °C and 16 °C. The results showed that 4th instar of T. drosophilae was suitable for storage at 14 °C and 16 °C. At 14 °C and 16 °C, emergence rate of 1-day-old pupae was not significantly different from that of control, and it was still 68.28% after storage for 20 d at 14 °C, indicating that suitable storage stage and long-term storage temperatures were 1-day-old pupae, 14 °C and 16 °C. Shui et al.36 found that survival rate of Neoseiulus californicus (Mesostigmata: Phytoseiidae) was lower than 50% when stored at 4 °C, 7 °C and 10 °C for 10 d. Pandey and Johnson37 indicated that to avoid death, Anagyrus ananatis (Hymenoptera: Encyrtidae) in pre-pupae to pupae stage must be temporarily stored at 10.1 °C for less than 2 weeks. Wang et al.38 showed that T. drosophilae and Pachycrepoideus vendemmia (Hymenoptera: Pteromalidae) in California population continued at 12 °C for 1, 2 or 3 months and then moved to 23 °C for feeding, and emergence rate of parasitoids decreased with low-temperature storage time extension. In addition, Chouioia cunea (Hymenoptera: Eulophidae), Diaeretiella rapae (Hymenoptera: Braconidae), Encarsia Sophia (Hymenoptera: Aphelinidae) and Habrobracon hebetor (Hymenoptera: Braconidae) reached same conclusion39–42. These results are consistent with this experiment, indicating that storage temperature and duration are crucial factors affecting emergence rate of parasitoids42. Parasitoids stored at low temperatures will consume lots of energy to overcome external environmental pressure, and energy consumption will affect their development and emergence. Therefore, their emergence rate will generally decrease with storage temperature reduction and storage time extension25,43.

Adult female parasitoids are most effective sex on biological control, sex ratio of parasitoids after low-temperature storage is very crucial to improve its field control efficiency44. Low temperatures usually affect sex ratio of parasitoids because of differences in male and female mortality after long periods of storage at low temperatures45. For example, Yi46 study showed that with storage time extension at low temperatures, the ratio of female emergence increased. In another case, after low-temperature storage, male and female mortality rates were similar. For example, Lin et al.47 stored Fopius vandenboschi (Hymenoptera: Braconidae) of different insect states at 13 °C for 10, 15, 20 and 25 d, and results showed that there was no difference in sex ratio compared with control. Wang48 and Shi et al.49 also obtained same results in a semblable study of Exorista civilis (Diptera: Tachinidae) and Cotesia plutellae (Hymenoptera: Braconidae). The results showed that there was no significant difference in sex ratio between 4th instar and 1-day-old pupae after storage for 10, 15 and 20 d at 14 °C and 16 °C, respectively.

Parasitism rates are usually quantified by providing a large number of hosts50,51. Some studies reported that parasitism rate of parasitoids decreased with cold storage time extension52. Jalali and Singh53 showed that the parasitism of Trichogrammatoidea eldanae (Hymenoptera: Trichogrammatidae) was affected by storage temperature and time. After storage for 14 d at 2 °C and 5 °C, its parasitism rate was 0. Al-Tememi and Ashfaq54 and Özder55 showed that the parasitism rates of T. cacoeciae and H. hebetor decreased with storage time extension after removal of cold storage. This study showed that parasitism rates of T. drosophilae were significantly affected by storage temperature and time. The result is consistent with previous. Research showed that insect reproductive organs are particularly susceptible to temperature. Long-term exposure at low temperatures may lead to not only delayed egg maturation but also malformation of female adult reproductive organs in extreme cases56. At the same time, it can also lead to male sterility. Because female T. drosophilae adult usually mates only once, mating with an infertile male can lead to fertilisation failure. However, it is not clear whether mating disorder is caused by behavioural changes in males, females, or both.

In some case, low-temperature lead a significant impact on offspring57. For example, offspring produced after prolonged storage at low temperatures have different biological indicators and population parameters than offspring of unstressed parents, even if they live under conditions without environmental stress. This transgenerational effect may be transmitted through reproductive cells of parents58. Studies have shown that long-term storage at low temperatures can reduce emergence rate and sex ratio of F1 generation of parasitoids. For example, sex ratio of T. ostriniae F1 generation decreased with low-temperature storage time extension. Askari et al.59 and Forouzan et al.60 found same in studies of Lysiphlebus fabarum (Hymenoptera Braconidae), Psix saccharicola (Hymenoptera: Platygastridae), H. hebetor and other insects. This study show that long-term storage at low temperatures can reduce emergence rate and sex ratio of F1 generation. However, mechanism by which hypothermia damage is passed on to next generation through female reproduction is unknown.

Low-temperature storage will affect the growth and development of insects, such as extending emergence time. The development of most insects will return to normal after hypothermia, but time required for some insects to complete development after hypothermia storage is increased61. In some studies, both development time and emergence duration increased with storage time extension62. Zhang and Wu63 showed that developmental duration of ovulatory Tetrastichus hagenowii (Hymenoptera: Eulophidae) was significantly longer than control after storage for 10 and 20 d at 10 °C. Archer et al.64 showed that the developmental period of L. testaceipes and A. pyralophagus increased by 3–4 and 2–6 times, respectively, compared with control after low-temperature storage. Benelli et al.65 showed that developmental period of E. larvarum in larval stage was prolonged by 5–7 d compared with control after storage for 5 d at 15 °C and 20 °C. Meanwhile, low-temperature storage may not only prolong emergence time of parasitoids but also affect emergence time distribution, and thus affect the emergence uniformity of parasitoids. However, some parasitoids still maintained slow development during cold storage, so time required for their development after treatment was shorter than others. Xu et al.66 showed that emergence duration of 1-day-old pupae T. brontispae was shorter than control after being released from low temperatures. Mills and Nealis67 showed that emergence duration of Ceranthia samarensis (Diptera: Tachinidae) after cold storage decreased with treatment extension. This study showed that emergence duration of 1-day-old pupae was gradually shortened with storage time extension at 16 °C and was significantly shorter than control after storage for 20 d at 16 °C, indicating that T. drosophilae still maintained slow development during low-temperature storage.

A large number of studies have shown that lifespan of emerging adults will be shortened after parasitoids released from long-term cold storage68. The reason may be that adult parasitoids do not have ability to accumulate lipid reserves, which also means that resource allocation strategy of parasitoids is limited by energy69. For example, parasitoids often choose to devote a lot of energy to reproduction after being subjected to low-temperature stress, leaving relatively little energy for their own survival. In extreme cases, adult lifespans may be very short, with some adults dying within hours of emergence70. Kidane et al.42 after storing 10-day-old E. Sophia at 8 °C and 12 °C for 1, 2 and 3 weeks, respectively, showed that lifespan of emerging adult was not affected after one week of storage at 8 °C and 12 °C, but it decreased with storage time extension. Levie et al.71 showed that with increase in storage duration, the lipid storage of A. rhopalosiphi gradually decreased and the lifespan of its emerging adults was significantly shortened. Similar results were also obtained in study of Aphidius colemani (Hymenoptera: Aphidiinae)72. This study also support above conclusions. Female lifespan after storing 4th instar and 1-day-old pupae at 14 °C for 15 and 20 d was significantly lower than that of control. Male lifespan after storage for 20 d at same temperature was also significantly lower than that of control.

In addition, long-term cold storage can cause abnormal development of parasitoids. For example, Tezze and Botto73 found that proportion of wing deformities in Trichogramma sp. increased with cold storage time extension. Morphological changes in wings of parasitoids can severely impair some of characteristics associated with their health, such as field flight and dispersal ability. Therefore, flight ability is another crucial characteristic affected by cold storage. Cold storage can seriously affect motor ability of parasitoids by inducing neuromuscular dysfunction, resulting in motor incoordination74. In addition, lipid and glycogen reserves are thought to be energy source for flight of various parasitoids, and energy expenditure and muscle changes during cold storage will lead to reduced mobility of emerging adults. Ayvaz et al.75 showed that in Trichogramma sp. in pupal stage after low-temperature storage, flight ability and walking speed of adults decreased. Luczynski et al.76 showed that flight ability of Eretmocerus eremicus (Hymenoptera: Aphelinidae) decreased with prolonged storage time. This study were similar to above conclusions. The flight rate of emerging T. drosophilae in 4th instar after cold storage was significantly affected by treatment temperature. The flight rate of emerging T. drosophilae in 1-day-old pupae was significantly affected by their interaction between storage temperature and time. With storage time extension, flight rate of emerging T. drosophilae decreased gradually.

In the end, effects of cold storage on parasitoids are not necessarily negative.61 Yang et al.77 showed that starvation of 5-instar and female Arma chinensis (Hemiptera: Pentatomidae) adults stored at 15 °C was improved compared with control. Gao78 showed that adult C. chilonis could enter dormancy state at 4 °C and their lifespan was extended to 10 d, which was 2.45 times compared with control. This phenomenon was found by Wang et al.79 in E. sorbillans and Qi et al.80 in Ophraella communa (Coleoptera: Chrysomelidae). In this study, it was found that starvation lifespans of 4th instar and 1-day-old pupae after cold storage were significantly longer than control, indicating that stress resistance of T. drosophilae may be improved after treatment. Although relationship between stress resistance increase and gene expression is unknown, the expression of many genes can be influenced by cold storage treatment during recovery process81. The restoration of protein homeostasis and accumulation and release of neuropeptides during stress and recovery processes are of significant importance for successfully resisting low-temperature stress. After T. drosophilae undergoes low-temperature stress, its starvation lifespan increases. Whether heat shock proteins and neuroendocrine signals play a regulatory role is worth further in-depth study82,83.

Materials and methods

Insect rearing

Trichopria drosophilae and D. melanogaster were collected from the orchards in Fuzhou City, Fujian Province, China, and the breeding host of T. drosophilae was D. melanogaster. Hosts and natural enemies were reared according to rearing methods previously described by Liu84. Primarily, they were bred in laboratory under certain conditions [25 °C ± 1 °C, 65% ± 5% RH, 12:12 h (L:D)] at the Joint FAO/IAEA Division Cooperation Center for Fruit Fly Control in China, Fuzhou, China, for more than 30 generations, and established a stable indoor breeding population for test.

Effect of cold storage on the emergence rate of T. drosophilae

In small square boxes, parasitoids were provided with hosts according to the ratio of parasitoids:hosts = 1:5. After parasitised for 24 h, the pupae were taken out and placed in a climate chamber (25 °C ± 1 °C, 70% ± 10% RH, 14 L:10 D). Here, 7200 parasitic host pupae (100 in each Petri dish) were taken for testing after 4 d. The parasitic pupae developed into 4th instar, 1-, 2- and 3-day-old pupae under original climate chamber conditions. Trichopria drosophilae samples were stored at 8 °C and 10 °C in a refrigerator and 12 °C, 14 °C and 16 °C in a climate chamber, respectively, for 10, 15 and 20 d, and then moved to origin conditions to development. After the first emergence of T. drosophilae, the emergence number was observed and recorded every 8 h. Adult parasitism rate, F1 emergence rate and sex ratio

According to the above conditions, T. drosophilae was fed with honey water until emergence after 4 d. Six treatment groups were set up. Each treatment group selected 500 D. melanogaster pupae for 50 pairs of T. drosophilae that emerged and mated for 24 h to parasitise on the same day. After parasitised for 24 h, the pupae were taken out and placed in a climate chamber. The emergence number and adult sex were observed and recorded every 8 h. When there was no more T. drosophilae, all pupae were dissected under a stereoscope to calculate the adult parasitism rate (P%). Emergence rhythm and adult (tolerance starvation) lifespan

The emergence number of T. drosophilae was observed and recorded every 24 h, from first emergence to absence, and the emergence duration and uniformity of T. drosophilae were counted. Twenty pairs (female:male = 1:1) of T. drosophilae adults that emerged within 8 h were placed in a Petri dish alone and observed every 8 h until all adults died, and the durations of adult tolerance starvation were determined. Another part with the same number of adults was fed with cotton balls dipped in 10% honey water (replace it daily) and observed every 8 h until all adults died, and the adult lifespan was determined. The experiment was repeated thrice.

Data analysis

SPSS 21.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used for data processing. The effects of the storage temperature and time of the 4th instar and 1-day-old pupae on the biological parameters of T. drosophilae were investigated by univariate and two-factor analysis of variance, and the interaction of the whole model was investigated. When an interaction existed, one-way ANOVA was used to analyse the difference between storage times at the same temperature and the difference between different temperature at same storage time. Statistical results were expressed as mean ± SE, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant, and multiple comparison results were marked using the letter-marking method.
