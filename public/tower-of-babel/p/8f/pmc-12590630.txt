Effects of prenatal bifidobacterium supplementation on the gut microbiome in preterm infants of preeclamptic mothers
Authors: Linlin Yin, Jing Zhao, Zhiping Zhou, Jun Zhang, Xiaoli Qu, Jie Shi, Feng Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study examined the relationship between maternal preeclampsia (PE) and gut microbiota colonization in preterm infants and analyzed the effects of prenatal Bifidobacterium supplementation.MethodsThis observational study included 45 preterm infants categorized according to their mothers’ exposure status during pregnancy. Group A (healthy controls, n = 15) included infants born to healthy mothers who received no supplementation; Group B (PE+Bifidobacterium, n = 15) included infants whose mothers had PE and received Bifidobacterium supplementation as part of routine clinical management; and Group C (PE only, n = 15) included infants born to mothers with PE who did not receive Bifidobacterium supplementation. All enrolled infants were followed from birth for subsequent analyses. The initial postnatal fecal samples of the infants were collected and analyzed using 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Microbial diversity within the intestinal microbiota was evaluated using alpha diversity (within-sample) and beta diversity (between-sample) analyses. To identify taxon-specific differences among groups, we performed linear discriminant analysis effect size and differential abundance analysis, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. The functional potential of the gut microbiota was inferred based on Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways via the PICRUSt2 algorithm.ResultsAlpha diversity analysis revealed significantly greater microbial diversity in the fecal microbiota of preterm infants born to healthy mothers (Group A) than in those delivered by mothers with PE, regardless of prenatal Bifidobacterium exposure. Taxonomic profiling revealed distinct microbial community structures across groups: Group A exhibited significant enrichment of Bacteroides at all taxonomic levels, along with an elevated abundance of Clostridium at the class and order levels. Group B showed a markedly greater relative abundance of Actinobacteria at the phylum level and Rothia at the genus level, whereas Group C was dominated by Proteobacteria (phylum level) and Streptococcus (genus level). All intergroup differences were statistically significant following Benjamini‒Hochberg correction (q < 0.05). A functional analysis of the gut microbiota revealed 53 KEGG pathways with significant overall group differences (p < 0.05), among which 23 pathways were significantly different in at least two groups (q < 0.05). Notably, the activity of the LPS biosynthesis pathway was significantly upregulated in Group C compared with Group A (q = 0.001). Although LPS biosynthesis activity was reduced in Group B relative to Group C (q = 0.018), it remained elevated compared to Group A (q = 0.001), suggesting incomplete mitigation of endotoxin risk. Additionally, glycolytic activity was significantly impaired in Group C relative to Group A (q = 0.003) but was partially restored in Group B compared to Group C (q = 0.022).ConclusionsMaternal PE impaired early-life gut microbiota establishment in preterm infants, manifesting in reduced microbial diversity, enrichment of pathogenic Proteobacteria and Streptococcus, and consequent functional dysbiosis characterized by elevated endotoxin biosynthesis potential and compromised energy metabolism. Although prenatal supplementation with Bifidobacterium partially restored the microbial compositional balance, promoting beneficial bacteria, reducing LPS synthesis activity, and partially improving glycolytic function, it failed to fully reverse endotoxin-related risks, indicating the need to develop more effective microbiota-targeted strategies to comprehensively optimize metabolic and immune homeostasis.

Background

Preeclampsia (PE) is a complex hypertensive disorder of pregnancy that affects approximately 5–8% of pregnancies globally [1–3]. Typically manifesting after 20 weeks of gestation, PE is characterized by hypertension and proteinuria and is often accompanied by multiorgan dysfunction. As a leading cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality worldwide, PE predisposes offspring to adverse outcomes, including preterm birth, fetal distress, fetal growth restriction, neonatal hypoglycemia, impaired neurodevelopment, and even death [4, 5].

PE arises from multifactorial etiological pathways involving both maternal and fetal factors, although the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Recent scientific focus has shifted toward understanding the association between the microbiome and PE, which can disrupt early-life gut microbiota colonization [6, 7]. During early-life gut microbiota colonization, the meconium microbiota reflects critical maternal–fetal microbial transmission processes, whereas the subsequent fecal microbiota, shaped by multifactorial influences, diverges significantly from the meconium microbiota [8–10]. Dysbiosis of the meconium microbiota substantially increases the risk of inflammatory bowel diseases, necrotizing enterocolitis, late-onset sepsis, neurodevelopmental disorders, and allergic conditions, which may persist into adulthood and impact lifelong health trajectories [11–14].

Neonatal gut microbiota colonization is influenced by numerous factors. While this colonization was traditionally believed to begin at birth, increasing evidence has demonstrated the presence of bacteria in the placenta, umbilical cord, and amniotic fluid, suggesting that fetal microbial exposure also occurs in utero [15, 16]. The maternal gut, vaginal, and skin microbiota serve as primary sources for neonatal meconium microbiota colonization, whereas subsequent microbial stabilization is influenced by delivery mode, gestational age, and feeding practices [17]. Studies have demonstrated that the majority (63%) of neonatal meconium microbiota can be traced to maternal gut bacteria [18, 19]. Maternal complications during pregnancy, including PE and gestational diabetes mellitus, may further alter the colonization of offspring gut microbiota through undefined mechanisms [20, 21]. However, whether and how PE specifically affects the neonatal gut microbiota remain largely unexplored.

Given the high prevalence of preterm birth among mothers with PE and the strong correlation of the maternal and meconium microbiota, this study isolated the specific effects of PE on the meconium microbiota, selecting preterm infants with comparable gestational ages and complications as controls to minimize confounding factors. This investigation not only advances our understanding of long-term health outcomes in offspring from PE pregnancies but also provides novel insights for early disease prevention and precision medicine strategies, ultimately improving maternal and neonatal health outcomes.

Methods

Research design

This observational study was conducted in the Neonatal Intensive Care Units of Chengdu Pidu District Maternal from July 2024 to May 2025. Preterm infants were enrolled and categorized into three groups on the basis of maternal clinical characteristics: Group A (healthy reference, n = 15) consisted of infants born to healthy mothers without prenatal Bifidobacterium supplementation; Group B (Bifidobacterium-exposed, n = 15) included infants born to mothers with PE who received Bifidobacterium supplementation during pregnancy as part of clinical care; and Group C (nonexposed, n = 15) included infants born to mothers with PE who received no supplementation. All infants were followed from birth for gut microbiota analysis, and the detailed clinical medical history of both mothers and offspring was recorded. The first postnatal fecal sample was collected after the initiation of feeding during hospitalization prior to the newborn’s initial antibiotic exposure (as verified via the electronic medical order system, with a mean collection time of 2.1 ± 0.8 hours after birth), transported to the laboratory under secure conditions at 4 °C, and stored at − 80 °C until subsequent DNA extraction.

Both maternal and neonatal samples in this study were covered under a single informed consent form, which explicitly included the following provisions: (1) authorization to collect maternal clinical data and biological specimens; (2) permission to obtain neonatal clinical data and meconium samples; and (3) clarification that samples would be used for mother–infant pair research. This consent procedure was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Chengdu Pidu District Maternal and Child Health Hospital (Approval No. 2025–56).

Recruitment of subjects and inclusion criteria

The diagnosis of PE in this study followed the guidelines of the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists [22]. The diagnostic criteria for PE included the following: after the 20th week of gestation, the patient’s blood pressure reached at least 140/90 mmHg on two separate occasions, and their proteinuria reached 300 mg. In the absence of proteinuria, PE can also be diagnosed if any of the following conditions are present: a platelet count below 100,000 × 109/L, a serum creatinine concentration exceeding 1.1 mg/dL or doubling in the absence of other renal diseases, elevated liver transaminase concentrations twice the normal level, pulmonary edema, or cerebral or visual symptoms. Preterm birth was defined as delivery occurring between 20 and 36 weeks of gestation.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) mothers diagnosed with PE or healthy pregnant women without severe pregnancy complications (including placental abruption and gestational diabetes mellitus); (2) maternal age between 18 and 40 years; (3) singleton pregnancy; (4) informed consent obtained from the mothers; (5) preterm birth (gestational age < 37 weeks); and (6) informed consent obtained from family members.

The exclusion criteria were (1) maternal history of chronic diseases (e.g., hypertension, diabetes mellitus, autoimmune diseases); (2) maternal use of antibiotics or probiotics other than routine Bifidobacterium supplementation within the past 3 months; (3) maternal history of intestinal disorders (e.g., inflammatory bowel disease, irritable bowel syndrome); and (4) neonates with congenital malformations or severe genetic metabolic disorders.

Gut microbiota analysis

DNA extraction, quantification, and amplification

Fecal microbial genomic DNA was extracted using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA quality was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis conducted at 5 V/cm for 20 min, with concentration and purity determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of bacterial 16S rRNA genes were amplified using specific primers: 338F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806 R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). Paired-end libraries were constructed and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform (Illumina, Inc.) following standard protocols.

Bioinformatics analysis of sequencing data

Raw sequencing reads were initially demultiplexed using the QIIME2 demux plugin, followed by quality control, trimming, denoising, paired-read merging, and chimera removal via the QIIME2 dada2 plugin to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Taxonomic classification was performed against the V3–V4 region of the Greengenes 13_8 database (99% similarity threshold) trimmed to match the 338F/806 R primer regions using the QIIME2 feature-classifier plugin with a 70% confidence threshold. Finally, contaminant mitochondrial and chloroplast sequences were systematically removed using the QIIME2 feature-table plugin to obtain the final amplified feature sequences.

Alpha diversity analysis

Alpha diversity refers to the diversity of biological communities within a specific region or ecosystem and serves as a comprehensive indicator reflecting both species evenness and richness. Commonly used indices include observed operational taxonomic units (OTUs), Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson, where higher index values indicate greater richness and complexity of the microbial communities present in the samples.

Beta diversity analysis

Beta diversity analysis compared microbial communities using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity (abundance-based), weighted UniFrac (phylogenetic and abundance-weighted), and unweighted UniFrac (presence/absence only) distances. Smaller values indicate higher similarity between samples. PERMANOVA was applied to all distance metrics to assess significant structural differences among groups.

Rarefaction curve analysis

Rarefaction curves were constructed by randomly subsampling sequencing data from all samples at varying sequencing depths and calculating corresponding alpha diversity indices. These curves plot species diversity indices against sequencing depth to evaluate the adequacy of sequencing volume and indirectly reflect species richness within samples. Curve stabilization (plateau phase) indicates that the sequencing depth sufficiently captures the biodiversity information present in the samples.

Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis

The linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) method was employed to identify microbial taxa whose abundance differed significantly among groups, with the goal of identifying characteristic microorganisms for each group, defined as those whose LDA scores exceeded a predefined threshold, which represented taxa whose relative abundance was significantly greater in a specific group than in other groups. In this study, the significance thresholds were set at an absolute LDA score greater than 2 and a Benjamini–Hochberg FDR-corrected q value < 0.05 for differential microbial feature selection.

Differential abundance analysis (DESeq2)

The DESeq2 method was employed to perform multiple comparisons between groups to identify microorganisms exhibiting statistically significant differences between each pair of groups, with statistical significance determined by a Benjamini–Hochberg corrected q value < 0.05.

Functional analysis

PICRUSt2 leverages 16S rRNA sequences from sequenced bacterial genomes to infer the gene functional profiles of their common ancestors, predicts functional profiles for unsequenced taxa in the Greengenes database, and constructs a comprehensive lineage-wide functional prediction profile for Archaea and Bacteria. By mapping the obtained microbial composition to this database, it predicts metabolic functions of the microbiota. Following KEGG functional annotation, the Dunn test was applied to evaluate significant differences in predicted microbial community functions between groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 and bioinformatics tools. Normally distributed continuous data are expressed as the mean ± SD and were compared by ANOVA, while nonnormally distributed data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as percentages and were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Alpha diversity indices were compared with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and differential abundance analysis at the genus and species levels was conducted using DESeq2 and LEfSe, with significance defined as a Benjamini–Hochberg FDR-corrected q value < 0.05. Beta diversity based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, weighted/unweighted UniFrac distances UniFrac distances was assessed by PERMANOVA, with post hoc power analysis performed via Monte Carlo simulation (999 permutations) to estimate the statistical power at the observed effect size and sample size. KEGG pathway abundances predicted by PICRUSt2 were first screened by the Kruskal–Wallis test (p < 0.05), followed by Dunn’s post hoc test with FDR correction.

Results

General baseline characteristics of participants

This study included 56 participants, comprising 38 pregnant women with PE who were divided into two groups according to clinical management: 19 who received prenatal Bifidobacterium supplementation (Group B) and 19 who did not receive supplementation (Group C), along with 18 healthy pregnant women who did not receive supplementation and served as controls (Group A). Participants in the supplemented group received Bifidobacterium triple viable capsules (Bifico®) as part of standard care, containing Bifidobacterium longum, Lactobacillus acidophilus, and Enterococcus faecalis (≥1.0 × 107 CFU per 420 mg dose, twice daily). Supplementation was initiated upon PE diagnosis (median 28 gestational weeks, IQR 26–30) and continued until delivery, with adherence verified through pharmacy records (86% of participants consumed ≥80% of prescribed doses). During follow-up, four participants were lost from each PE group (Groups B and C), and three were lost from the healthy control group. Maternal demographic characteristics (age, education level, occupation), prepregnancy status (weight, height, history of chronic disease, abortion, and cesarean section), and lifestyle factors (dietary habits, smoking/alcohol use) were collected via questionnaires and medical records (Table 1). LEfSe analysis of neonates’ first fecal samples revealed significant differences in taxonomy: Group A presented elevated Clostridium abundance at the class and order levels along with increased Bacteroides abundance across all taxonomic levels. Functional analysis of the gut microbiota

Functional analysis of the gut microbiota across groups revealed the top 20 most abundant annotated functions in neonatal fecal samples, as shown in in Fig. 4b. Among the 357 KEGG pathways analyzed, the results of the Kruskal–Wallis test revealed 53 pathways with significant overall group differences (p < 0.05), 23 of which were significantly different in at least two groups according to Dunn’s test (q < 0.05). Notably, Dunn’s post hoc analysis revealed significant differences in two metabolic pathways among preterm infant groups: LPS biosynthesis abundance was higher in Group C than in Group A (q = 0.001) and Group B (q = 0.018), with Group B lower than Group C but higher than Group A (q = 0.001). Conversely, glycolytic pathway abundance was higher in Group A than in Group C (q = 0.003) and Group B (q = 0.047), and higher in Group B than in Group C (q = 0.022). Box plots show abundance distributions with significances, all Benjamini-Hochberg corrected, as shown in Fig. 4c.

Discussion

In this study, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was used to characterize the initial postnatal fecal microbiota in preterm infants across three clinically defined groups: those born to mothers with PE who received Bifidobacterium supplementation (as part of routine care), those born to mothers with PE without supplementation, and those born to healthy controls without supplementation. Comparative analysis revealed variations in microbial diversity and community structure across the groups, suggesting potential associations between maternal health status and early gut microbiota establishment.

At the phylum level, comparative analysis revealed distinct microbial profiles among the groups: compared with the other groups, preterm infants born to mothers with PE presented a greater relative abundance of Proteobacteria, whereas those born to healthy mothers presented a greater predominance of Bacteroidetes. In the group supplemented with maternal Bifidobacterium, we observed a comparatively increased abundance of Actinobacteria. The existing evidence indicates that low Actinobacteria levels are associated with immune dysfunction and developmental delays [23, 24], potentially leading to long-term health consequences [25, 26]. Proteobacteria enrichment is correlated with inflammation, dysbiosis, and infection susceptibility [27–30], possibly through TLR-4 activation, which increases enterocyte apoptosis, impairs mucosal healing, elevates proinflammatory cytokines, and reduces mesenteric blood flow, contributing to intestinal ischemia and necrosis [31]. Notably, elevated Proteobacteria abundance has been reported in mothers with PE [32] and in growth-restricted piglets [33]. In contrast, Bacteroidetes play crucial roles in immuno-metabolic processes through anti-inflammatory metabolites (e.g., short-chain fatty acids), maintaining intestinal barrier integrity and immune homeostasis [34–36].

On the basis of the potential for maternal–fetal microbial transmission, we hypothesize that maternal PE may be vertically transmitted to offspring, resulting in an increased Proteobacteria/Bacteroidetes ratio in the neonatal gut microbiota, which could exacerbate adverse health outcomes such as preterm birth through TLR-4-mediated inflammatory mechanisms, whereas antenatal supplementation with Bifidobacterium may restore the microbial structure by promoting Actinobacteria colonization, thereby modulating offspring immune and metabolic development.

At the genus level, compared with the infants of non-PE mothers, preterm infants born to mothers with PE presented a significantly greater relative abundance of Streptococcus in the meconium microbiota, while those born to non-PE mothers presented increased Bacteroides abundance, and preterm infants from Bifidobacterium-supplemented mothers with PE presented higher Rothia abundance. Interestingly, compared with the other groups, the non-PE group also presented significantly increased relative abundances of the Clostridia class and Clostridiales order, which may be related to metabolic alterations. Studies have shown that sodium butyrate alleviates PE in pregnant rats by modulating the gut microbiota and short-chain fatty acid production, where butyrate is primarily generated by Clostridiales and Rothia through dietary fiber fermentation, which is crucial for intestinal barrier function, immunity, and metabolic health [37].

High levels of Streptococcus correlate positively with elevated total cholesterol [38], a biomarker and potential contributor to the pathogenesis of PE. Although the precise mechanisms remain unclear [39–41], we speculate that maternal PE may promote Streptococcus enrichment in offspring through cholesterol metabolic pathways, whereas Bifidobacterium intervention potentially increases butyrate production by facilitating the colonization of Rothia and Clostridiales, thereby modulating gut barrier integrity and immunometabolic functions via short-chain fatty acid-mediated mechanisms. However, contrasting findings report Fusobacterium (which is known to disrupt the intestinal epithelium and promote bacterial translocation) enrichment in PE placentas [42], possibly indicating that additional factors beyond the maternal/placental microbiota influence preterm gut colonization, warranting further investigation.

Consistent with previous reports of reduced microbial diversity associated with PE [43], comparative analysis indicated higher alpha diversity in the meconium microbiota of infants born to non-PE mothers. Notably, although antenatal Bifidobacterium supplementation promoted the enrichment of beneficial taxa such as Actinobacteria, the α-diversity remained lower in the supplemented group than in the unsupplemented group, suggesting that the protective effects of Bifidobacterium may stem primarily from modulating specific functional taxa rather than comprehensively reconstructing the microbial ecosystem. This discrepancy may also be associated with clinical factors such as supplementation timing, dosage regimens, or strain specificity; these issues warrant further investigation.

Functional prediction analysis of the gut microbiota revealed significant activation of LPS biosynthesis pathways and impaired glycolytic activity in the PE nonintervention group, which is consistent with the observed enrichment of Proteobacteria and proinflammatory state in this cohort [28]. Although antenatal Bifidobacterium intervention partially reversed these functional abnormalities by significantly reducing LPS synthesis and enhancing glycolytic activity, it failed to fully restore LPS levels to those of the healthy control group, indicating that the protective mechanisms likely involve modulation of key metabolic pathways rather than comprehensive functional restoration, highlighting the need for optimized intervention strategies to more effectively mitigate endotoxin risk in future studies.

The findings of this study underscore the significant impact of maternal PE on early gut microbiota colonization in preterm infants. While our research provides valuable insights into neonatal gut microbial communities, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, as an exploratory investigation facing practical challenges in the frequent sampling of preterm infants (particularly very-low-birth-weight infants) in the NICU setting, this study adopted a sample size of 15 per group, in line with those of comparable microbiome studies [20]. Although a formal a priori power analysis was not conducted, post hoc power analysis indicated that this sample size did not meet optimal statistical power requirements. Second, while the neonatal gut microbiota undergoes rapid, dynamic changes, this study relied solely on initial single-timepoint meconium samples and lacked longitudinal tracking data, thus limiting inferences regarding microbial developmental trajectories. Furthermore, because the study did not include systematically collected maternal prenatal dietary data in clinical records, potential dietary influences on intergroup microbial variations cannot be excluded. Future studies should use larger sample sizes, implement multi-timepoint sampling designs to track microbial dynamics, systematically collect maternal–infant dietary and medication data to enable correction for potential confounders, and integrate metagenomic sequencing with metabolomic approaches to elucidate microbiota‒host interactions.

Collectively, our findings demonstrate distinct gut microbial alterations in PE-associated preterm infants and partial restoration through antenatal Bifidobacterium supplementation, highlighting the need for awareness and therapeutic strategies to mitigate the adverse neonatal impacts of PE through early intervention and microbiota-based management.
