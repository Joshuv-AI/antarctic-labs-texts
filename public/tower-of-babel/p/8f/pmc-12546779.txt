Elevated LL-37/FPR2 Axis and its Regulatory Role for Gingival Fibroblasts in Periodontitis
Authors: Tingting Lin, Zhurong Cui, Yue Shen, Ruhan Yang, Weijun Yu, Shucheng Hu, Yuanjie Shi, Bin Jiang, Min Jin, Eryi Lu, Yuting Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and aimsLL-37, an antimicrobial peptide with dual pro-/anti-inflammatory roles, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of periodontitis, yet its specific action through its receptor Formyl peptide receptor 2 (FPR2) remains unclear. This study aimed to elucidate the expression profile of the LL-37/FPR2 axis in periodontitis and its regulatory effects on gingival fibroblasts (GFs).MethodsFifty-seven periodontitis patients and fifty-seven healthy controls were included to assess the levels of LL-37 and FPR2 in gingival tissues, gingival crevicular fluid and GFs, as well as to assess their correlation with disease progression. Primary human GFs were isolated and subsequently stimulated with Porphyromonas gingivalis lipopolysaccharide alongside varying concentrations of LL-37. To elucidate the functional role of FPR2 in this system, targeted gene silencing was performed using small interfering RNA.ResultsBoth LL-37 and FPR2 were upregulated in gingival tissues or gingival crevicular fluid from periodontitis patients, showing a significant positive correlation. FPR2 expression increased in GFs under periodontal inflammatory conditions, and high concentration of LL-37 exacerbated pro-inflammatory response in GFs via FPR2. Clinically, elevated LL-37 and FPR2 correlated with disease severity, particularly in patients with higher LL-37 expression. Patients with co-upregulation of both LL-37 and FPR2 exhibited more severe periodontitis.ConclusionsThe LL-37/FPR2 axis was co-upregulated in periodontitis-affected gingival tissues and correlated with disease severity. The LL-37/FPR2 axis exacerbated pro-inflammatory response in GFs under periodontal inflammatory conditions, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target for mitigating periodontal inflammation.Clinical RelevanceWe found that the co-upregulation of LL-37/FPR2 in periodontitis-affected gingival tissues, positively correlates with clinical parameters (PD/CAL). These findings provide new insights into the dual role of LL-37 in periodontal inflammation and suggest novel strategies for targeted therapy by selectively inhibiting high-concentration LL-37/FPR2 signaling.Keywords: Periodontitis, LL-37, FPR2, Gingival fibroblasts, Pro-inflammatory response

Introduction

Periodontitis, the sixth most prevalent global disease affecting 50-90% of the population, is characterized by progressive destruction of periodontal tissues, causing gingival recession, periodontal pockets formation, alveolar bone loss, tooth mobility, and eventual tooth loss.1,2 Its pathogenesis involves complex interactions between specific pathogens and host immune responses, leading to irreversible periodontal damage.3 Therefore, it is imperative to elucidate the underlying immune mechanisms to enhance therapeutic approaches for this condition.

Gingival fibroblasts (GFs), as the predominant cellular constituents of gingival connective tissue, serve dual roles as both structural components and active participants in immune regulation during periodontitis pathogenesis.4, 5, 6 Positioned at the oral mucosal interface, GFs serve as the first cellular defense against bacterial invasion. In response to pathogenic microbes or damage-associated signals, GFs mount a coordinated immune response by producing pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α) and chemokines, which simultaneously recruit immune cells and amplify pro-inflammatory cascades.4,6, 7, 8 Through dynamic intercellular communication with immune cells, GFs orchestrate a sophisticated regulatory network that modulates periodontal tissue destruction and repair. Targeted regulation of GF-mediated immune responses emerges as a promising therapeutic approach for managing periodontal inflammation.

LL-37, the sole human cathelicidin antimicrobial peptide, is mainly produced by phagocytic leukocytes and epithelial cells. It mediates diverse biological responses, especially exhibiting broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity and immunomodulatory functions. A growing body of evidence highlights the pivotal role of LL-37 in the pathogenesis of periodontitis.9,10 Elevated levels of LL-37 have been observed in gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) and gingival tissue,11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 where it exerts antimicrobial, immunomodulatory, and tissue regenerative effects.9,10 Notably, LL-37 appears to function as a “double-edged sword” in periodontal immunomodulation. LL-37 mitigates periodontal inflammation by inhibiting pro-inflammatory factors or promoting anti-inflammatory factor secretion.17, 18, 19 Conversely, LL-37 also acts as a pro-inflammatory mediator that amplifies innate immune response in periodontal tissues.20,21 However, the mechanisms underlying these contradictory effects remain unresolved.

Formyl peptide receptor 2 (FPR2), a G protein-coupled receptor, has been identified as a functional receptor for LL-37, mediating pro-inflammatory effects.22 Upon activation by LL-37, FPR2 enhances both innate and adaptive immune response by recruiting neutrophils, monocytes, and T cells, and stimulating the production of pro-inflammatory mediator Leukotriene B4.22, 23, 24 Furthermore, the LL-37/FPR2 signaling axis promotes neutrophil extracellular trap formation and exacerbates microglial activation, thereby contributing to diabetic retinopathy and experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis progression.25,26 These studies suggest a potential role for the LL-37/FPR2 axis in periodontal inflammation. Additionally, FPR2 can respond to various ligands under different pathological conditions, such as serum amyloid A, amyloid beta, Annexin A1, and Resolvin D1.27, 28, 29 Serum amyloid A induces pro-inflammatory effects through FPR2,30, 31, 32, 33 whereas Annexin A1 and Resolvin D1 exert anti-inflammatory effects,34, 35, 36 highlighting the ligand-dependent dual role of FPR2 in modulating inflammation. In the context of periodontitis, a recent study revealed that Annexin A1 attenuated periodontal inflammation through FPR2.37 However, it remains unclear whether LL-37-mediated FPR2 activation contributes to periodontitis pathogenesis, specifically its cell-specific actions in human periodontal tissues such as human GFs.

In this study, we revealed significant upregulation of both LL-37 and FPR2 in gingival tissues affected by periodontitis, with their expression levels showing significant positive correlation. GFs from periodontitis patients demonstrated marked FPR2 overexpression, and LL-37 activated FPR2 at high concentration, which exacerbated pro-inflammatory response in GFs. The co-upregulation of LL-37/FPR2 exhibited a relationship with clinical parameters of periodontitis severity, further suggesting their synergistic contribution to disease progression.

Material and methods

Analysis of gene expression omnibus (GEO) dataset

The publicly available GEO dataset GSE1613438 was obtained from the National Center of Biotechnology Information for analyzing the expression of LL-37 and FPR2 in healthy and inflamed gingival tissues. The data were generated using the GPL570 platform, which measured genome-wide gene expression via microarray hybridization. For genes represented by multiple probes, expression values were averaged to ensure robust quantification.

Study design and participants

This study was conducted in the Department of Stomatology, Renji Hospital, School of Medicine, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for cross-sectional studies. Prior to subject enrollment, approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (Approval No. LY2024-069-B). The study adhered to the principles of the Helsinki Declaration, and written informed consent was acquired from all participants.

Patients with periodontitis who underwent periodontal flap surgery and healthy individuals with impacted teeth intended for extraction were enrolled in our study. For the analysis of LL-37 and FPR2 mRNA expression in gingival tissues via real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‑qPCR) and the measurement of LL‑37 concentration in GCF using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), a sample size of 30 per group was determined based on power calculations from preliminary experiments. Due to the limited amount of gingival tissue obtained from each biopsy, the remaining material was insufficient for additional protein-level assays, histological examinations, or the isolation of GFs. Therefore, an additional 6 pairs of periodontitis patients and healthy controls were enrolled for Western blot, and another 6 pairs were recruited for immunohistochemistry (IHC) analyses of LL‑37 and FPR2 protein expression. Furthermore, primary GFs were isolated from 15 additional periodontitis patients and 15 healthy controls.

Based on the classification of periodontal status (2017),39,40 inclusion criteria for the periodontitis group were as follows: (1) diagnosed with stage II-IV periodontitis, whose maximum probing depth (PD) ≥ 5 mm, clinical attachment loss (CAL) ≥ 3 mm at the most severe site, bone loss ≥ 15% in radiographic examinations; (2) needed periodontal flap surgery in the mandibular posterior region; (3) age range of 18-65 years; (4) acquisition of signed consent. Inclusion criteria for the periodontal healthy groups were as follows: (1) PD ≤ 3 mm, bleeding on probing (BOP) < 10% and no CAL, no history of periodontitis and no history of pericoronitis; (2) age range of 18-65 years; (3) acquisition of signed consent. Subjects were excluded with (1) history of smoking or substance abuse; (2) accompanied by systemic diseases related to periodontal lesions (e.g., diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, systemic lupus erythematosus, and chronic renal diseases); (3) taking any medication which could affect periodontal health conditions over the past quarter; (4) menstruation, pregnancy, lactation, or menopause. After screening, 57 patients with periodontitis and 57 healthy individuals were included in the final analysis as the flow diagram presented (Figure S1). All participants responded during the entire study period.

Sample collection

The demographic information was obtained by interviewers, and the clinical characteristics were collected by a specialist of periodontology (Table S1). The clinical parameters, including PD and CAL, were performed at 6 sites (mesial-buccal, mid-buccal, distal-buccal, mesial-lingual, mid-lingual, and distal-lingual) and the deepest pocket site was chosen for data and sample collection.

Inflamed gingival tissues were obtained from 57 periodontitis patients during periodontal flap surgery, while healthy gingival tissues were collected from 57 healthy individuals during impacted tooth extraction procedures. A total of 36 pairs of gingival tissue samples were stored at -80°C; among these, 30 pairs were allocated for RNA extraction and 6 pairs for protein extraction. An additional 6 pairs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological analysis, and another 15 pairs were processed immediately for the isolation of primary GFs. Besides, 30 pairs of GCF samples were collected from the same 30 periodontitis patients and 30 healthy individuals whose gingival tissues had been used for mRNA analysis. The GCF sampling was performed 1 day after the periodontal examination, from the buccal interproximal surfaces of single-rooted teeth. Prior to collection, supragingival plaque was carefully removed using sterile curettes. GCF was absorbed using sterile paper points (02taper, 25#, Meta Dental Co., Seoul, Korea), which gently inserted approximately 1 mm into the gingival crevice and left in situ for 30 seconds to allow saturation while minimizing tissue trauma. For each participant, 6-8 saturated paper points were pooled, and any visibly blood-contaminated point was discarded. Samples were stored at –80°C for further analysis.

Primary human GFs isolation and culture

Primary human healthy and inflamed GFs were isolated from healthy and inflamed gingival tissues, respectively, as described previously.41 Briefly, gingival tissues were rinsed by PBS and the epithelium was removed. The remaining tissue was minced into 1 mm3 fragments and distributed in a 6-well culture plate. Tissue fragments were secured under sterile glass coverslips and cultured in low glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (Gibco). Cell growth was monitored by an inverted microscope, with minimal disturbance during the initial 72-hour period. Upon reaching approximately 70% confluence, cells were passaged. Cell identity was confirmed by positive staining for vimentin and negative staining for cytokeratin. The GFs were used for experiments between passages 3 to 5.

Small interfering RNA (siRNA)

Human siNC and siFPR2 (siFPR2-1: 5’-ATGAAGAAGTGTCCTATGAGTCT-3’, siFPR2-2: 5’-TCCTGTGTAAGTTAATTCACATC-3’, siFPR2-3: 5’-CACAAAAAGGGCATGATTAAATC-3’) were synthesized by Haixing Biosciences (Jiangsu, China). Primary human GFs were seeded in 6-well plates (Corning, New York, USA) at 2.5 × 10⁵ cells/well in complete DMEM. Upon reaching 70% confluence, these cells were transfected with siNC and siFPR2 using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Specifically, transfection complexes were prepared by separately diluting 5 μL of Lipofectamine 3000 and 50 nM siRNA in 125 μL of Opti-MEM I Reduced Serum Medium (Gibco, Cat# 31985070). The diluted siRNA and Lipofectamine 3000 were combined at a 1:1 ratio, incubated for 15 minutes at room temperature, and added dropwise to the cells cultured in antibiotic-free complete DMEM. After 6 h incubation, medium was replaced with fresh complete DMEM, and cells were harvested 48 hours post-transfection for further analysis. Based on knockdown efficiency (>75%; Fig. 3C), siFPR2-1 was selected for subsequent functional experiments.

RT-qPCR

TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) was used for RNA extraction. The extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio, Otsu, Shiga, Japan). RT-qPCR was performed with FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master mix (Roche, Nutley, NJ, USA) on a PCR thermocycler (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH, and relative values were calculated using the 2^(-ΔCT) method. The primers used were synthesized by Sangon Biotech. The sequences of the primers used were shown in Table S2.

ELISA

The GCF samples were eluted from paper points by incubation in 20 μL of a 100 mM Tris alkaline extraction solution (pH 9.85) per paper point. The mixture was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 minutes to separate the eluate. LL-37 concentrations were quantified using the commercially available ELISA kit (HK321, HycultBiotech, Uden, Netherlands), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted using RIPA Lysis Buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) with 1% PMSF. Protein concentrations were measured using the Bicinchoninic Acid Protein Assay Kit (EpiZyme, Shanghai, China). Protein samples were diluted, denatured, loaded onto sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gels, and then transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and incubated overnight with primary antibodies against FPR2 (sc-57141, Santa Cruz, Delaware, CA, USA) and β-actin (4970, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). After washing with PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20, the membranes were treated with the secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology) and visualized using Chemiluminescence Reagents (EpiZyme). Blot quantification was performed using ImageJ software (X64, version 2.1.4).

Histological analysis

Gingival tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin-embedded 4-μm sections were cut, and undergone hematoxylin-eosin staining after deparaffinization and rehydration. IHC was performed to analyze LL-37 and FPR2 expression in paraffin-embedded gingival tissues sections. The specimens were deparaffinized and rehydrated in graded alcohol series. After blocking with 5% BSA, sections were incubated with the following primary antibodies: LL-37 (ab69484, Abcam) and FPR2 (NLS1878, Novus Biologicals), followed by secondary antibodies, and counterstained with hematoxylin. All slides were scanned using a Pannoramic MIDI II scanner (3DHISTECH) and imaged with a Nikon microscope (Model 2084). Positivity rate across all cells were calculated by automated image analysis using ImageJ (v2.1.4). For each gingival tissue section, the mean value was calculated from 6 randomly selected fields. Thresholding was applied to separate positive staining, and the positivity rate was calculated as (number of FPR2/LL-37-positive nuclei / total nuclei) × 100%.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC)

mIHC staining was performed to analyze the Vimentin and FPR2 expression in paraffin-embedded gingival tissues sections and the Vimentin, Cytokeratin and FPR2 in primary GFs. The process was performed on the following antibodies and fluorescent dyes in the flowing order: Vimentin (ab92547, Abcam)/HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (GB122053, Servicebio, Wuhan, China)/iF555-Tyramide (G1233, Servicebio), Cytokeratin (GB122053, Servicebio)/HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (GB27301, Servicebio)/iF647-Tyramide (G1232, Servicebio), FPR2 (NLS1878, Novus Biologicals)/Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (GB25303, Servicebio), according to manufacturer’s instruction. All slides were scanned using a Pannoramic MIDI II scanner (3DHISTECH) and imaged with a Nikon microscope (Model 2084).

Calcium mobilization assay

Calcium mobilization assay was performed using a Fluo-4 AM Calcium Assay Kit (Beyotime, Cat# S1061S). Human GFs were loaded with the kit’s Fluo-4 AM working solution (prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions) and incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes in the dark. After dye loading, the cells were washed twice with the provided buffer and then incubated for another 20 minutes to complete dye de-esterification. Real-time fluorescence (excitation/emission = 490/525 nm) was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader. After recording a 60-second baseline, cells were stimulated with LL-37, and fluorescence was recorded for 5 minutes. Responses were calculated as ΔF/F₀, where F₀ is the average baseline fluorescence.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 and are presented as mean ± SEM. For comparisons between 2 groups, the Mann-Whitney U test was employed, and 1-way ANOVA with Dunn's multiple comparisons test was used for multi-group analyses. The diagnostic potential of LL-37 and FPR2 in indicating disease status was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis by GraphPad Prism 9.0. Specifically, the cut-off value was calculated based on the maximum Youden’s index (Youden's index = sensitivity + specificity - 1) derived from the ROC analysis. Associations between variables were assessed via Spearman’s rank correlation test. Significance levels were denoted as ns, no significance, *P < .05, ⁎⁎P < .01 and ⁎⁎⁎P < .001.

Results

LL-37 and FPR2 are upregulated in gingival tissues of periodontitis patients

The GSE16134 dataset,38 comprising gene expression profiles of 241 inflamed gingival tissues from periodontitis patients and 69 healthy controls, was obtained to evaluate LL-37 and FPR2 expression levels. Transcriptomic analysis revealed significantly elevated levels of both LL-37 and FPR2 in periodontitis-affected gingival tissues compared to healthy controls (Figure 1A). This upregulation of LL-37 in periodontitis patients was further confirmed by RT-qPCR of gingival tissues and ELISA of GCF (Figure 1B-C). Similarly, the increased expression of FPR2 was confirmed by RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis (Figure 1D-E). Moreover, IHC staining corroborated these findings, and revealed that the upregulation of LL-37 and FPR2 predominantly occurred in the gingival connective tissues (Figure 1F). Given that LL-37 serves as a key endogenous ligand for FPR2, Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed to assess their relationship. The results revealed a moderate correlation (r = 0.5155, P = .004) between FPR2 and LL-37 mRNA expression and a stronger correlation (r = 0.6423, P < .001) between FPR2 mRNA and LL-37 protein levels (Figure 1G). Collectively, these results suggested that LL-37 and FPR2 were upregulated in periodontitis-affected gingival tissues, with a significant correlation between their expression.

FPR2 is upregulated in GFs from periodontitis patients

To elucidate the role of LL-37-mediated FPR2 activation in periodontitis-associated inflammation, we first sought to identify FPR2-expressing cell populations within gingival connective tissues. Given the established role of GFs as pivotal effector cells in periodontal inflammation, known to actively participate in disease progression through the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and matrix-degrading enzymes, we focused our investigation on this cell population. mIHC assays revealed both a significantly higher proportion of FPR2+Vimentin+ fibroblasts and increased FPR2 expression levels in these cells within inflamed gingival tissues, as demonstrated by robust colocalization of vimentin and FPR2 (Figure 2A). We subsequently isolated primary GFs from periodontitis patients and healthy controls (Figure 2B), with cell identity confirmed by positive vimentin staining and absence of cytokeratin expression (Figure 2C). Notably, mIHC analysis showed substantially enhanced FPR2 expression in periodontitis-derived GFs compared to healthy controls (Figure 2C). These observations were further validated at both mRNA and protein levels, with RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses consistently demonstrating elevated FPR2 expression in disease-state GFs (Figure 2D-E). Thus, these results underscored that FPR2 was upregulated in GFs from periodontitis patients.

LL-37 activates FPR2 to enhance inflammatory response in GFs

To investigate whether LL-37-mediated FPR2 activation participates in inflammatory response in GFs, we stimulated healthy GFs with Porphyromonas gingivalis lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to mimic periodontitis-associated inflammation, followed by treatment with LL-37 at varying concentrations (2, 4, and 8 µM). Intriguingly, low-dose LL-37 (2 µM) suppressed LPS-induced pro-inflammatory cytokines, IL-1β, IL-8, and CCL2, whereas higher concentrations (4 and 8 µM) potentiated their expression (Figure 3A-B). As previous reported, LL-37 can activate FPR2 to induce calcium mobilization and exert pro-inflammatory effects.22 To delineate FPR2’s role, we silenced FPR2 using siRNA (Figure 3C). A low concentration of LL-37 (2 µM) induced a calcium response in LPS-stimulated GFs that was unaffected by FPR2 knockdown (Figure 3D-E). Correspondingly, FPR2 silencing did not alter the anti-inflammatory effect of 2 µM LL-37 on LPS-induced cytokine expression (Figure 3F). In contrast, a high concentration of LL-37 (4 µM) triggered a robust calcium influx, which was partially abolished by FPR2 knockdown (Figure 3G-H). Furthermore, FPR2 silencing partially abrogated the pro-inflammatory effects of 4 µM LL-37 (Figure 3I). Further validation in periodontitis-derived GFs confirmed that FPR2 inhibition via siRNA significantly attenuated pro-inflammatory cytokine production (Figure 3J-K). These data demonstrated that higher concentration of LL-37 activated FPR2, which exacerbated inflammatory response in GFs in periodontitis.

Combined analysis of LL-37 and FPR2 reveals positive correlation with periodontitis clinical parameters

We examined ROC curves to evaluate the ability of LL-37 and FPR2 mRNA expression levels to indicate the status of periodontitis. As demonstrated in Figure 4A, the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated as 0.7089 (CI:0.5768-0.8410) for LL-37 (Figure 4A), 0.7950 (CI: 0.6837-0.9063) for FPR2 (Figure 4B), and 0.7567 (CI: 0.6371-0.8762) for the combination of LL-37 and FPR2 (Figure 4C), indicating that both LL-37 and FPR2 reflected the local periodontal status, with FPR2 showing superior discriminatory capacity.

We next investigated the correlation between LL-37, FPR2 and clinical parameters in periodontitis patients using Spearman’s correlation analysis. The LL-37 mRNA expression levels showed significant positive correlations with PD (r = 0.6608, P < .001) and CAL (r = 0.5977, P < .001) (Figure 5A). Similarly, the FPR2 mRNA expression levels demonstrated even stronger positive correlations with PD (r = 0.5946, P < .001) and CAL (r = 0.6435, P < .001) (Figure 5B). Based on previous findings that higher concentration of LL-37 activated FPR2, exacerbating inflammatory response, we stratified periodontitis patients into 2 groups (LL-37low and LL-37high) using the optimal cutoff value (0.012296) determined by ROC curve analysis of LL-37 mRNA expression levels (Figure 5C). As expected, the LL-37high group exhibited significantly greater PD and CAL compared to the LL-37low group (Figure 5D), along with elevated FPR2 expression (Figure 5E). Further subgroup analysis revealed that in LL-37low groups, neither LL-37 nor FPR2 expression correlated with PD or CAL (Figure 5F). Intriguingly, in the LL-37high group, LL-37 and FPR2 expression maintained positive associations with both PD and CAL (Figure 5G). Periodontitis patients were further classified into 4 subgroups based on LL-37 and FPR2 mRNA expression levels, using optimal cutoffs derived from ROC analysis (LL-37: 0.012296; FPR2: 0.4788). The subgroups were defined as follows: LL-37lowFPR2low, LL-37lowFPR2high, LL-37highFPR2low and LL-37highFPR2high. The LL-37highFPR2high subgroup exhibited the most severe periodontal destruction, demonstrating significantly greater PD and CAL values compared to the LL-37lowFPR2low subgroups. Although the differences did not reach statistical significance, a strong increasing trend was also observed in the LL-37highFPR2high subgroup relative to the LL-37lowFPR2high and LL-37highFPR2low subgroups (Figure 5H). These findings suggested that the combined effect of high expression of LL-37 and FPR2 was closely linked to the severity of periodontitis, as reflected by clinical parameters.

Discussion

LL-37 has been implicated in periodontitis pathogenesis,9,10 yet its specific action through FPR2 in human GFs during periodontitis remains unclear. In this study, we demonstrate that LL-37 and FPR2 are significantly upregulated in periodontitis-affected gingival tissues, with their levels positively correlated. GFs from patients express higher FPR2, and high concentration of LL-37 activates FPR2, worsening inflammation. The LL-37/FPR2 co-upregulation correlates with clinical severity, suggesting their synergistic role in periodontitis progression (Figure 5I). Our study uncovers that the LL-37/FPR2 axis drives periodontal inflammation via a concentration-dependent mechanism in GFs, a cell type central to periodontal pathogenesis that had not previously been investigated in this context.

Our study demonstrated that LL-37 was significantly elevated in both gingival tissues and GCF of periodontitis patients, consistent with previous reports.11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 Regarding its receptor FPR2, a recent study revealed increased FPR2 expression in ligation-induced murine periodontitis;31 however, its expression pattern in periodontitis patients remained unclear. Here, we found that FPR2 was upregulated in gingival tissues of periodontitis patients, as documented in the database GSE16134 and confirmed in our clinical samples. A bioinformatics study of GSE173078 (12 healthy controls vs. 12 periodontitis patients) and GSE10334 (90 periodontitis patients) also substantiated the upregulation of FPR2.42 Notably, we observed a significant positive correlation between FPR2 and LL-37 expression levels. These findings suggest a coordinated upregulation of LL-37 and FPR2 in periodontitis-affected gingival tissues, prompting further investigation into their functional interplay in disease pathogenesis.

We next identified the pro-inflammatory role of the LL-37/FPR2 axis in GFs during periodontitis. Given that ligands typically activate their receptors to exert biological functions, we examined FPR2-expressing cells in periodontal tissues expressing FPR2. IHC analysis demonstrated pronounced FPR2 upregulation within the gingival connective tissue compartment. Given the established role of GFs as primary instigators and amplifiers of inflammatory cascades in periodontitis,4,6 we specifically analyzed FPR2 expression in these cells and revealed significantly enhanced FPR2 expression in GFs under periodontal inflammatory conditions. The LL-37/FPR2 axis exerts multiple pro-inflammatory effects on immune cells in diabetic retinopathy and experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis progression.22, 23, 24, 25, 26 Previous studies have demonstrated the role of FPR2 in modulating fibroblasts that FPR2 activation via different ligands regulated fibroblast proliferation and extracellular matrix production, thereby influencing fibrotic processes.43,44 Intriguingly, we found that a low concentration (2 µM) of LL-37 suppressed LPS-induced inflammatory responses in GFs, whereas a high concentration (≥4 µM) exacerbated them, consistent with prior studies.17,18,45,46 Notably, calcium mobilization assay indicated that high concentrations of LL-37 primarily activated FPR2. Further, FPR2 inhibition selectively abolished the pro-inflammatory effects of high-concentration LL-37 while preserving the anti-inflammatory activity at lower concentrations. Thus, we demonstrated a novel concentration-dependent role of the LL-37/FPR2 axis in which higher levels of LL-37 activate FPR2 to regulate GFs during periodontitis-related inflammation.

Prior studies have reported conflicting effects of LL-37 in GFs, showing both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory roles under different conditions,18,45 yet without elucidating the underlying receptor-specific mechanisms. Beyond its activation by LL-37, FPR2 can also respond to various ligands, such as serum amyloid A, Annexin A1 and Resolvin D1, which elicit opposing immune functions,27, 28, 29 underscoring the context-dependent role of this receptor in inflammatory regulation. Our study explained this paradox by establishing FPR2 as the central receptor responsible for the concentration-dependent duality of LL-37, directing a switch between inflammatory and protective responses. In the context of periodontitis, recent evidence revealed that Annexin A1 attenuated periodontal inflammation through FPR2.37,47 These findings suggest that the role of LL-37/FPR2 in periodontal pathogenesis may involve complex interactions among several other ligands, warranting further investigation to elucidate potential synergistic or antagonistic effects within the periodontal microenvironment.

Our study showed that LL-37 amplified pro-inflammatory cytokines, IL-1β, IL-8, and CCL2, via FPR2 in GFs under periodontal inflammation. Previous studies have demonstrated that activating FPR2 by serum amyloid A similarly induces IL-8, CCL2, and IL-1β in monocytes, neutrophils and keratinocytes.31, 32, 33 Among these cytokines, IL-1β is involved in the host immune response within periodontal tissues, while IL-8 and CCL2 recruit immune cells, including macrophages and neutrophils, to amplify the pro-inflammatory response in periodontal tissues.3,6 A bioinformatics study showed that FPR2 expression in gingival tissues of periodontitis was positively correlated with neutrophil infiltration,42 which may potentially resulted from FPR2’s regulation of IL-8 and CCL2 in GFs and warranted further investigation. Our IHC staining showed that LL-37/FPR2 expression was primarily upregulated in gingival connective tissues, which consist of fibroblasts, vascular endothelial cells and immune cells. We also observed the expression of LL-37 in epithelial cells and FPR2 in vascular endothelial cells. FPR2 can promote endothelial progenitor cell proliferation, migration, and lumen formation, thereby contributing to neovascularization.48 The expression and regulatory roles of FPR2 have been demonstrated in various immune cells, such as neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells, T cells, and so on.49,50 Thus, although we focused on the effects of the LL-37/FPR2 axis on GFs, its functions in other cell types might be worth investigation for a comprehensive understanding of their role in periodontitis.

Our clinical investigations further established that LL-37 and FPR2 co-upregulation correlated with clinical severity in periodontitis patients. Previous studies revealed a positive relationship between levels of GCF LL-37 and PD, CAL at the sampled sites.13,51 Our ROC curve and correlation analysis confirmed that both LL-37 and FPR2 expression in gingival tissues indicated the disease status and showed positive correlations with clinical indicators, PD and CAL. Based on our hypothesis that high concentration of LL-37 play a more significant pathogenic role, we stratified patients into LL-37low and LL-37high groups. The LL-37high group exhibited significantly greater PD and CAL values compared to the LL-37low group. Notably, the correlation analysis revealed stronger associations between LL-37 levels and clinical parameters in the LL-37high group. The results were consistent with previous findings in generalized aggressive periodontitis.15 Interestingly, patients with high LL-37 levels also exhibited elevated FPR2 expression. More importantly, significant correlations between FPR2 and disease severity were observed in the LL-37high group, with no such associations detected in the LL-37low group. Furthermore, patients exhibiting concurrent high expression of both LL-37 and FPR2 presented with the most severe clinical manifestations, showing significantly greater PD and CAL values or at least increasing trend compared to the LL-37lowFPR2low, LL-37lowFPR2high and LL-37highFPR2low subgroups. While these findings require validation in larger cohorts, these clinical findings supported our experimental data that high concentration of LL-37 activated FPR2 to exacerbate periodontal inflammation.

Further research is needed to refine the study. Large cohort studies are needed to confirm and extend current observations in clinical patients. Notably, our present study comprised an in vitro functional exploration. Given the complexity of the inflammatory milieu in periodontitis, future work employing an in vivo model is essential to strengthen the reliability and clinical relevance of our findings. Furthermore, we propose that high concentrations of LL-37 activate FPR2 to exert pro-inflammatory effects. Therefore, elucidating the dynamic changes in LL-37 concentration during periodontitis progression, as well as identifying its major cellular sources under disease conditions, will be crucial for further understanding the role of the LL-37/FPR2 axis in periodontitis and for developing targeted therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that LL-37 and FPR2 were elevated in gingival tissues from periodontitis patients, with a significant positive correlation. FPR2 was notably increased in GFs periodontal inflammatory conditions, and high concentration of LL-37 exacerbated pro-inflammatory response in GFs via FPR2. Accordingly, the co-upregulation of LL-37 and FPR2 was associated with clinical parameters indicative of periodontitis severity, suggesting their synergistic contribution to disease progression. Our findings provided valuable insights into the potential involvement of the LL-37/FPR2 axis in modulating GFs during periodontitis and highlighted its promise as a therapeutic target for mitigating periodontal inflammation.
