Unveiling the Uncommon: Gastrointestinal Mucormycosis Unmasked by Gastrointestinal Bleeding
Authors: Yash Kanani, Sanjana Bhagwat, Rutvij Tope, ChetanKumar Oswal, Chaitanya Kumbhar, Harshal Gadhikar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gastrointestinal mucormycosis is a rare but life-threatening invasive fungal infection carrying mortality rates approaching 85%. We report 3 cases of gastric mucormycosis managed at our institution. Two patients presented atypically—one with refractory seizures and another with severe respiratory distress—resulting in diagnostic delays and subsequent fatal outcomes despite antifungal therapy. A third patient, diagnosed promptly following early esophagogastroduodenoscopy and biopsy, achieved complete clinical recovery. Rapid disease progression demonstrated the aggressive nature of this condition, but early diagnosis greatly improved outcomes. These cases collectively highlight the critical importance of maintaining a high index of clinical suspicion, pursuing early endoscopic biopsy of atypical gastric ulcerations, and initiating timely antifungal therapy to optimize patient prognosis.

INTRODUCTION

Mucormycosis is a life-threatening invasive fungal infection caused by fungi of the order Mucorales. Although rhino-orbito-cerebral disease is the most common clinical presentation, cases of gastrointestinal (GI) mucormycosis have increased over the past few decades. GI involvement accounts for approximately 7%–13% of cases, with the stomach being the most frequently affected site (approximately 58%), followed by the small and large intestines (42%).1 Traditionally associated with immunocompromised states and premature infants, GI mucormycosis is increasingly being reported in immunocompetent individuals.2 Mortality rate is about 85% due to perforation and GI hemorrhage.3 Owing to its aggressive course, nonspecific presentation, and high mortality, diagnosis is frequently delayed, and many cases are recognized only at postmortem examination.3

We report a total of 3 cases managed at our institution—2 cases of isolated primary gastric mucormycosis and 1 case of gastric and colonic mucormycosis. Two of the cases presented atypically, resulting in diagnostic delays that ultimately contributed to fatal outcomes. The third case, in which the diagnosis was established early, enabled prompt initiation of therapy and was associated with patient survival.

A comprehensive review of the literature revealed a paucity of reported cases of primary gastric mucormycosis originating from a single institution. Our report is unique due to inclusion of both a successfully treated case, and cases characterized by atypical clinical presentations leading to delayed diagnosis and adverse outcomes. As such, this report emphasizes the critical role of early consideration, timely diagnosis, and early therapeutic intervention in determining patient prognosis.

CASE REPORT

Case 1

A 34-year-old woman with hypothyroidism was admitted with fever, altered sensorium and recurrent seizures refractory to multiple antiepileptic drugs. Thyroid function tests were unremarkable. Laboratory test results showed worsening of hypocalcemia over time, mild hypokalemia, hyperchloremia, and hyperphosphatemia. She was intubated for airway protection. Initial neuroimaging, electroencephalography (EEG) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis was unremarkable. She lost consciousness during in the intensive care unit. Laboratory tests, including polymerase chain reactions (PCR), blood and urine cultures, autoimmune encephalitis panel etc returned negative. However, a positive rickettsial PCR was obtained and appropriate antimicrobial therapy was initiated. Yet, her seizures persisted, necessitating plasma exchange therapy. After 3 cycles of plasma exchange, the patient developed hemodynamic instability accompanied by lower GI bleeding. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) revealed a large, irregular gastric ulcer with necrotic margins. Histopathological examination of biopsy specimens demonstrated broad, aseptate hyphae consistent with mucormycosis. Liposomal amphotericin B was promptly initiated; however, the patient's clinical condition deteriorated rapidly. She developed acute kidney injury and acute liver failure secondary to severe shock, even requiring 1 episode of cardiopulmonary resuscitation but subsequently died of multiorgan failure. She never gained consciousness during admission.

Case 2

A 53-year-old man with decompensated chronic liver disease presented with melena and a significant drop in hemoglobin. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed high-risk esophageal varices and a 1.5–2.0 cm gastric ulcer (Forrest class III) in the body of the stomach (Figure 1). Biopsy from the ulcer edge demonstrated broad, thick, aseptate fungal hyphae (Figure 1), consistent with mucormycosis. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) showed evidence of gastric wall thickening but no perforation.

(A) Histopathology slide (10×) with hematoxylin and eosin demonstrating fungal hyphae. (B) Gastric ulcer (Forrest class III) in the body of the stomach. (C) Healed ulcer after appropriate antifungal treatment.

Laryngoscopic examination showed no evidence of rhino-orbito-cerebral involvement. Liposomal amphotericin B was initiated but discontinued due to worsening renal function. The patient was subsequently treated with isavuconazole for 6 weeks, resulting in complete resolution of GI bleeding. Follow-up EGD demonstrated complete healing of the gastric ulcer (Figure 1) and CT showed resolution of previous wall thickening.

Case 3

A 44-year-old man with uncontrolled diabetes mellitus was diagnosed with severe acute respiratory distress syndrome, necessitating noninvasive ventilatory support. The patient was intubated and stabilized. One week later, he developed melena, a drop in hemoglobin levels, and coagulopathy.

(A) Esophagogastroduodenoscopy of case 3 showing a gastric ulcer suspected to be a stress ulcer. (B) Esophagogastroduodenoscopy 4 days later showed extensive ulcer expansion.

Histopathology slide (40×) with hematoxylin and eosin demonstrating broad aseptate ribbon-like hyphae in markedly necrotic gastric tissue.

EGD revealed a large, oozing gastric ulcer in the body (Figure 2A). Hemostasis was achieved but 4 days later the patient had recurrent melena. Repeat EGD and colonoscopy evaluation revealed extension of the gastric ulcer into the fundus (Figure 2B). Sigmoidoscopy showed extensive ulcers with overlying blood clots in the sigmoid colon. Biopsy confirmed angioinvasive Mucormycosis (Figure 3). The patient was initiated on Amphotericin B.

A subsequent abdominal CT demonstrated colonic perforation with peritonitis. Despite aggressive surgical and antifungal interventions, the patient died of refractory shock and multiorgan failure.

DISCUSSION

Mucormycosis is an opportunistic fungal infection acquired through direct tissue inoculation or inhalation of environmental spores. Rhino cerebral and pulmonary forms represent the most frequently encountered manifestations4; however, isolated primary gastrointestinal (GI) mucormycosis remains exceedingly rare, with only a limited number of cases documented in the literature. Given the inherent diagnostic challenges and the characteristically rapid disease progression, GI mucormycosis carries a grave prognosis, with mortality rates approaching 85%.3 Consequently, the diagnosis is established ante-mortem in only approximately 25% of cases.3

Recognized predisposing factors include diabetes mellitus, persistent neutropenia, hematologic malignancies, HIV infection, hepatic cirrhosis, and immunosuppressive therapy administered in the context of solid organ or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.1 Two of our patients were known to have risk factors (uncontrolled diabetes mellitus and decompensated chronic liver disease).

In our report, one patient presented with refractory seizures and another presented with severe respiratory distress—both presentations seemingly unrelated to the GI tract. Convulsions were possibly secondary to a combination of systemic inflammatory reaction syndrome and electrolyte derangements, which only worsened with renal failure. Owing to the atypical presentations, gastric mucormycosis was diagnosed after a delayed presentation of GI bleeding. Since the pharyngeal region is the primary site of mucor infection, it is possible that prolonged sepsis and/or trauma contributed to stress ulcers, which were colonized by inhaled pharyngeal fungal pathogens.5 Of both these atypical patients, despite the initiation of appropriate systemic antifungal therapy, neither one survived. By stark contrast, we also describe a case where prompt EGD and biopsy resulted in early initiation of treatment and complete clinical recovery. Therefore, our cases highlight the pivotal consequences of maintaining a high index of suspicion for mucormycosis, and pursuing early biopsy of atypical gastric ulcers. In the patients who did not survive, time from admission to histopathological diagnosis was 27 days and 14 days, respectively. For the patient who survived, the same timeframe was 13 days. We would like to propose a door-to-diagnosis interval as a potential predictor of prognosis. We can hypothesize that the lower the door-to-diagnosis interval, the more favorable the prognosis. However, larger studies would be needed to confirm our hypothesis.

On endoscopic examination, ulcers are the predominant manifestation, often large lesions with irregular, rolled, sharply demarcated necrotic margins, occasionally covered with black eschar and associated with adjacent vascular thrombosis—features that may closely resemble ischemic injury or malignancy.1 The rapidity of disease progression is illustrated in case 3, wherein marked ulcer extension was documented over a period of merely 4 days, highlighting the potentially fatal consequences of delayed intervention.

GI mucormycosis predisposes affected individuals to life-threatening complications, including massive GI hemorrhage, bowel perforation, localized ischemic infarction, and disseminated systemic infection. These complications were seen in our patients as well.6 Contrast CT scan may aid in ruling out bowel ischemia or perforation.

Owing to its rarity and atypical presentation, diagnosis of primary gastric mucormycosis is challenging. Atypical presentations include bacterial pneumonia, septic shock, mucormycosis secondary to abdominal injuries (blunt trauma, gunshot wounds etc), rib fractures etc.5 Nevertheless, as our cases demonstrate, early diagnosis and prompt therapeutic intervention are of paramount importance in improving patient outcomes. Further dedicated studies are warranted to develop more robust diagnostic and management frameworks for this condition.
