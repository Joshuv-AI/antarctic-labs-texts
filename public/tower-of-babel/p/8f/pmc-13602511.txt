Optimizing COVID-19 vaccination for older adults: superior efficacy of heterologous regimens in reducing infection and mortality rates among patients aged 80 years and older during the Omicron BA.5/BF.7 outbreak
Authors: Songlin Qiu, Yijun Chen, Awei Sheng, Bo Shen, Sailing He, Yufen Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFollowing the adjustment of COVID-19 control policies in mainland China, particularly during the Omicron BA.5 and BF.7 waves, evaluating vaccine effectiveness in individuals aged ≥ 80 years is of critical importance.Patients and methodsThis retrospective study included 2,325 hospitalized patients aged 80 years and older from four hospitals between December 9, 2022, and January 27, 2023. We assessed the associations of vaccination dose, vaccination regimen, and the interval since the last vaccination with SARS-CoV-2 infection and mortality. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to adjust for baseline imbalances across vaccination groups. An exploratory NAb analysis was conducted in 138 patients with paired serum samples at admission and discharge to evaluate immune responses associated with different vaccination strategies.ResultsIn the unadjusted analyses, SARS-CoV-2 infection and mortality rates decreased with increasing vaccine doses. After adjustment for age, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia, receipt of 1, 2, and 3 vaccine doses was associated with lower odds of SARS-CoV-2 infection compared with no vaccination, with adjusted ORs of 0.644 (95% CI: 0.445–0.931, P = 0.019), 0.579 (95% CI: 0.407–0.823, P = 0.002), and 0.448 (95% CI: 0.332–0.607, P < 0.001), respectively. Three-dose vaccination was also associated with lower odds of mortality (OR = 0.563, 95% CI: 0.380–0.835, P = 0.004). In the regimen-based model, CoronaVac plus ZF2001 was associated with lower odds of infection (OR = 0.456, 95% CI: 0.235–0.885, P = 0.020) and mortality (OR = 0.410, 95% CI: 0.123–0.878, P = 0.047) after adjustment. In the exploratory NAb subgroup analysis, patients receiving CoronaVac plus ZF2001 showed higher NAb levels at both admission and discharge.ConclusionsDuring the Omicron BA.5/BF.7 wave, receipt of three COVID-19 vaccine doses was associated with lower risks of SARS-CoV-2 infection and mortality among hospitalized patients aged 80 years and older. The heterologous regimen of CoronaVac plus ZF2001 was associated with lower risks of infection and mortality and higher NAb levels. These findings support the potential value of optimized booster and heterologous vaccination strategies for improving COVID-19-related outcomes in this high-risk older population.Clinical trialN/A

Introduction

Following the comprehensive adjustment of epidemic prevention policies in China on December 9, 2022, there was a rapid increase in COVID-19 infection rates. According to data from the Central Disease Control and Prevention Center, by February 7, 2023, the infection rate among the Chinese population had reached 82.4%, with the majority of deaths occurring among older adults aged 60 years and older [1]. A meta-analysis encompassing 611,583 patients with COVID-19 across five countries revealed significant differences in mortality rates among various age groups. The study observed a mortality rate of 9.5% for patients aged 60 to 69 years, whereas the mortality rate for those aged 80 years and older reached 29.6% [2]. Furthermore, studies have indicated that advanced age, racial background, male gender, regions characterized by heightened social vulnerability, as well as underlying health conditions such as diabetes and chronic liver disease, are pivotal factors influencing the risk of COVID-19 infection and the severity of its outcomes [3–7].

As of August 2022, data regarding the COVID-19 vaccination coverage among individuals aged 60 years and older in China revealed that 92.3% had received at least one dose, 88.6% had completed the primary series, and 72.4% had received a booster dose. However, the vaccination rates among those aged 80 years and older were comparatively lower, with only 71.9% completing the primary series and 46.7% receiving a booster dose [8]. A study conducted by Arregocés-Castillo et al. evaluated the effectiveness of vaccines in preventing COVID-19-related hospitalization and mortality among individuals aged 60 years and older in Colombia. The findings revealed that the vaccine’s efficacy in preventing mortality following hospitalization due to COVID-19 decreased by 22.6% in those aged 80 years and older compared to those aged 60 to 69 years [9]. This reduced effectiveness may be attributed to the increased risk of adverse outcomes associated with aging, comorbidities, and diminished immune response [10].

Research indicates that the efficacy of different types of vaccines exhibits significant variability among the elderly population. A study indicated that older adults who received two doses of Sinovac, followed by a heterologous booster of BNT162b2, showed higher neutralizing antibody(NAb) titers and experienced better protective efficacy than those who received three doses of Sinovac [11–14]. Increasing evidence supports that heterologous vaccination strategies may offer better tolerability and enhance both humoral and cellular immune responses [15]. For example, a study in Brazil found that a heterologous booster of BNT162b2 after two doses of CoronaVac significantly increased the NAb levels and improved protection against hospitalization and severe disease, which is particularly relevant for older adults with weaker immune responses [16]. Similarly, studies from China indicate that participants vaccinated with the heterologous Ad5-nCoV vaccine exhibit higher levels of NAb and enhanced immunogenicity compared to those inoculated with the homologous inactivated vaccine [11].

Currently, most older individuals in China have received inactivated vaccines, with only a small proportion receiving adenoviral vector vaccines or recombinant protein vaccines. However, research on the real-world effects of different vaccination statuses on infection and mortality among individuals aged 80 and older following the comprehensive adjustment of epidemic prevention policies is still lacking. Given the high risk of severe outcomes and mortality from COVID-19 among older adults, particularly those aged 80 years and older, it is crucial to investigate the effectiveness of COVID-19 vaccines in this population. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of various vaccine types and dosing regimens, with a particular focus on heterologous vaccine combinations, in mitigating COVID-19 infection and reducing mortality among individuals aged 80 and older. By conducting a detailed analysis of vaccine strategies, this study seeks to provide empirical evidence to support the development of more effective public health policies to better protect this high-risk older cohort.

Patients and methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective analysis included 2,325 patients aged 80 years and older who were hospitalized between December 9, 2022, and January 27, 2023, across four campuses within the same healthcare system of Taizhou Hospital of Zhejiang Province: the main campus of Taizhou Hospital, the East Campus of Taizhou Hospital, the Luqiao Campus of Taizhou Hospital, and the Rehabilitation Hospital. All four campuses were managed under a homogeneous administrative framework and followed unified standards for medical quality control, admission criteria, and diagnostic and therapeutic procedures.

Because this was a retrospective real-world study, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. The sample size was determined by the number of eligible hospitalized patients with complete available data during the study period. The inclusion criteria were as follows: [1] age ≥ 80 years; [2] hospitalization between December 9, 2022, and January 27, 2023; [3] admission to one of the four campuses within the same healthcare system of Taizhou Hospital of Zhejiang Province; and [4] availability of clinical data, vaccination records, and SARS-CoV-2 RT-PCR or rapid antigen test results. To ensure the accuracy of the analysis, we excluded 505 patients with missing vaccination information, because these cases precluded reliable assessment of the association between vaccination status and clinical outcomes. In addition, 287 patients who received COVID-19 vaccination within 0–13 days before illness onset were excluded, as this short interval may not have allowed sufficient immune protection to develop and could therefore have affected the evaluation of vaccine-related associations. Given that only 13 patients had received a fourth vaccine dose, this subgroup was also excluded because the sample size was too small to provide sufficient statistical power for subgroup analysis. Consequently, 1,520 hospitalized patients aged 80 years and older were included in the main analysis (Fig. 1).

The inclusion process of study participants

COVID-19 vaccination in the study population began in April 2021 and was subsequently implemented widely in the target population. By December 9, 2022, more than 69.4% of adults aged 80 years and older had received at least one dose of a COVID-19 vaccine. Based on the laboratory testing records available in this study, none of the participants had documented positive SARS-CoV-2 RT-PCR or rapid antigen test results before December 9, 2022.

Data collection

Vaccination information for all patients in this study was obtained from the Zhejiang Province Vaccination Management System, which provides detailed records including vaccination dates, doses, vaccine manufacturers, and vaccination sites. We also collected basic patient information, including age, sex, underlying health conditions, disease severity, date of onset, date of discharge, and SARS-CoV-2 reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) or rapid antigen test results. Disease severity was classified based on the criteria used in the Reference Protocol for Diagnosis and Treatment (December 2022 edition) published by the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine of Peking Union Medical College Hospital [17]. Mortality outcomes were determined based on the available hospitalization records and discharge information; systematic post-discharge mortality follow-up was not available. In this study, SARS-CoV-2 infection was defined based on documented positive RT-PCR or rapid antigen test results in the available medical records. The date of symptom onset and the corresponding test results were retrospectively extracted from the available clinical records. Because of the retrospective design and the limitations of the available data, infections were not further classified as community-acquired or hospital-acquired in the primary analysis.

For the NAb analysis, an exploratory immunologic subanalysis was conducted within the main study cohort. This subanalysis included 138 patients aged 80 years and older who met the overall study inclusion criteria, had received recombinant protein vaccines, adenoviral vector vaccines, or heterologous combination vaccination regimens, and had serum samples available for NAb testing at both admission and discharge. This subgroup was primarily used to compare immune responses associated with different vaccination strategies.

Analysis of anti-SARS-CoV-2 NAb

In this study, we employed a surrogate viral neutralization test based on antibody-mediated inhibition to assess the neutralizing capacity of antibodies in serum samples against the interaction between the SARS-CoV-2 RBD and the ACE2 receptor. SARS-CoV-2 NAb detection was performed using a one-step competitive chemiluminescent immunoassay (Shenzhen Yhlo Biotech Co., Ltd., iFlash3000). The procedure involved the following steps: NAb in the sample were bound to the SARS-CoV-2 RBD antigen immobilized on magnetic beads, forming an antigen-antibody complex. Subsequently, ACE2 labeled with acridinium ester was added. The RBD-coated beads bound to unbound ACE2 in the sample. Under the influence of a magnetic field, the magnetic particles were attracted to the reaction tube wall, and unbound substances were removed by washing. Following the addition of the pre-trigger and trigger solutions, the number of SARS-CoV-2 NAb in the sample was inversely proportional to the relative light units detected by the optical system of the instrument.

The cutoff value for this assay was set at 10.00 AU/mL, as determined using a receiver operating characteristic curve analysis of 300 samples (150 negative and 150 positive). Samples with results below 10.0 AU/mL were classified as non-reactive, while results equal to or above 10.0 AU/mL were classified as reactive. According to the manufacturer’s specifications, this assay exhibited good performance compared with pseudovirus neutralization tests, with a sensitivity of 95.3% and a specificity of 100.0%. Furthermore, the direct chemiluminescence assay showed good agreement with traditional plaque reduction neutralization tests, with a positive agreement rate of 98.5% and a negative agreement rate of 96.1%. To evaluate within-batch precision, six serum samples from patients with COVID-19 and varying NAb titers were analyzed in ten consecutive runs, achieving a coefficient of variation (CV) of ≤ 10%. Additionally, to assess between-batch precision, a positive quality control sample was tested over a 15-day period, achieving a CV of ≤ 15%.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables that followed a normal distribution are expressed as means ± standard deviations; those that did not follow a normal distribution are presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Comparisons of categorical variables between groups were performed using the chi-square test, while the Mann–Whitney U test was employed for continuous variables that did not meet the normality assumption. The Kaplan–Meier estimator was used to assess the cumulative incidence rates between the vaccinated and unvaccinated groups. To account for potential confounding arising from baseline imbalances across vaccination groups, we performed additional univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses to assess the associations between vaccination status and clinical outcomes, including SARS-CoV-2 infection and mortality. Two multivariable models were constructed. In Model 1, vaccination dose was included as the main exposure variable, with adjustment for age, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, cardiovascular disease, and other underlying comorbidities. In Model 2, vaccination regimen was included as the main exposure variable, with adjustment for the same covariates. The results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).To ensure data integrity and result accuracy, we excluded samples with missing vaccination information rather than employing imputation methods to fill in missing data, thereby reducing potential bias. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0), and cumulative incidence analyses for infection and mortality were performed using R software (version 4.1.1). The level of statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the older adults in this study

In this study, a total of 1,520 patients aged 80 years and older were included, with a median age of 85 years (IQR, 83–89 years). Among them, 62.3% were male and 37.7% were female. The infection rate of SARS-CoV-2 was 71.8%, and the mortality rate was 12.6%. The participants were categorized into five groups based on their vaccination type: 57 patients received the ZF2001 vaccine, 785 patients received the CoronaVac vaccine, 45 patients received a combination of two doses of CoronaVac and one dose of ZF2001, 83 patients received a combination of two doses of CoronaVac and one dose of Ad5-nCoV, and 91 patients received the Ad5-nCoV vaccine. Additionally, 459 patients did not receive any vaccine (Table 1), (Fig. 1).

The efficacy of the vaccine dosage in the patients

Among the 1,520 hospitalized patients aged 80 years and older, approximately 1.6% exhibited mild to moderate symptoms, 92.9% exhibited severe symptoms, and 5.5% exhibited critical symptoms, with an overall mortality rate of 12.5% (Fig. 2A). Further analysis was performed to examine the relationship between the vaccination dosage and disease severity. The results indicated that patients who received three doses of the vaccine had the best outcomes in terms of critical illness and mortality rates compared to those who were unvaccinated (P = 0.012, Fig. 2B).

The effectiveness of COVID-19 vaccines in preventing infection and death among patients aged 80 and above significantly improves with increasing vaccination doses. We observed a substantial reduction in the infection rate of SARS-CoV-2 with each additional dose of the vaccine (P < 0.001, Fig. 2C), and a notable decrease in mortality rate (P = 0.001, Fig. 2D). The observed reduction in infection and mortality rates with increasing vaccine doses may be related to enhanced neutralizing antibody responses after booster vaccination, further strengthening of immune memory, and cumulative protective effects in older adults.

From December 9, 2022, when COVID-19 restrictions were fully lifted, until January 28, 2023, we conducted a 50-day follow-up of the patients. During this period, the cumulative infection (P = 0.001, Fig. 2E) and mortality rates (P = 0.039, Fig. 2G) were significantly higher in the unvaccinated group than in the vaccinated groups. The subgroup analysis revealed that older patients who received three doses of the vaccine had a significantly lower cumulative infection rate than those who received two doses, one dose, or no vaccine (P = 0.006, Fig. 2F). However, in the mortality outcome analysis, although the cumulative mortality rate was lower in the three-dose vaccine group than in the two-dose, one-dose, and unvaccinated groups, the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.180, Fig. 2H). This result may have been influenced by a smaller sample size or other uncontrolled variables.

The effectiveness of administering different types of vaccines

In this study, we assessed the impact of different vaccination strategies on the infection and mortality rates among individuals aged 80 years and older. The results indicated that while the heterologous combination of two doses of CoronaVac and one dose of ZF2001 was associated with the lowest infection rate (P = 0.029, Fig. 3A), this vaccine combination did not demonstrate statistical significance in reducing mortality (P = 0.164, Fig. 3B). This lack of significance may be due to inconsistencies in the vaccination doses. Furthermore, when evaluating the impact of different vaccine doses and brands, the results indicated that the heterologous combination group receiving two doses of CoronaVac and one dose of ZF2001 exhibited a significantly lower infection rate (P < 0.001, Fig. 3C) and the lowest mortality rate (P = 0.002, Fig. 3D).

Consequently, we further analyzed patients who received three doses of different types of vaccines to evaluate the effectiveness of the vaccination strategy in preventing infection and reducing mortality. The findings revealed that both the three-dose inactivated vaccine regimen and the two doses of CoronaVac plus one dose of the ZF2001 heterologous combination significantly prevented infection (P = 0.005, Fig. 3E). Additionally, these vaccine combinations demonstrated high efficacy in reducing mortality rates (P = 0.047, Fig. 3F). These results highlight the significant differences in the effectiveness of various vaccination strategies for preventing COVID-19 infection and improving survival outcomes, underscoring the importance of selecting appropriate vaccine combinations.

Adjusted associations between vaccination status and clinical outcomes

To control for potential confounding due to baseline imbalances across vaccination groups, we performed additional univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses for SARS-CoV-2 infection and mortality outcomes. Considering the potential collinearity between vaccination dose and vaccination regimen, two separate multivariable models were constructed. In Model 1, vaccination dose was included as the main exposure variable, with adjustment for age, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, cardiovascular disease, and other underlying comorbidities. In Model 2, vaccination regimen was included as the main exposure variable, with adjustment for the same covariates.

For SARS-CoV-2 infection, after adjustment in Model 1, receiving 1 dose (OR = 0.644, 95% CI: 0.445–0.931, P = 0.019), 2 doses (OR = 0.579, 95% CI: 0.407–0.823, P = 0.002), and 3 doses (OR = 0.448, 95% CI: 0.332–0.607, P < 0.001) was associated with lower odds of infection compared with no vaccination. In Model 2, after adjustment, CoronaVac vaccination (OR = 0.473, 95% CI: 0.357–0.627, P < 0.001) and the heterologous regimen of CoronaVac plus ZF2001 (OR = 0.456, 95% CI: 0.235–0.885, P = 0.020) remained associated with lower odds of infection (Table 2).

Association between the interval since the last COVID‑19 vaccination and infection and mortality outcomes

After excluding patients who received COVID-19 vaccination within 0–13 days before illness onset, we further analyzed the association between the interval from the last vaccination to symptom onset and infection and mortality outcomes. Given that most participants had completed a three-dose regimen of CoronaVac, this analysis focused primarily on this specific population. Patients were categorized into three groups according to the time interval between the last vaccination and symptom onset: 0–6 months, 6–12 months, and > 12 months.

The distribution of patients across the three time‑interval groups was as follows: 194 patients in the 0–6 months group (63 SARS‑CoV‑2–negative and 131 positive), 228 patients in the 6–12 months group (90 negative and 138 positive), and 116 patients in the > 12 months group (39 negative and 77 positive). As shown in Fig. 4, no statistically significant differences were observed among the groups in terms of infection rates (P = 0.285) or mortality rates (P = 0.397). Although the mortality proportion was numerically lower in the 0–6 months group, this difference did not reach statistical significance.

Analysis of the NAb levels in 138 older adults who received different types of vaccines

Based on the preliminary clinical analysis, heterologous combination vaccination, including two doses of CoronaVac followed by one dose of ZF2001, was associated with more favorable clinical outcomes. To further investigate the immunologic correlates of these findings, we performed an exploratory NAb analysis in a subgroup of 138 older adults from the main cohort who had paired serum samples available at both admission and discharge and had received different vaccine types. The results indicated that patients who received the heterologous combination vaccine (two doses of CoronaVac and one dose of ZF2001) had the highest NAb levels at both admission and discharge (Fig. 5A). Moreover, after receiving three doses of the vaccine, the heterologous combination vaccine (two doses of CoronaVac and one dose of ZF2001) helped to consistently maintain the highest NAb levels at both time points (Fig. 5B). These findings suggest that heterologous combination vaccines may have a notable advantage in enhancing NAb levels, thereby improving protection against COVID-19.

Discussion

The widespread SARS-CoV-2 infection in China primarily occurred after the adjustment of the comprehensive adjustment of epidemic prevention policies. Individuals aged 80 years and older, with weakened immune systems, often experience higher COVID-19 infection rates. A meta-analysis of 59 studies, including 36,470 patients, indicated that the risk of COVID-19 infection in patients aged 70 years and older appeared to be 65% higher than that of patients who were younger than 70 years. Previous studies have reported that COVID-19 vaccination is associated with a significant reduction in COVID-19 symptoms, hospitalization, and mortality among older adults [18, 19]. However, during the wave of infections with the Omicron BA.5 and BF.7 subvariants in mainland China, the rapid spread of the virus caused public panic and led to excessive strain on medical resources [20]. Therefore, it is crucial to reassess whether the infection and mortality rates associated with COVID-19 are consistent with those previously observed after the comprehensive adjustment of epidemic prevention policies.

Using real-world hospitalization data from mainland China during the circulation of the Omicron BA.5 and BF.7 subvariants, this study evaluated the association between COVID-19 vaccination status and SARS-CoV-2 infection and mortality outcomes among patients aged 80 years and older. A higher number of vaccine doses was associated with a lower risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection, and this association remained after adjustment for age, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia. In the regimen-based analysis, CoronaVac vaccination and the heterologous regimen of CoronaVac plus ZF2001 were also associated with lower infection risk after adjustment. For mortality outcomes, three-dose vaccination, CoronaVac vaccination, and the CoronaVac plus ZF2001 regimen remained associated with lower mortality risk in the adjusted analyses. However, the analysis of the interval since the last vaccination did not show statistically significant differences in mortality across time-interval groups. Therefore, any clinical outcome evidence suggesting waning vaccine protection over time should be interpreted cautiously.

This study further explored the association between the number of vaccine doses and SARS-CoV-2 infection and mortality outcomes among individuals aged 80 years and older. In the unadjusted analyses, SARS-CoV-2 infection and mortality rates showed decreasing trends with increasing numbers of vaccine doses, and patients who received three vaccine doses appeared to have more favorable outcomes in terms of disease severity and mortality proportion. This finding is consistent with the direction of the study by Jara et al., which showed that booster vaccination provided a high level of protection against COVID-19, including prevention of severe disease and death, among individuals who had completed the primary CoronaVac vaccination series [12]. After further adjustment for age, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, cardiovascular disease, and other underlying comorbidities, the association between vaccination dose and infection outcomes remained evident: compared with unvaccinated individuals, receipt of 1, 2, and 3 vaccine doses was associated with lower infection risk. Among these, three-dose vaccination was associated with the lowest infection risk (OR = 0.448, 95% CI: 0.332–0.607, P < 0.001). For mortality outcomes, only three-dose vaccination remained associated with lower mortality risk after adjustment (OR = 0.563, 95% CI: 0.380–0.835, P = 0.004), whereas the associations of one-dose and two-dose vaccination with mortality did not reach statistical significance. Therefore, our findings support the view that completion of booster vaccination may be associated with more favorable infection and mortality outcomes in older patients [21]. However, because this was a retrospective observational study, residual confounding cannot be excluded. In addition, although the cumulative mortality analysis showed a lower mortality trend in the three-dose group, the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.180), indicating that mortality-related findings should be interpreted cautiously in light of sample size, number of events, and potential confounding factors.

In the analysis of different vaccination regimens, the unadjusted results showed that patients who received heterologous vaccination regimens, particularly two doses of CoronaVac plus one dose of ZF2001, had lower infection and mortality rates. After further adjustment for age, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, cardiovascular disease, and other underlying comorbidities, the heterologous CoronaVac plus ZF2001 regimen remained associated with a lower risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection (OR = 0.456, 95% CI: 0.235–0.885, P = 0.020) and was also associated with a lower risk of mortality (OR = 0.410, 95% CI: 0.123–0.878, P = 0.047). This finding is consistent with the direction of previous studies on heterologous booster vaccination. Previous studies have suggested that heterologous vaccination regimens, such as AZD1222 followed by BNT162b2 or mRNA-1273, may be more effective than some homologous regimens in reducing COVID-19-related hospitalization and mortality [22]. Immunologic studies have also shown that heterologous vaccination schedules can enhance RBD-specific memory B-cell responses and S1-specific T-cell responses and may induce broader humoral and cellular immune responses [15]. In line with the NAb analysis in the present study, the CoronaVac plus ZF2001 heterologous regimen may be associated with stronger humoral immune responses and more favorable clinical outcomes.

In addition, we explored the association between the interval from the last COVID‑19 vaccination to symptom onset and mortality outcomes in older adults. The results showed that the differences in mortality rates among the different time‑interval groups did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.397). Although a numerically lower mortality proportion was observed among patients vaccinated within 0–6 months, this difference was not statistically significant; therefore, this finding should be interpreted as a trend rather than evidence of a definitive time‑dependent effect of vaccination on mortality. The changes in mortality proportions observed with longer intervals since vaccination may also be related to limited sample size and insufficient statistical power and should be interpreted with caution. Notably, previous studies have demonstrated that humoral immune responses induced by different types of COVID‑19 vaccines gradually wane over time. For example, a study conducted in Chile demonstrated that serological IgG positivity rates began to decline 7 weeks after receiving a booster dose of the CoronaVac vaccine. Similarly, individuals who received the ChAdOx1 vaccine exhibited decreased antibody levels 18 weeks after receiving the booster dose, whereas those who received BNT162b2 exhibited decreased antibody levels after 23 weeks [23]. These findings provide biological context for the waning of vaccine‑induced immune responses over time, but they cannot be directly used to explain the non‑significant differences observed between the interval since last vaccination and mortality outcomes in the present study.

Our study results indicate that patients who received a heterologous combination vaccine regimen (two doses of CoronaVac and one dose of ZF2001) not only demonstrated higher levels of NAb but also exhibited superior clinical outcomes in terms of infection and mortality rates. Elevated NAb levels are typically associated with stronger immune protection, which clinically translates to lower infection and mortality rates. Previous studies have shown that heterologous vaccination regimens, such as two doses of ChAdOx1 combined with one dose of BNT162b2, induce more robust humoral and cellular immune responses, providing effective protection against circulating SARS-CoV-2 variants [24–26]. This suggests that heterologous vaccination can elicit a broader and more durable immune response, enhancing protection against different variants. This finding offers valuable insights into vaccination strategies, particularly emphasizing that heterologous combination vaccines may be a crucial approach for improving vaccine efficacy among older adults.

Although SARS-CoV-2 has entered a phase of continuous evolution and endemic circulation, older adults, particularly those aged ≥ 80 years, remain a key high-risk population for severe COVID-19 and death. As of December 2024, the WHO continued to prioritize periodic revaccination for high-risk groups and recommended considering revaccination every 6–12 months depending on population risk level; the WHO also noted that currently approved COVID-19 vaccines continue to provide protection against severe disease and death. In December 2024, the CDC also stated that COVID-19 remains an important cause of morbidity and mortality among adults aged ≥ 65 years and immunocompromised individuals, and therefore recommended additional 2024–2025 vaccine doses for adults aged ≥ 65 years [27]. In addition, recent real-world studies have shown that updated vaccines can still reduce the risks of hospitalization and severe outcomes in the context of the circulation of multiple emerging lineages [28]. Therefore, we believe that the current relevance of our study mainly lies in emphasizing that, among individuals aged ≥ 80 years—an extremely high-risk population—increasing the number of vaccine doses, optimizing booster vaccination strategies, and paying attention to waning protection remain of clear clinical and public health value.

Overall, this study provides valuable real-world data on the relationship between vaccination status and SARS-CoV-2 infection and mortality rates among adults aged 80 years and older following the adjustment of China’s dynamic zero-COVID strategy. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although 1,520 hospitalized patients aged 80 years and older were included in this study, the sample sizes of some vaccination subgroups, particularly recipients of recombinant protein vaccines, adenoviral vector vaccines, and heterologous vaccination regimens, were relatively small. This may have limited the statistical power and robustness of comparisons across different vaccination strategies. Second, the observation period was relatively short, spanning approximately 50 days from December 9, 2022, to January 27, 2023. Mortality among older adults with COVID-19 may occur after prolonged hospitalization or after discharge. Because this was a retrospective study and systematic post-discharge mortality follow-up data were not available, the mortality rate reported in this study mainly reflects in-hospital or short-term outcomes and may underestimate the true mortality burden among patients aged 80 years and older. Third, the NAb analysis was conducted only in a subgroup of 138 patients with paired serum samples available at both admission and discharge. The relatively small sample size and potential selection bias should be considered. Therefore, these immunological findings should be regarded as exploratory and interpreted with caution, rather than being directly generalized to the entire study cohort. In addition, NAb levels were assessed only at admission and discharge, and follow-up data at 3 months, 6 months, or longer after infection were not available, limiting our ability to fully characterize the dynamic changes in antibody levels and the duration of long-term immune protection. Fourth, the data in this study were obtained from four campuses within the same hospital system rather than four independently managed hospitals. Because these four campuses were managed under standardized protocols for medical quality control, admission criteria, clinical workflows, critical care management, and major clinical pathways, differences in healthcare delivery and patient admission practices across campuses were likely to be relatively small. Nevertheless, campus-level clustering effects were not further incorporated into the main statistical analyses, nor was a multilevel model used to adjust for potential inter-campus differences. Therefore, unmeasured campus-level factors affecting infection rates, disease severity, or mortality outcomes cannot be completely excluded. Fifth, because this was a retrospective observational study, comorbidity burden, sex, timing of presentation, disease severity, treatment measures, and other unmeasured confounding factors may have influenced the results. In addition, the analysis of the interval since the last vaccination was exploratory; the sample sizes were unevenly distributed across different time windows, and the differences in infection and mortality rates did not reach statistical significance. Therefore, the findings are insufficient to support a definitive time-dependent effect of vaccine-induced protection. Finally, this study focused on adults aged 80 years and older in Taizhou, China, and mainly reflected short-term clinical outcomes during the Omicron BA.5/BF.7 wave after the adjustment of epidemic prevention policies. Therefore, the generalizability of the findings may be limited by geographic setting, study period, circulating variants, and population immune background. Future studies with larger sample sizes, broader vaccine types, wider geographic coverage, systematic post-discharge follow-up, and longer observation periods are needed to more comprehensively evaluate the long-term protective effects and real-world clinical value of COVID-19 vaccination strategies in very old adults.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrated that increasing the number of vaccine doses, particularly through the use of heterologous combination vaccines (such as two doses of CoronaVac and one dose of ZF2001), significantly enhanced protection among older adults, reducing the risk of both infection and mortality. Therefore, we recommend promoting booster vaccinations among the older adults and prioritizing heterologous combination vaccines to achieve optimal protective effects. In conclusion, this study showed that, during the Omicron BA.5/BF.7 wave, a higher number of COVID-19 vaccine doses, particularly completion of a three-dose vaccination schedule, was associated with lower risks of SARS-CoV-2 infection and mortality among hospitalized patients aged 80 years and older. In addition, the heterologous regimen of CoronaVac plus ZF2001 remained associated with lower risks of infection and mortality and was accompanied by higher NAb levels. These findings suggest that optimized booster and heterologous vaccination strategies may help improve COVID-19-related clinical outcomes in older adults and provide real-world evidence to support vaccination strategy development for high-risk individuals aged 80 years and older. However, given the retrospective observational design of this study, these findings should be interpreted as associations between vaccination status and clinical outcomes rather than definitive causal effects. Larger, longer-term, and more systematically designed prospective studies are needed to further validate the long-term protective effects of different vaccination strategies in older adults.
