Impact of exogenous direct electric current on the expression of mRNA related to OPG, in SaOS-2 cells using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction: A qualitative and quantitative analysis
Authors: Aparajita Pandey, Ashish Agrawal, Parimal Das, Ritu Dixit, Prashant Ranjan, Sushit kumar sonu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo assess OPG gene expression in SaOS-2 cells after exposure to pulsed direct current (30 μA/10 s, square wave) using qRT-PCR at different time points (5, 7,12, and 24 h).Materials and methodsThe study investigated the effects of direct current (DC) electrical. stimulation on SaOS-2 cells by exposing experimental. groups to DC (30 μA, 10-s pulses) for 5, 7, 12, and 24 h, while control groups received no stimulation. Stainless steel electrodes were utilized, and both groups were cultured under identical. conditions. Qualitative assessments included cell morphology analysis through phase contrast microscopy, while quantitative evaluations involved MTT assays for cell viability and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) for osteoprotegerin (OPG) gene expression. RNA was isolated after stimulation, followed by complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis for gene analysis. Data were analyzed to assess stimulation-induced cellular and genetic responses.ResultsDirect current stimulation induced time-dependent cytotoxicity in SaOS-2 cells, with cell death increasing from approximately 10 % at 5 h to about 52 % at 24 h qRT-PCR reveals significant downregulation of OPG expression, nearly eliminated between 12 and 24 h (p < 0.0001), indicating strong inhibitory effects on both cell viability and gene expression.ConclusionDirect electrical. stimulation downregulated OPG expression in SaOS-2 cells in a time-dependent manner, with a significant decrease observed as early as 5 h. The MTT assay reveals time-dependent cytotoxicity from DC stimulation. Reduced OPG expression suggests potential. enhancement of osteoclastic activity, indicating a possible role of DC stimulation in bone remodelling, which warrants further investigation.

Introduction

For ages, the duration of orthodontic treatment has been a significant concern, often spanning from a few months to several years. It demands considerable patience and dedication from the patient. Orthodontic procedures to accelerate tooth movement and reduce treatment duration are categorized as surgical (e.g., corticotomy, piezoincision) and non-surgical (e.g., laser radiation, vibration, electromagnetic waves, pharmacological methods).1,2

Converse piezoelectricity is one such non invasive method which can reduce duration of orthodontic tooth movement. It occurs when an external electric current is applied on a crystalline material which generates a voltage and triggers a biochemical response.3 Carter et al. in his review concluded the potential of converse piezoelectricity to increase bone formation and decrease bone resorption.3 Rachmat et al. evaluated the effect of electric fields on osteosarcoma cells (MG 63) and concluded that the number of cells decreased each day which indicated that the electric fields affect the proliferation of osteosarcoma cells.4 Bones possess piezoelectric properties, due to the organized and patterned structure of collagen and hydroxyapatite crystals allowing them to generate electrical and biochemical signals in response to mechanical activity, which in turn stimulates bone remodelling.3

Various osteosarcoma cell lines include MG-63, Saos-2 and U-2 OS UMR 106 and the ROS 17/2.5, 6, 7 Christoph et al. compared three osteosarcoma cell lines (MG-63, Saos-2 and U-2 OS) with normal human osteoblasts by immunocytochemistry in which Saos-2 cells revealed the most mature osteoblastic labelling profile.7 In 2004, Sahm et al. studied the voltage-dependent effects of alternating current on human osteoblast morphology, gene expression, and protein accumulation.6 Osteogenic differentiation genes COL1A1, ALP, and BGLAP were investigated.6 These cells can be fully differentiated to mimic the natural behaviour of osteoblastic cells. Hence Saos2 cells capable of full differentiation, mimic osteoblastic behaviour and closely resemble human bone cells, making them suitable for experimentation.8,9

Biomarkers are potential tool to assess the bone remodelling capacity.10 The Receptor Activator of Nuclear Factor kappa B (RANK)/RANK ligand (RANKL)/osteoprotegerin (OPG) system, along with the more recently identified leucine-rich repeat-containing G protein-coupled receptor 4 (LGR4), are crucial in both bone and vascular mineralization.10 OPG expression in osteoblasts is influenced by various cytokines, hormones, growth factors and is triggered by various signalling pathway such as Wnt/b-catenin pathway.11 The canonical Wnt pathway regulates mesenchymal stem cells and osteoblast progenitor proliferation and differentiation, while also modulating osteoclast-driven bone resorption.11 Jagged1/Notch1 signalling indirectly regulates the OPG/RANKL ratio in stromal cells, while noncanonical Wnt, JAK/STAT, and Hedgehog pathways interact with Wnt/β-catenin, collectively maintaining bone homeostasis.12,13 The human OPG gene (gene symbol: TNFRSF11B), located on chromosome 8 (8q24.12), encodes a receptor composed of 401 amino acids.11 Human and mouse OPG share 85 % similarity in their amino acid sequences.11 Additionally, OPG is a member of the TNF receptor superfamily.11 The Tumour Necrosis Factor superfamily of ligands (TNFSF) and receptors (TNFRSF) communicates between different cell types during development, particularly in the skin, bone, and lymphoid organs.14,15

Real-time PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction), also known as quantitative PCR (qPCR), is a widely used and powerful technique for analyzing gene expression. This method enables the quantification of specific nucleic acid sequences in real time during the amplification process. By using fluorescent dyes or probes that bind to the DNA, researchers can monitor the accumulation of the target sequence after each PCR cycle. The use of qRT-PCR in this method is significant as it provides a sensitive and reliable approach to quantitatively and qualitatively analyze mRNA expression, enabling precise evaluation of OPG gene regulation in response to direct electric current in SaOS-2 cells.16

Literature remained silent on the alteration in gene expression in relation to direct electric current stimulation. Therefore, an original study was conducted to find out the effect of external electrical stimulation on TNFRSF11B gene, which encodes the OPG gene to the receptor for OPG synthesis.

Materials and methods

Saos2 cells culturing

Saos2 cells were purchased from the National. Centre for Cell Culture. The cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) with 15 % v/v Fetal. Bovine Serum (FBS) and 1 % Penicillin/Streptomycin (10.00 U/ml). Proliferative cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5 % CO2 (Thermo Scientific). Subculturing involved washing the cell monolayers twice with calcium- and magnesium-free phosphate-buffered saline after removing the old medium. Cells were trypsinized with 200 μL of trypsin-EDTA (Sigma Aldrich, 0.25 %) to detach them. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 2–3 min until they detached from the surface. The trypsin was then neutralized by adding fresh media. Saos2 cells were passaged at a ratio of 1:2. Regular maintenance included replacing the medium every 2–3 days and monitoring cell health and confluency under a phase contrast microscope (Leica Microsystems).

Experimental. Design

Saos2 cells were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. Stainless steel electrodes (18-8/304 grade) were sterilized using 70 % ethanol, phosphate-buffered saline, and UV light overnight. Cells were electrically stimulated (30 μA, 10-s intervals), with experimental. groups divided by duration: E1 (5 h), E2 (7 h), E3 (12 h), and E4 (24 h). as illustrated in Fig. 1. Control groups received no stimulation but had electrodes connected under identical culture conditions. Cellular morphology and viability of both experimental and control groups were documented at 5-, 7-, 12-, and 24-h using phase contrast microscopy (Leica Microsystems). 2 μL of medium was collected from each of the experimental. cells that received electrical. stimulation at 5-, 7-, 12-, and 24-h. The liquid thus collected was subjected to an ELISA test for OPG protein expression measured in ng/ml.Fig. 1Experimental setup.Fig. 1

Experimental setup.

RNA isolation and quantification

After treatment, cells were washed with cold PBS, lysed using 1 mL TRIZOL (Thermo Scientific), and homogenized. 0.2 mL chloroform was added, mixed, and incubated for 2–3 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to separate the phases. The RNA-containing aqueous phase was transferred, mixed with 0.5 mL isopropanol, incubated for 10 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to form an RNA pellet. The pellet was washed with 75 % ethanol, centrifuged at 7500 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, air-dried for 5–10 min, and resuspended in 25 μL of RNase-free water. After complete resuspension of the RNA, its quality was checked using a 1 % agarose gel. RNA was quantified using a NanoDrop1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific).

cDNA preparation and first-strand cDNA synthesis

For genomic DNA removal, 2 μg RNA was 2.5 U of RNAase-free DNase I-treated (2.5 U DNase I, 37 °C, 30 min) in a volume of 25 μL. The reaction was terminated by adding 2.5 μL 50 mM EDTA and incubated at 65 °C for 10 min. RNA was quantified using a NanoDrop1000 spectrophotometer. cDNA was synthesized using SuperScript™ III First-Strand Synthesis kit (cat no of kit) with random hexamers following the manufacturer's protocol.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR) and data analysis

The effect of electrical stimulation on the expression of Osteoprotegerin (OPG) was determined through Quantitative Real Time PCR assays using OPG-specific primers adapted from (reference). The experiment was performed for all experimental conditions (5, 7, 12, and 24 h) and their respective untreated controls in triplicate using SYBR Green (Puregene) in the 7500 Real-Time RT-PCR System (Applied Biosystems). β-actin was used as an internal control for normalizing expression data.

Data was presented as the fold change in mutant expression compared to wild-type, along with the standard error of the mean. To assess transcript levels, comparative Ct (ΔΔCt) fold change calculations were performed. One-way ANOVA was used to identify significant differences in mean mRNA levels between control and experimental groups. Additionally, t-tests were conducted to compare the results for each group. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

mTT assay and data analysis

Cell viability after direct current (DC) stimulation was measured using the MTT assay. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 1 × 105 cells per well and stimulated for 5, 7, 12, or 24 h at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator. After treatment, the media were removed, and 200 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well. Plates were incubated for 4 h in the dark at 37 °C. After incubation, 100 μL from each well was transferred to a 96-well plate, and 100 μL of DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals. Plates were shaken for 10 min, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm. Absorbance, which correlates with cell viability, was recorded for control, treated, DMSO, and media-only groups. The absorbance values were normalized using DMSO and media-only controls. After normalization, the percentage of viable cells was calculated with the formula provided below. The viability percentages for both the experimental and control groups were plotted and compared to visualize the effects of electrical stimulation at different time points on cell viability. A Student t-test was performed to assess statistical significance, with a p-value <0.05 indicating statistical significance.

All experiments were repeated in three independent biological experiments. For each experiment, three biological replicates were used per condition, and each biological replicate was measured in technical triplicate; data are presented as mean ± SD.

Effect of stimulation on cell morphology

SaOS-2 cells, commonly used in bone cancer research, were examined under phase contrast microscopy (Nikon Eclipse, 20x). Before experimentation, cells mainly appeared spindle-shaped with some round cells, measuring 15–20 μm in length. They were mostly isolated, with occasional small clusters, showing a spread-out morphology and visible central nuclei. Cytoplasmic granules and vacuoles were also observed. After 5 h of direct current stimulation, cells appeared more elongated, although their overall morphology remained unchanged. Cell death was observed near the stainless-steel electrode sites.

After 7 h of direct current electrical stimulation, SaOS-2 cells appeared shrunken, as shown in Fig. 3(b2) after the same duration. Morphologically, the cells exhibited a reduction in size and a shrinking appearance compared to the untreated control after 12 h. We also observed significant cell death after 12 h of stimulation, as clearly visible in Fig. 3(c2). Discussion

Large bone defects, non-unions, and open fractures remain major challenges in orthopaedics, while prolonged treatment time is a key concern in orthodontics. Tissue engineering strategies that deliver osteoprogenitor cells with osteoconductive and osteoinductive scaffolds offer potential for faster bone remodelling. Direct current (DC)17 stimulation has been widely studied in tissue engineering, especially for cardiac18 and nerve tissues,19 and shows promise in bone regeneration due to the piezoelectric nature of bone. DC can influence cell proliferation and differentiation, but its exact role in bone remodelling is not yet fully understood.17,20

Since the 1980s, studies have highlighted the potential of direct current (DC) in bone cell stimulation. Ferrier et al.21 showed enhanced cell migration under DC, while Zhuang et al. 22 reported changes in TGF-β, BMP-2, and mRNA expression. In this study, we examined the effect of DC on osteoprotegerin (OPG) gene expression (TNFRSF11B) in SaOS-2 cells. OPG is key to bone remodelling, inhibiting osteoclast activity by interacting with RANKL.

SaOS-2, a widely used osteoblast-like cell line, was chosen for its reliability in bone-related research.11 Initially, cells displayed spindle-shaped morphology with occasional round cells, consistent with Rodan et al.9 After 5 h of direct current (DC) stimulation, cells became more elongated without major changes, aligning with Mobini et al.23 However, by 7 h, signs of shrinkage and cell death appeared, particularly near electrodes—indicative of early apoptosis, as also noted by Genovese et al.24 Prolonged exposure (12–24 h) led to marked cell death and irreversible damage, consistent with Caputo et al.25 Localized damage near electrodes suggests that current density gradients influence cellular response. MTT assay results supported this, showing minimal cytotoxicity at 5 h, a sharp rise by 12 h, and a plateau at ∼51–52 % by 24 h. This pattern mirrors findings by Chaudhari et al. 26 and Caputo et al.,25 emphasizing a critical threshold between 7 and 12 h where cytotoxicity rapidly escalates.

TNFRSF11B is a member of the tumour necrosis factor receptor (TNF) superfamily and is a key regulator of bone homeostasis.27 Upregulation was seen in the study done by Cancel et al.28 who demonstrated the effect of DC stimulation on neuroactive genes in isolated astrocytes. However, mixed findings have been reported regarding TNFRSF11B expression by Hirai et al.,29 he demonstrated that α1B-adrenergic receptor signalling regulates the circadian expression of Tnfrsf11b in osteoblasts, indicating that its expression is time-dependent and influenced by circadian rhythms. Their findings support the idea that up regulation of TNFRSF11B plays a crucial role in bone remodelling.

The transcriptional and translational efficiency of TNFRSF11B is influenced by secondary structures of mRNA, such as hairpins, pseudoknots, and G-quadruplexes, which affect ribosome movement and mRNA stability.30 This may be probable reasons to our findings where cDNA obtained during RT-PCR, exhibited down regulation of OPG protein expression. Other reasons for decreased OPG protein expression could be reduced half life of mRNA which could have encoded less cDNA and thus reduced expression of OPG,as also concluded by Mauger et al.31 who emphasized that RNA quality plays a pivotal role in downstream gene expression studies, ensuring reliable results in qRT-PCR analysis.

qRT-PCR analysis revealed a significant, time-dependent downregulation of OPG expression, with the steepest decline between 5 and 12 h of DC stimulation. By 24 h, OPG levels were markedly suppressed (p ≤ 0.0001), indicating that prolonged electrical stimulation strongly affects gene regulation. These findings align with Mobini et al.23 and Chaudhari et al.,26 who also reported altered gene expression and reduced OPG mRNA under DC stimulation. As Presnyk et al.32 suggested, non-optimal coding may reduce mRNA stability and protein output. This downregulation may disrupt the RANK/RANKL/OPG axis and impact osteoblast-to-osteocyte differentiation, as highlighted by Prideaux et al.33

The observed down-regulation of OPG expression highlights potential applications in orthodontics. Bone is a dynamic and metabolically active tissue that constantly undergoes re-modelling. This process involves the breakdown of old bone by osteoclasts, followed by the creation of new bone by osteoblasts, primarily through the mechanisms of resorption and formation.11 Modulating OPG expression can influence osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption.

Limitations

While this study included bioelectric modulation of bone remodelling, however it included few limitations too. Firstly the study was conducted on single osteosarcoma-derived cell line (SaOS-2), which does not fully appreciate the in vivo bone micro environment. Secondly, only one biomarker OPG was evaluated while other regulators were not included such as RANK L, limiting scope of the study. Additionally, localized cell death near electrodes suggests that electrode composition, current density gradients, or electrochemical byproducts may have introduced confounding artefacts.

Future directions

The finding of this study opens avenues for using DC stimulation to alter bone remodelling in clinical scenarios, potentially enhancing orthodontic treatment efficiency by facilitating controlled bone resorption and promoting faster tooth movement. It also aims to enhance patient outcomes by alleviating stress, boosting compliance, and shortening treatment durations in orthodontic procedures. However, further research is warranted to explore gene-level alterations in RANKL and other proteins involved in osteoclast differentiation, to fully harness the therapeutic potential of bioelectric stimulation in orthodontic and bone remodelling treatments.
