Myofascial Release Techniques as an Adjunct Treatment in Individuals With Frozen Shoulder: A Systematic Review
Authors: Farnaz Farshchi, Mahzad Farshchi, Mobina Choobkar, Mehrnoosh Pourarshad, Nader Farshchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimFrozen shoulder is characterized by pain and progressive restriction of shoulder range of motion (ROM), leading to functional limitations. Myofascial release (MFR) techniques are commonly used in clinical practice, mainly as an adjunct to conventional physiotherapy, yet their effectiveness in frozen shoulder remains unclear. This systematic review aimed to evaluate the effects of MFR, primarily as an adjunct intervention, on pain, functional disability, and shoulder ROM in individuals with frozen shoulder.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in PubMed, ScienceDirect, Scopus, Google Scholar, ProQuest, and Web of Science up to November 2025. Eligible studies investigated the effects of MFR in individuals with frozen shoulder. Pain, functional disability, and shoulder ROM were extracted as primary outcomes. Methodological quality was assessed using a modified Downs and Black checklist.ResultsEleven studies met the inclusion criteria. Participants (n = 383) were aged between 22 and 70 years across studies. Only 3 of 11 trials explicitly reported clinical stage or stage‐related descriptors; most trials did not specify the freezing/frozen/thawing stage. The methodological quality of the included studies ranged from moderate to high, with no studies classified as having low methodological quality. Most studies applied MFR as an adjunct to conventional physiotherapy, while a smaller number used MFR as a stand‐alone intervention. Adjunct MFR to conventional therapy generally demonstrated greater improvements in pain, functional disability, and shoulder ROM (most commonly abduction and external rotation) compared with conventional therapy alone or pre‐intervention values.ConclusionInterventions incorporating MFR appear to be beneficial in reducing pain, improving functional ability, and increasing shoulder ROM in individuals with frozen shoulder, particularly when used as an adjunct to conventional physiotherapy or exercise programs. Evidence for stand‐alone MFR is limited, as the majority of available studies have implemented MFR in combination with other treatments.

Introduction

The term “capsulitis,” commonly known as “frozen shoulder,” refers to a prevalent shoulder condition characterized by a global restriction in the shoulder's range of motion (ROM), following a specific capsular pattern [1]. In the glenohumeral joint, this pattern is defined by a greater limitation of external rotation, followed by abduction and then internal rotation, reflecting capsular tightness and fibrosis of the joint capsule [2]. Frozen shoulder affects approximately 2–5% of the general population; many studies report a higher prevalence in women compared to men, although the magnitude of this difference varies between studies [3, 4]. This condition typically develops in individuals aged 40–70 years [1]. Pathologically, frozen shoulder is associated with synovial inflammation, thickening and fibrosis of the joint capsule, and the development of adhesions within the subacromial or subdeltoid bursa. Adhesions may also involve the long head of the biceps tendon and contribute to obliteration of the axillary fold. Consequently, patients experience a gradual and progressive loss of both active and passive ROM in the glenohumeral joint [5].

The myofascial system is a continuous network of connective tissue that envelops muscles, bones, and neurovascular structures and contributes to force transmission and movement coordination [6]. Alterations in fascial structure or tension may produce stiffness, altered biomechanics, and pain through both mechanical and neurophysiological pathways [7, 8]. Myofascial trigger points within shoulder girdle muscles (e.g., subscapularis) have been implicated in restricted shoulder ROM and pain in frozen shoulder [9, 10].

There are several conservative approaches available to treat frozen shoulder, such as strengthening exercises, stretching, proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation (PNF), and mobilization techniques aimed at relieving pain and improving the ROM in the glenohumeral joint. One significant technique is myofascial release (MFR) or soft tissue manipulation, which effectively addresses soft tissue dysfunction by alleviating tightness and restrictions to promote efficient movement [11]. In MFR, pressure is applied to the muscles and fascia to soften and stretch the myofascial complex, with the intention of restoring optimal length, relieving pain, and enhancing function. This technique improves the viscoelastic properties of the muscle associated with trigger points, thereby positively affecting the biomechanics of shoulder motion, resulting in less pain and improved functionality. Research has shown that MFR can significantly enhance the mechanical properties of muscles in individuals suffering from chronic shoulder pain [9].

In recent years, several systematic reviews have examined the effectiveness of MFR for spinal conditions, such as low back and neck pain [12, 13, 14, 15]. For frozen shoulder, however, the available evidence remains limited. Although a systematic review by Ughreja et al. [16] investigated myofascial trigger points in adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder), their review included only four studies and focused on a broad range of “release‐based” interventions—such as dry needling, subscapular nerve block, and the Niel–Asher technique—rather than on manual MFR techniques specifically. Furthermore, several clinical trials on manual MFR for frozen shoulder have been published after 2019, which were not captured in the review by Ughreja et al. [16]. Given these differences in scope and eligibility criteria, there is still no systematic review dedicated exclusively to manual MFR in individuals with frozen shoulder. Recognizing this gap, the present review aims to comprehensively collect and analyze studies that investigate the effects of manual MFR on pain, functional disability, and shoulder ROM in patients with frozen shoulder.

Methods

Study Protocol and Registration

This review protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42025637106). It was conducted using the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome (PICO) procedure and included all relevant published articles from the inception of the search process up to November 2025. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines were followed to complete the procedure.

Databases and Search Strategy

Electronic databases such as Science Direct, PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar, ProQuest, and Web of Science were utilized to carry out the bibliographic search without language restrictions. Gray literature sources, including theses and conference papers indexed within ProQuest, Scopus, and ISI Web of Science, were also screened as part of the search process. The search keywords for this review, along with Boolean operators, were as follows: (“myofascial release” OR “muscle manipulation” OR “myofascial therapy” OR “fascial manipulation” OR “trigger point therapy” OR “deep tissue massage” OR “active release technique” OR “myofascial treatment”) AND (“adhesive capsulitis” OR “frozen shoulder” OR “adhesive capsulitides” OR “adhesive shoulder capsulitis”) AND (“pain” OR “function” OR “functional disability” OR “range of motion” OR “kinematics”).

Eligibility Criteria

The inclusion criteria for selecting articles were as follows [1]: participants must be individuals diagnosed with frozen shoulder [2], the intervention must involve MFR delivered either as a stand‐alone intervention or as an adjunct to conventional physiotherapy, exercise therapy, or other conservative treatments [3], the studies must compare MFR with a pre‐intervention [4], the studies must compare real MFR with either sham MFR, conventional therapy, or exercise therapy [5], experimental designs must include randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or quasi‐experimental studies, and [6] outcomes must relate to pain, shoulder ROM, and functional disability. Given that MFR is most commonly applied alongside conventional physiotherapy in clinical practice, both stand‐alone and adjunct applications were considered eligible. Studies that involved other types of interventions, such as surgery or treatments other than MFR, as well as studies focusing on conditions other than frozen shoulder, were excluded from this review.

Screening and Selection

A search was initially conducted using the PubMed database, and the search strategy was subsequently adjusted for other databases (Supporting Information S1: Appendix 1). Two researchers independently carried out the search process. After removing duplicates with EndNote software, the titles and abstracts were evaluated for eligibility by the same two researchers. Following this, the full texts of the eligible studies were obtained. The two researchers then began the paper selection procedure according to the established inclusion criteria. In the event of any disagreements between the researchers, two additional researchers were brought in to make the final decision.

Quality and Risk of Bias Assessment

The methodological quality (risk of bias) of the included studies was independently evaluated by two authors using an adapted version of the Downs and Black checklist. The checklist was modified to better suit the objectives and design of the present review, as some items in the original instrument were not fully applicable to the characteristics of the included studies. The adapted tool consisted of 17 items (Table 1), including eight items related to reporting (items 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 9, and 10), one item assessing external validity (item 11), four items evaluating internal validity (items 14, 15, 18, and 20), three items addressing internal validity related to confounding (items 22, 23, and 26), and one item assessing statistical power (item 27). Each item was scored as “yes” (1 point) or “no/unable to determine” (0 points). Based on criteria commonly applied in previous systematic reviews using this tool [17, 18], studies were categorized as high methodological quality when scores were ≥ 75% of the maximum score, moderate quality when scores ranged between 50% and 74%, and low methodological quality when scores were < 50%.

Results

Studies Characteristics

A flowchart for the selection of studies based on PRISMA guidelines is presented in Figure 1. The search strategy was conducted without language restrictions; however, all eligible studies retrieved through the search were published in English. The final evaluation included 11 articles involving 383 participants suffering from frozen shoulder. A list of excluded full‐text studies is presented in Supporting Information S1: Appendix 1, along with the reason for exclusion. Across the 11 included studies, participant ages ranged from 22 to 70 years. Three studies explicitly reported clinical stage or stage‐related descriptors (Das et al. [27]: stage II; Makwana et al. [21]: stages I–II; Naz et al. [23]: sub‐acute). Several other trials reported participant features such as unilateral [23, 24, 25] vs bilateral [22] involvement or diabetic etiology (e.g., Challey et al. [22] enrolled bilateral diabetic cases) or duration of symptoms for at least 3 months [19, 24, 26, 27, 28, 29], but did not assign a formal freezing/frozen/thawing stage. Because only 3/11 studies reported stage in a comparable manner, we could not stratify outcomes by frozen‐shoulder stage.

Flow diagram of the study selection based on PRISMA.

The methodological quality scores of the included studies ranged from 10 to 15 out of a possible 17 points. According to the predefined thresholds, nine studies with scores ≥ 75% of the maximum score were classified as high quality [19, 21, 22, 23, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29] and two studies with scores between 50% and 74% as moderate quality [20, 24]. No study was categorized as having low methodological quality based on the predefined quality thresholds. Eight of the papers employed a randomized, parallel‐group controlled design, where one group received MFR therapy while the control group underwent conventional therapy, exercise therapy, or other types of soft tissue release interventions [19, 21, 22, 23, 26, 27, 29]. Additionally, three quasi‐experimental studies compared the treatment outcomes of MFR in individuals with frozen shoulder to matched asymptomatic controls [28], or they involved non‐random allocation of participants into experimental and control groups [20, 24]. We excluded case studies due to a high risk of bias [30, 31]. Out of eight RCT studies, two incorporated assessor's blinding [25, 29] and two used patients' blinding [26, 27], while the remaining RCT studies did not implement any blinding [19, 21, 22, 23]. The treatment duration for MFR varied among the studies, ranging from one to twelve sessions. In general, the MFR technique targeted several muscles, including the deltoid, pectoralis major and minor, trapezius, infraspinatus, teres major, levator scapulae, serratus anterior, and subscapularis.

Notably, almost all included studies applied MFR in combination with conventional physiotherapy or another therapeutic intervention. The only exceptions were the studies by Naz [23] and Neha [29], which evaluated MFR as a standalone intervention and compared it directly to the muscle energy technique and Cyriax soft tissue release, respectively.

The outcome measures included pain, shoulder function, and shoulder ROM. Pain was most commonly assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) or the Numeric Pain Rating Scale (NPRS), and in some studies, pain sensitivity was also evaluated using the Pain Pressure Threshold (PPT). Shoulder function was evaluated using validated patient‐reported outcome measures such as the Shoulder Pain and Disability Index (SPADI), the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) questionnaire, or the Shoulder Disability Questionnaire (SDQ). ROM was measured clinically by a goniometer in most studies and, in a few studies, by functional tests such as the Apley Scratch Test, and in one study by the Liberty electromagnetic tracking system (Table 2).

To provide a clearer representation of treatment effects, when possible, Table 2 summarizes the pre‐ and post‐intervention values, between‐group differences, and reported p‐values across the included studies. A summary of the results from these studies is as follows:

Effect of MFR on Pain

The pain outcomes were assessed in all included studies. Comparing MFR treatment to the pre‐intervention state, all studies demonstrated a significant reduction in pain after a period of MFR treatment in patients with frozen shoulder [19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 26, 27, 28, 29]. When MFR was used in conjunction with other treatments, such as conventional therapy (ultrasound + TENS) [19, 20, 25], supervised exercises, and home exercise programs [26], it proved more effective in relieving pain than any of these treatments used alone. However, one study indicated that both conventional physiotherapy and MFR combined with conventional physiotherapy were equally effective in improving pain for patients with frozen shoulder [27]. It is noteworthy that in the majority of these trials, MFR was applied as an adjunct modality rather than a stand‐alone intervention; therefore, improvements in pain may reflect the combined therapeutic effect of MFR and conventional physiotherapy.

In comparisons between MFR and other manual interventions, only one study evaluated both techniques as stand‐alone treatments, showing that stand‐alone MFR was more effective than stand‐alone muscle energy technique in reducing pain in individuals with frozen shoulder [23]. Regarding proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation (PNF), one study—where both PNF and the comparator intervention were delivered alongside conventional physiotherapy—reported that PNF was more effective than MFR in alleviating pain [24]. Two studies compared MFR with Cyriax soft tissue release; however, their treatment formats differed. In one trial, both techniques were applied as stand‐alone interventions, and MFR demonstrated superior pain reduction [29]. In contrast, another trial combined both Cyriax soft tissue release and MFR with conventional physiotherapy, and this study reported better outcomes for Cyriax soft tissue release [21]. These differences in treatment format (stand‐alone vs. adjunct) may partly explain the conflicting findings between studies.

Effect of MFR on Shoulder Function

The shoulder function outcomes were assessed in eight studies [19, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 29]. All studies demonstrated significant improvement in functional disability of the shoulder after a period of MFR treatment in patients with frozen shoulder when comparing results to their pre‐intervention state. When comparing MFR combined with conventional treatment to conventional treatment alone, three studies indicated that adding MFR as an adjunct to conventional treatment may provide better benefits for shoulder function in these patients [19, 20, 25]. Conversely, one study found that both conventional physiotherapy alone and MFR combined with conventional physiotherapy were equally effective in improving functional disability in patients with frozen shoulder [27].

Regarding the comparison between MFR and other manual techniques, one study that compared stand‐alone MFR with stand‐alone muscle energy technique reported that MFR produced greater improvements in functional disability in individuals with frozen shoulder [23]. Another study evaluated both MFR and Cyriax soft tissue release as stand‐alone interventions and found that MFR resulted in superior reductions in disability scores [29]. Additionally, one study compared PNF with MFR, with both techniques delivered alongside conventional physiotherapy, and reported that PNF was more effective in enhancing shoulder function [24]. Differences in whether the interventions were administered as stand‐alone treatments or as adjuncts to physiotherapy may partly explain the variability in findings across studies.

Effect of MFR on Shoulder ROM

The shoulder ROM outcomes were assessed in all studies [19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29], except for one study [24]. Across the included trials, ROM outcomes were presented for multiple movement directions, including flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, internal rotation, and external rotation. Although the specific set of movements varied between studies, the most commonly assessed directions were abduction and external rotation, reflecting their functional relevance and typical restriction patterns in frozen shoulder. The direction‐specific pre–post changes and between‐group differences for each study have been provided in the Results column of Table 2.

Across trials, MFR delivered as an adjunct to conventional physiotherapy [19, 20, 25] or supervised exercises and home exercise programs [26] generally produced larger direction‐specific ROM gains than conventional therapy alone or pre‐intervention values. Improvements were consistently observed across several planes of motion, with gains frequently reported in external rotation, abduction, and flexion, while internal rotation and extension also showed measurable but more variable changes. The magnitude of these improvements differed between studies, which likely reflects variation in intervention protocols and comparator groups. In contrast, one study indicated that both conventional physiotherapy alone and MFR combined with conventional physiotherapy were equally effective for the improvement of shoulder ROM in patients with frozen shoulder [27].

In studies evaluating stand‐alone interventions, the only trial directly comparing isolated techniques showed that stand‐alone MFR produced greater improvements in shoulder ROM—particularly in movements such as abduction and external rotation—than stand‐alone muscle energy technique [23]. Two additional studies compared MFR with Cyriax soft tissue release, although their intervention structures differed. When both techniques were applied as stand‐alone interventions, MFR resulted in larger ROM gains across several directions of movement [29]. In contrast, when the two techniques were delivered as adjuncts to conventional physiotherapy, the combination including Cyriax soft tissue release demonstrated superior ROM improvements [21]. These inconsistent findings likely reflect differences in whether the manual techniques were applied alone or integrated with standard physiotherapy, which may influence their relative effectiveness.

Discussion

Deep muscle dysfunction and alterations in fascial structure can contribute to shoulder pain and impaired mobility. Changes within the myofascial system—which normally provides structural support and load distribution—may reduce its protective function and predispose individuals to dysfunction. In recent years, the clinical application of MFR has grown substantially, with an increasing number of studies exploring its role in musculoskeletal rehabilitation [12, 13, 14, 15].

The overall findings of this review indicate that MFR, particularly when used as an adjunct to conventional physiotherapy, is associated with improvements in pain, functional disability, and shoulder ROM. The underlying mechanisms proposed for these effects include both mechanical and neurophysiological pathways. Mechanically, MFR may facilitate reorganization of fascial collagen, reduce soft‐tissue stiffness, and restore the extensibility of restricted structures [32]. Neurophysiologically, stimulation of afferent pathways—including A‐delta fibers—and activation of descending inhibitory systems may contribute to analgesia, while modulation of fascial mechanoreceptors, such as Ruffini endings, may reduce sympathetic tone and enhance muscle relaxation [33, 34]. These mechanisms align with reported improvements in pain and ROM in other musculoskeletal conditions, including low back pain [12, 13] and chronic neck pain [14, 35]. It should be noted that in most of the included studies, MFR was not used as a standalone intervention but rather as part of a multimodal rehabilitation program that also included conventional physiotherapy modalities such as therapeutic exercises, ultrasound, TENS, or joint mobilization. Therefore, the improvements observed in pain, ROM, and functional outcomes may reflect the combined therapeutic effects of these interventions. Only two studies [23, 29] evaluated MFR as an isolated treatment, which limits the ability to attribute the observed clinical improvements specifically to MFR alone. Therefore, the current evidence supports MFR primarily as a complementary technique within multimodal rehabilitation programs.

When comparing MFR with other manual therapies, the available evidence remains mixed and appears to depend strongly on the intervention format. For example, in two studies that evaluated MFR and Cyriax soft tissue release using different delivery models, stand‐alone MFR produced greater improvements in pain, disability, and ROM [29], whereas Cyriax outperformed MFR when both were combined with conventional physiotherapy [21]. These divergent results likely reflect differences in treatment context—stand‐alone versus adjunct application—as well as variations in dosage and duration of MFR, factors known to influence the responsiveness of soft tissues [36].

Similarly, the limited evidence comparing MFR with other manual techniques provides mixed but informative insights. The only study evaluating MFR against PNF suggested that PNF may offer greater improvements in pain and functional outcomes under certain conditions [24], possibly due to its neuromuscular activation and stretching components that facilitate immediate functional gains [37]. In contrast, the study by Naz et al. directly compared stand‐alone MFR with stand‐alone muscle energy technique and found MFR to be more effective in reducing pain, improving functional disability, and enhancing shoulder ROM [23]. This superiority may be partly explained by MFR's combined mechanical and neurophysiological mechanisms, including remodeling of collagen fibers, reduction of fascial stiffness, and normalization of fascial tension — as shown in systematic reviews and biomechanical studies of MFR effects on ROM and tissue flexibility [38, 39]. However, because both the PNF and muscle energy technique comparisons are each based on a single study, the current evidence is insufficient to determine the relative efficacy of MFR versus these techniques, underscoring the need for further high‐quality trials. Importantly, none of the included studies directly compared MFR with corticosteroid injections, a commonly used medical intervention in the management of frozen shoulder. This gap in the existing literature limits our ability to contextualize the relative clinical value of MFR compared with standard medical care. Future trials should therefore include direct comparisons between MFR and corticosteroid injection to clarify their comparative effectiveness

MFR is designed to influence both the biomechanical properties and the neurobiological behavior of fascia [39]. By reducing soft‐tissue cross‐links, increasing tissue extensibility, and modulating autonomic activity through mechanoreceptor stimulation [40], MFR may help alleviate pain and restore shoulder mobility. These effects may be further enhanced when MFR is combined with active exercise therapy, supporting the clinical practice of integrating manual therapy with targeted rehabilitation programs.

Although a meta‐analysis was initially considered, substantial heterogeneity across the included trials rendered quantitative pooling inappropriate. Outcome measures varied widely, with different instruments used to assess pain, disability, and ROM, creating non‐comparable scoring structures. Pain intensity was assessed using tools such as the VAS, NPRS, or PPT, while functional disability was evaluated using questionnaires including the SPADI, DASH, and the SDQ. Although these instruments assess related constructs, they differ in scaling methods and clinical interpretation, which complicates direct comparison of results across studies. Similarly, shoulder ROM was measured using different approaches, including goniometry, Apley's scratch test, and electromagnetic tracking systems. These methodological differences may contribute to variability in reported outcomes and should be considered when comparing results across studies. Intervention protocols also differed in terms of whether MFR was used alone or in combination with other physiotherapy modalities, the anatomical regions treated, session frequency, and total treatment dose. Control groups incorporated a broad range of comparators—including conventional physiotherapy, Cyriax techniques, muscle energy technique, and PNF—further limiting clinical homogeneity. Additional variability related to patient characteristics (e.g., clinical stage, unilateral vs. bilateral involvement, diabetic vs. idiopathic origin) also contributed to methodological inconsistency. Given these factors, a narrative synthesis provided the most rigorous and transparent approach for interpreting the evidence. Future studies should aim to standardize outcome instruments, clearly report direction‐specific ROM, and provide complete statistical data to enable meaningful quantitative synthesis.

An important source of clinical heterogeneity among the included studies was the limited reporting of frozen shoulder stage. Only three [21, 23, 27] of the eleven included studies clearly described the clinical stage of the condition. This is a relevant methodological issue because frozen shoulder progresses through distinct phases (freezing, frozen, and thawing), each characterized by different levels of pain, inflammation, and range‐of‐motion restriction. Consequently, the response to rehabilitation interventions such as manual therapy or MFR may vary depending on the stage of the disorder. The absence of consistent stage reporting therefore, limits the ability to interpret the observed treatment effects and reduces the clinical applicability of the findings. Without stage‐specific data, it remains unclear whether the improvements reported in the included studies are equally relevant across all phases of the condition.

Limitations

‐ Although most included trials demonstrated moderate to high methodological quality, several studies lacked adequate blinding procedures. This limitation may introduce potential bias, particularly for subjective outcomes such as pain and functional disability, and should therefore be considered when interpreting the certainty of the evidence.

‐ Diagnostic criteria for frozen shoulder were not consistently reported across the included studies, and many relied primarily on clinical presentation, which may introduce diagnostic heterogeneity.

‐ The MFR technique varied across different studies.

‐ In most included studies, MFR was combined with other physiotherapy interventions, making it difficult to determine the independent effect of MFR.

‐ There was no comparison made between genders within the research.

‐ The absence of follow‐up after the completion of MFR treatment sessions makes it difficult to assess the long‐term effects of the treatment.

‐ The self‐MFR technique, which is initially demonstrated by a therapist, is clinically relevant because many individuals can perform it as part of home‐based programs. However, no studies have examined self‐MFR in individuals with frozen shoulder, highlighting the need for future research in this area.

‐ Most included studies did not specify the clinical stage of frozen shoulder, preventing stage‐specific analysis of MFR effectiveness.

Therefore, future studies should address these limitations to improve the methodological quality and interpretability of evidence regarding MFR in frozen shoulder.

Conclusion

The available evidence suggests that MFR techniques appear to be effective in improving pain, functional disability, and shoulder ROM in individuals with frozen shoulder. Importantly, most studies implemented MFR as an adjunct to conventional physiotherapy, and this combined approach consistently produced superior outcomes compared with conventional therapy alone. Only limited evidence is available for stand‐alone MFR. Currently, there is insufficient evidence to support the superiority of other techniques, such as PNF or Cyriax soft tissue release techniques, over the MFR technique. However, the certainty of the current evidence should be interpreted with caution due to methodological limitations in several included studies, including the limited use of blinding procedures. Future clinical trials should also clearly report the clinical stage of frozen shoulder and consider stage‐specific analyses to improve the clinical applicability of findings.
