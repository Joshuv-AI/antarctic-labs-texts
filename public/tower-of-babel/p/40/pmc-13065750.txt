Unveiling the hidden viral biodiversity and potential ecological functions with global coral holobiont virome database
Authors: Mengjie Wu, Xiaobin Wen, Shuchen Liu, Huu Hao Ngo, Zhonghua Cai, Jin Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Viruses are integral yet underexplored components of coral holobionts, with their roles in shaping microbial diversity, modulating symbioses, and contributing to Darwin’s paradox remaining largely unresolved. Here, we present the Global Coral Holobiont Virome Database (GCHVD), encompassing 76,755 viral contigs and 36,860 unique viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs) identified from 36 coral species across 18 regions worldwide. The virome is dominated by Uroviricota, Nucleocytoviricota, Preplasmiviricota, and Artverviricota, with lytic lifestyles prevailing. Host identity emerged as the primary determinant of viral community structure, exerting a stronger influence than geographic factors. Extensive virus–microbe interaction networks revealed that viruses enhance biogeochemical cycling by augmenting host metabolic processes. Functional profiling uncovered a diverse repertoire of auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs) associated with P, Fe, S, N, and CH₄ metabolism. Moreover, controlled microcosm experiments demonstrated that viral addition reshapes microbial community composition, enhances diversity, and drives elemental cycling within the holobiont. Together, these findings establish viruses as previously overlooked regulators of coral symbioses, orchestrating microbial dynamics, fueling nutrient fluxes, and sustaining reef productivity. Our work provides new insights into resolving Darwin’s paradox from a viral ecological perspective.

Introduction

Viruses are nearly ubiquitous across ecosystems and play pivotal roles in shaping global biogeochemical cycles. By exerting top-down control on microbial community structure and function, they regulate ecological dynamics in habitats ranging from marine and soil systems to the rhizosphere, coastal zones, and ruminant environments1–5. These effects occur primarily through lytic infection and temperate conversion6, enabling viruses to restructure microbial assemblages and stabilize host–microbe networks. Mechanistically, viruses modulate host metabolism and ecosystem processes via horizontal gene transfer (HGT)7 and the expression of viral auxiliary metabolic genes (vAMGs)8,9. For instance, Caudovirales regulate α-Proteobacteria metabolism by influencing DNA methylation and bacterial–eukaryotic interactions, including commensal/mutualistic symbiosis, effector-mediated crosstalk, and immune modulation, between coral holobiont bacteria and eukaryotic partners (coral host and Symbiodiniaceae)10. While vAMGs broadly reprogram host metabolic pathways, reshaping fluxes of C, N, P, S, and CH₄ and thereby sustaining ecosystem functions11,12. With advances in sequencing technologies and computational frameworks, virome research has expanded to ecosystems beyond the traditional focus, including coral reefs10,13.

Coral reefs are ecological hubs that host diverse microorganisms, algae, bacteria, fungi, archaea, and viruses, within intricate symbiotic networks13. This diversity underpins reef productivity, as complementary metabolic capacities accelerate recycling and transformation of both macro- and trace elements14. Rapid nutrient turnover supports highly efficient trophic webs, directly linking biodiversity with ecosystem function15. Historically, this coupling was framed as “Darwin’s Paradox,” which highlighted the contradiction between the extraordinary biodiversity and productivity of coral reefs and their persistence in oligotrophic waters16–18. However, recent oceanographic assessments have reframed this paradox19, showing that many reefs occur in mesotrophic rather than oligotrophic settings and that microbial turnover is far more dynamic than previously recognized. Thus, high reef productivity arises from accelerated microbial metabolism and nutrient recycling within symbiotic networks, rather than contradicting nutrient scarcity19. Within the holobiont, bacteria and zooxanthellae have well-established roles: bacteria facilitate nitrogen fixation and organic matter decomposition, while zooxanthellae supply photosynthetically derived carbohydrates20,21. In contrast, viruses, despite being recognized as master regulators of microbial turnover and biogeochemical cycling, remain underexplored in reef systems. Their influence may reinforce or even redefine current ecological theories, yet their omission leaves a critical gap in understanding reef productivity and fully resolving Darwin’s Paradox.

For decades, investigations into coral-associated viruses were constrained by methodological limitations, including difficulties in viral isolation, shallow sequencing depth, incomplete functional annotation, and the underrepresentation of coral viral genomes in reference databases10. These bottlenecks hindered assessments of viral contributions to holobiont functional diversity22,23. Recently, however, the field has been transformed by bioinformatics pipelines tailored for viral analysis (e.g., CheckV24, VirSorter225, VIBRANT26, and DeepVirFinder27) and expanding genomic repositories (e.g., Efam28), enabling robust viral detection in metagenomic datasets. Consequently, the functional and community-level characterization of coral viromes has emerged as a research frontier29–31. Early studies reveal that coral-associated viruses are remarkably diverse and ecologically specific, with the potential to shape microbial communities and mediate nutrient cycling10,14,29. Notably, phages infecting Vibrio mediterranean have been isolated from coral mucus, and cultivation–metagenomic approaches have uncovered molecular mechanisms of virus–host interactions29. At a global scale, analyses of coral–bacteria–phage interactions reveal genomic evidence for nested symbioses among these partners10. Yet systematic evaluations of viral biodiversity, host interactions, and vAMG-mediated functions within coral holobionts remain limited32, and experimental validation of predicted functions is scarce33,34. Thus, comprehensive mapping of coral-associated viruses, their distribution, diversity, life strategies, and functional capacities, is urgently needed to understand how viral regulation underpins reef productivity and resilience, and to resolve Darwin’s Paradox.

In this study, we hypothesize that coral viruses, through their diversity and host-specific interactions, are central to structuring microbial communities and driving key biogeochemical processes. To test this, we combined large-scale analyses of global coral omics datasets with controlled microcosm experiments. Specifically, our work: (1) established the Global Coral Holobiont Virome Database (GCHVD) to define the composition, diversity, and functional potential of coral viromes; (2) quantified the relative influence of host identity, geography, and viral lifestyle strategies (temperate or lytic) on viral community assembly; (3) investigated the roles of vAMGs in regulating biogeochemical cycling and productivity within coral symbioses; and (4) conducted mesoscale viral exposure experiments to directly assess viral impacts on symbiont community structure and function. Together, these efforts provide a theoretical foundation for understanding how viruses regulate coral holobiont dynamics and sustain high reef productivity, offering a mechanistic resolution of the long-standing coral reef productivity paradox from a viral perspective.

Results

Overview of the coral viromes

GCHVD was constructed from 513 publicly available datasets, comprising 460 metagenomes and 53 viromes from 36 coral species across 18 regions (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Fig. 1; Supplementary Data 1–2). High-quality contigs ( ≥ 5 kb) obtained from multi-sample assemblies yielded 76,755 viral contigs. Clustering at 95% average nucleotide identity (ANI) produced 36,860 unique vOTUs. Of these, ~37.44% could be assigned to known viral taxa (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Data 3), spanning four major phyla: Uroviricota, Nucleocytoviricota, Preplasmiviricota, and Artverviricota (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Data 3). Among classified vOTUs, 14.45% were resolved to the family level (Supplementary Fig. 2A) and 12.80% to the genus level (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Notably, Metaviridae (Metavirus) accounted for a large fraction of taxonomic diversity within coral holobionts (Supplementary Fig. 2). In addition, 1588 vOTUs were identified as nucleocytoplasmic large DNA viruses (NCLDVs), and 69 as Lavidaviridae (virophages; Supplementary Data 3). To examine viral survival strategies, potential lifestyles were inferred for vOTUs. Across all samples, lytic viruses (67.72%) were significantly more abundant than temperate viruses (32.28%) (P = 2.1e-13, Fig. 1C and Supplementary Data 4). This lytic dominance persisted across healthy, bleached, and diseased states (Supplementary Fig. 5). To identify host targets of lysis and lysogeny, we compared lysogeny ratios (LyRs) in coral-associated bacteria and archaea. Overall, LyRs did not differ significantly between archaea and bacteria (Fig. 1D). However, taxon-specific patterns emerged: 55% of bacterial classes exhibited complete lysogeny, compared to only 20% of archaeal classes (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Data 5). In bacterial taxa, Desulfobacteria, Planctomycetia, Clostridia, and Spirochaetia exhibited higher LyRs, whereas Bipolaricaulia, Humimicrobiia, and Thermoleophilia exhibited lower values. For archaea, Methanosarcinia, Poseidoniia, and Nanoarchaeia displayed relatively high LyRs, in contrast to Thorarchaeia and Nitrososphaeria, which showed lower values (Supplementary Fig. 6; Supplementary Data 5). It should be noted that, as described by Trubl and Probst35, some viruses classified as lytic might actually be temperate viruses, as technical limitations in the detection of integration genes prevent the full identification of temperate viruses, leading to their potential misclassification.

To recover complete viral genomes, vOTU quality was assessed using CheckV24. vOTUs were generated by clustering viral contigs with 95% nucleotide identity, where one vOTU represents a group of homologous viral contigs. A total of 4355 vOTUs (corresponding to 76,755 contigs) were at least 50% complete, including 1217 complete genomes (Fig. 1E; Supplementary Data 6). Of all vOTUs in the dataset, 11.81% achieved ≥50% completeness; notably, the contigs corresponding to these high-completeness vOTUs accounted for 27.95% of all contigs with ≥50% completeness (Fig. 1F). Most of these high-completeness genomes ( ≥ 50% completeness) exceeded 10 kb in size. Notably, to ensure comprehensive coverage of the coral virome, we also retained and analyzed small viral contigs that met our predefined screening criteria: linear contigs ≥5 kb or circular contigs ≥1.5 kb, with all candidates harboring at least one conserved viral hallmark gene (e.g., capsid, terminase, or integrase).

Coral virome diversity and biogeographic patterns across latitudinal gradients

To investigate geographical influences on virus–microbe community assembly, GCHVD samples were stratified into low-, mid-, and high-latitude groups. Prior to downstream analyses, we performed binning on metagenomic assembled sequences of each sample using MetaBAT2 (default parameters)36, followed by contamination and completeness assessment of bins via CheckM37 for filtering. The filtered bins were then subjected to dereplication with dRep (parameters: -sa 0.95 -comp 40 -conn 20 -nc 0.30)38 to obtain non-redundant bins, from which microbial operational taxonomic units (mOTUs) were ultimately defined. Meanwhile, viral open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted, and all viral genes were clustered with CD-HIT (parameters: -c 0.6 -aS 0.8 -n 4 -g 1) to generate non-redundant viral protein clusters (vPCs); both datasets were integrated into GCHVD as virus-microbe association modules. Rarefaction analyses indicated that richness of viruses (vOTUs), microbes (mOTUs), and viral genes (vPCs) saturated between 100–200 samples, confirming sufficient coverage of diversity (Supplementary Fig. 7). Diversity analyses revealed significant latitude-dependent patterns: α-diversity of vOTUs, mOTUs, and vPCs peaked in mid-latitude regions compared with low- and high-latitude sites (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Fig. 8; P < 0.05). Similarly, β-diversity analyses demonstrated significant latitudinal differences (Stress=0.001, R2 = 0.066, and P = 0.001) for both vOTUs and mOTUs (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Fig. 9). Consistent with these results, microbial community composition also displayed latitudinal structuring: mid- and low-latitude corals were dominated by Bacteroidota and Asgardarchaeota, whereas high-latitude regions were enriched in Pseudomonadota and Thermoplasmatota (Supplementary Fig. 10). At the phylum level, viral composition differed by latitude: Uroviricota dominated high- and mid-latitude regions, whereas Artverviricota prevailed in low-latitude regions (Supplementary Fig. 11). To evaluate whether coral viruses exhibit microbe-like biogeographic patterns, we analyzed latitudinal diversity gradients (LDGs) and distance–decay relationships (DDRs). Viral (vOTUs and vPCs) and microbial (mOTUs) richness peaked at mid-latitudes (Fig. 2C). Biogeographic analyses further highlight viral community dynamics. DDRs revealed parallel patterns in vOTUs, mOTUs, and vPCs, with slope coefficients of similar steepness (Fig. 2D). Venn analyses showed that vOTU richness peaked in mid-latitude regions (73.6%), far surpassing low- (14.1%) and high-latitude (3.1%) regions. This pattern underscores the reduced role of geographic isolation in microbial dispersal—owing to high exchange and mixing across latitudes, yet simultaneously reveals significant endemism in mid-latitudes (Fig. 2E), where viral community structure is most distinct. The distribution of vPCs exhibited a similar pattern with vOTUs (Fig. 2E). Despite these trends, latitude explained only a modest fraction of community variation: 3.1% for mOTUs, 19.4% for vOTUs, and 6.9% for vPCs (Fig. 2F). However, the explanatory power of hosts on community variability was as follows: mOTUs accounted for 45.3%, vOTUs for 81.5%, and vPCs for 73.8% (Supplementary Fig. 13).

To assess cross-habitat associations, we constructed a weighted viral gene network (Supplementary Data 7). This analysis yielded 16,768 viral clusters (VCs), including 4935 from coral reefs, 3743 from marine habitats, 3952 from soil, 3294 from humans, and 844 from RefSeq reference viruses (Fig. 2G; Supplementary Data 8). Among coral reef VCs, 77.79% were exclusive, highlighting strong habitat specificity (Fig. 2H). Of those shared with other environments, 18.89% overlapped with marine, 5.75% with soil, and 0.89% with human habitats. Notably, seven Caudoviricetes VCs were ubiquitous across all habitats, suggesting certain coral viruses may serve cross-habitat ecological roles.

Interactions between coral viruses and their host

From assembled contigs, we recovered 1541 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) comprising 1253 bacterial and 38 archaeal mOTUs (Supplementary Data 9). These groups showed distinct taxonomic, ecological, and health-related profiles (Supplementary Results Text 1 Section I; Supplementary Data 10; Supplementary Fig. 14–15). Virus–host associations between mOTUs and vOTUs were established using a two-step sequence alignment pipeline (CRISPR spacer matching and direct virus–host genome alignment) combined with lowest common ancestor (LCA)39 assignment. This approach identified 32,370 virus–host associations involving 18,973 vOTUs (Supplementary Data 11). Asgardarchaeota was the most frequent predicted host phylum, followed by Cyanobacteriota and Bacteroidota (Fig. 3A). Viral and host abundances were positively correlated at the phylum level (R² = 0.2155, P < 1.708e-9, Supplementary Fig. 16). Across 40 phyla, viral abundances generally exceeded host abundances, with Bdellovibrionota exhibiting the highest virus-to-host ratio (VHR) and Asgardarchaeota the lowest (Fig. 3A). At the virus–host pair level, 16,854 associations showed significant positive correlations (P < 0.05; Fig. 3B), and ~87.2% of pairs displayed VHRs positively linked to host abundance (Fig. 3C). A co-occurrence network, shown in Fig. 3D, further revealed 99.7% of edges reflected positive virus–bacteria/archaea correlations (Spearman’s ρ > 0.6, P < 0.01), indicative of widespread synergistic interactions or niche sharing. Even after stringent filtering ( | ρ | > 0.8, P < 0.001), 36 robust associations persisted. Artverviricota viruses were largely confined to Flavobacteriaceae and Flammeovirgaceae, whereas Uroviricota viruses exhibited broader tropism, infecting both these bacterial families and distant lineages, such as Pseudomonadota and Asgardarchaeota (Fig. 3D; Supplementary Data 12).

To investigate virus–host specificity, we first analyzed the prokaryotic composition of 36 coral species, which revealed significant differences in bacterial and archaeal communities across species (Supplementary Fig. 17; Supplementary Results Text 1 Section II). We then constructed an evolutionary relationship ring diagram to examine viral–host associations. A conserved pattern emerged: homologous viruses across different coral species consistently are inferred to target the same host taxa at the genus level, and all are found to exhibit lytic lifestyles. These associations are derived from sequence-based analyses rather than direct experimental evidence of infection. For example, lytic Bracoviriform infected Acidaminococcaceae in Pocillopora damicornis (PD) and Pachyseris speciosa (PS), while lytic Pandoravirus and Heptavirus targeted Flavobacteriaceae in Leptogorgia sarmentosa (LS) and Acropora cytherea (AC; Fig. 3E). Functional capacities encoded by coral viromes

Of these, 188,448 genes were assigned to known orthologous clusters, with 24.52% remaining functionally undefined (Fig. 4A). Genes related to viral replication and transcription were prominent, particularly those in “Replication, recombination and repair,” “Cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis,” and “Transcription” (Fig. 4A). To assess contributions to biogeochemical cycles, we identified 2,190 vAMGs associated with C, N, P, S, CH4, and Fe metabolism, including 604 high-confidence genes (Supplementary Data 14–15). Collectively, coral vAMGs spanned 19 key metabolic functions (Fig. 4B), with P metabolism (1106 vAMGs) and Fe metabolism (315 vAMGs) dominating. Within P metabolism, phosphate starvation-inducible genes (phoH, 56 vAMGs) were most abundant, complemented by organophosphate hydrolysis (phoA/D/U) and transport (pstS/B/C) genes. Purine- and pyrimidine-related genes were also detected, including purA (adenylate succinate synthase) and thyA (thymidine synthase) (Fig. 4B, D). For Fe metabolism, 192 vAMGs were identified, encompassing transmembrane transport (exbB/D, fecA), ferrous ion transport (feoA/B), regulation (furB), and heme synthesis (hemA/B/C). Beyond P and Fe, 85 carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZyme) vAMGs were detected in C metabolism, including glycosyltransferases (GT4), glycoside hydrolases (GH73), and nagZ/glgA/P, which influence host TCA and pentose phosphate pathways. vAMGs linked to aerobic CH4 oxidation (acs, fwdB, hdrD) were also present, supporting host energy acquisition. Similarly, 11 N-cycle vAMGs were identified, including glnA (organic N metabolism) and nifH/D/K (N fixation). For sulfur cycling, both assimilatory (cysE/K/Q) and dissimilatory (dsrC/E/F) sulfate-reducing genes were recovered (Fig. 4C, D). To further characterize AMGs, vAMGs were mapped to genomic contigs, and representative protein 3D structures were predicted (Supplementary Fig. 18–19; Supplementary Results Text 2).

These nodes interacted with multiple hosts (mainly Bacteroidota, Cyanobacteriota and Pseudomonadota) and were broadly linked to C, N, P, S, CH4, and Fe metabolism. Finally, to evaluate viral distribution and functional heterogeneity across coral species, we profiled viral composition. Most species contained high proportions of classified viruses (up to 65.5%). At the phylum level, Uroviricota and Artverviricota dominated, followed by Cruciviricetes, Fuselloviricota, and Nucleocytoviricota (Supplementary Fig. 20). At the genus level, viral profiles were strongly host-specific (Fig. 5B). Most coral species were dominated by Errantivirus, while Mycedium elephantotus (ME), Acanthastrea echinata (AE), Acropora cytherea (AC), Fungia sp. (FU), and Pachyseris speciosa (PAS) were dominated by Metavirus. In contrast, Sinsheimervirus prevailed in Pocillopora (PO) and Montastraea cavernosa (MC; Fig. 5B).

Network analysis of coral virus–host–AMG interactions revealed Caudoviricetes (3,351 vOTUs) as the dominant viral group across most host species (Fig. 5B, C), which potential mediate key metabolic modulation through targeted associations with host taxa. For instance, sequence-based annotations suggest that Caudoviricetes may target Deferribacterota and Pseudomonadota in Goniastrea edwardsii (GE) and Acropora millepora (AM), potentially modulating Fe metabolism and P cycling via predicted AMGs. In A. millepora, Caudoviricetes may also tend to Desulfobacteraceae and Bacteroidota, which could influence CH₄, S, and N metabolic pathways; in Isopora palifera (IP), they may infect Bacteroidota and Bacillota, with predicted AMGs possibly driving carbohydrate metabolism (Fig. 5C).

Verification of the ecological function of viruses in Acropora digitifera coral

Discussion

The extraordinary diversity of viruses in coral symbiotic systems and their potential ecological impacts is increasingly recognized10,29. Previous studies have sporadically reported coral reef viruses, but these were restricted to a few species or regional scales29,32. Notably, Wallace et al.10 constructed a comprehensive coral viral database using 677 samples, with Caudoviricetes as the dominant taxon and additional viral realms incorporated into the dataset. However, existing resources remain constrained by two key limitations: they either focus on specific viral lineages, or are restricted to regional scales or a narrow range of host coral species. To address the set of limitations and systematically characterize global DNA viral diversity associated with coral symbionts, we compiled 513 published coral metagenomic and viromic datasets (Fig. 1A), identifying 36,860 vOTUs and constructing the GCHVD (Supplementary Data 1–2). To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first comprehensive virome database for coral symbionts. The GCHVD substantially expands the known repertoire of coral-associated viruses and provides a global framework for investigating their diversity, taxonomic structure, and ecological roles across reef habitats. Within the database, we identified viruses from the phyla Uroviricota, Nucleocytoviricota, Preplasmiviricota, and Artverviricota (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Data 3). Most belonged to the class Caudoviricetes, which includes tailed bacterial and archaeal dsDNA viruses with icosahedral capsids10. Remarkably, 62.56% of detected vOTUs (Fig. 1B) could not be assigned to existing viral taxa, consistent with the high proportion of unclassified “viral dark matter” reported in Scleractinia10. Among the remaining 37.44%, genus-level resolution was achieved for 4948 vOTUs. Notably, earlier coral viral studies achieved classification only at the family level10,14. In contrast, our analysis extends resolution to the genus level, identifying major groups, such as Metavirus, Errantivirus, and Bracoviriform (Supplementary Fig. 2B and Supplementary Data 3). This higher-resolution framework expands the taxonomic landscape of coral viruses and enables more refined profiling of viral assemblages within coral symbionts. We acknowledge that the current version of GCHVD focuses primarily on double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) viruses, as our study relied on DNA-based metagenomic sequencing and assembly pipelines optimized for dsDNA virus recovery, with RNA viruses not included. Given that RNA viruses are also a highly significant category, they exert a substantial impact on coral symbiosis stability, microbial community dynamics, and coral health40. In the future, we will integrate metatranscriptomic data to recover RNA viral sequences, enhancing the GCHVD database’s comprehensiveness.

Analyses of the GCHVD revealed that coral-associated viral communities exhibit strong habitat specificity: approximately 77.79% of viral clusters were exclusive to coral reefs, a far greater proportion than those shared with marine non-coral-reef habitats, terrestrial soil habitats, or human-associated environments (Fig. 2F–H; Supplementary Data 7–8). We propose that this specificity is driven by the unique microenvironmental conditions of coral symbiotic systems (Supplementary Discussion Text 1). Community structure analyses further demonstrated strong host dependence in viral composition, coupled with distinct biogeographic patterns along latitudinal gradients (Fig. 2A, B; Supplementary Discussion Text 1). However, latitude explained <20% of community variation (Fig. 2E), supporting previous observations that spatial position alone rarely determines viral distributions41. Instead, coral virome assembly appears to be more strongly shaped by biological factors, including host identity, metabolic traits, and symbiotic interactions than by geographic distance (Fig. 3). These findings underscore that while latitude influences viral assemblages, host–virus interactions are the dominant structuring force. This highlights the necessity of considering host–virus co-structuring processes when investigating the ecological and evolutionary dynamics of coral holobionts10,14.

The relationship between viral and host abundance can be interpreted through the “Kill-the-Winner (KtW)” or “Piggyback-the-Winner (PtW)” models⁶. Marine virome studies generally report temperate viruses as dominant, reflecting an adaptive advantage in low-biomass, high-stress environments¹. In contrast, our relative abundance data reveal a distinct pattern: lytic viruses are predominant in coral viromes (Fig. 1C; Supplementary Data 4), consistent with observations from intertidal systems³⁵. This divergence may stem from the unique symbiotic and nutritional dynamics of coral ecosystems. Studies on soil⁹ and rhizosphere conditions⁸ suggest that nutrient-rich, host-dense environments typically favor temperate viruses, while coral holobionts inhabit oligotrophic microenvironments with regulated host densities. These conditions may tentatively align more closely with KtW dynamics for high-abundance hosts and PtW for low-abundance taxa, though this remains an inference based on relative abundance. Notably, lysogeny showed strong host specificity: archaeal communities (low relative abundance) exhibited high temperate proportions ( > 40%; Fig. 1D; Supplementary Fig. 6; Supplementary Data 5). This pattern is consistent with PtW’s prediction of increased relative lysogeny rate with decreasing host abundance, which may help low-abundance archaeal hosts mitigate risks associated with small population sizes⁶, though this is a speculative interpretation limited by the lack of absolute abundance data. Conversely, dominant bacterial populations had higher lytic proportions, which aligns with the potential viral strategy of exploiting abundant hosts to maximize infection opportunities, and tentatively supports the ecological role of lytic viruses in regulating dominant taxa under KtW dynamics41. Coexistence networks revealed a strong positive correlation between viral and bacterial host relative abundances (Fig. 3D; Supplementary Data 12), providing further correlative evidence for these patterns. Moving forward, methods enabling absolute quantitative analysis at the fine taxonomic level are still required to validate these inferred viral-host interaction patterns and clarify the exact regulatory mechanisms of viral lifecycles in coral holobionts.

Beyond lifestyle strategies, coral viral communities also differ in ecological modes, including specialist versus generalist strategies (Fig. 3D; Supplementary Discussions Text 2). Host–virus associations exhibited coral species-specific patterns, suggesting long-term co-evolutionary coordination among corals, symbionts, and their viruses, reminiscent of oil reservoir systems42. For instance, Montastraea caverosa, enriched in Spirochetes, selected for specialized viruses, whereas Eunicella gazella, dominated by Nanoarchaeota, supported distinct viral assemblages (Fig. 3E). Evolutionary analyses further revealed conserved associations, such as Bracoviriform consistently infecting Acidaminococcaceae across hosts, highlighting stable, long-term interactions14. Similarly, conserved lytic relationships emerged: Pandoravirus in Leptogorgia sarmentosa and Heptavirus in Acropora cytherea both targeted Flavobacteriaceae. Such recurrent strategies suggest that lytic viruses regulate keystone microbial populations, release nutrients, and sustain symbiotic nutrient cycling, paralleling soil phages that relieve nutrient limitation through host lysis. However, because virus–host links here are inferred from relative metagenomic abundances, future studies employing absolute quantification at fine taxonomic resolution are needed to fully resolve viral predation models in complex coral environments.

Coral reefs are often described as “oases in marine deserts,” thriving in nutrient-depleted waters, a paradox termed Darwin’s paradox17 or Darwin’s mystery16. Decades of research have yielded transformative insights into coral biogeochemistry (Supplementary Discussions Text 3), yet many critical questions remain, particularly regarding viral contributions. Recent discoveries demonstrate that viruses regulate biogeochemical cycles in diverse habitats by encoding vAMGs. These genes modulate processes, such as C utilization in global ocean43, N metabolism in oceanic waters44, P cycling in cold seep sediments45, S oxidation in pelagic oceans46, and CH₄ metabolism in groundwater47. In coral ecosystems, microbial communities are often constrained by N, P, and Fe availability13,48, conditions that emphasize the adaptive significance of vAMGs. Our analysis revealed coral viromes encode AMGs spanning multiple elemental cycles, particularly P and Fe metabolism, thus expanding the understanding of nutrient regulation in coral holobionts (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Data 14–15). Notably, 1106 P metabolism-related vAMGs were identified, reflecting the pervasive P limitation in coral microbiomes. Key enriched genes included phoH (phosphate starvation-inducible), pstS (high-affinity phosphate binding), and phoA/D/U (organophosphate hydrolysis), which collectively enhance host P utilization (Fig. 4B, C). PhoH supports host survival under P starvation by regulating energy metabolism, while pstS and its transport partners (pstB/C) facilitate uptake of low-concentration phosphate. Additionally, energy-coupling genes (adk/ppa) bolster ATP synthesis, sustaining viral replication and host basal metabolism under nutrient stress. Previous studies demonstrated that marine P availability influences allocation between hosts and viruses, as viral particles contain proportionally more P than bacteria49. This suggests a mutualistic metabolic adaptation, wherein viral vAMGs enhance host P acquisition, indirectly stabilizing host environments while securing viral reproductive success50,51.

Like phosphorus, iron is a limiting trace element for zooxanthellae photosynthesis and respiratory chains, and its metabolic balance is crucial for coral health52. In this study, we identified 192 iron metabolism-related vAMGs in the coral virome. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first time to fill the gap in the characterization of viral roles in coral holobiont Fe-nutrient. These virally encoded genes including iron ion transmembrane transport genes (exbB/exbD), outer membrane transport genes (fecA), transcriptional regulation gene (fecR), Fe²⁺ uptake genes (feoA/feoB), iron regulation gene (furB), and heme synthesis/metabolism family genes (hemA/B/C/D/K/L/Y) (Fig. 4C, D). Collectively, these genes are inferred to support host iron acquisition, transport, and homeostasis. Heme synthesis–related genes (hemA/B/C) may further optimize porphyrin production, which could sustain respiratory and photosynthetic electron transport chains52. Thus, vAMGs are important to provide supplemental nutrients for limiting elements (P and Fe) in host metabolic pathways (detailed content are described in Supplementary Discussions Text 4).

A central challenge in coral virome research is the reliance on metagenomic inference for AMG function, which carries inherent uncertainties due to the lack of experimental evidence linking viral genes to biological phenotypes53. Our viral exposure experiment using A. digitifera addresses this limitation, representing the first controlled study to validate viral-driven microbial community restructuring and functional relevance in a coral holobiont system. This experiment distinguishes our work from Wallace et al.’s prior study, which focused on identifying virus-host relationships but lacked experimental validation of viral exposure effects in coral ecosystems. By integrating physiological monitoring, 16S rRNA sequencing, and metatranscriptomics, we provide evidence for how coral-associated viruses shape microbiome structure and metabolic function, strengthening the robustness of our findings and addressing the field’s call for caution in AMG interpretation53.

To further establish the role of viruses in the coral holobiont, we conducted virus exposure experiment using A. digitifera as a model species (Fig. 6). This study aimed to verify whether the coral symbiotic microbial communities restructure their structure and function via viral involvement, thereby providing indirect evidence for viral-host-microbial ecological interactions. The results (Fig. 7) suggest that viruses may contribute to adjustments in the structure and shifts in the functional traits of microbial communities, thus maintaining the stability of the coral holobiont system. Microbial richness (Chao index) and species diversity were significantly higher in virus-treated groups compared with controls, following the trend VH > VL > C (Fig. 7A, B). This dose-dependent pattern suggests that increasing viral load intensifies community disruption, generating ecological niches that facilitate the expansion or recruitment of additional mOTUs49. For example, control groups were dominated by Pseudomonadota (e.g., Ruegeria and Rhizobiaceae), whereas Thermodesulfobacteriota markedly increased in abundance under viral treatment (VL and VH; Fig. 7C). This shift suggests that Pseudomonadota may be preferentially targeted by phages, potentially creating ecological niches for Thermodesulfobacteriota, whose physiological characteristics might be more compatible with post-viral conditions. Thus, the lysis of dominant taxa by viruses could facilitate colonization by rare taxa (e.g., Thermodesulfobacteriota), which may contribute to increased community richness (Fig. 7D, E) and enhanced biodiversity. Metatranscriptomic analysis uncovered distinct changes in metabolic gene expression across treatments, with upregulated genes spanning C, N, P, S, Fe, and CH₄ cycles, providing evidence for metabolic shifts in the coral holobiont in response to viral exposure (Fig. 7F). These gene expression changes are correlated with viral exposure and reflect adaptive responses of the microbial community or indirect effects of viral lysis, with potential implications for holobiont nutrient cycling. Notably, viral exposure had no significant impact on zooxanthellae density or photosynthetic efficiency (Fig. 6B, C), indicating viral specificity for symbiotic bacteria rather than the coral host or its algal symbionts. This specificity highlights the targeted role of viruses in regulating bacterial communities within the holobiont, without disrupting the foundational coral-zooxanthellae mutualism, an important observation that underscores viruses as potential regulators of coral health.

Co-occurrence network analysis corroborates these findings: in virus-treated groups, connectivity within core modules sharply declines, while peripheral modules disassociate and reassemble, underscoring the dominance of lysis in shaping community structure (Supplementary Fig. 22). Although viral lysis suppresses certain functional bacteria, seemingly reducing metabolic potential, virus-mediated reprogramming provides ecological buffering potential6. By redirecting elemental fluxes, such as channeling carbon toward viral replication and leveraging iron metabolism to sustain host respiration, viruses dynamically modulate the functional profiles of coral-associated microbiomes. This supports the view that viral-mediated lysis-associated processes could play a beneficial role in coral reef ecosystems14, challenging the long-standing perception of viruses as mere biological destroyers. Instead, through lysis-driven restructuring and metabolic reallocation, viruses act as regulators of community stability and contributors to coral resilience, offering a mechanistic basis for resolving aspects of Darwin’s paradox. Recent perspectives, however, suggest a need to revisit Darwin’s paradox. Morais et al19. argue that coral reefs occupy a wide spectrum of nutrient regimes, spanning phosphate, nitrate, iron, and silicate concentrations across tropical oceans. Critically, these nutrients are not exclusively oligotrophic but often fall within mesotrophic ranges. Thus, coral reefs may not be universal “oases in marine deserts” but rather context-dependent systems. Nevertheless, vAMGs continue to play pivotal roles in nutrient acquisition and redistribution within coral symbionts, reinforcing their contribution to reef productivity. Notably, due to focus on 16S rRNA gene sequencing and metatranscriptomics analysis in the viral exposure experiments, we did not characterize the taxonomic composition or abundance of enriched viruses, nor their direct overlap with GCHVD. Although we cannot confirm overlap in viral taxa or abundances between the experiments and GCHVD, the experiments reinforce the biological relevance of the predicted virus-host associations in GCHVD, these experiments demonstrate that viral activity can directly shape microbial communities, and GCHVD specifically links these communities to particular viral lineages. In future studies, it is recommended to perform viromes sequencing and analysis synchronously during the viral enrichment phase. After identify the high-abundance viral taxa in coral holobiont, it facilitates construct virus-host-AMGs association networks, and identify the key regulators to homeostasis of the coral holobiont.

This study provides key insights into the distribution, diversity, and ecological roles of viruses in the coral holobiont by integrating global metagenomic analyses with virus exposure experiments. We expand current understanding of vAMGs, highlighting their importance in biogeochemical cycling within coral symbioses. Despite this progress, several limitations remain. First, reliance on existing public coral metagenomes limits geographic and temporal scope; future research must incorporate broader sampling across regions and years to capture intercontinental and interannual dynamics. Second, our exposure assays used mixed viral filtrates; future studies should employ single-phage co-culture systems to establish causal relationships with greater precision. Finally, quantifying viral contributions (especially vAMGs53) to coral reef ecology remains essential for elucidating their roles in shaping microbial communities, driving elemental cycles, and reprogramming host metabolism. Addressing these gaps will help illuminate viral dark matter and provide critical evidence toward resolving Darwin’s paradox.

Methods

Construction of global coral holobiont virome database

The Global Coral Holobiont Virome Database (GCHVD; n = 513) was assembled from publicly available metagenomic datasets (n = 460) and viromic datasets (n = 53). Sources included Google Scholar, the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), the European Nucleotide Archive, and previously published datasets from Wallace et al. 10. The datasets were visualized on a global map using the R package maps (v3.4.2) (Fig. 1A). All samples were collected in situ, excluding aquarium and ambient seawater samples, and were required to include detailed metadata, such as geographic location (n = 18 sites), coral health status (healthy, diseased, or bleached), and host coral taxonomy (n = 36 species).

Identification of viral contigs from GCHVD

Raw sequence data underwent quality filtering with fastp, retaining only reads with Q scores > 20 and > 40% base coverage, while discarding reads with > 10 bp contiguous Ns. Valid reads were individually assembled using MEGAHIT (v1.2.1)54 with default parameters. Read utilization rates were then calculated by mapping valid reads back to assemblies with SOAP2.21. This pipeline yielded 220,360,419 contigs (Supplementary Fig. 1). After assembly, candidate viral contigs were identified from assembled contigs by four tools in parallel: DeepVirfinder (v.1.0, score ≥ 0.8 and P < 0.05)27, VirSorter2 (v2.2.4, score ≥ 0.6)25, VIBRANT (v1.2.1, default parameter)26, and viralVerify (v1.1, default parameter)55. Consolidated predictions underwent genome integrity and contamination assessment through CheckV (v1.0.1)24, with retention criteria including: (i) ≥5% genome completeness and ≤10% host contamination rate; (ii) linear contigs ≥5 kb or circular contigs ≥1.5 kb; as well as (iii) presence of at least one conserved viral hallmark gene (e.g., capsid, terminase, or integrase). These filtered contigs were dereplicated and clustered into vOTUs using MMseqs2 (--min-seq-id 0.95 -c0.8 --cov-mode 1 -e1E-05 --cluster-mode2 --cluster-reassign)56, following the MIUVIG guidelines1 for viral species-level clustering. The workflow included: (i) creating a nucleotide database from filtered contigs; (ii) clustering with parameters set to 95% average nucleotide identity (ANI) and 85% alignment coverage (--min-seq-id 0.95, --cov 0.85, --cov-mode 1, --cluster-mode 2); and (iii) selecting the longest contig as the representative sequence for each vOTU. This pipeline yielded 36,860 non-redundant vOTUs. We acknowledge that linear alignment-based dereplication may have limitations in handling genomes with large insertions, deletions, or frame shifts (common in fragmented metagenomic assemblies), which could potentially affect clustering accuracy; however, this approach aligns with standardized virome research practices and MIUVIG recommendations to ensure reproducibility.

Host assignment and viral lifestyle prediction

Taxonomic annotation was performed sequentially with PhaBOX257 (host prediction), CAT (v5.2.3)58 (classification), and BLASTn59 against the IMG/VR v4 database. Abundance was estimated as RPKM using CoverM (v0.7.0)60 with parameters: contig --min-read-aligned-percent 75 min-read-percent-identity 95 min-covered-fraction 75 --contig-end-exclusion 0 -m rpkm. Viral communities in GCHVD were stratified by latitude (low, medium, high), coral health status, and host species for compositional analysis. Viral lifestyles were inferred by detecting lysogeny-associated genes (integrase, recombinase, provirus, transposase, repressor) and further classified as temperate or lytic using PhaBOX2 (--task phatyp)57.

Metagenomic assembly and microbial binning

MAGs were binned from contigs with MetaBAT236. Bins were assessed with CheckM (v1.0.12)37 and filtered at < 20% contamination and > 40% completeness. This threshold was selected to balance data quality and the retention of ecologically relevant taxa, as coral holobiont microbial communities are dominated by low-abundance symbionts whose genomes are difficult to assemble; a stricter 10% contamination cutoff would have excluded a large proportion of rare but functionally important MAGs. High-quality bins were dereplicated and clustered into mOTUs using dRep (v2.3.2)38 at 95% average nucleotide identity, producing 1295 non-redundant bacterial and archaeal mOTUs.

Bacterial and archaeal genomes were taxonomically classified with GTDB-Tk (v2.4.0)61, and phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using the Genome Taxonomy Database. Absolute genome abundances were estimated with CoverM (v0.7.060, with the parameters genome --min-read-aligned-percent 70 --min-read-percent-identity 90 --min-covered-fraction 75 -m count) and converted to relative abundances. Taxa were stratified by latitude, coral health status, and host species to refine compositional profiles. Prophage sequences were initially identified using VirSorter2 (v2.2.4)25; to mitigate false positives from MAG contamination (20% cutoff) and binning errors, candidate prophages were further validated by inspecting flanking host genomic features (tRNA loci, integrase/recombinase genes) and filtering against contaminant sequences identified in MAGs. Genomes harboring verified prophages were classified as temperate viruses.

Virus–host linkage prediction, co-occurrence network, and phylogenetic tree construction

The virus–host linkage prediction pipeline employed two primary alignment strategies. First, CRISPR spacer sequences were identified across all bacterial and archaeal genomes using PILER-CRISPR62 and the CRISPR recognition tool (CRT)63, with outputs merged into a unified dataset. Viral genomes were aligned against these spacers, retaining matches with ≤1 kb combined mismatch/gap and >95% spacer coverage; this approach captures signals from both lytic and temperate viruses that have infected the host microbes. Second, viral genomes were directly aligned to bacterial and archaeal genomes, retaining hits with ≥ 95% nucleotide identity and alignment lengths of >1 kb, a criterion that preferentially enriches for temperate viruses (due to their genome integration into host chromosomes) while also detecting some lytic viruses with extensive host sequence homology. Host assignment was then performed using a LCA approach³⁵, yielding a total of 32,370 predicted virus–host relationships.

For co-occurrence network analysis, the 500 most abundant viruses and 500 most abundant bacteria/archaea were selected. Pairwise Spearman’s rank correlations were calculated, and to mitigate Type I errors from multiple comparisons, we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Significant associations were retained based on the criteria of ρ > 0.7 and FDR-adjusted P < 0.05. In the resulting network, nodes represented taxonomic units and edges represented positive or negative correlations. Network construction and visualization were performed in Gephi (v0.10.1)64 using an edge-weighted spring-embedded layout. To construct a circular phylogenetic tree of vOTUs, we first established stringent screening criteria for vOTUs: only those with genus-level taxonomic annotation themselves, and whose associated bacterial/archaeal hosts had family-level taxonomic annotation (derived from virus-host association analysis), were selected for subsequent phylogenetic tree construction. To enhance the visualization clarity and information integration of the circular tree, we simultaneously annotated four key attributes onto it: (1) the genus-level taxonomic assignment of each vOTU; (2) the viral lifestyle type (e.g., lytic or temperate); (3) the family-level taxonomic information of the corresponding bacterial/archaeal hosts; and (4) the vOTU abundance distribution profiles grouped by different coral host species.

Identification of viral functional genes and vAMGs related to biogeochemical cycles

Functional annotation of viral genes was performed using eggNOG-mapper65 and kofam_scan66 to assign KEGG_ko identifiers. Viral genes with valid KEGG functional annotations were selected as candidate AMGs. These candidates were further aligned against five biogeochemical process-specific databases using Diamond, with strict thresholds of >30% identity, e-value < 1e-5, and bit score >50. The databases included: (1) dbCAN322 (carbohydrate metabolism); (2) NCycDB (nitrogen metabolism, https://github.com/qichao1984/NCyc)67; (3) SCycDB (sulfur metabolism)68; (4) PCycDB (phosphorus metabolism)69; (4) MCycDB (methane metabolism, https://github.com/qichao1984/MCycDB)70; and (5) FCycDB (Fe metabolism). Only genes with consistent functional annotations between KEGG and the above domain-specific databases were retained as high-quality AMG candidates (hereafter referred to as vAMGs). Validation of vAMGs followed a two-step framework to ensure viral origin and confidence: (i) vAMGs were required to be adjacent to viral hallmark genes identified via alignments against RefSeq viral database and geNomad_db (identity >30%, e-value < 1e-5, bit score >50). Based on the distribution of flanking viral hallmark genes, vAMGs were classified into two confidence levels: high-confidence vAMGs (flanked by viral hallmark genes on both sides) and medium-confidence vAMGs (flanked by one viral hallmark gene on either side); (ii) host origin was assigned by aligning vAMG-encoded proteins against the mOTU database, with only hits meeting >90% identity and coverage retained. Finally, all viral genes (including vAMGs) were clustered using CD-HIT with parameters -c 0.6 -aS 0.8 -n 4 -g 1, yielding 372,571 non-redundant vPCs.

Viral gene sharing network between the global coral virus genome dataset and other habitats

Predicted viral proteins were further clustered with NCBI Viral RefSeq v211 using vConTACT2 (v0.11.3)71 to construct a gene-sharing network. A total of 4935 coral viral genomes were compared with an equal number of high-quality viral genomes randomly sampled from IMG/VR v4, comprising: (i) 3952 soil genomes (grassland, forest, agricultural land), (ii) 844 RefSeq genomes, (iii) 3743 marine genomes, and (iv) 3294 human-associated genomes (intestinal and oral).

Preparation of viral suspensions from Acropora digitifera

Field samples of A. digitifera colonies were collected in June 2025 from Dapeng Bay, Shenzhen, China (22°33′N, 114°27′E). From each colony, five replicate samples ( ~ 15 cm³, including mucus, tissue, and skeleton) were excised using sterilized chisels and hammers, sealed in ziplock bags with ambient seawater, and transported to the laboratory within 2 h. Each sample was subdivided into five ~3 cm³ subsamples (n = 25), homogenized to a sand-like consistency with a sterile mortar and pestle, and suspended in 150 μL sterile artificial seawater (ASW). The suspension was transferred into centrifuge tubes containing 10–15 sterile glass beads, mixed with 40 mL ASW (a total of 25 centrifuge tubes, total ASW: 1.0 L), and vortexed ( ~ 600 rpm, 5 min) to lyse coral cells and algal symbionts. After centrifugation (3000 g, 15 min, 4°C), the supernatant was sequentially filtered through an 8.0 μm membrane to remove coral cells and Symbiodiniaceae, while retaining bacteria and viruses11. The filtrate was processed by tangential flow filtration (TFF) using parallel 0.2 μm and 100 kDa Labscale® Sampler polysulfone filters (Millipore) connected to a peristaltic pump (Longer BT100-2J, 10 rpm). Filters were sanitized with 0.1 M NaOH before use. The TFF filtrate was further concentrated with 100 kDa Amicon centrifugal units (Sigma-Aldrich; 3200 g, 15 min), producing 240 mL of concentrated viral suspension (designated as VH). From this, 160 mL was autoclaved to generate an inactivated control (C), and 8 mL was diluted 10-fold with 72 mL of autoclaved viral solution to produce a low-concentration suspension (VL; Fig. 6).

Virus exposure experiment at the mesocosm scale

To validate the ecological role of viruses within the coral holobiont, we conducted a mesocosm experiment using an artificially constructed near-natural aquatic system that bridges laboratory and field conditions. Nine coral tanks were established and divided into three groups: the control group (C), the low-concentration virus mixture filtrate group (VL), and the high-concentration virus mixture filtrate group (VH), each with three replicates. Each tank contained 12 A. digitifera specimens (3 cm³), yielding a total of 108 experimental coral specimens. Before the experiment, all specimens were pre-cultured for 7 days under stable environmental conditions (temperature 25°C, salinity 35 psu, light intensity 100 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹, 12:12 h light-dark cycle) to ensure physiological stability.

At the start of the trial, each coral fragment was removed from its tank. Using sterile pipettes, 2 mL of inactivated virus filtrate (C group), low-concentration filtrate (VL group), or high-concentration filtrate (VH group) was applied dropwise onto the coral surface. After a 10-min incubation to allow adsorption into the mucus layer, specimens were returned to their tanks. Samples were collected at 0, 3, and 6 days to measure zooxanthellae density, chlorophyll content, photosynthetic efficiency (Fv/Fm), and photosynthesis-to-respiration ratios (PG/RD). On day 7, corals from all groups were harvested for microbial community and functional analyses (16S rRNA gene and metatranscriptomic sequencing). All manipulations were performed under sterile conditions, and environmental parameters remained constant to minimize external interference. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM) confirmed the presence of diverse viral particles at different concentrations in the VL and VH treatments (Fig. 6), following methods described by Huang et al72.

DNA and RNA extraction

A 3 × 3 cm fragment of A. digitifera was collected and placed in sterile sample bags. Coral tissues were detached from the skeletal matrix using a high-pressure pump (0.2–0.3 MPa) with sterile artificial seawater (0.22 μm filtered). Total DNA and RNA were extracted from the tissue suspension using the RNeasy PowerSoil DNA Elution Kit and RNeasy PowerSoil Total RNA Kit (Qiagen), respectively, following the manufacturer’s protocols. For DNA analysis, concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). To characterize bacterial communities, the hypervariable V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified with primers 338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806 R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). The 20 µL PCR mixture contained 4 μL 5× Fast Pfu buffer, 2 μL 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 μL of each primer (5 μM), 0.4 μL Fast Pfu polymerase, 10 ng template DNA, and ddH₂O to reach the final volume. Cycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 27 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s; and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Purified amplicons were pooled in equimolar amounts and subjected to paired-end sequencing (2×300) on an Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform (Illumina) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). For RNA analysis, purity was assessed via NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and integrity via an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with an RNA6000 Nano chip. Qualified RNA samples were used for cDNA library construction with the TruSeq™ RNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina), followed by metatranscriptome sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (Majorbio, Shanghai, China).

Microbial communities and metatranscriptomic analysis

For 16S rRNA gene analysis, raw sequences were merged using FLASH v1.2.11, and low-quality reads (quality score < 25%), truncated sequences ( < 75% of tag length), and chimeras were removed. High-quality sequences were clustered into OTUs using UPARSE 7.1 at a 97% similarity threshold. Alpha diversity was quantified with the Chao1 index in the vegan R package, and group differences were assessed with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Taxonomic differences were identified using LEfSe (http://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/LEfSe) with LDA score > 2 and P < 0.05. OTU overlap among treatments was visualized with VennDiagram v1.6.16, while community heatmaps were generated in R v3.3.1 with vegan v2.4.3. Network topology metrics, including nodes, edges, average path length, connectance, betweenness centralization, average degree, and modularity, were calculated using igraph in R, and visualized using Gephi v0.10.1.

For metatranscriptomic analysis, raw reads were quality-filtered by removing short ( < 50 bp), low-quality (Q < 20), or ambiguous (N-containing) reads with Sickle, followed by additional trimming with fastp v0.19.7. Read counts per sample were generated with RSEM v1.3.1, and sequencing quality (alignment efficiency, saturation, read distribution) was evaluated with DEGseq v1.36.0. Gene expression levels were normalized as fragments per kilobase per million mapped reads (FPKM) and analyzed for differential expression using edgeR v3.20.2. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined by FDR < 0.05 and fold change ≥ 2. Functional annotation was performed by BLASTP v2.2.28+ against the KEGG database, and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment of DEG sets was assessed using ClusterProfiler, with significance determined by Fisher’s exact test.

Statistical analysis

During this study, physiological parameters ( ≥ three replicates) were reported as means ± standard deviation. One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was performed in SPSS v26.0, with statistical significance set at P ≤ 0.05. Sequencing and bioinformatics data analyses, including taxonomic diversity, Pearson correlation, and AMG abundance, were conducted in R v3.4.2 and GraphPad Prism 9.5. The α- and β-diversity metrics were calculated using the vegan package (v2.6-4) in R. For α-diversity, Shannon indices were used, and between-group differences were assessed via the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. For β-diversity, non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was applied, with clustering significance validated by ANOSIM (999 permutations). Taxonomic abundance differences were evaluated using Welch’s t-test in STAMP.
