Leak during term neonatal CPAP stabilisation: does resuscitation device design affect delivered PEEP – a bench study
Authors: Viktoria Gruber, Stephanie Morakeas, Murray Hinder, Thomas Drevhammar, Mithilesh Dronavalli, Mark Brian Tracy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMask leak is common during newborn resuscitation, impacting delivered ventilation. Less is known about the effect of leak during assisted spontaneous breathing with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP).AimCompare CPAP performance in the presence of leak of two T-piece resuscitation devices, with differing design-imposed resistances: high resistance Neopuff and low resistance rPAP.MethodsDevices were tested on a term dynamic lung model (compliance 1.0 mL/cmH2O, tidal volume (VT) 23 mL) at three incremental leak levels and set positive end expiratory pressure (PEEP) 5 cmH2O, 7 cmH2O and 9 cmH2O. Continuous leaks were generated by differing-length tubes open to atmosphere. Measured parameters were mean pressure, fluctuations around set pressure (∆P), VT and leak flow.Results2437 breaths were analysed (1216 rPAP, 1221 Neopuff). Leak reduced PEEP, with the largest reduction at the highest leak and 9 cmH2O set PEEP (Neopuff 2.6 cmH2O vs rPAP 7.0 cmH2O). Higher delivered PEEP was associated with higher leak flows (Neopuff 4.4 L/min vs rPAP 8.2 L/min, 9 cmH2O set PEEP). VT reduced with Neopuff compared with rPAP (Neopuff 18 mL vs rPAP 23.2 mL, no leak, 9 cmH2O set PEEP) and was affected by delivered PEEP.ConclusionThe delivered support differed between devices in the presence of leaks. rPAP maintained pressures closer to the set PEEP value at all leak levels, and higher leak flows were seen with the maintained distending pressure. Neopuff’s higher resistance led to reductions in VT that were more pronounced at low leak levels.Keywords: Neonatology, Resuscitation, Child Health

Background

Numerous physiological changes accompany the transition to spontaneous breathing at birth. As fetal lung fluid clears, there are dramatic changes in lung compliance, accompanied by falls in airway resistance.1 2 In a normal transition to breathing, this process occurs quickly and begins with the first breaths.3 Approximately 5% of all newborn babies will need breathing assistance via positive pressure ventilation (PPV) or continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP). This facilitates open airways, fluid clearance and establishment of an appropriate physiological functional residual capacity (FRC).4

During newborn resuscitation, changing between PPV and CPAP is common, as is the use of CPAP generally to support lung aeration after resuscitation. However, resuscitation devices with this capability in a single unit are limited to T-piece resuscitator (TPR) and flow-inflating anaesthetic bags using flow resistance to generate PEEP. Distending pressure can be provided via TPR to a spontaneously breathing newborn with respiratory distress and as secondary support following PPV. Control of delivered peak inspiratory pressure and positive end expiratory pressure (PEEP) during PPV is considered more accurate with TPR compared with self-inflating bags.5

Support with distending pressure between 5 cmH2O and 9 cmH2O is the recommended first-line therapy for preterm infants with respiratory distress,6 7 with a facemask or nasal interface.4 7 8 This has become increasingly common in late preterm and term infants with respiratory distress, although there is insufficient evidence to support or refute its use.6,10 The application of CPAP splints the airways, improving lung expansion with higher FRC and preventing atelectasis. It reduces airway resistance, work of breathing and leads to stabilisation of the respiratory pattern.11 12

Discussions have focused on investigating leak compensation and factors that influence interface leak during long-term treatment, rather than the performance of different CPAP-generating devices in the presence of leak at resuscitation.13 14

Resuscitation devices can have a different imposed resistance due to their design or the method of generating distending pressure.15 16 Theoretical negative impacts of high resistance systems have been associated with increased rates of pulmonary air leak,17 18 and inadvertent PEEP has been documented.19,21 The relation between leak, delivered pressures and resistance has not been well described. We investigated delivered distending pressures at several leak levels during simulated spontaneous breathing using a dynamic lung model, simulating a term newborn with respiratory distress. Two resuscitation devices with high and low imposed expiratory resistance were compared. Both devices are designed to provide PPV and CPAP pressure after successful transition to breathing. The aim was to describe the relation between imposed device resistance and delivered ventilation parameters at several leak levels during simulated spontaneous breathing.

Methods

Resuscitation devices

Two TPR resuscitation devices with different designs and methods of generating distending pressures (CPAP) were compared in a bench test model simulating term spontaneous breathing: Inspire rPAP (Inspiration Healthcare, Croydon, UK) and the Neopuff TPR (Fisher and Paykel Healthcare, Auckland, New Zealand). Leak was investigated at three set PEEP levels (5 cmH2O, 7 cmH2O and 9 cmH2O). rPAP was used with a total flow of 10 L/min for 5 cmH2O and 7 cmH2O and 11 L/min for 9 cmH2O (the increased fresh gas flow needed to achieve 9 cmH2O PEEP). For Neopuff, a total flow of 10 L/min was used for all PEEP levels.

Respiratory function monitor

A Florian respiratory function monitor (RFM) (Acutronic, Medical Systems AG, Zug, Switzerland) was used to measure leak flow rates at the induced leaks. Before data collection, the Florian RFM was calibrated (airway pressure, flow and volume) and verified by a traceable reference ventilation analyser (PF-300 IMT Medical, Buchs, Switzerland) and a 10 mL precision syringe (Model 5520, Hans Rudolph, Shawnee, USA).

Neonatal active lung model

Devices were connected to a leak port and then the Neonatal Active Lung Model (GINA V1-4.0, Schaller Medizintechnik, Dresden, Germany), which simulated spontaneous breathing in our model (settings, see table 1). The GINA was set to represent a term infant with respiratory distress (compliance 1 mL/cmH2O, respiratory rate 50 /min, inspiratory time 0.4 s, Ra2). For comparison, these settings were chosen as per previous publications.19 22 23

This experiment was conducted with non-humidified gas (21% FiO2) at ambient room temperature. Both resuscitation devices can be used with a face mask, while the rPAP can also be used with nasal prongs. No face mask or patient interface was used in this bench test. Leaks were continuous via differing-length side port tubes.

Leaks

Tygon tubing cut to three different lengths was connected to an endotracheal tube connector (distal to flow sensor connected to GINA online supplemental fig 1S) via a male luer lock connector (internal diameter 2.5 mm) (L0 refers to no leak; tubing length L1: 19.3 cm, L2: 5 cm, L3: 2.2 cm; internal/outer diameter online supplemental table 1S). This generates a continuous leak during inspiration and expiration. Each leak was introduced incrementally, starting at 0 for each collected data sequence. Before recording, the GINA was equilibrated for 30 min and calibrated.

Data collection

Data were separately collected from the GINA and the Florian RFM Flow Sensor next to the induced leak over 2 min for each setting. Data output from GINA was used for mean pressure, delta pressure and tidal volume (VT) measurements. Data from Florian were used for mean leak flow.

GINA data were outputted in National Instruments Technical Data Management Streaming file format at a sample frequency of 5 ms. Using a TDMS viewer, data were converted into an Excel file. Data from the Florian flow sensor were analysed using Spectra software (Grove Medical, London, England).

GINA data were imported into Stata V.18 MP (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). Each respiratory cycle was identified in Stata by changes in pressure of respiratory muscle parameter.

The measured parameters included the mean pressure, delta P (ΔP; maximum – minimum airway pressure), VT and mean leak flow. Mean values including 95% CI for those parameters are reported in table 2. A spontaneous breathing cycle with the used settings without leak, or with the device connected, was recorded as a comparison baseline.

Data analysis

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) for repeated measures was used to determine differences in mean with 95% CI for measured parameters (p, ΔP, VT) at different set PEEP levels between the two tested devices and leak levels. Differences between predicted means determined by multiway ANOVA were reported with p values adjusted. F test using Box’s conservative epsilon; p values<0.05 were considered statistically significant. Bonferroni corrections of estimates were made to adjust for multiple comparisons. Florian data for leak flow rates were collected at 200 Hz and assessed with mean flows and SD to calculate 95% CI.

Patient and public involvement

Patient and public were not involved in this bench test.

Results

2437 breaths were analysed (1216 with rPAP, 1221 with Neopuff). 104 simulated breaths were analysed with no device connected to generate an unsupported volume delivered by the GINA to compare with device-supported volumes.

Pressure and leak

rPAP maintained pressures closer to set value at all investigated leak levels compared with Neopuff. The highest mean pressure drops were seen at the highest set PEEP and leak. Delivered pressure with a set PEEP of 9 cmH2O was 2.6 cmH2O with Neopuff and 7 cmH2O with rPAP at leak 3 (table 2 and figure 1).

Mean pressure of Neopuff and rPAP at different leak levels (1, 2 and 3) for set PEEP 5 cmH2O, 7 cmH2O and 9 cmH2O. PEEP, positive end expiratory pressure.

Reduction in delivered pressures in the Neopuff with higher leak also led to a decrease in pressure swings. ΔP is the variation in pressures above and below a set mean pressure. Mean ΔP for Neopuff was 11.4 cmH2O at no leak, with a reduction to a mean of 5 cmH2O at the highest leak (leak 3) for set PEEP of 9 (table 2 and figure 2). This reduction in ΔP was associated with reduced delivered mean pressures. ΔP was lower for rPAP but showed a small increase with higher leak, 1.9 cmH2O at no leak versus 3.5 cmH2O for leak 3.

Pressure swings (ΔP) for Neopuff and rPAP at different leak levels and set PEEP 5 cmH2O, 7 cmH2O and 9 cmH2O. PEEP, positive end expiratory pressure.

VT and leak

A pronounced reduction of VT with Neopuff could be seen compared with spontaneous breathing GINA with no device connected. Mean VT 18 mL (PEEP 9, leak 0) versus mean 23.2 mL spontaneous breathing. A drop in delivered pressures resulted in an improved VT (18 mL vs 21.9 mL leak 0 vs leak 3 and set PEEP 9) for Neopuff. With rPAP, higher leak led to a small VT drop (23.2 mL vs 22.3 mL leak 0 vs leak 3, PEEP 9).

Flow and leak

Mean leak flows were higher with the low-resistance device. The differences in leak flow were more pronounced at the highest investigated leak levels and with highest set PEEP, whereas they remained similar at lower leak levels and the lowest PEEP level. Mean leak flow was 0.7 L/min vs 0.8 L/min Neopuff versus rPAP (leak 1, PEEP 5) and 4.4 L/min vs 8.2 L/min Neopuff versus rPAP (leak 3, PEEP 9). The higher measured leak flows for the rPAP device were associated with the maintained distending pressure (table 2, figure 3).

Mean leak flow for Neopuff and rPAP at induced leak levels (1, 2 and 3) for set PEEP 5 cmH2O, 7 cmH2O and 9 cmH2O. PEEP, positive end expiratory pressure.

Discussion

This bench study shows that in the presence of leak and simulated term spontaneous breathing, the lower imposed resistance resuscitation device (rPAP) maintained delivered distending pressure closer to the set pressure at all leak levels compared with the higher resistance device (Neopuff).

The maintained pressure leads to higher leak flows (figure 3) for rPAP compared with Neopuff. These findings were expected and related to the maintained distending pressure. The higher resistance of Neopuff resulted in larger pressure swings with marked VT reductions (table 2). For rPAP, there were minor reductions of VT at higher leak levels. These findings are consistent with our prior study examining delivered volumes and work of breathing comparing rPAP and Neopuff with GINA lung simulator in a leak-free setup.24 The study indicated that the resuscitation device used and the pressures delivered must be evaluated when interpreting leaks.

Airway management training and the use of RFMs aim to reduce leak and can display leak in real-time, providing feedback to the user. They can assist airway optimisation and delivery of target volumes, but are not currently recommended for use in resuscitation guidelines.25,27 The level of leak will likely vary for PPV and spontaneous breathing, between different interfaces and be higher at higher set PEEP. The type of face mask or nasal interface is also likely to affect leak level and the delivered distending pressure. The recommended pressures in guidelines do not consider leak or the ability to maintain a pressure when a leak is present.4 7

In bench tests, leak has mainly been studied for positive-pressure ventilation. Most of these studies have used a continuous leak model, with a constant leak during inspiration and expiration.28 A novel dynamic leak model has shown the dynamic aspect of pressure changes during PPV and their impact on leak.29

Studies investigating leak during CPAP application are limited to the displayed leak accuracy of ventilators.30 Schmölzer et al arbitrarily considered a mask leak of 75% during PPV as clinically relevant.14 This leak definition is dependent on determining expiratory VTs. A 75% leak approximately corresponds to leak level one in our model. Higher levels could not be examined using expiratory VT (ie, leak close to 100%).

Following current resuscitation guidelines, a PEEP of 5–6 cmH2O is recommended for newborn stabilisation.7 In a systematic review of nasal CPAP support of preterm infants, the effect estimate suggested a possible benefit of moderate to high-level CPAP support.31 Maintenance of prescribed CPAP levels to increase FRC requires stable distending pressure not only at initial stabilisation but also for extended periods of treatment time.32 CPAP pressure was also found to promote lung growth and pulmonary function in moderately preterm infants, showing a higher number of lung alveoli in premature primates.33

The response to leak could be one factor explaining the lower intubation rate seen with rPAP compared with T-piece systems in preterm stabilisation in a recent RCT.34 A higher tolerance for leak, maintained CPAP levels and higher VT performance during transition might reduce the risk of intubation. Reduced intubation rates in this trial could also potentially be explained by rPAP being used with prongs. These may have less potential to induce a trigemino-cardiac reflex and have a reduced dead space, allowing faster changes of oxygen concentration.34 35

Technical considerations

The differences in response to leak can be explained by how distending pressure is generated. For Neopuff, PEEP is generated by backpressure, with the bias flow leaving the circuit via an adjustable flow resistance (small hole).16 Adding an interface leak will give a reduced resistance and be equivalent to opening the adjustable resistor. For rPAP, PEEP is generated by two high-pressure jets, and interface leak will be compensated by gas entrainment. The gas entrainment does not completely compensate for the leak, resulting in a reduced PEEP and increased resistance. For both systems, there was a direct correlation between resistance, measured fluctuation around set pressure (ΔP) and decreased VT. For Neopuff, this explains the marked ΔP decrease and VT increase with leak and for rPAP the minor ΔP increase and VT decrease with leak.

Limitations

This bench test study has focused on and was limited to two of the most commonly used resuscitation devices capable of transitioning from PPV to CPAP support during full resuscitation and spontaneous breathing. We modelled term newborns with respiratory distress due to the likelihood of normal lung compliance and the potential impact of device-imposed expiratory resistance. For infants after initial stabilisation and established transition to breathing who do not need PPV, other CPAP devices could have been used.

The study did not include interfaces that add resistance, such as prongs or endotracheal tubes. We plan future studies using a respiratory model that simulates preterm physiology with low lung compliance and reduced VTs.

The GINA lung model could not respond to the loss of distending pressure, for example, by reducing FRC, compliance and changing respiratory rate. In this aspect, the model does not mimic real infants. The tests do not separate inspiratory and expiratory resistance, but these are closely linked in passive systems. Active systems, such as ventilators, allow them to be independently controlled and separated, while the two resuscitation systems do not allow this. The present study does not evaluate the benefits of high or low expiratory resistance, but on the pressure maintained during an induced leak. High expiratory resistance devices may also impart higher work of breathing over CPAP treatment time.16 Our study included two resuscitation devices that deliver PEEP in a spontaneous breathing model. Investigating PPV followed by irregular or inadequate breathing requires a different method.

An increase in fresh gas flow (11 L/min) to achieve a PEEP of 9 using rPAP was needed, whereas Neopuff was set to 10 L/min for all tested PEEP levels. The model was limited to term infant settings. For settings mimicking a smaller infant ΔP and measured VT would be less but delivered CPAP likely to be similar (not investigated).22

Clinical importance and further research

To the best of our knowledge, no studies have focused on the performance of different TPR resuscitation devices in the presence of leak. Leak could be an unrecognised factor in clinical trials comparing resuscitation devices and will be highly dependent on how well different brand devices can maintain CPAP pressure. Device performance is an identified knowledge gap in the ILCOR Consensus of Science statement on the use of CPAP for term respiratory distress in the delivery room.10 Leaks are difficult to measure, affect delivered PEEP levels and may impact key performance aspects during newborn stabilisation. The clinical translation and importance of these bench-test findings need further investigation.

Conclusion

With simulated spontaneous breathing, the delivered support differed between the two TPR resuscitation devices when challenged with leaks. rPAP maintained pressures closer to the set PEEP value at all leak levels, and higher leak flows were seen with the maintained distending pressure. Neopuff’s higher imposed resistance showed reductions in VT that were more pronounced at low leak levels. Device variation of delivered CPAP pressures in the presence of interface leak is an important finding of this study. Further bench and clinical studies are needed to evaluate the effect of leak on the delivered support and patient outcomes.
