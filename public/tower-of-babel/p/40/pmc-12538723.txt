Surgical treatment intracardiac thrombosis and pulmonary embolism complication of Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia: a case report
Authors: Di Yu, Xuming Mo, Yuzhong Yang, Nishant Patel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) is a common community-acquired pneumonia in children, which can uncommonly lead to intracardiac thrombosis. Previous studies have suggested that anticoagulant therapy and thrombolytic therapy were the main treatment options.Case presentationWe report the case of a 7-year-old boy with MPP complicated by intracardiac thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. The patient presented with persistent fever, chest pain, and elevated D-dimer levels. The computed tomography angiography (CTA) and echocardiography revealed a thrombus extension from the right inferior pulmonary vein to the left atrium. Considering the risk of thrombus detachment, surgical thrombectomy was performed under cardiopulmonary bypass. Two months follow-up revealed no evidence of new thrombus formation and D-dimer levels normalized.ConclusionsIn cases of MPP complicated by intracardiac thrombus formation, particularly involving the left heart chambers, surgical thrombectomy may be a viable treatment option. D-dimer served as an important biomarker for both the early detection of thrombosis and the monitoring of treatment efficacy.

Background

Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) is identified as the primary type of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) in Chinese children aged five and above [1], resulting in a regional pandemic every 3–7 years [2]. In pediatric patients, MPP can induce a hypercoagulable state that may result in thrombosis, which may be associated with antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL), including anticardiolipin (aCL), lupus anticoagulant complex (LA), and β2 glycoprotein antibody [3]. In recent years, there have been many published studies in the literature on MPP combined with pulmonary embolism, cerebral infarction, and deep vein thrombosis in pediatric patients [4]. While such instances of intracardiac thrombosis are relatively uncommon [5–7], there have been reports of successful treatment with a combination of anticoagulation and thrombolysis therapy. However, surgical intervention in such cases has been sparsely documented [8]. Herein, we present a case of MPP complicated by intracardiac thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, successfully treated with surgical thrombectomy, offering an effective approach to clinical therapy.

Case presentation

A 7-year-old male, weighing 21 kg, presented with a history of intermittent fever and cough for ten days as a main complaint. Serum test revealed a high titer of anti-mycoplasma pneumoniae IgM (> 1:160). Despite prior treatment with erythromycin, methylprednisolone, and ceftriaxone in another hospital, the patient continued to have a fever with a peak temperature of 39.5 °C. One day prior to admission, the patient experienced chest pain and a laboratory test showed elevated D-dimer levels (5058 ng/mL). A computed tomography (CT) scan revealed high-density consolidation in the right lower lobe, prompting admission to our hospital’s respiratory department. Upon admission, the patient had a temperature of 39.2 °C and a peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) of 100%. Further laboratory tests revealed elevated C-reactive protein [CRP, 35.9 mg/L, reference range (RR) 0–10 mg/L], white blood cell count [20.62 × 10^9/L, RR 4.3–11.3 × 10^9/L], erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) [61 mm/h, RR ≤ 20 mm/h], and Interleukin-6 (IL-6) [42.52 pg/mL, RR ≤ 5.4 pg/mL]. Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) nucleic acid test was positive, while aCL and LA were negative. Anticoagulant therapy with nadroparin (100 IU/kg/d) was initiated, along with anti-infection treatment using erythromycin (30 mg/kg/d) and piperacillin and sulbactam (4:1, 150 mg/kg/d), and anti-inflammatory therapy with methylprednisolone (2 mg/kg/d).

On the second day, the patient’s chest pain worsened. An electrocardiogram was performed, and no ST-segment or T-wave changes were found. Subsequently, transthoracic echocardiography was carried out to exclude coronary artery and intracardiac structural abnormalities, and no abnormalities were detected. On the fourth day, CT angiography (CTA) of the chest showed filling defects in the right inferior pulmonary vein (RIPV) and left atrium (Fig. 1A), along with consolidation in the right lower lobe of the lung (Fig. 1B). The patient was urgently transferred to the cardiac intensive unit (CCU). Echocardiography revealed a mass (22.3 × 8.04 mm^2) with extension from the RIPV to the left atrium, obstructing the RIPV ostium and partially floating in the left atrium (Fig. 1C). Considering the risk of thrombus detachment, cerebral infarction, and abdominal organ embolism, written informed consent was obtained from the patient’s parents, and an emergency operation was conducted under cardiopulmonary bypass. Through a right atrial septal incision, a sizable thrombus was identified in the left atrium extending to the RIPV (Fig. 1D). The thrombus was dissected from the pulmonary vein (Fig. 1E), and the necrotic right lower lobe of the lung was simultaneously resected (Fig. 1F). Postoperatively, nadroparin (100 IU/kg/d) was continued for anticoagulation. Pathological examination confirmed thrombosis (Fig. 1G) and acute suppurative pneumonia with necrosis and abscess formation (Fig. 1H). Subsequently, D-dimer levels gradually decreased from 2077 ng/mL (first postoperative day) to 628 ng/mL (third postoperative day), with a transient increase to 2326 ng/mL (eleventh postoperative day). Due to a hypoechoic (21 × 4 mm^2) to the wall of the right internal jugular vein observed by echocardiography on the twelfth postoperative day (Fig. 1I), nadroparin was intensified (3000 IU/20 kg). Then an ultrasound examination of the head and lower extremity vessels was performed and found no additional thrombi. Ultrasound showed a reduction in the mass to 9 × 4 mm^2 by the twenty-first postoperative day and its complete disappearance by twenty-eighth postoperative day. Concurrently, D-dimer levels decreased to 440 ng/mL by twenty-eighth postoperative day, after which warfarin was introduced as the anticoagulant. The initial warfarin dose was 2 mg/d, with each 20% increase in dosage to maintain an international normalized ratio (INR) of 1.5–2.5. Coagulation function, D-dimer levels, and platelet counts were monitored for bleeding with the maximum warfarin dose which was not exceeding 0.2 mg/kg/d. By the thirty-second postoperative day, D-dimer levels had normalized (268 ng/mL) and two months after surgery, a CTA confirmed the absence of a filling defect in the patient.

Discussion and conclusions

China is currently experiencing a concurrent outbreak of pediatric respiratory pathogens, especially MP [9]. MP infection can lead to a hypercoagulable state and thrombosis, which pose life-threatening risks if diagnosis and treatment are delayed. Previous studies have found that MP associated thrombosis predominantly occurs in the pulmonary vasculature, with in situ pulmonary artery thrombosis being the primary cause of pulmonary artery embolism, rather than thromboembolic [3, 10]. However, intracardiac thrombosis in pediatric MP infections is rare, especially when involving the left heart chambers [5, 7, 10].

In this study, we present a case of a 7-year-old child with MPP accompanied by left atrium thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. The mechanism underlying thrombosis in MP infection included direct invasion and damage to the vascular endothelium, leading to local thrombosis [11, 12]. Additionally, MP infection may trigger the immune system, generating cross-reactive antibodies that promote thrombus formation [12]. The inflammatory response induced by MP infection can also result in elevated levels of inflammatory markers, such as CRP, which can activate the coagulation system and induce a hypercoagulable state [13]. Furthermore, aPLs have been implicated in thrombosis following MP infection [12]. While, aPLs were not detected in this patient, which might be due to pre-admission methylprednisolone use, which could attenuate inflammatory responses and suppress immune system activation [3, 14].

Chest pain is the most frequent symptom of pulmonary vascular thrombosis, although some patients may be asymptomatic. Therefore, early detection of thrombosis is critical for timely intervention and improved clinical outcomes. Liu et al. reviewed 43 children with MPP associated thrombosis and suggested that D-dimer >5.0 mg/L, particularly >11.1 mg/L could facilitate the early diagnosis of thrombosis [3]. In our case, the patient exhibited a history of chest pain and elevated D-dimer levels. Thrombosis of the left atrium and RIPV were incidentally identified within 2 weeks of MP infection, which was consistent with the previous studies [3, 12]. Moreover, D-dimer is a critical biomarker for monitoring the efficacy of thrombosis treatment. In our case, during anticoagulation therapy, an abnormal elevation of D-dimer levels and the discovery of venous thrombosis underscored the value of D-dimer as a clinical decision-making guide.

According to the American Society of Hematology guidelines anticoagulation is the first-line treatment for pediatric pulmonary embolism [15]. However, there is no unified standard treatment for intracardiac thrombosis following MP infection. Described therapeutic strategies in previous studies include anticoagulation therapy with low molecular weight heparin to prevent thrombus enlargement and mitigate the risk of embolic events. In addition to anticoagulation, thrombolytic therapy has been used in acute thromboembolism. Nevertheless, there is a risk of thrombus detachment leading to a new thromboembolic scenario [7]. Surgical thrombectomy was first described in a 9-year-old boy with a right ventricular thrombus associated with MP infection in 2010 [8] and may be a critical treatment option in cases with a high risk of embolic events or when anticoagulation and thrombolytic therapies have failed. In our case, the thrombus extended from the RIPV to the left atrium, concurrent with a pulmonary embolism. Considering the risk of thrombus detachment, we performed a thrombectomy after obtaining written informed consent from the patient’s parents. The surgical outcome was favorable, with no postoperative complications observed. Furthermore, a follow-up CTA conducted two months after surgery revealed no evidence of new thrombus formation.

In conclusion, for patients with MP infection accompanied by intracardiac thrombus, particularly in left heart chambers, surgical thrombectomy may be a viable option. Early diagnosis is essential for these rare cases and can significantly improve patient outcomes. D-dimer serves as an important biomarker in the early detection of thrombosis and is instrumental in guiding clinical decision-making and therapeutic strategies.
