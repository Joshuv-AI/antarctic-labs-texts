Dimensional Accuracy and Adaptation of Monolithic Zirconia Crowns Fabricated by a Novel Material Jetting Technique: An In Vitro Study Based on Clinical Laboratory Data
Authors: Jizhe Lyu, Siyuan Wang, Huiye Wang, Xu Yang, Minxian Ma, Xiaoqiang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and aimsAdditive manufacturing technology has the potential to produce accurate restorations in the field of prosthodontics. This in vitro study aimed to investigate the dimensional accuracy and marginal and internal fit of monolithic zirconia crowns fabricated with advanced customized jetting (ACJ) technology, a material jetting method, based on clinical laboratory data.MethodsThe three-dimensional (3D) printed tooth preparation casts for ceramic complete crowns of the maxillary central incisor and mandibular first molar (n = 10 for each tth, a total of 20) were collected from a dental laboratory. All casts were digitally scanned, and 40 crowns were fabricated by using ACJ and subtractive manufacturing (SM) methods (n = 10 per manufacturing method for incisors and molars). The dimensional accuracy was determined by superimposing the scanned crown data with the corresponding design data. Marginal and internal fit were evaluated with a dual scanning method.ResultsSignificant differences were found in the dimensional accuracy and adaptation among the ACJ and SM groups (P < .05). ACJ crowns demonstrated significantly higher accuracy and lower root mean square (RMS) values in the overall, external, marginal, and intaglio regions compared with SM (P < .001). Regarding adaptation, ACJ crowns exhibited smaller marginal gaps, occlusal gaps in molars, and axial gaps in incisors than SM crowns (P < .05).ConclusionMonolithic zirconia crowns fabricated by using ACJ demonstrated superior dimensional accuracy and adaptation compared with SM based on clinical laboratory data.Clinical RelevanceAdvanced customized jetting technology enables the fabrication of monolithic zirconia crowns with favorable dimensional accuracy and marginal and internal fit. This capability demonstrates its significant potential for complete crown production based on clinical laboratory data.Key words: Additive manufacturing, Zirconia, Accuracy, Adaptation

Introduction

Zirconia has been proven to possess excellent mechanical properties, aesthetic performance, and biocompatibility.1, 2, 3 These characteristics, combined with its versatility, make zirconia a material of choice in modern prosthetic dentistry.4 Zirconia crowns are fabricated by computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technologies, which involve either subtractive manufacturing (SM) or additive manufacturing (AM) methods.5 SM is widely employed in dentistry,6, 7, 8 however, it presents several limitations, including material wastage and restricted reproduction of intricate surface geometries due to the size of milling burs and the number of milling machine axes.9, 10, 11 The use of AM has increased significantly in recent years,12, 13, 14 as it enables the fabrication of customized, complex three-dimensional (3D) structures in a more sustainable manner.13,15 AM reduces raw material waste and alleviates the tooling stresses commonly associated with milling.16 Material jetting (MJ) is an established AM technique that involves jetting liquid materials onto a build platform through precisely controlled print heads.17 The advanced customized jetting (ACJ), a type of MJ technology, enables the fabrication of restorations by simultaneously depositing build and support materials through 512 nozzles distributed across 24 print heads, constructing the restoration in a layer-by-layer manner with a layer height of 5 to 10 μm.18, 19, 20 Previous in vitro studies have shown that ACJ achieves superior dimensional accuracy and adaptation compared with SM,21, 22, 23, 24 yet its clinical performance remains unknown in clinical applications.

Dimensional accuracy and adaptation are critical determinants of the long term performance of dental restorations.25 Previous research has shown that both SM and ACJ can achieve satisfactory outcomes in these parameters.21,22,26,27 Nevertheless, such investigations have primarily focused on in vitro standard controlled tooth preparations. In real-world clinical practice, prepared teeth may present sharper line angles due to inherent tooth morphology or limited clinical experience, thereby complicating crown fabrication. Inadequate detail reproduction during manufacturing may lead to crown rocking or compromised seating accuracy.28,29 Optimal marginal and internal adaptation are essential for the long term stability of restorations.30, 31, 32, 33 Proper marginal adaptation reduces the risk of microleakage,34, 35, 36 while minimal and uniform internal gaps promote even stress distribution within complete crowns.37,38 However, research on the dimensional accuracy and adaptation based on data generated during routine clinical practice remains limited.

This study aimed to evaluate the dimensional accuracy and adaptation of ACJ-fabricated zirconia crowns for incisors and molars based on clinical laboratory data. The null hypotheses were that no significant differences would be observed in dimensional accuracy and adaptation between ACJ and SM zirconia crowns, and that no differences would exist between incisor and molar zirconia crowns.

Materials and methods

Specimen preparation

This study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the checklist for reporting in vitro studies. A priori sample size estimation was performed by using a software program (G*Power v.3.1.9.6, Heinrich-Heine University) with a 2-way ANOVA statistical test method. Means and standard deviations were extracted from previously published studies, and the sample size calculation was powered to detect the main effect of manufacturing method.21 With a significance level of 0.05, a statistical power of 0.80, numerator df of 1, the total sample size (N) was determined to be 40, with a sample size (n) of 10 per group.

The 3D printed tooth preparation casts were collected from a dental laboratory of a specialized stomatological hospital (the authors' affiliation) over the course of 3 months. All teeth were prepared by active prosthodontic specialists within the department of prosthodontics. Tooth preparations for monolithic zirconia complete crowns on the maxillary left central incisor and mandibular right first molar were randomly selected (n = 10 for incisors and molars, a total of 20). Models with incomplete or damaged casts, unclear preparation margins, or absence of adjacent teeth have been excluded.

All casts were digitally scanned by using an intraoral scanner (TRIOS 3, 3Shape A/S). Twenty complete crowns were then designed by using CAD software (DentalCAD 3.0 Galway, exocad GmbH) based on the scanned data. The data of designed crowns were saved in standard tessellation language (STL) format, and subsequently imported into both the SM and ACJ systems for crown fabrication.

In the SM system, crowns were fabricated from partially sintered zirconia blocks (ST, Upcera) by using a 5-axis milling system (DWX 51D, Roland DG Corp), followed by final sintering at 1530°C for 2 hours. In the ACJ system, fabrication was performed by using an inkjet printer (PGJ180). Zirconia ceramic formulation droplets (Zrcoop), encapsulated by support materials, were precisely ejected onto a temperature-controlled building platform at a constant layer height of 5 to 10 μm. The support suspension consisted of 31% by weight sodium carbonate dissolved in glycol ethers with stabilizing additives, while the zirconia suspension contained approximately 45% zirconia particles dispersed in glycol ethers with stabilizing additives. Platform heating facilitated rapid solvent evaporation, enabling the formation of zirconia ceramic layers. The printed components were subsequently sintered at 1450°C for 4 hours to achieve sufficient density. In total, 40 crowns were fabricated by using SM and ACJ (n = 10 per manufacturing method for incisors and molars).

Dimensional accuracy evaluation

After fabrication, the crowns were scanned with an intraoral scanner (TRIOS 3, 3Shape A/S), and the scan data were imported into a measurement software program (Geomagic Studio 2013, 3D Systems). The acquired data were aligned with the CAD reference data by using a reference best-fit alignment method. Adaptation evaluation

Marginal and internal adaptation of the crowns was assessed by using a dual scanning method.40,41 First, the tooth preparation casts were scanned with the intraoral scanning system (TRIOS 3, 3Shape A/S). Then, a low-viscosity silicone impression material (Fit Checker II, GC Corp) was evenly applied to the intaglio surface of the crowns, which were subsequently seated on the abutment teeth. A constant vertical force of 20 N was maintained during the full polymerization of the silicone. The thickness of the silicone film indicated internal and marginal discrepancies. Following polymerization, the crown was removed, and the abutment teeth with polymerized silicone films were rescanned with the intraoral scanner. The 3D comparative analysis was performed by using a specialized metrology software program (Geomagic Control X, 3D Systems). Adjacent teeth served as reference landmarks for best fit alignment. The 3D discrepancy between data was evaluated on marginal, axial, and occlusal regions across 30 standardized measurement points per region. The annotation points for three measurement areas are shown in Figure 1. Each group provided 300 measurement points from 10 crowns per region for detailed analysis, and the average of 30 points is recorded as the fit for this region of a crown. Statistical analyses were performed using the mean value per crown as the unit of analysis. Alignment accuracy was confirmed by using adjacent teeth landmarks, maintaining RMS values below 20 μm as the acceptance threshold.21

Statistical analysis

The data were processed by using statistical software programs (IBM SPSS Statistics v27.0, IBM Corp). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality of data distribution and residuals. The Levene’s test was used to test the homogeneity of variance. Normally distributed data were analysed with a 2-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

Results

Dimensional accuracy

The dimensional accuracy of the overall, external, marginal, and intaglio regions of incisor and molar crowns fabricated by ACJ and SM are presented in Table 1. Data of the accuracy test met the normal distribution (P > .05 in the Shapiro-Wilk test). The 2-way ANOVA was used and revealed significant difference in manufacturing method across all four regions (P < .001). The post hoc actual power of sample size was calculated by a software program (G*Power v.3.1.9.6; Heinrich-Heine University), and the actual power calculated was 0.93. Compared with SM, ACJ crowns demonstrated significantly lower RMS values for both incisors and molars in the overall, external, marginal, and intaglio regions (P < .001). Incisor crowns exhibited significantly lower RMS values than molar crowns in the overall and external regions (P < .05), while no significant difference was observed in the marginal region (P > .05).

Adaptation evaluation

The adaptation data of the marginal, axial, and occlusal surfaces of incisor and molar crowns fabricated by using ACJ and SM are presented in Table 2. The Shapiro-Wilk test indicated that the data follow normal distribution (P > .05). Regarding the manufacturing methods, ACJ crowns exhibited significantly smaller marginal gaps (59.9 ±15.1 μm and 85.5 ±17.1 μm respectively) and occlusal gaps (116.5 ±59.2 μm and 220.3 ±65.0 μm respectively) than SM crowns in molars and smaller axial gaps in incisors (61.1 ±17.0 μm and 76.0 ±13.4 μm respectively) (P < .05). Besides, ACJ showed similar marginal gaps, occlusal gaps in incisors, and axial gaps in molars compared with SM (P > .05). With respect to tooth type, incisor crowns demonstrated larger margin discrepancies in ACJ, larger axial and occlusal discrepancies in SM than molar crowns (P < .05).

Discussion

This study investigated the dimensional accuracy and adaptation of complete crowns fabricated with ACJ and SM by using tooth preparation data generated during routine practice. Significant differences were found between ACJ and SM crowns, as well as between incisor and molar crowns. Thus, the null hypotheses were rejected.

The dimensional accuracy of the restoration directly affects its clinical performance and longevity. Accuracy in the intaglio and marginal regions directly influences crown seating and margin adaptation, and inaccuracies in the external region may also increase clinical chairside time.25 In the present study, ACJ and SM crowns showed significant differences in dimensional accuracy across the overall, external, marginal, and intaglio regions. ACJ crowns exhibited superior dimensional accuracy compared with SM crowns. Both manufacturing methods showed RMS values met clinical requirements (<100 μm).18 Previous studies have also reported relatively high accuracy of ACJ crowns. Zhu et al evaluated the accuracy of ACJ fabricated complete crowns using an in vitro standardized model and reported comparable dimensional accuracy between ACJ and SM crowns.23 Lyu et al reported that ACJ crowns had better overall and external dimensional accuracy and comparable accuracy at the margin and intaglio regions between ACJ and SM.21 The findings of the present study also indicate that ACJ achieves high accuracy, but the difference compared with SM was more pronounced than previously reported. This may be attributable to the use of clinically realistic preparations in the current study. Compared with the controlled standardized tooth preparations, imperfect clinical procedures could produce sharper line angles and irregularities on tooth preparations in clinical practice. Due to the diameter limitations of milling burs, SM technology has difficulty reproducing fine details smaller than 1 mm,9 which can result in positive errors in grooves and negative errors in intaglio regions. The high dimensional accuracy of ACJ can also be attributed to its fine layer thickness of 5 to 10 μm, which could reproduce intricate details.20 Incisor crowns demonstrated significantly higher dimensional accuracy than molar crowns in the present study. This may be because molars have more complex anatomical grooves, where positive errors were predominantly observed.

Adaptation refers to the marginal and internal gaps between the restoration and the abutment tooth, with a marginal gap of <120 μm considered clinically acceptable.33 In this study, ACJ crowns exhibited significantly smaller margin gaps in molar crowns and similar margin gaps in incisor crowns compared with SM crowns. This can be attributed to its higher accuracy. ACJ crowns demonstrated better accuracy than SM crowns at both the marginal and intaglio surfaces. Previous studies have also demonstrated that ACJ exhibits superior marginal adaptation compared with SM.21,23 However, previous studies reported comparable axial and occlusal adaptation between SM and ACJ.21,23 In this study, molar crowns fabricated using the ACJ demonstrated significantly better occlusal adaptation than SM technique. The difference may be because previous studies used standardized preparations with rounded line angles, whereas sharp line angles are frequently encountered in clinical practice. In the SM workflow, CAD software needs to compensate for thin areas of the preparation to ensure that the corresponding regions of the crown can be manufactured by milling burs. This can create larger occlusal gaps between the crown and the tooth preparation in SM group.31,32 An increased occlusal gap can adversely affect stress distribution across the crown surface, impeding uniform load dissipation and potentially compromising the long term stability of the restoration.37 Furthermore, inadequate internal fit may weaken the adhesive interface, predisposing restorations on shorter abutment teeth to debonding. Positive deviations along axial surfaces may also impair seating accuracy, thereby compromising marginal adaptation. Incisors exhibited poorer adaptability than molars across most regions. This may be attributed to the thin and sharp morphology of incisor preparations.29 In SM, the fixed bur radius limits the minimum machinable dimensions and angles, resulting in overmilling of thin, sharp line angles in the intaglio surfaces of crowns. This overcompensation leads to larger internal gaps between the crown and the tooth preparation.9,10,11,30 In contrast, the rounded features of molars allow for superior clinical performance and greater stability, as they are less affected by this limitation. The present study utilized clinical dental laboratory data, making the findings more reflective of actual clinical settings.

Currently, no gold-standard method exists for evaluating the marginal and internal fit of dental crowns. Conventional silicone replica techniques are limited to 2-dimensional sectioning and may be affected by material deformation, potentially compromising accuracy. In this study, a digital superimposition method was applied to enable non-destructive 3D assessment. The intraoral scanner used in this study had an accuracy of 6.9 μm according to the manufacturer, which is sufficiently high for the small scans of 3 teeth model.18,21

Limitations of the present study included that only one type of ACJ and one SM system were evaluated. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to all available systems. The sample size was limited to 10 crowns per group, which restricts the generalizability of the findings. Although the manufacturer reports a trueness of approximately 6.9 μm for the intraoral scanner in the present study, scanning accuracy may vary depending on object geometry and acquisition conditions. Therefore, absolute values should be interpreted cautiously, while the standardized protocol ensures valid intergroup comparisons. As an emerging manufacturing approach, ACJ demonstrates promising fabrication precision. Although subtractive milling of zirconia restorations is supported by well-established industrial workflows, ACJ remains less widely adopted and currently involves higher equipment and material costs. Future studies involving long term clinical follow-ups are needed to better evaluate the durability, clinical longevity, and reliability of monolithic zirconia crowns based on clinical laboratory data.

Conclusions

This study compared the accuracy and adaptation of a novel material jetting technique versus milled monolithic zirconia crowns based on clinical laboratory data. Within the limitations of the current study, both ACJ and SM crowns offer dimensional accuracy and adaptation within clinically acceptable ranges. Monolithic zirconia crowns fabricated by using ACJ demonstrated superior dimensional accuracy and adaptation compared with those fabricated by using SM based on clinical laboratory data.
