Longitudinal immune-inflammatory profiles and mortality in older adults with sepsis: a multicentre prospective cohort study
Authors: Xianwen Wang, Miaomiao Fu, Chunhui Li, Jialin Jin, Na Cui, Xuelian Liao, Zhanwen Wang, Zhihong Zuo, Qihang Huang, Lina Zhang, Zhaoxin Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Population ageing is reshaping the epidemiology of critical illness. Older adults account for an increasing proportion of patients with severe infection and sepsis, and age remains one of the strongest determinants of infection-related morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. Compared with younger adults, older individuals are more likely to develop organ dysfunction after infection and have poorer short- and long-term outcomes once sepsis occurs [2, 3]. However, the biological features that distinguish uncomplicated infection from sepsis in older adults remain incompletely defined, particularly in large prospective cohorts with serial immune-inflammatory assessment.

Ageing is accompanied by progressive remodelling of the immune system, commonly referred to as immunosenescence, together with chronic low-grade inflammation, or inflammaging [4, 5]. These changes include impaired adaptive immune responses, altered lymphocyte subset composition, reduced immune reserve, and increased circulating inflammatory mediators [4–6]. During sepsis, these pre-existing age-related alterations may interact with acute infection-induced immune dysregulation, resulting in a complex state of concurrent hyperinflammation and immunosuppression [7–9]. This dual process is clinically important because persistent inflammation, lymphocyte depletion, and impaired cellular immunity have been associated with secondary infections, organ failure, and death after sepsis [8–11]. Nevertheless, most previous studies have focused either on selected immune markers, mixed-age populations, or single time points, limiting the ability to define ageing-specific immune-inflammatory patterns across the infection severity spectrum.

A clearer understanding of these patterns is needed to improve biological risk stratification in older adults with sepsis. In particular, it remains uncertain whether immune-cell depletion and cytokine activation in sepsis among older adults merely reflect differences in age, sex, and comorbidity burden, or whether they persist after adjustment for these confounders. It is also unclear whether early immune-inflammatory abnormalities remain observable during early follow-up and are associated with adverse outcomes. In this study, we defined older adults as individuals aged 60 years or older because the cohort was designed to address ageing-related immune-inflammatory profiles in the older-adult population commonly considered in geriatric clinical research. Patients younger than 60 years were not enrolled because the primary objective was to characterize immune-inflammatory heterogeneity within older adults rather than to compare immune responses across the adult age spectrum. Therefore, in this multicentre prospective observational cohort, we characterized immune and inflammatory biomarker profiles across healthy older adults, older adults with nonsevere infection, and older adults with sepsis. We further examined age-stratified immune-inflammatory patterns, model-estimated longitudinal profiles from ICU day 1 to day 7, and the association between day-1 immune-inflammatory biomarkers and in-hospital mortality. We hypothesized that, after accounting for measured demographic and comorbidity differences, sepsis in older adults would be associated with a circulating immune-inflammatory pattern of lower cellular immune markers and higher cytokine levels, and that early immune-inflammatory separation among patients with sepsis would be associated with mortality.

Methods

Study design and participants

This multicentre prospective observational cohort study was conducted in four tertiary hospitals in China between June 2023 and October 2024. The study aimed to characterize immune-inflammatory profiles across the spectrum from health to nonsevere infection and sepsis in older adults, and to examine longitudinal immune-inflammatory profiles and their association with in-hospital mortality among patients with sepsis. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) recommendations [12] and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [13]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legally authorized representatives. The study protocol was approved by the local ethics committees of all participating centres.

Eligible patients were aged 60 years or older and had clinician-confirmed suspected or documented infection. The age threshold of 60 years was selected to define the older-adult population of interest. Patients younger than 60 years were not enrolled because the study was designed to characterize immune-inflammatory profiles within older adults rather than to compare younger and older adults. Patients were excluded if they died or were discharged within 24 h after enrollment, or if informed consent could not be obtained. Age-matched healthy older adults recruited from a health screening centre served as healthy controls. These participants were intended to provide a reference profile of relatively healthy older adults and were not hospitalized noninfected controls. Hospitalized noninfected older adults were not included in the present study. Participants were categorized as healthy controls, nonsevere infection, or sepsis. Sepsis was defined according to Sepsis-3 as infection associated with an acute increase in Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score of at least 2 points [14]. Infected patients not meeting this criterion were classified as having nonsevere infection. The study design and participant classification are shown in Fig. 1. Complete screening logs with the total number of initially assessed individuals and detailed exclusion counts were not uniformly available across centres; therefore, Fig. 1 presents the final analytic cohort, eligibility criteria, exclusion criteria, group definitions, and longitudinal biomarker availability in the sepsis cohort. ICU day 1 was defined as the calendar day of ICU admission and study enrollment.

Data collection, biomarkers, and outcomes

Clinical data were collected using standardized case report forms, including demographics, comorbidities, infection characteristics, pathogen categories, organ dysfunction, organ-supportive therapies, routine laboratory indices, and immune-inflammatory biomarkers. Infection-related variables included infection source, pathogen category, and multiple infection. Treatment-related variables included antimicrobial therapy, glucocorticoid exposure, immunomodulatory drug use, vasoactive agent use, and mechanical ventilation when available. These variables were summarized descriptively because treatment exposure may reflect both baseline severity and subsequent clinical decision-making. Frailty, functional status, detailed nutritional assessment, baseline long-term immunosuppressive therapy before admission, antimicrobial timing, cumulative corticosteroid dose, vasopressor dose, renal replacement therapy, and detailed source-control timing were not uniformly available and therefore could not be incorporated into the primary adjusted models. Biomarker assessment included circulating lymphocyte subsets, cytokines, complement components, and immunoglobulins. The prespecified key biomarkers included lymphocyte count, total T-cell count, cluster of differentiation 4 (CD4+) T-cell count, cluster of differentiation 8 (CD8+) T-cell count, natural killer (NK)-cell count, interleukin (IL)−6, IL-8, IL-10, complement component 3 (C3), and albumin.

For patients with sepsis, biomarker measurements were scheduled on ICU day 1, day 3, and day 7 whenever available. Missing repeated measurements occurred because of death, discharge, transfer out of the ICU, or inability to obtain scheduled samples. The previous clinical infection-stage framework, including early, severe, and recovery phases, was not retained in the revised analysis to avoid overlap with the fixed time-point longitudinal strategy.

The primary clinical outcome was in-hospital mortality. Time from enrollment to in-hospital death or discharge was used in survival analyses, with patients discharged alive censored at hospital discharge.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2. Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Baseline comparisons across healthy controls, nonsevere infection, and sepsis were descriptive and were assessed using standard parametric, non-parametric, or categorical tests as appropriate.

To account for baseline imbalance, adjusted cross-sectional comparisons were performed using multivariable linear regression models for prespecified biomarkers. Sepsis was compared separately with healthy controls and nonsevere infection. Models were adjusted for age, sex, hypertension, coronary heart disease, diabetes, autoimmune disease, and malignant tumour. Skewed biomarkers were log-transformed where appropriate, and outcomes were standardized to allow comparison across biomarkers. Results are reported as standardized β coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Overlap-weighted analyses were performed as sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the adjusted cross-sectional findings after balancing baseline covariates. These adjusted and weighted analyses were intended to reduce measured imbalance but were not interpreted as establishing sepsis-specific biology independent of all potential confounding.

Age-stratified analyses were conducted separately in healthy controls and patients with sepsis using the age categories 60–69, 70–79, and ≥ 80 years. These analyses were descriptive and intended to explore whether age-related immune-inflammatory patterns differed between healthy ageing and sepsis.

Model-estimated longitudinal profiles in the sepsis cohort were analysed using linear mixed-effects models with patient-level random intercepts to account for repeated measurements among available scheduled measurements [15]. Fixed effects included time point, in-hospital outcome group, and the time-by-outcome interaction. Time was modelled categorically as ICU day 1, day 3, and day 7. Models were adjusted for age, sex, hospital, hypertension, coronary heart disease, diabetes, autoimmune disease, and malignant tumour. Model-estimated marginal means and 95% confidence intervals were derived for each biomarker at each time point. Because repeated measurements were affected by death, discharge, ICU transfer, and unsuccessful scheduled sampling, missingness was considered potentially informative. Standard mixed-effects models assume missing-at-random mechanisms conditional on observed covariates, an assumption that may not be fully satisfied in this setting. Therefore, longitudinal results were interpreted as associations among available scheduled measurements rather than unbiased population-level recovery trajectories. Sampling availability was summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Associations between day-1 biomarkers and in-hospital mortality were assessed using Cox proportional hazards models. Univariable Cox analyses were first performed for candidate clinical and biomarker variables. A prespecified multivariable model included age, sex, hospital, major comorbidities, SOFA score, IL-10, and NK-cell count. Age was modelled per 10-year increase. IL-10 and NK-cell count were transformed where appropriate and standardized; hazard ratios (HRs) for biomarkers are therefore presented per 1-standard-deviation increase. A sensitivity model replaced IL-10 with IL-6.

P values from the principal adjusted cross-sectional analyses, longitudinal mixed-effects models, and univariable survival analyses were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. Findings were considered statistically robust when they remained significant after false-discovery-rate correction. All tests were two-sided.

Additional details regarding data preprocessing, overlap-weighted sensitivity analyses, longitudinal mixed-effects models, survival analyses, and multiple-testing correction are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

A severity-related gradient was observed for routine laboratory and immune-inflammatory biomarkers. Lymphocyte count declined from 1.80 × 109/L in healthy controls to 1.21 × 109/L in nonsevere infection and 0.65 × 10⁹/L in sepsis. Albumin and platelet counts were also lower in sepsis, whereas urea and creatinine were higher. Lower circulating cellular immune markers were prominent across the infection spectrum. Median total T-cell counts decreased from 1091 cells/μL in healthy controls to 748 cells/μL in nonsevere infection and 387 cells/μL in sepsis. CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell counts showed similar reductions. The reduction in circulating NK-cell counts was particularly marked, with median NK-cell counts falling from 480 to 168 cells/μL and 78 cells/μL across the three groups. In contrast, cytokine levels increased substantially with infection severity. Median IL-6 increased from 1.65 pg/mL in healthy controls to 45.50 pg/mL in sepsis, while median IL-10 increased from 0.00 to 4.00 pg/mL. Unadjusted biomarker gradients are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Infection characteristics and ICU therapies among patients with sepsis are summarized in Supplementary Table 3. Lung infection was the most common infection source, followed by abdominal and bloodstream infection. Gram-negative bacteria, non-fermenting bacilli, and fungal infection were frequently identified. Most patients received antimicrobial therapy, and substantial proportions received glucocorticoids, vasoactive agents, and mechanical ventilation. These findings indicate clinically important heterogeneity in infection source, pathogen distribution, and treatment exposure within the sepsis cohort.

Adjusted circulating immune-inflammatory patterns associated with sepsis

Given baseline differences in age, sex, and comorbidity burden, adjusted cross-sectional analyses were performed for prespecified key biomarkers. After adjustment for age, sex, hypertension, coronary heart disease, diabetes, autoimmune disease, and malignant tumour, sepsis remained associated with a consistent circulating immune-inflammatory pattern (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 4). Compared with healthy controls, sepsis was associated with higher IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 and lower lymphocyte count, lymphocyte percentage, total T-cell count, CD8⁺ T-cell count, NK-cell count, C3, and albumin after false-discovery-rate correction. A similar pattern was observed when sepsis was compared with nonsevere infection, with additional reductions in C4. These findings suggest that, after adjustment for measured demographic and comorbidity variables, sepsis was associated with a circulating immune-inflammatory pattern involving higher cytokine levels, lower cellular immune markers, and lower albumin. Because healthy controls were recruited from a health screening centre and residual confounding may remain, these results should not be interpreted as representing pure sepsis-specific biology.

Overlap-weighted sensitivity analyses supported these findings. Adequate covariate balance was achieved after weighting, with all maximum absolute standardized mean differences below 0.1 (Supplementary Table 5). Weighted analyses showed stepwise reductions in lymphocyte, T-cell, and NK-cell counts from healthy controls to nonsevere infection and sepsis, accompanied by progressive increases in IL-6 and IL-10. Thus, the adjusted circulating immune-inflammatory pattern associated with sepsis was directionally consistent across complementary approaches to measured confounding control.

Age-stratified immune-inflammatory patterns

Age-stratified analyses were performed separately in healthy controls and patients with sepsis (Supplementary Table 6). Among healthy controls, ageing was associated mainly with B-cell decline and low-grade cytokine elevation. B-cell counts decreased from 188 cells/μL in participants aged 60–69 years to 117 cells/μL in those aged ≥ 80 years, while NK-cell percentages and IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 levels increased with age.

Among patients with sepsis on ICU day 1, the age-related pattern differed. Absolute lymphocyte and T-cell counts did not vary substantially by age, but the percentages of total T cells, CD4⁺ T cells, and CD8⁺ T cells declined with advancing age. Conversely, NK-cell counts and percentages increased across age strata. Cytokine, complement, and immunoglobulin levels showed no major age-related differences in the sepsis cohort.

The main effect of outcome remained significant after false-discovery-rate correction for all eight prespecified biomarkers. Among patients with available scheduled measurements, non-survivors had consistently higher model-estimated SOFA scores and higher IL-6 and IL-10 levels across follow-up. In contrast, survivors maintained higher lymphocyte counts, total T-cell counts, CD8⁺ T-cell counts, NK-cell counts, and C3 levels from ICU day 1 to day 7. Significant overall time effects were observed for SOFA score, lymphocyte count, total T-cell count, CD8⁺ T-cell count, IL-6, IL-10, and C3. No time-by-outcome interaction remained significant after correction, indicating that the available data supported persistent between-group separation among measured samples rather than robust evidence of divergent temporal recovery or deterioration.

Day-1 biomarkers associated with in-hospital mortality

Day-1 associations with in-hospital mortality were examined using multivariable Cox regression models (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 8). In the prespecified model, higher SOFA score was associated with increased mortality risk (HR 1.087 per point increase), as was higher IL-10 (HR 1.448 per 1-SD increase). Conversely, higher NK-cell count was associated with lower mortality risk (HR 0.821 per 1-SD increase). Age was also associated with mortality when modelled per 10-year increase. In a sensitivity model replacing IL-10 with IL-6, higher SOFA score and IL-6 were associated with increased mortality, whereas higher NK-cell count remained associated with lower mortality risk. These findings suggest that early mortality risk in older adults with sepsis was associated with a combined clinical and circulating immune-inflammatory profile involving organ dysfunction, cytokine activation, and lower cellular immune markers.

Discussion

In this multicentre prospective cohort of 1,851 older adults, we observed a consistent circulating immune-inflammatory pattern across the continuum from health to nonsevere infection and sepsis. After adjustment for measured demographic factors and major comorbidities, sepsis was associated with lower circulating lymphocyte, T-cell, CD8⁺ T-cell, and NK-cell counts, together with higher IL-6 and IL-10 levels. Among patients with sepsis, non-survivors showed consistently higher model-estimated organ dysfunction and cytokine levels and lower cellular immune markers among available scheduled measurements from ICU day 1 to day 7. In addition, day-1 SOFA score, IL-10, and NK-cell count were associated with in-hospital mortality in adjusted Cox models.

These findings support the current concept that sepsis is not simply uncontrolled inflammation, but a dysregulated host response involving simultaneous inflammatory activation and immune suppression [2, 14, 16–18]. This concept is particularly relevant in older adults, who have high infection-related mortality and limited physiological reserve [2, 16–18]. Our data extend previous work by showing that the transition from nonsevere infection to sepsis in older adults is accompanied by an adjusted circulating immune-inflammatory pattern of lower immune-cell counts and higher cytokine levels. Because frailty and functional status were not uniformly collected, residual confounding by baseline vulnerability, infection characteristics, and treatment exposure cannot be excluded.

Ageing-related immune remodelling may provide a vulnerable substrate for this response. Immunosenescence and inflammaging are associated with altered lymphocyte composition, impaired adaptive immunity, clonal expansion of differentiated T cells, and chronic low-grade cytokine activation [8, 9, 19–21]. In our cohort, healthy ageing was mainly associated with B-cell decline and modest cytokine elevation, whereas sepsis showed more profound cellular immune depletion. These findings suggest that sepsis in older adults may reflect an acute immune-inflammatory disturbance superimposed on baseline immune ageing, rather than a simple acceleration of normal ageing biology.

Lower circulating cellular immune markers were a prominent feature of the observed circulating immune-inflammatory pattern. Septic lymphopenia has been associated with persistent immunosuppression, secondary infections, chronic critical illness, and mortality [22–26]. The reduction in circulating CD8⁺ T-cell counts may reflect impaired cytotoxic immune surveillance and reduced cellular immune reserve. Lower circulating NK-cell counts were also associated with mortality. This finding is biologically plausible because NK cells participate in cytotoxic responses, cytokine production, and innate–adaptive immune crosstalk [27–29]. However, our study measured only circulating NK-cell numbers and did not assess NK-cell cytotoxicity, cytokine production, receptor expression, apoptosis, or tissue redistribution. Therefore, lower NK-cell counts should be interpreted as a circulating immune phenotypic marker associated with adverse clinical status rather than evidence that NK-cell depletion is a causal driver of mortality or a validated therapeutic target. Future studies incorporating functional NK-cell assays are needed to determine whether reduced circulating NK-cell counts reflect true depletion, impaired production, apoptosis, redistribution to infected tissues, or treatment-related effects.

Cytokine activation represented the second major component of this dysregulated state. IL-6 increased markedly with infection severity and remained higher in non-survivors, consistent with its role in systemic inflammation, endothelial activation, coagulation disturbance, and organ injury [30–32]. IL-10, although anti-inflammatory, may indicate compensatory immunosuppression, monocyte deactivation, and immune paralysis in sepsis [8, 9, 19]. The association of IL-10 with mortality, and the directionally consistent sensitivity analysis using IL-6, suggest that both inflammatory and counter-inflammatory activation are relevant to adverse outcomes.

The longitudinal findings should be interpreted cautiously. Mixed-effects models showed between-group separation among available scheduled measurements from ICU day 1 to day 7, rather than robustly divergent temporal slopes. Because follow-up sampling was affected by death, discharge, ICU transfer, and unsuccessful sample acquisition, these profiles should not be interpreted as complete immune recovery trajectories or unbiased population-level temporal changes. The early between-group separation observed in our cohort is consistent with transcriptomic and clinical endotyping studies showing that sepsis includes distinct host-response states at or near presentation [33–37].

These findings may inform future development of immune-guided risk stratification and trial enrichment strategies, but they should not be considered ready for direct clinical implementation. SOFA score remains essential for organ dysfunction assessment, but it does not directly quantify host immune reserve. Combining organ dysfunction with circulating lymphocyte subsets, NK-cell count, and selected cytokines may help characterize clinically relevant immune-inflammatory heterogeneity in older adults with sepsis. However, these results should not be interpreted as a ready-to-use prediction model or as proof of a therapeutic target. Future studies should externally validate these biomarkers and integrate functional assays, including monocyte human leukocyte antigen–DR (HLA-DR), ex vivo cytokine responses, T-cell exhaustion markers, and NK-cell cytotoxicity. Such work may support biomarker-guided immunomodulatory strategies, including interleukin-7 (IL-7), granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), and precision immunotherapy approaches [38–42].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all centres were tertiary hospitals in China, which may limit generalizability. Complete screening logs with the total number of initially assessed individuals and detailed exclusion counts were not uniformly available across centres; therefore, only the final analytic cohort could be reported, limiting assessment of patient selection and potential selection bias. Second, healthy controls were recruited from a health screening centre rather than from hospitalized noninfected older adults. Although multivariable adjustment and overlap weighting were used to reduce measured imbalance, residual confounding by hospitalization status, frailty, nutritional reserve, functional status, baseline immunosuppressive therapy, infection characteristics, and treatment exposure remains possible. Third, antimicrobial timing, cumulative corticosteroid dose, vasopressor dose, renal replacement therapy, and source-control timing were not uniformly available. Fourth, repeated sampling was affected by death, discharge, ICU transfer, and unsuccessful sample acquisition, making informative attrition unavoidable. Therefore, longitudinal analyses should be interpreted as model-estimated profiles among available scheduled measurements rather than complete recovery trajectories. Finally, this study measured circulating phenotypic biomarkers rather than immune function; mechanistic causality and therapeutic implications require validation in studies incorporating functional immune assays.

Conclusions

In this multicentre prospective cohort of older adults, sepsis was associated with a circulating immune-inflammatory pattern across the infection severity spectrum. After adjustment for measured demographic factors and major comorbidities, older adults with sepsis showed lower circulating lymphocyte, T-cell, CD8⁺ T-cell, and NK-cell counts, together with higher cytokine levels. Among patients with sepsis, this immune-inflammatory separation was already evident on ICU day 1 and remained observable among patients with available follow-up measurements, with non-survivors showing higher organ dysfunction and cytokine levels and lower circulating cellular immune markers. Day-1 SOFA score, IL-10 or IL-6, and NK-cell count were associated with in-hospital mortality. These findings suggest that early combined assessment of organ dysfunction, cytokine activation, and circulating cellular immune markers may help characterize clinically relevant immune-inflammatory heterogeneity in older adults with sepsis. Because of the observational design, the association between NK-cell count and mortality should not be interpreted as causal, and external validation with functional immune assessment is required before clinical application.
