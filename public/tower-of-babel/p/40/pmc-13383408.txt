Impact of professional experience and practice setting on clinical decision-making and anxiety in peri-implantitis management: a cross-sectional survey study
Authors: Burcu Kanmaz, Mehmet Gümüş Kanmaz, Nilay Er, Ahu Uraz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeri-implantitis management is clinically demanding and may be accompanied by professional anxiety; however, the extent to experience level and practice setting shape treatment decisions and anxiety has not been fully clarified. Previous surveys have described clinicians’ knowledge and attitudes toward peri-implantitis, yet evidence integrating therapeutic preferences with clinician anxiety remains scarce. This study assessed Turkish periodontists’ awareness and treatment priorities for peri-implant diseases and examined the influence of professional experience, postgraduate exposure, and workplace setting on treatment selection and anxiety during severe peri-implantitis management.MethodsA cross-sectional, web-based survey was conducted between April and December 2025 among periodontology specialists in Turkey. The questionnaire collected demographic and professional characteristics, education and awareness regarding peri-implant diseases, clinical approaches to peri-implantitis, and anxiety using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI). BAI scores were categorized as minimal (0–7), mild (8–15), moderate (16–25), and severe (26–63). Group comparisons were performed with chi-square tests. Multivariable binary logistic regression evaluated predictors of choosing surgical (vs. non-surgical) peri-implantitis therapy and predictors of the presence of anxiety (BAI ≥ 8). Statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0.ResultsData from 184 periodontists were analyzed (79.3% female; mean professional experience 14.50 ± 9.16 years). Most participants reported minimal anxiety during severe peri-implantitis management (78.3%), with mild (12.0%), moderate (6.5%), and severe anxiety (3.3%) less frequent. In regression analysis for treatment selection, private (vs. public) practice independently predicted a higher likelihood of choosing surgical modalities (OR = 2.212, 95% CI 1.171–4.177; p = 0.014). In regression analysis for anxiety, greater specialist experience was associated with lower odds of anxiety (OR = 0.839, 95% CI 0.760–0.925; p < 0.001), while gender and practice setting were not significant.ConclusionsAmong Turkish periodontists, anxiety related to severe peri-implantitis management was generally low and decreased with increasing specialty experience. Practice setting, rather than experience or anxiety, was the key independent predictor of choosing surgical treatment, with private-sector clinicians more likely to select surgical approaches. These findings support the value of structured postgraduate exposure, continued professional development, and early-career support to strengthen confident, evidence-based peri-implantitis care across diverse practice environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08540-y.Keywords: Anxiety, Dental implants, Peri-implantitis, Periodontics

Background

In recent years, dental implants have become a cornerstone in the rehabilitation of edentulous patients, driven by advances in osseointegration, surface technologies, and restorative-driven surgical planning [1, 2]. While these developments have reinforced the predictability of implant therapy, reported survival outcomes vary significantly across cohorts. Long-term retrospective evaluations report cumulative survival rates as high as 82.6% over 15 years; however, despite generally favorable outcomes, the long-term stability of osseointegrated implants remains vulnerable to biological complications affecting the peri-implant soft tissues and supporting bone [3, 4].

Similar to natural dentition, dental implants are susceptible to biofilm-mediated inflammatory infections. Bacterial accumulation on implant surfaces can initiate and sustain peri-implant mucosal inflammation, leading to progressive bone loss in compromised individuals [5, 6]. Epidemiological evidence underscores the magnitude of this issue, with a systematic review reporting weighted mean prevalence estimates of approximately 43% for peri-implant mucositis and 22% for peri-implantitis [7]. As implant therapy becomes ubiquitous, peri-implant diseases have evolved from occasional complications into a significant public health burden [8].

The effective prevention and management of these conditions necessitate structured multi-level strategies extending from initial treatment planning to long-term supportive care. Contemporary guidelines emphasize that prevention must be integrated into the surgical and prosthetic phases and sustained through periodic monitoring [9]. In this context, clinicians’ awareness of diagnostic indicators, risk-based decision-making, and evidence-based management protocols is of critical importance, particularly for specialists who routinely manage advanced peri-implant lesions.

Several survey-based studies have examined clinicians’ knowledge, education, and treatment preferences regarding peri-implantitis in different countries and professional groups [10–14]. These studies have highlighted considerable variability in training experiences, diagnostic preferences, referral patterns, and therapeutic choices. However, they did not specifically integrate treatment preferences with clinicians’ anxiety when managing severe peri-implantitis, nor did they focus on how postgraduate exposure and practice setting jointly shape these responses within a national sample of periodontists. Accordingly, the present study extends the existing literature by evaluating both clinical decision-making and anxiety in the same analytic framework.

Concurrent with these clinical demands, dentistry is recognized as a profession associated with substantial occupational stress, which may manifest in adverse psychological and physiological outcomes [15, 16]. The management of severe peri-implantitis may further intensify this burden because of clinical uncertainty related to defect morphology, patient-level risk factors, implant-surface characteristics, and variable treatment responses. Moreover, the absence of uniformly predictable outcomes, especially in advanced cases, can reinforce the perception of uncertainty during clinical decision-making [17]. Therefore, this study aims to assess periodontists’ awareness and therapeutic priorities regarding peri-implant diseases. Furthermore, it investigates the influence of professional experience on procedure-related anxiety and examines how factors such as the practice environment shape clinical decision-making in the management of severe peri-implantitis.

Methods

Study design and ethical considerations

This cross-sectional, survey-based study was conducted between April 2025 and December 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Izmir Tinaztepe University (Decision No: MUKCE2025/19, Date: 20/02/2025) and was conducted in strict adherence to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Digital informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to accessing the survey. The anonymity and confidentiality of all participants’ data were rigorously maintained throughout the study.

Participants and setting

The study population consisted of specialists in Periodontology practicing in Turkey, as these clinicians are recognized as the primary providers responsible for the management of biological peri-implant complications. The inclusion criteria were defined as: (1) holding a specialty or PhD degree in Periodontology, and (2) active involvement in dental implant surgery and/or peri-implantitis therapy in clinical practice.

Sample size calculation

A priori power analysis was conducted to determine the minimum sample size required to achieve sufficient statistical power for multivariate logistic regression analyses. Using G*Power software (Version 3.1.9.4, Düsseldorf, Germany), the calculation aimed to detect a medium effect size (odds ratio ≈ 1.5–2.0) with an alpha error probability of 0.05 and a statistical power of 95% for models including up to six independent variables (e.g., experience, workplace, education). The analysis indicated a minimum requirement of 147 participants. Consequently, the final sample of 184 periodontists exceeded this threshold, ensuring robust statistical power for the planned multivariable analyses.

Data collection and survey instrument

Data were collected using a structured, web-based questionnaire (Google Forms) distributed via Turkish Periodontology Association communication channels, professional email lists and social media platforms. The survey items related to peri-implantitis were largely adapted from the instrument developed by Polymeri et al. [10] designed to assess education, knowledge, attitudes, and professional behavior. Items were retained or contextually modified to align with the specific aims of the present study and the local practice setting [10]. In addition, the Beck Anxiety Inventory [18] was used to measure the anxiety experienced by clinicians in cases of severe peri-implantitis. BAI scores were categorized as minimal anxiety (0–7), mild anxiety (8–15), moderate anxiety (16–25), and severe anxiety (26–63).

The final survey comprised four distinct sections:

Demographic and Professional Profile: Age, gender, workplace setting, years of professional experience, and implant practice volume.

Prior to the main study, a pilot test was conducted with 30 specialists to evaluate the clarity and reliability of the questionnaire; internal consistency was found to be high (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.863). The final survey instrument was provided as a supplementary file.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 25.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk tests. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). For comparative analysis, the Chi-square test was employed to evaluate differences in categorical variables (e.g., treatment choices, knowledge levels) across different subgroups. Cronbach’s alpha was calculated to verify the internal consistency of the scales used. To identify predictors of surgical treatment choice and factors influencing professional anxiety levels, multivariate binary logistic regression analysis was performed. For regression analyses, preferred therapeutic approach was dichotomized as non-surgical (education/maintenance and non-surgical debridement with or without adjunctive medications) versus surgical (flap surgery, regenerative surgery). Implant removal/replacement and laser therapy responses were excluded from this dichotomized outcome due to low frequency. Practice setting was recorded in five categories and recorded for regression analyses as public (public university hospitals and public oral health centers) versus private (private clinics, private polyclinics, and foundation/private universities) and ‘presence of anxiety’ was defined as BAI ≥ 8 (mild/moderate/severe) and ‘absence of anxiety’ as BAI 0–7 (minimal). For all statistical tests, a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Internal consistency was acceptable in the pilot sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.863) and was re-estimated in the final study sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.870). A total of 184 periodontists were analyzed. Most participants were female (79.3%, n = 146) and the largest age group was 31–40 years (59.8%, n = 110). Participants most commonly worked at public universities (42.4%, n = 78), followed by private polyclinics (23.9%, n = 44). Weekly working time was most frequently 35–40 hours (38.0%, n = 70) or > 40 hours (30.4%, n = 56). Mean professional experience was 14.50 ± 9.16 years. During residency education, the reported training volume devoted to implant surgeries was most often 21–30% (27.2%, n = 50). Mean duration of residency was 4.25 ± 1.12 years and mean specialist experience was 8.75 ± 9.00 years. Most participants reported having peri-implant disease management during residency (87.0%, n = 160) (Table 1).

Across clinical parameters used in peri-implant disease assessment, most respondents rated probing depth, radiographic bone loss, bleeding, and suppuration as “important” or “very important,” with no significant group differences (all p > 0.05). A significant difference emerged for “mucosal recession / implant exposure” (p = 0.001), with the group without peri-implant disease management during residency more frequently endorsing this parameter as “very important.” Regarding what was considered important for successful treatment, the “very important” category predominated for oral hygiene care and plaque control in both groups (p > 0.05). However, ratings differed significantly for mechanical debridement (p = 0.009), chemical surface decontamination (p = 0.025), and local/systemic antibiotic therapy (p = 0.049), with the group lacking peri-implant disease management during residency more often selecting “very important” for these modalities. In the analysis of specific surgical interventions, significant group differences were observed for guided tissue regeneration (p = 0.002), resective surgical procedures (p < 0.001), implantoplasty (p = 0.014), and photodynamic therapy (p = 0.027). Autogenous graft, allograft, xenograft, laser treatment, and air polishing did not differ significantly between groups (all p > 0.05) (Table 3).

The proportion of clinicians maintaining records of peri-implant disease cases did not differ by peri-implant disease management during residency (p = 0.865). For teeth adjacent to implants diagnosed with peri-implantitis, periodontal infection was the most frequently reported finding overall (75.0%, n = 138), without a significant difference between groups (p = 0.500). When asked about the primary etiologic factor for peri-implantitis, responses differed significantly between groups (p < 0.001), although “poor oral hygiene and periodontal problems” was the predominant choice overall (81.5%, n = 150). Treatment selection for peri-implantitis also differed significantly (p = 0.022). Overall, the most frequently selected approach was mechanical debridement with medication therapy (47.8%, n = 88), followed by flap surgery with implant surface detoxification (30.4%, n = 56). Among reasons for avoiding peri-implantitis treatment, “unpredictability of treatment outcomes” showed a significant between-group difference (p = 0.022) and was more frequently endorsed by those without peri-implant disease management during residency (Table 4).

Discussion

This cross-sectional survey evaluated how postgraduate clinical exposure, years of specialty experience, and practice setting influence Turkish periodontists’ perceptions, therapeutic preferences, and anxiety related to the management of severe peri-implantitis. Overall, the findings indicate that structured educational exposure and accumulated clinical experience are associated with greater self-perceived competence and lower anxiety, whereas practice setting independently influences the likelihood of choosing surgical interventions.

Participants who reported peri-implant disease management during postgraduate training consistently described higher self-perceived levels of education regarding peri-implant disease risk factors, diagnosis, and treatment than those without such exposure. This finding is concordant with previous survey-based studies showing that formal education and hands-on training are strongly associated with clinicians’ confidence and decision-making in peri-implantitis management [10, 11]. By contrast, awareness of major established risk factors such as poor oral hygiene, periodontitis history, smoking, and diabetes mellitus was high in both groups, suggesting that foundational knowledge about peri-implant disease is broadly disseminated among specialists.

The greater emphasis placed on non-surgical modalities and adjunctive therapies by clinicians without postgraduate exposure may reflect a more conservative therapeutic philosophy when familiarity with advanced peri-implantitis cases is limited. Similar tendencies have been reported previously, with less experienced or less specifically trained clinicians showing stronger preferences for mechanical debridement and antimicrobial adjuncts over surgical approaches [12–14, 19]. At the same time, the current evidence base continues to show that non-surgical therapy alone often has limited capacity to resolve advanced peri-implantitis, particularly when deep pockets and complex osseous defects are present [9, 19].

One of the most notable findings of the present study is that working in the private sector was the only independent predictor of choosing surgical treatment for severe peri-implantitis. This observation is consistent with earlier reports suggesting that treatment decisions are influenced not only by clinical variables but also by organizational, contextual, and patient-related factors associated with the practice environment [20–23]. Private practices may offer greater flexibility in scheduling, easier access to advanced surgical instruments and biomaterials, and patient populations with stronger expectations for definitive interventions. It is also conceivable that financial structures in private care may influence thresholds for recommending surgery; however, the present survey did not assess reimbursement models, treatment fees, or financial incentives directly. Accordingly, any interpretation in this regard must remain cautious and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. In the present dataset, implant replacement/removal was selected infrequently and was excluded from the dichotomized regression outcome because of sparse data. Therefore, the study cannot robustly determine whether private practice is associated with a lower or higher threshold for implant removal. Future studies using case-vignette designs or multinomial modelling with adequately represented removal decisions would be valuable to examine this issue more directly.

The absence of significant associations between anxiety and gender or working sector suggests that anxiety in this context is more closely linked to individual clinical experience rather than demographic or workplace characteristics. Studies in dentistry have shown that higher anxiety levels can influence treatment decisions and may lead to more defensive or conservative approaches [24]. Therefore, the inverse relationship between experience and anxiety observed in the present study underscores the importance of mentorship, continued professional development, and early-career support in managing complex peri-implantitis cases.

Although mechanical debridement with medication therapy was the most frequently preferred overall approach, the findings can be interpreted within the framework of the EFP S3-level clinical practice guideline [9]. The guideline emphasizes biofilm control, risk-factor management, and supportive care as the foundation of treatment. In cases where inflammation persists after non-surgical measures, surgical therapy may be indicated to provide access for implant-surface decontamination and pocket reduction. The choice between reconstructive, resective, and combined approaches should be guided by defect morphology, implant characteristics, and maintainability, while implantoplasty may be considered in selected circumstances. Implant removal is generally reserved for implants with a hopeless prognosis or those that cannot be maintained predictably. Framing the present findings against this stepped treatment model helps explain why clinicians may still begin with non-surgical strategies even when they recognize the likely need for surgery in advanced lesions.

An important contribution of this study is the assessment of anxiety specifically in relation to the management of severe peri-implantitis. Most participants reported minimal anxiety, which may reflect the surgical background of periodontology specialists and their familiarity with inflammatory disease management. Nevertheless, longer specialist experience was significantly associated with lower odds of anxiety. This finding is consistent with previous literature indicating that increasing clinical experience may reduce uncertainty and stress during decision-making [25–27]. At the same time, the use of a general anxiety instrument in a procedure-specific context requires caution.

The use of the BAI in this study also warrants careful interpretation. The instrument offers a pragmatic and widely recognized estimate of anxiety severity [18, 28], but it does not constitute a procedure-specific measure for peri-implantitis treatment. In addition, prior psychometric studies have noted overlap between BAI scores and depressive symptomatology and variability in optimal cut-off values across settings [28]. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting self-reported anxiety symptoms accompanying severe peri-implantitis management rather than a formal psychiatric diagnosis or a fully validated task-specific anxiety construct.

The predominance of women in the present sample also deserves to be mentioned. Although specialty-specific workforce statistics for Turkish periodontists are limited, recent evidence from Turkish dental academia suggests that women now represent a substantial proportion of the dental workforce in Turkey [29]. In the present study, however, gender was not associated with the likelihood of selecting surgical treatment in the multivariable model, nor was it associated with the presence of anxiety in the regression analysis. Thus, the high proportion of female respondents appears more relevant to sample composition than to treatment philosophy in the present dataset.

In the present study, standardized weekly or monthly peri-implantitis case-volume data were not available; therefore, the relationship between actual case volume and treatment preference could not be directly tested. However, an additional exploratory chi-square analysis using implant surgery training volume during residency (% of residency) as an available indicator of prior surgical exposure showed no statistically significant association with treatment choice. Because this variable does not directly reflect current peri-implantitis case volume in practice, the absence of standardized case-volume data should still be regarded as a limitation, as direct exposure to peri-implantitis cases may influence confidence, treatment thresholds, and the perceived need for surgery independently of years of experience alone.

Taken together, the findings highlight the need for comprehensive peri-implant disease management training during postgraduate education. Incorporating case-based learning, supervised surgical experience, and evidence-based decision frameworks may enhance clinicians’ confidence and reduce anxiety. In addition, the observed influence of practice setting on treatment choice suggests that broader dissemination of clinical guidelines and continuing education may help harmonize care across different healthcare environments.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the reliance on self-reported responses introduces potential response and recall bias. The survey captured stated preferences rather than directly observed behavior. The BAI was used as a general anxiety-severity instrument in a procedure-specific context and therefore should not be interpreted as a validated task-specific tool. Moreover, direct clinical case-volume measures and financial variables were not incorporated into the models. Finally, because the study included only periodontists practicing in Türkiye, generalizability to other countries and health systems may be limited.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, postgraduate clinical exposure to peri-implant disease management was associated with higher self-perceived educational competence, while private-sector practice independently predicted a greater likelihood of choosing surgical therapy. Anxiety related to severe peri-implantitis management was generally low and decreased with increasing specialist experience. These findings emphasize the importance of structured postgraduate training, evidence-based continuing education, and early-career support in promoting confident peri-implantitis care across diverse practice environments.
