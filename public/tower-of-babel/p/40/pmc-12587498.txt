Maternal and paternal determinants of infant health: a cross-sectional study in four countries during the COVID-19 era
Authors: Ahmed A Amer, Samar A Amer, Mohamed A Helmy, Mai M Albuqami, Mohamed A S Amer, Mahmoud T Hefnawy, Hanaa S Said, Hamza A Abdul-Hafez, Batool Turki Gharaibeh, Muna Ali Mugibel, Sama Hesham Samir, Mohamed Sh Ramadan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfant health is profoundly influenced by socioeconomic factors, parental lifestyle, and prenatal care. Understanding the critical role of all these factors can guide targeted public health interventions to reduce infant morbidity and mortality.AimsTo investigate the impact or association of various maternal and paternal factors, including periconceptional care, pregnancy-related health conditions, antenatal care, smoking, radiation, nutrition, SARS-CoV-2 infection, and COVID-19 vaccination, on infant health in Egypt, Palestine, Yemen, and Jordan.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 445 mothers from August 2024 to October 2024 using a pretested and well-structured questionnaire. A well-trained physician conducted a face-to-face interview to collect data. Statistical analysis was performed to identify significant associations.ResultsThe mean ±SD maternal age was 27.72 ± 6.4 years, and the paternal age was 32.35 ± 7.25 years. Most mothers were Palestinian (34.8%) and Egyptian (26.7%), with 62.7% residing in urban areas. Key findings revealed that smoking (P = 0.042), inadequate intake of folic acid during pregnancy (P = 0.003), and non-prescription drug use (P = 0.008) were significantly associated with infant diseases. High income levels (P < 0.05) and routine prenatal visits (P = 0.03) showed significant associations with healthy infants. Chronic parental illnesses, being unaware of pregnancy for a longer time, hyperemesis gravidarum, and maternal COVID-19 during pregnancy were all important factors that were linked to a higher incidence of infant diseases (P = 0.05).ConclusionThis study highlights the importance of socioeconomic factors, healthy parental lifestyle, and prenatal care in improving infant health outcomes. Public health initiatives should focus on promoting awareness of maternal health, early medical intervention, and socioeconomic support to enhance maternal and infant well-being. To explore the long-term impacts of these factors and develop targeted strategies for vulnerable populations, further research is necessary.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-23462-0.Keywords: Infant health outcomes, Parental lifestyle, Maternal Dietary Habits, Preconception, Pregnancy, Antenatal, Supplement, Natal care, COVID-19

Introduction

During pregnancy, women often adopt healthier lifestyles and demonstrate increased receptivity to health advice and behavior change support from antenatal care providers [1, 2]. Embracing positive behaviors, such as maintaining a healthy diet and engaging in physical activity, can significantly reduce the risk of excessive gestational weight gain, gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, and delivery complications [3–6]. Importantly, these practices not only support maternal health during pregnancy but also contribute to long-term benefits for both mothers and their children [7, 8].

Pregnancy represents a critical period marked by heightened nutritional demands due to the mother's and the developing fetus's physiological and metabolic needs [9]. Adequate nutritional intake during this time is essential for a healthy pregnancy and favorable birth outcomes. To meet these increased demands, women should consume a diverse range of foods to ensure sufficient nutrient intake. Dietary diversity, defined as the variety of food types consumed over a specific period, serves as an indicator of adequate nutrition and food quality [10].

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines malnutrition as a cellular imbalance between the body's demand for nutrients and energy to ensure specific functions, growth, and maintenance. Maternal malnutrition remains a significant global public health concern, with approximately 10% of women worldwide and 20% of women in Africa suffering from it. However, food myths and misconceptions can negatively influence dietary choices and affect nutritional status both before and during pregnancy, leading to undernutrition in pregnant women and adverse outcomes for the fetus. Inadequate intake of micro- and macronutrients during pregnancy can result in preterm deliveries, congenital abnormalities, poor intrauterine growth, and other complications [10–15]. Modifiable risk factors during pregnancy, including tobacco use, poor nutrition, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, and gestational weight gain outside recommended ranges, can significantly affect maternal and fetal outcomes [16]. Complications such as spontaneous abortion, small or large for gestational age, preterm birth, neonatal intensive care admission, and poor obstetric outcomes are associated with adverse maternal outcomes [17–21]. Adverse infant and child outcomes include obesity and developmental delays [17, 18]. The clustering of these modifiable risk factors during pregnancy further exacerbates them through cumulative effects [22, 23].

Approximately 10% of pregnant women worldwide smoke tobacco, 10% consume alcohol, and 68% experience gestational weight gain outside recommended ranges [2]. However, these prevalence rates vary significantly across countries and demographic groups, with some populations reporting much higher rates [16]. Evidence-based international clinical guidelines recommend addressing health risks, including alcohol and tobacco use [24], poor nutrition, physical inactivity, and excessive or insufficient gestational weight gain, as part of routine prenatal care during initial appointments and throughout pregnancy [16, 24, 25].

Paternal factors such as age, height, weight, race/ethnicity, anthropometric parameters, education, and smoking have been studied in relation to birth outcomes. However, most research has traditionally focused on individual paternal factors, with limited attention to the combined influence of multiple factors [26]. Additionally, disparities in child health outcomes, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, underscore the need for a more comprehensive understanding of contributing factors.

In 2015, the United Nations adopted the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) to promote the health and well-being of all children. Despite this, in 2022, an estimated 4.9 million children under the age of five died, translating to 13,400 deaths per day. Infectious diseases such as pneumonia, diarrhea, and malaria remain leading causes, along with preterm birth and intrapartum-related complications [27].

Egypt, Palestine, Yemen, and Jordan are developing countries with high birth rates and shared challenges in infant health, including limited access to quality antenatal care, high maternal malnutrition, and socioeconomic disparities. Despite global efforts, region-specific data on infant health determinants remains limited. The absence of such data makes it more challenging to address medical needs, especially during healthcare crises and pandemics, when timely and evidence-based interventions are crucial. The availability of such data can help in developing targeted healthcare strategies to control infant morbidity, enhance neonatal care, and improve overall health outcomes.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the association between multiple maternal and paternal factors and child health outcomes. Specifically, it examines the influence of paternal lifestyle factors, SARS-CoV-2 exposure, and COVID-19 vaccination, alongside maternal factors such as periconceptional care, pregnancy-related health conditions, natal care, smoking, radiation, and nutrition. By focusing on child health in Egypt, Palestine, Yemen, and Jordan, this study seeks to address gaps in knowledge and provide insights that may guide future public health interventions.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted from August 2024 to October 2024 at primary healthcare centers in one large (urban) and one small (rural area) in Egypt, Palestine, Yemen, and Jordan.

Participants

This study specifically targeted adult mothers who had recently given birth to living infants in the past two years or less, attended well-baby clinics, or pediatric outpatient clinics in the primary health care centers (these clinics focus on the health and development of infants and children by offering check-ups, vaccinations, developmental screenings, and general care). This study excluded mothers with complex medical, psychological, or mental disorders due to their potential impact on recall and outcome accuracy.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using the formula n = Z2P(1-P)/d2, where n stands for the sample size, Z for the desired level of confidence, P for the expected prevalence, and d for the precision or effect size. The sample size was calculated using the following parameters: a confidence interval of 95%, an expected frequency of 50%, an acceptable margin of error of 5%, an assumption of an estimate of 50% for the prevalence parameter (paternal and maternal determinants) and an unlimited population (as it was impossible to determine the precise number of mothers who had if they had a living infant aged 24 months or less, attended well-baby clinics, or pediatric outpatient clinics in the selected PHCCs), and a design effect of 2.00. This was based on a pilot study with 20 mothers that was conducted initially to establish the feasibility and sample size of the investigation. The minimum number of responses required was 270. To enhance the study's efficacy, the sample size was increased to 455.

Sampling techniques

In this study, we selected investigators or researchers to collect data from Egypt, Palestine, Jordan, and Yemen. The investigators chose a random sample of mothers who met the selection criteria from the accessible or nearby PHCCs (one large [urban] and one small [rural] area).

Data collection

The preparation for the data collection tool

The questionnaire we used was based on earlier studies [26, 28–32], it was first written in English and later translated into Arabic. Two English-speaking translators conducted the back translation, consulting the original panel to address any concerns or issues. Three specialists in Pediatrics, Family Medicine, and Obstetrics and Gynecology validated the questionnaire to guarantee its reliability and content validity.

The quality control methods for the data collection tool

A pilot trial was conducted with twenty mothers of each nationality, excluded from the main investigation, to validate the questionnaire. We intentionally solicited feedback from the participants, including inquiries about the clarity of the instructions, logistical, technological, and other issues, as well as the difficulty of responding to certain questions. After gathering and examining the results of the pilot survey, all submitted input was addressed, potentially correcting or selecting the most effective types of inquiries. The reliability of the questionnaire was conducted using Cronbach's alpha, which yielded a result of 0.84.

The data collection process

A well-structured, pre-tested, and pre-coded questionnaire was used to collect data. Well-trained physicians collected the data through face-to-face interviews. The questionnaire was designed in Arabic using Google Forms and distributed to all registered physicians across the participating PHCCs to collect data in an online form.

Study variables

Monthly income

Refers to the combined earnings of all family members, regardless of whether they contribute fully or partially to household expenses. It is considered enough when it adequately covers all family needs and more than enough when it exceeds expenses, allowing for savings or.

Infant diseases

Refer to the infant-diagnosed diseases using the International Classification of Diseases-10 (ICD-10), which is a global tool that serves a wide range of purposes and provides critical knowledge about the extent, causes, and consequences of human disease and mortality.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using SPSS software (Version 25.0), which was used for data coding and analysis. The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to assess the normal distribution of the data. We presented and summarized the data using frequencies and percentages for qualitative data and using means and standard deviations (SD) for quantitative data.

Chi-square tests were used for assessing associations between parental lifestyle factors (like smoking, chronic diseases, and total nutritional consumption) and infant health outcomes for categorical (qualitative) variables. However, if any expected cell count is less than 5%, like Cannabis Usage, the P-values of Fisher's Exact Test (f-) should be presented instead. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Post-hoc analyses were conducted for variables with more than two categories that are statistically significant, e.g., cigarette smoking, to facilitate a comprehensive comprehension of the specific group that is propelling the association and determining which group is contributing to the statistical significance.

Logistic regression is a statistical method that is used to predict the likelihood of the probability of an infant developing a disease, accounting for confounding variables. It highlights its core concepts, such as odds ratios, logit transformation, and model interpretation, to model the connection between a dependent variable of infant disease (yes and no) and one or more independent variables as (as they are independent of each other; linearity in the logit and absence of multicollinearity including (during pregnancy, did you suffer from hyperemesis or any chronic infections, and other variables like father's age (y), mother's age (y), the order of the infant in the family, and the total nutritional consumption score.

The dietary habits assessment's total nutritional consumption score consists of several questions, each tailored to evaluate specific aspects of nutrition. The cereal and bread consumption question scores responses on a scale from 0 to 3. The response"I never eat it"receives 0 points. 1 point for"Less than 6 servings,"2 points for"From 6 to 11 servings,"and 3 points for"More than 11 servings."A similar scoring system was applied to the meat and legumes question, ranging from 0 to 3 points. For milk and dairy products, responses are scored as follows: 0 points for"I never eat it,"1 point for"Less than 3 servings,"2 points for"From 3 to 5 servings,"and 3 points for"More than 5 servings."

The same scoring scale was used for fruit consumption, while vegetable intake is also assessed on a scale from 0 to 3. Water consumption is evaluated using a comparable scoring approach. Nutritional supplements—calcium, vitamin D, iron, folic acid, and multivitamins—are scored from 0 (not committed) to 3 (always committed). The total score for each item ranges from a minimum of 0 to a maximum of 3, reflecting overall dietary habits. The cumulative score across all items has a maximum possible value of 33 and a minimum of 0 [29]. The Welch Two Sample T-test was conducted to compare the mean total nutritional consumption score between two groups of infants’ disease status (No and Yes).

Ethical considerations

The institutional review board (IRP) at Zagazig University registered this survey under the number 24–0363. Before participating in the study all invited mothers complete and check an informed consent box. Checking the consent box and completing the voluntary survey were considered evidence of providing valid, informed consent. The IRB application for this study, which received approval, described this consent process.

Infant health and sociodemographic associations

A bar chart (Fig. 1) illustrates that 63.7% of infants were healthy. Malnutrition was the most common condition (11.7%), followed by neonatal diseases (9%), congenital defects (5.5%), and unspecified conditions (3%). Chronic respiratory and cardiovascular diseases were rare, affecting 1.9% and 1.1% of infants, respectively. Mental health disorders (0.8%), malignant cancers (0.4%), and benign tumors (0.2%) were the least reported. The highest frequency of infant diseases was observed among Egyptian participants (41.2%). Parental behaviors significantly influenced infant health. Smoking history was reported by 16.2% of fathers and 8.5% of mothers, while current smoking was noted in 40.2% of fathers and 1.8% of mothers. Hookah smoking was observed in 14.8% of fathers and 2.7% of mothers. Cannabis and alcohol use were infrequent (0.9% and 0.7%, respectively). Chronic diseases were reported in 6.7% of parents, with hypertension and diabetes showing significant associations with poorer infant health (p = 0.004). Maternal nationality (p = 0.035) and income (p = 0.028) were also critical determinants. Parental substance use, including smoking (p = 0.042), cannabis (p = 0.006), and alcohol (p = 0.003), had significant negative impacts on infant health.

Pregnancy-related factors and infant health

More than half of the mothers (52.3%) reported no significant exposures during pregnancy (Fig. 3, Table 2). Key exposures included unawareness of pregnancy (16.6%), psychological stress (8.3%), use of non-prescription medications (6.3%), and lack of regular antenatal follow-up and folic acid supplementation (5.3%). Unintended pregnancy despite contraception was reported in 9.6%, while radiation exposure and medical warnings from doctors were noted in 3.3% and 2.6%, respectively.

Maternal nutrition and infant health

Folic acid deficiency before or during pregnancy was significantly associated with infant disease (53.1%). Calcium supplementation was reported by 26.5% of mothers, vitamin D by 24.2%, iron by 39.7%, and folic acid by 44.7% (Table 3). While dairy consumption significantly influenced infant health, other dietary habits, including intake of cereals, bread, meat, legumes, fruits, and vegetables, showed no significant effects.

Discussion

Despite the importance of quality prenatal care, a relatively small percentage of mothers or fathers invest in their health and well-being before becoming pregnant. This cross-sectional study, which included 445 mothers, aimed to investigate the impact or association of various maternal and paternal factors on infant health. This study revealed that around 145 (32.6%) of infants had disease; according to the ICD-10, the most common diseases in descending order were malnutrition, followed by diseases that occurred within the first month after birth, congenital defects, other unspecified conditions, and then chronic respiratory and cardiovascular. This study highlights the importance of socioeconomic factors, healthy parental lifestyle, preconception, antenatal, and antenatal care, in improving infant health outcomes.

Demographic factors and their associated with infants'health

The average maternal and paternal ages were 27.72 ± 6.35 and 32.35 ± 7.25 years, respectively. Most mothers were Palestinian (34.8%), Egyptian (26.7%), or Yemeni (15.7%). Residency was predominantly urban (62.7%), while 37.3% lived in rural areas. Income levels varied, with 69% reporting their income as"enough,"16% as"more than enough,"and 23% as"not enough."These findings align with those of Alfayez et al. [33], who examined Saudi women's attitudes toward newborn screening programs.

Maternal nationality showed a significant association with infant health, with Palestinian mothers reporting higher rates of poor infant health, potentially reflecting disparities in healthcare access and socioeconomic conditions. Lower income levels were also strongly correlated with adverse child health outcomes [34], as 55.2% of parents covered medical expenses out of pocket. These findings highlight the role of financial stability in infant health and the need for supportive policies.

Parental substance use, including smoking (P = 0.042), cannabis (P = 0.006), and alcohol consumption (P = 0.003), significantly impacted child health. Maternal smoking during pregnancy is a well-documented risk factor for miscarriage, low birth weight, prematurity, and perinatal mortality [35–39], emphasizing the need for public health interventions.

Chronic parental illnesses, such as diabetes and hypertension, were also linked to poorer infant health [45], likely due to their impact on caregiving and overall family well-being [46]. Conversely, factors such as residence, smoking cessation, hookah use, addiction status, immune and neuropsychiatric conditions, and family history of cancer showed no significant association with infant health, possibly due to confounding variables.

Parental lifestyle factors and their associations with infants'health

A majority of mothers (71.0%) had no prior smoking history, indicating a predominantly non-smoking population. Former smoking was reported by 8.5% of mothers and 16.2% of fathers, with higher cessation rates among fathers. Current smoking showed a pronounced gender disparity, with 1.8% of mothers and 40.2% of fathers actively smoking, consistent with global trends of higher male smoking prevalence, particularly in certain cultural contexts.

Addiction-related issues were minimal, with 96.9% of mothers reporting no substance dependence. Cultural perceptions and question phrasing may have influenced responses, particularly regarding non-substance-related behaviors.

Chronic diseases were reported in 6.7% of mothers and 18.4% of fathers, suggesting potential lifestyle influences on health. Biological, cultural, and socioeconomic factors may contribute to the observed gender disparity. Immune-related conditions, including multiple sclerosis, lupus, and rheumatoid arthritis, were reported by 2.0% of participants, potentially reflecting regional variations in incidence. Neuropsychiatric disorders were also low (2.0%), possibly due to underreporting, lack of diagnosis, or cultural stigma.

A family history of cancer was noted in 7.6% of participants, with maternal history (3.4%) being more prevalent than paternal history (1.1%), suggesting potential hereditary risks. Additionally, 4.0% reported the same type of tumor within their family, while 2.2% had a personal history of tumors. Despite genetic predispositions, 96.0% remained cancer-free, highlighting the potential role of environmental and protective factors in mitigating risk.

Associations of dietary intake and nutritional supplementation with infant health

The consumption of milk and dairy products was significantly associated with reduced infant disease prevalence (P = 0.014). Dairy products provide essential nutrients, including calcium, vitamin D, and protein, which support fetal bone and nervous system development [42, 43]. However, the study found no significant associations for other dietary groups like cereals, bread, fruits, vegetables, or water intake, indicating that these foods may have a less direct impact on infant health.

The most consumed supplements were in descending order: folic acid (75%), iron (72%), calcium (64%), and vitamin D (53.3%), with no significant associations with the frequency of infant disease. They used these supplements for preventative and medicinal immunological enhancement to promote the health of both infants and mothers. The price, accessibility, and acceptability of dietary supplements may account for the significant variations in usage frequency across different studies [40].

Regarding the consumption of diet or sugar-free beverages containing aspartame, 13.3% of participants reported consuming these drinks, while 71.9% indicated they did not. The limited consumption of aspartame-sweetened beverages suggests a preference for traditional sugary drinks or a shift toward natural sweeteners due to taste preferences, health consciousness, or emerging trends favoring healthier alternatives.

Antenatal and natal factors and their association with infant health

The results suggest that high-risk pregnancies identified by medical professionals are associated with increased child morbidity as Mothers who received a doctor's warning during pregnancy reported a significantly higher incidence of infant diseases (66.6% vs. 31.6%, P = 0.02). Similarly, folic acid deficiency before or during early pregnancy was significantly associated with child disease (P = 0.003), highlighting the protective role of folic acid in preventing neural tube defects and other congenital abnormalities [32, 42].

A delayed awareness of pregnancy was also linked to higher infant disease risk (16.6%, P = 0.04), possibly due to postponed prenatal care or lifestyle adjustments. Non-prescription drug use during pregnancy was another significant factor (P = 0.008), emphasizing the need for healthcare professionals to educate pregnant women on the safe use of medications. Routine prenatal visits were associated with improved infant health outcomes (P = 0.03), reinforcing the importance of consistent antenatal care in reducing neonatal mortality and promoting maternal and infant health [33, 41, 44].

Pregnancy-related hyperemesis (P = 0.030) and chronic infections (P < 0.00005) were significantly associated with increased infant illness. Hyperemesis can lead to maternal malnutrition and dehydration, affecting fetal development, while chronic infections may involve direct pathogen transmission or immunological responses [45]. Mode of delivery also played a role, with cesarean sections associated with poorer infant health outcomes (P < 0.05), potentially due to complications such as delayed breastfeeding or neonatal respiratory issues.

Admission to neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) was strongly associated with infant disease (P < 0.00002), as premature birth or underlying health conditions often necessitate incubation. Low birth weight, delayed motor development, and impaired social or mental development significantly correlated with child illness, with P -values ranging from 0.00002 to 0.00000001.

Associations of parental SARS-CoV-2 infection and COVID-19 vaccination with infant health

Exposure to COVID-19 during pregnancy showed a significant association with higher infant illness frequency (P = 0.05). The results indicate a significant association between SARS-CoV-2 exposure during pregnancy and infant disease (P = 0.05), suggesting that maternal infection during pregnancy may pose risks to infant health. parents who were not vaccinated had a higher prevalence of infant disease (P = 0.02). These findings underscore the relevance of both parental COVID-19 exposure and vaccination status in influencing infant health. Potential mechanisms include maternal inflammation, fever, or vertical transmission of the virus, all of which could impact fetal development. In agreement with other studies [46, 47]. Further research is guaranteed to understand these relationships fully.

Strengths and limitations

This study has many strengths, including a multi-national, targeted, relatively large sample of mothers from four low- to middle-income countries (Egypt, Jordan, Yemen, and Palestine). Researchers are increasingly focusing on identifying maternal risk factors associated with adverse birth outcomes. The impact of paternal factors on birth outcomes has received significantly less attention. This study is one of the few studies to examine the influence of multiple parental factors, including paternal lifestyle factors, SARS-CoV-2 virus exposure, and COVID-19 vaccination on infant health.

Moreover, this study also had many limitations, e.g., the cross-sectional design prevents establishing causality, and reliance on self-reported data introduces recall bias. Mothers reported paternal factors rather than fathers explicitly, which is particularly concerning. One of the limitations is that there are significant variations in the sample size collected from different nationalities; for example, Palestinian mothers constituted the largest group, followed by Egyptian and Yemeni mothers. The variability may be attributed to the flow rate in the selected primary care centers, in addition to the long waiting time that facilitates face-to-face interviews for data collection. Furthermore, the findings may not be generalized beyond the study’s specific cultural and geographical context and include only mothers who meet the specified inclusion and exclusion criteria attending PHCCs. Although we selected investigators or researchers to collect data from their institutions or nearby PHCCs e.g. Ahmed A. Amer, Mohamed SH. Ramadan, Mahmoud T. Hefnawy and Hanaa S. Said from Egypt; Hamza A. Abdul-Hafez from Palestine; Batool Turki Gharaibeh from Jordan; and Muna Ali Mugibel from Yemen. They chose a random sample of mothers who meet the selection criteria from the accessible or nearby PHCCs, but this study cannot be called population based.

Implications for clinical practice

The findings of this study emphasize the critical need for integrating comprehensive maternal and paternal health assessments into routine antenatal care. By identifying and addressing modifiable risk factors such as smoking, improper folic acid intake, and chronic infections early in pregnancy, healthcare providers can significantly reduce adverse infant health outcomes. Additionally, the study underscores the importance of prioritizing maternal COVID-19 vaccination as a preventive measure during pregnancy. Clinicians should adopt a holistic approach that includes dietary counseling, stress management support, and guidance on safe medication used to optimize maternal health and, by extension, infant well-being. These insights call for the incorporation of targeted education and screening protocols in clinical settings to mitigate preventable risks and enhance neonatal health outcomes.

Conclusions

This study highlights the significant impact of maternal and paternal factors on infant health, with malnutrition being the most common issue, followed by neonatal diseases, congenital defects, and chronic conditions. Maternal determinants such as inadequate folic acid intake, poor prenatal care, chronic infections, and unhealthy dietary choices, particularly the harmful effects of hydrogenated oils, were linked to adverse outcomes. Paternal smoking, substance use, and chronic diseases, including hypertension and diabetes, were also associated with poorer infant health. Socioeconomic disparities further influenced outcomes, with lower income levels correlating with increased infant morbidity. Additionally, maternal SARS-CoV-2 infection heightened infant disease risk, while parental vaccination, particularly with Pfizer, had a protective effect. Delivery-related factors, including mode of delivery, jaundice, and NICU admission, were also significant.

Recommendations for future research

Based on the findings of the study, future research should prioritize longitudinal designs to better capture the cumulative and long-term impacts of parental and maternal factors on infant health.

Investigating the interplay between socioeconomic status, prenatal care accessibility, and parental health behaviors will provide a more profound understanding of their collective influence on infant morbidity and mortality. Moreover, further research is needed to confirm many findings, including the hazardous effect of hydrogenated oils and the protective effect of vegetable oils, to promote infant health and explore underlying mechanisms.

Additionally, comparative analyses across diverse cultural and healthcare contexts could identify region-specific risk factors and intervention strategies. Studies should explore the biological mechanisms underlying the associations between maternal infections, folic acid supplementation, and infant health outcomes.

The role of paternal factors, such as smoking and chronic illnesses, in infant health should also be examined with greater granularity, as they remain underexplored. Such research can inform evidence-based public health policies and tailored interventions to mitigate disparities in infant health globally. These findings emphasize the need for integrated parental health assessments in antenatal care, prioritizing nutritional guidance, smoking cessation, maternal vaccination, and improved prenatal access to reduce preventable risks. Future research should explore the long-term effects of these factors and develop targeted interventions to enhance infant health outcomes.
