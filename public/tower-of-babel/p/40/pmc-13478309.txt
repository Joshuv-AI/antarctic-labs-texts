Development of a model to predict nicotine pharmacokinetics from oral nicotine pouches
Authors: Matthias Manne Knopp, Jacob Rune Jørgensen, Xia Summer Li, Shuzhong Mark Zhuang, Jesse Rensch, Jeffery Edmiston, Fadi Aldeek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oral Nicotine Pouches (ONPs) have emerged as a promising non-combustible nicotine alternative to traditional tobacco products. Regulatory approval of ONPs in the United States requires comprehensive studies to demonstrate that new tobacco products are appropriate for the protection of public health. Conventional nicotine pharmacokinetic (PK) studies typically depend on expensive and time-intensive human trials. To address this challenge, a predictive PK model to forecast buccal absorption of nicotine from ONPs was developed using in vitro data. A novel dissolution method employing a µDISS Profiler was used to generate nicotine dissolution and dynamic pH profiles for three commercially available ONPs: ZYN Cool Mint 6 mg, DRYFT Spearmint 7 mg, and VELO Mint 4 mg. The model integrates experimental in vitro data with established nicotine PK parameters to predict clinical PK profiles. It effectively forecasted key PK metrics, including AUC, Cmax, and Tmax, while emphasizing the pivotal role of buccal/saliva pH in nicotine permeability and absorption. This methodology shows promise as a reliable, cost-efficient, and time-saving potential alternative to traditional clinical studies, offering potential data and insights for regulatory applications and product development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53537-w.

Introduction

With increasing awareness of the health-risks posed by chemical toxicants associated with smoking, there has been a notable shift towards non-combustible nicotine delivery solutions, such as heated tobacco products, electronic vapor products, patches, gums, lozenges, sprays, and oral nicotine pouches (ONPs)1,2. ONPs offer a smokeless/non-inhalable and tobacco leaf-free means of delivering nicotine, offering potentially reduced-risk alternatives to traditional tobacco products. ONPs deliver nicotine through the buccal mucosa, bypassing the gastrointestinal tract and thus, first-pass hepatic metabolism3. ONPs thereby result in a similar onset of action to alternative buccal nicotine delivery solutions such as gums and lozenges, yet with superior bioavailability compared to gums4.

In the United States, ONPs are subject to rigorous review and authorization process by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Center for Tobacco Products. This is conducted through the Premarket Tobacco Product Application (PMTA) process, which requires that manufacturers provide extensive data demonstrating that the marketing of the new tobacco product would be appropriate for the protection of public health before it can receive market approval5. To gather the required data, some PK studies traditionally rely on human clinical trials, which are both time-consuming and costly. Alternative approaches such as the development of predictive pharmacokinetic (PK) models that accurately predicts buccal absorption (i.e. PK parameters) of nicotine may not only provide critical insights during the early stages of product development but could potentially expedite the regulatory approval process. The development of such predictive models would require physiological understanding and robust in vitro input6. Since the buccal absorption of nicotine from ONPs is influenced by numerous factors, including the physicochemical properties of nicotine, pH of the product and oral cavity, salivatory flow, and nicotine dissolution from the pouch, incorporating these factors into a predictive PK model is essential7.

Several in vitro dissolution methodologies, including those employing standard United States Pharmacopeia (USP) dissolution apparatuses8–15, have been developed, validated, and recognized by the FDA to facilitate the discrimination, comparison, and equivalence assessment of oral tobacco products, including ONPs16,17. While these methodologies have proven to be valuable tools for regulatory applications, particularly in the context of product-to-product comparison and equivalence determination17, their full potential for establishing in vitro-in vivo correlation (IVIVC) has yet to be explored.

Recently, a novel in vitro dissolution method has been developed to predict in vivo nicotine dissolution from ONPs using a low-volume µDISS Profiler dissolution setup with a custom-made sinker and artificial saliva. This method was designed to assess nicotine dissolution from oral tobacco-derived nicotine (OTDN) pouches (ZYN Dry Smooth) and snus products (General Pouched Snus White Portion Large). The study demonstrated that the method could produce distinct nicotine dissolution profiles for the two product types and exhibited strong biorelevance, as the in vitro nicotine dissolution closely matched in vivo nicotine extraction data18.

In recent years, the use of physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) models has gained traction in drug development and regulatory science. PBPK models simulate the PK profiles of drugs by integrating detailed anatomical, physiological, and biochemical parameters19. Although PBPK models have been used to predict nicotine exposure from various delivery systems, there is a general lack of literature on modelling buccal absorption from ONPs owing to the overall complexity of the underlying processes6. Furthermore, while PBPK models can provide valuable insights they have some limitations, e.g., high data dependency, including the need for precise physiological/drug-specific parameters20.

This study aims to address this knowledge gap by developing a simple predictive PK model specifically designed to predict the buccal absorption of nicotine from ONPs based on in vitro data generated using a slightly modified version of the method suggested by Knopp et al.18. The model is based on a simple mathematical convolution approach using only experimental in vitro data (nicotine dissolution and dynamic pH profiles), average participants weight, and well-established nicotine PK parameters (apparent volume of distribution, and plasma half-life) as input. The model was validated using clinical data of three commercially available ONP products, namely ZYN Cool Mint 6 mg, DRYFT Spearmint 7 mg, and VELO Mint 4 mg. This combined approach of predictive modeling and clinical validation provides a robust framework for understanding nicotine absorption from ONPs and supports the development of in vitro tools for future product evaluation.

Materials and methods

Materials

Saliva Orthana (artificial saliva) was purchased from Biofac A/S (Kastrup, Denmark). It is a commercially available lubricating mouth spray with a neutral pH that simulates human saliva. It contains porcine gastric mucin 3500 mg, methyl-4-hydroxybenzoate 0.1 g, benzalkonium chloride 2 mg, EDTA disodium salt, H2O (E386) 0.05 g, H2O2 250 ppm, xylitol 2.0 g, peppermint oil 5 mg, spearmint oil 5 mg, NaCl 0.045 g, KCl 0.063 g, CaCl2 0.030 g, K2HPO4 0.010 g, and KOH 0.076 g (per 100 mL aqueous solution). Nicotine reference standard (> 99.9%) was sourced from Łukasiewicz IPO (Warsaw, Poland). The three ONP products (ZYN Cool Mint 6 mg, DRYFT Spearmint 7 mg, and VELO Mint 4 mg) were purchased online. The manufacturing dates printed on the product labels for the ZYN, VELO, and DRYFT nicotine pouch products indicated that the products were approximately four to six months old when received. All testing was performed within two months of receipt. Products were stored under ambient conditions and used as received. Manufacturers of all nicotine pouch brands included in this study are listed in the Competing Interests section.

Clinical study

The Advarra Institutional Review Board (IRB) (Columbia, MD, USA) reviewed and approved the clinical study protocol and relevant documents before study initiation. The clinical study was conducted in accordance with FDA regulations as described in the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) 21 Parts 50 and 56, Department of Health and Human Services regulations as described in 45 CFR 46, guidelines resulting from the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) E6 Good Clinical Practice (GCP), the European Union (EU) directive 2001/20/EC, and the ethical principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki. Because this study evaluated commercially available marketplace products, it was not registered on a clinical trial registry. All participants reviewed, signed, and dated the informed consent form prior to participation in the clinical study. The nicotine PK data of the three pouch products (ZYN Cool Mint 6 mg, DRYFT Spearmint 7 mg, and VELO Mint 4 mg), available as Supplementary Information, were obtained through a randomized, 7-way crossover design involving healthy adult smokers of combustible cigarettes.

Generally healthy adult males and females (no less than 40% of either gender), 21–45 years of age, were enrolled. Participants were adult self-affirmed exclusive smokers (≥ 10 cigarettes per day) of combustible cigarettes for at least 12 months (no oral tobacco product use in the past 30 days), not planning to quit smoking in the next 3 months, ≥ 500 ng/ml cotinine measured in urine, willing and able to use the highest nicotine labelled product, and not pregnant or planning to become pregnant (for females). All recruited subjects were given tobacco quitting information (Quit Assist) at screening and at the end of the study. 34 subjects were randomized using a 7 × 7 Latin Square strategy into different product use sequences on Day 1. Participants were confined to the clinic from Day 1 to the end of the study (Day 7). To assist in the acclimation to using these products, participants took the study products home for a minimum of 3 days and use at least one of each product. In addition, each afternoon (on Days 1–6), participants took part in a 4-hour ad libitum product use episode with the assigned product. After overnight abstinence, participants used the same product (the following morning) during a controlled use PK session (1 pouch under the top lip for 30 min). Blood samples were taken and assessed for nicotine concentrations at approximately 5 min before product placement (for baseline) and 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 45, 60, 90 and 120 min after product placement. The nicotine in blood samples was measured in accordance with Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) conditions using validated methods by a third-party laboratory as described in Liu, et al.21.

Although the nicotine pouch products do not capture all ONPs on the market, they are representative of commonly marketed nicotine pouches in the U.S. marketplace. These products were chosen because they are all dry pouches and exhibit distinct dissolution characteristics. Previous research indicated that on! Mint 8 mg and ZYN Cool Mint 6 mg nicotine pouches display similar nicotine dissolution release rates12, so our focus was placed on the products with more differentiated properties: ZYN Cool Mint 6 mg, DRYFT Spearmint 7 mg, and VELO Mint 4 mg.

In vitro nicotine dissolution

The in vitro nicotine dissolution experiments were conducted using a slightly modified version of the procedure described by Knopp et al.18. In brief, dissolution was carried out using a mini bath from the µDISS Profiler from Pion Inc. (Billerica, MA, USA) with six vessels without the UV-probes connected. The pouches were weighed, loaded in custom-made sinkers, and placed on the bottom (mesh up) of 20 mL dissolution vessels along with 20 mm cross-shaped magnetic stirrers operating at 150 rpm. The experiment was initiated by the addition of 10 mL of Orthana artificial saliva (preheated to 37 °C) to each of the six dissolution vessels. The pH of the artificial saliva was adjusted to 7.0 (using 1 M NaOH or 1 M HCl) at t = 0 min, and samples of 250 µL were taken at t = 5, 10, 15, and 30 min. At each time point, the pH was measured again, and the sampled volume was replaced with 250 µL of preheated Orthana artificial saliva. The samples were diluted immediately with 500 µL ethanol 96% and 500 µL acetonitrile to precipitate mucin from the saliva and avoid potential precipitation of the nicotine upon cooling. The diluted samples were then centrifuged at 9,600 × g for 10 min and the supernatant was analysed directly for nicotine content using HPLC-UV. Dissolution testing was performed on six units per product for all three ONPs. Sink conditions were maintained throughout the dissolution testing. Each pouch contained ≤ 7 mg of nicotine, while 10 mL of artificial saliva was used as the dissolution medium, resulting in nicotine concentrations well below the solubility limit of nicotine at pH 7.0. Continuous stirring at 150 rpm and replacement of each 250 µL sampled volume with fresh, preheated artificial saliva further ensured that nicotine concentrations did not approach saturation during the testing period.

Quantitative analysis of nicotine in vitro dissolution

A HPLC-UV method was developed for the quantification of nicotine using a Vanquish Flex HPLC system from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, USA). A reversed-phase Kinetex C18 100 Å column (4.6 × 100 mm, 5 μm) from Phenomenex (Værløse, Denmark), maintained at 40 °C, was used for the separation. The mobile phase consisted of 75% v/v 15 mM ammonium formate (adjusted to pH 10.5 with triethylamine) and 25% v/v acetonitrile, and chromatographic separation was performed under isocratic elution conditions. A volume of 10 µL was injected and eluted at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min, and nicotine was detected at a wavelength of 260 nm after approximately 4.3 min. The concentration of nicotine was calculated using the mean value of the peak areas obtained from the standard curve. The analytical method was validated for linearity, accuracy (recovery), range, precision (repeatability), limit of detection (LOD), and limit of quantification (LOQ) prior to application. The triplicate calibration curve for nicotine demonstrated acceptable linearity (r² = 0.9998) across a concentration range of 1.95–500 µg/mL, with a y-intercept corresponding to 0.86% of the response at the target concentration (200 µg/mL). Accuracy was confirmed by recovery values ranging from 98.95 to 99.62% at 50%, 100%, and 150% of the target concentration. Method precision, assessed via retention time, peak area, and peak height, showed relative standard deviations between 0.12 and 0.50%. The LOD and LOQ were determined to be 0.23 µg/mL and 0.75 µg/mL, respectively.

Results and discussion

Clinical study

A total of 34 participants (21 males and 13 females) were randomized into the study, with 25 participants (17 males and 8 females) completing all required study procedures. Nine participants did not complete the 8-day in-clinic study: seven withdrew for personal reasons, and two due to adverse events (moderate arm pain from vein trauma and mild stomatitis). The higher withdrawal rate may be linked to the timing of the study during the early COVID-19 pandemic, before vaccines were available. The study population was predominantly white (79%) and non-Hispanic or Latino (97%), with an average age of 33.8 years. On average, participants reported smoking 17.2 cigarettes per day and had a smoking history spanning approximately 14 years. Baseline-adjusted plasma nicotine PK profiles of the three ONPs are presented in Fig. 3 together with the PK predictions based on the in vitro pH and nicotine dissolution profiles. Among the tested products, ZYN Cool Mint 6 mg demonstrated the highest nicotine exposure, as indicated by the highest nicotine peak concentration (Cmax) and area under the curve (AUC0−120 min). This was followed by DRYFT Spearmint 7 mg, while VELO Mint 4 mg exhibited the lowest nicotine uptake. The reduced nicotine uptake observed for VELO Mint 4 mg can be attributed to multiple factors. Firstly, VELO Mint 4 mg contains less nicotine compared to ZYN Cool Mint 6 mg and DRYFT Spearmint 7 mg, which inherently limits the amount of nicotine available for absorption. Secondly, nicotine release from these pouches is influenced by various other product attributes including pouch material, moisture content, flavouring agents, physical design, and nicotine formulation22,23. Lastly, the effect on saliva pH as a result of formulation dissolution will influence the permeability of nicotine for which reason the in vitro method, described in the following section, will evaluate both pH and dissolution profiles.

In vitro pH and nicotine dissolution profiles

The nicotine in vitro dissolution profiles from three commercially available ONPs (ZYN Cool Mint 6 mg, DRYFT Spearmint 7 mg, and VELO Mint 4 mg) and the resulting pH-profiles in the artificial saliva are shown in Fig. 1. The µDISS Profiler system was selected for dissolution testing because its low-volume dissolution vessels (10 mL) and controlled temperature environment more closely approximate the limited fluid volume and thermal conditions of the oral cavity compared with conventional USP apparatuses that require substantially larger media volumes. The system also enables rapid pH monitoring and efficient sampling without disturbing the pouch, which is critical for capturing dynamic pH changes and nicotine dissolution kinetics relevant to oral nicotine pouch use.

The pH of the artificial saliva was adjusted to 7.0 at t = 0 min to mimic the resting pH of human oral cavity, which typically ranges between 6.8 and 7.424. To account for the influence of product pH, composition, and nicotine dissolution on saliva pH during use, the pH of the product in artificial saliva was monitored during the dissolution experiment. As shown in Fig. 1B, the pH of the artificial saliva increased over time in the presence of DRYFT and ZYN ONPs, eventually reaching a pH of around 7.5. Despite its basicity, nicotine dissolution is not expected to affect the pH due to its relatively low amount in the pouches compared to other excipients. In contrast, no significant change in the pH of the artificial saliva was observed by VELO.

During the first 10 min of dissolution, ZYN Cool Mint 6 mg exhibited the highest amount of nicotine released, surpassing both DRYFT Spearmint 7 mg and VELO Mint 4 mg, which showed comparable levels of nicotine dissolution. By 30 min, the amount of nicotine dissolved was 6.8 mg from ZYN (113% of its label claim), 4.7 mg from DRYFT (67% of its label claim), and 2.8 mg from VELO (70% of its label claim). These differences in nicotine dissolution profiles could be attributed to variations in the inherent characteristics of the products. Factors such as the type of filler material used, the composition and porosity of the pouch material, and other formulation differences can all influence the rate and efficiency of nicotine dissolution23,25. For instance, filler materials with differing absorption or binding properties may affect how nicotine is retained or released. Similarly, the pouch material thickness, porosity, and material composition can also impact the diffusion of nicotine. Additionally, differences in the formulation, such as the presence of stabilizers or pH modifiers, can further contribute to the observed differences in dynamic pH profiles. It should be noted that the present study did not experimentally characterize formulation- or material-specific factors such as filler type, pouch material composition, porosity, or excipient binding behavior. These factors were referenced as mechanistic considerations based on prior literature rather than experimental outcomes from this work. Importantly, these aspects have been explicitly investigated in recent publications from our team, which demonstrated how pouch materials, filler properties, and excipients influence nicotine release and permeation dynamics22,23.

Based on mean values, the predicted AUCs were 116%, 110% and 106% relative to the clinical AUCs, while the predicted Cmax were 90%, 88% and 92% relative to the clinical Cmax for ZYN, DRYFT and VELO, respectively. While all predicted PK parameters were within the 95% CI of the clinical values, potential differences could be explained by individual PK parameters differing from the fixed values used in the present model, i.e. T1/2 of 2.0 h and Vd of 2.0 L/kg.

Regulatory considerations

The predictive framework described in this study has potential implications for regulatory science and the evaluation of ONPs. The model integrates in vitro nicotine dissolution and dynamic pH profiles with established nicotine PK parameters to forecast plasma nicotine exposure and recapitulates clinical AUC and Cmax with an accuracy of 88–116% for the three ONPs tested. Within the current regulatory environment, such an approach is best viewed as a complementary tool that can strengthen the mechanistic understanding of product performance and support the overall weight of evidence, rather than as a full replacement for human PK studies. In the drug-product context, several regulatory authorities (e.g., FDA and EMA) acknowledge that a robust, Level A IVIVC can, under defined conditions, allow in vitro dissolution data to act as a surrogate for certain bioequivalence studies, thereby reducing the need for additional clinical investigations. However, these expectations are typically based on extensive validation across multiple formulations, demonstration of predictive performance within prespecified error limits, and clear definition of the model’s context of use. In contrast, regulatory pathways for nicotine-containing tobacco products are governed by a different statutory framework, and current FDA Center for Tobacco Products recommendations focus primarily on in vitro dissolution as a discriminatory and comparative tool to support product characterization and product-to-product comparisons (e.g., in the context of PMTAs or substantial equivalence), rather than as a direct surrogate for human exposure endpoints (as reflected in current memoranda and recommendations on dissolution for smokeless and orally placed tobacco products)16,17.

In this context, the present model could have several near-term regulatory applications:

Limitations

The model developed in this study relies on in vitro nicotine dissolution and pH profiles to predict in vivo buccal absorption from ONPs. While the experimental conditions were designed to represent a physiological average of key oral conditions, differences in salivary flow rate, saliva buffering capacity, oral movements, and pouch positioning are not explicitly represented. Since these factors may influence nicotine release and buccal pH, the model predictions should be interpreted as mean estimates that do not capture the full extent of inter-individual variability in nicotine release and absorption. In addition, the model exclusively predicts nicotine absorbed via the buccal mucosa and does not (yet) account for nicotine that is swallowed with saliva during product use. Consequently, the model does not explicitly account for secondary nicotine absorption occurring in the gastrointestinal tract, which may influence plasma concentration profiles, especially after pouch removal (beyond 30 min). Incorporating physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PB-PK) modeling could more accurately capture these effects.

Conclusion

The PK model developed in this study marks a significant step forward in understanding and predicting buccal nicotine absorption from ONPs. By integrating experimental in vitro data with established PK parameters in a clinical study, the model provides a reliable framework for evaluating nicotine dissolution and absorption dynamics across different ONPs. Notably, the study highlights the influence of product-specific factors, such as nicotine dose, nicotine dissolution, and product pH that could impact salivary pH and nicotine uptake, with ZYN Cool Mint 6 mg demonstrating increased nicotine PK compared to DRYFT Spearmint 7 mg and VELO Mint 4 mg. The ability of the model to accurately predict PK outcomes achieved in clinical settings, including AUC, Cmax, and Tmax, underscores its utility as a cost-effective alternative to traditional clinical studies. Beyond its immediate application in product development, this approach also has the potential to streamline regulatory approval processes, reduce reliance on human studies, and enhance the scientific foundation for evaluating ONPs and other oral nicotine delivery systems.
