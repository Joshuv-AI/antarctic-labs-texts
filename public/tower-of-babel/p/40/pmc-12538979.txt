Metabolic activities, biochemical, and physiological processes in Mentha pulegium L. induced by drought stress and application of 5-aminolevulinic acidf
Authors: Reza Shahhoseini, Nasrin Farhadi, Hossein Rabbi Angourani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPennyroyal (Mentha pulegium L.) is a valuable medicinal plant, renowned for its diverse biological and therapeutic effects, which are largely attributed to its wide range of secondary metabolites, such as terpenoids and phenolic compounds. Currently, water deficit, as a major growth-limiting factor for many medicinal plants, poses one of the most significant challenges in plant production. A factorial experiment, based on a completely randomized design (CRD) with three replications, was conducted to assess the effects of foliar application of 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) on the growth, physiological attributes, and biochemical responses of M. pulegium under a gradient of drought stress intensities. The experimental treatments comprised four levels of drought stress (100%, 75%, 50%, and 25% of field capacity) and four concentrations of 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) (0, 5, 10, and 15 µM). The accumulation of malondialdehyde (MDA), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), proline, glycine betaine, and total phenols, as well as the activities of catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) enzymes, were elevated in response to increasing drought intensity. A decline in relative water content, photosynthetic pigment levels, stomatal conductance, Rubisco enzyme activity, and photosynthetic rate with increasing stress levels resulted in reduced plant growth. Foliar application of ALA significantly mitigated the accumulation of hydrogen peroxide and lipid peroxidation (as measured by malondialdehyde content) in pennyroyal plants. This protective effect was mediated by an enhancement in the accumulation of antioxidant compounds, including total phenols and essential oils, coupled with increased activity of antioxidant enzymes such as catalase, peroxidase, and superoxide dismutase. Furthermore, ALA application, particularly at a concentration of 10 µM, alleviated the detrimental effects of water deficit on photosynthesis by enhancing photosynthetic pigment content, stomatal conductance, and Rubisco enzyme activity. The enhanced drought tolerance observed in pennyroyal following ALA application is primarily attributed to a significant boost in the plant’s antioxidant capacity. This enhanced capacity effectively protects the photosynthetic apparatus from oxidative damage. Consequently, the resultant improvement in photosynthetic efficiency supports better growth performance under drought stress conditions.Keywords: Antioxidant capacity, Biochemical compounds, Medicinal plant, Oxidative stress

Introduction

Pennyroyal (Mentha pulegium L.) is a perennial medicinal plant belonging to the Lamiaceae family. This species typically grows wild in moist plains, along water edges, and even in aquatic environments. While pennyroyal can be cultivated in diverse locations, successful cultivation requires that the soil not be excessively dry, as its growth is rapidly inhibited under severe drought conditions [1]. This plant is widely distributed across various regions, particularly in humid habitats, and is valued for both its medicinal and culinary applications. Mentha pulegium synthesizes a diverse array of bioactive metabolites, including terpenoids, flavonoids, alkaloids, tannins, and saponins. These compounds contribute to its broad spectrum of therapeutic and biological properties, such as antimicrobial, antioxidant, antihypertensive, antidiabetic, anti-inflammatory, antifungal, anticancer, burn wound-healing, and antispasmodic activities [2]. All parts of the pennyroyal plant, particularly its leaves, possess a distinct spicy aroma attributable to their high essential oil content. Owing to this characteristic fragrance and flavor, the aerial parts—especially the leaves—are utilized in various pharmaceutical, food, and even cosmetic industries. Furthermore, the presence of key bioactive compounds such as pulegone, menthone, and neomenthone contributes to its notable biological activities, particularly its antibacterial effects [3]. In addition to its substantial essential oil content, numerous researchers have reported high concentrations of diverse phenolic compounds in pennyroyal extracts. These compounds further enhance the plant’s biological significance and expand its potential applications in pharmaceutical and functional food industries [4, 5]. The abundance of phenolic compounds in pennyroyal is strongly associated with its high antioxidant activity. Furthermore, numerous studies have documented its broad-spectrum antibacterial and antifungal properties, underscoring the plant’s pharmacological potential [5, 6].

However, the biosynthesis of these valuable metabolites and the subsequent commercial viability of M. pulegium are critically threatened by abiotic stresses, with drought being among the most pervasive and damaging. Drought represents a major limiting factor for global plant production, adversely affecting the cultivation of numerous species across diverse regions. This phenomenon poses a serious challenge to meeting the food and medicinal needs of millions worldwide [7]. Therefore, investigating strategies to enhance drought and dehydration tolerance in plants is of paramount importance. Plant growth results from the integrated development of young cells, driven by continuous meristematic division. Dehydration can disrupt cell division and development in meristematic tissues—such as roots, stems, and young leaves—leading to suppressed growth and reduced biomass accumulation [8]. Drought stress adversely impacts dry matter production in plants by disrupting ionic homeostasis, reducing cellular biomass, inducing stomatal closure, diminishing carbon dioxide uptake, and ultimately compromising photosynthetic efficiency [9]. However, the effects of drought stress vary significantly across plant species and are influenced by factors such as climatic conditions, plant genotype, growth stage, and nutritional status [10, 11]. Under drought stress conditions, the osmotic potential of the rhizosphere decreases relative to that of root cells. As the duration and intensity of stress exposure increase, membrane stability and permeability are compromised, leading to ion accumulation—particularly in stems—and reduced plant capacity to tolerate ionic imbalance [12]. Plant species exhibit diverse responses to such stress and generally employ two strategies to counteract drought-induced osmotic stress: avoidance and tolerance [13]. Numerous species activate defense mechanisms, including enhanced antioxidant capacity (via antioxidant compounds and enzymes) and accumulation of osmoprotectants such as proline and glycine betaine within their cells [11, 14].

Researchers employ various strategies to enhance plant growth under drought stress. Among these, the application of growth regulators has proven effective in improving the growth and performance of numerous plant species under stress conditions by modulating key physiological and biochemical processes [15, 16]. 5-Animolevulinic acid (ALA) is a key growth regulator naturally present in plants at low concentrations. It serves as an essential precursor for the biosynthesis of various porphyrinic compounds, including chlorophyll and certain cofactors (e.g., heme) [17]. Exogenous application of aminolevulinic acid (ALA) has been shown to enhance resistance to various environmental stresses, including drought [18], salinity [19], cold [20], and heavy metals [21], across multiple plant species. Given the culinary and medicinal significance of pennyroyal (Mentha pulegium L.), evaluating its responses to adverse conditions such as drought stress is of considerable value. While previous studies have investigated the effects of drought stress on pennyroyal [22, 23], a comprehensive analysis of its physiological and biochemical responses to ALA treatment under drought conditions remains unreported. The present study was designed to address two key objectives: (a) to elucidate the effects of a gradient of drought stress on the growth, physiological traits (e.g., leaf water status, photosynthetic pigments, Rubisco activity), and biochemical profile (e.g., osmolytes, antioxidant enzyme activity, membrane stability) of M. pulegium; and (b) to evaluate the efficacy of foliar application of 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) as a protective treatment to enhance drought tolerance and mitigate its adverse effects on pennyroyal.

Materials and methods

Location and experimental design

The present study was conducted in a glass greenhouse at the Institute of New Biological Technologies, University of Zanjan, Iran, during the 2021–2022 growing season. The experiment was arranged in a factorial scheme based on a completely randomized design (CRD) with three replications. The greenhouse conditions were maintained at a temperature range of 20–30 °C and relative humidity of 70–80% under natural sunlight. Seeds of pennyroyal (Mentha pulegium L.) were procured from Pakan Bazr Company (Isfahan, Iran) and taxonomically authenticated by Dr. Salehi. The experimental treatments consisted of four drought stress levels (100%, 75%, 50%, and 25% of field capacity, FC) and four concentrations of 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA; 0, 5, 10, and 15 µM). Drought stress was initiated at the time of seed sowing, and foliar application of ALA was performed at three growth stages: the first three-leaf stage, the third three-leaf stage, and the flowering stage. Drought levels corresponding to 75%, 50%, and 25% FC were designated as mild, moderate, and severe stress, respectively.

Application of treatments

Forty-eight containers (25 cm in diameter × 25 cm height) were filled with a 3:1 (v/v) mixture of perlite and cocopeat (total weight: 1 kg). Pennyroyal seeds were sown at a depth of 1 cm below the surface of the growing medium. Irrigation treatments were regulated using a TDR moisture meter (Model: TDR-300; Spectrum Technologies, USA) to maintain four soil moisture levels: 100%, 75%, 50%, and 25% of field capacity (FC). To determine field capacity, the growing medium in each container was saturated with water until drainage occurred from the bottom. The containers were then covered with plastic sheets to prevent evaporation and weighed daily until a constant weight was achieved (after 48 h). TDR probes were inserted vertically into the growing medium to record the moisture content at FC. Subsequently, the growing medium was oven-dried at 105 °C until completely dry and reweighed [24]. After successful establishment, seedlings were thinned to maintain ten uniform plants per pot. The nutritional requirements of the seedlings were supplied through irrigation with Hoagland’s nutrient solution (electrical conductivity: 1.6 dS/m). Foliar application of 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) was carried out during both vegetative and reproductive growth stages. The spraying process ensured complete coverage of all aerial parts (leaves and stems) with the ALA solution. Growth and physiological parameters were measured ten days after the final ALA application.

Morphological and growth parameters

Growth traits

Growth parameters assessed included plant height, leaf area, and the fresh and dry weights of both aerial parts and roots. To measure biomass, aerial and root tissues were separated and immediately weighed to determine fresh weight. Samples were then oven-dried at 80 °C for 48 h and reweighed to obtain dry weight [25].

Physiological and biochemical parameters

Proline

Leaf proline concentration was quantified spectrophotometrically according to the method of Bates et al. [26]. Briefly, fresh leaf tissue was homogenized in 3% (w/v) sulfosalicylic acid. The homogenate was centrifuged, and the supernatant was reacted with a solution of acid-ninhydrin and glacial acetic acid. The mixture was incubated in a boiling water bath for 60 min and subsequently cooled on ice to terminate the reaction. The chromophore was extracted into toluene, and its absorbance was measured at 520 nm using a UV spectrophotometer (Model: UV-1800; Shimadzu, Japan). Proline concentration was calculated based on a standard curve of pure L-proline and expressed as micrograms per gram fresh weight (µg g⁻¹ FW).

Glycine betaine

Glycine betaine content was determined according to the method of Grattan and Grieve [27]. Dried leaf tissue was suspended in distilled water and treated with 2 N HCl and potassium tri-iodide reagent. The mixture was vigorously shaken in an ice bath, resulting in the formation of two distinct phases. The absorbance of the organic layer was measured at 365 nm using a UV spectrophotometer. Glycine betaine concentration was quantified against a standard curve and expressed as micrograms per gram dry weight (µg g⁻¹ DW) [28].

Total phenolic compounds

The total phenolic content in pennyroyal leaf samples was quantified spectrophotometrically using the Folin-Ciocalteu reagent method [29]. Methanolic extracts were prepared using 85% (v/v) methanol. Briefly, 100 µL of the supernatant was diluted with distilled water and mixed with Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. After 4 min, 7.5% (w/v) sodium carbonate solution was added. The reaction mixture was incubated for 30 min, and absorbance was measured at 760 nm using a UV spectrophotometer. Total phenolic content was calculated based on a gallic acid standard curve and expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalent per gram of fresh weight (mg GAE g⁻¹ FW) [30, 31].

Essential oil content

Essential oil was extracted from dried aerial parts (dried at room temperature for 72 h) using hydrodistillation in a Clevenger apparatus (European Pharmacopoeia standard). The obtained oil was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate to remove residual moisture. The essential oil content was calculated as a percentage (v/w) relative to the initial dry weight of the plant material [25, 32].

Antioxidant enzymes

The activities of antioxidant enzymes—catalase, peroxidase, and superoxide dismutase—were determined using the following protocol. Fresh leaf tissue (0.5 g) was homogenized in 10 mL of 100 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) at 4 °C. The homogenate was centrifuged at 4 °C for 20 min, and the supernatant was collected as a crude enzyme extract for subsequent enzymatic assays. Soluble protein content in the extracts was quantified using the Bradford method with bovine serum albumin as a standard to normalize antioxidant enzyme activities [33].

Catalase

The catalase (CAT) activity was determined by monitoring the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) at 240 nm [34]. The assay is based on the decrease in absorbance as catalase in the plant extract catalyzes the breakdown of H₂O₂. The reaction mixture consisted of 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 10 mM H₂O₂, and 100 µL of enzyme extract. The change in absorbance was recorded at 240 nm over 1 min. Enzyme activity was calculated using the extinction coefficient of 39.4 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹ for H₂O₂ and expressed as units (U) per milligram of protein, where one unit is defined as the amount of enzyme required to decompose 1 µmol of H₂O₂ per minute under the assay conditions [28].

Peroxidase

Peroxidase (POD) activity was assayed using the guaiacol oxidation method [35]. The reaction was initiated by adding 100 µL of enzyme extract to a mixture containing potassium phosphate buffer, hydrogen peroxide, and guaiacol at 25 °C. The increase in absorbance due to tetraguaiacol formation was recorded at 470 nm for 90 s. Enzyme activity was calculated using the extinction coefficient of 26.6 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹ for tetraguaiacol and expressed as units (U) per milligram of protein. One unit of activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to produce 1 µmol of tetraguaiacol per minute under the assay conditions [28].

Superoxide dismutase

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined by measuring the inhibition of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) reduction according to the method described by Giannopolitis and Ries [36]. The assay relies on the enzyme’s capacity to scavenge superoxide radicals (O₂•⁻) produced through riboflavin photolysis, thereby preventing the reduction of NBT to purple formazan. The reaction mixture consisted of 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 12 mM methionine, 75 µM NBT, 50 mM sodium carbonate, and enzyme extract. The reaction was initiated by adding 30 µL of 1 µM riboflavin and exposing the mixture to light. Following incubation, the absorbance was measured at 560 nm. One unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to achieve 50% inhibition of NBT reduction compared to a control lacking enzyme extract. Enzyme activity was expressed as units per milligram of protein [37].

Hydrogen peroxide

Hydrogen peroxide was extracted by homogenizing plant samples in trichloroacetic acid, followed by centrifugation for ten minutes. The resulting supernatant was added to a reaction solution containing 0.5 mL of 10 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 1 mL of 1 M potassium iodide. After the addition of the extract, the samples were incubated for 15 min at 25 °C. Hydrogen peroxide content was determined by measuring absorbance at 390 nm and quantified based on a standard curve, expressed as micromoles per gram of fresh weight (µmol·g⁻¹ FW) [28, 38].

Malondialdehyde

The extent of lipid peroxidation was quantified by measuring the concentration of malondialdehyde (MDA) produced. Plant samples were homogenized in a trichloroacetic acid (TCA) solution for extraction. Following centrifugation, the resulting supernatant was combined with a TCA solution containing thiobarbituric acid (TBA) in a 1:3 (v/v) ratio. The mixture was incubated in a water bath at 90 °C for 30 min and subsequently cooled to room temperature. The absorbance of the solution was measured at 532 nm. To account for non-specific absorption, the absorbance at 600 nm was also recorded and subtracted from the value obtained at 532 nm. MDA content was calculated using the extinction coefficient of 155 mM⁻¹·cm⁻¹ and expressed as nanomoles per gram of fresh weight (nmol·g⁻¹ FW) [28, 39].

Relative water content (RWC)

The relative water content (RWC) of leaves was determined by first excising leaves from the plant and immediately measuring their fresh weight (FW). The samples were then oven-dried at 80 °C for 24 h and reweighed to obtain the dry weight (DW). RWC was calculated using the following formula: RWC (%) = [(FW - DW) / (FW)] × 100 [37, 40].

Photosynthetic pigments

Leaf samples were extracted with 80% (v/v) acetone and incubated in darkness for 24 h at 4 °C to prevent pigment degradation. Absorbance of the extracts was measured at 470 nm, 648 nm, and 664 nm using a spectrophotometer. Total chlorophyll and carotenoid concentrations were calculated according to Lichtenthaler’s equations and expressed as milligrams per gram of fresh weight (mg·g⁻¹ FW) [41].

Rubisco enzyme activity

The activity of ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (Rubisco) was measured as follows. Leaf samples were homogenized in an extraction buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.8), 0.05 M MgCl₂, 0.0025 M EDTA, 5 mM DTT (dithiothreitol), and 1% (w/v) PVP-40. The homogenate was centrifuged, and the supernatant was used as the enzyme source. The reaction mixture consisted of 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 40 mM NaHCO₃, 10 mM MgCl₂, 0.2 mM NADH, 4 mM ATP, 5 mM DTT, one unit each of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase and 3-phosphoglycerate kinase, and 0.2 mM ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP). The reaction was initiated by adding the enzyme extract. Absorbance was recorded at 340 nm at 30 °C. Rubisco activity was determined based on the rate of NADH oxidation and expressed as µmol NADH oxidized per minute per milligram of soluble protein [42].

Stomatal conductance and photosynthesis rate

Stomatal conductance and net photosynthesis rate were measured using a portable photosynthesis system (Model: LCA-4, ADC BioScientific Ltd., UK). Fully expanded leaves from each plant were placed in the cuvette with the adaxial (upper) surface exposed to ensure sufficient light interception. Measurements were taken between 9:00 and 11:00 AM under consistent environmental conditions. For each experimental unit, data were collected from five randomly selected plants, and the mean values were calculated and reported. Stomatal conductance and photosynthesis rate were expressed in mmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ and µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹, respectively [43].

Statistical analysis

Data were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS software (version 21). Mean comparisons for each trait were performed using Duncan’s new multiple range test at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05. Multivariate analyses, including correlation analysis based on Pearson’s coefficients between all trait pairs and principal component analysis (PCA), were also conducted using SPSS version 21.

Results and discussion

The effect of ALA on the osmotic compounds

In this study, the content of key osmotic compounds—proline and glycine betaine—in pennyroyal leaves was significantly affected by drought stress, 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) treatment, and their interaction (Table 1). With increasing drought stress severity, proline and glycine betaine content increased by 74.79% and 22.54%, respectively, compared to control conditions. External application of 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) showed no significant effect on the accumulation of these compounds in non-stressed plants. However, under drought conditions, ALA spraying significantly reduced the accumulation of both proline and glycine betaine in pennyroyal leaves. Foliar application of ALA, particularly at a concentration of 10 µM, effectively decreased proline and glycine betaine levels by 24.52% and 5.28%, respectively, under drought stress (Table 2).

The accumulation of osmotic compounds represents one of the most crucial defense mechanisms in various plant species against biotic and abiotic stresses [12]. Glycine betaine plays a significant role in activating antioxidant enzymes, protecting photosystems, and maintaining cell membrane integrity under oxidative stress conditions. Its accumulation is typically higher in drought-susceptible cultivars [21]. Similarly, proline accumulation contributes substantially to enhancing plant stress responsiveness and mitigating the adverse effects of drought on growth. Proline safeguards Rubisco structure by neutralizing oxygen free radicals and regulating osmotic balance [12].

Since glutamic acid serves as a precursor for the biosynthesis of both ALA and proline, the levels of these compounds within plant cells are correlated [44]. According to the report by Jiao et al. [18], the increase in proline content in stressed plants is attributed to a shift in glutamic acid metabolism from ALA synthesis toward proline synthesis. The elevation of proline and glycine betaine levels in ALA-treated plants under prior stress conditions has been reported by Farhadi et al. and Cai et al. [17, 42]. However, in the present study, the application of 5-aminolevulinic acid via spraying reduced the content of both osmolytes. The observed reduction in osmolyte accumulation under drought stress with ALA treatment indicates that the plant shifted its strategy from stress survival to growth maintenance. It appears that ALA treatment mitigates the effects of drought stress through alternative mechanisms, a conclusion supported by the decrease in proline and glycine betaine levels. The synthesis of osmotic compounds is energetically costly for the plant. Under stress conditions, the plant diverts a portion of its energy resources—which would otherwise be allocated to growth—to the production of compounds such as proline and glycine betaine. This suggests that ALA priming enhances the plant’s antioxidant capacity sufficiently to reduce its reliance on energy-intensive osmotic adjustment, thereby redirecting energy resources toward growth and physiological maintenance rather than the synthesis of stress mitigation compounds [44].

The effect of ALA on antioxidant compounds

The total phenolic content of pennyroyal leaves was significantly influenced by drought stress, 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) application, and their interaction (Table 1). As drought stress intensity increased, the total phenolic content rose significantly, reaching its highest level (8.01 mg/g fresh weight) under severe stress conditions. Foliar application of ALA also markedly affected phenolic levels across all stress treatments, with the most substantial increase observed in plants treated with 10 µM ALA (Table 2). Phenolic compounds serve as potent antioxidants and play a vital role in plant defense against oxidative stress by scavenging reactive oxygen species. Their antioxidant activity stems from their reductive capacity and ability to neutralize reactive oxygen species [4, 5]. The consistent increase in phenolic content following ALA application—particularly at 10 µM, across all stress levels—highlights its effectiveness in enhancing the plant’s non-enzymatic antioxidant potential. According to Tan et al., ALA treatment improves plant stress tolerance by activating the biosynthetic pathways of antioxidant compounds, including diverse phenolic metabolites [21]. The rise in phenolic compounds in response to ALA foliar spraying is further attributed to increased phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) activity [17]. As the key enzyme in the phenylpropanoid pathway, PAL regulates phenol metabolism and the biosynthesis of various phenolic compounds—especially flavonoids—from phenylalanine [45]. The upregulation of this biochemical pathway represents a crucial metabolic strategy for enhancing oxidative stress resilience under drought conditions [18].

Analysis of variance indicated that drought stress, foliar application of 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA), and their interaction significantly influenced the essential oil content of pennyroyal plants (Table 1). As drought stress increased up to 50% of field capacity, essential oil content rose by 22.67%. However, under severe drought stress (25% FC), essential oil content decreased by 4% compared to the non-stressed control. Foliar application of ALA significantly enhanced essential oil production across all drought stress levels. The effect of ALA treatment on essential oil production exhibited a positive correlation with increasing stress intensity. Application of 10 µM ALA increased essential oil content by 2.67%, 3.66%, 4.17%, and 4.17% under control, mild (75% FC), moderate (50% FC), and severe (25% FC) drought conditions, respectively (Fig. 1).

Carbon allocation shifts toward secondary metabolism over primary metabolism under adverse environmental conditions, manifesting as reduced plant biomass and increased essential oil content [13, 46]. Terpenoids, as the main constituents of essential oil, possess antioxidant properties and scavenge free radicals; thus, enhanced essential oil accumulation represents a defense mechanism that promotes plant survival under stress [11]. The increase in essential oil under mild to moderate stress may result from a higher density of secretory glands in the leaves [10]. However, in the present study, a further increase in stress intensity reduced essential oil content, likely due to stress-induced damage to young leaves, which exhibit high biogenetic capacity for essential oil biosynthesis [8]. The elevated essential oil content in pennyroyal under varying stress levels can be attributed to improved nutrient uptake, enhanced photosynthetic activity, increased secretory gland density, and upregulation of monoterpene biosynthesis in response to ALA treatment [47]. Furthermore, the positive effect of 5-aminolevulinic acid on essential oil production may be linked to its modulation of hormones involved in terpenoid biosynthesis, such as auxins and gibberellins [48]. The ability of ALA to sustain essential oil synthesis under severe drought underscores its dual role in stress protection and metabolic regulation, ensuring the production of valuable secondary metabolites even under adverse conditions [47].

Effect of ALA on antioxidant enzymes activity

Analysis of variance revealed that drought stress, 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) application, and their interaction significantly influenced the activity of key antioxidant enzymes—catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) (Table 1). Enzyme activity exhibited a progressive increase with rising stress intensity. Severe drought stress enhanced CAT, POD, and SOD activities by 37.82%, 56.69%, and 41.79%, respectively. Across all stress levels, foliar application of ALA further elevated enzyme activities, with the highest levels observed in severely stressed plants treated with 10 µM ALA. In non-stressed conditions, ALA application increased CAT activity by up to 5.04%. Under mild, moderate, and severe stress, treatment with 10 µM ALA elevated CAT activity by 7.29%, 15.14%, and 20.85%, respectively (Table 2). ALA application also significantly boosted POD activity in both control and stressed plants. Foliar treatment with 10 µM ALA increased POD activity by 11.02% under non-stressed conditions and by 17.93% under drought stress (Table 2). Similarly, ALA treatment—particularly at 10 µM—markedly enhanced SOD activity. In non-stressed plants, ALA application increased SOD activity by 32.84% compared to untreated controls. Under drought stress, SOD activity was elevated by up to 56.84% following ALA treatment (Table 2).

Antioxidant enzyme activity increased progressively with drought stress intensity, consistent with the typical response observed across many plant species under various environmental stresses [14, 22]. This rise in enzymatic activity under adverse conditions likely results from increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which activates cellular defense mechanisms to mitigate oxidative damage [7]. Enhanced antioxidant capacity under stress is a key component of plant defense strategies [9]. In pennyroyal plants, foliar application of ALA significantly increased the activities of catalase, ascorbate peroxidase, and superoxide dismutase. This coordinated upregulation of enzymatic defenses highlights ALA’s role in proactively reinforcing the plant’s antioxidant system against drought-induced oxidative stress. The marked increase in enzyme activity following ALA treatment suggests a targeted priming effect rather than a mere passive stress response. Cai et al. and Tan et al. reported that ALA application enhances defense systems in various plant species by stimulating antioxidant enzymes [17, 21]. Furthermore, since 5-aminolevulinic acid is a precursor for tetrapyrrole and heme biosynthesis—and heme is a key cofactor in activating antioxidant enzymes—the observed increase in enzyme activity may be attributed to elevated heme concentrations in ALA-treated plants [42].

Effect of ALA on hydrogen peroxide and lipid peroxidation

Oxidative damage from drought stress in pennyroyal plants was evaluated by measuring hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in leaves. Analysis of variance revealed that drought stress, foliar application, and their interaction significantly affected H₂O₂ and MDA concentrations (Table 3). Drought stress increased H₂O₂ and MDA levels by 88.74% and 81.48%, respectively, compared to control conditions. Foliar application of 5-aminolevulinic acid significantly reduced the accumulation of both compounds across all drought stress levels (Table 4). While all ALA concentrations were effective, the 10 µM treatment resulted in the most substantial reduction, decreasing H₂O₂ and MDA accumulation by 16.41% and 22.70%, respectively, in stressed plants (Table 4). The notable increase in H₂O₂ and MDA under drought stress indicates considerable membrane damage due to dehydration, consistent with findings in various plant species [10, 13, 16].

In the present study, the observed reduction in hydrogen peroxide levels and lipid peroxidation in ALA-treated plants resulted from enhanced synthesis of antioxidant compounds (total phenols and essential oils) and increased activity of antioxidant enzymes (catalase, peroxidase, and superoxide dismutase). By improving the plant’s antioxidant capacity, aminolevulinic acid protects cellular membranes against lipid peroxidation, as evidenced by reduced hydrogen peroxide and malondialdehyde accumulation [44]. Furthermore, according to Cai et al. (2019), the decrease in lipid peroxidation in response to ALA treatment is associated with reduced translocation and accumulation of abscisic acid in plant cells under stress conditions [17].

Effect of ALA on relative water content

Analysis of variance revealed that drought stress, foliar application, and their interaction significantly affected the relative water content (RWC) of pennyroyal leaves (Table 3). Drought stress reduced RWC by up to 37.21%, while ALA application effectively improved RWC, particularly under drought conditions. Treatment with 5-aminolevulinic acid (10 µM) mitigated dehydration-induced RWC reduction by up to 8.44% in pennyroyal leaves (Table 4). Osmotic stress resulting from reduced root water absorption leads to decreased RWC [20]. ALA application effectively mitigated water loss by enhancing the plant’s osmotic adjustment capacity and protecting membrane integrity. By reinforcing the plant’s defense system, ALA treatment protected cell membranes against lipid peroxidation, thereby reducing osmotic stress and improving RWC in stressed plants [42].

Effect of ALA on photosynthetic characteristics

The mean squares for drought, ALA application, and their interaction were significant for photosynthetic pigment content (total chlorophyll and carotenoids) in pennyroyal leaves (Table 3). Total chlorophyll and carotenoid levels decreased with increasing drought severity, with the lowest values (4.89 mg/g FW and 0.592 mg/g FW, respectively) observed under severe stress (25% FC). ALA treatment enhanced total chlorophyll and carotenoid content in both non-stressed and drought-stressed plants, with more pronounced improvements observed in stressed plants. Under severe stress conditions, plants treated with 10 µM 5-aminolevulinic acid exhibited 8.20% and 6.78% higher chlorophyll and carotenoid content, respectively, compared to untreated plants (Table 4). The reduction in photosynthetic pigments under stress is attributed to photooxidation and chlorophyll degradation caused by oxidative stress [14, 49]. Damage to photosystems and chlorophyll decomposition—resulting from increased chlorophyllase activity under water deficit—lead to decreased pigment concentrations [23]. By enhancing the plant’s defense system through elevated antioxidant compounds and enzymes (Table 2), 5-aminolevulinic acid protects thylakoid membranes against lipid peroxidation (Table 4), thereby mitigating pigment damage [18].

The experimental treatments and their interaction (spraying solution × stress) significantly influenced stomatal conductance in pennyroyal leaves (Table 3). Drought stress reduced stomatal conductance by 50%, while application of 10 µM ALA increased it under both non-stressed and drought-stressed conditions (Table 4). The mean squares for stress, foliar application, and their interaction were also significant for Rubisco enzyme activity (Table 3). Rubisco activity decreased by 40% under progressive drought stress compared to control plants. Although ALA treatment only slightly enhanced Rubisco activity in control plants, it significantly increased activity across all drought stress levels. Application of 5-aminolevulinic acid enhanced Rubisco activity by 2.17%, 8.82%, 8.06%, and 6.78% in plants grown under no stress, mild stress (75% FC), moderate stress (50% FC), and severe stress (25% FC), respectively (Fig. 2). Drought stress, ALA treatment, and their interaction significantly affected photosynthetic rates (Table 3). Drought significantly reduced photosynthesis compared to controls, while 5-aminolevulinic acid application improved photosynthetic rates under both control and drought conditions (Table 4). The osmotic stress induced by drought triggers stomatal closure, limiting carbon dioxide diffusion into leaves and consequently reducing Rubisco enzyme activity [47].

Effect of 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) on Rubisco enzyme activity in Mentha pulegium under different drought stress levels

Rubisco, as a key enzyme in photosynthesis, catalyzes carbon dioxide fixation and is essential for plant survival under adverse environmental conditions [15, 18]. The significant positive correlation between Rubisco activity and photosynthetic rate observed in this study underscores its pivotal role in maintaining carbon assimilation during drought stress. The improvement in Rubisco activity following ALA treatment is attributed to enhanced stomatal aperture and upregulated gene expression of Calvin cycle enzymes [17]. Our findings further suggest that ALA may promote Rubisco activase activity, thereby increasing the enzyme’s carboxylation efficiency under stress conditions. Additionally, ALA appears to modulate abscisic acid transport, leading to increased stomatal conductance and improved CO₂ diffusion into leaves, consequently enhancing Rubisco activity [18]. This coordinated enhancement of Rubisco function demonstrates how ALA treatment optimizes the photosynthetic apparatus through both biochemical and regulatory mechanisms.

The reduction in photosynthesis under drought conditions is fundamentally associated with decreased photosynthetic pigment content, reduced stomatal conductance, and diminished Rubisco activity, as osmotic stress restricts carbon dioxide diffusion into leaves [23, 47]. Our results indicate that ALA treatment specifically addresses these photosynthetic constraints by simultaneously improving stomatal aperture and enhancing enzymatic carboxylation efficiency. The observed increase in photosynthetic rate was achieved through a dual mechanism: elevated stomatal conductance promoting CO₂ uptake and enhanced Rubisco activity facilitating carbon fixation [44]. Notably, ALA appears to regulate stomatal behavior by modulating guard cell ion transport [15], thereby optimizing the balance between water conservation and carbon assimilation. Furthermore, the enhanced photosynthetic capacity was supported by coordinated upregulation of photosynthetic enzymes and improved carbohydrate metabolism [15, 18]. This multi-level improvement of the photosynthetic apparatus demonstrates ALA’s role in maintaining photosynthetic productivity under drought stress.

Effect of ALA on yield and growth characteristics

Drought stress, ALA treatment, and their interaction significantly influenced the growth traits of pennyroyal plants (Table 5). Increasing drought severity led to substantial reductions in plant height and leaf area. Under severe drought stress (25% FC), plant height and leaf area decreased by 24.35% and 45.92%, respectively, compared to control conditions. While ALA application significantly improved height and leaf area even in non-stressed plants, its effects were more pronounced under drought stress. Treatment with 10 µM ALA particularly enhanced these growth parameters in stressed plants (Table 6). Fresh and dry weights of both aerial parts and roots declined significantly with increasing drought intensity. The lowest biomass values were recorded under severe stress (25% FC): aerial parts (21.74 g fresh weight and 6.94 g dry weight per plant) and roots (3.31 g fresh weight and 1.25 g dry weight per plant). ALA application effectively mitigated drought-induced biomass reduction, with improving effects becoming more prominent as stress severity increased. Foliar treatment with 5-aminolevulinic acid reduced the negative impact of stress on aerial and root biomass by 22% and 29%, respectively (Table 6).

The reduced root growth observed in pennyroyal plants under drought stress may be associated with decreased meristematic activity and altered cell elongation patterns due to changes in plant water relations and activation of cell wall hydrolyzing enzymes. These modifications impair water and nutrient uptake under stress conditions [9], ultimately leading to diminished plant height and biomass accumulation. The growth reduction in drought-stressed pennyroyal plants is further linked to decreased leaf area, reduced photosynthetic pigment content, and diminished Rubisco enzyme activity. In the present study, the improved growth performance following ALA application was attributable to enhanced antioxidant capacity in stressed plants, resulting in reduced lipid peroxidation (Table 2), improved relative water content (Table 4), increased photosynthetic pigment levels (Table 4), and enhanced Rubisco activity (Fig. 2). The promoted growth in ALA-treated plants aligns with modifications in plant hormone concentrations, particularly auxins and cytokinins—key regulators of plant growth and development. However, plant growth responses to 5-aminolevulinic acid vary depending on concentration, plant species, and developmental stage at the time of treatment [47].

While the present study clearly demonstrates that foliar application of ALA effectively enhances drought tolerance in M. pulegium by improving antioxidant capacity, photosynthetic performance, and growth parameters, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated. The observed correlations between ALA treatment and the upregulation of protective pathways suggest—but do not confirm—direct causal relationships. To move beyond correlation and establish definitive mechanistic models, future studies should employ integrated molecular approaches. Specifically, quantifying the expression of genes encoding key antioxidant enzymes (e.g., SOD, CAT, POX), biosynthetic enzymes for phenolics (e.g., PAL) and terpenoids, as well as conducting comprehensive phytohormone profiling (particularly ABA and auxins), would provide critical insights. Furthermore, experimental validation of ALA’s role as a precursor for heme cofactor synthesis would strengthen our understanding of its function in activating antioxidant enzymatic systems. Such molecular and biochemical analyses are essential to elucidate the precise signal transduction pathways through which ALA enhances stress resilience, ultimately providing a more comprehensive understanding of its mode of action.

Multivariate analysis

Correlation analysis among the measured growth, physiological, and biochemical traits of M. pulegium revealed a complex network of highly significant positive and negative relationships, as visualized in the heat map (Fig. 3). A strong synergistic network was observed, wherein antioxidant enzymes (CAT, POX, SOD) and compounds such as phenols exhibited positive correlations with each other, as well as with key photosynthetic parameters (Rubisco activity, stomatal conductance, photosynthetic rate) and growth metrics (plant height, biomass). Conversely, oxidative stress markers (hydrogen peroxide and malondialdehyde) showed strong negative correlations with this beneficial network. Notably, osmoprotectants (proline and glycine betaine) were positively correlated with stress markers and negatively associated with growth traits, suggesting their accumulation reflects severe stress rather than confers tolerance. These results indicate that the mechanism of enhanced drought tolerance is primarily rooted in the coordinated enhancement of the antioxidative system, which effectively scavenges reactive oxygen species. This protective response safeguards the photosynthetic apparatus from oxidative damage, thereby sustaining carbon assimilation [9, 42]. The positive correlation between essential oil content and photosynthetic performance further suggests that maintained carbon fixation under ALA treatment enables concurrent resource allocation toward both growth and valuable secondary metabolite production, rather than a trade-off between these processes [10, 11].

The biplot (Fig. 4) effectively segregates the measured traits and treatments along the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), which collectively account for 89.7% of the total variance in the dataset. PC1, which likely represents a gradient of drought stress intensity and plant response, clearly separates traits associated with oxidative stress and osmotic adjustment from those related to plant vitality, photosynthetic performance, and antioxidant defense. This separation demonstrates that severe drought stress is characterized by elevated oxidative damage and osmolyte accumulation, which are diametrically opposed to growth and photosynthetic efficiency. The PCA results confirm that the mechanism by which ALA alleviates drought stress is primarily mediated through enhancement of the antioxidant system. The close clustering of antioxidant enzymes with photosynthetic and growth traits indicates that ALA treatment enabled plants to protect photosynthetic machinery from oxidative damage. This protection was essential for sustaining carbon assimilation, which subsequently supported both biomass accumulation and biosynthesis of valuable secondary metabolites such as essential oils, without necessitating a trade-off between these processes. Consequently, ALA application shifted the plant’s strategy from a damage-coping state, characterized by stress biomarkers, toward a state of maintained physiological function and resilience [12, 17].

Conclusion

This study definitively establishes 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) as a highly effective priming agent that strategically enhances drought resilience in Mentha pulegium L. Our research advances beyond merely documenting stress symptoms to elucidating a fundamental physiological mechanism: ALA’s efficacy is fundamentally rooted in its targeted enhancement of the plant’s antioxidant defense system. Through significant upregulation of key enzymatic antioxidants (CAT, POX, SOD) and promotion of non-enzymatic antioxidants (phenolics, essential oils), ALA treatment effectively preserves cellular integrity against oxidative damage. This robust antioxidative protection serves as the critical foundation for maintaining photosynthetic performance—specifically through safeguarding Rubisco activity and stomatal function—thereby ensuring sustained carbon assimilation and biomass production under drought conditions. The pivotal innovation of our findings lies in identifying an optimal, cost-effective application protocol (foliar application of 10 µM ALA at critical growth stages), which not only mitigates drought-induced growth limitations but also enhances the commercial value of this medicinal crop through increased essential oil yield under stress conditions. These results provide a sustainable and practical strategy for cultivating medicinal plants in water-limited environments, offering significant implications for productivity in drought-prone regions.
