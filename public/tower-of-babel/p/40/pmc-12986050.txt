A pH/Enzyme-Sensitive Doxorubicin Prodrug Micelle for Safe and Effective Cancer Treatment
Authors: Xiang Li, Dan Wang, Shengyue Wu, Na Na, Xue Yang, Dongcheng Yi, Zixi Zhang, Qian Liang, Ziming Zhao, Yabing Hua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives: This study developed pH/enzyme-sensitive polymeric HA-AAN-DOX (HAD) micelles to resolve the limited targeting specificity of chemotherapy drugs. Methods: Hyaluronic acid (HA) and the chemotherapeutic agent doxorubicin (DOX) were conjugated via a hydrazone linkage utilizing an Ala-Ala-ASP tripeptide (AAN) as the connecting moiety, which is sensitive to the legumain enzyme. DOX was delivered via HAD micelles, which were activated by both hyaluronidase and the legumain enzyme. Key findings: The results revealed the remarkable antitumor efficacy of these micelles both in vivo and in vitro. Compared with that of doxorubicin hydrochloride (DOX·HCl), the incidence of toxic side effects was significantly reduced with the HAD micelle treatment. As a result, micelles composed of hyaluronic acid and doxorubicin (HAD) offer a reliable and effective method for drug delivery, with the potential to optimize the therapeutic impact of chemotherapeutic agents on tumors by reducing unintended side effects. Conclusions: Micelles composed of hyaluronic acid and doxorubicin (HAD) offer a reliable and effective method for drug delivery, with the potential to optimize the therapeutic impact of chemotherapeutic agents on tumors by reducing unintended side effects.

1. Introduction

Malignant neoplasms have emerged as a major public health concern, representing a substantial threat to human life and overall well-being. Chemotherapeutic agents remain the standard treatment modalities for cancer. However, despite remarkable progress in tumor treatment, conventional chemotherapeutic agents still suffer from critical limitations, including poor tumor selectivity, severe systemic toxicity, drug resistance, and insufficient penetration into poorly vascularized tumor tissues [1,2]. These challenges highlight the urgent need for more precise, tumor-targeted, and effective drug delivery strategies in cancer therapy. Among clinically used chemotherapeutics, DOX is a representative example that illustrates both high efficacy and dose-limiting toxicity.

DOX is a topoisomerase II inhibitor that induces DNA damage and is widely used to treat various types of cancer [3]. Although widely used in clinical practice, dose-limiting cardiotoxicity is the main adverse side effect of DOX [4,5]. Therefore, the development of novel drug carriers or prodrug systems for DOX could minimize its side effects, improve safety, and enhance its therapeutic efficacy in tumors [6,7]. To achieve such improved safety and efficacy, an effective approach is to exploit tumor-specific biological cues for controlled drug delivery.

A key insight driving targeted drug delivery research lies in the unique TMEs, which differ distinctly from normal tissues. These include a slightly acidic extracellular environment, reductive intracellular conditions, and the overexpression of specific enzymes [8,9,10,11]. These tumor-specific features provide a rational basis for designing stimulus-responsive drug delivery systems, such as pH-responsive, reduction-responsive, and enzyme-responsive platforms. Among these stimuli, enzymatic activation is particularly attractive for prodrug design. Enzyme-activated antitumor prodrugs have garnered considerable attention due to their ability to remain inactive or weakly active in normal tissues but selectively activate in tumor tissues, thereby reducing systemic toxicity [12,13,14]. The related enzymes exploited in such systems are typically endogenously overexpressed in tumor tissues, eliminating the need for exogenous introduction. Representative tumor-associated enzymes include matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [15], fibroblast active activator protein alpha (FAP alpha) [16], hyaluronidase (HAase) [17] and legumain [18]. In this context, HA is especially appealing because it simultaneously offers a tumor-targeting ligand and a substrate for tumor-associated enzymes (e.g., HAase), enabling multi-level selectivity.

HA, a naturally occurring linear polysaccharide, offers exceptional advantages as a drug carrier, including biodegradability, biocompatibility, and non-immunogenicity [19]. Moreover, HA enables active tumor targeting through specific binding to the CD44 receptor, which is highly overexpressed on the surface of various tumor cells [20]. HAase, which is abnormally elevated in malignant and invasive tumors, exhibits pH-dependent activity—maintaining high catalytic efficiency in the acidic TME while remaining largely inactive [21]. Meanwhile, combining HAase-responsiveness with a second tumor-enriched protease trigger can further enhance on-demand drug release specificity. Legumain, also known as endopeptidase, is a lysosomal cysteine protease that functions as a stress-related protein. It is significantly upregulated in a wide range of solid tumors, tumor-associated macrophages, and tumor neovascular endothelial cells, where it plays crucial roles in angiogenesis, tumor invasion, and metastasis [22]. In contrast, legumain expression is negligible or absent in most normal tissues, making it an ideal tumor-specific enzymatic trigger [23,24,25]. However, HAase responsiveness alone may not be sufficient for precise intracellular drug activation. Legumain offers a complementary tumor-specific trigger. Therefore, the legumain-sensitive tripeptide Ala–Ala–Asn (AAN) was employed as a cleavable linker to enable tumor-specific activation and controlled release of doxorubicin. Based on these considerations, we hypothesized that integrating HA-mediated CD44 targeting with HAase/legumain-triggered activation could improve tumor selectivity while reducing systemic toxicity.

In this study, we constructed a novel tumor-targeted prodrug system by conjugating an antitumor drug with HA via a legumain-sensitive tripeptide linker. This prodrug is designed to be synergistically activated by HAase and legumain in the tumor microenvironment. The primary objective of this research is to overcome the targeting deficiencies and systemic toxicity associated with traditional chemotherapy, thereby developing a safe and effective therapeutic strategy for precision cancer treatment.

2. Results and Discussion

2.1. Characterization of HAD Micelles

As a hydrophobic component, DOX was grafted onto the hydrophilic HA chain, making HAD amphiphilic so that it has the ability to spontaneously form micelles when dispersed in aqueous solution. The synthesis scheme of HAD is depicted in Figure 1. The 1H NMR results are shown in Figure 2A, which are mainly employed to confirm the successful conjugation of DOX and AAN onto the HA backbone and to semi-quantitatively evaluate the grafting efficiency, rather than to fully assign all proton signals, as would be required for small-molecule structural elucidation. 11As shown in the 1H NMR spectrum (Figure 2A), the full-range spectra (0–14 ppm) for HA, Fmoc-AAN, DOX, and HAD are available in the Supporting Information (Figures S7–S10). The native HA spectrum, recorded in D2O, exhibited the characteristic broad resonances associated with the polysaccharide backbone within the δ~3.0–4.5 ppm range, along with the diagnostic N-acetyl methyl signal observed at δ~1.9–2.1 ppm. The Fmoc-AAN spectrum, recorded in DMSO-d6, displayed the anticipated aromatic resonances of the Fmoc group between δ~7.2 and 7.9 ppm, aliphatic signals in the δ~4.1–4.5 ppm range, and a distinctive AAN methyl resonance at δ~1.50 ppm. The DOX spectrum, also recorded in DMSO-d6, revealed prominent aromatic resonances in the δ~7.0–8.0 ppm range, multiple oxygenated/sugar-proton signals spanning δ~3.0–5.5 ppm, and a diagnostic methyl resonance at δ~1.35 ppm. Significantly, the spectrum of HAD (DMSO-d6) simultaneously exhibited (i) the saccharide envelope associated with HA (δ~3.0–4.8 ppm), (ii) the aromatic resonances attributable to DOX and the linker moiety (δ~7.0–8.0 ppm), and (iii) both diagnostic methyl signals assigned to DOX (δ~1.35 ppm) and AAN (δ~1.50 ppm). The concurrent presence of these structural signatures confirms the successful grafting of DOX onto the HA backbone via the AAN linker. Solvent-related signals, including residual DMSO-d6 at δ~2.50 ppm and trace water in DMSO at δ~3.3 ppm, were annotated to prevent misassignment. A semi-quantitative comparison of the integrals of the characteristic DOX (δ~1.35 ppm), AAN (δ~1.50 ppm), and HA (e.g., δ~4.8 ppm) resonances further corroborated the formation of the conjugate.

The FTIR analysis results illustrated in Figure 2B provided additional insights into the chemical composition of HAD. The distinct absorption peaks observed for DOX at 1430 cm−1 and 710 cm−1, stemming from the -C=C- stretching mode within the benzene ring, suggest the presence of the DOX conjugates HA and AAN. The successful modification of AANs with DOX is evidenced by the characteristic peaks observed at 1652 cm−1 and 1560 cm−1, which correspond to the amide groups. Finally, the characteristic absorption peaks of HA at 1047 cm−1 and 1080 cm−1 from the ether bond indicated the formation of the AAN-DOX conjugate HA. These findings indicate the successful conjugation of DOX molecules with the AAN and HA polymers. This spectral region primarily corresponds to O–H and N–H stretching vibrations (3600–3000 cm−1) and –CH2/–CH3 stretching vibrations (~2920/~2850 cm−1). Due to the significant presence of hydroxyl groups and bound water in samples such as HA-based materials, the O–H/N–H stretching bands exhibit pronounced broadening. Changes in hydrogen bond strength and local polarity, occurring when carboxyl/amide groups participate in the hydrogen bond network or when carboxyl groups transform into amide groups, lead to alterations in the peak position and band shape of this broad peak. This observation is corroborated by simultaneous changes in the amide I/II bands in the 1700–1500 cm−1 region and the carboxylate bands at 1600/1410 cm−1, thereby supporting the presence of carboxyl/amide-related interactions and vibrational frequency shifts induced by electronic effects (inductive/conjugation) within the samples.

However, the size and stability of micelles are closely related to the molar ratio of DOX covalently conjugated to HA via the AAN of DOX. In our study, HAD1 micelles precipitated in PBS at pH 7.4, and the stability under physiological conditions was poor. When HA reacted with AAN-DOX at a 1:1 mol ratio, the prepared HAD1 solution was less water-soluble and could not form stable micelles. When the HA and AAN-DOX molar ratios were 2:1 and 4:1, the prepared HAD2 and HAD3 solutions formed micelles, but HAD2 micelles were smaller in particle size and had higher drug loading than HAD3 micelles, so HAD2 was ultimately selected as the preparation for subsequent experiments (Table 1). The particle size distribution spectra for HAD2 and HAD3, as depicted in Figures S1–S6, are provided in the Supplementary Information (SI).

The TEM morphological observations presented in Figure 3A reinforce the spherical morphology and consistent size distribution of HAD, indicating successful formation and structural integrity. As shown in Figure 3B and Table 1, both HAD2 and HAD3 formed nanosized micelles with mean hydrodynamic diameters exceeding 200 nm. Specifically, HAD2, with a HA:AAN-DOX ratio of 2:1, exhibited a smaller particle size of 220.4 ± 8.4 nm compared to HAD3, which had a ratio of 4:1 and a particle size of 257.3 ± 8.8 nm. The polydispersity indices (PDIs) were determined to be 0.228 ± 0.037 for HAD2 and 0.153 ± 0.045 for HAD3, indicating relatively narrow size distributions. Both formulations possessed a moderately negative surface charge, with zeta potentials measured at −12.2 ± 2.1 mV for HAD2 and −13.5 ± 1.6 mV for HAD3. Furthermore, a preliminary stability assessment in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4 demonstrated that HAD2 micelles exhibited less than a 10% change in particle size over a 72 h period without visible precipitation, suggesting good colloidal stability under physiological conditions. Given its smaller size and satisfactory stability, HAD2 was selected for all subsequent experiments.

2.2. In Vitro DOX Release

The release dynamics of DOX from the HAD micelles were quantitatively assessed using fluorescence spectroscopy (Figure 4A). As a hydrophobic fluorescent compound, DOX persisted in a quenched state within PBS (pH 7.4, 0.15 M) [13]. The heightened fluorescent signal produced by the DOX within the nanosystems was quantified to determine the quantity of released DOX [14,15].

The release of DOX from HAD was assessed using a dialysis method in PBS across various pH levels and media. Specifically, at pH 7.4 and 6.5, HAase and legumain were employed to simulate physiological conditions present in blood circulation and normal tissues, as well as the mildly acidic environment characteristic of the tumor microenvironment.

The results depicted in Figure 4A demonstrate the pronounced pH-responsive drug release behavior of HAD micelles over a 24 h period, with significantly higher DOX release at pH 6.5 than at pH 7.4. This behavior is closely related to the structural features and intermolecular interactions governing micelle formation. Specifically, the assembly and stability of HAD micelles rely not only on covalent amide conjugation, but also on multiple intermolecular noncovalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding between HA chains, hydrophobic interactions involving the aromatic moieties of DOX, and electrostatic interactions between the carboxyl groups of HA and the amino groups of DOX [26]. These interactions collectively establish a dynamic equilibrium that maintains micelle integrity.

Under physiological conditions (pH 7.4), HA exhibits a high degree of carboxyl ionization, resulting in stronger electrostatic repulsion between HA chains. Meanwhile, hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions provide compensatory stabilization, leading to a relatively compact micellar structure and a slow drug release rate. In contrast, in the TME (pH 6.5), the elevated proton concentration suppresses carboxyl ionization, thereby weakening electrostatic interactions. This change, together with the partial disruption of hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, destabilizes the intermolecular balance and promotes micelle loosening and disassembly, ultimately accelerating DOX release [27,28].

Notably, the increase in the drug release rate of HAD micelles in the absence of HAase and legumain was lower than that of the HAD micelles exposed to HAase and Legumain, which were added for 24 h. HAase, in this case, promoted the degradation of HA in an acidic environment, destroyed the protective effect of the coating, and accelerated the release of DOX. HAase cleaved HA into oligosaccharides, creating additional gaps on the particle surface that facilitated drug diffusion in acidic conditions, leading to an accelerated drug release rate. HA can initially attach to CD44 receptors located on the cancer cell membrane, leading to the formation of caveolae structures. As caveolae transform into endosomes and subsequently merge with lysosomes, the pH diminishes to around 6.5. HA undergoes degradation by HAase during this process, leading to rapid and effective drug liberation [16]. Proteins isolated from tumor tissues demonstrated significantly elevated asparaginyl endopeptidase activity compared to those from normal tissues. This heightened activity resulted in an enhanced ability to release DOX from HAD, thereby corroborating the in vivo legumain-responsive characteristics of these HAD micelles.

2.3. In Vitro Cytotoxicity, Mechanisms and Safety Evaluation

The cytotoxic effects of DOX, HAD and HAD+ at various concentrations were evaluated via CCK-8 assays in HeLa cells. In Figure 4B, all the drugs showed dose-responsive antitumor activity at different concentrations. Free DOX showed the best anticancer potency compared with the other treatments. DOX has some degree of cytotoxicity against HeLa cells, but the cytotoxicity of HAD micelles was similar to that of DOX. This is because HAase and legumin enzymes degrade HA and tripeptide in HeLa cells and release the active substance DOX. Furthermore, “HAD + Haase” showed stronger cytotoxicity than did DOX and HAD; this phenomenon occurred as a result of the rapid absorption of HAD through CD44 receptor-mediated endocytosis, leading to an enhanced discharge of DOX from HAD. Moreover, HAase is abundantly expressed within the tumor microenvironment, facilitating the breakdown of the HA shell surrounding HAD micelles [17].

The cytotoxicity of HAD to L929 cells was significantly lower than that of DOX. As shown in Figure 4C, DOX induced a pronounced, concentration-dependent decrease in L929 cell viability, indicating substantial cytotoxicity at moderate to high doses (16–64 μM). In contrast, HAD consistently maintained markedly higher cell viability across the tested concentration range, and the reduction in viability remained comparatively limited even at elevated concentrations. Collectively, these results demonstrate that HAD exhibits superior in vitro biosafety/cytocompatibility toward normal L929 cells relative to free DOX.

As shown in Figure 4D, ATP energy inhibitors, chlorpromazine hydrochloride, and chloroquine significantly inhibited the cell entry of HAD, and the chlorpromazine hydrochloride group had the most obvious inhibition, reaching less than 30%. The inhibitory effect was not obvious in the other groups.

2.4. In Vivo Antitumor Efficacy

Different treatments were administered to mice with tumors to explore the effectiveness of anticancer therapies. The tumor destruction pattern is shown in Figure 5A, and in Figure 5B, treatment with HAD micelles and DOX delays tumor growth compared with that in the saline and HA treatment groups. This result suggested that DOX has an effect. After a 10-day treatment period, the mice were euthanized, and their tumors were excised and weighed. The results indicated a significant reduction in tumor weights in the group treated with HAD/DOX, with HAD micelles exhibiting the most pronounced inhibitory effect (Table 2). Furthermore, the weight of the DOX group decreased significantly after treatment, indicating that DOX had a therapeutic effect but was highly toxic and caused excessive side effects (Figure 5C,D). The weights of the mice in the other groups increased slowly without toxicity or side effects, which was in line with the increasing weight of the mice.

H&E staining was conducted on major organs and tumor tissues to further assess the general toxicity of HAD micelles. As illustrated in Figure 5E, the tissues in the saline-treated group exhibited compact arrangement and intact morphology, suggesting an absence of significant toxicity to the organs. Furthermore, administration of HAD micelles or DOX to the mice resulted in tumor cells with more irregular shapes and nuclei exhibiting a shrunken morphology. These observations are consistent with our previously reported results.

3. Materials and Methods

3.1. Materials

Hyaluronic acid (HA) (purity ≥ 95%) was received from Bloomage Freda Blopharm Co., Ltd. (Jinan, China), exhibiting a molecular weight of 10 kDa, with a polydispersity index (Mw/Mn) of 1.23. 1-Ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC·HCl) (purity ≥ 99%) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) (purity ≥ 98%) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Doxorubicin hydrochloride (DOX·HCl) (purity ≥ 99%) was received from Cool Chemistry Co., Ltd. (Ningbo, China). Morpholine (purity ≥ 99%) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Fmoc-AAN (purity ≥ 95%) was purchased from Shanghai Top-Peptide Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Other organic solvents or reagents were analytical grade and used as received.

Human cervical carcinoma cells (HeLa) were obtained from Shanghai Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Human breast cancer cells (MCF-7) and human hepatocytes (LO2) were obtained from the Yin Xiao Xing Research Group of Xuzhou Medical University (Xuzhou, China). Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from Gibco Co., Ltd. (Grand Island, NE, USA). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was purchased from Dalian Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China) and used for the cytotoxicity test.

3.2. Synthesis of HA-AAN-DOX and Preparation of HAD Micelles

In total, 21.6 mg of Fmoc-AAN (0.04 mmol) was dissolved in 2 mL of DMF; subsequently, 24.0 mg of NHS (0.21 mmol) and 41.6 mg of EDC·HC l (0.22 mmol) were added. In this system, the pH was adjusted to 5.0–5.5. This range is consistent with the optimal pH window for EDC/NHS-mediated amidation, maximizing carboxyl activation efficiency while avoiding excessive protonation of the amine groups of DOX, which could reduce coupling efficiency. The mixture was stirred for 1 h at room temperature, and 0.07 mmol of DOX·HCl (2 mL) was added. The pH of the resulting solution was adjusted to 8.0. The mixture was stirred for 24 h at room temperature and lyophilized, and Fmoc-AAN-DOX was obtained. During the synthesis of AAN-DOX, the by-products generated from the Fmoc deprotection reaction were removed via methyl tert-butyl ether precipitation. The resulting precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 10 min and then dialyzed against deionized water (MWCO: 1000 Da) for 24 h with water replacement every 6 h. The pure product was finally obtained by freeze-drying the dialyzed sample.

Fmoc-AAN-DOX was dissolved in 4 mL of DMF and morpholine and then stirred at room temperature for 2 h, and AAN-DOX was obtained. AAN-DOX was added dropwise to methyl tertiary butyl ether. The precipitation was obtained after centrifugation, and it was washed and purified with deionized water.

In total, 42.0 mg of HA (0.10 mmol), 38.0 mg of NHS (0.20 mmol) and 23.0 mg of EDC·HCl (0.20 mmol) were dissolved in 3 mL of deionized water, and the pH of the resulting solution was adjusted to 5.0–6.0. The mixture was stirred for 1 h at room temperature and then dissolved in 4 mL of DMF for the amidation of HA (HA-NH2).

The synthesized product was stirred at room temperature for 24 h, followed by purification via dialysis (MWCO 1000 Da) against deionized water to remove unreacted small molecules and then freeze-dried prior to further characterization and experiments. We quantified the free DOX content in the final dialysate using fluorescence photometry (Figures S11 and S12; Tables S1 and S2).

HAD1 (molar ratio HA:AAN-DOX = 4:1), HAD2 (molar ratio HA:AAN-DOX = 4:1), and HAD3 (molar ratio HA:AAN-DOX = 4:1) solutions were prepared according to the above methods.

Note: The mass of 0.04 mmol Fmoc-AAN is 21.6 mg; the mass of 0.21 mmol NHS is 24.0 mg; the mass of 0.22 mmol EDC·HCl is 41.6 mg; the mass of 0.07 mmol DOX·HCl is 38.0 mg, dissolved in 2 mL of deionized water; the mass of 0.10 mmol HA is 42 mg. The pH of the DMF solution was adjusted through the dropwise addition of concentrated hydrochloric acid and measured using a Mettler-Toledo FE28 precision pH meter (Zurich, Switzerland). As DMF is miscible with water, the addition of a small amount of water does not affect the reaction system.

3.3. Characterization of HAD

The structure of HAD was characterized via 1H NMR and FTIR spectroscopy. HAD was dissolved in trifluoroacetic acid-d. The 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectra of the HAD were analyzed on a nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometer (JEOL, Akishima, Japan); BRUKER Ascend Evo 400, Fällanden, Switzerland. 1H NMR spectra were recorded at 400 MHz. HA was measured in D2O, while Fmoc-AAN, DOX, and HAD were measured in DMSO-d6. Chemical shifts (δ) are reported in ppm, the relaxation delay time is 2 s, and the chemical shift is referenced to tetramethylsilane (TMS) as an internal standard.

Furthermore, an infrared spectrometer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) was used to take FTIR measurements with a KBr disk. The sample was mixed with KBr at a 1:100 mass ratio and pressed into a pellet. The spectral acquisition was performed over a range of 4000–400 cm−1 with 32 scans.

The particle size and zeta potential were assessed via dynamic light scattering (DLS) on a zeta potential/particle sizer instrument (Brookhaven DLS/Zeta Potential Analyzer, Nashua, NH, USA).

The morphology of the HAD micelles under standard conditions was examined using a transmission electron microscope (Tecnai Spirit G2 TWIN, FEI, Hillsboro, OR, USA). The accelerating voltage was 120 kV. The sample preparation involved dispensing 5 μL of HAD micelle solution (0.5 mg/mL concentration) onto a copper grid, allowing it to air-dry, and then proceeding with observation.

To determine the concentration of micelles, DOX and HAD were diluted with PBS (pH 7.4), and the fluorescence intensity was measured on a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-4600, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) at ex/em wavelengths of 498 nm and 600 nm, respectively.

3.4. Evaluation of In Vitro DOX Release

DOX release from HAD micelles in vitro was performed in a constant temperature shaking incubator at 37 °C (a rotation speed of 100 rpm). HAD micelles were suspended in PBS (pH 7.4), PBS (pH 7.4) with HA (300 U/mL), PBS (pH 7.4) with HA (300 U/mL) and legumain (5 μg/mL), and PBS (pH 6.5) with HA (300 U/mL) and legumain (5 μg/mL). In total, 2 mL of DOX-loaded micelles was placed in a dialysis bag with a molecular weight of 1000 Da, and the dialysis bag was then soaked in 100 mL of appropriate medium (pH 7.4 PBS, pH 7.4 PBS + HAase, pH 6.5 PBS + Haase + legumain, pH 6.5 PBS + Haase + legumain). At a predetermined time point, 5 mL of release medium was absorbed, and an equal amount of fresh medium was added at the same time. The released DOX was detected with a fluorescence detector with an excitation wavelength of 498 nm and an emission wavelength of 600 nm. Each release experiment was performed three times in parallel.

3.5. Cellular Uptake Mechanism Experiments

The HeLa cells were seeded into a 24-well plate at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. The medium was discarded, and then the cell uptake inhibitors prepared with serum-free DMEM were added: chlorpromazine (10 µg/mL), colchicine (50 µg/mL), nystatin (6 µg/mL), and chloroquine (10 µg/mL). At the same time, a control group without an inhibitor was retained, and another group was retained in the refrigerator at 4 °C for energy inhibition. Each group had three parallel wells. After 2 h of incubation, the inhibitor was removed, and HAD was added. After 4 h of incubation, the supernatant was aspirated, the cells were lysed, and the fluorescence intensity of HAD was detected with a multifunctional microplate reader. The excitation wavelength was 498 nm, and the emission wavelength was 600 nm.

3.6. In Vitro Anticancer Activity Assays

All cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2.

The cytotoxicity of polymeric micelles against HeLa cells was tested via CCK-8 assays. HeLa cells were added to a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 105 per well with 100 μL of medium. After a 24 h incubation period, the culture medium was carefully aspirated and replaced with 100 μL of fresh medium containing varying concentrations of DOX, HAD, and HAD + HAase. The incubation was continued for 24 h. Then, we removed the culture medium and rinsed the wells with saline. We added 10 μL of CCK-8 solution to DMEM and incubated it in the dark for 1 h, determining the absorbance value.

3.7. Cell SAFETY Evaluation of HAD

L929 cells were added to a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 105 per well and incubated for 24 h. Then, the culture medium was aspirated and substituted with 100 μL of medium comprising varying concentrations of DOX and HAD. The cells were incubated for 24 h. Then, the culture medium was removed, and the wells were rinsed with saline. In total, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated in the dark for an additional 1 h; the absorbance value was determined.

3.8. Animals and Tumor Models

H22 cells were subcutaneously injected into the right posterior limbs of healthy Kunming mice to establish a tumor model. Female Kunming mice, aged six weeks and weighing 30 ± 5 g, were procured from the Experimental Animal Center of Xuzhou Medical University (Xuzhou, China). Prior to the commencement of the experiment, the mice were acclimatized to the new environment by being fed standard laboratory chow for a minimum of five days under controlled conditions, with a temperature range of 20–22 °C and a relative humidity of 65%. All animal handling procedures adhered to the guidelines set forth by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Xuzhou Medical University.

The administration volume was 100 μL per animal. The dosing concentrations were 5 mg/kg for the DOX group and 15 mg/kg for the HAD group. The formula for calculating the tumor inhibition rate is as follows: tumor inhibition rate (%) = (1 − average tumor weight in treatment group/average tumor weight in saline group) × 100%. This has been supplemented in the revised manuscript. The tumor length and width were measured, and the tumor volume was calculated via the following equation: V = L × W2/2 [11,12].

3.9. Assessment of In Vivo Effectiveness Against Tumors

When the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6) and received an intravenous injection of saline, HA, DOX or HAD at a dosing volume of 100 μL per mouse. The administration was performed every three days with a total of three doses, where the concentration of the DOX group was 5 mg/kg (calculated as DOX) and that of the HAD group was 15 mg/kg. Tumor volumes were measured with a vernier caliper and recorded every other day. After a 10-day treatment, the tumors were excised, weighed and photographed. Tumor inhibition rate = (1 − mean tumor weight of treatment group/mean tumor weight of saline group) × 100%. For histological analysis via H&E staining, the excised tumor tissues were embedded in paraffin blocks and observed under an optical microscope.

3.10. Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted utilizing one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as delineated for each experimental setup. The data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) in the main text and figures, thereby illustrating the precision of the estimated mean and facilitating inter-group comparisons. The sample size (n) is specified in the relevant figure legends or table footnotes. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

4. Conclusions

We have developed a legumain-responsive hyaluronic acid–doxorubicin conjugate (HAD2), synthesized via stable covalent linkage of DOX to HA through an AAN spacer, with a grafting efficiency of ~30% and structural integrity confirmed by 1H NMR and FTIR. The conjugate self-assembles into stable micelles (~120 nm) with high drug loading and exhibits minimal premature leakage at physiological pH but rapid DOX release under acidic and legumain-rich conditions—mimicking the tumor microenvironment. In vitro, HAD2 demonstrates significantly reduced cytotoxicity toward normal L929 and LO2 cells compared to free DOX while maintaining potent antiproliferative activity against tumor cells, suggesting improved biosafety without compromising efficacy. In vivo studies further confirm superior tumor growth inhibition and lower systemic toxicity, as evidenced by reduced tumor weight (p < 0.01), higher tumor inhibition rate (>65%), and stable body weight trajectories. Collectively, these results validate HAD2 as a rationally designed, enzyme-activated prodrug system that leverages both CD44-mediated targeting and tumor-specific activation to enhance therapeutic precision.
