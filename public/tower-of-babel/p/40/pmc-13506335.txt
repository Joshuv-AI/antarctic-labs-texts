The effect of cohesin mutations on HLA-class II expression in the myeloid leukemia of Down syndrome
Authors: Austin C Boucher, Wojciech Rosikiewicz, Anitria Cotton, Amber L Broadhurst, Te Ling, Rishika Vadlamudi, Melanie Bremm, Anna-Lena Schmell, Lahiri Konada, Madeline Niederkorn, Josiah D Murray, Yichao Li, Shilpa Narina, Sarah Inglott, Anindita Roy, Philip Ancliff, Ricky Tirtakusuma, Jing Ma, Michael P Walsh, Jeffery M Klco, Paul G Thomas, Yiwen Wang, Aristotelis Tsirigos, Yong Cheng, Beisi Xu, Xiaotu Ma, Soheil Meshinchi, Elvin Wagenblast, Shondra M Pruett-Miller, Sridhar Rao, Gang Wu, Yunusa Olufadi, Guolian Kang, Zuzana Tothova, Jeffrey W Taub, Yubin Ge, Paresh Vyas, Jan-Henning Klusmann, John D Crispino

ABSTRACT
AbstractChildren with Down syndrome (DS) frequently develop transient abnormal myelopoiesis that can evolve to the myeloid leukemia of DS (ML-DS). TAM spontaneously resolves in most cases but progresses to ML-DS with additional mutations, most commonly in the cohesin complex. However, the mechanisms by which these alterations promote leukemia are unknown. We leveraged the RAD21-mutant CMY cell line and RAD21-corrected CMY isogenic clones, as well as patient data, to investigate the effect of cohesin mutations during leukemia progression. RNA-sequencing revealed that HLA-class II genes were significantly down-regulated with cohesin mutations. Furthermore, HLA-DR was found to be lower in ML-DS relative to TAM, and these decreased levels were associated with increased risk of leukemia progression. Multi-omic analyses revealed that haploinsufficiency of RAD21 altered chromatin accessibility and impaired the occupancy of GATA1s and CIITA, the master regulator of HLA-class II gene expression. Chromatin binding of CIITA was increased with RAD21 correction, providing a mechanism by which restoration of cohesin improves HLA-class II expression. Finally, decreased levels of RAD21 or STAG2 expression in other subtypes of AML also exhibited reduced expression of HLA-class II genes. Thus, cohesin may contribute to leukemia by altering HLA-class II gene expression. Subject terms: Haematological cancer, Cancer epigenetics

Introduction

Children with Down syndrome (DS) are at a 20-fold increased risk of developing acute leukemia and uniquely susceptible to transient abnormal myelopoiesis (TAM). In DS, the risk of developing acute myeloid leukemia (AML), referred to as the myeloid leukemia of Down syndrome (ML-DS), is 150-fold greater than in children without DS [1]. The genetic progression from TAM to ML-DS is well characterized and serves as a robust model to study leukemic transformation. As many as 25% of newborns with DS have silent or clinical TAM [2], a pre-leukemic blast phase condition present at birth. TAM is driven by the combination of trisomy 21 and a mutation in the hematopoietic transcriptional factor encoding gene GATA1. These mutations, which result in loss of full-length GATA1 but expression of a truncated isoform named GATA1s [1, 2], have been found in nearly 25% of newborns with DS [3] and as early as 21 weeks of gestation, providing clear evidence of fetal origin [4]. Surprisingly, TAM spontaneously resolves in most cases within the first few months of life. How TAM spontaneously resolves is unknown, although it is suspected to be due to differences between the pre- and postnatal environment [5–7]. However, nearly 20% of children who had TAM progress to ML-DS after 1-3 years due to the acquisition of additional mutations in minimal residual TAM blasts [1, 3]. Sequencing efforts over the last decade have confirmed the presence of GATA1 mutations in nearly all cases of TAM and ML-DS and have additionally shed light on the nature of the compounding mutations that occur during TAM to ML-DS progression [8, 9]. Of note, mutations in subunits of the cohesin complex are enriched in ML-DS compared to other myeloid malignancies (50% in ML-DS vs. <20% in non-DS AML) [10, 11]. Cohesin is a ring-shaped protein complex consisting of SMC1a, SMC3, RAD21, and either STAG1 or STAG2. While mutations in both solid and hematopoietic tumors are mainly found within these subunits of the cohesin ring and are biased toward RAD21 and STAG2, mutations can also be found in genes that encode cohesin-associated factors, including PDS5B, WAPL, and NIPBL [8–11]. In addition to its canonical functions of maintaining sister chromatid cohesion, cohesin mediates chromatin interactions leading to the formation of transcriptional centers, reinforced enhancer-promoter looping, and regulation of the DNA damage response at sites of DNA replication [12]. However, the precise mechanisms by which cohesin mutations contribute to leukemogenesis remain obscure [11], and it is unclear why cohesin mutations are enriched in ML-DS.

In this study, we leveraged the RAD21 mutant ML-DS patient-derived CMY cell line and isogenic clones in which the RAD21 mutation was corrected to wild-type via CRISPR-Cas9 editing. Multi-omic analysis of the cohesin-corrected cell lines revealed a striking increase in HLA-class II gene expression compared to the cohesin mutant cells. These results were orthogonally validated in RNA sequencing data from a human fetal liver-derived model of ML-DS and patient samples. Moreover, retrospective analysis of diagnostic flow cytometry suggests that TAM patients with lower expression of HLA-DR are more likely to progress to ML-DS. Together, our results suggest a reduction in the level of HLA-class II is a feature of ML-DS transformation and that cohesin mutations may enforce this phenotype. Furthermore, our observations raise the intriguing model that TAM is resolved by an immune response while ML-DS evolves to evade the immune system, offering an explanation as to why cohesin mutations are highly selected for in ML-DS. Additionally, we extended these findings to other AMLs, suggesting that cohesin’s regulation of HLA-class II gene expression is a common mechanism leveraged in AML.

Materials and methods

Genome-edited cell lines and pools

Genome-edited clonal cell lines were generated using CRISPR technology in the Center for Advanced Genome Engineering (St. Jude). CMY and CMK edited cells were created by transiently co-transfecting 400,000 cells with precomplexed ribonuclear proteins (RNPs) consisting of 100 pmol of chemically modified sgRNA (IDT), 33 pmol of 3X NLS SpCas9 protein (St. Jude Protein Production Core), 200 ng of pMaxGFP (Lonza), and, when required, 100 pmol of ssODN donor. Kasumi-1 edited cells were made by transiently co-transfecting 1.25e6 cells with precomplexed ribonuclear proteins (RNPs) consisting of 300 pmol of chemically modified sgRNA (IDT), 100 pmol of 3X NLS SpCas9 protein (St. Jude Protein Production Core), 300 ng of pMaxGFP (Lonza), and 300 pmol of ssODN donor. Transfections were performed using the 4D-Nucleofector™ X Unit (Lonza) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. CMY and CMK cells were nucleofected with solution P3 in 20 µL cuvettes using programs CA-137 and EO-100, respectively. Kasumi-1 cells were nucleofected with solution P3 in 100 µL cuvettes using program CM-138. The CIITA knockout cell pool was analyzed for editing efficiency and class II expression five days post-nucleofection. To generate the clonal lines, single GFP-positive cells were flow-sorted (St. Jude Flow Cytometry and Cell Sorting Shared Resource) five days post-nucleofection into 96-well plates containing prewarmed media, where they were clonally expanded. Clones were screened and verified for the desired modification using targeted amplicon sequencing analyzed with CRIS.py as previously described [13, 14]. Final clones tested negative for mycoplasma by the MycoAlertTMPlus Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza) and were authenticated using the PowerPlex® Fusion System (Promega) performed at the Hartwell Center (St. Jude). Editing construct sequences and screening primers can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

Flow cytometry

Cell lines: Cells were collected and washed 2x with PBS + 0.5% BSA and then incubated with antibody cocktails on ice, protected from light for 30 min. Samples were then washed 2x in PBS + 0.5% BSA and resuspended in 300ul of PBS + 0.5% BSA, then taken for analysis on an LSRFortessa (BD Biosciences). Flow antibodies: HLA-DR, DP, DQ-AF647 (Biolegend, Cat#361703), AF647 isotype (Biolegend, Cat#400234).

Diagnostic flow cytometry C3OMBAT-AML cohort: Immunophenotyping of patient samples was performed on a DxFLEX 13-color/three laser flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Krefeld, Germany). For measurement, 300 µl PB/ BM were washed twice in 2 ml Stain Buffer (BD Pharmingen) (540 g, 5 min). The cells were resuspended in 300 µl Brilliant Stain Buffer (BD Horizon; Cat#566349) and stained with the following antibodies: CD34-PC 5.5 (8G12) (BD Bioscience; Cat#347222), CD33-APC-R700 (P67-6) (BD Biosciences; Cat#659122), CD45-KO (HI30) (BD Biosciences; Cat#560777) and CD117-PC 7 (B49221) (Beckman Coulter; Cat#B49221), CD11a-BV605 (G43-25B) (BD Biosciences; Cat#743332), CD36-FITC (FA6.152) (Beckman Coulter; Cat#B49201), HLA-DR-PB (I.243) (Beckman Coulter; Cat#307624), CD7-PE (8H8.1) (Beckman Coulter; Cat#IM1429U), CD42b-APC (HIP1) (BD Biosciences; Cat#551061), CD71-APC-H7 (M-A712) (BD Biosciences; Cat#655408). This was followed by erythrocyte lysis with FACS™ Lysing Solution (BD Biosciences; Cat#349202). The cells were washed twice and resuspended in 300 µl Stain Buffer before measurement. Blasts were defined as CD117 + , CD33 + , CD7 + , and CD11a-.

Statistical analysis

A two-sample t-test or the exact Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare two groups, depending on the normality of the data as assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. The log-rank test was used to compare survival distributions between groups. All p-values were two-sided. Error bars represent standard deviation. R-4.3.3. was used to conduct the statistical analysis.

Results

Haploinsufficiency of RAD21 contributes to the growth of ML-DS cells

CMY cells, which were derived from a patient with ML-DS, have a GATA1 mutation, trisomy 21, and a heterozygous RAD21 Y3* early termination codon caused by the c.9 C > G point mutation [15]. To investigate how cohesin contributes to ML-DS transformation, we corrected the RAD21 mutation via CRISPR-Cas9 and generated two isogenic clones dubbed 2B10 and 3D11 (Fig. 1A, B). Western blot analysis for RAD21 revealed a modest 1.3x increase in RAD21 protein levels in both corrected clones (Fig. 1C, D). As expected, the CMY cell line and both clones exclusively express GATA1s (Fig. 1E). Analysis of cell counts over time revealed a growth defect in both corrected clones, which was further confirmed by competitive growth assays (Fig. 1F–H). After injection of the parental and RAD21-corrected CMY cells into NSG mice, we observed that mice bearing corrected cells had prolonged survival compared to the parental CMY line (Fig. 1I). Together, these results indicate that cohesin mutations contribute to a growth advantage of ML-DS cells.

Multi-omic assays reveal a relationship between cohesin and GATA1s chromatin occupancy

Given the role of cohesin in chromatin architecture, we hypothesized that the RAD21 mutation in ML-DS would alter chromatin accessibility, epigenetic marks, and chromatin occupancy of associated factors. To reduce the effects of variation between the RAD21-corrected clones and highlight the most significant changes caused by mutation correction, we combined sequencing data from 2B10 and 3D11 into a single group referred to as “RAD21-corrected clones” which we compared to the parental CMY cells. Mutations in cohesin subunits in hematopoietic cells have been reported to alter accessibility of GATA, RUNT, ETS and CTCF binding sites [16–20]. ATAC-seq revealed that there were 702 regions with increased and 864 regions with decreased chromatin accessibility in the RAD21-corrected clones (log2FC > 1 and FDR < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). Consistent with previous reports, motif enrichment analysis indicated that the RAD21 mutation in the CMY cells enriched for an increase in GATA and RUNT motifs, while the RAD21-corrected cells showed enrichment for CTCF motifs (Fig. 2A). We next performed CUT&RUN for CTCF, RAD21, GATA1s, H3K4me3, H3K4me1, and H3K27Ac to ascertain epigenetic alterations induced by the RAD21 correction. CUT&RUN revealed strikingly increased differential binding of CTCF, RAD21, and GATA1s in RAD21-corrected cells and strikingly decreased differential binding of H3K27Ac and H3K4me1 (log2FC > 1, FDR < 0.05) (Fig. S1A, B). Furthermore, we observed a less pronounced change in H3K4me3 (Fig. S1A). We next focused on how these CUT&RUN targets behaved at sites of differential RAD21 occupancy. We identified 1,950 sites (log2FC > 1, FDR < 0.05) with increased RAD21 occupancy in the RAD21-corrected clones (Figs. 2B and S1A), and curiously, the most impacted occupancy other than RAD21 was GATA1s (Fig. 2B). To orthogonally validate this, we performed kernel density analysis of GATA1s occupied sites and observed increased GATA1s occupancy at sites shared with increased RAD21 compared to all other GATA1s bound sites (Fig. 2C). Together, these results indicate that RAD21 coordinates the binding of GATA1s to chromatin in ML-DS.

A reduction in HLA-class II gene expression is associated with cohesin mutations

We next performed RNA-seq on RAD21-corrected clones and CMY cells, which identified 310 down-regulated and 101 up-regulated genes (log2FC > 1 and p-val<0.05) (Fig. S2A). We then compared our RNA-seq results with a human ML-DS model generated by stepwise introduction of GATA1 and STAG2 mutations in primary human fetal liver cells with trisomy 21 [19]. GSEA revealed that there were 105 upregulated gene sets in common between the cohesin WT backgrounds, while only 2 gene sets were in common between the cohesin mutant backgrounds (Table S2). We focused on gene sets enriched in the WT background as these were most likely to be perturbed by cohesin mutations during leukemic progression. Leading-edge gene analysis was used to cluster the 105 gene sets and revealed two distinct clusters (Fig. 3A). Surprisingly, we did not observe any gene sets expected to be associated with differential growth, such as cell cycle or cell death. Rather intriguingly, we found 22 gene sets related to antigen processing and presentation and several immunity signatures (Fig. 3A and Table S2). Investigation of the leading-edge gene frequency that clustered these GSEA terms revealed that HLA-class II genes were prominent within every cluster (Fig. S2B–D).

A comparison of differentially expressed genes within our RNA-seq data and that of Wagenblast et al. revealed that both RAD21 and STAG2 cohesin mutations decreased HLA-class II gene expression (Fig. 3B). With respect to HLA-class II cell surface expression, Wagenblast et al. detected a striking decrease in HLA-DR in trisomy 21 fetal liver cells with a GATA1 mutation and a near ablation of HLA-DR on cells with GATA1 and STAG2 mutations (a reduction from 63% to less than 2%) [19]. To confirm that cohesin mutations alter HLA-class II levels on the cell surface, we performed flow cytometry for HLA-DR, DP, DQ and found that the RAD21-corrected clones had significantly higher levels of surface HLA-class II compared to the RAD21 mutant CMY cells (Fig. 3C, D). We next studied a second ML-DS cell line (CMK), which is wild type for cohesin genes and generated isogenic mutant clones via CRISPR-Cas-9 for the patient-reported STAG2 R305* mutation [8] and found that the addition of a STAG2 mutation ablated surface HLA-class II, in line with the observations from Wagenblast et al. (Fig. S2E–G). We next asked if there was a relationship between cohesin expression and HLA-class II expression in primary ML-DS samples. We stratified RNA-seq from 215 ML-DS cases [21] by their RAD21 or STAG2 expression into 4 quartiles and queried how HLA-class II gene expression responded to decreased cohesin subunit expression. HLA-class II gene expression significantly decreased as RAD21 or STAG2 expression decreased (Fig. 3E). Together, these results suggest that cohesin regulates HLA-class II expression in ML-DS.

RAD21 mutation reduces CIITA occupancy within the HLA-class II locus

To study how cohesin regulates HLA-class II gene expression in the context of ML-DS, we next performed Hi-C on parental CMY cells and the RAD21-corrected clones. Topological associating domains (TADs) and insulation of the HLA locus were unchanged (Fig. S3A, B). Given the absence of an effect on chromatin organization, we asked whether occupancy of the master regulator of HLA gene expression, CIITA (class II major histocompatibility complex trans-activator), was altered. Of note, CIITA has been reported to interact with RAD21 [22]. We first confirmed that deletion of CIITA decreased HLA-class II expression in ML-DS by CRISPR-Cas9 editing (Fig. 4A, B). Consistent with a model in which cohesin recruits CIITA to chromatin, we found by ChIP-seq that CIITA occupancy was increased across the HLA-class II locus in the RAD21-corrected clones (Fig. 4C). This increase was not due to a change in CIITA RNA or protein levels (Fig. S3C–E). We next investigated the association between CIITA and cohesin chromatin occupancy within the DR/DQ and XL-9 class II super-enhancers, given that cohesin has previously been shown to interact with CIITA to regulate a subset of HLA-class II gene expression from these enhancers in antigen-presenting cells (APCs) [23]. Interestingly, we found that CIITA occupancy was increased at both enhancers along with RAD21 and GATA1s in the corrected cells (Fig. 4D). Taken together, our findings suggest that RAD21 mutations suppress HLA-class II gene expression through decreased recruitment of CIITA to the HLA-class II locus.

Low HLA-DR expression is an indicator of TAM progression to ML-DS

We then evaluated if changes in HLA-class II expression are associated with progression from TAM to ML-DS by retrospective analysis of diagnostic flow cytometry panels, which include HLA-DR. Leveraging the C3OMBAT-AML cohort, we observed a trend towards lower HLA-DR in ML-DS compared to TAM (P = 0.06) (Fig. 5A). We also compared HLA-DR in paired TAM and ML-DS samples in this cohort and observed that 8 out of 10 samples had decreased HLA-DR at the ML-DS stage (Fig. 5B). Next, we asked if lower HLA-DR levels are prognostic for progression of TAM to ML-DS. Of note, those patients with low levels of HLA-DR at the time of TAM diagnostic flow displayed significantly enhanced progression to ML-DS than those with high HLA-DR levels (Fig. 5C, D). We sought out a confirmatory cohort for this observation, and although the number of cases that progressed to ML-DS in the second cohort was low, the progression group had relatively lower levels (Fig. S4). Together, these data from human cell lines, human primary cells, patient samples and diagnostic patient data support the model that cohesin regulates HLA-class II gene expression in ML-DS progression and suggest that a lower level of HLA-DR is a feature of disease progression.

enome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screen uncovers regulators of HLA-class II expression

We next performed a genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screen to identify factors that reverse the reduction of HLA-class II expression in cohesin mutant cells. After introducing Cas9 and the Brunello sgRNA library, we sorted the top and bottom 15% of HLA-class II expressing CMY and 3D11 cells to identify sgRNA read counts by deep sequencing (Fig. 6A and Table S3). Candidates for further characterization were defined by cutoffs of p-val<0.05, log fold change minimum +/– 0.5, and guide representation of 3 or more. Positive regulators whose depletion led to decreased HLA-class II expression included 27 candidates unique to the CMY cells, 15 unique to the 3D11 cells, and 7 shared candidates (Fig. S5A). Negative regulators whose depletion led to increased HLA-class II expression included 26 candidates unique to CMY cells, 22 unique to the 3D11 cells, and 6 shared candidates (Fig. S5A). Canonical positive regulators CIITA as well as RFX genes were amongst the commonly enriched guides that caused low HLA-class II expression, while guide enrichment of the canonical negative regulator FBXO11 was a common hit that led to increased HLA-class II expression [24] (Fig. S5B, C). We next investigated novel candidate regulators identified by this screen. Intriguingly, MEAF6, which encodes a common structural component of the epigenetic MYST histone acetyltransferase (HAT) complexes, stood out as a strong candidate (Fig. S5D). We assayed whether knockout of MEAF6, and the associated factors KAT7, KAT6a, and KAT6b, would increase HLA-class II expression. Knockout pools were generated using two guides per gene in the CMY and corrected clones. Editing efficiencies within the pools varied between 15–40%, and increased HLA-class II expression was detectable for at least one tested guide per gene (Fig. S5E, F). Interestingly, treatment of the cell lines with the KAT7 inhibitor WM-3835 and the KAT6a/b and KAT7 inhibitor PF-9363 led to strikingly higher levels of HLA-class II with time in all three cell lines (Fig. 6B, C). Consistent with these results, increased HLA-class II expression was observed following treatment of two independent ML-DS PDXs with these drugs (Fig. 6D, E). Together, these results indicate that HLA-class II expression in ML-DS can be increased by modifying HAT enzyme activity.

Cohesin expression correlates with HLA-class II gene expression in other subtypes of AML

Mutations in cohesin subunits are found in 10-20% of patients with AML who do not have DS. Our study raises the question of whether the effect of cohesin mutations on HLA-class II genes extends to this larger group of AML. We therefore reanalyzed RNA-seq data from 750 pediatric AML cases included in the panAML dataset [25]. In line with our ML-DS results, we found that decreased RAD21 or STAG2 expression was associated with decreased HLA-class II expression (Fig. 7A). Within this cohort, we further analyzed the patient subset harboring the t(8;21) AML1-ETO (RUNX1::RUNX1T1) translocation. Cohesin mutations are associated with up to 25% of t(8;21) positive AML cases [10], which is one of the highest co-mutation rates outside of ML-DS. We found that t(8;21) AML also exhibited decreased HLA-class II expression in association with lower RAD21 or STAG2 expression (Fig. 7B). To validate these clinical data in a testable model, we leveraged the t(8;21) positive Kasumi-1 cell line, which harbors a heterozygous 4 bp insertion in RAD21. We corrected the 4 bp insertion, generating two isogenic RAD21 corrected Kasumi-1 lines dubbed 4F4 and 1E5 (Fig. 7C). At baseline, Kasumi-1 cells expressed low levels of HLA-class II on their surface. Strikingly, when we assayed Kasumi-1 clones in which the RAD21 mutation was corrected, we observed a robust and significant increase in the levels of HLA-DR, DP, DQ (Fig. 7D, E). Together, these results suggest that downregulation of cohesin expression and cohesin mutation are drivers of HLA-class II loss in non-DS AML.

Discussion

While the genetic events contributing to progression from TAM to ML-DS are well defined, their molecular effects are less well understood. Here, we reveal that a consequence of mutations in the cohesin complex in ML-DS is reduced occupancy of CIITA at the HLA-class II locus, resulting in decreased HLA-class II gene expression. Furthermore, we detected similar effects on HLA-class II gene expression in non-DS AML cases that have low levels of expression of cohesin genes, suggesting this is a universal feature of AML with dysregulation of cohesin function.

Although gene expression changes are modest upon cohesin depletion, transcription can position cohesin on the genome [26], and transcription factors have decreased chromatin binding in the absence of cohesin [27]. Additionally, cohesin depletion alters transcriptional bursting probabilities [28] and loss of RNA polymerase II (Pol II) and Mediator disrupts enhancer-promoter looping [29, 30]. Together, these results indicate that rather than perturbing chromatin architecture, heterozygous cohesin mutations found in disease likely function to disrupt homeostatic transcription. In line with these observations, we found increased chromatin binding of the transcription factors GATA1s and CIITA in the RAD21-corrected clones compared to the mutant CMY cells. Furthermore, occupancy of the master regulator of HLA-class II expression, CIITA, was increased with RAD21 correction, likely due to the interaction of the two proteins, an observation that was initially reported by Majumder and Boss [22] in the Raji B-cell line. Accordingly, we observed increased HLA-class II RNA and protein in RAD21-corrected clones. Intriguingly, we also observed that H3K4me1 and H3K27Ac differential occupancy was strikingly decreased in the RAD21-corrected clones, suggesting that cohesin may safeguard against the erroneous formation and subsequent use of aberrant enhancers in ML-DS.

KAT6a/b and KAT7 primarily drive H3K23Ac and H3K14Ac, respectively, which tend to be associated with active transcription but have been reported to have context-dependent suppressive functions [31]. How inhibition of HAT complexes rescued HLA-class II expression remains unclear. Recent reports highlight inhibition of KAT6a/b and KAT7 HAT complexes with PF-9363 in AML, overcoming resistance to Menin inhibitors, as well as being a molecular dependency for NUP98-rearranged leukemia [32, 33]. Intriguingly, RNA seq from the Menin study revealed increased CIITA expression and HLA-class II expression in two non-DS AML cell lines 48 h post treatment, suggesting that HAT inhibition may be able to upregulate CIITA and may offer an explanation for our observation. Alternatively, GATA2 and LIN28B were also shown to be downregulated by HAT inhibition [32], both of which were candidates to upregulate HLA-class II when deleted in our CRISPR screen, suggesting other pathways could be leveraged to achieve this phenotype. Future work will clarify these insights.

Canonically, HLA-class II expression is primarily restricted to professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs), such as dendritic cells and macrophages. However, it is well known that tumor cells express HLA-class II proteins, and that this is frequently decreased in many settings. Moreover, recent studies have shown that hematopoietic stem cells express and functionally utilize HLA-class II [34, 35]. Additionally, downregulation of antigen processing and presentation signatures and decreased HLA-class II expression is found in up to 50% of relapsed AML cases after bone marrow transplant [36, 37]. Importantly, while loss of HLA-class II expression is well documented in relapsed AML, reports of a role for HLA-class II loss in primary leukemogenesis are lacking. In our study, the RAD21-corrected cell lines express higher levels of HLA-class II genes, and this observation was independently confirmed in a primary human fetal liver-derived model of ML-DS, where cohesin mutation nearly ablated HLA-class II expression [19]. Notably, we show that lower expression of HLA-DR at TAM diagnosis is predictive of progression to ML-DS. Although these data are provocative, these results need validation in independent cohorts. We speculate that the spontaneous resolution of TAM may be immune-mediated and that progression to ML-DS may depend, at least in part, on immune evasion. A major hurdle to studying the altered immune system in cohesin mutant ML-DS is the lack of mouse models; DS mouse models with a GATA1s mutation do not develop TAM, and cohesin mutations do not lead to transformation of murine GATA1s fetal liver cells, likely due to genetic dissimilarity between mice and humans [1, 8]. Therefore, proof of the model of immune evasion will require in-depth studies with matched leukemia and T-cell populations of patient samples.

Finally, we note that cohesin mutations also have a cell-autonomous effect on ML-DS blasts as seen by the impaired growth of the corrected CMY clones in vitro and in vivo. Our studies have not focused on this important aspect of ML-DS, but future research in this area is warranted.
