Understanding Chinese college students’ emotions and attributions during the COVID-19 epidemic: an analysis based on Sina microblog
Authors: Yue Zhang, XinXing Duan, Bo Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe outbreak of COVID-19 resulted in various restrictive measures imposed by the Chinese government and colleges, including home quarantine and online teaching. In this context, the Internet has emerged as a crucial medium for college students to express their emotions. Measuring and evaluating emotions from online texts can provide a precise representation of the emotions of college students.MethodsThis study selected 18,300 texts posted by college students on Sina microblog in 2020 and used a text-mining method to evaluate their emotions during the COVID-19 epidemic. First, an emotion attribution system was constructed using a content analysis approach combined with emotion attribution theory. Then, a four-level Bayesian classifier was developed to evaluate and analyze the online emotions of college students along two dimensions: emotion validity and emotion attribution.ResultsThe results indicated that the overall validity of college students’ emotions was negative, with low levels of arousal. Emotion varies greatly across several attribution dimensions: external attributions were associated with much more positive emotions than internal attributions, and unstable attributions were associated with higher emotional arousal.ConclusionsIt can be concluded that emotion attributions in the epidemic are dominated by external stable attributions such as system attributions and university attributions, with prominent characteristics of responsibility attributions. This means that college students tend to investigate the causes of the negative outcomes of epidemic events and who is accountable for them. The results of the study contribute to a deeper understanding of the psychological reaction mechanisms of college students during sudden public crisis events and can aid colleges and universities in improving mental health education and psychological intervention.Keywords: COVID-19, China, College students, Emotion valuation, Emotion attribution

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) declared the COVID-19 pandemic as a public health emergency of international concern (PHEIC) at the start of the year 2020 due to its highly contagious nature, its ability to mutate easily, and the difficulties associated with its management [28]. The COVID-19 pandemic introduced extensive alterations across multiple sectors, particularly impacting education prominently. In response to lockdowns and social distancing protocols, universities were forced to rapidly transform their teaching and management methods [27]. To prevent or contain the spread of the epidemic on campuses, the Chinese government and universities put in place a series of restriction policies, such as the postponement of school semesters, the implementation of online teaching, and closed management. These policies, although effectively controlled the spread of the epidemic, inevitably disrupted the normal academic lives of college students [32].In this context, COVID-19 became a source of stress and emotional distress for many students. The virus'rapid spread, strict isolation measures, and numerous associated public opinion events all elicited strong emotional responses from college students. Individual emotions arise from physiological changes triggered by situational stimuli and their cognitive interpretations of these changes. The latter, called attribution, determines one's emotional response. Environmental changes induce temporary emotional patterns that directly reflect an individual's immediate psychological response to specific situations. Examining these emotions can reveal individuals'attitudes toward public events, helping relevant administrators formulate personalized intervention measures for future similar situations [36]. In sudden public crisis events, individual’s stress reactions are naturally activated when they perceive a threat in their environment [22], which in turn ultimately affects their physiological processes and behavioral response patterns through cognitive attributions and emotional activation [9].

Contemporary college students, as digital natives of the Internet era, tend to express and share emotions via social media [40]. Additionally, COVID-19 pandemic-related campus closures further reinforced social media as students'primary information-sharing channel [65]. Among these platforms, Sina microblog distinguishes itself through civilianized communication, multimedia integration, and fragmented information dissemination, attracting a massive user base. With a high Alexa ranking facilitating viral spread of news and trending social topics, it has become a critical venue for emotional expression and information exchange. The Blue Book of China's Social Mentality notes that 74.3% of microblog content involves self-expression, underscoring its role in shaping public emotional discourse [56]. According to Sina microblog's 2020 User Development Report, the platform had 511 million monthly active users and 224 million daily active users in September 2020, with nearly 80% of users belonging to the post-90s (48%) and post-00s (30%) generations. By September 2024, as stated in the 2024 User Consumption Trends Report, these figures reached 587 million monthly and 257 million daily active users, with the proportion of young users from these generations remaining close to 80% [64]. Additionally, Sina microblog has launched the “Weibo Campus” topic specifically for college students, making it a key social platform for students to share campus life, exchange ideas, and express feelings. These data reveal a significant trend towards younger users on Sina microblog, characterized by distinct intergenerational differences, making it a primary platform for college students to vent their online emotions. Furthermore, research on media usage behavior among Chinese college students has found that Weibo is the preferred platform for interactive discussions on public opinion regarding public events [66].

2020 was a pivotal year in China’s COVID-19 prevention and control efforts. Empirically examining the emotional characteristics and attributions of college students during this period can facilitate an accurate understanding of their internal logic in attributing public health emergencies, thereby providing references for universities to handle similar crises in the future. Grounded in emotion attribution theory, this study analyzed Sina microblogs posted by college students related to COVID-19, aiming to identify and decode their online emotional expressions and corresponding attributions. The study employs a multi-step research process, including: (1) crawling Sina microblog trending topic data with Python and generating a text-based epidemic event graph based on students’ associated epidemic event constructions. (2) selecting representative Sina microblog texts, which are categorized at three levels using content analysis; developing an emotion attribution system for college students during epidemic occurrences; and dimensionally partitioning the system using Weiner’s three-dimensional attribution theory. (3) building a four-layer Bayesian classifier to further refine the classification of students’ emotions; and combining the attribution system with emotion attribution theory to interpret students’ online emotions. The ultimate goal of this study is to offer a holistic perspective on the online emotions of college students during the COVID-19 pandemic, which will expand research ideas on the generation of online emotions among college students, thus helping individuals overcome emotional trauma when crisis strikes [47], and providing a foundation for universities to assess, educate, and intervene the mental health of college students.

Theoretical basis and literature review

Theoretical basis

Emotion valuation theory

The evaluation of conventional emotions often involves two main approaches: a categorical approach and a dimensional approach. The categorical approach focuses on different aspects of emotions, such as physiological mechanisms and external manifestations, aiming to classify emotions into a limited number of distinct and fundamental emotions [41]. For example, Ekman classified basic emotions into six categories: happiness, sadness, anger, fear, disgust, and surprise, which are widely used and recognized by the academic world as a more classical emotion classification [15]. Plutchik posited eight pure emotions: happiness, sadness, anger, fear, expectation, surprise, disgust, and acceptance, and suggested that all other complex emotions are derived from these eight [44]. Chinese psychologist Lin Chuan Ding classified emotions into 18 categories on the basis of the traditional Chinese “seven emotions”, including quietness, joy, hatred, anger, grief, and pity [30].In China, the most widely adopted emotion classification framework is the seven-category system developed by Dalian University of Technology, which distinguishes emotions into seven distinct categories: happiness, contentment, surprise, anger, sadness, fear, and disgust[30, 32, 71]. In contrast, dimensional approaches treat emotions as a highly interconnected continuum, asserting that isolating individual emotions for independent analysis is impractical,instead, these approaches focus on capturing core emotional dimensions. For example, Russell’s valence-arousal two-dimensional model identifies “valence” (positive/negative affect) and “arousal” (activation level) as fundamental axes explaining most emotional variance. Specifically, arousal refers to the degree to which emotional stimuli trigger physiological responses ranging from anxiety to relaxation [50]. The two-dimensional theory has dominated emotion research to this point [4]. Additionally, Jeanne Tsai defined emotion valuation by categorizing emotions into eight categories based on valence and arousal, and she created the emotion valuation theory [25]. Emotion valuation theory has demonstrated proven efficacy in accounting for the consistency of emotional responses across diverse contexts. This theory plays a pivotal role in understanding and predicting individual emotional reactions, thereby providing a robust theoretical foundation for the measurement and evaluation of emotions in this study.

This study aims to assess the online emotions of college students using two models, i.e., Dalian University of Technology’s seven classifications of emotions and the two-dimensional valence-arousal model. The emotions will be classified into four dimensions: Positive high-arousal, positive low-arousal, negative high-arousal, and negative low-arousal (see Fig. 1).

Emotion valuation model

Emotion attribution theory

Attribution, originating from social psychology, refers to the process of inferring the causes of behaviors. Specifically, it is a cognitive process whereby individuals determine the underlying causes of outcomes through internal cognitive processes (e.g., perception, thought, inference) [45]. The attribution theory is the study of how humans generate explanations for causes [38] and since it is proposed by Fritz Heider, the theory has been a popular topic in social psychology research. Heider [24] proposed that individuals attribute the causes of their feelings and behaviors to two primary motivations: the need to establish a consistent understanding of their environment and the drive to exert control over it. People can only fulfill the “desire to understand and regulate their surroundings” by attributing others'behaviors and predicting subsequent actions based on these attributions [24]. Schachter later integrated attribution theory into emotion research by linking physiological arousal with cognitive interpretations of emotions, and proposed a framework for emotional dimensions categorized by internal and external attribution sources [19]. Later, Weiner et al. expanded the dimensions of attribution and constructed a three-dimensional attribution model including internal and external source(internal–external) dimensions, stability (stable-unstable) dimensions, and controllability (controllable-uncontrollable). The locus of attribution refers to whether the cause is perceived as external or internal. Stability relates to the likelihood of attribution changing over time, while controllability refers to whether the cause can be altered by individual will [59]. Weiner’s model organically linked attribution to motivation and emotion, paving the way for new frontiers in attribution research. Over decades, attribution theory has garnered increasing scholarly attention and been continually refined. Chinese scholars have validated its applicability within Chinese cultural and societal contexts [68]. Following Weiner’s three-dimensional attribution system, this study distills the emotion attribution system of college students in COVID-19 context from three dimensions: site, stability, and controllability.

Literature review

Literature on network emotion

In the field of network emotion research, the classification of network emotions largely follows traditional emotion classification methods, with the most commonly used method being the seven-point method developed by the Information Retrieval Research Laboratory at Dalian University of Technology [6, 63, 68]. However, due to the complex and diverse nature of online emotions, especially with usual grouping and aggregation, the two-dimensional model of valence-arousal is often employed to better capture its dimensionality. On the valence dimension, research has shown that negative emotions are more contagious and spread more quickly than positive emotions, as demonstrated by Liu Cong et al. in their investigation of the propagation of emotions on microblogs, who categorized emotions as positive, negative, and neutral [63]. In the realm of emotion attribution research, there are two main areas of focus: influencing factors and the application of theory. Studies have indicated that emotion attribution is a useful tool for understanding the psychological reactions of the public in regards to current political concerns in China, and that it can be influenced by both sociocultural and macro factors [51],as well as micro factors such as the nature of the event, individual personality, and subjective expectations [59]. Additionally, research has shown that individuals attach significance to characteristics that align with their expectations [26]. Emotion attribution theory has been predominantly applied in education and psychology, but in recent years, its application has expanded to other domains to explain behavioral and emotional generation [17]. For example, a recent Chinese study used this theory to analyze the nuances of online emotions in anti-corruption cases and explore their underlying causes [45]. However, there is a lack of research using emotion attribution theory to investigate the psychology and behavior of college student groups.

Literature on sentiments and mentality during COVID-19

COVID-19 has profoundly impacted physical health, work-life conditions, and particularly social mentality. Studies show that pandemic-related elevated stress can elicit negative emotions, with stress levels directly correlating with the intensity of negative affect [23]. This, in turn, can have a negative effect on both their mental and physical health [20].The fear of potential transmission and the severity of the outbreak was a constant source of stress for individuals during the pandemic, leading to social emotions such as fear and anger [39].The outbreak also impacted the national economy and social production, causing increased safety concerns. However, with the effective implementation of epidemic preparedness, individuals'perceptions of stress shifted from a fear of life safety to concerns about economic and income security [43]. Moreover, the widespread use and development of modern media, facilitated by epidemic prevention measures such as city closures, has made the public more vulnerable to fluctuating online opinions and social sentiment [16]. Studies have confirmed that different social groups perceive and respond to risk differently during a public crisis.

In the case of college student groups, research has revealed that high levels of anxiety and depression are prevalent, although the findings are not uniform [37]. For example, Bian Huiyuan et al. investigated the emotion status of the college student population between January 30 and February 4, 2020, and discovered that the level of anxiety and depression among them was higher than normal [2].Li Weidong et al. conducted a survey of college students during the closed management period in 2022 and found that campus lockdown had a significant impact on the depression of college students [34]. The research by other Chinese scholars also confirmed the severity of depressive emotions among college students in the post-pandemic era [12, 21, 35]. However, not all studies on the emotion of college students showed negative results; Beijing Chen et al. found that college students were predominantly happy in the early stages of the epidemic, based on a web-based questionnaire conducted nationwide with 11,320 college students [6]. While existing studies have documented basic emotional expressions during COVID-19, gaps remain in understanding emotion classification and genesis. Methodologically, most contemporary research relies on online surveys or standardized scales for emotion assessment, often adopting cross-sectional designs with small samples and risk of Hawthorne effect. Consequently, longitudinal studies on pandemic-related emotions among diverse college student populations are limited. This study aims to characterize online emotions expressed by college students on Sina microblog in 2020, analyze their attributional logic from a psychological perspective, and deepen insights into undergraduates’psychological response mechanisms during abrupt public crises.

Research methods

Sina microblog trending is a well-liked piece of material that the microblog creates based on the search habits of its users. It is distinguished by its interactivity, topicality, and timeliness. According to sociology, online public opinion is not just a flow of information, it also involves netizen participation [69]. The logic of microblog trending turns into public opinion as a reflection of self-media public opinion, producing online spectatorship. While observing and participating in discussions, users continue to add value to trending subjects, and certain trending news events develop into public opinion events. The link between online opinion formation and media consumption demonstrates mutual promotion and gradual integration [29]. Therefore, this study used content analysis and emotion analysis based on text mining to interpret college students’ online emotions using Sina microblog trending topic data as the research object.. Data collection and pre-processing

This study crawled significant data using the Sina microblog’s legal API (Application Program-mining Interface) and evaluated it using Python after authentication. We crawled the Sina microblog trending topic data from January 1, 2020 to December 31, 2020, which totaled 104,253 entries. The data includes topic titles, topic rankings, topic buzzes, topic timings, durations, debates, and readings. To acquire microblogs that accurately reflect the emotions of college students online, it is necessary to filter and analyze epidemic events affecting college student groups. As a result, we manually read and filtered the trending topic data related to “COVID-19,” “college students,” “universities,” and “colleges” and compiled a total of 358 valid trending topics. We manually annotated trending topic texts with semantics and categorized them based on topic attributes, yielding eight distinct topic types, including postponement of school semesters, online teaching, return to school, postponement of examinations, enclosed management, online recruitment, online graduation, and peak staggering holidays. We described the topics used to construct the event graph of the college student epidemic (see Table 1) and crawled the microblog text data of the eight topics, the basic contents of which are: ID, basic user information, publication date, and microblog content.

Content analysis

The method of analyzing the content of a specific research object or research sample is known as content analysis. It enables valid and reproducible conclusions to be drawn about the content by analyzing the amount of information contained in the sample content and its variations [46]. Using Weber’s method, the following steps were taken to conduct content analysis on texts that had been cleaned [57]:

Sampling for analysis. To ensure the external validity of the sample, we ranked the microblogs in the database according to “comments, retweets, and likes” in accordance with Yin’s belief that “qualitative research objects should have good typicality and representativeness” [67]. From each of the eight epidemic events, 100 texts with the highest overall ranking were selected,a total of 800 texts were selected for analysis and labeled with the format event + serial number. Fine-grained emotion analysis

The processed comments are attributed and categorized by emotion category using a microblog emotion attribution classifier. The particular procedures are as follows: First, a fine-grained emotion classification of the microblog text content of each event topic is designed, followed by an attribution classification of the text under each sentiment in order to investigate the emotion of microblog users under various attributions. In this study, supervised machine learning is used to treat the sentiment analysis problem as a classification problem, and the labeled training set is used to train the machine learning algorithm in order to obtain a classification model for subsequent sentiment classification.

Emotion attribution classifier design

Our data analysis focused on two dimensions: attribution and emotion. For attribution analysis, we extracted attributional dimensions from Sina microblog text data to develop an emotion attribution index system for college students'pandemic-related posts. This system served as the theoretical basis for constructing an emotion attribution classifier. Leveraging the Chinese Emotion Vocabulary Ontology Database and the online emotion lexicon compiled by the Information Retrieval Research Laboratory of Dalian University of Technology, the model characterizes Chinese words or phrases from multiple perspectives, including lexical category, emotion type, intensity, and valence [45]. The emotions in the emotion vocabulary ontology are divided into 7 major categories and 21 subcategories, and the major categories are happy, good, anger, sadness, fear, disgust, and surprise [63], And each word corresponds to a polarity within each emotion category. Meanwhile, the 7 fine-grained emotions were classified based on their valence-arousal degree. Examples of emotion classification are shown in Table 5. In this study, “Anger” means negative emotions with high arousal, like insults or more intense words. “Disgust” means negative emotions with low arousal, like dislike, sarcasm, and questioning. “Fear” means negative emotions like panic. “Happy” means having positive emotions with high arousal, like joy. “Good” means positive emotions with low arousal, like approval and fondness. “Sadness” means negative emotions with low arousal.

This study created a four-layer Bayesian emotion attribution classifier using the Python 3.7 Sklearn library. The classification of texts for emotion consists of two steps. First, the positive sentiment tendency, negative sentiment tendency, and neutral sentiment tendency of microblog comments are classified by the first layer classifier, followed by the import of texts with positive and negative sentiment tendency into the second layer classifier, which includes seven categories of emotions: happy, good, surprise, anger, sadness, fear, and disgust. The categorized texts are then classified for emotion attribution, and the first-level attribution is employed as a Bayesian first-layer attribution classifier, which is imported into the second-layer attribution classifier for classification of emotion attribution categories.

Manual data labeling

Cross-validation was employed for manual data annotation to minimize human error. From a de-weighted corpus of 18,300 comments, 2,000 were randomly selected. After training five labelers, the first annotation pass involved each labeler randomly tagging 400 comments. For the second pass, the 2,000 comments were assigned to the five labelers without overlap (400 comments per labeler), ensuring no prior annotations reappeared for the same labeler. Final annotations were determined by comparing the two passes: inconsistent results were resolved via a third-party annotation using majority vote, and expert review panel adjudicated cases where all three annotators disagreed.

Model accuracy test

80% of the manually annotated data were designated as training data and 20% as test data. The test set and training set were divided using Sklearn’s random division function. In order to improve the accuracy of word classification, we constructed and added the “COVID-19” custom dictionary, which has 1242 proper nouns based on literature and news reports. After text feature extraction and text representation, the TF-IDF text feature item weighting model is applied to identify whether or not a given word is a keyword. The results of the experiments indicate that the model obtains the maximum level of precision when the number of feature words is set to 9000. Finally, we utilized SPSS to examine the consistency between hand labeling and model testing findings. The Cronbach alpha coefficient of the two is 0.896, demonstrating the classifier’s suitability for usage in this study.

Emotion attribution system construction

The eight first-level attribution codes were employed to build an emotion attribution system, and attribution dimensions were categorized in accordance with Weiner's attribution theory [59]. First, separating the attribution site When internal attributions were made, emotional causes were attributed to human attributes including ability, personality, mood, effort, attitude, or disposition. When external attributions are made, the origin of an emotion is ascribed to the environment or an objective event, such as a task or social context [58]. In this study, internal attributions contain three dimensions: input attributions, ability attributions, and physical and mental attributions. External attributions include five dimensions: system, interpersonal, university, epidemic, and environment. Individually perceived behavioral events can be categorized as controllable or uncontrollable based on their ability to be removed, weakened, or strengthened. Generally speaking, controllable elements are those that can be transferred by an individual's will, such as effort, diligence, laziness, etc., whereas uncontrollable factors are those that cannot be transferred, such as ability, opportunity, etc. In most instances, individuals cannot control or alter these factors at will [10]. In this study, only the input attribution is a controllable attribution, whereas the rest are uncontrollable. Thirdly, the stability of attribution is examined. Stability elements, such as talent, personality, task difficulty, and temperament, are resistant to change over time, but instability factors, such as chance, effort, opportunity, and the external environment, are susceptible to change. In the case of the COVID-19 epidemic, recent research indicated that crises induced by public emergencies are unstable, with uncertainty being the defining characteristic [61, 62]. China has established a long-term mechanism for epidemic prevention and control, including a series of measures such as the delineation of risk areas, refinement of isolation methods, and promotion of vaccination, in order to respond with strategic stability to the uncertainty of the epidemic situation [60]. Under the guidance of national policies, universities have developed and improved their management systems with relative stability and long-term efficacy in response to actual crisis situations [13]. In the process of controlling epidemics, social isolation is one of the most prevalent and effective methods, and it has inevitably led to the alienation of interpersonal relationships. Nevertheless, Robin Dunbar, a psychologist at Oxford University, concluded from historical studies that the negative effects of temporary social alienation on interpersonal relationships are brief and limited. Due to the characteristics of policy continuity and stability in China, as well as the criteria for classifying attribution dimensions in the existing literature [14], we considered system, interpersonal, university, and ability attributions as stability attributions, and epidemic, environment, input, and physical and mental attributions to be instability attributions. Discussion

College students show clear negative emotion pointing to COVID-19

Consistent with some previous findings [48], the online discussion of the COVID-19 epidemic among college students was dominated by negative emotions [32] Nevertheless, it contradicts the findings of Chen Beijing et al. [6]. Positive emotions predominate in most current studies of student emotions during the pandemic, though findings remain inconsistent. This may be attributed to three factors: First, most student emotion surveys rely on questionnaires, which limit sample size and hinder large-scale investigations; second, variability in subject group selection—such as geographic location, school tier, and gender—leads to divergent outcomes due to demographic differences; third, studies vary in their chosen temporal scope for emotional assessment [17]. The majority of current studies on students’ emotions during the epidemic were conducted over a broad time period to measure emotions, and the results of student emotion studies conducted over different time periods vary [32, 53]. This study conducts a longitudinal analysis of online emotions using text-mining sentiment analysis, circumventing the sample size limitations of self-report surveys and the temporal constraints of traditional emotion assessments. This approach enhances the generalizability of online emotion research findings to the broader student population.

The results of emotion analysis showed that the dominant negative emotions of college students were fear, anger, sadness, and disgust, with low-arousal negative emotions predominating and high-arousal negative emotions supplementing. During the COVID-19 epidemic, anxiety and depression were more prevalent among college students, who were found to have mental health issues on average [42]. Inadequate knowledge of the epidemic, fear of being infected, and fear of discrimination were identified as significant contributors to the mental health problems of college students [31]. However, this study found that the academic burden of home study combined with online teaching and the unknowns about the start of school, as well as the mandatory nature of the epidemic prevention measures, were significant sources of negative emotions among college students, corroborating the previous research's conclusion that a general low energy level and predominance of negative feelings are evident during lockdowns [5]. During the epidemic, the external environmental order of college students'daily lives was disrupted, their psychological quality was challenged, their cognitive characteristics, social adaptability, and psychological personality were affected, especially their social adaptability, so they were unable to adjust their psychological adaptation to the general social environment in a timely manner, their academic life could not be reasonably arranged, and they were prone to mood swings and depression. Existing research indicates that online learning maladjustment and academic pressure are significant determinants of negative emotions among university students [33, 53], which is consistent with the results of this study. In addition, the internal attribution revealed that the psychological traits and cognitive factors of college students play a significant role in their emotion generation. On the one hand, college students are in early adulthood, their self-awareness is growing, and their cognitive level, need level, emotion, attitude, and will are maturing [37]. However, the personality traits of college students are relatively immature. The willpower, self-confidence, affordability, self-control, and lack of a sense of social responsibility exhibited by the alterations in the social macroenvironment are easily transformed into “fuel” for negative emotions during the epidemic. This is consistent with the findings of Rosenfeld et al.’s study [49].

In addition, research has revealed that a portion of college students also exhibited positive emotions towards the pandemic, predominantly characterized by low arousal levels. The orientation of these positive emotions primarily revolves around the anticipation of resuming school and classes, the expectation for the swift containment of the pandemic, as well as the gratitude and respect towards the diverse entities that have selflessly contributed to epidemic prevention and control [2]. During the pandemic, confronted with the panic stemming from the unknown, the adequate supply of materials and resource connections played a pivotal role in stabilizing and stimulating the positive emotions of college students. Utilizing the Weibo platform, college students expressed their understanding, appreciation, and support for their institutions. Additionally, when making positive comments, college students tended to convey calm and rational positive emotional attitudes, rather than more excited ones [55].

The differences in emotions caused by different emotional attributions were more significant

Findings support Weiner’s “outcome-dependent” and"attribution-dependent"emotion hypotheses, showing that college students’ emotions exhibit both commonalities and attribution-based variations. Disgust, a low-arousal negative emotion, was significantly evident in both internal and external attributions, likely because it represents students’most intuitive emotional reaction to the pandemic and falls under “outcome-dependent” emotions. Negativity is a hallmark of online expression, with prior research confirming that cyberspace anonymity and group expression contagion amplify negative online emotions [71]. Furthermore, the negative nature of the epidemic makes it easier to induce negative psychological stress responses [11]. Additionally, prior research has demonstrated that negative low arousal emotions are typical of “outcome-dependent” emotions during unexpected and unanticipated events [8]. We also found that “attribution-dependent” emotions do exist in the discussion of epidemics, and different attributions resulted in variations in emotional expression. Since the epidemic is an uncontrollable external force [61], humans have played a passive role in its control. Change caused by outside intervention not only increases uncertainty but also decreases a sense of control [49], which makes the negative emotions of college students more significant in external attributions than internal ones. While policies are most strictly enforced at the student level—for instance, campus closure measures were more lenient for faculty and staff due to job-related obligations—college students’ psychological states were shaped by shifts in external regulations and differential enforcement. In general, mandatory, normalized, and differentiated prevention policies increased students’ sense of helplessness, giving rise to negative emotions such as sadness, disgust, and irritability, and even triggering antagonistic perceptions of policies and management systems, leading to “structural resentment” toward society [72]. Consequently, stable attributions were associated with lower overall emotional arousal. By contrast, unstable attributions—such as the rapid mutation of the novel coronavirus or large-scale outbreaks in specific regions—elicited relatively strong feelings of anger and fear.

Additionally, it was discovered that emotion attributions in the epidemic are dominated by external stable attributions such as system attributions and university attributions, with prominent characteristics of responsibility attributions. This means that college students tend to investigate the causes of the negative outcomes of epidemic events and who is accountable for them [10]. Existing research has confirmed that individuals exhibit heightened sensitivity and stronger emotional responses [54] and responsibility attribution motivations when confronting negative events compared to positive or neutral one [70], Specifically, during epidemic prevention and control, experiencing negative emotions drives individuals to identify the sources of these feelings, often attributing them to factors such as the pandemic’s emergence, inadequate government or institutional responses in prevention and control, and others. This behavior helps pinpoint targets for negative emotion venting, providing a psychological basis for emotional transfer as these feelings evolve[69]. These findings align with the results of this study.

During the epidemic, college students experience uncertainty in a variety of ways, including uncertainty regarding future sociopolitical and cultural values. Excessive uncertainty can shake the meaning of an individual's existence, so individuals develop a desire to eliminate or maintain manageable levels of uncertainty. The motivation to alleviate uncertainty drives individuals to seek information to achieve cognitive closure and maintain a sense of control; this is the motivation to attribute responsibility [1]. The responsibility attribution motivation of college students is consistent with van den Bos and Lind’s research on uncertainty management theory: the purpose and psychological significance of responsibility attribution are to alleviate uncertainty and maintain a sense of control. Among them, the sense of certainty emphasizes the predictability of the future and the explanation of causality, whereas the sense of control emphasizes the individual's ability to influence the outcome [3]. In terms of the distribution of positive emotions, external attribution is notably higher than internal attribution. This indicates that, despite the relatively prominent negative expressions of college students towards the external environment, the implementation of a series of epidemic prevention and control measures has led to an increase in their recognition of the political environment, government effectiveness, and judicial system. This underscores that epidemic prevention measures have also elicited certain positive psychological effects [31]. However, due to the textual nature of online emotions; they are more indirect in expression than traditional emotions, therefore, we need to further connect the emotional subject with the emotional text to confirm the psychological motivation of such emotions, which will be our next research direction.

Conclusion

In this study, emotion analysis was conducted on two dimensions based on the emotion attribution theory: emotion valuation and emotion attribution. First, 18,300 microblog texts related to eight epidemic events were crawled by crawler software, and attribution analysis was performed using the content analysis method on the microblog texts. Weiner's three-dimensional attribution system was used to classify an emotion attribution system comprising eight first-level attributions: epidemic attribution, system attribution, environment attribution, university attribution, interpersonal attribution, physical and mental attribution, input attribution, and ability attribution. Second, a Bayesian classifier was developed to classify text according to fine-grained emotion classification and emotion attribution classification. At the emotion level, the online emotion sentiment type of the student group during the epidemic was dominated by negative emotion, with low arousal and overall negative emotion validity. The long-term home isolation caused by the epidemic, the academic burden posed by online teaching, and the epidemic itself were the primary causes of negative emotions. The primary components of the positive emotions were the anticipation of resuming classes and the hope for the effective control of the epidemic. There were significant differences in emotions across attribution dimensions at the attribution level. Positive emotions were significantly greater for external attributions than for internal attributions in terms of emotional validity. Stable attributions were associated with low emotional arousal, such as disappointment and dissatisfaction with policy formulation and implementation, whereas unstable attributions were associated with relatively high emotional arousal, such as fear of the spread of the epidemic and anger at Internet rumors.

Using a text mining-based online emotion measurement method, this study measured and analyzed the online emotions of students on microblog during the COVID-19 epidemic. The researchers gained a more in-depth understanding of the characteristics and changing trends of online emotions, confirmed the viability of text mining technology for online emotion measurement, and enhanced the insufficient depth of traditional questionnaire and interview research. It utilized emotion attribution theory to investigate the generation of college students'online emotions, to broaden the research horizon of emotion research and the application scope of emotion attribution theory, to provide a baseline scale reference for college students'state of mind early warning, and to provide a theoretical reference for colleges and universities to assess, educate, and intervene in students'mental health during emergency situations. Because the emotion attribution classifier is the primary instrument for emotion attribution analysis in this work, the classification accuracy is constrained and is influenced by manual annotation. Therefore, there are still some shortcomings in the classification process that need to be addressed in the future by integrating more advanced models (such as neural networks) to train and perform language related tasks on large amounts of text. For example, using the BERT language model to help improve the accuracy and efficiency of emotion analysis [27]. Furthermore, due to the anonymity of Sina microblog, the study could only obtain basic user information such as gender and age, and thus did not conduct a more detailed classification of college students based on dimensions such as educational stage, grade, major, and university type. Additionally, as the study only focused on data from 2020, more information is needed about college students’ emotion in the post-epidemic era (2021–2022). In order to obtain more precise results, future research can start with the demographic variable characteristics of user groups and analyze online emotions based on more specific demographic variable dimensions in conjunction with interviews and questionnaires. Also, our next research focus will be on the emotions of college students during the normalization of COVID-19 in 2021 and 2022.
