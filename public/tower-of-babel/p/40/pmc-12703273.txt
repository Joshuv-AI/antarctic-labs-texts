The WHO Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use 6th edition and the WHO Selected Practice Recommendations 4th edition: update and future direction
Authors: Nancy Kidula, James N Kiarie
Published: 2025

The World Health Organization (WHO), in line with its mandate to set global standards, has for over 25 years collaborated with stakeholders to issue evidence-based normative contraceptive guidelines so as to reduce unnecessary medical barriers to contraception. The Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use (MEC) present WHO recommendations on the safety of various contraceptive methods in the context of specific health conditions and physical characteristics. The Selected Practice Recommendations for Contraceptive Use (SPR) provide guidance on how to use contraceptive methods safely and effectively once the client is deemed eligible. These guidelines are used by policymakers and programme managers across the six WHO regions to develop their national policies, guidelines and tools.

The development of these guidelines involves a stringent process outlined in the WHO Handbook for Guideline Development.1 To ensure that recommendations are based on the best available evidence, comprehensive systematic reviews are conducted and subjected to quality assessment. WHO regularly updates the guidelines to align with evolving evidence. In this regard, the MEC has been revised five times since 1996 and is currently in its 6th edition, while the SPR, first launched in 2002, is now in its 4th edition.

Between November 2022 and July 2024, WHO set up a Guideline Development Group (GDG) comprising a multidisciplinary group of experts from diverse regions who, together with an evidence team, supported the update of these guidelines. The GDG’s mandate was to review and, where appropriate, update the recommendations in the MEC 5th edition and SPR 3rd edition. Broadly, eight topics encompassing 15 key questions were prioritised at a scoping GDG meeting based on requests from member states, a stakeholder survey, potential for public health impact, and availability of significant new evidence directly relevant to the MEC and SPR. Availability of new evidence was determined through a Continuous Identification of Research Evidence (CIRE) process. Full systematic reviews were commissioned on the eight topics. For each of the key questions, the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was used to appraise the quality of evidence for all the critical outcomes of interest and guide the formulation of recommendations. Details of this methodology are described in the appendices of the MEC 6th edition2 and the SPR 4th edition.3

This special issue of BMJ SRH highlights four systematic reviews that provided evidence for the MEC and SPR revisions. The first review by Ti et al 4 relates to the ‘Safety of progestogen-only contraceptives (POCs) during breastfeeding’, considering concerns around the risk of adverse infant and breastfeeding outcomes. Overall, new evidence on POCs including injectables was reassuring with no harmful effects on breastfeeding or infant outcomes even when initiated less than 6 weeks’ postpartum. The second review, also by Ti et al,5 addresses a common concern on the ‘Safety of intrauterine devices (IUDs) during breastfeeding’. Evidence from new studies suggests an increased risk of uterine perforation in breastfeeding women using IUDs. However, results around breastfeeding and infant outcomes were reassuring. While emergency contraceptive pills (ECPs) are commonly used, there is no WHO guidance around their repeated use. Thus, the third systematic review, by Steyn et al,6 looks at the ‘Safety of repeated use of ECPs in the same cycle’. While the results do not show evidence of harm, the complexity of interpreting the term ‘repeated use’ due to lack of clarity on quantity and timeframe of ECP intake was noted as an area for further research. Concerns around women’s experiences during IUD insertion, such as pain and difficult placements, are addressed by Tufa et al7 in their review on ‘Use of non-pharmacological techniques to ease interval IUD placement’. Evidence was mainly from single studies and the quality of evidence was deemed low. This is an area that needs further research.

This special issue also includes a hypothetical case study by Liberty et al 8 on the, ‘Breastfeeding and progestogen-only contraception’, whose aim is to practically illustrate how to implement the updated MEC and SPR recommendations. Additionally, there is a narrative review by Osoti et al 9 on ‘Return to fertility after subcutaneous use of depot medroxyprogesterone acetate - DMPA SC 104 mg’ addressing concerns about whether return to fertility differs when DMPA is given intramuscularly versus subcutaneously. The updated MEC and SPR also drew on evidence from several Cochrane systematic reviews which have been published separately related to medications to ease IUD insertion,1012 as well as drug interactions between HIV antiretroviral medications (ARVs) and hormonal contraception.13

Informed by this evidence, the MEC 6th edition has updated recommendations on the use of POCs and IUDs among breastfeeding women; clarified the safety of repeated use of emergency contraceptives in a single cycle; reviewed recommendations on drug interactions between ARVs and hormonal contraceptives in alignment with current ART regimens; and included recommendations on hormonal contraceptive use in women on pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP). For the SPR, the 4th edition has new recommendations around medications to ease interval IUD insertion. Details on the topics reviewed and the full recommendations can be found in the main guideline documents.2 3

The GDG identified key knowledge gaps, enumerated in the two guideline documents, where further research is needed to improve client-centred contraceptive services. WHO encourages research to address key knowledge gaps related to the safety and use of contraceptives. In the meantime, WHO will continue to monitor published evidence pertinent to the MEC and SPR, and invites comments and suggestions for improving the guidelines and their implementation.
