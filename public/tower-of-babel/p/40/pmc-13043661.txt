Selection of porin and LPS mutants during infection by phage T4 facilitates the emergence of β-lactam resistance in Escherichia coli
Authors: Justine Le-Boulch, Erika Charneau, Anne Chevallereau, Arnaud Gutierrez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding all sources of selective pressure that contribute to the emergence of antibiotic resistance is essential for developing sustainable antimicrobial strategies. Here, we investigated the interaction between phage T4 and Escherichia coli MG1655 to determine whether mutations conferring phage resistance also shape the genetic background for β-lactam resistance. Using experimental evolution, whole-genome sequencing, and targeted genetic reconstructions, we identified mutations in porins and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) biosynthesis as the predominant routes to phage T4 resistance. Precise allelic replacements and isogenic strain comparisons demonstrated that these mutations not only protect against phage predation but also create a genetic context that facilitates the emergence of β-lactam resistance, including resistance to carbapenems. Together, these findings provide compelling evidence that phage-driven selection can establish bacterial genetic backgrounds predisposed to antibiotic resistance. This work highlights the evolutionary risks associated with phage therapy and underscores the need to account for genetic trade-offs when developing alternative antimicrobial strategies.

Introduction

The emergence of antibiotic resistance in Gram-negative bacteria represents a major threat to clinical practice1,2, particularly when resistance compromises last-line agents such as carbapenems or later-generation cephalosporins3. In these organisms, resistance mediated by β-lactamases can be further amplified by reduced outer membrane permeability resulting from the loss or alteration of porins4,5. Such permeability defects can, for example, lead to reduced susceptibility to extended-spectrum and later-generation cephalosporins, including cefepime. In addition, although carbapenems are poorly hydrolysed by most β-lactamases, decreased antibiotic influx caused by porin loss or alteration can result in clinically meaningful resistance and treatment failure6,7. Clinical isolates exhibiting this phenotype are increasingly reported and are often associated with altered porin profiles together with the expression of extended-spectrum β-lactamases belonging to the AmpC or OXA-48 families8,9.

In Gram-negative bacteria, outer membrane porins play a central role in this process. Porins such as OmpC form channels that facilitate the uptake of small molecules, including β-lactam antibiotics4. Alterations in porin expression or structure, therefore, directly affect antibiotic susceptibility. In addition to porins, lipopolysaccharides constitute a major structural component of the outer membrane and contribute to its barrier function. Modifications of the LPS can alter membrane permeability by changing surface charge, restricting antibiotic diffusion, and interfering with the proper assembly or function of porins10–12.

Importantly, these same surface structures also serve as receptors for bacteriophages, creating an evolutionary trade-off between phage resistance and antibiotic permeability13–15. Changes selected to evade phage infection may inadvertently reduce antibiotic entry and thereby potentiate resistance when β-lactamases are present. Bacteriophages are among the most abundant biological entities on Earth and exert strong selective pressure on bacterial populations16,17. Phage–bacteria interactions are often characterized by antagonistic coevolution, in which bacterial surface structures evolve to evade phage adsorption while maintaining essential physiological functions18,19. Because surface antigens such as porins and lipopolysaccharides are simultaneously involved in environmental interactions, nutrient uptake and phage recognition, phage-driven selection frequently targets traits with pleiotropic consequences. These dynamics can generate second-order evolutionary effects that shape bacterial adaptability beyond phage resistance alone.

The clinical relevance of such evolutionary trade-offs has gained renewed attention in the context of phage therapy, which is being reconsidered as a treatment option for infections caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria20,21. While phage therapy offers several advantages, including high specificity and activity against antibiotic-resistant strains, bacterial resistance to phages often evolves rapidly through modifications of surface receptors21. Increasing interest has therefore focused on exploiting the fitness costs and collateral effects associated with phage resistance, an approach known as phage steering22. However, the consequences of phage-imposed selection on antibiotic susceptibility remain poorly understood, particularly with respect to clinically important resistance phenotypes such as non-carbapenemase carbapenem resistance.

In the Escherichia coli K-12 MG1655 strain, the virulent phage T4 requires both the major porin OmpC and the inner core of lipopolysaccharides to adsorb and initiate infection13. Because porins are key entry pathways for β-lactam antibiotics and LPS is essential for proper porin assembly in the outer membrane, phage predation targeting these structures may select for mutations that alter membrane permeability. We therefore used the well-characterized laboratory strain E. coli K-12 MG1655 to investigate how phage-imposed selection can promote the emergence of antibiotic resistance. This strain expresses the major porins OmpC and OmpF, displays a truncated rough LPS lacking the O-antigen due to inactivation of the wbbL gene23, and carries no known active antibiotic resistance determinants or virulence factors24.

We investigated the evolution of phage resistance in E. coli MG1655 under different selective regimens and analysed the resulting genetic and phenotypic consequences. We show that exposure to phage T4 rapidly selects convergent mutations affecting OmpC and inner-core LPS biosynthesis, and that specific combinations of these mutations can facilitate the coincidental emergence of β-lactam resistance, including resistance to carbapenems.

Results

Impact of ompC and ompF inactivation on antibiotic resistance

Although the role of the major porins OmpC and OmpF in antibiotic diffusion is well established (Fig. 1A)4, the extent to which their loss contributes to β-lactam resistance varies across experimental and clinical settings. To precisely assess the impact of porin inactivation in our experimental conditions, and to evaluate its interaction with the expression of β-lactamases from different classes, we used isogenic Escherichia coli MG1655 deletion mutants lacking ompC, ompF, or both genes (ΔompC, ΔompF, and ΔompCF). We first characterised the antibiotic susceptibility profiles of the porin-deficient mutants by measuring minimum inhibitory (MIC) concentrations for 11 clinically relevant antibiotics using E-test strips. As shown in Fig. 1B and Fig. S1, single inactivation of either ompC or ompF had little or no effect on antibiotic susceptibility. In contrast, simultaneous inactivation of both porins resulted in a significant increase in MICs for several β-lactams, particularly cephalosporins such as ceftriaxone, cefoxitin, and cefepime, as well as the carbapenems meropenem and ertapenem. Although these increases were reproducible and statistically significant, they remained below clinical resistance breakpoints for all compounds tested. No effect was observed for imipenem and trimetoprime.

Clinical studies have reported clinically relevant carbapenem and later-generation cephalosporin resistance associated with the combination of porin loss and expression of specific β-lactamases6,8,25. To test this interaction in our experimental system, we cloned eight β-lactamase genes from different families into the same expression vector (Fig. 1A and Fig. S2A) and measured their effects on meropenem and cefepime susceptibility in both the parental strain and porin-deficient backgrounds (Fig. 1C).

Under our standardized expression conditions, only the carbapenemases NDM-1 and KPC-2 conferred meropenem resistance exceeding the susceptible clinical breakpoint in the parental strain. In the ΔompCF background, expression of these enzymes resulted in a further increase in meropenem MIC, crossing the resistance breakpoint, confirming that moderate carbapenemase activity can be amplified by porin loss. Notably, our condition of expression of OXA-48, which had little effect on meropenem susceptibility in the parental background, was sufficient in the ΔompCF mutant to reach the resistance breakpoint for both meropenem and cefepime. Similarly, expression of the cephalosporinases AmpCCMH4 and CMY-2 in the porin-deficient background resulted in meropenem MIC exceeding the susceptible breakpoint. Expression of CTX-M-15 in porin-deficient mutants led to cefepime resistance without affecting meropenem susceptibility (Fig. 1C).

β-lactamase inhibitors are essential components of our arsenal to fight against strains producing β-lactamase. We examined whether porin inactivation affected the activity of β-lactamase inhibitors by assessing susceptibility in the presence of clavulanic acid, avibactam, and vaborbactam. Under our experimental conditions, loss of the two major porins did not significantly alter inhibitor activity, suggesting that porin inactivation does not substantially impair the diffusion or efficacy of these compounds (Fig. S2B).

Phages T4 and Bas 47 use porins and LPS to infect E. coli MG1655

To target both major outer membrane porins and lipopolysaccharides, we used two virulent bacteriophages with complementary receptor specificities. We selected the well-characterised phage T4 and the recently isolated phage Bas47 from the Basel phage collection14. Phage T4 is a member of the Tevenvirinae subfamily, genus Tequatrovirus, and has been extensively characterised in E. coli26. Under our experimental conditions, phage T4 efficiently infected E. coli MG1655 and nearly eliminated the bacterial population within 90 min when introduced at a multiplicity of infection of 0.1 (Fig. 2B). We next confirmed the receptor usage of phage T4 by measuring adsorption and plaque formation on isogenic deletion mutants. Deletion of ompC or disruption of inner-core LPS biosynthesis, resulting in a deep rough LPS phenotype (ΔgalU), markedly impaired phage adsorption (Fig. 2C) and reduced plaque formation by approximately three orders of magnitude (Fig. 2D). Consistently, in liquid culture, inactivation of either ompC or galU was sufficient to prevent phage adsorption and allowed bacterial growth in the presence of phage T4 (Fig. 2E). No plaques were detected on the double mutant ΔompCΔgalU, indicating that both OmpC and core LPS are required for efficient infection. In contrast, phage T4 formed plaques with comparable efficiency on the ΔompF mutant and the wildtype strain, confirming that OmpF is dispensable for T4 entry (Fig. 2D).

To validate our experimental approach and extend our analysis to a phage targeting a different porin, we included phage Bas47 as a complementary model. Bas47, a recently isolated virulent phage14, is phylogenetically related to phage T4 but belongs to a distinct genus, Mosigvirus. In our experimental conditions, Bas47 efficiently killed E. coli MG1655 (Fig. 2B). Adsorption and plaque assays (Fig. 2C–D) demonstrated that Bas47 relies on OmpF, but not OmpC, for productive infection, as plaque formation was abolished in the ΔompF mutant while remaining unaffected in the ΔompC background (Fig. 2D). As observed for phage T4, Bas47 infection was also impaired in the ΔgalU mutant and completely abolished in the ΔompFΔgalU double mutant (Fig. 2D). This requirement for LPS may reflect a role for LPS as a co-receptor or an indirect effect on OmpF assembly and surface exposure in the deep rough LPS background40. Consistent with these findings, inactivation of either ompF or galU was sufficient to prevent Bas47 adsorption (Fig. 2C) and allowed bacterial growth in liquid culture in the presence of phage Bas47 (Fig. 2E).

In addition to direct inactivation of porins or LPS biosynthesis genes, resistance could also arise through mutations in regulatory systems controlling porin expression, such as the EnvZ–OmpR two-component system27,28. However, because loss of major porins or deep alterations of LPS structure can impose fitness costs, different selective regimes may favour distinct evolutionary solutions. To obtain a comprehensive view of the genetic diversity underlying resistance to phage T4, we isolated resistant clones upon the application of three independent selection protocols designed to impose different constraints (Fig. 3B): (i) direct selection of spontaneous resistant mutants on phage T4-covered agar plates (T protocol), (ii) selection of motile resistant clones in semi-solid medium containing phage T4 (S protocol, Fig. S3), and (iii) short-term co-evolution in liquid culture with daily bottleneck passages for four days (E protocol, Fig. S4). We then characterised phage resistant mutants using a stepwise analytical pipeline that included targeted genotyping of the ompC locus, phenotypic assessment of resistance to phage T4 and cross-resistance to phage Bas47 as a proxy for alterations affecting LPS or OmpF, followed by whole-genome sequencing of a representative subset of clones (Fig. 3A).

We hypothesised that the T protocol, which primarily selects for survival, would favour null mutations in ompC or LPS biosynthesis genes, whereas the S and E protocols might enrich for mutations with lower fitness costs, such as regulatory or missense mutations, due to additional selection pressures linked to motility or competitive growth.

We first analysed the ompC locus in 96 independent T clones. Among these, 59 clones (approximately 62%) carried mutations in ompC, 48 of which (approximately 85%) were nonsense mutations predicted to abolish ompC expression (Fig. 3C). Analysis of 17 independently isolated S clones revealed a similar pattern, with 10 clones (approximately 59%) harbouring ompC mutations, including 9 nonsense mutations (Fig. 3C). For the E protocol, two clones were analysed per lineage from six independent co-evolution experiments. Eleven of the twelve clones examined (approximately 91%) carried nonsense mutations in ompC. In five of the six lineages, the two analysed clones carried distinct ompC mutations, indicating independent emergence and fixation of resistance mutations under sustained phage pressure.

We next selected representative clones from each protocol, covering the full spectrum of identified ompC mutations as well as clones retaining the parental allele, and confirmed their resistance to phage T4 in liquid culture (Fig. 3D; Fig. S5). To assess cross-resistance phenotypes, potentially indicative of additional alterations affecting LPS or OmpF, we tested the same clones for susceptibility to phage Bas47 (Fig. 3D; Fig. S5). Cross-resistance to Bas47 was most frequently observed among clones isolated using the T protocol, where approximately 60% of clones displayed resistance to both phages, compared with 30% for S clones and 41% for E clones (Table S1). Notably, several cross-resistant clones carried ompC mutations that do not impair Bas47 infection, suggesting the presence of additional mutations outside the ompC locus. Conversely, some clones retained the wild-type ompC allele, indicating that resistance likely arose through mutations affecting LPS biosynthesis or porin regulation.

Whole-genome sequencing of a subset of T, S, and E clones (Table S1) confirmed that cross-resistance to Bas47 was consistently associated with mutations in genes involved in inner-core LPS biosynthesis, such as galU and waaG, but not in genes involved in outer-core or side-chain assembly, such as waaJ or waaO (Fig. 3E; Table S1). In T clones, resistance mutations were restricted to ompC and LPS biosynthesis genes, and missense mutations in ompC were frequently accompanied by additional mutations affecting LPS synthesis. In contrast, S clones also harboured mutations in the EnvZ–OmpR regulatory system controlling porin expression. In E clones, cross-resistance additionally arose through mutations in the Rcs-phosphorelay system, which regulates colanic acid synthesis29.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that resistance to phage T4 and cross-resistance to phage Bas47 consistently emerge through alterations in surface-exposed envelope components, with porins and inner-core LPS biosynthesis representing the dominant genetic targets. Despite the diversity of evolutionary trajectories imposed by distinct selection regimes, phage resistance repeatedly converged on functionally connected pathways shaping outer membrane permeability. More complex selection environments further favoured regulatory mutations affecting surface antigen expression, highlighting the central role of envelope architecture in bacterial adaptation to phage predation.

Phage-driven selection potentiates β-lactam resistance in specific genetic backgrounds

To assess whether phage-driven selection could influence antibiotic resistance, we measured the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of meropenem in 20 T clones, 17 S clones, and 12 E clones using broth microdilution with 1.5-fold serial dilutions (Fig. 4A). Meropenem was selected because its activity is robustly affected by porin loss under our liquid culture conditions (Fig. 1D). Overall, most phage-resistant mutants displayed only marginal changes in meropenem MIC compared to the wild-type strain (Fig. 4A). However, because clinically relevant resistance to meropenem typically requires a combination of reduced outer membrane permeability and β-lactamase expression (Fig. 1E), we next tested whether expression of the AmpC-type β-lactamase CMY-2 altered susceptibility in these genetic backgrounds.

Expression of CMY-2 resulted in increased meropenem MIC in five mutants relative to the ancestral strain expressing the same enzyme (Fig. 4A). A comparable increase was observed when these five mutants expressed the OXA-48 β-lactamase (Fig. 4A), indicating that the resistance phenotype was not restricted to a single β-lactamase family. Further susceptibility testing using E-test strips revealed that these five clones also exhibited altered resistance profiles to cefoxitin and ertapenem upon CMY-2 expression (Fig. S6B). Notably, all five clones showed a modest but consistent increase in susceptibility to colistin (Fig. S6B), consistent with alterations of outer membrane integrity.

Phenotypic scoring against phages T4 and Bas47 revealed that all five clones displaying increased β-lactam resistance were cross-resistant to both phages (Fig. S6C). Whole-genome sequencing confirmed that these clones carried a combination of mutations inactivating ompC together with mutations in LPS inner-core biosynthesis genes, resulting in a deep-rough LPS phenotype (Fig. 4B). No such combination was detected in phage-resistant clones that did not show increased antibiotic MICs.

Together, these results demonstrate that while phage resistance alone is generally insufficient to confer clinically meaningful carbapenem resistance, phage-driven selection can generate specific envelope-altered genetic backgrounds that potentiate β-lactam resistance when β-lactamases are expressed. In particular, the coincident disruption of OmpC and LPS inner-core biosynthesis emerges as a key determinant enabling this synergistic resistance phenotype.

Genetic basis of the combined resistance to phage T4, phage Bas47 and β-lactams

To confirm these findings and directly test our model (Fig. 5A), in which inactivation of ompC combined with impaired LPS inner-core synthesis leads to reduced porin-mediated permeability, we constructed defined mutants carrying clean deletions of genes identified during the selection experiments. Specifically, we generated deletions in waaG and waaC, which are involved in LPS inner-core synthesis, and in waaO, which contributes to outer-core biosynthesis, both in the parental background and in a ∆ompC background. We then measured meropenem susceptibility in these strains, with or without expression of the β-lactamases CMY-2 or OXA-48 (Fig. 5B). Deletion of waaC or waaG alone was sufficient to increase the meropenem MIC in strains expressing OXA-48. In the ∆ompC background, deletion of waaG or waaC, but not waaO, resulted in a marked increase in meropenem MIC when either CMY-2 or OXA-48 was expressed. These results indicate that disruption of LPS inner-core biosynthesis, but not outer-core modification, synergizes with the loss of OmpC to potentiate β-lactam resistance.

Because waaG mutations were recurrently identified in phage-selected clones, we further examined whether this genetic combination affected susceptibility to other β-lactam antibiotics. We found that deletion of waaG, when combined with ompC inactivation and CMY-2 expression, significantly increased resistance to cefepime and ertapenem (Fig. 5C). Together, these data confirm that the combined alteration of porin expression and LPS inner-core biosynthesis is sufficient to recreate the resistance phenotypes observed in phage-selected mutants, thereby validating the genetic basis of the phage-driven β-lactam resistance model.

Discussion

Bacteriophages are major drivers of bacterial evolution, and their predation imposes strong selective pressure on surface antigens that are essential for host recognition and infection. In Gram-negative bacteria such as E. coli, phages frequently rely on outer membrane components, including LPS and porins, for attachment. These structures also play central roles in environmental adaptation, immune recognition, nutrient uptake, and envelope stress responses11,31. In addition, LPS and porins critically influence antibiotic diffusion across the outer membrane, LPS through its physicochemical barrier properties and surface charge, and porins by acting as entry channels10,32. Phage-driven selection targeting these receptors is therefore expected to have pleiotropic consequences, including coincidental effects on antibiotic susceptibility.

Here, we show that selection for resistance to phage T4, which requires both the major porin OmpC and the LPS inner core for adsorption, can promote the emergence of genetic backgrounds that favour increased β-lactam resistance when combined with the expression of specific β-lactamases. Phage-selected variants frequently exhibited cross-resistance to phage Bas47, which targets overlapping surface structures, indicating convergent evolutionary trajectories affecting the outer membrane. Whole-genome sequencing revealed that this cross-resistance was associated with combined mutations in ompC and genes involved in LPS inner-core biosynthesis, such as galU and waaG. These mutations prevent adsorption of both phages and simultaneously alter envelope properties that modulate antibiotic permeability.

Importantly, the antibiotic resistance phenotypes observed in this study remain modest in absolute terms. Even when β-lactamases such as CMY-2 or OXA-48 were expressed, the resulting increases in meropenem or cefepime MIC did not reach the clinical breakpoints typically associated with high-level resistance and were substantially lower than those observed with strong β-lactamase expression systems or clinical resistance plasmids. However, these phage-selected genetic backgrounds clearly potentiate the impact of β-lactamase activity and therefore represent permissive or predisposing states that could facilitate the subsequent acquisition of clinically significant resistance under additional selective pressures. Such backgrounds may be particularly relevant in environments where β-lactamase genes are readily acquired through horizontal gene transfer, as is the case for bla genes of the AmpC and OXA-48 families3,8,33–35.

The mechanistic basis for this synergistic effect likely extends beyond simple porin loss. Disruption of LPS inner-core biosynthesis generates a deep-rough phenotype that is known to affect outer membrane integrity and protein assembly11,12,36,37. In this context, altered LPS structure may impair the proper folding, stability, or surface exposure of OmpF, the remaining major porin in ∆ompC strains. In addition, envelope perturbations caused by LPS defects are known to activate the RpoE (σE)-mediated stress response, which can downregulate porin expression as part of a broader protective program38,39. Although our data do not directly demonstrate σE activation, this regulatory pathway provides a plausible mechanism linking LPS alterations to reduced OmpF-mediated permeability and warrants further investigation.

Traditionally, reduced antibiotic permeability associated with LPS modifications has been attributed primarily to the presence or structural variation of the O-antigen10,40,41, which can sterically hinder antibiotic diffusion and increase resistance to hydrophobic compounds. In contrast, the laboratory strain used here lacks the O-antigen, allowing us to uncover an alternative mechanism in which LPS inner-core perturbations indirectly reduce permeability by modulating porin function and regulation. Our results, therefore, suggest that LPS can influence antibiotic susceptibility not only through direct barrier effects but also through its impact on porin assembly and expression.

In E. coli, the host range and resistance to virulent phages are generally constrained by receptor availability at the cell surface15. Additional bacterial defence mechanisms can also contribute to phage resistance42. In recent years, a rapidly expanding diversity of anti-phage defence systems has been described, including restriction–modification, abortive infection, CRISPR–Cas systems, and numerous newly identified immunity modules43. How the presence, diversity, and regulation of these defence systems influence the evolutionary dynamics of phage resistance remains incompletely understood17. Importantly, whether selection acting on intracellular phage defence mechanisms can also generate collateral effects on antibiotic susceptibility, either directly or through associated fitness trade-offs, remains largely unexplored. Integrating receptor-based resistance with the growing landscape of bacterial defence systems will be essential to fully understand how phage pressure shapes bacterial evolution and its unintended consequences for antimicrobial resistance.

These findings have important implications for phage therapy. While phages are increasingly considered as promising alternatives or complements to antibiotics, particularly against multidrug-resistant pathogens, our data indicate that prolonged or poorly controlled phage exposure may inadvertently select for envelope modifications that facilitate antibiotic resistance under certain conditions. This risk is likely to be context-dependent and influenced by the nature of the phage receptors, the bacterial genetic background, and the availability of antibiotic resistance determinants. Importantly, the evolutionary trade-offs associated with phage resistance, including fitness costs, altered virulence, or increased susceptibility to other antimicrobials such as colistin, underscore the need for careful evaluation of phage–host interactions in therapeutic settings.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that phage-driven selection can generate specific envelope-altered genetic backgrounds that potentiate β-lactam resistance without directly conferring high-level resistance on their own. The plasticity of the outer membrane, and particularly the interplay between LPS structure, porin regulation, and stress response pathways, emerges as a central factor shaping these evolutionary outcomes. A deeper understanding of these mechanisms will be essential for designing phage-based interventions that minimize unintended consequences and effectively complement existing antimicrobial strategies.

Methods

Bacteria and phage propagation

All the bacterial strains used in this study have been propagated using Lysogenic broth (LB) Miller formulation from Difco. Bacterial strains were stocked and inoculated in and from 15% glycerol frozen stocks. LB supplemented with 15 g/L bacto-agar from Difco was used as solid medium; soft medium was formed using 7.5 g of bacto-agar and swimming medium with 2.8 g. When needed for selection kanamycin (100 µg/ml), chloramphenicol (30 µg/ml) or ampicillin (100 µg/ml) was added to the media. Bacteriophages were stocked directly on the LB at 4°C, when needed phage population was amplified on our wt sensitive bacterial strain. All phages and bacteria strain used in this study are listed Table S2.

Gene inactivation using site specific homologous recombination

Deletion mutants of the porin genes and genes involved in LPS synthesis were constructed using site-specific recombination mediated by the λ-Red system, as described by Datsenko and Wanner44. The primers listed in Table S2 were used to amplify resistance cassettes via PCR using the pkD3 or pKD4 vector as matrices, generating linear DNA fragments containing homology regions for recombination. This linear DNA was introduced into bacteria expressing the λ-Red system carried by the pKD46 vector by electroporation, and recombinant bacteria were selected on the appropriate antibiotic-containing media. Resistance markers were removed using FRT recombination mediated by the expression of the Flp recombinase by the pCP20 vector, as described in the same study.

Phage and bacteria growth dynamic in liquid

The overnight cultures were diluted 1000-fold in fresh LB medium to a final volume of 5 mL to reach approximately 2–3 × 10⁶ CFU/mL. 2 × 10⁵ PFU/mL was added to achieve a MOI of 0.1. At each time point, 2 samples of 200 to 500 µL were collected. The first sample, to quantify phages, was thoroughly mixed with chloroform at a 10:1 ratio and pelleted for 5 min. The supernatant was recovered and serial dilution were spotted on a soft-agar comprising a lawn of a phage sensitive strain. The second sample, to quantify bacteria, was centrifugated at 14,000 rpm for 5 min, the pallet was washed twice with of a solution of MgSO₄ at 10⁻² M and the number of CFU was then determined by spotting on LB agar plates.

Phage sensitivity measure by plaque assay on soft-agar and phage titration

Phages preparations were serially diluted 10-fold, and 5 µL of each dilution was dropped onto soft-agar inoculated from overnight cultures (20-fold dilution) with either the sensitive strain for titration or the tested strain to for sensitivity assessment. The number and appearance of plaque-forming units were recorded after incubation overnight at 37°C.

Phage adsorption assay

Bacterial cultures were grown at 37 °C on flasks at an OD600 of 0.4 ( ≈ 108CFU/ml) and a control group containing only growth medium were infected with phage at an MOI of 0.001. Infected cultures were kept shaking at 37 and 500 µl samples were collected every 2 min and mix 10:1 with chloroform. The samples were vortexed thoroughly for 30 s and then pelleted for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was serially diluted and spotted on a soft-agar comprising a lawn of a phage sensitive strain.

Bacterial phage sensitivity profile assayed in microtiter plates

Growth kinetics reflecting the sensitivity to phages were performed on microplates. Overnight cultures were diluted 10000-fold in LB medium containing or not ≈ 104 phage to obtain an MOI of 0.1. After addition of 20 µl of the mineral oil plates were incubated at 37°C with orbital shaking in an Infinite® M Nano plate reader and OD600 readings were acquired every 5 min for 16 h.

Subcloning of genes encoding β-lactamases

In order to normalize the expression of the β-lactamases across our conditions, we subcloned them in the pTRC99K vector which derived from the pTRC99A vector and was used already for this purpose to expressed KPC-245,46. All the constructs are listed Table S2, all the new constructs were made using standard restriction/digestion-based cloning of PCR fragments. The PCR fragments including the genes coding β-lactamase were obtained upon amplification of genomic DNA from the NILS collection47.

Selection of T4 resistant mutants

The selection of the clones T was done as follow: a total of 96 Independent bacterial cultures were inoculated with up to 10 bacteria and let grow overnight. 5 mL of soft agar was inoculated with ≈108 bacteria and ≈ 10⁹PFU of phage T4. The soft agar was then poured onto LB plate. A Random colony was picked on plates after overnight incubation at 37 °C. To assure randomness, plates were marked by a marker spot before incubation, and the closest growing colony from the spot was chosen. The selection of the S clones was done using “dual” swimming squared agar plates consisting on a swimming area without phage and another with phage T4. To create such plates swimming agar was first was poured onto LB agar plate. The LB agar is serving as support to empty two-thirds of the area of the plates from both agar media. Then, Swimming agar seeded with 109 PFU/ml of phage T4 was poured in the emptied space. From independent overnight cultures, up to 10 bacteria were seeded in the swimming area without phage. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 3 to 7 days, and random sampling was performed by collecting bacteria from soft-agar and plating them on LB agar. From these plates a random colony was selected as for the T mutants. The coevolution procedure consisted on starting 6 independent bacterial culture by seeding up to 10 bacteria from an overnight culture in 5 ml of LB. upon reaching ≈ 108 CFU/ml the cultures were inoculated with the phage T4 at an MOI ≈ 0.1 and. Daily, for 4 days the bacteria-phage culture was propagated by 100-fold dilution in fresh LB medium. On the final day, after titrating the bacteria, a random two random colonies were selected as for the T mutants.

Antibiotic resistance assay using microdilution or E-test strip

We used either Microdilution or E-test to assess variation in the MIC of our strains. For microdilution, overnight cultures were diluted 10000-fold in LB medium and dispensed microtiter plate. Serial dilution of the tested antibiotic was added to the well to for a gradient concentration. Microtiter plates were incubated in at 37°C and upon 900 rpm agitation on a 1.5 mm orbital plate shaker after addition of 10 µl mineral oil. After 18 h of incubation, the OD₆₀₀ was measured using a Synergy H1 plate reader form Biotek. For the E-test strip the procedure recommended by the manufacturer (Biomérieux) was applied in order to obtain a confluent lawn of bacteria. E-test readings were done after 18 h incubation. When relevant, Kanamycin was added to ensure conservation of the pTRC vector.

Whole genome sequencing and analysis

Bacterial DNA was extracted from the glycerol stocks or overnight culture using the genomic DNA NucleoMag 96 tissue kit from Macherey-Nagel. The whole genome libraries were prepared and indexed using ILMN DNA (M) Tagmentation kit with IDT for Illumina DNA/RNA UD Indexes kit B. The sequencing was performed using Illumina paired-end sequencing 2x150bp on MiniSeq Platform. Illumina reads alignment to the reference genome (NC_000913.3) and mutation calling was performed using breseq pipeline 48.

Sanger sequencing

The amplified products or plasmids were checked using Sanger sequencing by Eurofins. Sample purification was performed using Wizard® SV Gel and PCR Clean-Up or Wizard® Plus SV Miniprep DNA purification.
