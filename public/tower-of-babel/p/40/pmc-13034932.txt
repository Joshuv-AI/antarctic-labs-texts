Prevalence and risk of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease in rheumatoid arthritis: Systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Sawai Singh Rathore, K.T. Mohammed Rizwan, Ogechukwu Samuel Obi, Shravya Krishna Kokkula, Dawn Adams, Gredeline Nhomme Jeudy, Laura Cicani, Ifeanyi Ononuju, Tahera Ahmadi, Bijay Mukesh Jeswani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease known for its systemic inflammatory effects and associated comorbidities. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) has been increasingly recognized as a significant comorbidity in RA patients. This meta-analysis aims to quantify the prevalence and relative risk of COPD in patients with RA compared to the general population.Methods:A systematic search of PubMed, Embase, and Google Scholar was conducted until April 30th, 2024. Studies were selected based on predefined inclusion criteria, focusing on those reporting data on COPD in RA patients. Random-effects models were used to estimate pooled prevalence rate and risk ratios, along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), to report the overall effect size. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. Statistical analyses were conducted using Review Manager and MedCalc software, with results pooled using the Mantel-Haenszel random-effects model. Heterogeneity was assessed using I2 statistics, and publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots, Egger regression, and Begg rank correlation tests.Results:Twenty-four studies with a combined population of 1,710,600 individuals were included. The pooled prevalence of COPD in RA patients was 7.06% (95% CI 4.56–10.13). Subgroup analysis showed a prevalence of 6.36% in Asia and 7.1% in the studies from the rest of the world. RA patients had a significantly higher risk of developing COPD compared to the general population, with an risk ratios of 1.58 (95% CI 1.37–1.82, P < .0001). The relative risk in Asian populations was 1.61 (95% CI, 1.19–2.18, P < .0001) compared to 1.56 (95% CI, 1.24–1.97, P < .0001) in studies from the rest of the world. According to Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, most studies were of high or moderate quality. According to Egger regression and Begg rank correlation tests, all analyses were free of publication bias.Conclusion:This meta-analysis offers strong evidence that individuals with RA are at a significantly higher risk of developing COPD. These findings emphasize the need for regular screening for COPD in RA patients and the implementation of proactive management strategies to reduce this risk. Further research is required to fully understand this relationship.

1. Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, inflammatory autoimmune disease that mainly affects the joints, causing pain, swelling, and potential joint damage. However, RA’s systemic nature means it can also have significant effects on various organs and systems in the body.[1] One such connection that has received increasing attention in recent years is the association between RA and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).[2] COPD is a progressive lung disease characterized by airflow limitation, chronic bronchitis, and emphysema, and it remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. This disease process with its acute exacerbations and complications, often impacts the quality of life of the patient.

The coexistence of RA and COPD presents a complex clinical challenge with significant implications for patient management and outcomes. Both diseases share common risk factors, such as smoking, and exhibit overlapping pathogenic mechanisms, including chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation.[3] Despite the growing recognition of the association between RA and COPD, the exact prevalence and relative risk (RR) of COPD in patients with RA remain underexplored. Previous studies have reported varying prevalence rates and risk estimates, often limited by differences in study design, population characteristics, and regional healthcare practices.

This meta-analysis aims to review and analyze the currently available studies to look for an association between RA and COPD. The goals in this study were: to estimate the prevalence of COPD in people with RA, and to calculate the RR of developing COPD among people with RA. Through this meta-analysis, we aim to gain insightful information about the direct correlation between RA and the development of COPD.

2. Methods

We carefully reviewed, analyzed, and prepared our manuscript following all the required Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis guidelines.[4]

2.1. Search strategy

A systematic search was done with PubMed, Embase, and Google Scholar until April 30th, 2024. We combined Medical Subject Headings terms and keywords. Subsequent search terms were ([pulmonary disease] or [chronic obstructive] or [pulmonary] or [disease] or [chronic] or [obstructive] or [chronic obstructive pulmonary disease] or [copd] or [emphysema] or [chronic bronchitis] and [arthritis] or [rheumatoid] or [arthritis] or [rheumatoid] or [rheumatoid arthritis] orand [prevalence] or [incidence] or [risk] or [association] or [comorbidity]). With the help of appropriate Boolean operators, studies were retrieved from all over the world. We manually reviewed the searched articles based on their titles and abstracts to select the qualifying studies. Duplicate citations were eliminated by importing the studies to Endnote X9 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia). The PRISMA flow diagram is depicted in Figure 1.

PRISMA 2009 flow diagram.[4] PRISMA = preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analysis, n = number of records.

2.2. Eligibility criteria

All qualified studies were included in this meta-analysis. To be competent for this meta-analysis, the article must fulfill the subsequent inclusion criteria: studies about COPD in RA; multicenter and observational studies were included, irrespective of the patient’s age, gender, and ethnicity. The exclusion requirements were established beforehand: if no data regarding COPD patients in RA was given, animal studies, reviews, letters to editors, duplicate publications, commentaries, posters, case series, non-English publications, and studies with incomplete information on smokers/alcoholics or clinical outcomes.

2.3. Study selection and data extraction

Two independent reviewers individually screened the studies for eligibility based on titles and abstracts, with a third reviewer’s help in case of disagreements. Information from these screened studies, such as author name, publication year, study design, sample size of the population, number of people in intervention and control groups, patient’s age, gender, associated comorbidities, medications used, and their respective P values for each variable, was extracted. The data extraction was done independently and cross-checked by 4 different reviewers.

2.4. Quality assessment

The Newcastle-Ottawa tool and the Cochrane risk of bias tool were used to ascertain the quality of our observational and randomized controlled trial studies, respectively. Two authors independently utilized the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale to assess the individual quality of every study. The following sections were rated per study: low bias risk (8–9 points), moderate bias risk (5–7 points), and high bias risk (0–4 points).

2.5. Statistical analysis

Review Manager (version 5.4; Copenhagen: The Nordic Cochrane Centre, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2014 software) and MedCalc Statistical Software version 19.6.4 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium; https://www.medcalc.org; 2021) were utilized for all statistical analyses. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were derived for dichotomous outcomes and pooled using the Mantel-Haenszel random-effects model. To represent our data visually, we employed forest plots for our analysis reports and funnel plots for any publication bias in our study. All P values < .05 were considered statistically significant. The I2 statistics were used to assess the heterogeneity of effect size estimates across these studies (low heterogeneity: I2 ≤ 25%; moderate: 25–70%; high: > 75%). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was employed in studies to decrease the heterogeneity.

After taking into consideration the title and abstract, 1071 articles were further excluded, leaving 107 papers that could probably be considered for this analysis. After extensive reviewing and filtering through the inclusion criteria, 24 articles with a study population of 1,710,600 were included in this study.[4–27] The included population had a median age of 55.05 years, with 69.96% being female. 3.2. Meta-analysis for prevalence of COPD in RA

The overall pooled random-effects estimate of the prevalence of COPD in patients with RA disease across studies was 7.06% (95% CI, 4.56–10.13). Test statistics results revealed high heterogeneity (I2 = 99.93%, P < .0001). This effect was pooled from 24 reports incorporating 697,704 patients with COPD and RA (Fig. 2).

Forest plot for prevalence of COPD in RA. COPD = chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, RA = rheumatoid arthritis.

Subgroup analysis was done according to the country’s region of origin. The pooled random effect estimate of prevalence was found to be 6.36% in Asia compared with 7.1% worldwide.

3.3. Meta-analysis for RR of COPD in RA

Meta-analysis results indicated that patients with RA had a higher risk of developing COPD when compared to patients without RA, with statistical analysis showing significant results with RR = 1.58, 95% CI 1.37–1.82, P < .0001. This outcome had high associated heterogeneity (I2 = 97.51%). The result was based on data from 253,498 RA patients (Fig. 3).

Forest plot for RR of COPD in RA. CI = confidence interval, COPD = chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, RA = rheumatoid arthritis, RR = relative risk.

Subgroup analysis was conducted based on the country of origin. The RR ratio for the development of COPD in RA was 1.61 (95% CI 1.19–2.18, P < .0001) in Asia, compared with 1.57 (95% CI 1.24–1.97, P < .0001) in studies from around the world (Fig. 4).

Forest plot for RR of COPD in subgroup analysis according to region: Asia and rest of the world. CI = confidence interval, COPD = chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, M-H = Mantel-Haenszel, RA = rheumatoid arthritis, RR = relative risk.

3.4. Risk of bias assessment

The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale was used to assess the risk of bias and quality of incorporated case-control and cohort studies. Out of 20 studies, 8 were of high quality, and 12 were of moderate quality, with an average score of 7.2. Collectively, the evidence employed in these analyses was ascertained as being of good quality.

3.5. Publication bias

Visual inspection of the standard funnel plots for all the analyses was identified as having moderate symmetry. Correspondingly, the evaluation of publication bias was also escorted with the help of Egger regression test and Begg-Mazumdar rank correlation test. For both of these tests, P < .05 was considered significant, and the analysis was considered to have publication bias. No evident publication bias was witnessed for most analyses done in this study. Hence, this analysis was considered to be almost without publication bias.

4. Discussion

In this meta-analysis, we examined the frequency and RR of COPD in patients with RA. Our investigation included data from 24 studies involving 1,710,600 individuals. The combined random-effects estimate showed that 7.06% of RA patients developed COPD (95% CI, 4.56–10.13). Further analysis revealed a slightly lower prevalence of COPD in RA patients in Asia (6.36%) compared to the rest of the world (7.1%). Various studies have reported different rates of COPD among RA patients.

The pooled estimate of COPD in patients with RA found in our study was 7.06%, which is comparable to the prevalence of COPD in RA, which was found in other studies. A United States-based cohort study that analyzed Medicare claims data from > 500,000 patients between 2008 and 2017 observed a prevalence of interstitial lung disease in RA of 2.0% and an overall prevalence of approximately 5.0%, with the majority of the patients developing RA-interstitial lung disease during the analysis period.[28] In another study of the prevalence of COPD in RA, they found a result of 15.5% in men with RA and 3.5% in women with RA.[10] Furthermore, our meta-analysis revealed that RA patients are at a significantly higher risk of developing COPD compared to individuals without RA. The pooled RR ratio was 1.58 (95% CI, 1.37–1.82, P < .0001). Subgroup analysis by region demonstrated that the risk was marginally higher in Asian populations (RR = 1.61) compared to studies from the rest of the world (RR = 1.57). Previous studies have also reported similar results. For instance, a study by Ma et al found an increased risk for developing COPD in RA patients.[29]

The connection between RA and COPD is complex and involves systemic inflammation, genetic factors, and environmental influences. RA is characterized by ongoing systemic inflammation driven by inflammatory cytokines like tumor necrosis factor-alpha, interleukin-6, and interleukin-1. These cytokines not only cause joint damage in RA but also have systemic effects that can harm lung tissue.[30] Chronic inflammation can lead to structural changes in the lungs, such as airway remodeling, fibrosis, and emphysema, which are key features of COPD. Genetic factors play a significant role in susceptibility to both RA and COPD. There are shared genetic loci that increase the risk for both conditions. For instance, variations in the genes encoding matrix metalloproteinases and alpha-1 antitrypsin have been implicated in both diseases.[3] matrix metalloproteinases are involved in the breakdown of extracellular matrix components, contributing to the tissue remodeling and destruction seen in both RA and COPD.[31] Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, a known genetic risk factor for COPD, has also been associated with an increased risk of RA, indicating a shared genetic pathway that predisposes individuals to both conditions.[32] These genetic overlaps offer an explanation for the observed comorbidity between RA and COPD.

Environmental factors, especially smoking, further increase the risk of developing COPD in RA patients. Smoking is a well-established risk factor for both RA and COPD. It can worsen systemic inflammation, raise oxidative stress, and trigger autoimmunity, all of which can contribute to the development of both diseases. Additionally, smoking can hinder the function of immune cells, making individuals more susceptible to infections, which can in turn cause more damage to the lungs and speed up the progression of COPD.[33] The combined effects of smoking and the underlying inflammatory processes in RA can significantly raise the chances of developing COPD. This interaction underscores the importance of smoking cessation interventions in RA patients in order to lower the risk of COPD and improve overall health outcomes.

The findings highlight the significance of thorough screening for COPD in patients with RA. Due to the increased risk, healthcare providers should prioritize regular pulmonary function tests and proactive management approaches for RA patients, to reduce the occurrence and advancement of COPD. Moreover, it’s crucial to strongly promote smoking cessation programs in this group to lessen the impact of both RA and COPD.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in interpreting the findings of this meta-analysis. The included studies showed substantial variation due to differences in design, patient populations, and methodologies. Potential biases in observational studies cannot be fully ruled out, and publication bias may exist. The meta-analysis mainly included observational studies, limiting the ability to establish causality and control for potential confounders. Regional or ethnic variations may limit the findings.

Future research should focus on longitudinal studies to better understand the temporal relationship between RA and the development of COPD. Investigating the underlying mechanisms linking these diseases will also be crucial in developing targeted therapeutic interventions. Furthermore, future studies’ standardized diagnostic criteria and uniform reporting will enhance comparability and reduce heterogeneity in meta-analysis.

This meta-analysis presents strong evidence that patients with RA have a significantly higher risk of developing COPD. These findings emphasize the importance of being more vigilant in clinical settings and actively managing COPD in RA patients. Managing this coexisting condition can lead to better overall outcomes and an improved quality of life for individuals dealing with RA.
