Physical activity influences immunity with an assist from the gut microbiome
Authors: David C Nieman
Published: 2025

In a series of experiments, Phelps et al.1 provided novel data linking moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), gut microbiota composition changes and the release of the short chain fatty acid (SCFA) formate, and enhanced antitumor immunity via the transcription factor Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway in cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. These data support the growing awareness that the clinical benefits of MVPA are achieved at least in part through enhanced immunity with support from the gut microbiome.

Acute and chronic physical activity has a substantial effect on the immune system.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 An acute bout of MVPA induces an increase in tissue macrophage function and circulating levels of natural killer cells, neutrophils, monocytes, dendritic cells, naïve B cells, and killer T cells (CD8+ T cells). Over time, regular MVPA at recommended workloads improves immunosurveillance and is an underlying factor in lowered incidence rates of respiratory infections, heart disease and some types of cancer.5

Mechanisms explaining the linkage between MVPA-induced perturbations in immunity and cancer have been explored in numerous cell culture and animal studies.7, 8, 9 No definitive consensus has been reached, but MVPA may operate through several immune and physiological pathways to hinder cancer development and progression. These include an enhanced interchange of tumor suppression-related immune cells between the lymphoid tissues and other body tissues and the circulation, lowered inflammation and oxidative stress, improved DNA repair capacity, regular surges in body temperature, blood flow, and oxygen delivery to tumors, changes in the hormonal milieu, and altered cell signaling pathways.7, 8, 9

There are limited data that MVPA may influence cancer risk and progression by altering gut barrier function and microbiome composition.10,11 Some but not all studies support positive MVPA-related changes in the gut microbiome composition.12,13 These changes include the growth of specific beneficial bacteria and an increase in the production of metabolites and SCFAs that exert anti-inflammatory and immune-regulatory effects.

Metabolites generated from the gut microbiome can influence intestinal immunity and reenter the body and influence host immunity.14 These metabolites include SCFAs and those produced from bile acids and tryptophan, and help modulate immune responses, support intestinal barrier function, and regulate inflammation.15,16 SCFAs are the most abundant gut-derived metabolites and influence epithelial barrier gene expression, and in a complex interaction regulate innate immune cells such as macrophages, neutrophils and dendritic cells, and T- and B-cell adaptive immunity.17 Emerging evidence supports that specific metabolites secreted by the gut microbiota help regulate immune cells within the tumor microenvironment and thus influence cancer treatment efficacy.18,19

Formate functions as a central intermediate metabolite in one-carbon metabolism (1C).20 1C reactions support nucleotide synthesis, amino acid metabolism, and epigenetic modifications. Formate can be generated in the liver from serine, glycine, methionine, choline, and methanol. About half of the endogenous pool of formate is produced in the colon by the action of bacteria on fiber substrates. Thus, formate is also classified as a minor short chain fatty acid. Dietary patterns influence blood levels of formate and precursors include dietary amino acids, choline, fruit pectin, aspartame, creatine, and caffeine.20 Dietary folate deficiency results in a decreased production of formate, and vitamin B12 deficiency has been linked to increased formate levels.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) are a type of immunotherapy that block proteins involved in inhibiting T cells from attacking cancer cells. ICI efficacy varies across different cancers and is most effective against tumors with a high number of mutations such as melanoma, lung cancer, bladder cancer, and some lymphomas. Research suggests that exercise can potentially enhance the effectiveness of ICIs by boosting the immune response and improving the tumor microenvironment. Some studies indicate that exercise can increase the infiltration of immune cells into tumors, which could make them more susceptible to ICI treatment.21

As emphasized by the authors, additional research is needed to determine the most effective exercise protocols to maximize the complex interactions between MVPA, the immune system, the gut microbiome, and cancer prevention and treatment. This study utilized a preclinical melanoma model, and additional studies are needed to determine if the underlying mechanisms revealed in this study apply to other types of cancer.
