What Is a Digital Twin in QSP, and Are We Doing It Right?
Authors: Justin Feigelman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe field of Quantitative Systems Pharmacology continues to innovate new methods to derive insights from clinical data. In the past few years, Digital Twins have emerged as a “new” way of deriving patient‐specific model parameterizations to inform possible outcomes for novel scenarios using limited clinical data. Here, we explore the meaning of Digital Twins for QSP, its relationship with definitions in other contexts, and principles guiding their efficient and correct generation using patient data.Keywords: Bayesian parameter inference, digital twins, quantitative systems pharmacology

Digital Twins seem to be the newest innovation in the field of quantitative systems pharmacology modeling. A literature search for the terms “digital twin” and “QSP” reveals over 100 publications applying or describing this methodology since 2024. But what is a digital twin, and is it something new? The term seems to take on different meanings according to the context. For example, in the fields of engineering or medical devices, a digital twin might represent an in silico model and parameterization that recapitulates a device's behavior, for example, motion or electrical current, with high fidelity [1]. In this case, the model and parameterization are thought to capture the system well enough that novel predictions can be made with great accuracy. In physiology, digital twins might reflect the incorporation of patient‐specific parameters such as weight or cardiac output that play an important role of in the prediction of derived quantities and physiological measures of interest [2]. Unlike engineered systems, digital twins for patients will typically require patient‐specific parameterizations due to intrinsic variability. Thus, while a QSP model itself may not be a digital twin, a digital twin can be constructed by combining the model with patient‐specific parameter values derived or estimated from patient data.

Digital twins have been developed and applied for various physiological settings including cancer, cardiology, respiratory and more [2]. These digital twins reflect individual patient physiology and are typically applied in a medical setting for determining the course of action or predicting response to therapy. Recently, digital twins have also been used for predicting counterfactual clinical trajectories for patients as if they had not been treated, that is, for a patient‐specific placebo control group used to estimate the benefit of treatment [3]. A common element of digital twins in these contexts is that they are updated as new data are gathered; indeed, this updating step is a key part of the NASEM definition for “digital twin” [2]. Model predictions are compared with the new data, and the parameters or latent states can be adjusted such that the new digital twin parameterization more accurately reflects the results, constituting a form of “online learning” (Figure 1A). Similar methods are frequently applied in diverse fields including communications, robotics, feedback control and others to update the internal state of the model based on newly obtained data, see e.g., [4] for a comprehensive overview of related methods and applications.

Comparison of methods for model parameterization. (A) Digital twins use an initial estimate of the parameters θ0 and model state variables X0 and subsequently update the model estimates using new data in an online setting to obtain a new estimate of θ and X at each timepoint T 1, …, T N . (B) ABC compares simulation outputs for a collection of parameter samples θ1,θ2,…,θK with the observed data D. Parameter samples for which the distance metric ψθiD is less than or equal to the threshold ε are kept in the final approximate Bayesian posterior sample set Θfinal. (C) MLE estimates the maximum likelihood parameter set θfinal that minimizes the error of the model predictions with the data D using a likelihood function. (D) Bayesian parameter estimation produces a set of samples θfinal from the posterior probability density which is proportional to both the prior distribution πθ and the likelihood PDθ based on the simulated outputs for each parameter sample, frequently obtained using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods.

In the case of QSP, the term digital twin seems to have taken on a new meaning. For example, the works of Susilo et al. [5], Joslyn et al. [6], and Chasseloup et al. [7] have recently applied digital twins to mean a parameterization of the model that closely captures available patient‐specific biomarker trajectories in an offline setting that is, after the data have been collected in a previous clinical trial. This represents a variation of the typical virtual population generation framework whereby candidate parameter samples are filtered until the resultant simulations are acceptably close to individual trajectories. These digital twins can then be used in a predictive context. However, unlike digital twins based on measurable mechanical or physiological quantities, twins of this nature often comprise parameters that are abstract and do not reflect physical, measurable quantities. Hence, they are more “individualized virtual patients,” a term used by Susilo et al. as well.

However, such an approach should be considered more carefully. As described in [6], the digital twins are produced by first sampling from a prior distribution (perhaps based on a previously generated virtual population) and then filtering for those samples where the model simulation falls “near” the available data. This example required that the squared, log‐transformed L2‐norm distance between simulated and observed biomarker trajectories for an individual was small, although a particular tolerance was not pre‐defined. Instead, the 10 samples with the smallest values were retained. Susilo et al. defined an objective function proportional to the linear L2‐norm distance and retained as digital twins either the 25 smallest‐valued samples or 25 random samples with distance less than 0.05 [5]. This approach constitutes a form of Simulation Based Inference [8] and strongly resembles a long‐established method known as Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC). In ABC, parameters are drawn from a prior distribution and simulations (or statistics thereof) are compared with a defined acceptance region using a predefined error tolerance (Figure 1B). The resulting samples approximate the true Bayesian posterior density since, unlike traditional Bayesian inference, samples are retained based on distance of the simulations to the data instead of defining a likelihood function [8]. These samples do not target the true posterior probability density given the data and therefore constitute only an approximate Bayesian posterior. Thus, these digital twins appear to be a reincarnation of ABC.

But is this even the right approach? If the goal is to produce a parameterization of the model that captures a set of trajectories for a single patient, then this is merely a parameter optimization problem. One must only define a likelihood function that reflects the discrepancy between simulation and individual data (Figure 1C). For example, Gaussian measurement errors are frequently assumed. Assuming uncorrelated errors between separate biomarkers, the likelihood function can be composed as the product of the separate likelihood functions. With such a likelihood function defined, any off‐the‐shelf local optimization algorithm such as gradient descent or global algorithm such as particle swarm optimization may be applied to obtain the maximum likelihood solution. This approach enables leveraging the extensive corpus of optimization theory and is likely to be significantly more efficient than filtering the simulated outputs of hundreds or thousands of parameters for close matches to the observed data. Notably, this differs from the usual virtual population setting where one is not trying to match any particular trajectory, but rather a range of behaviors, and thus does not lend itself to likelihood‐based inference methods. Some examples of this approach of digital twin generation by optimizing patient‐specific parameters can be found in the literature, for example in a QSP model of Gaucher's disease [9].

And yet, we can do better still. While local optimization methods will typically converge to solutions that resemble the data, assuming an adequate model and parameter search interval, they generate only a single parameter estimate for a single run. On the other hand, global optimization methods can produce multiple estimates using stochastic methods. The presence of multiple local minima with similar likelihoods is a fairly common occurrence in QSP models, warranting the use of such methods. However, this is an ideal setting for true (not approximate) Bayesian inference! We are interested in producing a comprehensive set of parameter samples that well capture an individual patient while also incorporating our prior beliefs for parameter values. With a Bayesian approach, parameter samples are drawn from the posterior probability distribution in proportion to both their likelihood given the data and their probability given the parameter prior distribution (Figure 1D). Modern Bayesian inference algorithms such as NUTS (No‐U‐Turn Sampler) or HMC (Hamiltonian Monte Carlo) [10] may more rapidly converge to the high‐density region of the posterior density compared with classical, random walk‐based methods by leveraging directional search information available in the gradients of the posterior density. Fortunately, computation of these gradients has become increasingly tractable in recent years due to the advent of automatic differentiation and related methods of key importance for deep learning technology. With these modern methods, one can easily and efficiently generate any number of “Bayesian digital twins” drawn from the true posterior distribution of the parameters. A set of digital twins generated this way has the desired properties of generating simulations that strongly resemble the individual data with frequencies that reflect both an explicitly assumed error model (and thus a likelihood) and parameter prior beliefs, in contrast to the ad hoc distance thresholds of ABC.

In summary, I propose we recognize that (1) digital twins as they appear in QSP literature may differ from the conventional definition by not strictly incorporating patient‐specific physiology and not updating in real time, (2) recently proposed distance‐based filtering methods for digital twin creation are actually a variation on Approximate Bayesian Computation, and (3) digital twin generation methods should move beyond inefficient acceptance/rejection‐based filtering approaches by utilizing widely successful Bayesian inference algorithms that are highly appropriate for this task. Once generated, these digital twins can be employed for precisely the same purpose of predicting the outcomes of novel treatment scenarios, but with the certainty that their sample distributions exactly reflect the Bayesian posteriority probability density and thus more accurately capture the real outcome uncertainties arising in a predictive context.
