Perceived organizational status and change-oriented organizational citizenship behavior: mediating role of psychological empowerment and moderating role of organizational error tolerance
Authors: Bingran Zhang, Zhi Li, Mi Lin, Min Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Change-oriented organizational citizenship behavior (CO-OCB) is essential for organizational adaptation in dynamic environments. This study investigated whether perceived organizational status promotes CO-OCB and whether psychological empowerment mediates this relationship. We conducted two vignette-based experiments with full-time employees in China (Study 1: N = 216; Study 2: N = 372) recruited from multiple industries and provinces through a professional data-collection platform, followed by a large-scale nationwide employee survey (Study 3: N = 1,017). Across the three studies, perceived organizational status significantly increased employees'CO-OCB, and psychological empowerment consistently mediated this effect. Organizational error tolerance strengthened the effect of perceived organizational status on psychological empowerment, and the moderated mediation was supported in Study 3. These findings extend understanding of how status perceptions shape change-oriented behavior among employees and offer actionable insights for fostering psychologically empowered workforces in supportive organizational climates.

Introduction

In the face of policy changes, technological advances, and social changes, employees with change-oriented organizational citizenship behavior (CO-OCB) can help organizations respond efficiently and flexibly to rapid socio-economic changes and emergencies and adapt to new environments [31]. CO-OCB refers to self-initiated employee behaviors that extend beyond the requirements of formal duties and are designed to support, facilitate, and implement organizational change [30], such as improving work processes, suggesting new ways of doing things, assisting colleagues in adapting to change, and supporting organizational innovation [44, 64].

Previous research suggests that employees' perceptions of the organization predict CO-OCB, such as procedural justice [8], organizational regulatory focus [45], and perceived reciprocal support [17]. Among these perceptions, perceived organizational status—an organizational member's judgment of their influence and prestige in the organization [13]—has attracted increasing attention because it is closely related to employees' job satisfaction [20], innovative behavior [5, 7], and performance in idea generation and problem-solving tasks [63]. Drawing on conservation of resources (COR) theory, scholars argue that employees' beliefs about resource gains and losses shape whether they are willing to invest additional effort in out-of-role activities [8, 38]. Employees with high perceived organizational status are more likely to feel recognized and capable of accessing needed organizational resources [34, 60].

Status and power represent central aspects of hierarchical judgments in organizations [21]. Employees who perceive high status often feel more autonomous and empowered by the organization [32] and are more willing to express ideas and engage in CO-OCB [49]. Psychological empowerment (PE) captures employees' subjective experience of meaning, competence, autonomy, and impact at work, and has been identified as a key motivational state underlying proactive behaviors. Organizational error tolerance (OET) refers to the extent to which organizations allow employees to make and discuss mistakes, learn from them, and continue to engage in innovative activities without fear of punishment [56]. According to COR theory, employees with low perceived organizational status view themselves as having few resources and are more likely to conserve energy and reduce CO-OCB [22], whereas a high level of organizational error tolerance can reduce the perceived risk of taking initiative and eliminate the concerns of low-status employees regarding possible negative outcomes of CO-OCB. Therefore, this study examines the effect of perceived organizational status on CO-OCB and the mediating role of PE, as well as the moderating role of OET.

To address these research questions, the present manuscript adopts a multi-study design consisting of three complementary empirical studies. Specifically, we conducted two vignette-based experimental studies (Studies 1 and 2) to establish the causal effects of perceived organizational status on PE and CO-OCB under different levels of OET. Study 3 is a large-scale questionnaire survey of employees in China that validates the full theoretical model in real organizational settings.

This study proposed and tested a theoretical framework (Fig. 1) of perceived organizational status facilitating employees' CO-OCB. First, based on COR theory, we analyze the relationship between perceived organizational status and employee CO-OCB, providing an important supplement to previous research on the relationship between organizational status and employee role-related behavior. While prior research suggests that organizational status may sometimes facilitate unethical behavior [9], our findings clarify its constructive role in promoting CO-OCB within a Chinese context. Second, we elucidate how perceived organizational status influences CO-OCB by identifying the mediating role of psychological empowerment and the moderating role of organizational error tolerance, thereby enriching the understanding of internal mechanisms from a COR perspective. By integrating social status theory with organizational contextual factors, we construct a unified "individual perception–psychological mediation–contextual moderation" framework that complements prior models [31]. Finally, our study reveals a new role for organizational tolerance—as a moderating variable rather than an independent variable—indicating that it not only directly influences employee behavior but also shapes the relationship between individual perception and behavior, providing new evidence for understanding the multifunctionality of organizational tolerance.Fig. 1The theoretical model of this study

Literature and hypotheses

Perceived organizational status and CO-OCB

Status is widely present in social and organizational environments, and the desire for status is a fundamental motivator for employees [6]. Employees focus on the dynamics of their organizational status, strive to demonstrate their value to obtain a higher status, and react strongly when their status is threatened [2]. Perceived organizational status is the judgment of an employee's relative position in an organization, characterized by the respect, prominence, and prestige that they feel they hold from the perspective of other organizational members [10].

Employees' status in an organization is associated with valuable resources that ultimately contribute to their survival and growth in the work field [10]. Employees who perceive a higher status in an organization perceive themselves as having a higher influence and respect in the organization [11]. According to the COR theory, employees prioritize the efficiency of their own resources when taking action to maximize the use of resources and minimize the waste of resources [66]. Therefore, employees with high perceived organizational status are likely to feel organizational support when performing CO-OCB, which helps them transform new ideas into innovative products and services. In addition, employees with high perceived organizational status usually possess a wealth of skills and experience [18], which enhances their ability to exhibit innovative behavior [28]. In contrast, employees with low perceived organizational status are likely to deny their ability to come up with novel ideas [60], which weakens their self-confidence and restricts their CO-OCB. The mediating role of PE

PE is the degree to which employees perceive four dimensions: meaning, competence, influence, and work autonomy. Meaning refers to the extent to which employees perceive the value of a job or task; competence refers to employees' belief that they have the skills needed to perform different tasks; autonomy refers to the discretion that employees have when doing their jobs; and influence refers to the perceived impact of employees on their immediate work environment [41].

Employees with high perceived organizational status view their strengths more positively than those with low perceived organizational status [29]. Moreover, they are more likely to interpret status signals as an affirmation of their unique and outstanding strengths, thus furthering the belief that their work is important and has a major impact and that they have the ability to get the job done and discretionary power. Employees with high perceived organizational status are usually actively involved in and influence the management decisions of the organization and can understand and incorporate their ideas into the organization's strategic plan. This process helps them feel psychologically empowered [27]. In addition, employees with high perceived organizational status tend to feel supported and recognized by the organization, feel affirmed in their values and abilities, and feel like an integral part of the team [3].H2: Perceived organizational status has a positive effect on PE.

Research has shown that PE enhances employee work behaviors, such as work engagement [37], innovative work behaviors [26], and organizational citizenship behaviors [39]. Highly psychologically empowered employees exhibit discretionary work behaviors when they perceive their work as meaningful, motivating them to increase their efforts to positively affect organizational outcomes [46]. Furthermore, highly psychologically empowered employees emphasize the importance of their work and decision-making autonomy and feel that their efforts are valued and their work is challenging. This motivational state generally inspires employees to engage in CO-OCB [30]. In addition, PE motivates employees to develop a sense of mission and responsibility, which in turn encourages them to feel confident in taking control and solving challenging tasks at work. The moderating role of error tolerance

Errors are inevitable in organizations, and effective error management is critical to organizational success [54]. Error management approaches focus on accepting and tolerating the occurrence of errors [15] and include discussing and sharing information about errors with colleagues, improving the ability to analyze and resolve errors, and seeking help when mistakes occur [55]. Organizational error tolerance refers to conditions in an organization that allow organizational members to take risks, pursue innovative solutions, and develop superior knowledge without worrying about the consequences of making mistakes [57]. High organizational tolerance for error mitigates the implicit pressure that "those in high positions must be perfect," allowing employees to act without fear that mistakes will undermine their status. Instead, they dare to explore new paths and take on greater responsibilities based on their own judgment, thereby enhancing their work capabilities [54]. This transforms the autonomy derived from "high status" into a tangible experience of empowerment. Additionally, high tolerance inherently conveys the organization's long-term trust in employees' capabilities. For high-status employees in particular, this trust resonates with their self-perception of being "assigned greater value," reinforcing their belief that autonomous actions will be embraced rather than constrained, thereby promoting their psychological empowerment experience. An organization's ability to tolerate mistakes helps reduce the stress and negative emotions that employees experience when confronted with mistakes [54]. Based on the COR theory, the occurrence of errors poses the threat of resource loss, such as job stress and emotional exhaustion [15]. Tolerating the occurrence of errors in an organization can motivate employees to regain resources [19]. Thus, employees are encouraged to try new things, with the risk mitigated [25]. Organizational error tolerance enables employees to obtain and maintain resources and organizational support to overcome the risk of risk-taking [55]. This is essential for them to produce novel and useful results. In addition, if an organization exhibits error tolerance, it motivates rewarding employee behavior. In organizations with high organizational error tolerance, even low-status employees experience discretion and trust in their work. Study 1: impact of perceived organizational status

Study 1 aimed to validate the effect of perceived organizational status on PE and CO-OCB. We used a between-subjects experimental design (perceived organizational status: high vs. low) and recruited participants through Credamo, a professional online survey platform in China that primarily targets working adults. After excluding incomplete responses and attention-check failures, 216 valid responses from full-time employees were retained for analysis. Because the platform does not systematically record the total number of initial survey accesses, this information is unavailable. All predefined exclusion criteria were applied prior to final analysis. Screening items (e.g., "Are you currently employed full time?","What is your job title?","Which industry do you work in?") ensured that only currently employed individuals were included. The final sample consisted of participants from various regions of China, of whom 51.4% were women, 56.5% were aged 35 years or younger, 56.0% had a bachelor's degree, and 30.1% had a graduate degree. To enhance the relevance of the experimental scenarios, all participants were instructed to respond by imagining themselves as organizational members embedded in the described work context. Because the experimental design relied on standardized vignette scenarios rather than participants' actual organizations, the analyses focused on perceived organizational status as situationally induced within the experimental context.

Pretest (trait-like tendencies)

Before the experimental manipulation, participants completed a pretest questionnaire designed to measure their trait-like tendencies in POS, PE, and CO-OCB. Although the pretest and posttest employed the same validated items (POS: [13]; PE: Spreinulltzernull, 1995[68] ; CO-OCB: [53]), the instructions given to participants were different. In the pretest, participants were asked to answer based on their general tendencies across organizations or in their usual work life (trait-like framing). In the posttest, after reading the experimental vignette, participants responded to the same items specifically anchored in the Company A scenario (state framing). This design follows the well-established trait–state distinction [47] and the principle of baseline adjustment (Cook & Campbell, 1979[69]), allowing us to control for stable predispositions while capturing situational effects.

Experimental materials and procedures

Study 1 followed a standardized experimental procedure. After providing informed consent, participants first completed a pretest questionnaire measuring their trait-like tendencies in perceived organizational status, psychological empowerment, and change-oriented organizational citizenship behavior. Participants were then exposed to an experimentally manipulated vignette describing their organizational status, followed by posttest measures anchored in the scenario. After completing the pretest, participants were randomly assigned to either the high-status group (n = 108) or the low-status group (n = 108). In the first part of the experiment, participants were informed that they would read a passage of material on organizational status characteristics. The content of the material was adapted from the manipulation procedure used in existing research [59] and combined with questions from the Perceived Organizational Status Scale [13], such as "I am very prestigious in my organization" and "I am very well known in my organization." The subjects were asked to read the following material:

High-status condition: "You are currently a marketing manager at Company A. You are very respected and loved by your colleagues and have high visibility and prestige in the company compared to managers at the same level."

Low-status condition: "You are currently a marketing assistant at Company A. You are not respected and loved by your colleagues and have very low visibility and prestige in the company compared to employees at the same level."

After the reading task was completed, we asked the participants to imagine that they worked for Company A and to describe their positional characteristics. This imagination and description task was designed to increase participants'engagement with the scenario and to ensure that the manipulated status information was cognitively processed before completing the outcome measures. Subsequently, participants completed posttest measures of psychological empowerment and change-oriented organizational citizenship behavior, followed by manipulation check items assessing perceived organizational status within the experimental scenario.

Measurement tools

All measures were administered in Chinese. For scales originally developed in English, we relied on established Chinese versions that have been widely used in prior research. To ensure consistency across studies, the same measurement instruments and response formats were applied in Studies 1, 2, and 3 unless otherwise noted. PE was measured using 12 items, such as "I have a great deal of autonomy in deciding how to do my job" and "I have mastered all of the skills I need to do my job" (Spreitzer, 1995[68]). Responses were rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree). The Cronbach's α coefficient was 0.959.

CO-OCB was measured using nine items, such as "I try to correct the organization's inadequate or incorrect work procedures and practices" and "I will work hard to adopt improved procedures for my work" [53]. Responses were rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree). The Cronbach's α coefficient was 0.947.

Perceived organizational status was measured using five items, such as "I am very prestigious in the organization" and "I am well known in the organization" [13]. Responses were rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree). The Cronbach's α coefficient was 0.930.

Study results

We first examined whether the manipulation of perceived organizational status was successful. The t-test results revealed that participants in the high perceived organizational status condition (M = 4.50, SD = 0.18) had significantly different scores from those before the manipulation (M = 3.90, SD = 1.01; t(108) = 6.130, p < 0.001, d = 0.827). Participants in the low perceived organizational status condition (M = 2.38, SD = 0.63) had significantly different scores from those before the manipulation (M = 3.85, SD = 1.13; t(108) = −11.862, p < 0.001, d = 1.607). Participants in the high perceived organizational status condition scored significantly higher (MHigh POS = 4.50, SD = 0.18) than those in the low perceived organizational status condition (MLow POS = 2.38, SD = 0.63; t(216) = −33.666, p < 0.001, d = 4.576). Thus, the manipulation of perceived organizational status was successful.

We next tested the hypothesized effects on the outcome variables. The t-test demonstrated that the participants' CO-OCB scores differed significantly between the two groups (MHigh POS = 4.20, SD = 0.22; MLow POS = 3.18, SD = 1.22; t(216) = 8.548, p < 0.001, and d = 1.164), supporting H1. The participants' PE scores differed significantly between the two groups (MHigh POS = 4.23, SD = 0.19; MLow POS = 3.20, SD = 1.21; t(216) = 8.727, p < 0.001, d = 1.189), supporting H2.

Study 2: the impact of perceived organizational status and organizational error tolerance

Study 2 aimed to provide causal evidence of the role of perceived organizational status and organizational error tolerance in the relationship between PE and CO-OCB. We used a 2 (perceived organizational status: high vs. low) × 2 (organizational error tolerance: high vs. low) between-subjects design. After excluding incomplete responses and those failing attention checks, 620 valid responses from full-time employees were retained for analysis. The total number of initial survey accesses was not systematically recorded by the platform. All predefined exclusion criteria were applied consistently prior to final analysis. Participants were recruited from diverse industries and provinces across China. The participants completed a survey through Credamo. Among the participants, 52.3% were women, 71.1% were aged 35 years or younger, 53.9% had an undergraduate degree, and 36.9% had a graduate degree. As Study 2 was designed as a vignette-based experiment anchored in a standardized hypothetical organization, the purpose of the participant description was to characterize the sample at a general level rather than to model variation across participants' actual organizations.

Experimental materials and procedures

Study 2 followed a structured experimental procedure. After providing informed consent, participants were first exposed to a standardized description of a hypothetical organization (Company A) containing embedded cues related to organizational error tolerance. Participants then completed the organizational error tolerance measure, based on which they were classified into high- and low-tolerance groups, and were subsequently randomly assigned to either the high- or low-status vignette condition before completing the outcome measures. All participants first read a structured description of Company A. These cues were designed to reflect how organizations respond to mistakes, without directly labeling the company as "high" or "low" tolerance. Together, these materials functioned as an implicit experimental stimulus intended to systematically induce variability in participants' perceptions of organizational error tolerance, rather than as a mere descriptive background. This approach was intended to capture participants' perceptions of organizational climate rather than impose an explicit manipulation, which is consistent with prior research on perceived organizational climates [12, 67]. The effectiveness and interpretability of these implicit error-tolerance cues were empirically validated in a pilot study prior to the main experiment.

For transparency and replicability, the full scenario materials describing Company A are provided in Appendix 1. Taken together, this procedure was designed to balance experimental control with ecological plausibility, allowing participants to form meaningful perceptions of organizational error tolerance without relying on explicit labeling.

Pilot study

To verify that the cues provided sufficient information, we conducted a pilot test (n = 60). After rating OET, participants answered whether they could clearly judge the company's attitude toward mistakes (1 = not at all, 7 = very clearly). The mean rating was 5.73 (SD = 0.91), significantly above the midpoint (t = 14.26, p < 0.001). In addition, 92% of participants recalled at least two tolerance-related cues and cited them as reasons for their judgments. These results show that participants not only interpreted the cues clearly but also consistently differentiated levels of organizational error tolerance. The pilot results therefore provide strong evidence supporting the validity of the implicit manipulation approach used in Study 2.

Grouping procedure

In the formal experiment, participants completed the OET scale [57] after reading the Company A description. Based on their ratings, the top 30% were categorized as the high-tolerance group (n = 186, M = 4.36, SD = 0.29), while the bottom 30% were categorized as the low-tolerance group (n = 186, M = 2.04, SD = 0.35; t(372) = 69.726, p < 0.001, d = 7.218). Within each OET group, participants were then randomly assigned to either the high- or low-status condition, following the same manipulation procedure as in Study 1. This resulted in a 2 (POS: high vs. low) × 2 (OET: high vs. low) factorial design with four experimental groups (n = 93 per group).

Measurement tools

Organizational error tolerance was measured using five items, such as "Leaders tolerate mistakes when employees pursue innovative solutions" and "The company understands that making mistakes is part of taking risks" [57]. Responses were rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree,5 = strongly agree). The Cronbach's α coefficient was 0.946.

PE, CO-OCB, and perceived organizational status were measured using the same methods as those used in Study 1. The Cronbach's α coefficients were 0.972, 0.962, and 0.952, respectively.

Study results

We first examined whether the manipulation of perceived organizational status was successful. Participants in the high perceived organizational status group scored significantly higher (M = 4.73, SD = 0.13) than those in the low perceived organizational status group (M = 1.97, SD = 0.31; t(372) = 112.552, p < 0.001, d = 11.611). Thus, perceived organizational status was successfully manipulated, and the extreme-group procedure yielded clearly differentiated levels of perceived organizational error tolerance. It should be noted that the very large effect sizes observed in Study 2 are partly attributable to the extreme-group design adopted for organizational error tolerance, which is known to restrict within-group variance and amplify between-group differences [42].

Study 3: full-model questionnaire survey

We extended the external validity of our findings in Study 3 by using a large-scale self-report questionnaire survey to test the overall research model.

Research sample

We distributed 1,100 electronic questionnaires to employees in China and obtained 1,017 valid responses (effective response rate = 92.5%). The final sample consisted of 499 men (49.1%) and 518 women (50.9%). In terms of age, 11.2% were under 25 years, 44.0% were aged 26–35, 27.8% were aged 36–45, 13.8% were aged 46–55, and 3.1% were over 55. Regarding education, 14.9% had below a bachelor's degree, 52.6% held a bachelor's degree, and 34.5% held a graduate degree.

Measurement tools

Perceived organizational status, PE, and CO-OCB were measured using the same methods as those used in Study 1. The Cronbach's α coefficients were 0.922, 0.969, and 0.941, respectively. Organizational error tolerance was measured using the same methods as those used in Study 2. Cronbach's α coefficient was 0.955.

Analysis and results

Discussion

This study examined a moderated mediation model using two experiments and one questionnaire survey. We investigated the effect of perceived organizational status on CO-OCB, the mediating role of psychological empowerment (PE), and the moderating role of organizational error tolerance. The results revealed that high perceived organizational status enabled employees to experience higher PE and exhibit higher CO-OCB. A high error-tolerant organizational context strengthened the indirect effect of perceived organizational status on CO-OCB through PE. By combining controlled experiments with a large-scale survey, the multi-study design strengthens causal inference and external validity, providing convergent support for the proposed relationships.

Comparison with recent empirical studies in Asian contexts

The present findings align with a growing body of empirical research in China and other Asian contexts emphasizing the importance of status-related perceptions in shaping proactive and change-oriented behaviors. In hierarchical organizational settings characterized by relatively high power distance, employees tend to be attentive to their standing and cautious about engaging in behaviors that may entail interpersonal risk [24, 52, 58]. Recent evidence further shows that perceived insider status and status differences predict proactive and innovative behaviors in Chinese organizations, including among younger employees [33, 62, 65]. Together, these studies suggest that employees' perceptions of organizational standing play a critical role in motivating discretionary change-oriented efforts.

Our findings complement this literature by showing that perceived organizational status influences CO-OCB through psychological empowerment. This is consistent with broader evidence identifying psychological empowerment as a key motivational mechanism translating contextual cues into extra-role outcomes [32, 43]. In the Chinese context, psychological empowerment has also been linked to challenge-oriented citizenship behaviors and insider cognitions (Xu et al., 2024 [71]). By explicitly modeling psychological empowerment as a mediator, the present study clarifies the micro-level psychological process through which status perceptions translate into change-oriented action.

Prior research in Asian settings has primarily focused on organizational-level predictors—such as leadership or climate—as direct antecedents of proactive behavior. For example, learning-oriented climates and error-communication norms reduce perceived threat and facilitate experimentation [5, 7], while organizational error tolerance has been shown to increase psychological empowerment, which in turn promotes CO-OCB [31]. Building on this work, we conceptualize organizational error tolerance not merely as a direct predictor but as a contextual boundary condition that shapes when perceived organizational status fosters psychological empowerment and subsequent change-oriented behavior.

This contextualized perspective is particularly relevant in settings where employees may hesitate to speak up due to relational concerns [58]. Error-tolerant and psychologically safe climates can reduce perceived interpersonal risk and legitimize trial-and-error, thereby strengthening the motivational role of status perceptions [12, 31]. By integrating perceived organizational status, psychological empowerment, and organizational error tolerance into a moderated mediation framework, the present study offers a more precise account of how individual perceptions and organizational contexts jointly shape CO-OCB in Chinese organizational settings.

Theoretical contribution

This study found a positive association between organizational status and CO-OCB. This finding is consistent with those of previous research, revealing a positive relationship between employees' perceived organizational status and extra-role behaviors [65] and between perceived power and CO-OCB [4]. According to the COR theory, employees with high perceived organizational status may feel that they have access to resources, such as power, information, and social support [5, 7, 61]. This accumulation of resources increases their ability to engage in change-oriented behaviors, as they can influence others and prompt change. Moreover, high perceived organizational status may reduce employees' concerns about losing resources, as they feel stable and secure. This sense of security may encourage them to invest in change-oriented behaviors that are risky but provide long-term benefits [25]. These behaviors are not only the result of personal traits or organizational culture but also of changing resource dynamics. Overall, our results suggest that perceived organizational status relates to change-oriented behavior through resource accumulation and protection mechanisms. This highlights the link between individual perceptions and discretionary behavior.

Moreover, our results indicate that perceived organizational status influences CO-OCB through psychological empowerment, consistent with prior evidence linking empowerment-related psychological states to CO-OCB [30]. This adds a layer of depth to our understanding of the mechanisms of perceived organizational status influencing CO-OCB. This mediation provides theoretical support to explain why employees with high perceived organizational status are likely to engage in change-oriented behaviors. These results link perceived organizational status, PE, and CO-OCB within a coherent framework, in which empowerment reflects employees' sense of autonomy and responsibility. This underscores the importance of psychological states when examining the effect of perceived organizational status on CO-OCB. The relationship between perceived organizational status and PE highlights the dynamic interaction between power and status in organizations. Research suggests that individuals with high perceived organizational status are likely to be perceived as having the ability and power to empower others, which may enhance their empowering behaviors toward others [50]. Individuals with high perceived organizational status may be more inclined to delegate [51], as they believe that this enhances organizational flexibility and adaptability, thereby promoting organizational change and innovation.

In addition, we examined the complex relationships between organizational error tolerance, perceived organizational status, PE, and CO-OCB. Previous research has shown that the degree of fit between employees' perceptions of resources and their work environment significantly impacts their work attitudes and performance [16]. Particularly in the public service sector, the work environment of employees is crucial for fulfilling their intrinsic needs for public services [48]. Our findings also suggest that the effect of perceived organizational status depends on the organizational environment. Organizations with high error tolerance typically foster an open and supportive work environment in which employees are likely to feel supported and trusted by the organization [23]. This environment contributes to employees' perceived organizational status; that is, they feel that they have status and influence in the organization. In organizations with high error tolerance, employees are likely to accept their mistakes and learn from them [35]. This experience enhances employees' self-confidence and self-esteem, promoting their belief that they hold a certain status and competence in the organization, thereby strengthening their perceived organizational status.

Management insights

Our findings suggest that employees' perceived organizational status enhance their CO-OCB. Therefore, we recommend that organizations take appropriate measures to enhance employees' perceived organizational status. Organizations can enhance perceived organizational status by strengthening transparent internal communication (e.g., regular employee meetings, open office hours, and structured feedback mechanisms), which helps employees understand decision-making processes, goals, and values. Encouraging employees to participate in teamwork and decision-making processes makes them feel that their opinions and contributions are valued. Organizations can establish employee representation systems, committees, and working groups to involve employees in important decisions and provide timely feedback on the results.

Furthermore, PE affects employees' CO-OCB. We recommend that organizations provide employees with training and development opportunities, equipping them with the necessary skills and knowledge to enhance their self-confidence and autonomy at work. Such opportunities may include skill training, leadership development, and problem-solving programs that help employees cope with work challenges and enhance PE. Moreover, leaders should demonstrate a supportive attitude, encourage employees to present ideas and solutions to problems, and give them appropriate autonomy and decision-making power. Leaders' empowering behaviors and supportive attitudes help build employees' sense of trust and self-confidence, enhancing their PE.

Finally, organizational error tolerance moderates the effects of perceived organizational status on PE and CO-OCB.Thus, enhancing organizational error tolerance is important for organizations to promote employees' CO-OCB. We suggest that organizations develop clear and unambiguous policies and processes to ensure that employees understand how the organization handles errors and how to correct and improve them. Clear policies and processes help employees understand the organization's expectations and reduce misunderstandings and mistakes. Encouraging employees to engage in trial and error and recognizing and supporting employees to learn from failures promotes continuous improvement and innovation in the organization.

Beyond providing instrumental resources, our findings suggest that organizations can foster employees' change-oriented organizational citizenship behavior by shaping employees' status-based identity—that is, their sense of being visible, valued, and influential within the organization. Practices such as transparent recognition systems, meaningful opportunities for voice, and respectful interpersonal treatment can strengthen employees' perceived standing and, in turn, enhance their psychological empowerment. In addition, cultivating a psychologically safe organizational climate is critical for translating perceived organizational status into proactive change-oriented behavior. Error-tolerant practices, such as learning-oriented debriefings, non-punitive responses to mistakes, and leader behaviors that emphasize improvement rather than blame, can reduce the interpersonal risks associated with speaking up and experimentation. Such practices are particularly important for enabling empowered employees, especially those with lower perceived status, to engage more actively in change-oriented organizational citizenship behaviors.

Research limitations and future directions

Despite the strengths of combining experimental and questionnaire-based methods, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged, which also point to promising directions for future research.

First, Studies 1 and 2 relied on vignette-based experimental scenarios anchored in a single fictional organization (Company A) and a specific functional context. Although this design enhances internal validity by holding organizational cues constant, it may limit the generalizability of the findings across industries, job types, and organizational structures. Status cues and their behavioral implications may differ substantially in real organizational settings. Therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing the experimental findings beyond the specific scenarios examined in this study. It should also be noted that organizational status may carry different meanings across organizations. In the present experimental studies, this potential source of variance was intentionally minimized by anchoring all participants to the same standardized vignette-based organizational context. By holding organizational background constant, the design reduces between-organization heterogeneity. This allows a clearer examination of the causal effect of perceived organizational status as a situational cue rather than an attribute of participants' actual organizations.

Second, Study 3 adopted a cross-sectional survey design, which constrains causal inference among perceived organizational status, psychological empowerment, and change-oriented organizational citizenship behavior. Although the hypothesized relationships are theoretically grounded and supported by complementary experimental evidence, the cross-sectional nature of the data does not allow for definitive conclusions regarding temporal precedence or dynamic changes over time.

Third, the manipulation of perceived organizational status in the experimental studies was based on formal hierarchical positions, which may partially overlap with perceptions of power and authority. Although this approach is consistent with prior research, future studies could disentangle status and power more rigorously by employing non-hierarchical status cues or by independently manipulating symbolic status and decision authority.

In particular, future research could adopt longitudinal or multi-wave designs to capture the dynamic evolution of perceived organizational status, psychological empowerment, and change-oriented organizational citizenship behavior over time. In addition, qualitative approaches, such as in-depth interviews or critical incident techniques, may provide richer insights into how employees interpret status cues and error-tolerant practices in everyday organizational contexts.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that perceived organizational status enhanced PE and CO-OCB. This study provides new evidence regarding working conditions that enhance CO-OCB and suggests that perceived organizational status can be effective in enhancing employees' CO-OCB by increasing PE. Error tolerance provides a new perspective for analyzing this process while identifying theoretical recommendations and practical guidance for organizational management.
