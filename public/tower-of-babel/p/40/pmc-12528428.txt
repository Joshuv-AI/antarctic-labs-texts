The relationship between perceived social support and quality of life among hospitalized patients with schizophrenia
Authors: Mary Lee Wakim, Carine Al Haddad, Chadia Haddad, Mariline Mouawad, Dory Hachem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with mental illness often experience limited Social Support, which is a contributing factor to isolation and negatively affects their psychological and emotional well-being, including their quality of life (QoL). This research explored the association between individuals’ perception of Social Support and quality of life among hospitalized patients with schizophrenia. A cross-sectional study was conducted at the Psychiatric Hospital of the Cross between the period of November 2024 and January 2025 and included 86 patients diagnosed with schizophrenia. Participants were selected from medical records and completed a self-reported questionnaire after receiving assistance from a medical intern. The questionnaire included the following instruments: Perceived Social Support (based on the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support MSPSS) and quality of life (according to WHO Quality of Life Scale WHOQOL-BREF). Statistical analyses was done using the SPSS version 25.0. Linear regression analysis using the Enter method was conducted, taking the total quality of life scale and subscales as the dependent variables. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics and Research Committee at the Psychiatric Hospital of the Cross (HPC-001-10-24). Informed consent was obtained from each participant at the beginning of the survey before having any access to the questionnaires and their participation. A statistically significant positive correlation was recognized between the total QoL and total individuals’ perception of Social Support (r = 0.259, p = 0.016). Family visits and individuals’ perception of Social Support were both significantly associated with higher QoL. Nevertheless, there was no significant correlation between perceived Social Support from friends and QoL (r = 0.083, p = 0.447). A first regression analysis taking the QoL as the dependent variable showed that the length of the current hospitalization and antipsychotic medication dosage were not significantly associated with QoL. Moreover, the regression model did not reach any significant effect (Adjusted R2 = 0.031; F (4, 78) = 1.628, p = 0.176). A second regression analysis, taking the QoL as the dependent variable, showed that a higher number of family visits (Beta = 11.23; 95% Confidence interval (CI) 0.72–21.75, p = 0.037) and higher perceived Social Support (Beta = 3.11; 95% CI 0.46–5.76, p = 0.022) were significantly associated with higher QoL. The model reached a significant effect (Adjusted R2 = 0.101; F (4, 83) = 3.33, p = 0.014). The findings indicate a positive relationship between individuals’ perception of Social Support, particularly from family and significant others, and the quality of life in hospitalized patients with schizophrenia. Introduction

Mental disorders contribute to 14% of the global burden of disease, with developing countries bearing the highest proportion1. In 2020, according to the WHO, 15% of global disabilities were attributed to mental disorders2,3, with schizophrenia being a significant contributor. Schizophrenia, a chronic psychiatric condition affecting 21 million people worldwide, has a lifetime prevalence of about 1%4. It presents with a range of symptoms, including positive, negative, and disorganized symptoms5,6 coupled with substantial morbidity and reduced life expectancy.

Social support is defined as the emotional and practical assistance received from others and has been shown to be associated with better health outcomes, including psychological well-being and improved quality of life (QoL)7. Patients with mental illness often face loss of social support, culminating in limited resources from relatives or mental health workers8. Lower levels of social support may be linked to increased isolation, stigma and reduced community integration, which threatens psychological, emotional well-being, and QoL and may assist in more psychiatric hospitalization(s)8.

Integration into a social network offers positive effects that are associated with better QoL9. In a descriptive cross-sectional research design, El-Moshed and Amr (2020) studied the association between perceived social support and recovery among 176 patients with schizophrenia10. The study established that perceived social support from family members and friends was significantly associated with greater recovery scores10. The authors recognized that patients with schizophrenia who have a reliable social circle feel respected and nurtured and are therefore less likely to feel discriminated, which enhances their recovery10.

This cross-sectional, observational study seeks to investigate the association between individuals’ perception of social support and QoL among hospitalized patients with schizophrenia. Understanding this relationship may help guide strategies to improve psychosocial care and family engagement in psychiatric settings.

Methodology

Study design and participants

The study is a cross-sectional observational study conducted at the Psychiatric Hospital of the Cross, between November 2024 and January 2025. The study included 86 participants diagnosed with schizophrenia who have no comorbidities or cognitive impairment. The following were the criteria that determined the selection of the patients: adults aged 18 years or older with a diagnosis of schizophrenia as per the DSM-5 criteria, and who were hospitalized for at least one year. Excluded subjects were those with a known comorbid psychiatric condition and those with severe cognitive impairment or intellectual disability.

Sample size

The minimum sample size was calculated using the G-Power software, version 3.0.10. The sample size was quantified based on power calculations assuming a medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.3), a confidence level of 95%, and a power of 80%. The minimum necessary sample size needed was 84.

The second method to calculate the minimum sample size was through the G*Power for a linear multiple regression (fixed model, R² deviation from zero) with four predictors: an alpha level of 0.05 and a medium effect size (f² = 0.15). The analysis indicated that a minimum of 85 participants were required to achieve an 80% power.

Procedure

The sample of clinical patients was selected from a list generated from the hospital’s computer software. Data collection was conducted through a self-reported questionnaire, with the assistance of a medical intern who also screened patients for eligibility. Participants completed a questionnaire comprising two sections: one addressing sociodemographic characteristics and the other including scales for assessing Perceived Social Support (PSS) and quality of life (QoL). After obtaining written informed consent, the interviews were conducted with the hospitalized patients. In order to facilitate accurate and honest responses, efforts were taken to make sure that the data collection setting was private and comfortable.

Assessments

The questionnaire used in this study was in Arabic; the native language of Lebanon. The following elements were included in the questionnaire:

The first part assessed the sociodemographic data (age, gender, education level and marital status), and clinical and social data (number of family visits, duration of illness, number of hospitalizations and medication adherence) for each participant.

The second part included the following scales:

WHO Quality of Life Scale (QoL) which was developed by the World Health Organization, assesses individuals’ perceived quality of life. The WHOQOL-BREF is a shorter version that evaluates various domains such as: physical health, psychological health, social relationships and environment. Accordingly, it is used in research studies to assess the overall satisfaction of individuals. It contains 26 questions and is completed by the patient themselves. There are four domains: Physical health (7 questions), Psychological health (6 questions), Social relationships (3 questions) and Environment (8 questions). Two additional questions assessed the quality of life and satisfaction. Each item is rated on a scale from 1 to 5. Each domain’s set of questions was used to compute scores reflecting different aspects of an individual’s QoL. Each domain score was standardized and typically transformed to a scale from 0 to 100. Higher scores indicated a higher quality of life and satisfaction in its respective domain. The Cronbach alpha value for the total scale in the current study was 0.796.

The Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) is a tool designed to assess an individual’s perceived social support from family, friends and significant others. The “significant other” subscale was defined as any individual whom the patient considers emotionally important, including a close relative, friend or trusted person. It is a 12-item self-reported questionnaire, with each item rated on a scale from 1 (very strongly disagree) to 7 (very strongly agree). The MSPSS total score and subscales range from 1 to 7, with higher scores indicating greater perceived social support. The Cronbach alpha value in the current study was 0.900.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was done using SPSS version 25.0. Quantitative data was presented using mean and standard deviation, and qualitative data was demonstrated using frequencies and percentages. Descriptive statistics were used to determine the health problems and benefits associated with teleworking. The internal consistency of the scales was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha.

The normality test of the quality of life scale was checked by the visual inspection of the histogram, where the skewness and kurtosis were below |1.96|. At that point, the bivariate analysis was conducted taking the total quality of life as the dependent variable. The Student T-test and ANOVA tests were used in the bivariate analysis to compare two or more than three means. Pearson’s correlation was applied to examine the association between continuous variables.

Linear regression analysis using the Enter method was conducted, taking the total quality of life scale and subscales as the dependent variables. Two regression models were performed: in the first model, the current hospitalization and antipsychotic medication dosage were considered as the independent variables. In the second model, the interaction between the number of family visits and perceived social support were considered as the independent variables. In addition, the effect of the interaction between the number of family visits and the perceived social support was assessed with quality of life subscales. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Sample description

The sociodemographic and other characteristics of the participants are displayed in Table 1. A total of 86 patients diagnosed with schizophrenia participated in the study. The majority of the participants were single (94.2%), males (80.2%), had a complementary and below educational level (75.3%), and were diagnosed with paranoid schizophrenia (75.3%). Almost half of them have a family history of psychiatric illness (42.5%), 74.4% were taking typical antipsychotics, 46.5% were taking atypical antipsychotics, and a lower percentage of participants were taking anticonvulsants (9.3%), antiepileptics (24.4%), benzodiazepines (22.1%) and antidepressants (30.2%). The participants’ average age was 52.41 ± 7.54 years, and the mean duration of illness and hospitalization was 24.64 ± 9.59, 11.52 ± 8.51 respectively. The mean number of family visits during the last month was 1.14 ± 0.92 and the mean chlorpromazine equivalent dose was 1117.35 ± 2249.42.

Description of the scales used

Table 2 describes the median, mean, SD, and the range of the scales used in this study. The mean total quality of life was 60.86 ± 13.45 with a range of 31.21–86.50. The highest scoring domain was the physical health domain (68.60%), followed by the psychological (65.74%), environmental (55.34%), and social relationships domains (53.77%). The total scale showed good internal consistency (α = 0.796), while the domain-level alphas indicated acceptable reliability. The mean total perceived social support was 4.18 ± 1.63 with a range between 1 and 7. The family subscale showed the highest percentage (68.85%), followed by support from significant others (67.14%). Friend support had the lowest percentage (43.14%). The total MSPSS had high internal consistency across all subscales and total scores (Cronbach’s α ranging from 0.822 to 0.911), indicating excellent reliability in this sample.

Correlations between quality of life and perceived social support

Table 4 presents the bivariate correlations between perceived social support, clinical and demographic variables, and the different domains of QoL. A significant positive correlation was found between perceived social support (MSPSS total) (r = 0.259, p = 0.016), support from family members (r = 0.323, p = 0.002), number of family visits (r = 0.271, p = 0.013), and QoL. There was no significant correlation between perceived social support from friends and QoL (r = 0.083, p = 0.447) (Fig. 1). When examining the QoL subdomains, total perceived social support was significantly associated with both social (r = 0.279, p = 0.009) and environmental QoL (r = 0.261, p = 0.015) domains. Support from a “significant other” was significantly associated with environmental QoL (r = 0.240, p = 0.026). Support from family was positively correlated with psychological (r = 0.263, p = 0.014), social (r = 0.263, p = 0.014), and environmental QoL (r = 0.344, p = 0.001) domains. Age was negatively associated with physical (r = −0.221, p = 0.041) and environmental QoL (r = −0.223, p = 0.039). Both duration of illness and the number of hospital admissions were negatively associated with physical QoL. Additionally, a higher number of family visits was significantly associated with better psychological (r = 0.359, p = 0.001) and environmental QoL (r = 0.334, p = 0.002).

In the second model, when considering the interaction between the number of family visits and perceived social support as independent variables, the results showed that a higher number of family visits (Beta = 11.23) and higher perceived social support (Beta = 3.11) were significantly associated with higher QoL. Discussion

The current study demonstrated that more than half of the patients had good QoL in almost all dimensions, as well as in the total score. In fact, most of the participants were elderly and adherent to their treatment plan, which was associated with better QoL and well-being. Furthermore, patients will have a more positive perception of their lives if they are residing in an establishment offering them housing and medical treatment. Both the social and environmental domains of QOL had lower scores in comparison to the other domains. Taking into account that mentally ill patients usually have fewer social and cognitive skills, it is expected for the social scores to be low due to limited social interactions. Additionally, most patients living in a confined hospital environment, with limited resources and restrictions, contribute to a lower environmental QoL.

This study showed a positive relationship between overall QoL and social support that was received from family members and significant others. This study proved that patients diagnosed with schizophrenia reported higher levels of life satisfaction when they experienced support (emotional and financial) from close relationships. Similarly, this was proven in other studies whereby higher levels of social support was shown to be correlated with greater QoL in a cross-sectional study conducted among 160 individuals with schizophrenia11. Another study conducted in 2022 in Ethiopia showed that individuals with poor social support had a higher relapse risk, emphasizing the importance of establishing robust social networks12.

The current study emphasized the importance of social support especially from family members. Regular visits and emotional and financial support from family members have been linked to greater adherence to treatment plans and a reduction in psychological distress, in addition to an established sense of belonging, which is essential in maintaining emotional security13,14. Therefore, based on these findings, it is important to build active participation from family members in the treatment plan of schizophrenic patients. Family members should also have more visitation privileges and should participate in educational programs that would educate them more about the disorder and its management.

This study; however, did not establish a significant correlation between overall QoL and perceived social support from friends. It is generally challenging for patients with schizophrenia to build strong bonds with friends due to the impact of their condition, resulting in social withdrawal and paranoid ideations15. Unlike familial bonding, friendships usually require mutual engagement and effort, which is difficult for schizophrenic patients15. Finally, the study managed to identify a significant positive correlation between the total QoL and overall perceived social support. This shows that total perceived social support contributed to better well-being of hospitalized patients with schizophrenia. This highlights the crucial role that social support plays, aiding patients with schizophrenia in maintaining stability and reducing levels of mood manifestations or relapse into their condition16.

In the current study, a higher number of family visits and greater perceived social support were significantly associated with higher QoL. The findings suggest that the QoL of individuals with schizophrenia is dependent on the frequency of family visits and their perceived level of support17. This significant association indicates that ongoing family contact creates a feeling of inclusion and reduces social withdrawal18. Consequently, patients receiving frequent visits from their family members may have less intensity of distress18. Similarly, the significant association between perceived social support and higher QoL sheds light on the importance of feeling supported. Accordingly, the feeling of having reliable support can contribute to better recovery in patients with schizophrenia16. Earlier research by El-Monshed et al. (2020) used a descriptive cross-sectional research design on 176 patients with schizophrenia to examine the association between perceived social support and recovery10. The study found that perceived social support had a statistically significant association with recovery10.

A descriptive correlational research design was conducted on 115 patients from five psychiatric inpatient units and one outpatient clinic from Port-Said Mental Health Hospital19. The study concluded that the majority of the studied patients (80.9%) have a low score in the social domain and almost two-thirds of them (67.8%) also have a low score in the environmental domain19. Correspondingly, Young (2010) found that individuals with severe mental illness had the least satisfaction with their social, environmental, and psychological domains20. In line with these studies, our current research found that higher perceived social support was significantly associated with higher social and environmental QoL. Patients with schizophrenia who have a trusted network with ongoing support are more involved in social events with better living standards16. In addition, it is proposed that the feeling of being supported by others was associated to positive outlooks regarding environmental QoL16. This implies better experiences precipitating feelings of security, inclusion, and welfare16.

In the context of this study, a higher number of family visits was proven to have a significant association with higher environmental QoL. In other words, patients whose families often visit them, recognize their environment as being of better quality21,22. This can be credited to the emotional comfort offered by families21,22. Haselden et al. (2019) concluded that family visits had a significant association with patients’ inclusive discharge plans23.

The interaction between perceived social support and the number of family visits with QoL was not significant in our study. There is no evidence that these factors were stronger or different when combined in comparison to when they were analyzed individually. The observed trend (p = 0.073) may warrant further investigation in future studies to explore potential interactive effects more thoroughly. These findings underscore the need for a more comprehensive understanding of the ways in which social and family influences impact the QoL of individuals with schizophrenia.

According to our study, QoL among patients with schizophrenia was not significantly associated with the duration of current hospitalization or the dose of antipsychotic medication. One reason might be that because QoL was self-reported, it might represent patients’ subjective opinions rather than their actual clinical condition. Also, participants in our research may have adapted to the setting over time since they were admitted to a long-term care mental health facility. Their QOL may become more stable or even better as a result of this adaptation, irrespective of how long they stay in the hospital or how much medication they take. Although antipsychotic drugs are useful in lowering psychotic symptoms, they might not directly enhance patients’ subjective wellbeing. These findings highlight the complexity of quality of life (QoL) in schizophrenia and underscore the need for future studies to address both psychosocial and rehabilitative needs in order to meaningfully improve QoL in this population.

Limitations

This research study has several limitations that should be considered. The first limitation is the relatively small sample size of the studied population and the inclusion of patients residing in only one psychiatric hospital, limiting the generalizability of the observations. The second limitation is the inability to demonstrate causality between perceived social support and QoL due to the cross-sectional nature of the study design. Thirdly, the reliance on subjective data for the scales utilized may generate potential bias, influencing the accuracy of the answers. Also, there is the potential for measurement bias, as chronic hospitalization may influence patients’ self-reported perceptions of social support and QoL. Symptom severity was not assessed in this study and; therefore, could not be included as a confounding variable. Additionally, all patients were recruited from a single site and long-term care units, resulting in no variability in ward type. In addition, perceived social support was measured exclusively using the MSPSS among patients. Social support from institutional staff or peer patients, who may be an important source of support for long-term in-patients, was not assessed in this study. Future research should consider incorporating additional measures, such as the DUKE-UNC Functional Social Support Questionnaire or tailored items, to better capture formal and peer support networks in institutional settings. Finally, interviewer bias is of concern as the data was collected via face-to-face interviews.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated a strong correlation between QoL and perceived social support in hospitalized patients with schizophrenia, finding that higher QoL was correlated with both more family visits and higher perceived social support. It reinforces the important role that social support plays in enhancing patients’ QoL. The results demonstrated the need to take into account social factors in the long-term treatment plan of affected individuals. Future research should reflect more on longitudinal studies, larger and heterogeneous samples of participants, and explore the diverse categories of social support. This study draws attention to the necessity of the implementation of hospital policies in order to promote more involvement of family members, that may markedly improve the QoL of these patients.
