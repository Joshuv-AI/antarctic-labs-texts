Well‐Being Unveiled: A Concept Analysis of Mental, Psychological, and Subjective Well‐Being
Authors: Leona Cilar Budler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimMental well‐being is a multifaceted concept that has garnered increasing attention in both academic and clinical settings. It is influenced by a range of factors, including interpersonal relationships, self‐perception, and environmental conditions. The aim of this paper is to differentiate among three distinct concepts: mental well‐being, psychological well‐being, and subjective well‐being. Additionally, we conducted an in‐depth examination of the concept of mental well‐being.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted across international databases from January to March 2023. The gathered data was then meticulously analyzed using methods for concept analysis by Walker and Avant. Papers were read and analyzed to describe the attributes, antecedents, and consequences of mental well‐being.ResultsThrough a comprehensive review of literature, we found that the key attributes of mental well‐being are interpersonal relations, self‐acceptance, and purpose in life. This concept's antecedents range from individual expectations and values to broader factors like environment and personal development. Furthermore, the consequences of mental well‐being extend beyond individual health, impacting social support, disease risk, and the effectiveness of mental health interventions.ConclusionsFindings contribute to a nuanced understanding of mental well‐being, offering valuable insights into both research and clinical practice. Concept analysis highlights the multifaceted nature of mental well‐being and provides a foundation for future research and intervention development.Keywords: concept analysis, mental well‐being, psychological well‐being, redefinition, subjective well‐being

Introduction

Well‐being is defined as “the combination of feeling good and functioning well; the experience of positive emotions such as happiness and contentment as well as the development of one's potential, having some control over one's life, having a sense of purpose, and experiencing positive relationships” [1]. Mental well‐being and mental health are used interchangeably throughout literature. Whereas psychological well‐being is used as an alternative phrasing for mental health and subjective well‐being as a synonym for happiness [2]. Despite the common usage of these terms, there remains a lack of consensus on their precise definitions. For instance, while some researchers argue for a multidimensional approach to well‐being that includes emotional, social, and psychological components [3, 4], others emphasize the need for clearer distinctions between these constructs to avoid conflating them in research and practice [5]. This ambiguity is particularly evident in studies examining the effects of various factors on well‐being, where the lack of clear definitions can obscure the relationships being investigated [6]. The definitions depend on culture and understanding, yet no clear definitions were given and differences between concepts were not described [7, 8].

Mental well‐being is a multifaceted concept that has garnered increasing attention in both academic and clinical settings [9, 10]. It is a dynamic construct that is influenced by a myriad of factors, ranging from individual personality traits to external environmental conditions [11]. Understanding mental well‐being is crucial not just for mental health professionals but also for policymakers, educators, and individuals who aim to improve the quality of life at both the individual and community levels [9]. Psychological well‐being is often regarded as a key dimension of mental well‐being, rooted in the principles of positive psychology. This concept emphasizes the positive aspects of an individual's mental state, incorporating elements such as self‐acceptance, personal growth, autonomy, and a sense of purpose in life [4, 12]. While psychological well‐being shares attributes with mental well‐being—such as purpose and self‐acceptance, it is typically viewed as a subset that specifically examines the functional aspects of mental health [13, 14]. The distinction between psychological well‐being and mental well‐being is crucial, as it allows for a more nuanced understanding of mental health that includes both functional and emotional dimensions. Subjective well‐being, in contrast, focuses primarily on the emotional aspects of mental health and is often equated with happiness or life satisfaction. It involves how individuals evaluate their lives, encompassing both emotional reactions and cognitive judgments [15, 16]. Unlike psychological well‐being, which explores various functional dimensions, subjective well‐being is primarily concerned with an individual's emotional state at a specific moment [17]. Although these concepts are interrelated, they are not interchangeable; mental well‐being serves as a broader term that encompasses both psychological and subjective well‐being [18, 19]. Psychological well‐being offers a more rounded look at an individual's mental state, considering factors like personal growth and purpose in life. Subjective well‐being, meanwhile, offers a snapshot of an individual's emotional state at a given time. Both are important for a comprehensive understanding of an individual's mental health and overall well‐being.

Aim

The aim of this paper is to differentiate among three distinct concepts: mental well‐being, psychological well‐being, and subjective well‐being, ensuring clarity and addressing conceptual overlaps. This concept analysis was conducted with an international perspective, including literature from diverse cultural and geographical contexts to enhance the global relevance and applicability of the findings.

Methods

Concept analysis is a rigorous process pioneered by the philosopher Wilson [20]. Alternative methodologies, such as grounded theory or framework analysis, were considered during the planning phase. However, grounded theory was deemed less suitable due to its focus on generating new theories rather than analyzing and refining existing concepts. Similarly, framework analysis, while effective for thematic organization, was excluded as it prioritizes qualitative data synthesis rather than concept dissection. Walker and Avant's [21] method was selected because it aligns with the primary aim of this study: to systematically define and distinguish between overlapping concepts by identifying their attributes, antecedents, and consequences. Although alternative methodologies such as Rodgers' evolutionary concept analysis were considered, they were not selected due to their emphasis on contextual evolution and temporality. Given the aim of this paper—to systematically differentiate three overlapping concepts at a specific point in time and identify their defining characteristics—Walker and Avant's structured eight‐step model was deemed more appropriate. It offers a clear framework for identifying attributes, antecedents, consequences, and empirical referents, which aligns directly with the analytical goals of this study.

The literature review was designed with an international scope, selecting databases that index publications from multiple countries and research traditions. No geographical or cultural limitations were imposed during the search process, allowing for the inclusion of studies from diverse global settings. A comprehensive literature review was performed between January and March 2023 in the following databases: PubMed, CINHAL, Medline, and PsycArticles. Databases were selected based on their relevance to mental health and well‐being research. Specific inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to ensure rigor and relevance. Inclusion criteria focused on peer‐reviewed studies that explicitly defined or analyzed mental well‐being, psychological well‐being, or subjective well‐being, literature reviews and articles published in English. Studies with ambiguous or unclear definitions, or those primarily focused on unrelated constructs, were excluded. Both empirical studies and literature reviews were eligible if they directly analyzed or defined any of the three well‐being concepts. The following search string was used with appropriate Boolean operators and MeSH terms: (mental wellbeing OR psychological wellbeing OR subjective wellbeing) AND (definition). Although the initial search strategy retrieved a broad range of publications referring to mental, psychological, and subjective well‐being, we applied a strict inclusion filter aligned with the primary aim of this concept analysis. Specifically, only articles that explicitly defined, described, or analyzed the concept of mental well‐being were included in the final synthesis. Studies focusing solely on psychological or subjective well‐being, without addressing mental well‐being as a distinct construct, were excluded. This focused approach was essential to ensure conceptual clarity and consistency with the chosen methodological framework.

Results

There is no internationally recognized definition of mental well‐being yet. Thieme et al. [24] discussed that mental well‐being is still under‐theorized and under‐explored. Researchers often use two different concepts, mental health and mental well‐being, interchangeably. Also, concepts of mental well‐being and psychological well‐being seem to be synonyms.

Mental well‐being is a complex, multi‐faceted and dynamic concept [25]. Ryff [25] named it psychological well‐being. Stewart‐Brown & Janmohamed [26] defined mental well‐being as a concept that covers subjective experience of happiness and life satisfaction, and positive psychological functioning, good relationships with others, and self‐realization. Mental well‐being covers two perspectives: (1) the subjective experience of happiness (affect) and life satisfaction (the hedonic perspective); and (2) positive psychological functioning, good relationships with others and self‐realization (the eudaimonic perspective) [26, 27]. Mental wellbeing is enhanced when an individual can achieve a sense of purpose in society and can contribute to the community [28, 29] added that one should “look beyond mental health services into wider public services; then beyond public services into our society as a whole” [29], p. 35.

Defining Attributes

The crucial aspect of the analysis is the determination of the defining cluster of attributes most frequently associated with the concept [22]. The most common attributes were those related to interpersonal relations [25, 26, 30], self‐acceptance [24, 25, 26], and purpose in life [25, 26].

Mental well‐being can be analyzed through the dual lenses of hedonic (pleasure‐oriented) and eudaimonic (meaning‐oriented) perspectives. Attributes such as interpersonal relations and positive affect aligned with the hedonic perspective, emphasizing immediate happiness and life satisfaction. Conversely, attributes like self‐acceptance and purpose in life reflected the eudaimonic perspective, focusing on long‐term fulfillment and self‐realization. There are overlaps between these perspectives; for instance, interpersonal relations can simultaneously enhance momentary pleasure and contribute to a sense of deeper meaning. These complexities underscore the multifaceted nature of mental well‐being and suggest that individuals may integrate or balance these perspectives differently depending on personal or contextual factors.

Case Studies

Walker and Avant [22] indicate that case studies can be cases from real life, constructed or in the literature. The purpose of listing the cases is to demonstrate what the concept is and is not by using the defining attributes.

Model Case

The model case presents a pure case of the concept [22].

Jane is a 35‐year‐old nurse and a mother of two young children. Despite working in a high‐pressure hospital environment, she demonstrates strong emotional regulation, engages regularly in self‐care practices such as early morning jogging and mindfulness meditation, and maintains a healthy work‐life balance. She communicates openly and empathetically with her partner, enjoys supportive friendships (interpersonal relations), and regularly checks in with her team members at work, cultivating a positive and trusting environment. She expresses a clear sense of purpose, both professionally and personally, and often reflects on her values and long‐term goals (purpose in life). She accepts herself, including her imperfections, and describes feeling content and resilient in the face of everyday challenges (self‐acceptance).

Borderline Case

The borderline case provides an example where most of the attributes of the analyzed concept are present.

Jane is a 35‐year‐old high school teacher and mother of two, currently navigating a period of personal transition following a recent divorce. She manages to fulfill her professional responsibilities and continues engaging in physical activities like yoga once or twice a week. She has a small circle of friends and maintains regular contact with her parents (interpersonal relations). While she is generally functional in daily life, she finds it difficult to express or explore her emotions and tends to avoid deeper conversations about her inner state. She describes herself as “getting through the day” and occasionally questions her sense of direction in life (purpose in life). Although she experiences moments of connection and satisfaction, her self‐acceptance fluctuates, and she often feels unsure of her worth.

Contrary Case

The contrary case provides an example of where the attributes of the analyzed concept are not present.

Jane is a 35‐year‐old woman who has been unemployed for over a year following the closure of her small business. Since then, she has become increasingly withdrawn, rarely leaving her apartment. She has little to no contact with friends or family and reports feeling chronically tired, unmotivated, and “numb.” She spends most of her days watching TV and sleeping irregularly. She avoids engaging in meaningful activities or seeking help, stating that “nothing really matters anymore.” Jane describes herself as a failure and believes she has nothing to offer others. She has no clear sense of purpose, no emotional stability, and a severely diminished sense of self‐worth.

Antecedents

Antecedents are the events or incidents that occur before the occurrence of the concept [22]. We identified the following antecedents of the concept of mental well‐being: expectations, values, environment, perception, fully functioning person, maturity, executive processes of personality, basic life tendencies, personal development, will to meaning, mental health, individuation, and self‐actualization.

Consequences

Consequences are the events or incidents that result from the occurrence of the concept. In our case, identified consequences of the concept of mental well‐being are better mental well‐being, better social support, reduced risk for disease and promoting length of life, increased effectiveness of mental health interventions, help in preventing mental illness and relapse, and extended knowledge of support for people and enhancing quality of life.

Empirical Referents

The usage of empirical referents enables us to recognize and measure the defining attributes [22]. Tools like the Warwick‐Edinburgh Mental Well‐being Scale (WEMWBS) can provide quantitative data on mental well‐being. The WEMWBS is a self‐report questionnaire designed to assess mental well‐being in adults. Developed collaboratively by the University of Warwick and the University of Edinburgh, the scale is widely used in both clinical and research settings. The scale consists of 14 positively worded items and covers various aspects of positive mental health, including positive affect (feelings of happiness, contentment), psychological functioning (clear thinking, self‐acceptance), and interpersonal relationships. Each item is scored on a 5‐point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“None of the time”) to 5 (“All of the time”). The overall score can range from 14 to 70, with higher scores indicating better mental well‐being. The WEMWBS has shown good to excellent internal consistency in various studies. The tool effectively measures aspects of mental well‐being, including positive affect, psychological functioning, and interpersonal relationships. However, we acknowledge that WEMWBS has limitations in capturing certain cultural nuances and may not be as sensitive to detecting mental health disorders. For example, while it performs well in Western populations, its applicability in diverse cultural settings has been questioned. Studies such as Shah et al. [34] and Taggart et al. [35] report that cultural differences in interpreting concepts like happiness, self‐acceptance, and interpersonal relationships may influence responses. The lack of culturally specific items could limit its sensitivity to the nuanced expressions of mental well‐being in non‐Western populations. Nevertheless, some culturally adapted versions of the WEMWBS have been developed and validated. For example, Shah et al. [34] validated the WEMWBS among South Asian communities in the UK, while Taggart et al. [35] assessed its psychometric properties across several European countries. In addition, the short version of the scale (SWEMWBS) has been proposed for use in large‐scale surveys, offering a more pragmatic option in culturally diverse contexts. Where cultural adaptation of WEMWBS is not feasible, alternative tools may be considered. The Mental Health Continuum–Short Form (MHC‐SF), developed by Keyes, has been validated across multiple cultures and includes emotional, psychological, and social well‐being domains. Similarly, the WHO‐5 Well‐Being Index, due to its brevity and simplicity, has shown good cross‐cultural applicability and may be more suitable for use in diverse global settings. The relationship between mental well‐being, disease risk, and social support has been supported by longitudinal studies. For instance, Ryff et al. [36] found that individuals with higher psychological well‐being had a lower incidence of chronic diseases and better health outcomes over time. Similarly, Huppert [37] demonstrated that mental well‐being predicts the strength of social connections, which in turn buffer against stress and promote resilience. These studies underscore the critical role mental well‐being plays in enhancing both physical health and social support networks. Long‐term data is essential for understanding the causal relationships between mental well‐being and its outcomes. Longitudinal studies can track changes in mental well‐being over time and establish temporal links between well‐being, disease risk, and social support. For example, such data can clarify whether improvements in mental well‐being precede reductions in disease risk or whether stronger social support networks enhance mental well‐being. By capturing these dynamics, long‐term research can inform targeted interventions and public health strategies that leverage mental well‐being to improve overall health outcomes.

While there are no other widely known tools for measuring mental well‐being, interviews can be used to assess various dimensions of mental well‐being. Qualitative methods such as semi‐structured or narrative interviews, followed by thematic analysis, can provide deeper insight into personal meanings, values, and contextual factors related to mental well‐being. For example, thematic analysis allows researchers to identify patterns across participant narratives, while interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA) is well‐suited for exploring how individuals make sense of their mental and emotional states. These approaches are particularly useful in populations where culturally sensitive or individualized understandings of well‐being may not be adequately captured by standardized scales.

Discussion

According to the definition of the World Health Organization (WHO), mental health is “a state of well‐being in which the individual realizes his or her own abilities, can cope with the normal stresses of life, can work productively and fruitfully, and is able to make a contribution to his or her community”. World Health Organization WHO [38] It is stated that mental health includes persons emotional, psychological, and social well‐being. Also, it affects the way we think, feel, act, handle stress, relate to others, and make healthy choices [39]. On the other hand, Galderisi et al. [40] proposed new alternative definition: “Mental health is a dynamic state of internal equilibrium which enables individuals to use their abilities in harmony with universal values of society. Basic cognitive and social skills; ability to recognize, express and modulate one's own emotions, as well as empathize with others; flexibility and ability to cope with adverse life events and function in social roles; and harmonious relationship between body and mind represent important components of mental health which contribute, to varying degrees, to the state of internal equilibrium.” Wren‐Lewis & Alexandrova [41] discussed that mental health includes psychological preconditions of pursuing any conception of the good life, including well‐being, without being identical to the concept of well‐being.

The findings provide a nuanced understanding of mental well‐being, highlighting its complexity and the interplay between its defining attributes and underlying perspectives. These insights contribute to advancing both theoretical clarity and practical applications, such as the design of targeted interventions that address both hedonic and eudaimonic dimensions. The interaction between these two dimensions can be observed in various real‐world contexts. For example, in workplace settings, initiatives such as recognition programs or team‐building activities may enhance subjective (hedonic) well‐being through increased positive affect, while opportunities for professional development and meaningful tasks foster eudaimonic aspects like autonomy and purpose. Similarly, in clinical interventions, approaches like positive psychotherapy or acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) integrate emotional satisfaction with long‐term value alignment, illustrating how these dimensions function synergistically [37, 42].

Mental well‐being is a state of positive psychological and emotional health [42]. Cilar Budler [30] performed research among Slovenian adolescents and asked them about their opinion on what mental well‐being is. The following definition was proposed: “Mental well‐being is a positive term which describes a positive state of an individual where he/she accepts himself/herself, can function in society, and can exploit their potential. Aspects that constitute mental well‐being are positive relationships with others, self‐acceptance, culturally supported values, and expectations.” They added that mental well‐being is closely related to mental health, everyday well‐being, reaction to certain situations, freedom to enjoy certain situations and self‐image. Stewart‐Brown & Janmohamed [26] state that mental well‐being covers the subjective experience of happiness and life satisfaction, positive psychological functioning, good relationships with others, and self‐realization.

Psychological well‐being is defined as “one's overall psychological condition. This condition has broad cognitive, emotional and behavioral implications and aspects”. Bar‐On [43] Psychological well‐being consists of autonomy, environmental mastery, personal growth, personal relationships, purpose in life, and self‐acceptance [44, 45]. Ryff's [25] multidimensional model of psychological well‐being has received the most empirical support [25]. It consists of six dimensions: (1) autonomy ‐ ability to regulate own behavior, resist social pressure, and follow convictions; (2) environmental mastery ‐ ability to manage the context and daily activities; (3) personal growth ‐ continuous process of developing own potential; (4) positive relationships with others ‐ establishment of close, trusting and meaningful bonds with others, as well as showing concern for the well‐being of others, and the expression of empathy, affection, and intimacy; (5) purpose in life ‐ setting objectives and goals which give meaning and direction to our lives; and (6) self‐acceptance ‐ ability to have a positive attitude and feelings of satisfaction and acceptance of ourselves. The eudaimonic view of well‐being claims that well‐being consists of more than just happiness [46].

While psychological well‐being, especially through Ryff's model, focuses on deep psychological functioning and long‐term fulfillment, mental well‐being is often framed more broadly, encompassing both hedonic (e.g., happiness, positive affect) and eudaimonic (e.g., purpose, self‐realization) components. This broader framing is reflected in measurement tools such as the WEMWBS, which includes emotional states, cognitive functioning, and interpersonal aspects [26, 31]. Importantly, these constructs are treated separately in empirical research. For instance, Keyes et al. [45] and Ryff [25] use distinct scales that do not overlap with WEMWBS, which supports the position that psychological well‐being may be considered a structured subset of the broader construct of mental well‐being. Thus, although interrelated, the two constructs differ both conceptually and empirically.

Subjective well‐being is “explained by everything else that could potentially be experienced, felt or happen in life, ranging from the circumstances of people's lives (income, employment, etc.) through to activities they engage in (sport, culture, arts, etc.), as well as the thoughts in their mind, which cover a whole range of possible mental health conditions.” [47] Subjective well‐being comprises the cognitive component of satisfaction with life as a whole, the presence of positive emotions (e.g., joy, interest, love, or pride) and the absence of negative emotions (e.g., loneliness, anger, anxiety) as affective components [44, 48, 49]. The hedonic view of well‐being reflects the well‐being as an outcome, consisting of an internal state of pleasure or happiness, and focuses on subjective well‐being [46].

These findings have significant implications for both public health policies and clinical practice. Evidence‐based interventions such as Mindfulness‐Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) provide substantial benefits in improving self‐acceptance and emotional regulation within clinical populations suffering from anxiety, depression, and chronic stress [50]. According to Kabat‐Zinn, MBSR enhances individuals' capabilities to manage their thoughts and emotions, leading to increased psychological flexibility and a heightened sense of self‐acceptance [50]. Research demonstrates that mindfulness interventions have been effectively implemented in clinical settings, highlighting their focus on self‐regulation and stress reduction [51]. Mindfulness‐Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) has gained recognition in hospitals and mental health facilities for aiding individuals in recovery from depression, though its specific impact relative to MBSR can vary among individuals [50]. In addition to individual‐oriented interventions, community engagement initiatives—such as volunteer programs and social support groups—play a pivotal role in fostering interpersonal relationships and social connectedness, which are crucial for mental well‐being [52, 53]. Programs designed to enhance social support not only promote bonding among participants but also improve their sense of belonging and provide emotional backing, essential components for cultivating mental resilience and overall health [52, 53]. Moreover, community wellness programs sponsored by governments, focusing on mental health and social connection, can significantly enhance the well‐being of at‐risk groups by strengthening their social support networks [51, 54]. The integration of mindfulness training and community‐based support systems not only addresses individual mental health issues but also promotes a holistic approach to public health, potentially leading to broader societal benefits [55].

Study Limitations

Although a comprehensive concept analysis of mental well‐being is essential, it's important to consider certain limitations. In the literature review and contextualization section, the selection process for the specific databases (PubMed, CINAHL, MEDLINE, and PsycArticles) was guided by their relevance to mental well‐being and health sciences research. However, the inclusion of additional or alternative databases, such as Scopus or PsycINFO, could have enriched the scope of the review, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the topic. Furthermore, by limiting the search to English‐language publications, the study may have unintentionally excluded culturally specific interpretations of mental well‐being. This introduces a potential cultural bias and may limit the generalizability of findings to non‐English‐speaking or non‐Western populations. While the selected databases do include international studies, future research should aim to expand both the linguistic and database scope to ensure a more globally inclusive conceptual analysis. Furthermore, the study highlights a lack of clear, internationally recognized definitions for mental well‐being. Efforts were not explicitly made to include non‐English language studies, which could have influenced the findings and provided broader insights into mental well‐being across different cultural contexts. The approach relies heavily on the researcher's interpretation, which can introduce bias. Walker's and Avant's method is designed to analyze a single concept in isolation, which may not capture the complexity of how it interacts with other concepts. However, we discussed similar yet different concepts—psychological well‐being and subjective well‐being. Another limitation of this study is that the findings are specific to the context in which the analysis was conducted, limiting generalizability. Finally, there's a risk of oversimplifying the complexity of the concept by breaking it down into attributes, antecedents, and consequences. The researcher should be aware of these limitations and take steps to mitigate their impact.

Future Research

Future research should adopt mixed‐methods approaches, combining qualitative and quantitative studies to validate findings across diverse populations. Collaborations with researchers from non‐Western and underrepresented contexts could help refine the conceptual distinctions and test their applicability in varied cultural settings. Moreover, longitudinal studies and meta‐analyses across multiple regions could further enhance the generalizability of findings by examining the stability of mental well‐being attributes over time and in different environments.

The distinctions between mental health, mental well‐being, and psychological well‐being have significant implications for interventions and policies. Understanding these distinctions can guide clinicians in tailoring interventions. For instance, while mental health interventions may prioritize addressing disorders, promoting mental well‐being could involve fostering attributes like self‐acceptance and purpose in life through positive psychology practices. Policies aimed at improving mental well‐being can leverage these distinctions to create holistic programs. For example, interventions promoting community engagement and interpersonal relationships may enhance mental well‐being at the population level. These distinctions can also inform the development of measurement tools that separately assess mental health and well‐being dimensions, enabling more targeted interventions.

Conclusion

Further research could be performed that directly compares mental health, mental well‐being, and psychological well‐being to better understand their similarities and differences. Given that mental well‐being and psychological well‐being share similar or sometimes the same attributes, there may be a need for new measurement tools that can distinguish between them effectively. Healthcare providers and researchers should be trained to understand the distinctions between these concepts to ensure accurate diagnosis and effective treatment. Public health campaigns could help educate the general public about the differences between mental health and mental well‐being, as well as their importance. By addressing these areas, future work can provide a more nuanced understanding of mental health and mental well‐being, thereby improving both research and clinical practice.
