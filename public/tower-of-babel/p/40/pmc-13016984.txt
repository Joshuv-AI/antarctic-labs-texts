Host preference and resting behavior of Anopheles coluzzii (Diptera: Culicidae) on the island of São Tomé, West Africa
Authors: Gustavo Rocha, Maria Júlia M Corrêa, Janete Marbel, João Viegas, Melina Campos, Marc Crepeau, Claire M Egan, Anthony J Cornel, Carla A Sousa, Gregory C Lanzaro, João Pinto, Maria Anice Sallum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHost preference and resting behavior of Anopheles coluzzii Coetzee & Wilkerson, 2013 were investigated on São Tomé and Príncipe Islands following implementation of an intensive malaria vector control program. Blood meals from indoor- and outdoor-resting females collected in 2021–2024 were identified using established PCR assays targeting mammalian- and avian-specific polymorphisms in the mitochondrial cytochrome b gene. In São Tomé, a total of 2,211 resting mosquitoes were collected, 90% of which were sampled outdoors. An overall human blood index (HBI) of 4% was obtained from 1,227 females analyzed. Most nonhuman blood meals were from pigs (40%) and dogs (50%). Príncipe Island exhibited an HBI of 79%, but only 17 female mosquitoes were sampled, 77% of which indoors. These findings highlight behavioral adaptations in An. coluzzii populations, toward zoophily and exophily, that may reduce the effectiveness of indoor-based vector control measures and therefore underscore the importance of novel strategies targeting outdoor-resting mosquitoes.Keywords: malaria, endophily, exophily, zoophily, anthropophily

Introduction

Female Anopheles mosquitoes require a blood meal for egg production and embryonic development. Host preference of mosquito vectors is a critical factor in pathogen transmission and in determining the vectorial capacity of a mosquito population (Garrett-Jones 1964). Anopheles mosquitoes, including African species, exhibit a broad range of host preferences, feeding on avian, mammalian, and reptilian hosts (Githeko et al. 1994, Mahande et al. 2007). The major African malaria vectors demonstrate considerable intraspecific plasticity in host preference, often modifying their biting behavior depending on the availability of local host species (Lefèvre et al. 2009, Orsborne et al. 2018). Anopheles coluzzii Coetzee & Wilkerson, 2013 and Anopheles gambiae Giles, 1902, 2 primary malaria vectors in Africa, are widely regarded as being highly anthropophilic and endophilic (Costantini et al. 1996, Mwangangi et al. 2003, Akogbéto et al. 2018, Perugini et al. 2020). However, some studies suggest that these mosquitoes may, under certain conditions, prefer dogs or cattle over humans (Duchemin et al. 2001, Sousa et al. 2001).

After feeding, female mosquitoes seek out resting sites for blood meal digestion and egg development (Pates and Curtis 2005). This resting behavior, particularly the tendency to rest indoors (endophily) or outdoors (exophily), has significant epidemiological implications (Heggenhougen et al. 2003). Endophilic mosquitoes are more vulnerable to indoor insecticide-based interventions such as insecticide-treated bed nets and indoor residual spraying (IRS), while exophilic species pose challenges for conventional adult mosquito control. Long-lasting insecticide-treated nets (LLINs) and IRS have been highly effective in reducing mosquito biting rates (Russell et al. 2011, Padonou et al. 2012, Ossè et al. 2013). However, there is evidence that these vector control measures may select for behavioral adaptations such as outdoor and zoophagic host-seeking (Bayoh et al. 2010, Reddy et al. 2011, Russell et al. 2011, Carrasco et al. 2019).

São Tomé and Príncipe (STP), an island nation in the Gulf of Guinea off the coast of Gabon in Central Africa, hosts a single malaria vector species, An. coluzzii (Pinto et al. 2000). A previous study in the region found a high human blood index (HBI) in indoor-resting An. coluzzii populations, but a tendency to blood feed on dogs in outdoor-resting individuals (Sousa et al. 2001). The availability of alternative hosts may contribute to this ­pattern. Household interviews conducted in 1997–1998 in the locality of Riboque (ca. 1,000 inhabitants), revealed that chickens were the most common potential nonhuman host (61%), followed by dogs (27%), pigs (8%), and other animals, such as goats and ducks (4%), highlighting the presence of diverse nonhuman hosts close to human dwellings (Sousa et al. 2001).

Since the early 2000s, the country has implemented intensive malaria vector control measures, including the scale-up of IRS and mass distribution of LLINs (Teklehaimanot et al. 2009, Lee et al. 2010). While these efforts brought STP to the brink of malaria elimination, malaria levels have remained unaltered since 2015 (Wang et al. 2022). Recent evidence indicates high levels of pyrethroid resistance in An. coluzzii, particularly on São Tomé Island (Corrêa et al. 2024). Pyrethroid resistance has also been increasing in other regions of sub-Saharan Africa following the intensive use of the same class of insecticides (Padonou et al. 2011, WHO 2024).

In this study, we investigated the host preferences and resting behavior of An. coluzzii in STP to better understand the mosquito population’s behavioral patterns following years of intensive vector control interventions.

Materials and Methods

Study Area

The islands of STP are located in the Gulf of Guinea, approximately 250 and 225 km off the west coast of Central Africa, respectively (Fig. S1). São Tomé Island is home to approximately 220,000 inhabitants, more than 90% of the country’s population. Most residents on this island live in wooden walled houses built on stilts, roughly 2 m off the ground. In Príncipe Island, most coastal houses are also elevated and constructed with wooden walls, whereas houses located in the center of the island are not elevated above the ground and made of a combination of wood, concrete, or metal structures. Mosquito collections took place in 6 localities in São Tomé Island: Bairro da Liberdade, Praia Gamboa, Nova Olinda, Ribeira Afonso, Micondó, and Neves; and in 11 localities in Príncipe Island: Rua dos Trabalhadores, Santo António Segundo, Santo António Praia, Praia Abade, Praia Burra, São Joaquim, Nova Estrela, Aeroporto, Hospital velho, Azeitona, and Alojamento (Fig. S1).

Mosquito Collections

Resting mosquitoes in São Tomé were collected in December 2021–May 2022 and May–November 2023, whereas in Principe collections took place in January–November 2024 (Fig. S1). Outdoor collections took place beneath houses, in pigpens, kennels, and in the vegetation surrounding human dwellings between 5 and 7 AM. In 2023 and 2024, collections were also carried out indoors in inhabited human dwellings, using the same aspiration method. Although sampling time was not formally recorded at each locality, collections followed a standardized protocol in which each collector would sample a household or outdoor resting site for approximately 10 min.

Collected Anopheles mosquitoes were morphologically identified to species or species complex using the key of (Coetzee 2020). Anopheles gambiae s.l. mosquitoes collected in 2021–2022 were stored individually in 2-ml tubes with 80% ethanol and sent to the University of California, Davis, for further processing. A subsample of 96 individuals was initially inspected under a microscope to determine the presence of blood in the abdomen. Due to difficulties in morphological assessment after preservation in ethanol, an alternative method was used to detect blood during DNA extraction. The color of the lysate was verified: a reddish-brown color indicated the presence of blood, while a clear color indicated its absence.

In São Tome, 2023, mosquito samples were transported alive from the field to the UCMI molecular biology laboratory at the University São Tomé and Príncipe, São Tomé City. Upon arrival, mosquitoes were immobilized by exposure to −20 °C for 5 min and classified according to their abdominal condition, as follows: unfed (Sella’s stage 1), blood-fed (Sella’s stages 2 and 3), half-gravid (Sella’s stages 4 and 5), and gravid (Sella’s stages 6 and 7), according to (Detinova 1963). In Príncipe, 2024, mosquitoes were killed and identified in the field. The abdominal contents of fully fed females were spread onto Whatman No. 1 filter paper, dried, and stored at room temperature. The remaining carcass was preserved in 2-ml tubes containing 80% ethanol. These samples were kept for analysis at the UCMI molecular biology laboratory.

Blood Meal Analysis

Individual samples collected in 2021 and 2022 were homogenized for DNA extraction using the optimized protocol of (Nieman et al. 2015) on a Biosprint 96 instrument (QIAGEN, Redwood City, California, United States). For samples collected in 2023 and 2024, 3-mm2 sections of the blood spot filter paper samples were extracted using the PeqGOLD Blood and Tissue DNA Kit (VWR Avantor, Lisbon, Portugal).

A multiplex PCR assay was performed to detect blood meals from 4 mammals using cytochrome B-specific markers for human, dog, pig, and goat DNA (Kent and Norris 2005). These hosts were selected based on their relevance as potential blood sources for An. coluzzii in São Tomé, as previously reported (Sousa et al. 2001). The PCR mix included 0.33 µM of each primer (Pig573F, Human741F, Goat894F, Dog368F, and UNREV1025), 2 µl of DNA template (∼9 ng), and 12.5 µl of GoTaq Green Master Mix (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin, United States) for the samples collected in 2021 and 2022, or 12.5 µl of VWR Taq Plus 2x (VWR Avantor, Lisbon, Portugal) for samples collected in 2023 and 2024, for a volume totaling 25 µl. The thermocycling conditions were 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95°C for 1 min, 58 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 1 min, a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min, and a 4 °C hold.

A distinct PCR assay was used to detect chicken blood using the primers described by (Ngo and Kramer 2003). The PCR mix and cycling conditions were identical to the methods for the mammal assay, with the exception of the annealing temperature, which was set at 56 °C rather than 58 °C.

The PCR products were separated by electrophoresis in a 1% agarose gel and visualized under UV light. The gel was stained with ethidium bromide at the University of California, Davis, and stained with Safe DNA Gel Satin x20000 (APExBIO Technology, Houston, Texas, United States) at the UCMI molecular biology laboratory in São Tomé.

Data Analysis

The HBI was estimated as the proportion of single or mixed blood meals taken from humans divided by the total number of blood meals analyzed (Garrett-Jones 1964). Differences in positive sites, outdoor and indoor collections, as well as differences in the distribution of predominant blood meals taken between indoor and outdoor resting mosquitoes were assessed using Fisher’s exact test. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to assess differences in the number of mosquitos collected per site between indoor and outdoor collections. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals for the HBI were calculated using exact Clopper–Pearson binomial methods. All statistical analysis were performed in excel and R (version 4.5.1).

The ratio between fully fed females and half-gravid + gravid females (F:HGG) resting indoors or outdoors was calculated as an estimate of the degree of endophily or exophily of the mosquito population. The ratio reflects whether a given resting site is preferentially used by females after blood feeding to complete at least part of their gonotrophic cycle. When applied to indoor collections, a ratio less than 1 indicates a predominance of half-gravid and gravid females, suggesting that females tend to remain indoors after blood feeding (endophily). In contrast, a ratio higher than 1 indicates a predominance of fully fed females, suggesting that females leave the indoor site after feeding (exophily). The same interpretation applies to outdoor collections, where a ratio less than 1 indicates exophilic resting behavior (Krafsur 1977, Ameneshewa and Service 1996).

Results

A total of 2,211 An. coluzzii mosquitoes (1,624 females and 587 males) were collected in the 6 localities sampled in São Tomé. On Príncipe Island, only 18 An. coluzzii mosquitoes (17 females and 1 male) were sampled in a total of 391 collection sites, corresponding to an average of approximately 0.05 mosquitoes per site. These mosquitoes were collected in 5 out of the 11 localities surveyed.

Host Preference and HBI

A total of 1,227 blood meals were analyzed. Most blood meals were from a single host species (87.2%), 7.5% were from mixed hosts (1.1% of human blood), and 5.3% tested negative (Fig. 1). On São Tomé Island, the overall HBI obtained for São Tomé Island was 3.7%, ranging between 0.8% in Praia Gamboa and 13.8% in Micondó. In Ribeira Afonso, HBI estimates of 3.4% (N = 237) and 2.2% (N = 139) were obtained for the 2021/2022 and 2023/2024 collections, respectively, a nonsignificant difference (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.21), No other temporal comparisons were made as localities differed between sampling periods (Fig. S1). Dogs and pigs were the most prevalent hosts in all localities surveyed with no apparent within-island geographic pattern (Fig. 1). Pig blood meals accounted for approximately 53.8% of all samples analyzed, followed by dog (40.5%), chicken (2.3%), and goat (1.4%) blood meals.

On Príncipe Island, humans were the predominant host, with an HBI of 78.6%. However, only 14 blood meals were analyzed for this island. Among the nonhuman hosts detected in Príncipe, only pig (2 samples) and goat (1 sample) blood meals were identified.

Resting Behavior

Indoor/outdoor resting behavior was assessed for the collections performed in 2023 and 2024 in São Tomé Island. Overall, outdoor collections yielded larger numbers of resting An. coluzzii, as evidenced by both the proportion of positive resting sites and the number of mosquitoes collected per resting site surveyed (Table 1). The proportion of positive resting sites was significantly higher outdoors than indoors (Fisher’s exact test, P < 0.001), and the number of mosquitos per resting site was also significantly greater in outdoor collections (Mann–Whitney U test, P < 0.001). This trend was not evident in Príncipe Island, where more An. coluzzii were caught indoors (N = 13). However, resting An. coluzzii were difficult to find in Príncipe, with only 17 total female mosquitoes sampled out of 391 surveyed sites (158 indoor and 233 outdoor). Therefore, comparison between indoor and outdoor resting fractions was limited to the 2 localities in São Tomé Island: Bairro da Liberdade and Ribeira Afonso.

For the 2023 collection, the distribution of indoor versus outdoor resting blood meals was also assessed at 2 localities on São Tomé Island: Bairro da Liberdade and Ribeira Afonso (Fig. 2). Discussion

This study identified an exceptionally zoophilic population of An. coluzzii on São Tomé Island. This mosquito population exhibits a noteworthy predilection for nonhuman hosts, particularly dogs and pigs, with an HBI of only 3.7%. This low HBI is highly uncommon in populations of primary mosquito vector species; it is usually characteristic of populations of nonvector, or secondary vector species, such as Anopheles coustani Laveran, 1900 in Africa or the neotropical Anopheles vestitipennis Dyar & Knap, 1906 (Muriu et al. 2008, Escobar et al. 2020). However, exceptional cases have been found in other populations of primary vector species. An overall HBI of 6%, and a pronounced preference for bovines was documented for An. coluzzii in the village of Dogo, Ghana (Orsborne et al. 2020). The authors explain this feeding preference by extrinsic factors such as host availability and resting site selection, rather than intrinsic genetic factors. A systematic review showed that resting site collection may greatly influence HBI estimates, with indoor-collected mosquitoes showing higher HBI than their outdoor resting counterparts (Orsborne et al. 2018). This trend was not identified in the present study, as no indoor-resting mosquitoes had fed on humans. This may suggest that the zoophilic behavior of An. coluzzii found in São Tomé Island may reflect a combination of ecological, behavioral, and potential genetic determinants. A genetic component for host preference (human vs. cow) was reported for Anopheles arabiensis Patton, 1905, a close sister species to An. coluzzii (Main et al. 2016).

In São Tomé Island, a survey carried out in 1986 found an HBI of 96% in indoor-resting An. gambiae s.l. (Ribeiro et al. 1990). Eleven years later, Sousa et al. (2001) reported an HBI of 94% for indoor-resting mosquitoes and 27% for mosquitoes resting outdoors. Both studies pre-dated the scaling-up of malaria vector control intervention in the early 2000s and clearly contrast with the low HBI estimates found in both indoor (0%) and outdoor (4.4%) resting mosquitoes in the present study. The markedly low HBI observed here may be associated with the intensified malaria control interventions that have been implemented in STP since the mid-2000s.

In 2005, a scale-up of IRS using α-cypermethrin and the widespread distribution of LLINs were implemented on the islands (Teklehaimanot et al. 2009, Lee et al. 2010). Evidence from other African settings shows that scaling up IRS and LLINs is linked to significant changes in the blood-feeding behavior of Anopheles vectors, including reduced anthropophily. For example, (Pombi et al. 2018) reported an HBI of only 20.1% for An. coluzzii populations in a village with high LLIN coverage in Burkina Faso. (Kreppel et al. 2020) observed a significant reduction in the HBI of An. arabiensis, from approximately 21% to 7% over a 4-year period following mass LLINs distribution in Tanzania. This was accompanied by an increased tendency to feed on cattle. Similarly, in Ethiopia, 2 rounds of bendiocarb IRS in 2 consecutive years (2013 and 2014) caused a relative adjusted reduction in HBI ranging between 3% and 10% across different collection methods and periods (Abate and Wale 2019). Altogether, these findings support the hypothesis that IRS and LLINs can drive changes toward zoophilic host selection behavior. Further studies involving direct measures of mosquito exposure to insecticides and feeding behavior shifts would be required to fully ascertain this hypothesis.

In São Tomé Island, 2023, more than 90% of the total mosquitoes collected were found outdoors, suggesting a largely exophilic An. coluzzii population. A previous study in São Tomé by (Sousa et al. 2001) found similar results, where the number of mosquitoes collected outdoors was 6-fold greater than those collected indoors. In that 1997–1998 survey, outdoor collections showed an F:HGG of 0.66, while indoor collections showed an F:HGG of 28.3, confirming a predominantly exophilic population (Sousa et al. 2001). However, this trend was not clearly evident in the present study. There was a general deficit of late-stage females in both indoor and outdoor collections, excluding the indoor collection of Bairro da Liberdade with an F:HGG close to 1. While the small sample sizes of indoor collections may have influenced this result, the relatively low number of half-gravid and gravid females collected outdoors is not so easily explained. One possibility is that outdoor resting sites may have been underrepresented in the present study if, for example, females tend to complete their gonotrophic cycle in resting sites further away from households. Another possibility is that the difference in the number of mosquitoes collected indoors and outdoors reflects the indoor/outdoor biting population. In São Tomé Island, An. coluzzii is markedly exophagic, with only about 20% of mosquito bites occurring indoors at night (Sousa et al. 2001, Charlwood et al. 2003). If this is the case, then An. coluzzii in São Tomé Island may display a more opportunistic resting behavior, with outdoor biting mosquitoes resting predominantly outdoors and indoor biting mosquitoes resting indoors. Further studies involving additional outdoor sampling will be required to ascertain the degree of exophily of An. coluzzii in São Tomé Island.

An increase in exophily may result from selective pressure exerted by prolonged application of IRS and LLINs, potentially driving the mosquitoes toward resting outdoors. While no quantitative data on insecticide use were available for this study, malaria vector control in STP has relied primarily on large-scale IRS campaigns and LLINs distribution campaigns (CNE 2023). Similar trends have been reported in Ghana, where extensive use of IRS and LLINs was associated with increased outdoor resting of An. coluzzii and An. gambiae, potentially as an adaptive response to avoid contact with insecticides applied indoors (Forson et al. 2022). (Kreppel et al. 2020) observed a significant increase in outdoor resting of An. arabiensis and An. funestus s.l. following mass LLINs distribution in Tanzania. Notably, the same study showed a sharp decline in the HBI of An. arabiensis, highlighting that vector control interventions may simultaneously drive shifts in both resting behavior and host preference.

An overall HBI of 78.6% was reported for Príncipe Island, where most females were collected indoors. This apparent indoor predominance may reflect opportunistic feeding or resting behavior rather than true anthropophily (Takken and Verhulst 2013). However, despite a substantial sampling effort, the low number of resting mosquitoes caught (17 females and 1 male) limits confidence in these estimates. Such low numbers may reflect increased challenges in identifying the main outdoor resting sites for An. coluzzii on this island. It can also be a consequence of the impact of prolonged indoor-based vector control measures in reducing this mosquito population, as observed in other settings (Ngom et al. 2021).

Conclusion

This study confirms the peculiar behavioral patterns of An. coluzzii found on São Tomé Island, characterized by a strong tendency to zoophily and exophily (Sousa et al. 2001). These patterns, particularly an increase in zoophily since the late 1990s, may reflect a behavioral response to sustained malaria vector control using IRS and LLINs. The low HBI recorded in São Tomé Island suggests that malaria transmission is low, which agrees with the low levels of malaria incidence recorded in the country, a promising step toward malaria elimination (WHO 2024). However, the strong exophagic and exophilic behavior of An. coluzzii in São Tomé, coupled with their increased pyrethroid resistance (Corrêa et al. 2024) raise concerns about the sustainability of current insecticide-based indoor vector control and calls for the implementation of alternative control strategies that target exphilic and zoophilic populations.
