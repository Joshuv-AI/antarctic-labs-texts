Effects of Facility Cultivation Shaping Soil Microbial Community Structure in Jujube Orchard
Authors: Zhibo Ma, Junxiao Wang, Chen Huang, Yan Cao, Yue Sun, Yaning Hu, Mian Fazli Basit, Jian Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Facility cultivation systems provide protection for jujube (Ziziphus jujuba) against rain-induced fruit cracking during the maturation and regulate the fruit ripening process. Prolonged cultivation within these controlled environments may alter the soil microbial community structure, potentially detrimentally affecting plant growth and fruit quality. There is a lack of information regarding the arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) and bacterial communities in orchards under facility conditions. This study compared the soil bacterial and AMF communities in jujube orchards under greenhouse and rain shelter conditions. Greenhouse cultivation significantly increased soil organic carbon (SOC), total nitrogen (TN), and electrical conductivity, while it decreased soil pH compared to rain shelters. These changes were associated with reduced α-diversity indices in both bacterial and AMF communities. Non-metric multidimensional scaling analysis demonstrated distinct differences between bacteria and AMF communities under the two cultivation types. The phyla Actinobacteria, Gemmatimonadetes, and Rokubacteria were identified as key contributors to the observed alterations in the bacterial community, while variations in the genus Glomus and Paraglomus were responsible for changes in the AMF communities between the two cultivation types. Redundancy analysis revealed that pH was the primary factor shaping microbial community structure across the two cultivation types. Using a Zi-Pi plot, we identified several keystone ASVs, which showed a positive correlation with pH, SOC, and TN. The findings highlight the significant impact of cultivation type on soil microbial community structure and function, which has important implications for optimizing cultivation practices and ensuring sustainable jujube production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00248-025-02574-5.

Introduction

Jujube (Ziziphus jujuba Mill.) an economically significant fruit tree species in the Rhamnaceae family, has been cultivated in China for over 4000 years and is now widely distributed across temperate regions globally [1]. Despite its excellent taste and rich nutritional value, which have increased its demand, rain-induced fruit cracking remains a significant challenge, causing substantial crop losses in numerous production areas [2]. Climate change predictions suggest that the increasing frequency of excessive rainfall likely increases the occurrence of fruit cracking (IPCC, 2013). Unfortunately, most current jujube cultivars exhibit limited tolerance to cracking, which has contributed to the decline of jujube cultivation in regions along the middle and lower reaches of the Yellow River since 2000 [3]. To mitigate this issue, facility cultivation techniques, such as rain shelters and greenhouses, have been introduced. These facilities not only protect crops from adverse weather conditions but also regulate the maturation and ripening processes, thereby enhancing fruit quality. Such agricultural practices significantly enhance the efficient utilization of natural resources and play a crucial role in boosting farmers’ incomes [4, 5].

The soil biota comprises a diverse array of microbes, including bacteria and fungi, which collectively form a microbiome characterized by high phylogenetic, taxonomic, and functional diversity. These organisms play a dual role in the soil ecosystem: they release essential nutrients that support plant growth, while also interacting directly with plants in the rhizosphere. Specifically, they can feed on or infect roots, form symbiotic relationships such as mycorrhizae, or promote plant growth through phytohormone production and the mitigation of plant stress signaling [6, 7]. This multifaceted microbiome is crucial for maintaining soil quality, promoting crop health, and enhancing agricultural productivity [8–10]. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) symbiosis, a widespread plant–microbial association, is especially beneficial under phosphate-deficient conditions. It is formed by obligate symbiotic fungi from the phylum Glomeromycota and occurs in 70–90% of terrestrial plant species. Studies indicate that plants allocate 10–20% of their photosynthetic carbon to AMF, which in turn supply over 50% of the plant’s nitrogen and more than 90% of its phosphorus [11, 12]. Moreover, AMF can enhance the host plants’ ability to cope with abiotic stress [13]. Our previous studies have demonstrated that AMF symbiosis improves jujube growth and physiological performance under salt stress [14]. The soil microbiome is susceptible to disturbances in chemical fertilizers. It is also highly sensitive to changes in soil physical and chemical properties, including soil moisture, total carbon content, pH, and nutrient availability [15–17]. Among the various factors influencing the soil microbiome, pH has been identified as a crucial driver that serves as a crucial driver of bacterial and fungal communities [4, 18]. Therefore, it is essential to monitor changes in the soil microbiome during jujube tree production and explore their potential connections with different cultivation practices.

Prolonged facility cultivation often necessitates frequent application of organic and chemical fertilizer, as well as pesticides, leading to a serious soil pollution and a deterioration in soil quality [19, 20]. Studies have shown that greenhouse soil is particularly prone to secondary salinization [21]. Long-term application of inorganic fertilizer in greenhouse environments has been shown to decrease the utilization rate of carbon sources, increase soil acidification, and reduce microbial species diversity [18, 22]. The abundance of microorganisms in facility-cultivated soils significantly differs from that in open-field soils, with bacterial populations generally decreasing and fungal populations relatively increasing, particularly those from the phylum Ascomycota [19, 23]. With prolonged facility cultivation, there is a notable decline in soil microbial metabolic activity and a reduction in the diversity of microbial metabolic groups [24]. Additionally, crop rotation in traditional monitoring systems is widely recognized as a sustainable practice that promotes soil ecological sustainability [25]. By diversifying crop rotation during the fallow period, this practice mitigates the negative effects of continuous cropping, thereby enhancing soil quality [26]. These findings highlight changes in microbial communities associated with vegetables in facility cultivation. However, fruit orchards may differ significantly from the vegetable ecosystems under facility. Unlike vegetable systems, orchard soil cannot be substantially improved through methods such as soil replacement or resting. Given the increasing trend of facility cultivation in the local jujube industry, this study focused on comparing the physiological and microbial differences between greenhouse and rain shelter cultivation modes; it is important to note that non-facility cultivation modes were not included in this study. Understanding the changes in soil environmental conditions, as well as the diversity and composition of microbial communities under different cultivation types, is of great significance for the effective management and production of jujube orchards.

Materials and Methods

Study Site and Sample Collection

The study was conducted in Dali County, Shaanxi Province, China (109°43′ ~ 110°19′E, 34°36′ ~ 35.02″N), located on the floodplain of the Yellow River. This region has extensive jujube orchards under facility cultivation, with 5000 hectares under greenhouses and 20,000 hectares under rain shelters. Greenhouses were constructed in the traditional Chinese style, while rain shelters were half-open facilities (Fig. S1). Jujube trees in greenhouses sprouted in early January and ripened by early June, whereas those in rain shelters sprouted in early March and ripened by mid-September. Given the differing growth seasons of jujube trees under the two cultivation types, we chose to sample during their respective harvest times to avoid experimental errors that might arise from differences in tree development stages. In this study, we randomly selected 19 greenhouses and 13 rain shelters for sampling (Fig. S1 and Table S1). Sampling was performed in greenhouses in June and in rain shelters at the end of October 2020, respectively.

Three 10 m × 10 m plots were marked uniformly at each orchard to produce a total of 96 plots. Within each plot, three sampling points were selected according to the three-point sampling method to obtain composite samples, thereby minimizing the variability of rhizosphere soil among individual plants. At each plot, fine root samples and rhizosphere soil were collected from the 0–20 cm depth layer after removing the litter layer. The fine root samples were gently washed with tap water and used for assessing mycorrhizal colonization rates and DNA extraction to analyze AMF diversity. The rhizosphere soil samples were passed through a 2-mm sieve to remove the stones and roots. Each sample was divided into three subsamples; one was air-dried for determining soil physical and chemical properties, another was stored at 4 °C to assess soil enzyme activity, and the third one was stored at − 80 ℃ for DNA extraction.

Soil Physicochemical and Biochemical Properties

Soil pH and electrical conductivity (EC) were determined using 1:2.5 (w/v) soil solutions [27]. Soil organic carbon (SOC), total carbon (TC), and total nitrogen (TN) were analyzed using an automatic carbon and nitrogen analyzer (Primacs SNC100-IC-E, Skalar, Netherlands) [28]. The ammonium nitrogen (NH4+-N) and nitrate nitrogen (NO3−-N) were extracted with 2 mol l−1 KCl solution (soil/water ratio 1:5, w/v), and available P (AP) was extracted by 0.5 M NaHCO3 (soil/water ratio 1:5, w/v, pH = 8.5) [29]. These concentrations were determined using a continuous flow analyzer (Auto Analyzer 3, SEAL, Germany). Available potassium (AK) was extracted with 1 M NH4OAc (pH = 7.0) and measured using flame atomic absorption spectrometry (900F03040404, PinAAcle, USA). All measurements were conducted in triplicate to ensure quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC).

Soil Enzyme Activity

Soil enzyme activities were determined using the assays described by [30]. For each orchard, three replicate soil samples were analyzed. Sucrase (Suc) activity was measured through 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid colorimetry using sucrose as the substrate and expressed as mg of glucose per gram of dry sample. Urease (Ure) activity was measured using indophenol colorimetry with urea as the substrate and reported as mg of ammonium per gram of dry sample [31]. Alkaline phosphatase (Alp) activity was evaluated using disodium phenyl phosphate colorimetry and expressed as mg of phenol per gram of dry sample [32].

Root Mycorrhizal Colonization, Spore Density, and Glomalin-Related Soil Protein (GRSP) Content

Roots were thoroughly rinsed with distilled water and then cut into 1 cm segments. Approximately 30 root segments from each treatment were stained with trypan blue. Subsequently, all roots were examined under a microscope at 200 × magnification. Root mycorrhizal colonization was quantified using the magnified intersection method [33]. Ten grams of dried soil was collected from each sample, and AMF spores were isolated using the wet sieve decanting method [34]. The number of AMF spores was recorded under a stereomicroscope, and spore density (SD) was calculated.

GRSP was determined using the method described by [35]. Briefly, for every 1.0 g of air-dried soil, 8 mL of 20 mmol/L sodium citrate (pH 7.0) was used to extract the total easily extractable glomalin-related soil protein (EE-GRSP). The extracts were autoclaved for 15 min at 121 °C, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 min. This procedure was repeated four times, and the supernatants were combined and centrifuged once more to prepare them for measurement. For the determination of the more resistant glomalin-related soil protein (DE-GRSP), the remaining soil sample in the tube was extracted with 8 mL of 50 mM sodium citrate (pH 8.0). This extract underwent a 60-min autoclaving process at 121 °C, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 min. The resulting supernatant was clarified, stored at 4 °C, and used for further analysis. Total GRSP is calculated as the sum of EE-GRSP and DE-GRSP. For each orchard, three replicate soil samples were analyzed.

DNA Extraction and Sequencing

Root DNA was extracted using a modified CTAB method [36]. Total genomic DNA was extracted from soil samples using an OMEGA-soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA). DNA was quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to determine quality and concentration. To assess soil bacteria diversity, the V3 − V4 hypervariable region of bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified with the primers 338 F (5′actcctacgggaggcagcag3′) and 806 R (5′ggactacnngggtatctaat3′) [37]. The ITS region of AMF was amplified using the primers AMDGR (5′cccaactatccctattaatcat3′) and AMV4.5NF (5′aagctcgtagttgaatttcg3′) [38]. DNA amplicons were quantified using a Microplate Fluorescence Reader (BioTek FLx800, USA) and sequenced using the paired-end 2 × 250 bp sequencing (PE250) strategy on the Illumina NovaSeq platform with NovaSeq 6000 SP Reagent Kit (500 cycles) at Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China).

After sequencing, the 16S rDNA and ITS raw data were processed using the QIIME2 pipeline. Chimera detection and filtering were performed using the DADA2 plugin in QIIME2, which employs a reference-based chimera detection algorithm to identify and remove chimeric sequences [39]. Reference-based and de novo chimera detection were performed using DADA2/Vsearch. For the 16S rRNA analysis, amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were clustered at a 100% similarity threshold using identified using DADA2, and taxonomy was assigned with the Silva database v.132 [40]. For the ITS, For ITS analysis, operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered at a 97% similarity threshold using Vsearch, and taxonomy was assigned using BLAST and the MaarjAM database [41, 42]. Raw sequences were deposited at the NCBI under the BioProject PRJNA542862 and PRJNA542862.

Construction of Co-occurrence Networks

Two types of co-occurrence networks were constructed, retaining only the OTUs/ASVs present in at least 50% of the samples for subsequent calculations. For all networks, we utilized TMM-normalized CPM counts, and robust correlations were established using Spearman’s correlation coefficients, with |ρ|> 0.6 and p < 0.05 [43]. All networks were visualized with Gephi [44]. To assess the potential topological roles of taxa within the networks, we classified nodes into four categories based on their within-module connectivity (Zi) and among-module connectivity (Pi) values: peripherals (Zi < 2.5, Pi < 0.62), connectors (Zi < 2.5, Pi ≥ 0.62), module hubs (Zi ≥ 2.5, Pi < 0.62), and network hubs (Zi ≥ 2.5, Pi ≥ 0.62) [45]. Network hubs, module hubs, and connectors are proposed as potential keystone taxa due to their significant roles in network topology.

Data Processing and Statistical Analysis

The measured environmental factors of all soil samples are tested for homogeneity of variances test, followed by normal distribution test. Data that did not conform to the normal distribution were analyzed by rank sum test, while those that conform to the normal distribution were tested by two-sided t-test to evaluate the significance of soil physical and chemical factors between two cultivation types. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v.20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The significant differences in α-diversity indices (Chao1 and Shannon) and microbial communities between the two cultivation types were assessed using The Wilcoxon rank-sum test and two-sided t-test. The correlation between α-diversity indices and environmental factors was analyzed using SPSS 20.0 for Pearson correlation analysis and two-sided t-tests. R software (version 4.4.0) was used to achieve the Venn diagram at the ASV/OUT level.

The differences in microbial communities under various cultivation types were analyzed using non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS), performed using the “vegan” package in R. To test for statistical differences between the microbial communities, Bray–Curtis analysis of similarity (ANOSIM) was employed [46]. Redundancy analysis (RDA) was used to evaluate the effects of soil properties on the bacterial and AMF community. Variation partitioning analysis (VPA) based on redundancy analysis with the “vegan” package was used to investigate the contribution of soil properties to the microbial community variation [47]. In LEfSe analysis, with the linear discriminant analysis (LDA) threshold set at 3.5, the Kruskal–Wallis test was conducted to analyze simultaneously the difference among varied groups and identify notable bacterial and AMF biomarkers in each treatment [48].

The Relationships Among Soil Factors and Community Structure

Non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) analysis is depicted in Fig. S6A and B. The soil microbial communities in greenhouse and rain shelter conditions were distinctly separated. Redundancy analysis (RDA) showed that soil physicochemical variables explained 42.89% of the variation in bacterial community structure and 44.39% of the variation in AMF community structure (Fig. 5A and B). Among these variables, pH was the primary factor influencing microbial community structure, with total carbon (TC) also significantly affecting bacterial community structure (Fig. 5A and B). Additionally, soil physical and chemical properties were combined into a single principal component, termed soil factors, for variation partitioning analysis. This analysis showed that soil factors explained 9.52% of the bacterial community structure, while cultivation types contributed 3.71% (Fig. S7A). In contrast, soil factors accounted for 20.19% of the variation in AMF community structure, with cultivation types explaining 6.80% (Fig. S7B). Discussion

Response of Soil Physicochemical Properties to Facility Cultivation Patterns

Facility cultivation provides a conducive environment for plant growth. However, it also significantly alters the soil’s physicochemical properties [49]. Studies show that facility cultivation can increase the total nitrogen and total phosphorus content in the soil. On the contrary, the soil in facility cultivation has a lower pH. In addition, greenhouse cultivation can lead to the loss of soil organic matter, which in turn has an adverse effect on soil productivity [19]. In vegetable greenhouse systems, intensive cropping practices combined with restricted natural rainfall infiltration reduce nutrient leaching, resulting in nutrient accumulation and pH modifications [4]. Our study found that greenhouse cultivation led to lower soil pH and higher electrical conductivity (EC) compared to rain shelter cultivation. Furthermore, the NH₄⁺-N, TN, and SOC were notably higher in greenhouses compared to rain shelter cultivation. However, no significant differences were observed in the availability of phosphorus (AP) and potassium (AK). This variation in nutrient dynamics between vegetable facilities and orchards may be attributed to the reduced use of fertilizers in orchard settings [4, 50]. Furthermore, although there was no difference in the availability of phosphorus (AP) and potassium (AK) between the two facility cultivation models, it might be because we only compared the differences in soil environmental factors between the two different types of facility cultivation. However, the comparison between facility cultivation and non-facility cultivation was not taken into consideration. In addition, the observed reduction in soil pH and increase in electrical conductivity (EC) in greenhouse systems may be linked to elevated concentrations of NH₄⁺-N, SOC, and extraradical glomalin-related soil protein (EE-GRSP) (Fig. S2). GRSP, a glycoprotein secreted by AMF, plays a critical role in maintaining soil health through its influence on soil physicochemical properties, particularly pH modulation [51]. Several studies have demonstrated that a decrease in soil pH can benefit the maintenance or enhancement of AMF diversity and GRSP content; this, in turn, positively influences soil fertility and enzyme activity [52, 53]. These findings suggest that the observed changes in soil properties in greenhouse systems may have significant implications for soil microbial communities and overall soil health.

The Effects of Facility Cultivation Types on Microbial Community Traits

The richness and diversity of soil microbial communities are crucial for maintaining agricultural soil productivity and stability [54]. Different cultivation practices can induce changes in soil physicochemical properties, which in turn significantly impact microbial community structure [18, 25]. In our study, greenhouse cultivation would reduce microbial diversity. Correlation analysis revealed that soil pH, SOC, and TN were the main factors influencing the diversity of bacteria and AMF, with pH having a more significant impact than SOC and TN (Fig. 3E). This discovery is consistent with prior studies conducted on various crops such as black pepper, cucumber, and banana [55, 56]. Additionally, the community structure of bacteria and fungi showed significant variation between the greenhouse and rain shelter cultivation modes, and pH and TN have the greatest influence on bacterial community structure, while soil pH and SOC are the primary factors affecting the formation of AMF community structure (Fig. 5C and D). Essel et al. also reported that the abundance of bacteria and fungi at the class level correlates with soil pH, total organic carbon, total nitrogen, NH4+-N, and NO3−-N contents [17]. Overall, TN, pH, and SOC were the main driving factors of soil bacteria and AMF community composition in facilities cultivated jujube orchards. However, further investigations are needed to elucidate the specific mechanisms underlying these interactions.

Effects of Cultivation Types on Soil Microbial Community and Network Structure

The interactions within soil microbial communities are complex and multifaceted, encompassing both positive and negative influences that significantly affect their structure, function, and responses to environmental changes [49, 55]. The complexity of microbial community networks is closely related to negative interactions. Negative interactions not only affect the structure and function of the community but also often manifest as resource competition or niche separation, which affects the connectivity and stability of the community [57]. In this study, the bacterial network under greenhouse conditions was more complex with higher negative interactions; this indicates that the bacterial community in the greenhouse demonstrates stronger competitive and inhibitory effects compared to that in rain shelters. These inhibitory effects are likely due to the production of antimicrobial compounds by certain bacterial taxa, which can inhibit the growth of other bacteria, as well as competition for limited resources such as nutrients and space (Table S5). Interactions between microbial taxa can help identify potential key taxa and determine the composition that sustains the symbiotic patterns of the community [17, 58]. Compared with the rain shelter, the greenhouse exhibited a lower pH, high EC, and SOC. Keystone taxa play distinct roles in the soil microbial network. The pH and SOC were the main factors affecting the microbial diversity in the two facilities (Fig. 3). Keystone taxa, ASV_60191 (family: Gaiellaceae), showed a significant negative correlation with EC. Additionally, ASV_57700 (family: Bacillaceae), ASV_228787 (family: 4b), ASV_49934 (genus: Sphingomonas); ASV_72307 (phylum: Actinobacteria), ASV_127423 (family: subgroup 7); ASV_202647 (family: Thermoanaerobaculaceae); and these ASVs were significantly positively correlated with pH and also significantly correlated with SOC (Fig. 7A and Table S6). These keystone taxa belong to the phyla Acidobacteria, Actinobacteria, Gemmatimonadetes, Proteobacteria, and Chloroflexi. Additionally, we also identified nine keystone taxa associated with SOC accumulation in the soil (Fig. 7).

Studies have shown that Actinobacteria and Gemmatimonadetes may serve as potential biomarkers for bacterial communities in arid soil environments characterized by relatively high salinity and low levels of organic matter [59]. In saline-sodic soils along a latitude gradient across Northeast China, Actinobacteria and Gemmatimonadetes were significantly enriched in high soil sodicity and salinity, while Acidobacteria and Proteobacteria were suppressed by high pH and salt stress in the saline-sodic soils [60]. Chloroflexi can thrive in diverse conditions, ranging from anaerobic to aerobic environments. Some taxa are also capable of fermentation, anaerobic respiration, and the degradation of complex organic compounds. The decline in Chloroflexi abundance may be related to high salt stress and low SOC availability [61]. Therefore, in the management of jujube orchard cultivation facilities, introducing salt-tolerant bacteria (such as Actinobacteria and Gemmatimonadetes) and increasing organic matter input (such as applying organic fertilizers or compost) can effectively address the high salinity in greenhouses and the high pH and low SOC issues in rain shelters. These measures not only enhance soil biological activity and nutrient cycling but also create a more favorable environment for plant growth.

The symbiotic interaction between plants and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi is often perceived as beneficial for both partners [11]. The AMF symbiosis enhances host nutrient acquisition and improves environmental stress resilience through microbial recruitment mechanisms [6, 62]. In both cultivation systems, all AMF community interactions showed positive associations across both cultivation systems, indicating distinct microbial response patterns to cultivation practices. Specifically, AMF communities demonstrated greater synergistic reciprocity with jujube trees compared to bacterial communities [63, 64]. Notable taxonomic shifts were observed between cultivation environments; Glomus emerged as the dominant genus in the greenhouse. Conversely, the abundance of Glomus decreased while that of Paraglomus increased in the rain shelter (Fig. 4B and D). This variation may be attributed to the differing adaptability of AMF from various genera to specific soil environments and host affinities [65]. The genera Glomus and Paraglomus could be widely detected in agricultural fields, indicating that they were better adapted to disturbed environments [52, 66]. Previous research has confirmed that Rhizophagus irregularis, a member of the Glomus genus, enhances the resistance of jujube seedlings to salt stress [14]. This evidence suggests that AMF diversity is specific, with Glomus exhibiting stronger adaptability to EC in jujube orchards, while Paraglomus may be better suited to high pH soil conditions. Future research endeavors focus on elucidating the co-adaptation mechanisms between these AMF species and jujube trees under compound abiotic stress. Such studies would provide critical theoretical support for the development of specialized microbial inoculants tailored for jujube cultivation.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive characterization of the community composition of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) and soil bacteria under different jujube cultivation types. Greenhouse soils exhibited higher SOC and TN levels, along with lower pH and higher salinity, compared to rain shelter soils, which showed higher alkalinity and lower salinity. The variations in bacterial community composition were primarily driven by differences in the abundance of the phyla Actinobacteria, Gemmatimonadetes, and Rokubacteria. In contrast, differences in AMF communities were attributed to variations in the genera Glomus and Paraglomus between the two systems. Additionally, key bacterial taxa in greenhouse soils were significantly correlated with soil pH and SOC, while most key bacterial taxa in rain shelter soils were significantly correlated with pH. The findings highlight the significant impact of cultivation type on soil microbial community structure and function, which has important implications for optimizing cultivation practices and ensuring sustainable jujube production. Several areas warrant further investigation. First, the long-term effects of facility cultivation on soil microbial communities and their resilience to environmental changes should be examined. Second, the functional roles of key bacterial taxa identified in this study should be investigated to understand their potential contributions to soil health and plant growth.
