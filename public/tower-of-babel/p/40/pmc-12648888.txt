Quantitative Control of Zn2+ Photorelease: A Step toward Decoding Mechanisms of Subsecond Metal Signaling in the Brain
Authors: Sayuri Niyangoda, Jodie Kearney, Piyanka Hettiarachchi, Jacob Theismann, Austin Shigemoto, Erin E. Hickey, Shawn C. Burdette, Michael A. Johnson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Free ionic zinc (Zn2+) exerts control over many aspects of neuronal function after its exocytotic release from vesicles by associating with conserved binding sites on regulatory proteins, including the dopamine transporter. While the use of photoreactive cages to investigate the influence of subsecond Zn2+ transients on neurotransmitter release and uptake is useful, a quantitative understanding of Zn2+’s regulatory effects requires knowing how much Zn2+ has been released. Here, we have addressed this issue by directly measuring 4-methoxy-3-nitrobenzaldehyde (3-NPA), a photoreaction byproduct from [Zn­(DPAeCageOMe)]+, as a reporter to quantify Zn2+ photorelease. We optimized a sawhorse waveform, which eliminated fouling and provided a linear oxidation current response as concentration was increased. We then demonstrated the utility of our method by measuring released zinc ions evoked by light exposure in whole brains harvested from adult zebrafish ex vivo. Our findings revealed that zinc, which was released at concentrations of about 1 to 4 μM in response to light application durations of 300 to 900 ms, significantly increased dopamine overflow and inhibited dopamine uptake. The profound effects of low concentrations of Zn2+ on release and uptake raise the possibility that Zn2+, which is released from terminals at concentrations of 10 to 20 μM, acts as a volume neuromodulator, with targets beyond respective postsynaptic terminals. Given that Zn2+ dysregulation is a well-established phenomenon in Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s diseases, application of our newly developed method may provide insight into these disorders.

Introduction

Zinc regulates many processes in the brain, including neurotransmission, synaptic plasticity, and neuronal signaling. The dysregulation of zinc homeostasis is implicated in numerous neurological disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and epilepsy, highlighting its critical importance in maintaining brain health. Present in both intracellular and extracellular environments, zinc exists in bound and unbound states. , Synaptic vesicles within select populations of glutamatergic neurons in the CNS are replete with free zinc ions (Zn2+) that, upon release, control synaptic plasticity, neuronal excitability, and receptor activity. , Zn2+ influences communication between neurons by interacting with AMPA receptors, NMDA receptors, and other receptor types. , Moreover, Zn2+ has been shown to inhibit the uptake of multiple neurotransmitters by inhibiting their transporters. Examples include the dopamine transporter (DAT), the glycine transporter (GLYT1b), GABA transporters (GAT2 and GAT4), and excitatory amino acid transporters (EAAT1 and EAAT4).

Glutamate is the major excitatory neurotransmitter in the vertebrate brain. Thus, it is not surprising that vesicular Zn2+ has been found in various regions richly innervated with terminals that release other neurotransmitters, including dopamine and serotonin. Throughout the striatum and limbic system, Zn2+-releasing glutamate terminals colocalize with the DAT, supporting the concept that vesicular Zn2+ release exerts control over dopamine signaling. This coordinated Zn2+ release regulates DAT function and dopamine receptor activity by association with allosteric binding sites. Given that dopamine plays important functions in motor control and cognition, as well as being implicated in multiple neurodegenerative disorders, understanding the interplay between Zn2+ release and dopamine release and uptake is essential for uncovering mechanisms that underlie neurological disorders.

Fast-scan cyclic voltammetry (FSCV) at carbon fiber microelectrodes is an electrochemical technique of choice commonly used for the subsecond quantitation of dopamine release and uptake in living brain tissue. , Advantages of this method include subsecond temporal resolution, micron spatial resolution, and good selectivity. ,− However, introducing free Zn2+ to brain tissue by simple bath application presents possible confounds when determining the effects of this cation on dopamine release and uptake. First, since it may take minutes to hours for Zn2+ to equilibrate throughout the tissue, bath application is incompatible with the temporal resolution required to measure the immediate, subsecond time scale effects of Zn2+ on dopamine release and uptake. Additionally, prolonged exposure to Zn2+ raises the possibility that longer term intracellular changes, such as protein upregulation, may occur, thereby complicating interpretation of dopamine release and uptake measurements. Therefore, our group recently introduced the concept of combining FSCV with the release of Zn2+ from light-sensitive photocages to assess the effect of free zinc ions on dopamine release and reuptake in zebrafish.

Photoactivatable caged compounds undergo chemical reaction and subsequent release of their target chemical species upon exposure to light of sufficient energy and intensity. , This photochemical approach has gained significant attention for biological applications due to its ability to selectively release the target molecule with precise spatial and temporal control, without the need for harsh external reagents. , Our previous findings, obtained using the caged Zn2+ compound [Zn­(DPAeCageOMe)]+, revealed that photoreleased Zn2+ significantly decreased dopamine uptake within hundreds of milliseconds, with this effect being more pronounced at longer durations of light exposure or shorter times between light exposure and dopamine measurement. These results demonstrated that the integration of caged compound photolysis with FSCV is a powerful approach that can be used to explore the relation between metal ion concentration and neurotransmitter regulation with high spatiotemporal precision.

A significant limitation of this approach, like other photoactivation strategies, is the difficulty in precisely quantifying the amount of Zn2+ released during the photo uncaging process. The degree of caged-compound photorelease has traditionally been determined indirectly using actinometry methods that measure the flux of photons striking the sample combined with knowledge of the quantum yield. However, this method is not conducted in situ and, therefore, does not account for interference of light by opaque tissues, such as that found in the brain. A more direct method, such as measuring Zn2+ at the site of release in the brain, is desirable. However, quantifying Zn2+ using analytical methods presents several challenges due to its inherent chemical properties.

Unlike some metals, Zn2+ lacks strong optical signals, making direct detection through UV–vis absorbance or fluorescence difficult. , Additionally, high background interference from biological and environmental matrices complicates accurate measurement. While techniques like atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) and inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) can determine total Zn2+ concentration, they are not suited for obtaining in situ measurements or for quantifying concentration changes on rapid time scales. The low redox activity of Zn2+ further limits its direct electrochemical detection, unlike redox-active metals, such as iron or copper. Previous studies that have successfully quantified Zn2+ with FSCV at carbon-fiber microelectrodes and plasma-treated gold microelectrodes have been published; however, these methods are not ideal for our application since very negative potentials must be applied to the electrode to force Zn2+ to accept electrons. − At these negative potentials, other molecules in the brain may be reduced, making it difficult to separate signals between Zn2+ and its interferents.

Given these challenges in measuring Zn2+, we have taken an approach in which we directly quantify 4-methoxy-3-nitrobenzaldehyde (3-NPA), an electroactive byproduct released from [Zn­(DPAeCageOMe)]+ during the photouncaging process. Because 3-NPA undergoes photorelease with Zn2+ at a 1:1 stoichiometry, its electrochemical signal serves as a proxy to measure how much zinc is released while not requiring electrode modification or change in electrode position. We have developed and optimized a waveform that is sensitive for the quantification of 3-NPA. We then used this method to quantify Zn2+ photorelease in live, intact zebrafish brains and establish a clear relation between dopamine reuptake inhibition and the amount of zinc photoreleased at given light exposure times.

Experimental Section

Chemicals

All chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO) and used as received. A 3 mM stock solution was prepared with the electroactive byproduct 4-methoxy-3-nitrobenzaldehyde (3NPA; lot number MKBR4377V) using ethanol. This solution was diluted to prepare 1 μM solution by dissolving in artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF; 126 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.2 mM NaH2PO4, 2.4 mM CaCl2, 1.2 mM MgCl2, 25 mM NaHCO3, and 20 mM HEPES, adjusted to pH 7.4 with 10 M NaOH). All solutions were made using ultrapure water (∼18 MΩ·cm). Zn2+ caged compound, DPAeCageOMe (2-[bis­(pyridin-2-ylmethyl)­amino]-2-(4-methoxy-3-nitrophenyl) acetic acid) was synthesized as described previously. For [Zn­(DPAeCageOMe)]+ preparation, the appropriate mass of DPAeCageOMe was measured and dissolved in zinc-free artificial cerebrospinal fluid ZnCl2 (lot no: MKCN6765, 98%, Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO) and DPAeCageOMe solutions were mixed in a 1:1 molar ratio to prepare [Zn­(DPAeCageOMe)]+ of 100 μM solution. The other byproduct di­(2-picolyl)­amine (DPA; lot number MKCP3723), was also obtained from Sigma (St. Louis, MO).

Animals

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with an animal use protocol approved by the University of Kansas Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Wild-type adult zebrafish (Danio rerio, AB strain) were bred and housed at the University of Kansas. The fish were maintained in three-liter tanks, with 15–20 individuals per tank, as part of a recirculating filtration system. Water quality was upheld through continuous chemical, biological, and mechanical filtration, complemented by a UV sterilization unit. The system water, purified via reverse osmosis, was maintained at 28 °C, with conductivity (∼800 μS cm–1) and pH (7.2) regulated using a YSI 5200A multiparameter monitoring and control instrument (Yellow Springs, OH). The zebrafish were fed twice daily and kept on a 16 h light/8 h dark cycle. Fish used for experimentation were 13 to 16 months old. An equal number of male and female fish were used in conducting each experiment.

Microelectrode Fabrication

Carbon-fiber working electrodes were fabricated following a previously established protocol. In brief, a 7 μm diameter carbon fiber (Goodfellow Cambridge Ltd., Huntingdon, U.K.) was drawn into a glass capillary tube (outer diameter: 1.2 mm, inner diameter: 0.68 mm; A-M Systems, Inc., Carlsborg, WA) and secured by sealing the glass around the fiber using a heated coil puller (PE-22, Narishige Int., East Meadow, NY). The exposed carbon fiber was then trimmed to approximately 100 μm beyond the tip of the pulled capillary, and an epoxy seal was applied using EPON resin 815C with EPIKURE 3234 curing agent (Miller-Stephenson, Danbury, CT). The electrode was cured at 100 °C for 1 h and subsequently cleaned by immersion in ultrapure isopropanol for 10 min. To establish electrical connectivity with the electrode holder, all electrodes were backfilled with a 0.5 M potassium acetate ionic solution, and a silver wire was inserted. Flow injection analysis was used to calibrate the electrodes against standard dopamine and byproduct solutions.

Electrochemical Data Acquisition In Vitro

All in vitro experiments were conducted using a custom-built flow injection cell enclosed within a specially designed Faraday cage. A Chem Clamp potentiostat (Dagan, Minneapolis, MN) with modified gain settings was employed for enhanced signal acquisition. Data collection and analysis were performed using TarHeel CV software (developed by M.L.A.V. Heien and R.M. Wightman, University of North Carolina, Chapel Hill, NC) in conjunction with a PCI 6711 multifunction I/O board (National Instruments, Austin, TX). All voltammetric parameters were optimized sequentially and each peak current was normalized to the current obtained for 1 μM dopamine solution to avoid discrepancies among electrodes.

Fast Scan Cyclic Voltammetry in Zebrafish Brains

Fast-scan cyclic voltammetry (FSCV) was employed to assess dopamine release and uptake in ex vivo zebrafish brains. The procedure for zebrafish brain extraction has been previously described. , Euthanasia was performed using the rapid chilling method, followed by decapitation. The brain was immediately harvested and transferred to the perfusion chamber. The brain’s viability was maintained by a continuous flow of oxygenated and pH-adjusted artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF). Before any measurements were taken, the system was stabilized at 28 °C, and the brain was given 40 min to equilibrate within the chamber.

The working electrode is then inserted at the desired location in the telencephalon. For dopamine measurements, a waveform of −0.4 V to +1.3 V to −0.4 V was applied at a scan rate of 400 V/s and update rate of 10 scans/s. For 3-NPA measurements, a waveform of −0.6 V to +1.4 V (held for 3 ms) to −0.6 V was applied at 600 V/s and 10 scans/s. The electrochemical equipment used consisted of a Dagan Chem-Clamp potentiostat (Dagan, Minneapolis, MN) that was modified to allow decreased gain settings, a personal computer with Tar Heel CV software (provided by R.M. Wightman, University of North Carolina, Chapel Hill, NC), a breakout box, and two National Instruments computer interface cards (PCI 6052 and PCI 6711, National Instruments, Austin, TX). To induce dopamine (DA) release in the whole brain, a series of biphasic stimulus pulses (15 pulses, 60 Hz, 2 ms duration, 350 μA) were applied locally to the dorsal nucleus of the ventral telencephalon (Vd) using two tungsten electrodes spaced 200 μm apart. A recovery period of 10 min was allowed between successive electrical stimulations.

Uncaging of the Photoactive Zinc Caged Compound

A schematic diagram describing the experimental setup for uncaging Zn2+ in the brain is shown in Figure A. The brain was continuously perfused with [Zn­(DPAeCageOMe)]+ dissolved in oxygenated aCSF, which was recirculated using a peristaltic pump. A 1 mm diameter optical fiber was positioned above the telencephalon, near the working electrode. A single light pulse from a mercury lamp was delivered through the optic fiber and gated by a shutter and controller (Sutter Instruments, Novato, CA). The light stimulation was controlled with a +5 V Transistor–Transistor Logic (TTL) signal sent from the Tar Heel software, ensuring synchronization between the light pulse and electrical stimulation. The mercury lamp’s light was transmitted to the sample using a fiber-optic cable (PolyMicro Technologies, Inc., Phoenix, AZ). The light exposure time was adjusted depending on the requirement. Following a predetermined delay time of 400 ms, set within the Tar Heel software, the electrical stimulus pulse(s) were applied, and dopamine release was quantified using FSCV.

Electrochemical Detection of the Byproduct in Zebrafish Whole Brain

Once the working electrode was inserted inside the brain and dopamine release and reuptake was recorded, the wavelength was changed to the optimized waveform which is sensitive to the byproduct. We then followed a timeline for optical and electrical stimulation as shown in Figure B. Here, the duration of light exposure (t light) was changed from 300 to 900 ms and the time from end of light exposure to stimulation (t delay) was held at 400 ms. As summarized in Figure A, a series of collection files with both dopamine and byproduct waveforms were obtained at different light exposure times to obtain a quantified relationship between the amount of Zn2+ released during each event of reuptake inhibition.

Data Analysis and Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 7 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA). Data from the experiments are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), unless stated otherwise. For statistical analyses, n = number of fish brains used. To quantify peak dopamine overflow ([DA]MAX), FSCV files were imported into Tar Heel software, where the maximum dopamine release current (i MAX) was determined. The [DA]MAX value was calculated by comparing the maximum dopamine release current with calibration curves obtained from standard dopamine solutions in a flow cell.

Dopamine uptake curves were modeled with GraphPad to determine first order rate constants (k). To analyze dopamine transporter (DAT) reuptake, the portion of the release curve where the current declined from approximately 90% to 40% of i MAX was selected to calculate kinetic parameters. If the current did not return to 40% of i MAX, a 4 s segment from the 90% point was modeled instead. Nonlinear regression analysis in GraphPad, using a plateau followed by a one-phase decay model, was applied with constraints set for the x and y values at 90%. Data files were excluded from modeling if the peak current was below 0.8 nA or if fewer than three data points were present between the 90% and 40% thresholds. After completing the modeling process, k was extracted.

Results and Discussion

Developing an Electrochemical Method to Quantitate 3-NPA with Sub-s Temporal Resolution

General Approach

The photocage employed in this work is DPAdeCageOMe (2-[bis­(pyridin-2-ylmethyl)­amino]-2-(4-methoxy-3-nitrophenyl) acetic acid). This molecule chelates Zn2+ and, upon irradiation with light of sufficient energy and intensity, releases Zn2+ and two innocuous byproducts, 4-methoxy-3-nitrobenzaldehyde (3-NPA) and 2,2′-dipicolylamine (DPA) with a quantum yield (moles uncaged/mol of photons) of 0.313 (Figure A). Previous work has demonstrated that the one-electron oxidation of benzaldehyde at glassy carbon electrodes occurs at positive potentials. The initial oxidation results in the formation of a carbonyl radical, which reacts with diatomic oxygen to form a peroxyradical. Through multiple pathways, the peroxyradical can undergo spontaneous reactions to form benzoic acid. Thus, a possible reaction pathway showing how 3-NPA might be oxidized in a similar fashion is shown in Figure B.

Photorelease of Zn2+ and oxidation of 3NPA. (A) The photouncaging reaction of [Zn­(DPAdeCageOMe)]+. The figure illustrates the breakdown of the caged compound into Zn2+ and byproducts. (B) Proposed pathway of 3-NPA oxidation.

FSCV traditionally employs a triangular waveform to detect electrochemically active analytes. When used to measure changes in dopamine levels, a waveform that scans from a holding potential of −0.4 V to a switching potential of +1.3 V and back to −0.4 V at a scan rate of 400 V/s and a frequency of 10 Hz is commonly used (the standard triangular waveform or traditional dopamine waveform). As shown in the representative color plot, current–time plot, and CV in Figure A, we did not observe a detectable faradaic signal upon applying this waveform to flow-injected 3-NPA. This lack of usable signal may occur because the oxidation product fouls the surface of the carbon-fiber electrode. Therefore, we sought to optimize the waveform to mitigate fouling and maintain electrode sensitivity.

Optimized waveform prevents fouling with 3-NP and preserves the oxidation signal. (A) Representative cyclic voltammogram, current–time plot, and color plot for the detection of 3NPA obtained using the triangular waveform. (B) Representative cyclic voltammogram, current–time plot, and color plot for the detection of 3NPA obtained with the optimized waveform. (C) Representative cyclic voltammogram, current–time plot, cyclic voltammogram and color plot for the detection of DPA obtained with the optimized waveform. Carbon-fiber microelectrodes have been shown to undergo surface regeneration at approximately +1.2 V (versus Ag/AgCl reference). Holding the electrode potential at these positive values has been used as a strategy to decrease electrode fouling; − therefore, we employed this approach to measure 3-NPA with FSCV. As shown in Figure B, we used an optimized waveform that had a holding potential of −0.6 V, scanned to +1.4 V at a rate of 600 V/s, held the potential at +1.4 V for 3 ms, and then scanned back to −0.6 V at a rate of 600 V/s. The use of this waveform resulted in a cleaner voltammogram and eliminated fouling. A comparison of the traditional dopamine waveform and the optimized waveform are shown in Figure A,B. The specific parameters of the optimized waveform were determined systematically to enhance the faradaic oxidation current and the resistance of the electrode to fouling. Details of this optimization process, along with representative raw data and pooled data (Figures S2 through S7), are provided in Supporting Information.

Comparison of waveforms used. (A) The standard triangular waveform and (B) the optimized waveform.

The other byproduct of photorelease, DPA, did not produce a measurable signal when using the optimized waveform (Figure C), indicating that it likely does not interfere with 3-NPA detection. To confirm that the oxidation of 3-NPA specifically involves the aldehyde moiety, we conducted flow cell analysis using the ketone analog of the same molecule. As shown in Figure S1, no detectable electrochemical signal was observed, supporting the conclusion that the aldehyde group is essential for the redox activity observed under the applied waveform. Because 3-NPA is released in a 1:1 ratio with Zn2+, these results afford us the ability to track and quantify Zn2+ photorelease in real-time.

Electrode Stability

The representative current–time plot, cyclic voltammogram, and color plot are shown in Figure and were obtained using flow cell analysis after injection of 1 μM 3-NPA solution. Even though we have a measurable signal, for reliable byproduct detection it is essential that the electrode maintains consistent performance across multiple measurements over multiple injections. To assess stability and resistance to fouling when using the optimized waveform, repeated injections of 1 μM 3-NPA were conducted using flow injection analysis, totaling 10 injections (Figure ). To track changes, we normalized the faradaic current to the absolute current obtained from the first injection. The current–time profile for the nine subsequent injections, along with cyclic voltammograms (CVs) from the first and last injections of 3-NPA, demonstrated no significant decrease in peak current or alterations in CV shape. Additionally, we observed no systematic decline in signal over time, indicating that our optimized waveform prevented electrode fouling during repeated 3-NPA exposure.

Limit of Quantitation

We generated a calibration curve for 3-NPA by using the optimized waveform and plotting current recorded against analyte concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 5 μM (Figure S8). The limit of quantitation, determined at S/N = 10, was found to be 0.32 ± 0.02 μM. Additionally, regression analysis demonstrated excellent linearity (R 2 = 0.9975), confirming that this optimized waveform is well-suited for the quantification of 3-NPA release, even at low concentrations.

Ex Vivo Zebrafish Brain

Our previous work demonstrated that subsecond Zn2+ photorelease inhibited dopamine (DA) reuptake, as assessed by the first-order rate constant (k) and half-life (t 1/2), while not significantly impacting electrically stimulated DA overflow ([DA]STIM). To validate the waveform in live brain tissue, we carried out photorelease measurements in zebrafish whole brain. A carbon-fiber microelectrode was positioned in the telencephalon to measure stimulated dopamine release. Once a dopamine signal was detected, an optic fiber was positioned so that light was directed toward the brain region where the working electrode was positioned and the brain was perfused with aCSF containing [Zn­(DPAeCageOMe)]+ (Figure A). Next, we applied to the working electrode the waveform that had been optimized for 3-NPA measurement. Almost immediately after exposure to light (t light = 500 ms, t delay = 400 ms), the current increased and we observed the formation of the CV for 3-NPA (Figure ). These data demonstrate the successful, real-time monitoring of 3-NPA photorelease in live zebrafish brain. We also carried out flow cell analysis for the other byproduct, DPA, under the same optimized waveform conditions. As indicated in Figure C, the resulting current vs time traces, cyclic voltammograms, and color plots did not show any evidence of redox activity, indicating that DPA is electrochemically inactive under the applied waveform parameters. This experiment demonstrates that our waveform switching approach allowed the quantification of byproduct 3-NPA release alongside dopamine measurements at the same recording site.

Next, we collected measurements at different light exposure durations, measuring stimulated dopamine release following Zn2+ photorelease and then 3-NPA photorelease without electrical stimulation (from 60 to 190 min). By performing this process across different light exposure durations (300–900 ms), we established a correlation between dopamine concentration and zinc release. Additionally, this approach allowed us to analyze the impact of Zn2+ on dopamine uptake inhibition, providing insight into how extracellular zinc influences dopamine reuptake regulation.

Figure B illustrates the relation between photoreleased zinc concentration, or [Zn2+] (left y-axis), dopamine overflow, or [DA]STIM (right y-axis), and light exposure duration (x-axis). It is important to note that the two y-axes are scaled differently. There was a significant overall effect of light exposure on [Zn2+]photo (p = 0.0134, F(1.636, 4.909) = 12.39) and linear trend (p < 0.0001, slope = 0.4466) as revealed by repeated measures one-way ANOVA. Analysis by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons showed that, when compared with the value at t light = 300 ms, [Zn2+]photo was significantly greater at 600 ms (p = 0.0414), 700 ms (p = 0.0215), 800 ms (p = 0.0175), and 900 ms (p = 0.0096). Thus, within this range of light durations, the amount of Zn2+ photoreleased increased linearly and substantially, indicating that we can easily control and quantify how much Zn2+ has been released.

Stimulated dopamine overflow increased progressively with longer light exposure durations, which correspond to higher concentrations of photoreleased Zn2+. Repeated measures one-way ANOVA revealed a significant linear trend (p < 0.0001, slope = 0.02777) and a significant overall effect of light exposure on dopamine levels (p = 0.0134, F(1.636, 4.909) = 12.39, n = 4). Dunnett post hoc analysis showed that, compared to measurement collected at t light = 300 ms, dopamine overflow was significantly greater at 600 ms (p = 0.0334), 700 ms (p = 0.0186), 800 ms (p = 0.0367), and 900 ms (p = 0.0249). Notably, significant differences were observed primarily between conditions with larger differences in light exposure time, while smaller increments did not yield statistically significant changes (e.g., between 300 and 500 ms, p = 0.2601).

To investigate the correlation between [Zn2+]photo and [DA]STIM, we normalized both parameters so that, for each recording session, the minimum value was set to zero and the maximum value was set to unity. Plots of the normalized data are shown in Figure S10A. Repeated measures two-way ANOVA revealed no significant interaction (p = 0.7396) between [Zn2+]photo and [DA]STIM and the two curves were not significantly different (p = 0.4796). However, we found a significant main effect of t light on both parameters (p < 0.0001). Further analysis revealed a significant correlation between the [Zn2+]photo and [DA]STIM curves (p = 0.0001, Pearson’s coefficient (r) = 0.9773). Thus, it is apparent that [DA]STIM tracks closely with [Zn2+]photo. Cumulatively, our findings suggest that substantial increases in Zn2+ photorelease are required to produce measurable effects on dopamine overflow. It is also important to note that our assay does not directly measure how Zn2+ application influences dopamine that has been released from terminals; thus, it is possible that values of [DA]STIM are heavily influenced by changes in dopamine uptake.

Repeated measures one-way ANOVA revealed a significant linear trend (p < 0.0001, slope = −0.06079) and a significant overall effect of light exposure on the reuptake rate constant (p = 0.0014, F(1.383, 4.15) = 52.39, n = 4). Dunnett post hoc analysis revealed the primary sources of variation that occurred between measurements obtained at t light = 300 ms versus those obtained at 600 ms (p = 0.0236), 700 ms (p = 0.0213), 800 ms (p = 0.0321) and 900 ms (p = 0.0014). Repeated measures two-way ANOVA of the normalized values (Figure S10B) revealed a significant interaction between the parameters [Zn2+]photo and [DA]STIM and time of light exposure. Finally, correlation analysis showed a significant inverse correlation between the two curves (p < 0.001, Pearson (r) = −0.9907).

Taken together, these results demonstrate a dose-dependent inhibition of dopamine reuptake by photoreleased Zn2+, with higher zinc concentrations leading to progressively slower clearance of dopamine. This dependence likely reflects Zn2+-mediated regulation of dopamine transporter (DAT) function, either through direct interaction with DAT or by modulating intracellular signaling pathways that regulate its activity. The more consistent and widespread significance of these comparisons suggest that dopamine reuptake is particularly sensitive to Zn2+ levels and may serve as a primary mechanism by which Zn2+ modulates dopaminergic tone.

Based on the concentration of caged Zn2+ used and the light intensity/duration applied, we estimate that photolysis generated free Zn2+ concentrations in the low micromolar range. This transient Zn2+ elevation was sufficient to alter dopamine neurotransmission, as evidenced by altered clearance kinetics. These findings suggest that Zn2+ may modulate dopamine release through presynaptic mechanisms, possibly by interacting with voltage-gated calcium channels or D2 autoreceptors, and influence uptake by modulating dopamine transporter activity. Importantly, the modulatory effect of photoreleased Zn2+ is likely not confined to a single synapse. As a diffusible ion, Zn2+ may act as a volume neuromodulator, influencing dopamine release and uptake at adjacent synapses or terminals. , This concept supports the idea that Zn2+ participates in broader neuromodulatory signaling, especially under conditions of high synaptic activity or stress when vesicular Zn2+ is coreleased and allowed to diffuse.

Conclusions

In this study, we successfully developed and optimized a waveform for the electrochemical detection of 3-NPA, a byproduct of zinc uncaging, providing a reliable approach to quantify zinc release. By carefully tuning the waveform parameters, we achieved selective and reproducible detection while minimizing electrode passivation. This optimized sawhorse waveform employed a switching potential of 1.4 V, accumulation potential of −0.6 V, a scan rate of 600 V/s and a 3 ms holding time. A plot of measurements obtained using this waveform against 3-NPA concentration was linear, and the electrodes did not foul upon multiple injections. This method was then validated through ex vivo testing in brain tissue, where we demonstrated its effectiveness in capturing dynamic zinc fluctuations in response to photostimulation. The ability to quantify zinc release with high temporal resolution alongside dopamine measurements enables a more comprehensive understanding of zinc’s role in neurotransmission. These findings establish a foundation for future studies investigating zinc’s involvement in synaptic regulation and neurochemical signaling under physiological and pathological conditions.
