Molecular and Biochemical Characterization of Insecticide Resistance in Field-Caught Aedes aegypti from Al-Madinah Al-Munawarah, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Jazem A. Mahyoub, Zainab Mohammad, Dina F. Alhashdi, Mahmoud A. Emara, Yaaser Q. Almulaiky, Shatha I. Alqurashi, Tariq S. Alghamdi, Ahmed S. Hashem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The global resurgence of mosquito-borne diseases such as dengue, chikungunya, and Zika represents a growing threat to public health, largely due to the rapid evolution of insecticide resistance in Aedes aegypti populations. This study investigates the molecular and biochemical mechanisms driving pyrethroid and DDT resistance in field-collected Aedes aegypti females from Al-Madinah Al-Munawarah, Saudi Arabia. Fifty adult female mosquitoes were analyzed to detect mutations in the voltage-gated sodium channel (Vgsc) gene and to evaluate detoxifying enzyme activities. Molecular genotyping revealed the coexistence of three major knockdown resistance (kdr) mutationsS989P, V1016G, and F1534Coccurring in both homozygous and heterozygous combinations. Approximately 36% of the samples exhibited triple mutant genotypes, indicating intense selection pressure and widespread fixation of resistance alleles within the local population. Complementary biochemical assays demonstrated elevated levels of catalase, glutathione S-transferase (GST), and esterases, alongside decreased acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity, compared with the susceptible laboratory strain. These findings confirm the synergistic contribution of target-site insensitivity and metabolic detoxification to the development of resistance phenotypes. Collectively, this work provides the first integrated molecular–biochemical evidence of multiple resistance mechanisms in Ae. aegypti from Al-Madinah. The results highlight an urgent need for adaptive vector management strategies that incorporate molecular surveillance, insecticide rotation, and nonchemical interventions to mitigate resistance evolution. This study contributes valuable insights into the regional dynamics of resistance in the Arabian Peninsula and emphasizes the necessity for sustainable, evidence-based mosquito control programs.

Introduction

Mosquito-borne viral diseases remain among the most persistent and rapidly expanding global public health challenges. Approximately half of the world’s population currently resides in regions where dengue virus transmission occurs, reflecting the widespread establishment of Aedes aegypti populations across tropical and subtropical zones. Dengue, chikungunya, and Zika viruses have collectively caused recurrent and severe outbreaks in numerous countries, with increasing frequency, geographic spread, and clinical burden. These trends are driven by rapid urbanization, globalization of trade and travel, and climate change, which collectively expand the ecological range of Ae. aegyptithe most efficient vector of these arboviruses. The species’ anthropophilic behavior, preference for urban habitats, and adaptation to domestic water containers facilitate its persistence in human settlements and complicate sustained control.

In the Arabian Peninsula, and particularly in Saudi Arabia, vector-borne viral diseases have become an emerging public health concern. Al-Madinah Al-Munawarah holds particular public health relevance as a major destination for Hajj and Umrah pilgrims, characterized by recurrent seasonal population influxes. This unique demographic dynamic increases the operational importance of sustained Ae. aegypti surveillance and effective vector control in the city. At least four major arboviruses have been documented in the Kingdomdengue virus (DENV), Crimean–Congo hemorrhagic fever virus, Rift Valley fever virus, and Alkhurma hemorrhagic fever virus. Among these, dengue is the most epidemiologically significant, representing the predominant arboviral disease transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes across urban centers. Neighboring countries such as Pakistan, , Afghanistan, Yemen, and Oman have also reported recurrent dengue outbreaks, underscoring the regional nature of the problem and the potential for cross-border transmission. ,

Dengue was first recognized in Saudi Arabia in October 1993, followed by the first confirmed outbreak in Jeddah in 1994 involving DENV-2, which dominated early circulation13. , Subsequent reports confirm persistent dengue activity in western Saudi Arabia, particularly Jeddah, Makkah, and Al-Madinah, where ecological and climatic conditions support vector proliferation. , Mass gatherings for Hajj and Umrah attract millions of visitors from dengue-endemic regions of Asia and Africa and may facilitate introduction of new viral serotypes and genotypes. , Recurrent outbreaks and importation risk can increase operational reliance on insecticides, intensifying selection pressure for resistance in local Ae. aegypti populations. Climate change and water-storage practices further promote Ae. aegypti proliferation; rising temperatures, irregular rainfall, and container-based urban water storage create productive breeding habitats, reinforcing dengue endemicity in the Peninsula..

Chemical insecticides remain a cornerstone of vector control, but intensive and sometimes unregulated use has accelerated insecticide resistance and threatens long-term program effectiveness. Resistance in Ae. aegypti has been reported from more than 50 countries20. Adult control has relied heavily on pyrethroids and, historically, organochlorines such as DDT; Ae. aegypti populations have evolved resistance through both target-site insensitivity and metabolic detoxification mechanisms. , At the molecular level, the most frequently reported pyrethroid-resistance mechanism involves knockdown resistance (kdr) substitutions in the voltage-gated sodium channel (Vgsc) gene, which reduce insecticide binding at neuronal membranes. Key substitutions include F1534C, V410L, S989P, V1016G, and V1016I, − with frequencies and geographic distributions reflecting local selection pressures. In Saudi Arabia, S989P, V1016G, and F1534C have been detected and are associated with reduced susceptibility to permethrin and deltamethrin. , The F1534C substitution occurs at variable frequencies in Pakistan, India, and Sri Lanka and is commonly linked to DDT and type I pyrethroid resistance, whereas stronger resistance to type II pyrethroids (e.g., deltamethrin) is often observed when F1534C co-occurs with V1016I or V410L. , In Thailand, V1016G confers high-level deltamethrin resistance when homozygous, while F1534C contributes mainly to permethrin resistance. , In Malaysia and Indonesia, the combined S989P/V1016G/F1534C genotype enhances resistance to both DDT and pyrethroids. , Notably, 989P and 1016G are most frequently reported in Asian populations, − whereas 1534C is globally widespread. ,,,

The combination of these mutations forms distinct haplotypes that produce varying degrees of resistance intensity. The V1016G mutation alone can confer measurable pyrethroid resistance, , whereas F1534C independently enhances resistance to first-generation pyrethroids like permethrin. When combined with V1016G or S989P, however, resistance extends to second-generation pyrethroids such as deltamethrin and alphacypermethrin. , This sequential accumulation of mutations suggests that F1534C likely evolved initially under DDT or type I pyrethroid exposure, followed by secondary selection for 1016 and 989 mutations that collectively broaden resistance spectra. Asian haplotypes containing the 989P+1016G combination exhibit particularly strong pyrethroid resistance, especially in homozygous individuals. The addition of the 1534C allele further decreases Vgsc channel sensitivity, producing a robust, multimutation resistance phenotype. ,

Metabolic detoxification provides a complementary route to resistance. Esterases, glutathione S-transferases (GSTs), and antioxidant enzymes such as catalase contribute to detoxification and management of oxidative stress generated during xenobiotic metabolism. , GSTs promote conjugation of xenobiotics with reduced glutathione, esterases hydrolyze ester bonds in pyrethroid molecules, and catalase neutralizes reactive oxygen species (ROS). − Excess ROS can damage lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids; , therefore, antioxidant enzymes help preserve cellular homeostasis. , Acetylcholinesterase (AChE), a serine esterase that terminates synaptic transmission by hydrolyzing acetylcholine, is the principal target of organophosphates and carbamates; these insecticides inhibit AChE via phosphorylation or carbamylation, causing neurotoxicity and death. Altered AChE sensitivity or regulation can therefore contribute to resistance against these classes.

Despite the importance of insecticide resistance, integrative evidence combining phenotypic susceptibility with both kdr genotyping and biochemical profiling remains limited for Ae. aegypti in Saudi Arabia. Previous work has often focused on larval susceptibility or limited kdr screening, with minimal characterization of detoxification and antioxidant pathways. Therefore, the present study provides an integrated molecular and biochemical characterization of insecticide resistance in Ae. aegypti collected from Al-Madinah Al-Munawarah. Specifically, we quantify resistance phenotypes to commonly used adulticides, determine the prevalence and combination patterns of three major Vgsc substitutions (S989P, V1016G, and F1534C), and measure activities of key detoxifying/antioxidant enzymes (AChE, GST, esterases, and catalase). By integrating these data sets, we aim to clarify the multifactorial nature of resistance and provide evidence to support sustainable, molecularly informed vector management in the Arabian Peninsula.

Materials and Methods

Study Area and Sample Collection

Larval stages of Aedes aegypti were collected from ten residential and peri-urban localities across Al-Madinah Al-Munawarah, Saudi Arabia (Figure ). Sites were selected to capture heterogeneity in urban habitat types and were sampled using a pragmatic, container-based approach (positive domestic and peri-domestic breeding sites identified during routine inspections). Larvae were collected as mixed instars from multiple positive containers per site, transported to the insectary, and reared to adults under standardized conditions (28 ± 2 °C, 75 ± 10% RH, 12:12 h light:dark). The sampling period (month/year) and approximate GPS coordinates for each collection locality are provided in Supplementary Table S1 to support reproducibility and assessment of spatial bias. Newly emerged adult females were morphologically identified using Rueda (2004) and allocated to molecular genotyping (individual mosquitoes) and biochemical assays (pooled replicates).

Laboratory Susceptible Strain

A laboratory susceptible Ae. aegypti strain (“Lab strain”) was maintained at the Dengue Fever Mosquito Research Unit, King Abdulaziz University (KAU), Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. The colony has been reared for ≥ 30 generations under strictly insecticide-free conditions and housed in a dedicated insectary area physically separated from insecticide handling/spraying to minimize inadvertent exposure (including insecticide odors/aerosols). Rearing conditions matched those of the field-derived mosquitoes (28 ± 2 °C, 75 ± 10% RH, 12:12 h L:D). The Lab strain was used as the susceptible reference comparator in WHO diagnostic-dose adult bioassays and for biochemical assays. Although comprehensive molecular characterization of this colony was beyond the scope of the present work, it is maintained without insecticide selection and used operationally as a susceptible reference; importantly, all biochemical measurements were performed in parallel under identical assay conditions for both field and laboratory strains to ensure valid comparative interpretation.

Adult Insecticide Susceptibility Bioassays (WHO Diagnostic Dose)

Adult susceptibility of the Al-Madinah field population was evaluated using the WHO tube test with diagnostic (discriminating) concentrations on impregnated papers for three pyrethroids (lambda-cyhalothrin 0.5%, permethrin 0.75%, and cyfluthrin 0.15%) and two organophosphates (malathion 5% and fenitrothion 1.0%). Diagnostic concentrations are insecticide-specific and are designed to yield near-complete mortality in susceptible strains; therefore, values differ across active ingredients. Tests used nonblood-fed females (2–5 days old) in four replicates of 25 per insecticide (n = 100), with matched controls. After 1 h exposure, mosquitoes were transferred to holding tubes, provided with 10% sucrose, and mortality was recorded after 24 h. Results were interpreted following the WHO criteria. Abbott’s correction was applied when control mortality was 5–20%, and tests were repeated if control mortality exceeded 20%.

Molecular Analysis

2.4.1. DNA Extraction and PCR Amplification

Genomic DNA was extracted from individual adult females using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany; Cat. No. 69506) following the manufacturer’s protocol, automated through the QIAcube DNA/RNA Purification System to ensure consistency and purity. Two primer sets were employed to amplify segments of the IIS6 and IIIS6 domains of the voltage-gated sodium channel (Vgsc) gene (Table ). Each 25 μL PCR reaction contained 12.5 μL of PCR Master Mix, 2 μL of each primer (forward and reverse), 2 μL of DNA template, and 6.5 μL of nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C (IIS6 primers) or 58 °C (IIIS6 primers) for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min.. PCR products were visualized on 1.5% agarose gels stained with ethidium bromide, and images were captured using a Biometra BDA Digital Compact Gel Documentation System (Germany). Amplicons were purified and sequenced commercially (Macrogen Inc., Seoul, South Korea).

2.4.2. Mutation Identification and Sequence Analysis

Sequence chromatograms were aligned with reference sequences from GenBank (Accession Nos. FJ479613 for S989P and V1016G, and AB517740 for F1534C) using the BioEdit Sequence Alignment Editor. Sanger chromatograms were visually inspected to confirm base calls at the target codons. Heterozygous genotypes were identified by the presence of overlapping peaks at the variant site, whereas homozygous genotypes showed a single peak. Point mutations in the Vgsc gene were identified by direct comparison, and allelic frequencies were computed to assess mutation prevalence.

Biochemical Assay

2.5.1. Sample Preparation

Field and laboratory samples were processed in parallel using the same homogenization, storage, and assay procedures to minimize technical variability and allow direct comparison of normalized enzyme activities. Adult female mosquitoes were pooled into replicates of five individuals per sample. Each 200 mg homogenate was prepared in ice-cold 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at a 1:5 w/v ratio using a glass–Teflon homogenizer. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected and stored at −20 °C until analysis.

2.5.2. Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) Activity

AChE activity was determined using Ellman’s colorimetric method. The reaction mixture (1 mL total volume) contained 20 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 2.6 mM acetylthiocholine chloride, 0.5 mM 5,5′-dithiobis­(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), and 0.1 mL of enzyme extract. Absorbance was measured at 412 nm for 1 min using a Jenway UV–Vis spectrophotometer. Enzyme activity was expressed as μmol of substrate hydrolyzed per minute per milligram of protein using a molar extinction coefficient of 13,600 M–1 cm–1. An eserine/physostigmine inhibition control was not included; therefore, activity is reported as total cholinesterase (AChE-like) activity and interpreted cautiously.

2.5.3. Catalase (CAT) Activity

Catalase activity was quantified following Bergmeyer’s method. The 1 mL reaction mixture consisted of 75 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 25 mM hydrogen peroxide, and 0.1 mL of enzyme extract. The decrease in absorbance was monitored at 240 nm for 1 min. One unit of CAT activity was defined as the amount of enzyme decomposing 1 μmol of H2O2 per minute per mg protein (ε = 0.0392 mM–1 cm–1).

2.5.4. Esterase Activity

Esterase activity was determined according to the protocol of Hammock and Sparks using p-nitrophenyl butyrate (p-NPB) as a substrate. The 1 mL reaction mixture contained 50 mM Tris–HCl buffer (pH 8.5), 100 mM p-NPB dissolved in DMSO, and 0.1 mL of enzyme extract. The reaction was incubated at 30 °C for 5 min, and the formation of p-nitrophenol was measured at 405 nm. One unit of enzyme activity corresponds to the formation of 1 μmol of p-nitrophenol per minute per mg of protein.

2.5.5. Glutathione S-Transferase (GST) Activity

GST activity was assayed using the method of Habig et al. The reaction mixture (1 mL) contained 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 6.5), 1 mM reduced glutathione (GSH), 1 mM 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (CDNB), and 0.1 mL enzyme extract. Absorbance was monitored at 340 nm for 1 min. Activity was calculated using an extinction coefficient of 9.6 mM–1 cm–1 and expressed as μmol/min/mg protein.

2.5.6. Protein Quantification

Total protein content was determined using the Bradford method with bovine serum albumin as the standard. Enzyme activities were normalized to protein content for all biochemical assays.

Statistical Analysis

Enzyme activity data are presented as mean ± SD. Prior to parametric testing, normality (Shapiro–Wilk) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test) were evaluated. Because comparisons were between two groups (field strain vs laboratory strain), differences were tested using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test when assumptions were met; otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. For mortality comparisons between strains, proportions were compared using Fisher’s exact test. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied. Analyses and graphics were produced using GraphPad Prism v10.0.

Results

Adult Insecticide Susceptibility (WHO Diagnostic-Dose Bioassays)

Adult susceptibility assays conducted using WHO diagnostic-dose impregnated papers demonstrated low 24 h mortality across all tested insecticides, confirming a resistant phenotype in the Al-Madinah field population. Mortality was 48% for lambda-cyhalothrin (0.5%), 61% for permethrin (0.75%), 58% for cyfluthrin (0.15%), 41% for malathion (5%), and 23% for fenitrothion (1.0%) (Table ). Control mortality remained ≤6%, validating test reliability. According to WHO criteria, these mortality values indicate resistance to both pyrethroids and organophosphates.

Molecular Analysis

Molecular analyses were successfully performed on all 50 adult female Ae. aegypti specimens collected from Al-Madinah Al-Munawarah. PCR amplification of the VGSC gene yielded the expected amplicons spanning the IIS6 domain (≈550 bp; targeting codons 989 and 1016) and the IIIS6 domain (≈500 bp; targeting codon 1534) (Figure ). Purified PCR products were genotyped by Sanger sequencing, which identified three key kdr substitutions in the field population: S989P, V1016G, and F1534C. Multiple sequence alignment of the VGSC IIS6 and IIIS6 fragments against the corresponding GenBank reference sequences confirmed the positions of the interrogated kdr loci, including S989, V1016, and F1534 (Figure ). Representative high-resolution annotated Sanger sequencing chromatograms illustrating wild-type, heterozygous, and homozygous base calls at codons 989, 1016, and 1534 are shown in Figures , , . Detailed genotype counts and frequencies at each locus (SS/SP/PP; VV/VG/GG; FF/FC/CC) and the observed multilocus genotype combinations are provided in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3, respectively. S989P and V1016G were in complete linkage disequilibrium, with identical genotype distributions at both codons: 12/50 (24%) wild type (SS/VV), 10/50 (20%) heterozygous (SP/VG), and 28/50 (56%) homozygous mutant (PP/GG) (Supplementary Table S2). At codon 1534, F1534C genotypes were FF in 20/50 (40%), FC in 16/50 (32%), and CC in 14/50 (28%) individuals (Supplementary Table S2). Multilocus genotype combinations across the three positions are summarized in Supplementary Table S3. Notably, 18/50 (36%) carried at least one mutant allele at each of the three loci (S989P + V1016G + F1534C), indicating a high prevalence of combined kdr profiles in the Al-Madinah population.

Discussion

Phenotypic confirmation and integrated resistance profile. The inclusion of WHO diagnostic-dose adult bioassays provides direct phenotypic confirmation that the Aedes aegypti population from Al-Madinah Al-Munawarah exhibits resistance to multiple adulticides. Mortality remained low against pyrethroids (lambda-cyhalothrin 48%, permethrin 61%, cyfluthrin 58%) as well as organophosphates (malathion 41%, fenitrothion 23%) at 24 h postexposure (Table ), supporting a multi-insecticide resistance phenotype. These standardized phenotypic end points strengthen the interpretation of the molecular (kdr) and biochemical findings reported in this study. The present study provides compelling molecular and biochemical evidence that Ae. aegypti populations from Al-Madinah Al-Munawarah have developed complex, multifactorial mechanisms of insecticide resistance, primarily to pyrethroids and organophosphates, with potential cross-resistance to DDT suggested by the kdr profiles reported in the literature. The detection of three key knockdown resistance (kdr) mutationsS989P, V1016G, and F1534Cat high frequencies in single, double, and triple combinations underscores a severe and evolving resistance scenario within local mosquito populations. This genetic landscape reflects a high level of selection pressure resulting from prolonged insecticide exposure and presents a significant obstacle for current vector control operations that rely predominantly on pyrethroid-based formulations. Differences in enzyme activity between field and susceptible laboratory strains may reflect broader metabolic and oxidative-stress responses that contribute to insecticide tolerance, including GST- and esterase-mediated detoxification and antioxidant defenses. However, because cholinesterase activity was measured without an eserine/physostigmine inhibition control and assays were conducted on pooled specimens, these results are interpreted as population-level functional signals rather than direct evidence of specific target-site mutations or gene-level mechanisms. As future directions, RNA-Seq–based transcriptomic profiling can provide mechanistic resolution by identifying specific detoxification genes and pathways associated with resistance and by capturing temporal dynamics after insecticide challenge. Integrating transcriptomics with matched individual-level genotyping and enzyme phenotyping would enable genotype–phenotype associations and strengthen resistance surveillance.

Linking kdr genotypes to bioassay outcomes. The high frequency of linked S989P/V1016G mutations and the substantial prevalence of F1534C and triple mutant combinations are consistent with the reduced pyrethroid mortality observed in WHO tube tests. Although LC50-based dose–response resistance ratios (RR) and genotype-stratified phenotype association analyses were not included here, the concordance between low diagnostic-dose mortality and the predominance of single, double, and triple kdr mutation patterns supports a functional contribution of target-site insensitivity to the observed resistance phenotype. Importantly, the molecular and biochemical markers are most informative when interpreted together with the WHO bioassay phenotype (Table ). Elevated detoxification/antioxidant activitiesparticularly GST and catalase, with a moderate increase in esterasemay reduce the effective internal insecticide dose and buffer oxidative stress generated during xenobiotic metabolism. Such “first-line” metabolic defenses could allow a proportion of mosquitoes to survive initial insecticide exposure, thereby increasing the likelihood that individuals carrying kdr alleles persist and reproduce under sustained operational pressure. Over time, this survival advantage may contribute to the enrichment and stabilization of linked kdr alleles (e.g., S989P/V1016G) and multilocus combinations observed in this population.

The predominance of the S989P and V1016G mutations in approximately 76% of field specimens, particularly in homozygous genotypes, suggests strong selection and genetic fixation within Ae. aegypti populations. Both mutations occur in the IIS6 segment of the voltage-gated sodium channel (Vgsc) gene, a crucial molecular target of pyrethroid insecticides. These substitutions have been widely recognized across Asia and the Middle East as major determinants of pyrethroid resistance. ,− The V1016G mutation, in particular, confers substantial resistance to type II pyrethroids such as deltamethrin, whereas the accompanying S989P mutation further stabilizes the altered conformation of the sodium channel, amplifying resistance intensity. ,, The third mutation, F1534C, located in the IIIS6 region of Vgsc, is typically associated with resistance to type I pyrethroids, including permethrin, and with cross-resistance to DDT. ,, When these three substitutions co-occur within the same haplotype, their interaction produces a synergistic effect that markedly enhances the resistance phenotype. Previous studies have demonstrated that triple mutant haplotypes (S989P + V1016G + F1534C) can confer up to a 50-fold increase in resistance to pyrethroids compared with wild-type mosquitoes. , The relatively high frequency (36%) of these triple mutant genotypes in the present study clearly indicates that Ae. aegypti in Al-Madinah has been subjected to prolonged and intense exposure to pyrethroid insecticides. Such exposure likely arises from both household insecticide use and routine municipal fogging programs. This finding aligns with similar reports from Sri Lanka, Malaysia, Indonesia, and other regions of Saudi Arabia, where combined kdr alleles have rendered traditional pyrethroid-based interventions less effective.

The molecular resistance pattern detected in Al-Madinah mirrors global trends in Ae. aegypti adaptation. In several Asian and Latin American regions, the co-occurrence of S989P, V1016G, and F1534C mutations has been associated with both operational control failures and rapid reinfestation following insecticidal treatment. ,, This evolutionary convergence indicates that similar selective forcesprimarily the widespread use of pyrethroidsare shaping resistance phenotypes worldwide. The identification of triple mutant haplotypes in Al-Madinah, therefore, places Saudi Arabia within a broader global framework of Ae. aegypti resistance evolution, suggesting that local control programs are facing challenges comparable to those encountered in other dengue-endemic regions. While kdr mutations provide a genetic foundation for resistance, the biochemical data from this study demonstrate that metabolic mechanisms act synergistically to reinforce and diversify the mosquito’s adaptive response. The observed reduction in acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity among field populations is consistent with possible selection affecting cholinergic pathways (e.g., altered AChE sensitivity), which may contribute to reduced susceptibility to organophosphates/carbamates; however, enzyme activity measurements alone cannot confirm ace-1 target-site mutations, and dedicated AChE inhibition assays and/or ace-1 genotyping would be required for definitive attribution. ,,, Notably, the reduced AChE activity occurs alongside low mortality to organophosphates at WHO diagnostic doses (Table ), suggesting that selection pressures in Al-Madinah may extend beyond pyrethroids and could have implications for the operational usefulness of organophosphate/carbamate-based rotations. Operationally, this combined phenotype underscores the need for cautious insecticide-rotation planning and emphasizes the value of integrating nonchemical components within IVM frameworks, rather than relying on sequential switches within the same neurotoxic target space.

Moreover, the significantly elevated activities of catalase and glutathione S-transferase (GST) observed in field populations provide strong evidence of an enhanced oxidative stress response to xenobiotic exposure. These enzymes play critical roles in maintaining cellular redox balance and in detoxifying reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated during insecticide metabolism. − Elevated GST activity, in particular, facilitates the conjugation of electrophilic insecticide metabolites with glutathione, promoting their solubilization and excretion while preventing oxidative damage to cellular components. Similarly, catalase functions as a primary antioxidant enzyme that decomposes hydrogen peroxide, one of the key ROS produced during xenobiotic metabolism. The upregulation of these enzymes in Al-Madinah populations is consistent with previous findings from Côte d’Ivoire, India, and Mexico, where GST and catalase overexpression have been strongly correlated with pyrethroid resistance. ,, These biochemical defenses provide mosquitoes with metabolic flexibility, enabling them to tolerate insecticidal stress from diverse chemical structures. Importantly, such enzymatic upregulation can complement kdr mutations by reducing the effective concentration of insecticide molecules reaching neural targets, thereby enhancing survival under exposure..

In the present data set, catalase and esterase showed the largest proportional increases relative to the laboratory strain (approximately ∼ 1.2-fold each), while GST exhibited a consistent elevation. This pattern suggests a resistance “signature” characterized by strengthened oxidative-stress buffering with complementary detoxification capacity. Similar co-occurrence of kdr mutations with elevated GST and antioxidant enzymes has been reported in multiple regions, indicating that the Madinah profile aligns with broader global trends rather than representing a wholly unique mechanism; however, the concurrent presence of high-frequency linked kdr alleles and measurable enzyme elevations supports an advanced, multimechanistic resistance architecture. Although the increase in esterase activity observed in this study was moderate compared with GST and catalase, it nonetheless supports the role of esterase-mediated detoxification in resistance development. Esterase can hydrolyze ester bonds in pyrethroids and organophosphates, converting these molecules into less toxic derivatives before they interact with neural targets. , Even modest increases in esterase activity may enhance tolerance, particularly when acting alongside kdr-based target-site insensitivity, and such combined resistance patterns have been associated with reduced efficacy of adulticiding interventions in other settings. ,,

The coexistence of multiple kdr mutations alongside elevated detoxification/antioxidant enzyme activity indicates that Ae. aegypti in Al-Madinah possesses a highly integrated resistance system. This integration reflects an advanced stage of evolutionary adaptation, where both molecular and metabolic mechanisms interact to sustain resistance even under varying environmental and chemical stressors, potentially reducing the effectiveness of conventional control approaches. Scope and limitations. While WHO diagnostic-dose assays provide standardized phenotypic confirmation of resistance, the present manuscript does not include larval LC50-based dose–response testing, resistance ratios (RR), or genotype-stratified phenotype association analyses (e.g., mortality/LC50 by specific haplotypes). Comprehensive dose–response toxicity and resistance-intensity data sets have been generated and are currently being analyzed in depth as part of a separate dedicated study; to maintain a clear mechanistic scope and avoid redundancy, we therefore report here the WHO diagnostic-dose mortality end points alongside molecular and biochemical markers. Additionally, direct bioassays to DDT and specific pyrethroids not tested here (e.g., deltamethrin) will further refine cross-resistance inference in future work.

From a public health perspective, these results indicate that continued reliance on pyrethroids as the primary tool for adult mosquito control in Al-Madinah is unsustainable. Thus, the widespread presence of triple kdr mutations and elevated detoxification enzymes substantially reduces pyrethroid efficacy, compromising outbreak response capabilities during dengue transmission peaks. To address this challenge, integrated vector management (IVM) strategies should be urgently adopted. Nonchemical measuressuch as environmental management, source reduction, and biological controlshould serve as the foundation of control efforts. When chemical interventions are necessary, the rotation of insecticides with different modes of action, such as neonicotinoids or organophosphates, should be prioritized. The inclusion of synergists like piperonyl butoxide (PBO) in pyrethroid formulations can also inhibit metabolic enzymes and temporarily restore insecticide efficacy. Equally important is the establishment of continuous molecular and biochemical surveillance systems. Regular monitoring of kdr allele frequencies and enzyme activity levels will allow early detection of emerging resistance trends and support evidence-based decision-making for vector-control programs. Integration of these molecular insights into operational frameworks will enhance the sustainability and cost-effectiveness of control efforts.

5. Conclusions

This study provides integrated phenotypic, molecular, and biochemical evidence that Aedes aegypti from Al-Madinah Al-Munawarah shows reduced susceptibility to commonly used adulticides and harbors a high prevalence of Vgsc kdr substitutions (S989P, V1016G, and F1534C), including multilocus combinations. Together, these findings are consistent with the emergence of pyrethroid resistance, with implications for the operational performance of chemical-based vector control when pyrethroids are heavily relied upon. In parallel, the field population exhibited altered enzyme activity profiles relative to the laboratory strain, including increased activities of key detoxification/antioxidant enzymes (e.g., GST, esterases, and catalase) and differences in acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity, supporting a potential contribution of metabolic/oxidative stress pathways alongside target-site resistance. Importantly, enzyme assays quantify functional activity at the population level (here, using pooled specimens) and therefore do not, on their own, establish specific causal molecular mechanisms or permit individual-level genotype–phenotype correlations. Accordingly, the conclusions have been framed to remain proportional to the methods employed.

From a public-health perspective, these results support the need for routine resistance surveillance in Al-Madinah Al-Munawarah and the implementation of evidence-based Integrated Vector Management (IVM). Operational priorities include reducing over-reliance on a single insecticide class, adopting rotation based on mode of action, and strengthening nonchemical measures such as source reduction and environmental management, with chemical interventions guided by local susceptibility data. Future work should include (i) resistance intensity assays and broader geographic/seasonal surveillance to contextualize temporal variation, (ii) designs enabling matched individual genotyping and individual biochemical measurements to test genotype–phenotype associations, and (iii) transcriptomic profiling (RNA-Seq) as a recommended next step to mechanistically validate detoxification pathways suggested by enzyme bioassays. Establishing a centralized national resistance repository would further support evidence-based decision-making and adaptive vector-control strategies in Saudi Arabia.
