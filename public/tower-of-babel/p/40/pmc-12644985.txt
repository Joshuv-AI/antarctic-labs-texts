Intracardiac myxoma of unusual topography and rare clinical presentation in the context of congenital polymalformative syndrome: a case report

Introduction

Cardiac myxoma is the most common benign cardiac tumour.1 It is a benign tumour with functionally malignant behaviour and has the potential for systemic embolization.1,2 Their preferred location is the left atrium (75% of cases) but all heart chambers can be affected.1,2 Involvement of the right atrium accounts for 15%–20% of cardiac myxomas. There are essentially two different morphological types (papillary or polypoid).2 Depending on the type of morphology, complications can range from cavitary or intravascular obstruction (polypoid) to the formation of systemic emboli (papillary).2 Some forms of myxoma are familial, others are isolated.2,3 Familial forms are caused in particular by variant of the PRKAR1A gene with the Carney complex.2,4

Case summary

A 28-year-old patient with ectrodactyly and ectodermal dysplasia disorder presented to the Emergency room with abdominal pain and anasarca. At the time of the emergency visit, the patient had been presenting with oedema of the lower limbs and abdominal distension for 3 weeks. The patient also complained of stabbing abdominal pain in the left flank accompanied by watery diarrhoea. He had no weight loss or anorexia. Physical examination showed malformation of the upper and lower limbs (bilateral ectrodactyly) without integumentary involvement, facial dysmorphia with retrognathism, and dental dysplasia.

The patient’s history included a congenital ectrodactyly and ectodermal dysplasia–clefting syndrome (EEC) with a singular phenotypic expression, namely malformation of the upper and lower limbs (bilateral ectrodactyly) without integumentary involvement, bilateral renal hypoplasia (justifying renal transplantation in 2003), facial dysmorphia with retrognathism, dental dysplasia, conductive hearing loss, undetermined visual impairment, and epilepsy.5 A kidney transplant was performed in 2003, but the patient’s outcome was marked by a transplant rejection in 2020, with resumption of haemodialysis for extra-renal purification 3 days per week. Home treatment included immunosuppressive therapy (tacrolimus and methylprednisolone), levetiracetam and osteoporotic prophylaxis (calcium and vitamin D). A thoracoabdominal CT scan showed no intrathoracic or intra-abdominal mass to explain the abdominal distension and oedema of the lower limbs.

A transthoracic cardiac ultrasound revealed a lobulated mass with a sessile infiltrative base at the level of the atrial side of the tricuspid valve in the proximity of the inferior vena cava, with partial obstruction (Figure 1A). Further characterization by contrast-enhanced cardiac MRI revealed a non-thrombotic, polylobulated budding mass at the broad base of the lateral wall of the right atrium extending from the tricuspid valve to the junction with the inferior vena cava with prolapse of the mass within it (Figure 1B). The lesion exhibited a homogeneous signal, appearing isointense with mild heterogeneous enhancement on T1-weighted sequences, and slightly hyperintense with subtle internal heterogeneity on T2-weighted images. It exhibited early heterogeneous arterial-phase enhancement, progressive contrast uptake, and complete washout on delayed imaging. These imaging features were not consistent with lipoma, myoma, or fibroelastoma. Fibroma and haemangioma were also considered unlikely due to the atypical location and the absence of persistent global enhancement, although the mass demonstrated slight heterogeneous late gadolinium enhancement.

The patient was subsequently referred to a tertiary centre for further assessment and diagnosis. A guided endovascular biopsy under transoesophageal echocardiography guidance was performed, enabling the aetiological assessment of the cardiac mass (Figures 1C, D, and E).6 The mass biopsy was in favour of a cardiac myxoma, confirmed secondarily by immunohistochemistry (Figure 1F).

The patient underwent a healthy margin resection of the right atrial lesion, after which he showed clinical improvement. No recurrence of myxoma was observed in the following years.

Discussion

The case describes a cardiac myxoma of unusual topography, occurring in the context of a congenital polymalformative syndrome that is probably hereditary but not typically associated with cardiac tumours.7

The congenital polymalformative syndrome had not yet been the subject of a genetic study and remained unidentified, as no genetic panels were available at the time of diagnosis (1994). The patient is scheduled to undergo genetic testing for ectrodactyly and ectodermal dysplasia–clefting (EEC) syndrome, including analysis of TP63 gene variants.

This myxoedematous tumour originating from the lateral wall of the right atrium on the tricuspid side, with extension to the inferior vena cava had an uncommon clinical presentation (inferior vena cava syndrome). Myxoma in the right atrium is uncommon, and extension from the right atrium to the inferior vena cava is even rarer. In this setting, inferior vena cava or atrial thrombi, mimicking a heart tumour, may pose a significant diagnostic challenge.8

Studies report the prevalence of myxoedematous tumours at 0.03% in the general population.2 This is the most common primary cardiac tumour, accounting for 50%–85% of benign cardiac tumours. It predominantly affects middle-aged women, with a female-to-male ratio of 3:1. Treatment options of cardiac myxoma are essentially surgical by complete resection of the mass, with excellent post-operative survival and a low recurrence rate.2–4

Some forms of myxoma are familial; others are isolated. Familial forms are caused in particular by variant of the PRKAR1A gene with the Carney complex.4 Carney’s complex is transmitted in an autosomal dominant fashion and consists of a multi-systemic disorder, i.e. a skin disorder with pigmentary anomalies (nevus coeruleus or lentigo), various endocrine gland disorders (thyroid tumour, testicular tumour, acromegaly, ACTH-independent Cushing’s syndrome, etc.) and the appearance of a single or multiple myxoma (cardiac or cutaneous), which may potentially affect all the heart chambers.4

Embryologically, these tumours are considered to originate from pluripotent mesenchymal cells.1, 2

With regard to morphogenesis and embryology, organogenesis of the heart extends from the 2nd to the 10th gestational week, with the formation of intracardiac cavities from mesodermal cells.9

It is agreed that cardiac malformations occur early in organogenesis. Limb development takes place between the 4th and 5th week from different embryological structures. Several genes are known to be involved in limb formation (hedgehog, WNT, FGF, etc.).10 There is therefore a time overlap between the formation of the various organs and limb development. Polymalformative syndromes may result from a variety of external factors (viral/bacterial infection, drug iatrogenesis, etc.), genetic variants,11 etc. In the case of the patient illustrated in this clinical report, the clinical history of the pregnancy showed no evidence of teratogenic drug exposure, viral or bacterial infection during gestation, or risky environmental exposure.

The polymalformative clinical picture of the index case covers a series of congenital syndromic disorders, except for the appearance of an intracardiac myxoma of unusual topography.

This clinical case highlights the possibility of unusual cardiac involvement in a likely hereditary polymalformative context and may help identify a genetic variant underlying this association. Based on this data, further genetic studies could confirm variants of the ectrodactyly and ectodermal dysplasia–clefting syndromes associated with the development of cardiac myxoma.

A possible hypothesis for the myxomatous burden observed in ectrodactyly–ectodermal dysplasia–clefting (EEC) syndrome involves variants in the TP63 gene, which plays a critical role in the development of limbs, skin, and the urinary tract.5 The p63 protein regulates the expression of multiple downstream genes involved in cell proliferation and differentiation. In animal models, such as Xenopus laevis, p63 has been shown to inhibit mesoderm formation.12 These findings suggest a potential role for p63 not only in epithelial development but also in epithelial tumorigenesis in humans, particularly in the development of atrial myxoma in EEC syndrome.12

Given the polymalformative context, a genetic origin could be considered, although no definitive link has been established.

In conclusion, this case highlights a rare association between a cardiac myxoma of unusual topography and a congenital polymalformative syndrome, raising the possibility of an underlying genetic variant (potentially in the TP63 gene) linking ectodermal, limb, and cardiac development. Further genetic investigation may help establish a novel genotype–phenotype correlation.
