A letter to the editor in response to the article titled “From knife to needle – the trend of vascular neurosurgery in Belgium” by Veken et al
Authors: Prashant Lakhe, Alok Umredkar, B.C. Anil Kumar, Varidh Katiyar
Published: 2025

Dear editor,

We read with great interest the article titled “From knife to needle – the trend of vascular neurosurgery in Belgium” by Veken et al. (Van Der Veken et al., 2025) The authors have chosen an excellent topic for analysis pertaining to the domain of cerebrovascular neurosurgery. The emphasis is correctly laid on the need to further investigate the reasons that underlie the changes in the trends. Endovascular neurosurgery has indeed revolutionized the field of cerebrovascular surgery. There should be no surprise to see a generalized inclination favouring endovascular therapies over fine microsurgical skull base techniques for cerebral aneurysms. At the same time the cost of intervention has also increased significantly. A few possibilities that may explain this trend can be summarized as follows:•With fewer number of people worldwide now performing difficult approaches for complex aneurysms, the trainee doctors have limited exposure to such cases•As more number of neurosurgeons are getting versed with endovascular techniques, they tend to offer the less invasive approach especially for proximal and complex aneurysms•Doctors from fields like radiology and neurology also have started performing endovascular procedures•A patient is more likely to opt for a scarless procedure in cases where both options are offered

An important point the authors have made is the need for dedicated training among resident and young neurosurgeons. The art of vascular neurosurgery is indeed very fine and demands dedicated efforts, years of practice and continuous efforts to better the skill set apart from the knowledge of anatomy. It takes years even after completion of formal residency and fellowships to master the technique of aneurysmal clipping. The learning curve is long and the possibility of untoward outcomes in spite of application of the finest skills may be disheartening for the young neurosurgeon (Zhao et al., 2015; Umredkar et al., 2010).

Aneurysmal sub arachnoid haemorrhage is an entity which can be challenging to treat in spite of the application of best neurosurgical and intensive care protocols. Every patient responds in a different way and the regional and demographic factors play an important role in the natural history (Ghosh et al., 2012). A better outcome may be expected if such a challenging entity is treated at a high volume centre by an experienced team. The importance of dedicated support staff trained and experienced in managing patients with cerebrovascular diseases needs to be emphasized especially during setting up a cerebrovascular facility.

Another important aspect mentioned by the authors is the need to assess the quality of training imparted to cerebrovascular surgeons. In general, formal training programs like residency and post doctoral fellowships help the trainees gain sufficient knowledge about the most common anatomical aspects of cerebrovascular surgery. However, it needs to be understood that in a field of medicine as unpredictable as neurosurgery, one rule doesn't apply to all. Rather, skill refinement is a continuous process which depends on individual's commitment to the particular speciality one choses. Hence, learning happens with every case and every surgery has its own learning curve.

As cerebrovascular surgery including both-micro neurosurgery and endovascular technique-has evolved, a more rational application of these meticulous techniques might see both these methods complimenting each other by a careful, individually tailored approach towards case selection. The facts mentioned by the authors however suggest a rather different scenario with a heavily skewed preference towards endovascular techniques. There is a reasonable debate and quite understandably so whenever it comes to selection of a particular approach when faced with a cerebrovascular pathology. With certain obvious advantages that an endovascular method offers like no post operative scar, decreased bleeding and wound related complications, patients may naturally seem to be inclined towards coiling of an aneurysm versus a clipping procedure which requires “cutting open” the cranium. But it is at this point where a complete picture needs to be presented to the patient by the cerebrovascular surgeon. The pre operative patient education should be undertaken with due importance and not just a mere formality. The detailed session should involve educating the patient and family the nature of disease, various options available and the fact that both-microsurgical and endovascular modalities-have their own set of risks and benefits and one is not superior to another except in clearly defined cases. In fact, a well educated patient might even be given an idea about the anatomy of the lesion. Studies have shown that preoperative educational counselling yields better patient satisfaction following surgery (Nahar et al., 2025).

It needs to be emphasized that the patient be treated at a high volume center equipped with comprehensive neurosurgical facilities including experienced vascular neurosurgeons trained in both the techniques, dedicated staff, indocyanine green enabled operating microscope, neuro-intensive care complimented by tools like trans cranial doppler and dedicated team for patient education and counselling. In fact a proper dietary advice by an experienced dietician can make a significant impact in the outcomes since these patients are prone for micro nutrient deficiencies. Aneurysmal sub arachnoid haemorrhage is an entity which not only challenges the patient's physiology but also tests the readiness of the treating team. Needless to mention, this is one condition where the neurosurgeon cannot afford to lower his guard right from the moment when the patient is admitted to the time of his discharge. With the great advances in the field of micro neurosurgery, the art of aneurysm clipping continues to evolve. Keeping this art alive would indeed be a tribute to all the great neurosurgeons who have left behind a great legacy.
