Research progress of base editing and prime editing tools based on the CRISPR/Cas system
Authors: Herui Gao, Song Gao, Guoshi Kan, Leonid N. Valentovich, Yingfeng An
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The base editor (BE) and prime editing guide RNA (pegRNA)-based prime editor (PE) technologies relying on the CRISPR/Cas system are very efficient gene editors that have been developed in recent years. The BEs include cytosine base editors (CBEs) that mediate the conversion of C to T, adenine base editors (ABEs) that mediate the conversion of A to G, glycosylase base editors (GBEs) that mediate the conversion of C to G, and the dual-base editors (DBEs) that mediate the simultaneous conversion of C to T and A to G. The BEs and PEs have been successfully applied for genome editing of various animals, plants, and microorganisms due to their advantages of high efficiency and independence of DNA double-strand breaks or donor DNA. The development process and characteristics of various BEs and PEs and their effectiveness of application are systematically introduced to provide a reference for selecting appropriate genome editing technologies. Moreover, the urgent issues that need to be addressed for more efficient and precise editing are summarized and prospected.

Introduction

In recent years, the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat (CRISPR) systems have been widely used in genome editing, including gene knockout, knockin, and correction.1 For these CRISPR systems, a CRISPR-associated (Cas) nuclease is commonly recruited by a single-guide RNA (sgRNA) to a specific genomic target for double-stranded DNA break (DSB), which may be repaired through the non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) pathway. The NHEJ pathway typically leads to insertion and deletion events (indels), resulting in gene inactivation.2,3 To avoid genes carrying destructive mutations, the homology-directed repair (HDR) pathway is often applied to repair DSBs with exogenous donor DNA templates to introduce more precise mutations.2 However, compared to the NHEJ pathway, the HDR-based gene correction is often inefficient. Moreover, it is difficult to achieve efficient and stable single-base mutations through DNA repair induced by DSBs.4 On the other hand, about 58% of human diseases are caused by single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), which are also the genetic basis of many important property variations of animals and plants.5 Recently, a series of BEs have been developed by fusing deaminases with a nicking variant of Cas (nCas) nuclease or deactivated Cas (dCas), which can achieve specific nucleotide conversion with minimal DSBs.6,7 The nCas or dCas nuclease can be localized to a specific location in the genome when combined with sgRNA, where specific nucleotides can be deaminated by deaminase of the fusion protein. Then the deaminated nucleotides can introduce site-directed mutations during DNA replication and repair. These BEs have been widely used for genome modification in various animals, plants, and microorganisms. Construction and optimization of cytosine base editors

Construction of CBEs through a fusion of uracil glycosylase inhibitor with APOBEC1-dCas9 or APOBEC1-nCas9

In 2016, the first generation of cytosine base editor (CBE) (BE1; Figure 1D) was developed by fusion of rat cytosine deaminase (APOBEC1) with the dCas9 through an XTEN linker of 16 amino acids, which can achieve cytosine to thymine conversion at positions 16–19 nt upstream of the PAM sequence NGG of target genes.8 When the fusion protein targets a genomic DNA with the guidance of sgRNA, the APOBEC1 can bind to the R-loop region of single-strand DNA (ssDNA) formed by dCas9 protein and deaminate the cytosine (C) within a certain range of the ssDNA to uracil (U). Then the U will be converted to thymine (T) through DNA replication or repair to ultimately introduce C to T conversion (Figures 1A and 1B). The second generation of cytosine base editor (BE2; Figure 1D) was constructed by fusion of the uracil glycosylase inhibitor (UGI) with the BE1 base editor. As a result, BE2 can improve editing efficiency compared to BE1 and inhibit the nucleotide excision repair of U. Similarly, the conversion of C to T can be achieved through DNA replication, with an editing efficiency of up to about 20%.44 The third generation of the base editor (BE3; Figures 1C and 1D) was constructed by replacing dCas9 with nCas9, allowing it only to cleave complementary DNA strand of sgRNA (i.e., target strand).11 As a result, BE3 could increase the efficiency of base editing to 50% and reduce off-target effects when compared with BE1 and BE2. The fourth generation of the base editor (BE4; Figure 1D) was constructed by adding a copy of the UGI gene and optimizing the linker sequence based on BE3. As a result, BE4 showed higher editing efficiency and a larger editing activity window (the range within which the BEs can effectively perform base editing on the target gene) than BE3.18 In addition, these cytosine base editors can mainly achieve precise single-base editing without DNA strand break or with a single DNA strand break, effectively minimizing genome damage caused by DSBs during the editing process.45 Based on BE3, an eBE-S3 base editor was constructed using additional co-expression of the UGI gene (2A-UGI). This improved system not only reduced the formation of undesired indels and substitutions but also increased the frequency of C-to-T editing, thereby boosting both the fidelity and efficiency of base editing.46 It is also reasonable to regulate the level of CBE synthesis and its nuclear localization by codon and nuclear localization signal (NLS) sequence optimization.7 Cytosine deaminases can not only be directly fused with Cas proteins but can also be recruited to form complexes with Cas proteins, thereby achieving the function of base editing. For example, the BE-PLUS based on the SunTag system (i.e., nCas9 concatenated with 10 copies of GCN4 being used to recruit APOBEC1 fused with scFv) was designed, which expanded the effective editing window from 4–7 nt to 4–16 nt upstream of the PAM sequence (Figure 3B).20 In 2016, human cytosine deaminase mutants were fused with the MS2 protein, which was recruited to the skeleton of sgRNA through the hairpin structure of MS2 RNA (Figure 3A). Optimization of CBEs through modification of Cas proteins

The CBE needs to recognize PAM sequences through Cas proteins (Figure 6B), which in turn affects the editing range of the BE in the genomes. Moreover, the expression levels of the Cas proteins and their specificity in binding to DNA may also affect the editing level and specificity. Therefore, modifying and replacing the Cas protein is expected to improve the editing range, level, and specificity of the editors. The earlier versions of CBE were constructed based on canonical Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 protein (SpCas9), and their targeting range was limited by the PAM recognition sequence 5′-NGG-3′. The SaCas9 protein from Staphylococcus aureus with PAM recognition ability different from that of spCas9 was used to construct a YEE-BE3 base editing system, breaking through the 5′-NGG-3′ PAM sequence limitation and reducing the editing window to only 1–2 nt upstream of the PAM sequence.11 As a result, the correction range of YEE-BE3 for human pathogenic single-base mutations has increased from 27% of BE3 to 66%. In addition, SpVQR-Cas9 with PAM sequence of 5′-NGA-3′, SpEQR-Cas9 with PAM sequence of 5′-NGAG-3′, SpVRER-Cas9 with PAM sequence of 5′-NGCG-3′, and SaKKH-Cas9 with PAM sequence of 5′-NNNRT-3′ were used instead of SpCas9 to construct CBE, resulting in up to 2.5-fold increase in target sequence range.11 Li et al.22 constructed dCpf1-BE by fusing Cpf1 (Cas12a) with APOBEC1, which can effectively target the T-enriched PAM region of 5′-TTTV-3′ (V = A, C, or G) with an editing window of 8–13 nt downstream of the PAM sequence. Due to the unique structure of Cpf1, it is impossible to design a nicked targeted chain and expose a non-targeted chain to APOBEC1. Therefore, the initial efficiency of dCpf1-BE was not high, similar to that of TALE-AID. Qin et al. used SaCas9 and SaKKH (SaCas9 variant) instead of SpCas9 to construct BE3-SaCas9 and BE3-SaKKH base editors, respectively. As a result, BE3-SaCas9 and BE3-SaKKH expanded the PAM sequence from 5′-NGG-3′ to 5′-NNGRRT-3′ and 5′-NNNRRT-3′, respectively. Moreover, the editing windows of BE3-SaCas9 and BE3-SaKKH expanded from 4–8 nt to 5–18 nt and 1–16 nt upstream of the PAM sequence, respectively.47 Hua et al.48 used nVQR Cas9 (Cas9 mutated variant) instead of SpCas9 to construct a BE3-VQR base editor that recognizes the PAM of 5′-NGA-3′ and has an editing window of 8–15 nt upstream of the PAM sequence. Moreover, Hua et al. expanded the editing scope of CBE by using xCas9 with PAM of NG, GAA, and GAT14 and Cas9-NG with PAM of 5′-NG-3′.19 Li et al. applied various variants of SpCas9 (e.g., SpG, SpCas9-NRRH, SpCas9-NRCH, and SpCas9-NRTH) to construct corresponding CBEs to expand the scope of PAM.49 Huang et al. replaced SpCas9 with a cyclic variant of Cas9 (CP-Cas9) to construct CP-CBEmax, which expanded its editing window from 4–7 nt to 4–11 nt.50 These results show that modification and replacement of the Cas proteins of the editors have different effects on the editing windows, including both narrowing and widening the editing windows. In some pathogenic point mutation events, precise correction of a single base is often required, and additional base modifications can bring side effects. However, the editing window of the classic CBEs is commonly 4–5 nt upstream of the PAM sequence (Table 1).8 Therefore, it is particularly necessary to develop a BE that accurately mutates a single base.51 However, it is worth mentioning that BEs with wide editing windows should be more conducive to editing certain sites under PAM constraints. In addition, BEs with a wider editing window should be valuable for achieving multi-point mutations within a certain range.

Optimization of CBEs through modification of cytosine deaminases

It is estimated that cytidine deaminases should have a direct impact on the editing activities of CBE, so numerous studies have been conducted to modify or replace cytidine deaminases to optimize CBE. It was found that replacing APOBEC1 of BE3 with human APOBEC3A could not only improve editing efficiency but also broaden the editing window from 4–8 nt to 2–13 nt upstream of the PAM sequence.13 Moreover, the amino acids corresponding to the ssDNA binding domain of APOBEC1 were mutated to construct YE1-BE3-FNLS, which could significantly reduce DNA off-target efficiency and improve gene editing efficiency.52 Ma et al.26 removed the C-terminus of human cytosine deaminase (hAID) to obtain AIDx and fused dCas9 with AIDx to develop a targeted AID-mediated mutagenesis (TAM) that can produce mutations from C to T, A, and G at bases 5–9 upstream of the PAM sequence. Nishida et al.18 used the cytosine deaminase PmCDA1 derived from sea lamprey to construct BEs, which can achieve 15%–55% targeted mutations in mammalian cells. Based on the structural similarity between APOBEC1 and APOBEC3G, Kim et al.11 predicted that W90Y or W90F substitution is associated with enzyme catalytic activity, while R126E or R132E is associated with substrate DNA binding. After performing combinatorial mutation and screening, it was found that YE1-BE3 (W90Y + R126E) can narrow the editing window to 1–2 nt while maintaining efficiency similar to that of BE3. The A3A-BE3 formed by replacing APOBEC1 with APOBEC3A can also effectively achieve gene editing, expanding its editing window to 3–12 nt. Gehrke et al.51 obtained the A3A (N57G)-BE3 base editor for specifically mutating the C of T motif by mutating the DNA binding site of APOBEC3A in A3A-BE3. When screening for RNA off-target editing mutants, they found that BE3-R33A and BE3-R33A/K34A can narrow the editing windows.53 Cheng et al.54 screened for cytosine deaminases from lamprey and changed their fusion position with nCas9 to identify a series of CBEs with different editing windows, among which broad-range CBEs have wide editing windows of 1–14 nt. Liu et al.55 introduced mutations based on the structure and sequence alignment of hAID and APOBEC3A, obtaining eAID (N51G)-BE4max with high activity and a narrow editing window for the GC motif. These results indicate that modification and replacement of cytidine deaminases can significantly improve the base editing efficiency, especially for some specific nucleic acid motifs, and affect the editing windows. Moreover, the smallest single-strand-specific cytidine deaminase was introduced to CBE, allowing the engineered CBE to be packaged into a single adeno-associated virus (AAV). Importantly, the deaminase from this clade was found to be robust in editing genomes of in soybean plants, which was inaccessible to original versions of CBEs.

Optimization of CBEs through modification of both Cas proteins and cytosine deaminases

Due to the fact that both Cas proteins and cytidine deaminases are important components of BEs, they can significantly affect editing efficiency and editing windows. Therefore, the replacement or modification of these two components simultaneously is reasonable for improving BEs. For example, Cas9 proteins with different PAM recognition abilities (e.g., spCas9 and saCas9) and APOBEC1 with different mutations were used to construct BEs; the resulting YEE-BE3 can break through the NGG sequence limitation and reduce the editing window to 1 or 2 nt. In addition, YEE-BE3 can introduce targeted mutations more precisely.11 Hua et al.56 fused Cas9-NG with hAID variants, expanding the recognition PAM to 5′-NG-3′ and changing the editing window to 4–14 nt. Wang et al.57 fused ScCas9 with hAID variants, expanding the recognition of PAM to 5′-NAG-3′ and changing the editing window to 3–7 nt. Juan Li et al.49 fused the SpCas9 variant with A3A deaminase to construct eA3A-SpG, eA3A-NRRH, and eA3A-NRCH base editors, which can easily find suitable sites for C to T base substitution. Zhong et al.58 expanded the editable range of the BE constructed by fusing PmCDA1 deaminase with Cas9-NG and extended the base editing window to positions 1–14 nt upstream of the PAM sequence.

Construction and optimization of ABEs

Construction of ABEs through a fusion of deoxyadenosine deaminases with nCas9

About half of human pathogenic single-nucleotide polymorphisms are caused by deamination of cytosine, leading to the conversion of C to T. Targeted editing of A to G is expected to fundamentally correct these genetic mutations.5 Adenosine can be converted into inosine through deamination, and inosine can pair with cytidine under the action of DNA polymerase and is ultimately replicated as guanosine during DNA repair.59 Therefore, adenine deaminase was fused with nCas9 to construct the adenine base editor ABE for conversion of A to G.6

ABE can perform DNA editing using a mechanism similar to CBE. The fusion product of deoxyadenosine deaminase and nCas9 binds to the target DNA sequence under the guidance of sgRNA, and the deoxyadenosine deaminase domain can convert A base to hypoxanthine (I). During DNA replication, the original A should be replaced by G at the target site (Figures 2A and 2B).6 The first generation of ABE (ABE2.1) was constructed by fusing the evolved bacterial tRNA-specific adenosine deaminase TadA variant (TadA∗) with the N-terminus of nCas9 while fusing the C-terminus of nCas9 with NLS (Figure 2C).6 ABE does not require inhibition of the activity of alkyl adenine DNA glycosylase (AAG), suggesting that the damage repair mechanism of inosine may be unrelated to AAG. The ABE editing in cells lacking AAG did not have improved efficiency or product purity. The proportion of indels can be ignored (≤0.1%)50,60 even for genome editing with the earlier versions of ABEs, and so far there is no report of significant off-target (A to non G) editing with ABEs.6,29,50Figure 2Schematic diagram of the ABE system(A) Deaminase-mediated base editing reactions. Optimization of ABEs through modification of deoxyadenosine deaminases

Precise ABEs are limited to the fusion of the TadA variant with Cas9. The native TadA is a homologous dimer, with one monomer catalyzing deamination and the other promoting tRNA substrate binding.61 To further improve editing efficiency, during deaminase modification the wild-type ecTadA monomer was fused with the directed evolved ecTadA∗ monomer to form a single-stranded heterodimer. This heterodimer fused with nCas9 to form the ecTadA-ecTadA∗-nCas9 protein. After the first three rounds of ABE evolution in David Liu’s Group, the resulting ABE3.1 prefers to mutate the YAC (Y = T or C) motif.6 After seven rounds of directed evolution, the ABE system ABE7.10 was selected, with an editing efficiency of up to 53%. The editing window of ABE7.10 is 4–7 nt upstream of the PAM sequence. The editing efficiency of ABE7.10 was further improved to construct ABEmax (Figure 2D) through codon sequence optimization and increasing the number of NLS.7 Moreover, introducing the F148A mutation to ABE7.10 (i.e., ABE7.10F148A) can not only reduce RNA off-target activity but also narrow the original editing window from 4–7 nt to 5–6 nt, making the base editing more precise.62 Richter et al. used phage-assisted continuous evolution methods to modify the deaminase of ABE7.10, resulting in ABE8e (Figure 2D). Compared with ABE7.10, ABE8e contains eight additional mutations, and the catalytic rate of deoxyadenosine deaminase increased 590 times.29 In some cases, ABE can generate additional C to G or T conversion in the TCN (N = A, T, C, or G) motif when C is at 5–7 nt upstream of the PAM sequence. Although the efficiency of this kind of conversion is not high (up to 11.2%), it should affect the precise editing of ABE.63 The mechanism behind this phenomenon needs further research. To maintain the high accuracy of ABE, the sgRNA should be designed to avoid the appearance of C in the TCN motif at positions 5–7 nt upstream of the PAM sequence.63 In order to improve the accuracy of ABE8e editing, Chen et al. conducted rational design and screening based on the cryoelectron microscopy structure of ABE8e and found that introducing key mutations L145T and N108Q into the activity pocket of TadA-8e deaminase (i.e., ABE9) can significantly reduce the editing window to 5–6 nt upstream of PAM sequence while retaining high editing activity. Moreover, the ABE9 can narrow the editing window to 1–2 nt, almost eliminating off-target editing of cytosine and the editing activity of C in the TCN motif. More importantly, ABE9 can introduce DNA/RNA off-target events to the background level, effectively avoiding various potential off-target hazards of traditional ABE. Theoretically, ABE9 is a precise targeting tool for correcting nearly 50% of inherited pathogenic SNVs.31 The highly active deoxyadenosine deaminase TadA-8e has been evolved using phage-assisted continuous evolution to perform cytidine deamination. Evolved TadA-8e with mutations at DNA-binding residues has altered enzyme selectivity toward deoxycytidine over deoxyadenosine deamination.64 Then the TadA-8e was further mutated through introducing N46L variant to eliminate its adenine deaminase activity. As a result, the base editor of Td-CGBE can realize highly efficient and precise C·G-to-G·C editing.65 Moreover, the CBE-T with engineered TardA has constructed to introduce high-efficiency on-target C·G-to-T·A mutations across a sequence-diverse set of genomic loci.66

Optimization of ABEs through modification of both deoxyadenosine deaminases and Cas proteins

Only about a quarter of known pathogenic mutations contain NGG PAM sites at appropriate positions, which can be used to achieve SpCas9-mediated base editing. Although CBEs are widely compatible with many orthologous Cas proteins, ABEs have limited compatibility with Cas proteins except for SpCas9.50,60 This incompatibility may be due to the fact that Cas proteins except for SpCas9 have a short binding time with DNA, coupled with the slow catalytic rates of the deoxyadenosine deaminases. After directed evolution, the resulting ABE8e showed compatibility with multiple Cas9 homologs.29 The TadA7.10 was fused with SpCas9 variants and homologous proteins (e.g., SaCas9, iSpyMacCas9, ScCas9, SpCas9-VRER, SpCas9-VQR, xCas9, and Cas9-NG) to successfully solve the compatibility problem of ABE base editing, achieving expanded PAM ranges and editing windows.47,48,56,57,67 In addition, Yan et al. fused TadA8e or TadA9 (TadA8e with V82S + Q154R mutations) with different Cas9 variants of SpCas9n, SpCas9n-NG, SpRYn, or ScCas9n. These Cas9 variants have high compatibility, and some tested target sites have base editing efficiencies of over 90%.53 Moreover, the ABEs based on the fusion of TadA8e/r with the Cas9 variant exhibited high efficiencies in converting A to G.68,69,70,71 TadA8e has optimized the nucleotide sequences based on the preference of rice codons and fused with SpCas9n-NG and SpGn to recognize the PAM sequence of NGN, as well as with SpRYn to recognize the PAM sequence of NNN.72 In addition, a set of highly efficient adenine single-base editors of hyABE8e-NG, hyABE8e-SpG, and hyABE8e-SpRY have been developed to introduce average editing efficiencies of 67.8%, 73.9%, and 78.8%, respectively, which were 26–30 times higher than that of the ABE 7.10 (2.6%). Subsequently, TadA8e was fused with SpCas9 variants to construct ABE8e-NRTH, ABE8e-NRRH, and ABE8e-NRCH, respectively, achieving A to G base substitution targeting the PAM site of 5′-NRNH-3′ in plants.49 In addition, TadA9 was fused with ScCas9 and Cas9 NG to construct ABE9-ScCas9 and ABE9-NG base editors, respectively. As a result, ABE9-ScCas9 and ABE9-NG can achieve base substitution upstream of PAM of 5′-NAG-3′ and 5′-NG-3′, respectively, in plant genomes.73 In addition to rational design of known protein components of BEs, the directed evolution techniques, especially the phage-assisted continuous evolution (PACE) strategies, are valuable for molecular modification of BEs.39,74 The foundation for realizing directed evolution is establishment of a sensitive and high-throughput screening strategy.

Construction of BEs that can introduce other types of base conversions

Although ABEs and CBEs can introduce base transition mutations of C to T, T to C, A to G, and G to A, which represent half of the known disease-associated SNVs, the remaining transversion mutations (i.e., purine to pyrimidine) cannot be restored by direct deamination.75 However, around 10% of human pathogenic SNVs can be restored only if the C to G corrections are introduced.12 Recently, a variety of base transversion editors based on DNA glycosylases have been developed. These base transversion editors include uracil-N-glycosylase (UNG) or N-methylpurine DNA glycosylase (MPG) to recognize deaminated bases (e.g., hypoxanthine and uracil), generating apurinic/apyrimidinic (AP) sites through hydrolyzing the N-glycosidic bond.76 Then versatile nucleotide replacements in the target sites can be introduced after the repairment of these DNA lesions through pathways of base excision repair (BER) and/or translesion synthesis (TLS).53,77,78 Kurt et al. discovered low-frequency C to G mutations during the ABE-based base edition.33 It was speculated that deaminase reacts with C to form U, and the U base is recognized and cleaved by UNG to form an abasic site that is further transformed into G after a series of DNA repair (Figure 3C). Therefore, the eUNG-BE4max(R33A) ΔUGI (i.e., CGBE1) was constructed by deleting the UGI element, fusing UNG, and introducing point mutations. Interestingly, several C-to-G base transversion editors were developed by simply replacing UGI with UNG in CBEs. The basic principle is that UNG excises the U that results from cytosine deamination. This triggers a TLS that favors the insertion of C opposite the AP sites, which results in a dominant C to G editing outcome after DNA repair and/or DNA replication. When UGI is removed from the CBE editor, the probability of increasing C to G mutations may be due to the fact that there is no UGI inhibition of UNG, which should in turn inhibit subsequent BER pathways. Therefore, Chen et al.34 developed C-to-G BEs (CGBEs) through direct fusion of various cytokines involved in the BER repair pathway. After comparison, it was found that rAPOBEC-nCas9-rXRCC1 had the highest efficiency. At present, research on CGBE is mainly based on CBE, integrating UNG or BER factors to improve the editing efficiency of C to G. It is a process of amplifying by-products, and significantly improving the efficiency of C to G editing is still a major challenge. Yuan et al.79 obtained optimized C-to-G BEs for C-to-G transversion by using deaminase and uracil-DNA glycosylase from different species and their relative positions, together with codon optimization. Koblan et al. developed CGBEs with diverse editing profiles through CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) screen-based identification of factors that affect C to G editing outcomes. These CGBEs enable the correction of disease-related transversion SNVs with >90% precision and up to 70% efficiency.80 In addition, Sun et al. optimized the GBE system by replacing human UNG with yeast Ung1, fusing the single-stranded DNA binding domain of Rad51 protein, and introducing R33A mutation in APOBEC1.35 As a result, the optimized GBE2.0 introduced up to 30.88% editing efficiency and 35.57%–92.92% of C to G conversion. Direct deamination cannot achieve A to T and A to C base transversion editing required for correction of ∼25% of human pathogenic SNVs.36 Interestingly, it was reported that MPG can yield an AP site through cleaving Hx, the deaminated product of adenine.81,82 As a result, nucleotide replacement might be possible during the repair of AP sites. Therefore, adenine transversions might be realized by incorporating MPG in ABE. Based on this, an adenine transversion base editor of AYBE has been developed through the fusion of human MPG (hMPG) to ABE8e.36 In addition, another adenine transversion base editor of AXBE has been developed, which works similarly to AYBE.12 The AXBE was constructed by the fusion of ABE8e with AAG from a mouse (mAAG). Tong et al.37 fused Cas9 nickase with MPG to construct a deaminase-free guanine base editor (gGBE); the G to T and G to C (i.e., G to Y) transversion can be used to correct 5% T to G and 10% C to G human pathogenic SNVs. This gGBE exhibited up to 81.2% base editing efficiency and up to 0.95 conversion ratio of G to C or G to T (i.e., G to Y) in both mouse embryos and human cells. Although ABE-based A to G editing on the opposite strand can introduce T to C base transition, no similar strategy can be used to achieve T to G base transversions required for correction of 19% of human pathogenic SNVs.36 The nCas9 was fused with engineered human UNG variants to construct gTBE. After several rounds of rational mutagenesis of the UNG in cultured human cells, a gTBE with higher activity of T to G or T to C (i.e., T to S) was obtained, which showed the outperformance when compared with other protein engineering strategies.38

The off-target effects of BEs can be divided into Cas9-dependent style80 and Cas9-independent style.60,62,83,84 Upon recognizing the target DNA, the Cas9-sgRNA binary complex unwinds the DNA double strand into single strands through helicase activity, facilitated by a deamination reaction catalyzed by the deaminase. The sgRNA may recognize and bind to other sites due to mismatches, resulting in off-target effects. To address this issue, a strategy of TaC9 was designed to reduce the off-target effects of BEs by introducing the TALE tool to the BE.23 The helicase activity of nCas9 is used to unwind the double-stranded target site, while the transcription-activator-like effector (TALE) fused with deaminase (Tad-TALE) was used to target the exposed substrate of the region near nCas9 for a deamination reaction (Figure 3G). The advantage of this design is that there is no Tad-TALE at the off-target site dependent on Cas9, so exposing dsDNA does not result in a deamination reaction. At the same time, there is no Cas9 present at the off-target site of Tad-TALE, so there is no exposed substrate for its catalysis. This strategy can decouple the helicase function of nCas9 and the catalytic function of deaminase and construct Tad-TALE to endow it with catalytic function. However, the TaC9 system requires the delivery of three components, making it more complex. The Deaminase-splitting is a valuable strategy for robust base editing. For example, the deaminases could be reconstructed with two inactive fragments based on the interaction system FKBP and FRB induced by rapamycin. The deaminase splitting is a valuable strategy for robust inducible base editing.85 In addition, the split-engineered BEs (seBEs) based on FRB could enable spatial-controlled base editing with 8.1-fold reduced off-target effects compared with the intact BEs (Figure 3D).86

Construction of dual base editors

The BEs described above can only perform single-base substitutions on the target sequence and cannot achieve simultaneous C to T and A to G base substitutions for the same target sequence. It was reported that fusion of cytosine deaminase and adenine deaminase with Cas protein can achieve both C to T and A to G base substitution events at the target site (Figure 3E). These dual base editors are very important because some diseases are caused by double-base mutations.39,40,41,42,43,67 To address this issue, researchers constructed SPACE,39 A&C-BEmax,40 ACBE,41 and Target-ACEmax42 dual base editors for base edition in mammalian cells and developed saturated targeted endogenous mutagenesis editors (STEMEs) for base edition in plants. The STEME was constructed by simultaneously fusing two deaminases (cytosine deaminase APOBEC3A and adenine deaminase ecTadA-ecTadA7.10) at the N-terminus of nCas9 and fusing one or two UGIs at the C-terminus of nCas9. As a result, the STEME can generate C to T base and A to G base replacement events at positions 1–17 nt and 4–8 nt upstream of the PAM sequence, respectively. The STEME system was applied to achieve site-saturation mutagenesis in the rice herbicide resistance gene OsACC to successfully introduce P1927F and W2125C mutations.43 Moreover, the SWISS system was developed to induce multiple editing types including C to T conversion, A to G conversion, and indels with efficiencies of up to 25.5%, 16.4%, and 52.7%, respectively. The efficiency of simultaneous occurrence of these three editing types is 7.3%. The development of the dual base editors has provided technical support for various applications such as protein engineering, directed evolution, and co-editing of multiple genes.43 In 2020, cytosine deaminase rAPOBEC1, adenine deaminase TadA, and nCas9 were used to construct fusion proteins in different ways. In addition, replacing rAPOBEC1 with hAID with higher mutation activity, optimization of codon used, optimization of NLS, tandem of multiple UGIs, and optimization of linker sequence were performed to construct a dual base editor A&C-BEmax. Compared with the single-base editors ABEmax and AID-BE4max, A&C-BEEmax showed a slightly decreased A to G conversion activity and a consistent editing window but an increase in C to T conversion activity. In addition, the editing window of A&C-BEmax was expanded from 3 to 13 nt to 2–17 nt upstream of the PAM sequence, and RNA off-target activity was significantly reduced.40 A codon-optimized cytosine deaminase PmCDA1 was constructed to show simultaneous A to G and C to T editing activity at 47 genomic targets.42 In addition, Target-AID was fused with the smaller miniABEmaxV82G to construct SPACE, which exhibited higher dual editing activity compared to the simultaneous expression of two single-base editors.39

BEs that depend on deaminase fusion typically deaminate within a narrow window (Figure 6D), and C or A nucleotides that are very close to the target C or A can also be converted, resulting in “bystander effect” base editing that is difficult to be avoided in some cases.6,20,33 In addition, these base editing processes are limited by the targeting range of the Cas protein domain, which requires the presence of a PAM sequence within a specific distance range from the target nucleotide. Furthermore, some BEs can introduce off-target editing in DNA and RNA.60,83 Although many of these drawbacks have been alleviated through the optimization of deaminase-based BEs (Figure 6C), these editors cannot introduce all 12 possible types of point mutations.

Construction and optimization of PEs

Construction of the PE for efficient genome editing

Due to the demand for precise and widely applicable gene editing techniques, PEs have been developed to directly write new genetic information into a target DNA site using an engineered reverse transcriptase (RT) fused with nCas9, programmed with a pegRNA that both specifies the target DNA site and encodes the desired sequence for editing (Figure 4). PEs can achieve all types of DNA point mutations, small insertions, and small deletions at target sites in genomes without introducing DSBs.87,88 However, the point mutation efficiency of the first generation of PE editor PE1 was 0.7%–5.5%, while the efficiency of indels was 4%–17%, indicating that there is still significant room for improvement.87Figure 4The strategy of genome editing with PEsThe PE system comprises nCas9 protein to induce a nick in the non-target strand, M-MLV RT enzyme fused with nCas9 to synthesize single-stranded DNA incorporating desired edition, and pegRNA containing PBS and template of M-MLV RT. nCas9 cleaves the nucleotide on the non-target strand, and the PBS binds to the complementary sequence on the non-target strand, resembling a “primer.” With the RT template, M-MLV RT can initiate the synthesis of a single-stranded DNA with the 3′-OH of the “primer.” Then the synthesized ssDNA with mutations might replace the original strand during the genome repair process.

Optimization of the PEs through modifying the RT enzymes

During the processes of synthesizing cDNA with RT enzymes, the progression of the RT enzyme may be affected by the structures of the single-stranded RNA templates.89 The RNA chaperones can release RT enzymes from the paused state by unstabilizing the structure of the RNA templates. In the PE process, adding the RNA chaperone of the nucleocapsid (NC) improved PE efficiency in plants.90 It was also observed that the RNase-H domain of MMLV RT in PE2 can be removed without affecting the editing efficiency.90,91,92,93 In addition, a set of PEs has been constructed using RT of Cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV RT), mature enzyme RT of Agathobacter rectalis (MarathonRT), and GsI-IIC RT from Geobacillus stearothermophilus (TGIRT III).20,94 Although the editing efficiencies of PEs constructed based on these RT variants reached the detection limit, these PEs exhibited much weaker activities than PE2 constructed based on MMLV RT. However, designing RT variants with higher activities and better substrate binding efficiencies may improve editing efficiencies. Another factor worth considering is that smaller RT variants can lead to the production of smaller PEs, making them easier to deliver into the nucleus for genome editing. To improve the performance of PE, the RT enzyme separated from nCas9 can also be recruited to the editing sites by the MCP-MS2 system (Figure 5G).20 In addition, the reverse transcriptase MMLV RT was introduced with five specific mutations (i.e., D200N, L603W, T330P, T306K, and W313F) to construct PE2 (Figures 4 and 5A), which improved the thermal stability of RT and the binding of RT and pegRNA and achieved 5.1-fold higher base editing efficiency than PE1.87,95 By adding two tandem N-terminal nuclear localization signals (i.e., SV40 NLS and vBP-SV40) at the C-terminus of PE2, PE2∗ was constructed to achieve almost complete nuclear localization, with a base substitution efficiency 1.9 times higher than PE2.96 By engineering the MMLV RT domain of PE2 and optimizing the codon of this domain to adapt to human cell expression, as well as adding additional C-terminal c-Myc NLS and introducing SpCas9 mutations of R22K and N394K, a more effective PE variant (PEmax) has been constructed. Optimization of the PEs through modifying the Cas proteins

Although PEs do not require precise localization of PAM at the target site, editing nucleotides far from the PE target site (>40 nt) remains a challenge.87 To expand the range of sequences that can effectively realize PE-based editing, researchers have constructed PEs using other Cas9 variants that have non-NGG PAMs.14,19,98 Without sacrificing editing efficiency, a set of PEs was constructed based on SpCas9 variants of PE2-VQR, PE2-VRQR, PE2-VRER, PE2-NG, PE2-SpG, and PE2-SpRY85.99 Some other PEs based on smaller Cas9 variants (e.g., SaCas9, SaKKH-Cas9, SauriCas9, and CjCas9) have been constructed, which have different PAM preferences.91,96,97 FNCas9 (Cas9 from Francisella novicida bacteria) recognizes NGG PAM but cleaves on the target DNA strand upstream of PAM. It was reported that PE constructed based on FNCas9 can introduce shifted editable base window compared with SpCas9, which may be due to the fact that FNCas9 cleaves on the target DNA strand upstream of PAM, although it also recognizes NGG PAM.100 In addition, a size-reduced SpCas9-based PE lacking the RNase H domain and an intein-split construct (PE2 p.1153) has been constructed for adeno-associated virus-mediated delivery into the liver. Although these PE variants have expanded the range of target DNA to be edited, the editing efficiency of these variants is much lower than the typical PE2 effect.91 The PEs based on other Cas9 proteins typically exhibit lower editing efficiency, indicating that these editing processes are highly sensitive to Cas9-pegRNA coordination, DNA target binding, or DNA cleavage. It is worth mentioning that smaller Cas proteins may promote viral packaging and facilitate PE delivery, but improving the compatibility between Cas domains and PEs is crucial.

Optimization of the PEs with other strategies

The 3′ extended end of pegRNA is crucial for initiating reverse transcription and creating the required editing template, which may be exposed to nucleases in the PE-pegRNA complex. The length of the primer binding site (PBS) and RT templates significantly affects the efficiency of PE.87 Therefore, it is necessary to test different pegRNAs with different PBS and RT template lengths for any given target site. Usually, PBS and RT templates are in the range of 8–15 nt and 10–20 nt, respectively.87 Several computational tools have been developed for the optimization of the pegRNA design, such as pegFinder,101 PrimeDesign,102 PE-Designer,103 PnB Designer,104 and pegIT.104 Given that PBS complements some regions at the 5′ end of pegRNA, the annealing may lead to pegRNA cyclization and hinder editing. To suppress cyclization, the 20-nt hairpin that forms the Csy4 recognition sit was fused to the 3′ end of the classical pegRNA.105 In addition, the fourth consecutive uracil in the pegRNA scaffold was mutated into cytosine to eliminate the assumed transcription termination signal, resulting in higher pegRNA expression. This system is called enhanced PE (ePE), and the base substitution efficiency is 1.9 times higher than PE3 without increasing indels.105 In addition, to stabilize the secondary structure of pegRNA, non-C/G pairs (apegRNA) in the hairpin were replaced with C/G pairs, resulting in a higher frequency of targeted indels compared with conventional pegRNA.106 Incorporating structured RNA motifs, such as ribonuclease-resistant RNA motifs (xrRNA) or evopreQ1 motif and Cr772 scaffold (epegRNA), can enhance its stability and prevent degradation by extracellular nucleases (Figure 5F) or circularizing the RNA template (petRNA; Figure 5G). In various cell types, the primer editing efficiencies introduced by the modified RNA motifs are improved by 2.5–4.5 times compared with that of the conventional pegRNAs.107,108 Based on PE2, an sgRNA that can cleave non-edited strands was used to construct improved PE3 and PE3b systems (Figures 4 and 5B).87 It was reported that the editing efficiency of the PE3 system is three times higher than that of PE2, with the highest editing efficiency reaching 78% in human 293T cells. However, due to the use of two sgRNAs, the risk of random indels in PE3 also increased.

To further improve the efficiency of PE editing, David Liu’s laboratory systematically examined 476 genes that may affect the PE system using Repair-seq CRISPRi. As a result, the DNA mismatch repair (MMR) pathway was found to significantly inhibit the editing efficiency of the PE system and increase the production of indels and other byproducts.97 The PE editing systems have been improved through the transient expression of a dominant negative MMR protein (MLH1dn). In six MMR-proficient cell types (e.g., pluripotent stem cells and primary T cells), the PE4 (PE2+MLH1dn; Figure 5A) system enhanced editing efficiency over PE2 by 7.7-fold, and the PE5 (PE3+MLH1dn; Figure 5B) system enhanced editing efficiency over PE3 by 2.0-fold.97 When paired pegRNAs were introduced to directly generate 3′ flaps complementary with each other, the steps of the MMR pathway should not be necessary.109 Using paired pegRNAs with the arranged complementarity between 3′ flaps could improve PE efficiency without elevating by-product proportion (Figure 5H). Moreover, this strategy could enable the installation of long indels, which is an advantage over the single-pegRNA version.20 In addition, the PE6 series (PE6a-g) was constructed through protein optimization using combined strategies of phage-assisted continuous evolution (PACE), non-continuous evolution (PANCE), and rational engineering. Among these PEs, PE6a-c were engineered for reduced editor size, PE6c-d exhibited improved activity on long and highly structured templates, and PE6e-g showed enhanced activity on a subset of targets (Figure 5D).110 Moreover, the short RNA-binding exonuclease protection factor (La) has been identified as a crucial component of PE. Then PE7 was constructed through fusion of La (1-194) with the C-terminal of PEmax, which showed enhanced editing efficiency and negligible off-target effects at eight genomic loci in the U2OS cell line (Figure 5E).111 Recently, generative artificial intelligence (AI) technologies have been utilized to generate a MLH1 small binder named MLH1-SB, which can disrupt the formation of MMR components through binding to the dimeric interface of PMS2 and MLH1. The improved PE architecture of PE7-SB has been developed through incorporating MLH1-SB into PE7, resulting in significantly higher PE efficiency than PE2 and PE7 in HeLa cells.112

Improvement of the delivery efficiencies of BEs

The delivery method based on adeno-associated viruses (AAV) is a commonly used method for delivering BEs into mammalian cells, and it is also one of the key steps in effectively achieving gene editing. The genome-carrying capacity of AAV is within 4.7 kb, while many BEs exceed this size, making AAV’s carrying capacity a significant application limitation. To further expand their application, researchers have achieved improved delivery efficiency by splitting the BEs into two parts, packaging them independently into AAVs, and co-transfecting them into cells.2,113 In addition, smaller Cas proteins86,86,113,114,115,116 and truncated deaminases have been used to construct BEs to facilitate AAV delivery.52,117,118 In addition, researchers have developed some transient-expression-based delivery technologies based on non-viral vectors, virus-like particles, lipid nanoparticles, etc., but the delivery efficiencies are still unsatisfactory.86 For gene therapy, delivery tools should be instantaneous, efficient, safe, and highly inclusive. Therefore, the safety of delivery systems based on viral vectors and non-viral vectors still requires further development and optimization. With the further development of gene editing technologies, more efficient base editing tools and delivery strategies should be used for genome editing of mammalian cells.119 Recently, AlphaFold2 has been used to predict deaminase proteins with many previously unknown properties. In addition, the smallest single-strand-specific cytidine deaminase has been engineered to construct CBE, which can be packaged into a single AAV. Importantly, this strategy has been used to identify a deaminase capable of robust editing in soybean plants, which was previously inaccessible to CBEs.120 The large-scale discovery efforts should substantially broaden the utility of both base and prime editing.

Construction and optimization of organelle genome BEs

As one of the most important organelles in eukaryotes, mitochondria are not only the energy metabolism center of cells but also the signal center of some important physiological processes.121 Mitochondria have circular DNA (mtDNA) with semi-autonomous replication ability, and mtDNA mutations or mitochondrial dysfunction are associated with multiple human diseases.122 How to transport sgRNA to mitochondria has always been a bottleneck problem in the application of CRISPR/Cas systems in the development of mitochondrial BEs, and there is currently no good solution. Early mitochondrial editors relied on the fusion of RNA-free nucleases of ZFNs or TALENs with mitochondrial-targeted signals, inducing double-strand breaks of mtDNA and rapid degradation of the linearized mtDNA.123,124,125,126 The DddAtox domain of deaminase DddA from Burkholderia is similar to APOBEC enzyme in structure and can convert cytosine to uracil in double-stranded DNA sequences. Mok et al. fused DddAtox with TALE and MTS to develop an RNA-free mitochondrial editor DdCBE (Figure 3H). It can catalyze precise editing of target sequences G to A in mtDNA, with an editing efficiency of 4.6%–49.0% in HEK293T cells. To avoid toxicity, DddAtox was split into two halves, DddAtox-N and DddAtox-C, which were introduced into mitochondria and assembled into active deaminase prior to base editing.24 To overcome the strict TC sequence-context constraint of DddA and expand the targeting scope, phage-assisted non-continuous and continuous evolution was used to evolve DddA variants.127 As a result, BEs with evolved DddA6 showed 3.3-fold improved mtDNA editing efficiencies at TC compared to canonical DdCBEs. In addition, BEs with evolved DddA11 showed a broadened HC (H = A, C, or T) sequence compatibility for both nuclear and mitochondrial base editing with increased editing efficiencies at CC and AC targets.127 Cho et al. constructed transcription-activator-like effector linked deaminases (TALEDs), composed of TALE DNA-binding arrays, DddA from the bacteria Burkholderia cenocepacia, and adenine deaminase TadA8e, which can induce A to G editing in human mitochondria (Figure 3F). The TALEDs were very efficient in human cells, resulting in up to 49% A to G editing frequencies at 17 target sites in various mitochondrial genes. This technology has broken through the difficulties of A to G editing in the mitochondrial genome. In addition, the TALEDs have the advantages of stable editing ability in human cells, no cytotoxicity, and minimal off-target effects in the mitochondrial genome.128

The DdCBE has also been used for editing chloroplast genome. For example, DdCBE achieved editing efficiencies of 23% in lettuce and rapeseed protoplasts.129 In Arabidopsis, the plastid-targeting signal peptide (PTP)-directed DdCBE was used to edit multiple genes in the chloroplast genome without introducing foreign DNA.130 Similarly, in rice, a chloroplast transit peptide (CTP)-mediated DdCBE introduced C-to-T mutations in the chloroplast gene psaA, potentially enhancing photosynthetic efficiency and yield (Figure 3H).131 These results show that the CRISPR-independent base editing tool of DdCBE provides a powerful technical foundation for precise manipulation of crop organellar genomes.

Off-target effects of the base editing systems

Traditional and predictable off-target effects

In addition to editing the target sites, the base editing systems may often edit other sites with high similarity to the target sites, which have only a few base mismatches with the target sites. This type of off-target effect is traditional and can usually be predicted using software. (e.g., Cas-OFFinder, E-CRISP, and CRISPR-P). In addition, the updated version of CRISPresso2 has been developed to facilitate analysis of sequencing data of multiple editing experiments from BEs.132 Another analysis tool of BE-Designer can provide all possible target sequences of BEs in DNAs that needs to be edited with valuable information including potential off-target sites.133 Moreover, an attention-based deep learning algorithm based on BE-DICT has been developed to predict base editing outcomes with high accuracy. In principle, the BE-DICT can be trained on any novel BEs, facilitating the application of base editing for research and therapy.134 In addition, an advanced deep learning model of BEdeepoff has been specifically designed to predict off-target effects of BEs. It provides more accurate off-target predictions by integrating sequence features and epigenetic features such as chromatin accessibility.135 The presence of the ones can then be determined by a combination of molecular biology methods. A possible reason for the presence of this type of off-target effect should be the fault tolerance of the sgRNA sequences, and the degree of off-target effects is commonly related to the number of mismatched bases between the sgRNA and the target DNA.136,137 The non-specific characteristics of Cas nuclease itself, as well as the prolonged expression and high concentration of deaminase-Cas fusion protein in vivo, can easily lead to the editing of non-target sites.138

Off-target effects within the genome

Some off-target sites are often randomly or irregularly distributed in the whole genomes but cannot be predicted by the current software. The optimized in vitro off-target evaluation methods (e.g., Digenome-seq, Endo VIII, and Endo V-seq) cannot truly and effectively reflect the in vivo specificity of base editing systems.70,136,137 Due to the fact that deaminases need to bind to ssDNA to function, the existing ssDNA (e.g., transcription regions and replication regions) within cells might become potential off-target sites for base editing systems on the genome-wide scale. It was found that in mouse embryos the off-target effects of the BE3 at 200 nt before and after the sgRNA-targeting sites were higher than that of the ABE.9 In combination with whole-genome sequencing analysis, the genome-wide off-target analysis by two-cell embryo injection (GOTI) technology was used to find that the BE3 can induce SNVs of C to T that are 20 times higher than spontaneous mutations in mice.60 In addition, BE3 and HF1-BE3 were found to cause increased SNVs of C-T at the whole-genome level in rice.84 Both of the above studies indicated that these SNVs of C to T are evenly distributed in chromosomes but show a trend of enrichment in the transcriptional active regions. Earlier studies have shown that overexpression of pyrimidine deaminase or UGI can lead to increased C-T SNVs in the genome of yeast, Escherichia coli, and human cell lines.139,140,141 These results imply certain risks in applying base editing systems to disease treatment at the current stage. On the other hand, it prompts researchers to explore whether it is cytosine deaminase, UGI, or the combined effect of the two components that cause off-target effects at the whole genome level, thus developing a more precise and safe CBE system. In contrast, the ABE system has higher specificity at the whole genome level, which may be due to the use of the adenine deaminase after directed evolution in the ABE system, as well as the absence of a UGI component in this system.

Initial studies have shown that CBE and ABE exhibit relatively lower off-target effects than Cas9, but the detection methods have certain limitations. The principle that DSBs can be captured by treating genomic DNA with the complex of CBE, uracil glycosidase, and sgRNA was used to develop an improved Digenome-seq method that can relatively fairly evaluate off-target editing of CBEs across the entire genome.137 A similar principle that DSBs can be captured by treating genomic DNA with the complex of ABE, endonuclease V, and sgRNA was used to improve Digenome-seq method to relatively fairly evaluate off-target editing of ABEs across the entire genome.70 Moreover, a modified GOTI method has been developed to evaluate sgRNA-independent off-target editing of CBE or ABE across the entire genome. It was found that embryos treated with CBE showed up to 20 times higher unpredictable off-target editing, while those treated with ABE did not show similar off-target phenomena.60 In addition, with the improved detection method, CBE was found to produce 5.3 × 10−7 bp Cas9-independent C mutations in plants.84 A large number of the same mutations was also found in pluripotent stem cells after the edition by CBE.142 David R. Liu’s research group established a strategy to efficiently detect Cas9-independent off-target DNA editing using bacterial rifampicin-resistance experiments and human cell R-loop experiments.88 This strategy was used to evaluate the previously constructed CBE mutants, and it was found that compared to BE4, mutants YE1-BE4, YE2-BE4, YEE-BE4, R33A+K34A-BE4, YE1-BE4-CP1028, YE1-BE4-NG, and AALN-BE4 maintained a 50%–90% of average on-target DNA editing levels, while reducing Cas9-independent off-target DNA editing by 10–100 times and Cas9-dependent off-target DNA editing by 5–50 times. In addition, these CBE mutants were found to efficiently edit DNA without improving RNA off-target editing activities.88 Among these mutants, BE4-YE1 exhibited the highest targeted editing activity and accuracy, which should be valuable for subsequent basic research and clinical applications.

Off-target RNA mutations introduced by BEs

Both cytosine deaminase and adenine deaminase have been reported to act on RNA, which may lead to off-target editing at the transcriptome level. In 2019, members of Keith Joung’s lab demonstrated this (effect) by sequencing the transcriptome when using BE3 and ABE systems in human cell lines. The BE3 was found to introduce C to U edits at a frequency of 0.07%–100% in 38%–58% of expressed genes, while ABEmax was found to introduce up to 100% editing of base A at the RNA level.143 David R. Liu’s laboratory and Hui Yang’s laboratory also demonstrated the presence of detectable off-target RNA editing of the ABE systems and CBE systems in mammalian cell lines.62,144 Moreover, ABEs have also been reported by different laboratories to generate a large amount of RNA level off-target editing of A to I.62,143,144

Strategies to improve the targeting specificity of the BEs

Strategies to reduce DNA off-target effects

There are several strategies to reduce DNA off-target effects (Figure 6A), including the following: (1) modification of sgRNA: it was reported that the use of shortened sgRNAs (gX17 or gX18) or extended sgRNAs (ggX20 or gX20) could reduce the off-target effects while maintaining the efficiency of BE3 or ABE systems136,137; (2) use of high-fidelity Cas nucleases: similar to CRISPR/Cas9, both CBEs and ABEs achieve DNA editing through nCas9-based sgRNA guidance. Therefore, off-target effects of CBEs and ABEs should be similar to that of CRISPR/Cas9, and using highly specific Cas9 variants (e.g., eSpCas9, SpCas9-HF, HypaCas9, and SuperFi-Cas9) instead of Cas9 in the BEs may result in decreased off-target effects.10,145,146 It was reported that the use of engineered Cas9 variants can reduce off-target base editing; (3) Cas-embedding strategy: deaminase (TadA or APOBEC1) was introduced into the middle of nCas9 at tolerant sites to significantly reduce gRNA-independent off-target editing147; (4) shorten the action time of deaminase-Cas nuclease fusion protein in vivo. For example, the ribonucleoprotein (RNP) formed by using a BE and sgRNA was directly injected into cells instead of that formed by DNA-based expression in vivo. As a result, the BE can rapidly degrade within the cells, thereby reducing off-target effects. Strategies to reduce off-target RNA mutations

The main strategy to reduce serious off-target RNA mutations of the base editing systems is to use engineered deaminase variants, which can generally reduce the efficiencies of RNA editing by modifying the RNA-binding sites of deaminases. For example, the modified CBEs of SECURE-BE3,83 eA3A-BE3,51,62 YE1-BE4,88 AALN-BE4,88 hA3G-CBE,148 transformer BE,149 AID-2S/AID-3S,150 and other next-generation CBEs have been developed.54,151 For ABEs, ABEmaxAW or ABEmaxQW,144 ABE7.10-F148A,62 SECURE-ABE,152 ABE8e-V106W,29 ABE-del153,153 and ABE9 (N108Q/L145T)31 were constructed to narrow the editing window and reduce RNA off-targeting effects. However, some of these mutations may have reduced deaminase activity. Thus, the choice of appropriate base editing systems must be a compromise between reduced efficiency and increased specificity.154

Application of base editing tools

Application of CBE

Since its development, the CBE editing system has been rapidly and widely used in genome editing of animal cell lines, human embryos, plants, and bacteria. In addition, the system has also been used for gene therapy, animal disease modeling, and the creation of important agronomic traits.

Application of CBE for gene therapy and construction of animal models

Due to the ability of CBE to convert bases without introducing double-stranded DNA breaks, it is convenient for people to use CBE to introduce missense mutations in the genomes. In addition, CBE can convert CAA, CAG, CGA, and TGG codons into stop codons TAA, TAG, and TGA to terminate gene expression in advance (Figure 6E). Therefore, CBE has been widely used for gene therapy, constructing animal models, and examining gene function.155,156 Various BEs have provided powerful technical means for precise gene therapy. For example, Komor et al.8 used BE3 to successfully correct mutations in the APOE4 gene associated with Alzheimer disease in mouse astrocytes. In addition, Zafra et al.12 corrected cancer-related Ctnnb1 S45F mutations in mouse liver cells by delivering codon-optimized FNLS-BE3. Koblan et al.7 corrected MPDU1 and HBB alleles in primary fibroblasts derived from patients using optimized ABE and CBE. By delivering BE3 into adult mice cells through lentivirus, mutations were introduced to the PCSK9 gene, and cholesterol level was significantly reduced.157 Yang et al.158 used the BE3 to perform point modifications on histone-related genes and explored the importance of epigenetic regulation in mouse embryonic development. Zeng et al.159 corrected the pathogenic FBN1 mutation of Marfan syndrome in human cells and heterozygous embryos using the CBE. Liang et al.160 repaired the HBB mutation of β-thalassemia in reconstructed human embryos. Villiger et al.69 treated liver disease by base editing in adult mice using an engineered intein-split base editor. Moreover, BE3 editor was injected into human cleaving two-cell and four-cell embryos to introduce up to 13-fold higher homozygotic nucleotide substitution efficiency as opposed to injections into MII oocytes or zygotes.161

The construction of animal models with gene mutations can be achieved through homologous recombination, but this approach is inefficient and time-consuming. Recently, animal models have been established in various animals through various BEs. The base editing technologies are most widely used in the construction of mouse models. For example, some disease models have been established in mice including albinism, muscular dystrophy, liver disease, and androgen insensitivity syndrome.72 Jin-Soo Kim’s laboratory has established mouse models of albinism and muscular dystrophy by editing mouse embryonic genes Tyr and Dmd, respectively, with BE3.162 In addition, Liang et al.163 established a mouse disease model of albinism using an HF2-BE2. Sasaguri et al.68 introduced mutations in the Psen1 gene associated with premature aging in mice using the BE3 and Targeted AID systems. Li et al.164 used hA3A-BE3-Y130F to obtain mice with androgen insensitivity syndrome through base editing in GC-rich regions. Animal models have also been established in large mammals such as rabbits, pigs, and sheep. For example, BE3, BE4-Gam, AID-nCas9, and hA3A-eBE-N57G have been used to construct rabbit disease models such as albinism, premature aging, double buttocks, and X-linked dilated cardiomyopathy.55,165,166,167 Li et al.168 used BE3 to edit TWIST2 and TYR genes in pig embryonic fibroblasts. Xie et al.169 achieved simultaneous C to T mutations of multiple gene targets in pigs and established corresponding disease models. In addition, SOC2 and FGF5 genes of sheep were mutated by the BE3 system, and the resulting sheep with increased growth indicators such as weight, size, and hair length were successfully obtained.170,171 In addition to mammals, some non-mammalian (e.g., zebrafish and Xenopus laevis) have constructed disease models based on CBE systems. For example, Zhang et al.172 established a disease model similar to human eye albinism by editing the Tyr gene of zebrafish embryos. Tanaka et al.173 used Targeted-AID to edit zebrafish chd and oep genes, observing the corresponding mutant phenotypes. Park et al. established an albinism disease model of Xenopus laevis by injecting the RNP of BE3.174 Based on the BE3 system, Shi et al.175 constructed a disease model of Xenopus tropicalis simulating human Holt-Oram syndrome and type 1A eyelid albinism. Three CBEs expressed under actin or nanos promoters were evaluated for germline editing rates in Drosophila melanogaster. As a result, the Rattus norvegicus-derived APOBEC1 (rAPO-1) displayed ∼99% base editing rates with undetectable indel formation.176

Application of CBE in plant breeding

Many plant agronomic traits in plants are determined by single-nucleotide mutations or changes in a few bases. Thus, the development of the CBE system provides a powerful tool for quickly, efficiently, and accurately creating plant mutants. For example, the CBEs constructed by fusing cytosine deaminase with the C-terminus of dCas9 and nCas9 were reported to achieve editing efficiencies of 4.3% and 18.3%, respectively, in rice callus tissue.177 The fusion of SpCas9-NGv1 and PmCDA1 was used to construct an optimized CBE editor, further expanding the targeting range of the cytosine base editing system in the rice genome.58,178 Furthermore, the base editing system based on CRISPR/Cas9-guided hyperactive hAID was effective for GC-sequence editing in rice.179 Zong et al. fused cytosine deaminase with the N-terminus of nCas9 and dCas9 and then fused UGI at the C-terminus of the Cas9 variant to construct nCas9-PBE and dCas9-PBE, respectively. When endogenous genes in rice, wheat, and corn were edited, the C to T base editing efficiency of nCas9 PBE was higher than that of dCas9 PBE.180 To further enhance the base editing activity,181 the PBE was modified by fusing two UGIs at the C-terminus of deaminase and the bacteriophage Mu Gam protein at the C-terminus of nCas9. As a result, the Gam protein has not effectively improved the efficiency of base editing in plants, unlike the performance of this protein in human cells.20 Lu et al.182 constructed S97L single amino acid substitution in the OsSLR gene of rice using a CBE base editor. Multiple gene editing was achieved in rice, wheat, and maize protoplasts, and corresponding mutant plants with a mutation efficiency of 45.8% were obtained. The CBE has been successfully used to construct herbicide-resistant arabidopsis, watermelon, wheat, and rice.183,184,185,186 In addition, the CBE has also been successfully applied in the genome editing of tetraploid cotton187 and used to create transgene-free plants resistant to RNA viruses.188

Application of CBE in microbial genome editing

For microorganisms with metabolic products as the main research target, people have a much higher tolerance for the unexpected consequences of off-target than in studies aimed at treating human diseases. Therefore, base editing techniques and strategies have been rapidly applied in the field of microbial synthetic biology. For example, CBE base editor pCBEcos-opt for Clostridium beijerinckii was constructed to generate C to T mutations in the xylR transcription factor gene of C. beijerinckii NCIMB 8052, resulting in a 10% higher xylose uptake by the obtained mutant strain than the wild-type strain.189 In Rhodobacter sphaeroides 2.4.1, stop codons were introduced using the CBE system (dCas9-CDA1-UL), resulting in the knockout of a number of genes involved in the biosynthesis of the antioxidant coenzyme Q10 and demonstrating the importance of these genes in the synthesis of this compound. Furthermore, by knocking out the gene crtB and the gene ppsR involved in light and redox signal regulation, the yield of coenzyme Q10 was increased by 6% and 39%, respectively.190 In addition, the CBE system was used to simultaneously introduce stop codons into genes and gene clusters such as olm, rpp, pks1, pks4, and pks8 in Streptomyces avermitilis MA-4680 to increase the yield of avermectin.191 Moreover, the CBE was used in Streptomyces hygroscopicus to introduce internal stop codons or missense point mutations in the five genes involved in the biosynthesis of hygromycin B, providing evidence to reveal the function of proteins HygJ HygL and HygD.192 A fast and powerful CBE base editor pCBEso was developed for genome editing in Shewanella oneidensis MR-1, which was used to quickly identify key genes involved in N-acetylglucosamine or glucose metabolism in S. oneidensis MR-1.193 In addition, the CBE base editor dCas9-CDA-ULstr was constructed to introduce internal stop codons in the rapS gene that inhibits rapamycin synthesis in Streptomyces rapamycinicus 2001, resulting in a 63.3% increase in rapamycin production.194 The CBE base editing system suitable for Pseudomonas putida KT2440 was constructed to increase the production of catechins by introducing internal stop codons in genes. As a result, compared to the original strain, a 611.17% increase in catechuic acid production during shake flask fermentation was realized with the edited stain.195 Multiplex automated Corynebacterium glutamicum base editing method (MACBETH) based on the BE has been developed to generate mutant libraries, with multiple genes inactivated for increasing glutamate production.196 Subsequently, the MACBETH strategy was further improved by replacing the wild-type nCas9 with nVQR Cas9 (D10A), nVRER-Cas9 (D10A), nxCas9 3.7 (D10A), or nCas9-NG (D10A) to expand the editing range of CBE in C. glutamicum. As a result, 1.7 g/L lysine was produced by the engineered strain, while the yield of lysine in the wild-type strain was almost undetectable.197 In addition, the base editor of CRISPR-CDA-nCas9-UGI was constructed to achieve in situ protein evolution in vivo, which was used to introduce mutations in the Sec transposase and enhance its transport capacity in Bacillus subtilis. Moreover, the CRISPRCDA-nCas9-UGI was used for the evolution of the antimicrobial resistance determinant bceB gene, resulting in mutant strains with higher antimicrobial resistance.198 To complement the Cas-protein-based BEs, random BEs were constructed by the fusion of different non-specific ssDNA-binding proteins with cytidine deaminase. Theoretically, these BEs can achieve C to T mutations throughout the entire genome of the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae. These random BEs successfully improved the tolerance of S. cerevisiae to isobutanol and acetate and increased the production of beta carotene.199 In addition, DNA helicase was fused with activation-induced cytidine deaminase to form the enzyme complex Helicase-AID, which can randomly introduce base mutations throughout the entire chromosome of a cell. This tool was used to perform multiple rounds of random mutagenesis in E. coli KH001 and S. cerevisiae 4742crt producing beta carotene, increasing beta carotene yields by 371.4% and 75.4%, respectively.200 Recently eSCBE3-NG-Hypa was used to introduce premature stop codons into a competitor pathway gene cluster in S. avermitilis, resulting in up to 4.45-fold increase in avermectin yield compared to the control.201 Moreover, the CBE was used to create premature stop codons in the BCL11A enhancer of hematopoietic stem cells, demonstrating the therapeutic potential of DSB-free gene knockout for treating hemoglobinopathies.202

Application of ABE

Similar to CBE, the ABE editing system has been widely used in genome editing of animal cell lines and human embryos for gene therapy and animal disease modeling. In addition, this system has been used in plants and microorganisms for the study of gene function and the creation of mutant variants.

Application of ABE for gene therapy and construction of animal models

The development of the ABE system provides a comprehensive and powerful tool for creating animal mutants and treating diseases caused by point mutations. The ABE system has been successfully used in various cell lines or embryos of human, mice, rats, rabbits, and zebrafish.20,164,203,204 ABE can modify internal stop codon mutations, and when targeting non-coding chains, the TAA, TAG, and TGA codons can be converted into CAA, CAG, and CGA, respectively. For example, missense mutations were introduced in the Tyr gene of mice using ABE7.10 encapsulated in a double trans-spliced AAV virus system.205 The mouse disease models were established by editing AR and Hoxd13 genes,206 and precise editing of mRNA splicing sites in animal cells was achieved using ABE.207 Liang et al.208 edited the variable splicing sites of Tyr and Dmd genes in mice and observed corresponding phenotypes of Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) in mice with Dmd mutations. Yang et al.27 established a mouse model of type I tyrosinemia caused by Fah mutation using ABE7.10. In addition, Yang et al.27 established a Pompeii disease rat model by editing Gaa D645G/I646V with ABE7.10. Liu et al.167 efficiently edited Dmd, Otc, and Sod1in genes in rabbits using ABE7.10, and the resulting rabbits with T279A mutant Dmd exhibited typical clinical symptoms similar to human familial X-linked dilated cardiomyopathy. Qin et al.28 optimized codons of zABE7.10 and successfully introduced musk, rps14, atp5b, and wu:fc01d11 mutations in zebrafish, among which the rps14 mutant showed corresponding phenotypes. Recently, the mutation in patient-derived fibroblasts was found to be corrected by ABE8e (V106W) at an efficiency of 7.4%. In addition, the plasma levels of C26:0/C22:0 could be significantly reduced by robust correction of the pathogenic variants with NG-ABE8e (V106W) delivered by AAV-mediated system.209 Another sample shows that the hepatocyte-like cells differentiated from the human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) edited by ABE showed a 1,000-fold decrease in argininosuccinate levels compared to the non-edited cells.210 The efficient SpG-ABE system has been used to correct some human nonsense mutations of DMD cohorts and restore dystrophin gene expression in iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes.211 Moreover, the DeepABE was found to be a reliable model for predicting ABE editing through demonstration of key concerns in designing a cardiac ABE experiment in vivo.212 ABEs can be used to mutate the start codon of a gene (e.g., i-Silence) (Figure 6E).213 The i-Silence approach can be used to edit four endogenous genes in HEK293T cells. As a result, high-throughput sequencing data showed editing frequencies of 60%–80% for all genes.

Application of ABE in plant breeding

In plants, the ABE system has been successfully used for the creation of gene mutations and corresponding traits in rice, wheat, thale cress, and turnip rape. For example, ABE-P1 (ABE7.10) and ABE-P2 (ABEsa) were used to achieve six mutations in rice, and simultaneous editing of C-T and A-G was achieved with modified BEs.48,56,214 Hua et al. shortened the TadAwt-TadA7.10 chimera to TadA7.10 monomer and fused it with nCas9 to construct an ABE-P1S base editor. Through testing in rice callus tissue, it was found that the editing efficiency of the TadA7.10 monomer was higher than that of the TadAwt-TadA7.10 chimera.215 Moreover, the PABE-7 version of the BE was used to edit multiple genes of rice and wheat, resulting in the corresponding wheat mutants and herbicide-resistant rice with ACC gene mutations.216 In addition, the ABE was used to create an ft mutant of Arabidopsis thaliana for early flowering and achieve variable splicing of rapeseed PDS gene mRNA.217 Zhong et al.218 developed an optimized AYBE base editor (ZmAYBEv3) for both A-to-T and A-to-C base conversions with high efficiency in maize.

Application of ABE in microbial genome editing

ABE can convert the start codon ATG into ACG, thereby disrupting the start of gene translation. In addition, ABE can introduce internal stop codons into target genes, thereby eliminating their expression and playing a role in gene inactivation.213 By terminating the function of genes related to metabolism, industrial microbial production can be optimized, and the development of synthetic biology can be promoted.219 Meanwhile, the application of multiple-base editing is expected to make complex metabolic regulation and genome editing possible.70 Tong et al. designed a CRISPR-aBEST system for Streptomyces base editing, which can convert A to G within a window of approximately 6–7 nt. The CRISPR-a-BEST exhibited a potential off-target effect at a negligible level.220 Zhang et al.221 constructed an ABE suitable for E. coli and S. aureus and used this BE to identify five functional residues of CntBC, the importer of the staphylopine/metal complex in S. aureus. In 2022, an ABE system of dxABE-PS for P. putida was developed by the fusion of xCas9 protein with the TadA∗ domain through a 32 aa XTEN linker. The xCas9 can recognize NG PAM, and the editing efficiency of dxABE-PS in P. putida KT2440 reached 100%, promoting the application of ABE in non-model microorganisms.70 In 2023, several ABE systems for Actinobacillus succinogenes have been developed, among which only nCas9(D10A)-ABE achieved point mutations from A to G with an editing efficiency of 100%.219 Moreover, a multi-site genome editing system based on CRISPR-cBE and CRISPR-aBE has been developed for Halomonas bluephagenesis to simultaneously inactivate up to six copies of the IS1086 loci by introducing a stop codon. As a result, the efficiency of inactivating two genes and three genes could reach 100% and 41.67%, respectively.222

Application of the PE systems

Compared to ABEs and CBEs, PE systems have the advantage of achieving all types of DNA point mutations, small insertions, and small deletions at target sites in genomes without introducing DSBs. The PE systems have been rapidly and widely used in genome editing of animal cell lines, plants, fungi, and bacteria since their development. In addition, these systems have also been used for gene therapy, animal disease modeling, and the creation of important agronomic traits. Application of the PE systems for gene therapy and construction of animal models

The use of PE systems to correct genetic mutations has broad application prospects. About 90% of human pathogenic genetic mutations are single-base mutations or indel of less than a dozen base pairs,5 which are exactly the types of DNA editing that PE systems are good at. In cultured cells, PE systems have been shown to correct genetic mutations of HEXA, HBB E6V, and CDKL5 that cause Tay-Sachs disease, sickle cell disease, and CDKL5 deficiency disorder, respectively.87,97 In addition, PE systems have also been shown to successfully correct mutations associated with Wilson disease, cystic fibrosis, and diacylglycerol O-acyltransferase 1 deficiency.253,254 Moreover, the PE system was used to insert 2 bp to correct the reading frame of DMD for the model of human DMD,255 and twinPE was also used to partially rescue DMD function.109 The PE system has also been applied to precisely delete the intronic splicing silencer within survival motor neuron (SMN) gene to recover SMN expression for the model of human spinal muscular atrophy.256 In addition, PE systems have been widely used to install genetic alleles protective for some diseases (e.g., cardiovascular disease, HIV infection, and prion disease),87,92,93,97 indicating that the therapeutic function of PE systems goes beyond the scope of directly correcting mutations that cause diseases. Unlike most genome editing tools that lack flexibility, PE systems can use pegRNA tandem arrays for several required edits, which is very beneficial for treating polygenic diseases such as coronary artery disease.234,257,258,259 Additionally, the twinPE and PE3 systems can edit DNA of at least 64 bp and 34 bp long, respectively,87,109 thereby multiple variants of a gene within the mutational hotspot can be corrected using these PE systems with single pegRNA or pegRNA pair. Furthermore, disease phenotypes in post-natal mice were rescued by targeting the eye and liver with the PE system, and the Fah transversion mutation within a mouse model was corrected using PE systems through hydrodynamic tail-vein injection and AAV delivery.20,246 Desirée et al. rescued the mouse model of phenylketonuria by correcting the causal Pah F263S mutation with PE adenoviruses.91 Moreover, Kim et al. corrected a genetic eye disease of Leber congenital amaurosis by injecting PE AAVs into mouse retinal pigment epithelium.246 The causal C to T mutation in RPE65 with 6.4% efficiency was reversed by these PE injections, and the visual function of these mice was improved. PE system exhibits greater editing flexibility than base editing, and much higher editing purity than HDR using CRISPR-Cas nucleases, making it highly suitable for creating genetically modified organisms with precise genomic changes. The rabbit embryos and dog embryos have been edited by PE microinjection and nuclear transfer of PE-corrected fibroblasts, respectively.70,247 In addition, microinjecting zygotes of mouse embryos with pegRNAs, sgRNAs, and PE2-encoding mRNA for efficient prime editing with frequencies as high as 47% have been demonstrated.248,249,250,260 The PE3 system introduced substantial indel byproducts in mouse embryos, whereas PE2 (without a nicking sgRNA) showed higher editing purity. Prime editing primarily induces undesired outcomes in mice. PE system has also been used to create non-mammalian animal models. For example, zebrafish models were made by injection of PE2 RNPs into zebrafish embryos to create pathogenic tyr and kras mutations with up to 6.5% efficiencies.251 Interestingly, PE3 did not show higher editing efficiencies than PE2, and both editors introduced similar frequencies of indel byproducts in these zebrafish experiments, suggesting differences between the DNA repair of embryos and that of the cultured cells.252 These studies indicate that PE editing can promote the creation of high-precision transgenic animal models, although in some cases the editing efficiency is not high. It was reported that the strategy of phage-assisted continuous evolution enhances prime-editing-assisted site-specific integrase gene editing (PASSIGE), enabling precise integration of large DNA of >10 kilobases into genome with the ability of recombinases.261 In addition, the CRISPR-Cas9 nickase was fused to both a serine integrase and reverse transcriptase to introduce programmable addition via site-specific targeting elements (PASTE), which can be used to realize target genomic recruitment and integrate desired payloads.262 Recently, the dual-AAV systems (i.e., v1em and v3em PE-AAV) enable therapeutically relevant editing with PE in mouse liver (up to 46%), heart (up to 11%), and brain (up to 42%) after optimization of factors limiting AAV-mediated PE system in vivo.263 Moreover, four circular RNA-mediated prime editor (CPE) systems, niCPE, nuCPE, sniCPE, and snuCPE, have been developed based on the smaller Cas12a protein. As a result, the efficiencies of niCPE and sniCPE reach up to 24.89% and 40.75%, respectively, without the selection of human cells.264

The BEs or PEs enable the flexible and rapid manipulation of genomes in large-scale experiments. Therefore, high-throughput screening for variants of uncertain significance (VUS) using BEs or PEs should be an important development direction.265 For example, the functional effects of 29,060 transition mutations were evaluated using sgRNA libraries of ABEs and CBEs to find 0.06% and 0.5% variants in non-tumorigenic lung cells that caused outgrowing and likely outgrowing phenotypes, respectively.266 Moreover, PEs can be used for high-throughput screening of VUS. For example, saturation prime editing (SPE) was applied to the NPC intracellular cholesterol transporter 1 gene (NPC1) to find that 410/706 assayed missense mutations being classified as harmful.267 In addition, BEs were combined with a PE to analyze broad spectrum of variants in the epithelial growth factor receptor gene (EGFR).268

Application of the PE systems in plant breeding

Due to the challenges posed by nutrient deficiency, salinity, drought, and bacteria to crop production, modifying plant tolerance to growth conditions, herbicides, and diseases can significantly increase crop yield. To achieve these goals, precise genome editing of plants can produce crops with desired characteristics faster and easier than traditional plant breeding techniques.269,270 Although the CRISPR/Cas nucleases and BEs have been widely used for editing plants, the unique advantages of the PE systems have motivated their application in plants.271 However, PE systems can edit the genomes of wheat and rice protoplasts with efficiencies commonly less than 10%.94,223,225 The editing efficiencies of PE systems in rice have been substantially improved by using enhanced pegRNA and PE architectures or a dual pegRNA approach.90,236,272,273,274 Moreover, it is possible to perform multiplex prime editing in rice using tandem arrays of pegRNAs.234 In addition, plants with herbicide-resistant OsACC alleles were identified from rice mutagenesis libraries constructed by the PE system.232 Recently, a PE tool for rice named NEPE base on epegRNA prime with N-PE2 has been developed to show high editing efficiency (9.0%–18.4%) at all test sites of rice, with a less than 0.2% frequency of indels.245 The PE system has been successfully used in genome editing of dicotyledon plants, which are more difficult than that of monocots. For example, a PE system optimized for plant expression has been developed and used to achieve up to 3% gene editing in diploid tomatoes.230 In addition, the PE system was also used for successful editing in the tetraploid potatoes and legumes (e.g., cowpeas, chickpeas, and peanuts), although with low efficiency.237,238

Application of the PE systems in microbial genome editing

Unlike the wide application of PE systems in animals and plants, the application of PE systems in microorganisms is still relatively rare. In 2021, Tong et al. applied the PE system to edit the genome of E. coli to find that the PE system can perform single-base pair substitution deletion with an efficiency of up to 40% under optimal conditions.80 However, the editing efficiency of the PE system was still very low in E. coli, so there is still an urgent need to improve the editing of the PE system through optimizing conditions. Recently, sbcB gene (coding a 3′–5′ DNA exonuclease) was identified as a key factor impeding prime editing in E. coli through genetic screening of 129 transposon mutants. Deletion of sbcB and two other genes of 3′–5′ DNA exonucleases of exoX and xseA resulted in to 100-fold decrease in the prime editing efficiency.275 It is worth noting that prime editing efficiency varies substantially across different editing types and editing loci.276 Future efforts are required to systematically profile the editing efficiencies in a high-throughput manner in bacteria to enable the effective design of optimal pegRNAs as that in human cells.277,278

Summary and outlook

To sum up, the developed single-base editors (e.g., CBEs, ABEs, and GBEs) can be used to efficiently realize transitions and transversions between bases. On this basis, bifunctional base editor DBEs were constructed, which can induce both C to T and A to G conversions. The establishment of PEs can realize the conversion between arbitrary bases, which is not affected by the target position, editing window, types of base conversions, and some other factors that are important for single-base editors. It opens up the possibility of developing personalized medicine approaches to address some difficult-to-treat genetic diseases.279,280

Although BEs have had many successful applications in gene editing of animals, plants, and microorganisms, further modifications to these BEs are still necessary. First, it is necessary to reduce the off-target effects of the BEs. Compared with classical gene editing methods based on Cas proteins and template DNAs, the gene editing windows of BEs are narrower; therefore, optimizing gene editing windows is a hot topic in the research field of BEs. Moreover, a BE might be split into two smaller protein components, each encapsulated in a vector such as AAV and delivered into the cell and then assembled into a complete BE inside the cell.86,281 Some editors have low editing efficiency, which limits their widespread application. The editing efficiency of different editors at the same site on the genome often varies greatly, which highlights the necessity of choosing BEs reasonably and also indicates the importance of improving base editing efficiency. In addition, in the field of metabolic engineering, multiple base editing based on base editors is expected to make complex metabolic regulation and genome editing possible.

Many strategies can be used to improve BEs, for example, replacing existing protein components, including Cas proteins and deaminases, to construct new BEs. With the continuous exploration and characterization of Cas proteins and deaminases, the possibility of constructing more efficient BEs has been provided. In addition, the fusion of different protein components282,283,284 is expected to affect the editing efficiency and editing window of BEs. With the continuous design and optimization of BEs, the BEs should become more efficient and precise genome editing tools in fields such as medicine, life sciences, agriculture, and food.
