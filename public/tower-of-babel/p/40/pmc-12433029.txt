A case report of Graves’ disease combined with pseudohypoaldosteronism type IID in a child
Authors: Junlan Yao, Xiangji Li, Wei Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGraves’ disease and pseudohypoaldosteronism type IID (PHA IID) are rare pediatric endocrine diseases with different etiologies and pathological features. Graves’ disease is caused by autoimmune thyroid stimulation, while PHA IID is an inherited renal tubular disorder characterized by hyperkalemia and hypertension due to mutations in the KLHL3 gene. A rare pediatric case of concurrent Graves’ disease and PHA IID with a KLHL3 gene mutation is reported. The clinical manifestations, diagnostic process, and treatment plans for the two conditions are discussed to provide a reference for the management of similar cases.Case DescriptionOn the initial hospitalization, the patient presented with sudden onset of altered consciousness, tachycardia, and electrolyte disturbances, including hyperkalemia and metabolic acidosis. Following thyroid function tests and thyroid ultrasonography, a diagnosis of Graves’ disease was made. Antithyroid treatment with methimazole and propranolol was administered, leading to an improvement in the blood gas and biochemical parameters. Although thyroid function was controlled, the patient’s hyperkalemia, hyperchloremia, metabolic acidosis, and hypertension remained refractory to treatment. Further genetic testing revealed a KLHL3 gene mutation, confirming the diagnosis of PHA IID. After treatment with hydrochlorothiazide (10 mg), the patient’s electrolyte imbalances and blood pressure normalized.ConclusionsThe simultaneous occurrence of Graves’ disease and PHA IID is rare in children. Clinicians should be alert to the possibility of such comorbidities in clinical practice. For patients with persistent hyperkalemia, particularly those with concomitant metabolic acidosis and hyperchloremia, early genetic testing can enhance diagnostic efficiency and optimize treatment strategies.Keywords: Pseudohypoaldosteronism (PHA), Graves’ disease, hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, hypertension, KLHL3 gene, case report

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study reports a case of a child with both Graves’ disease and pseudohypoaldosteronism type IID (PHA IID). Despite controlled thyroid function, persistent hyperkalemia and metabolic acidosis suggested another underlying cause. PHA IID was ultimately diagnosed by genetic testing, and the patient responded well to hydrochlorothiazide treatment.

What is known, and what is new?

• Graves’ disease and PHA IID are two independent childhood endocrine diseases. The former mainly presents with hyperthyroidism, while the latter leads to hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, and hypertension due to renal tubular dysfunction.

• This case report highlights the possibility of the comorbidity of these two rare diseases in children, suggesting that attention should be paid to potential comorbidities when managing Graves’ disease with complex electrolyte disturbances. This study also highlights the important value of genetic testing in the diagnosis of persistent hyperkalemia in children.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Clinicians managing children with Graves’ disease who continue to exhibit hyperkalemia after antithyroid treatment should be alert to the potential hereditary renal tubular disease. Genetic testing should be performed as early as possible in the presence of hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, and hyperchloremia to improve diagnostic efficiency and guide individualized treatment.

Introduction

Graves’ disease is an autoimmune disorder and is the most common cause of hyperthyroidism in both children and adults (1). The hallmark feature of this disease is the immune system mistakenly attacking the thyroid gland, resulting in the excessive secretion of thyroid hormones [thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3)] and the suppression of thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) secretion from the pituitary gland. This results in a series of systemic symptoms, including thyroid enlargement, exophthalmos, weight loss, palpitations, irritability, and anxiety (2). Although the diagnosis and management of Graves’ disease are well established in adults, the disease often presents in a more subtle and complex manner in pediatric patients, who usually have milder symptoms, leading to challenges in both diagnosis and treatment (3). The management of pediatric Graves’ disease requires consideration of its potential effects on development, alongside precise medication adjustments to minimize side effects (4-6).

Pseudohypoaldosteronism type II (PHA II), also known as familial hyperkalemia hypertension or Gordon syndrome, is a rare genetic disorder. This disease is primarily associated with mutations in the WNK1, WNK4, KLHL3, and CUL3 genes (7-9). PHA II typically has an insidious onset, and most patients present with incidental findings of hyperkalemia or hypertension, often without obvious clinical symptoms. The renal function of such patients is typically normal, although some may have associated developmental delays, enamel hypoplasia, migraines, episodic vertigo, and muscle weakness. Genetic diagnosis is a crucial tool in diagnosing the disease (10).

Few studies have explored the correlation between Graves’ disease and complex electrolyte disturbances, particularly in pediatric cases. This article presents the first known case of a 4-year-old girl with concurrent Graves’ disease and PHA IID, aiming to analyze the complexity of electrolyte imbalances in pediatric Graves’ disease, and explore the potential link with PHA II through a detailed case analysis and differential diagnostic process. The findings may provide valuable insights for the diagnosis and management of such rare cases. Additionally, the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in diagnosing and treating such cases is highlighted, providing a useful reference for the management of similar cases in the future. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2025-500/rc).

Case presentation

All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yichang Central People’s Hospital (No. 2025-229-01). Written informed consent was obtained from the patient’s legal guardians for publication of this case report and accompanying images. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

The patient was a 4-year-old female child, from a third pregnancy, second delivery, born at 34 weeks via cesarean section, with a birth weight of 1.75 kg. The mother had a history of premature rupture of membranes during pregnancy but denied any history of asphyxia or resuscitation at birth. The child’s intellectual development was normal, with no significant past medical history or family history of genetic diseases, autoimmune diseases, or thyroid diseases.

Initial admission

The patient was hospitalized at Yichang Central People’s Hospital on April 6, 2022 with a chief complaint of a “sudden onset of altered consciousness and a rapid heart rate for 1 hour”. On admission, blood gas analysis and biochemical tests were performed (Table 1), revealing metabolic acidosis, hypoglycemia, hyperkalemia, and hyperchloremia. The physical examination revealed exophthalmos, tachycardia (pulse rate: 163 beats/min), elevated blood pressure (124/70 mmHg), and grade II thyroid enlargement. Further thyroid function tests showed elevated free triiodothyronine (FT3), free thyroxine (FT4), thyroglobulin antibodies (TGAbs), thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor antibodies (TRAbs), and thyroid peroxidase antibodies (TPOAbs), as well as lower than normal TSH levels (Table 1). Thyroid ultrasonography indicated diffuse thyroid changes (Figure 1).

The patient was diagnosed with Graves’ disease. At the same time, other potential causes of coma were ruled out, including normal adrenal function, excluding adrenal glucocorticoid dysfunction, and normal pancreatic function, excluding insulin-induced hypoglycemia. Brain and pituitary magnetic resonance imaging showed no abnormalities, ruling out pituitary dysfunction or brain lesions as causes of hypoglycemia. Moreover, considering the patient’s normal height, growth hormone deficiency was not supported, and no signs of neurological or cardiovascular disease were observed. Genetic testing was recommended to further clarify the etiology, but due to cost issues, the parents did not consent to the continuation of testing.

Initial hospital admission treatment process

On admission, intravenous glucose supplementation and sodium bicarbonate were administered to correct acidosis. The patient’s consciousness gradually improved, and her subsequent mental responses were normal. To manage her hyperthyroidism, the patient was treated with oral methimazole and propranolol to control her thyroid hormone levels and reduce her heart rate. During hospitalization, acidosis and hyperkalemia were significantly alleviated. The hyperkalemia was suspected to be related to the hemolysis of the blood specimen, and therefore no further follow-up was performed. The patient was discharged on April 14, 2022 with medication (methimazole tablets: 4 mg per dose, twice daily; propranolol hydrochloride tablets: 16 mg per dose, twice daily).

Second hospital admission treatment process

One month after commencing oral medication antithyroid medication, the patient returned for a follow-up visit on May 8, 2022, showing abnormal thyroid function (elevated FT3 and low TSH), and persistent hyperkalemia and hyperchloremia (Table 1). The patient was re-admitted for treatment under the diagnosis of “hyperkalemia of unknown etiology, hyperthyroidism”. Upon admission, her physical examination results were as follows: pulse rate: 90 beats/min; blood pressure: 110/70 mmHg; consciousness: clear; mental response: normal; exophthalmos: mild; and thyroid enlargement: grade II.

Given the patient’s ongoing hyperkalemia, after a multidisciplinary team discussion, it was decided that her symptoms could be related to the side effects of propranolol. As a result, the treatment plan was adjusted as follows: the methimazole dose was maintained (4 mg, twice daily); propranolol hydrochloride was discontinued. Intravenous calcium gluconate, glucose with insulin infusion, alkalinization of urine, and furosemide were used as a potassium-lowering treatment.

Renin-aldosterone tests were performed in both the upright and supine positions, and the results were as follows: upright: renin activity, 17.81 µIU/mL; aldosterone, 279.00 pg/mL; aldosterone/renin ratio, 15.67; and supine: renin activity, 10.79 µIU/mL; aldosterone, 121.00 pg/mL; aldosterone/renin ratio, 11.21.

Twelve hours after symptomatic treatment, the patient’s serum potassium levels returned to normal. Two days later (May 11, 2022), after discontinuing treatment, her serum potassium levels rose again to 7.1 mmol/L, and a blood gas analysis revealed metabolic acidosis (Table 1), excluding the possibility that hyperkalemia was caused by propranolol.

The patient’s blood pressure was monitored every 8 hours during hospitalization, with her systolic pressure fluctuating between 91–112 mmHg and her diastolic pressure fluctuating between 62–75 mmHg. Her blood glucose levels were normal on multiple occasions. While the patient’s overall condition was stable, the cause of electrolyte disturbances, metabolic acidosis, and mild hypertension remained unclear. Genetic testing was recommended to further investigate the cause, but the parents once again declined due to financial reasons, and the patient was discharged. Upon discharge, it was advised that she continue oral methimazole (4 mg per dose, twice daily).

Outpatient follow-up

After discharge, the child sought treatment at another hospital, where the exact cause was not identified. It was recommended that sodium bicarbonate tablets be added to the previous treatment regimen. The child was intermittently followed up at this hospital’s outpatient clinic, where no further episodes of hypoglycemia or hypoglycemia-related symptoms were observed. Under continued treatment with methimazole, her thyroid function was generally maintained within normal levels, and no specific discomfort was reported. However, her metabolic acidosis, hyperkalemia, and hyperchloremia showed no significant improvement. Home blood pressure monitoring indicated a systolic pressure of approximately 110 mmHg and a diastolic pressure of approximately 70 mmHg.

After repeated efforts, the child underwent family exome sequencing (offered as a free service) in November 20, 2022. The genetic test revealed a heterozygous variant in the KLHL3 gene (Figure 2), which was identified as a de novo mutation. This result supported a diagnosis of PHA IID. The child returned for follow-up on March 9, 2023, and started treatment with hydrochlorothiazide (10 mg/day). After 1 week of treatment, follow-up tests showed the normalization of blood gas analysis and serum electrolytes (Table 2), with a significant decrease in blood pressure to 95 mmHg systolic and 60 mmHg diastolic. The dosage of hydrochlorothiazide was then reduced to 5 mg/day, and after 2 weeks, a re-evaluation revealed an increase in blood potassium to 6.1 mmol/L, prompting a return to the original dose of 10 mg/day.

Discussion

The coexistence of hyperthyroidism and electrolyte disturbances is rare, and the differential diagnosis is complex, requiring a comprehensive analysis of results from multidisciplinary collaboration.

On the initial hospitalization, the patient presented with exophthalmos, thyroid enlargement, and significant thyroid dysfunction. The diagnosis of Graves’ disease was confirmed by thyroid ultrasonography and elevated TRAb levels. At the same time, abnormalities related to the nervous system, adrenal function, and pancreatic function were initially ruled out as causes of coma and hypoglycemia. Although hyperkalemia was alleviated after the first treatment, persistent hyperkalemia during the second hospitalization, in conjunction with metabolic acidosis, hyperchloremia, and hypertension, suggested the need for further exploration of other potential underlying causes. The possibility of propranolol-induced side effects was considered in the initial treatment plan. Propranolol, a non-selective β-blocker, may induce hyperkalemia by inhibiting β-receptors, which in turn reduces the activity of Na+-K+-ATPase (sodium-potassium adenosine triphosphatase) on cell membranes, thereby decreasing the transport of potassium ions from extracellular to intracellular spaces, resulting in an increase in extracellular potassium (11,12). However, as hyperkalemia did not improve after the discontinuation of the drug, drug-induced hyperkalemia was further excluded.

Through targeted analysis, it was determined that the organs responsible for regulating the electrolytes, fluid and acid-base balance, and blood pressure were the kidneys or adrenal glands. Adrenal cortical insufficiency, particularly a decrease in aldosterone secretion, can lead to hyperkalemia and may also induce metabolic acidosis and hypoglycemia under stress conditions (13,14). However, the patient did not present with hyponatremia or hypotension, and both her cortisol and adrenocorticotropic hormone levels were normal, as were her aldosterone levels; thus, adrenal gland pathology was ruled out. The patient’s renal function and renal ultrasonography were both normal, with no signs of anemia or growth retardation, which excluded acute or chronic renal failure. Although renovascular abnormalities can lead to secondary hypertension, the patient’s renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system was normal, and no renal vascular abnormalities resulted in renal dysfunction; thus, this diagnosis was also excluded (15,16). Given the normal renin-aldosterone levels, conditions such as reninoma, pheochromocytoma, or aldosteronism were also not supported.

After excluding severe renal disease, the focus shifted to the renal tubules, which are crucial in regulating acid-base balance and water-electrolyte homeostasis. A common cause of chronic metabolic acidosis with electrolyte disturbances in children is renal tubular acidosis (RTA), with type IV RTA (hyperkalemic acidosis) matching the patient’s electrolyte profile (17). Treatment for type IV RTA with physiological doses of aldosterone-like hormones, such as fludrocortisone, can restore hyperkalemia and acidosis (18). If correction does not occur with aldosterone replacement therapy, PHA should be considered. The patient had not received aldosterone therapy, and previous oral treatment with sodium bicarbonate at another institution was ineffective, making it difficult to differentiate between type IV RTA and PHA. The literature suggests that PHA II and type IV RTA share many similarities in clinical presentation and molecular mechanisms, with PHA II potentially being a causative factor for type IV RTA; however, this remains a topic of debate (19). In either case, the etiology of tubular diseases is complex and may involve both congenital and acquired factors. Given the patient’s well-tolerated chronic electrolyte disturbances, metabolic acidosis, and hypertension without signs of systemic illness, a congenital hereditary tubular dysfunction was initially considered, and genetic testing was thus deemed crucial.

The patient was ultimately diagnosed with PHA IID (KLHL3 gene mutation) through genetic testing. The pathogenesis of PHA II primarily involves weakened regulation of the sodium chloride (NaCl) cotransporter (NCC) in the distal convoluted tubule epithelial cells, leading to increased reabsorption of sodium ions (Na+) and chloride ions (Cl−), which results in water and sodium retention and expanded blood volume (20). Concurrently, the secretion of renin and aldosterone is suppressed, and potassium excretion by the kidneys is reduced, leading to hyperkalemia and acidosis.

WNK kinases are key enzymes that regulate NCC activity. Mutations in the WNK1 and WNK4 genes can increase the activity of these kinases, thereby activating the SPAK signaling pathway, which weakens the inhibitory regulation of NCC and triggers the disease (21,22). The proteins encoded by the CUL3 and KLHL3 genes, cullin3 and Kelch-like protein 3, form a cullin3 ligase complex that interacts with WNK4 kinase, participating in the degradation of WNK4 and indirectly regulating NCC activity (23). Mutations in the CUL3 and KLHL3 genes can obstruct the formation of the cullin3 ligase complex or cause structural abnormalities, preventing interaction with WNK4, leading to increased NCC activity and, consequently, the development of the disease (24,25).

The treatment for PHA IID mainly relies on thiazide diuretics, which inhibit sodium reabsorption in the distal convoluted tubule and promote the excretion of potassium and chloride (26,27). After diagnosis, the patient was treated with hydrochlorothiazide, and 1 week later, her blood potassium and blood gas analysis results had normalized, with a significant reduction in blood pressure to near-normal levels. Subsequent adjustments in dosage were attempted, but elevated blood potassium levels indicated the need for a sustained effective dose (10 mg/day). Additionally, the patient’s hyperthyroidism was well controlled with methimazole, and no further related symptoms occurred. This suggests that the treatments for both conditions can be administered independently, but the ongoing monitoring of electrolytes, blood pressure, and thyroid function is necessary during follow-up to prevent recurrence.

The concurrent occurrence of Graves’ disease and PHA IID is extremely rare in pediatric cases. In this case, hyperthyroidism might have indirectly influenced the hyperkalemia, but no direct relationship between the mechanisms of the two diseases was identified. Notably, the treatment for hyperthyroidism might have masked part of the electrolyte disturbance, delaying the diagnosis of PHA IID. Following methimazole treatment, the patient’s thyroid function normalized, but her hyperkalemia and metabolic acidosis did not resolve, further supporting the notion that these are distinct coexisting conditions.

Conclusions

This case report highlights the rare comorbidity of Graves’ disease and PHA IID, offering valuable insights for the diagnosis of hyperkalemia in children. Through multidisciplinary collaboration and genetic testing, an accurate diagnosis was established. This case underscores the importance of considering genetic diseases when diagnosing complex conditions. For patients with persistent hyperkalemia, especially those with concomitant metabolic acidosis and hyperchloremia, early genetic testing can enhance diagnostic efficiency and optimize treatment strategies.
