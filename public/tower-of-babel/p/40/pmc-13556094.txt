Predictive factors, timing, and management of recurrent vitreous hemorrhage after pars plana vitrectomy in advanced proliferative diabetic retinopathy
Authors: Emine Çiloğlu, Göksu Hande Naz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the incidence, timing, predictive factors, pathophysiological mechanisms, and management outcomes of recurrent vitreous hemorrhage (RVH) following pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) in patients with advanced proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), and to contextualize these findings within the current literature.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 65 patients undergoing 25-gauge PPV for vitreous hemorrhage secondary to PDR with a mean follow-up of 15 ± 3 months. All patients received preoperative anti-VEGF injection 5 days prior to surgery. Demographic, systemic, ocular, and intraoperative variables were analyzed. Recurrent vitreous hemorrhage was classified as early (< 4 weeks) or late (> 4 weeks). Comparative univariate analyses were performed to identify factors associated with RVH.ResultsRecurrent vitreous hemorrhage developed in 13 of 65 patients (20.0%). Early RVH occurred in 11 patients (16.9%), whereas late RVH occurred in 2 patients (3.1%). Severe intraoperative bleeding (38.5% vs. 0%, p < 0.001), anticoagulant use (53.8% vs. 25.0%, p = 0.04), and dialysis (38.5% vs. 9.6%, p = 0.01) were significantly associated with RVH. Silicone oil tamponade was less frequently used in patients who developed RVH (23.1% vs. 65.4%, p = 0.01). Tractional retinal detachment was not significantly associated with RVH (p = 0.78).ConclusionRecurrent vitreous hemorrhage remains a clinically significant complication following PPV in advanced PDR. Severe intraoperative bleeding, anticoagulant use, and dialysis were significantly associated with RVH development, whereas silicone oil tamponade was associated with a lower frequency of postoperative hemorrhage. These findings may contribute to risk stratification and postoperative monitoring in patients with advanced PDR.

Introduction

Proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) represents the most advanced stage of diabetic retinal disease and remains one of the leading causes of visual impairment worldwide [1–3]. The global prevalence of diabetes mellitus has reached epidemic proportions, with current estimates exceeding 500 million individuals, and projections suggest a continued rise in prevalence over the coming decades [4].

Vitreous hemorrhage (VH) is a common and vision-threatening complication of PDR, resulting from rupture of fragile neovascular complexes that develop in response to retinal ischemia [5]. These neovascular structures lack normal vascular integrity, rendering them highly susceptible to bleeding even with minimal vitreoretinal traction. The natural history of VH varies considerably. While spontaneous clearance may occur in some cases, persistent or recurrent hemorrhage often leads to prolonged visual impairment, necessitating surgical intervention [6]. Additionally, VH may obscure fundus visualization, delaying timely treatment of underlying retinal pathology and increasing the risk of tractional complications.

Pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) has become the standard treatment for non-clearing VH, allowing removal of hemorrhagic vitreous, release of tractional membranes, and application of panretinal photocoagulation [7]. Advances in surgical techniques, including small-gauge vitrectomy systems, improved fluidics, and wide-angle visualization, have significantly enhanced surgical safety and outcomes.

Despite advances in surgical techniques, recurrent vitreous hemorrhage (RVH) remains a major postoperative complication with reported rates of RVH ranging from 10% to 40%, depending on patient characteristics, surgical techniques, and perioperative management strategies [8–10]. Recurrent vitreous hemorrhage can delay visual rehabilitation, increase patient anxiety, and in some cases necessitate repeat surgical intervention.

The pathophysiology of RVH is multifactorial and may be broadly categorized into early and late mechanisms. Early RVH, commonly defined as vitreous hemorrhage occurring within 4 weeks after surgery, is typically associated with residual intraoperative bleeding, incomplete hemostasis, or persistent neovascularization [11]. In contrast, late RVH, defined as hemorrhage occurring more than 4 weeks postoperatively, is often attributed to fibrovascular reproliferation driven by ongoing ischemia and VEGF-mediated angiogenesis [12, 13].

The introduction of anti-VEGF therapy has significantly altered the management of PDR. Preoperative anti-VEGF injections have been shown to reduce intraoperative bleeding, facilitate surgical dissection, and improve surgical outcomes [14–16]. However, RVH continues to occur, particularly in patients with advanced disease, suggesting that factors beyond VEGF-mediated angiogenesis contribute to its development.

The role of tamponade agents in preventing postoperative hemorrhage remains controversial, with some studies suggesting a mechanical stabilizing effect of silicone oil, while others report no significant benefit [10, 17–18].

Previous studies investigating RVH have primarily focused on ocular and surgical parameters, such as the extent of fibrovascular proliferation, completeness of photocoagulation, and surgical technique [8, 9]. However, the role of systemic factors, including anticoagulant use and renal failure requiring dialysis, has received relatively limited attention.

In this context, the present study aims to provide a comprehensive evaluation of RVH in a homogeneous cohort of patients with advanced PDR undergoing standardized surgical and pharmacologic management. Particular emphasis is placed on identifying factors associated with RVH, analyzing its clinical course, and comparing our findings with existing literature.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This retrospective study was conducted at a private eye clinic and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Adana City Training and Research Hospital (Approval No:1280) prior to data collection. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study population

A total of 65 consecutive patients who underwent PPV for vitreous hemorrhage secondary to PDR between May 2023 and February 2025 were included in the study.

The inclusion criteria were; presence of vitreous hemorrhage secondary to PDR, undergoing primary PPV, minimum follow-up duration of 12 months. Exclusion criteria were; vitreous hemorrhage due to other etiologies (e.g. retinal vein occlusion, trauma, uveitis), previous vitreoretinal surgery in the study eye, incomplete medical records, follow-up time less than 12 months.

Systemic and clinical data collection

Demographic and systemic data were collected from medical records, including age, sex, duration of diabetes mellitus, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels, insulin use, presence of hypertension, cardiovascular disease, anticoagulant therapy and dialysis status.

Ophthalmic evaluation

All patients underwent a complete ophthalmic assessment including measurements of best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) (expressed as Snellen equivalent and converted to logMAR for analysis), Slit-lamp biomicroscopy for anterior and posterior segments (when possible). B-scan ultrasonography was performed in cases with dense vitreous hemorrhage that was preventing visualization of the fundus.

The presence of tractional retinal detachment (TRD) was documented in all patients. Panretinal photocoagulation (PRP) status was recorded as complete, incomplete, or absent. At baseline, none of the patients had received complete PRP. Incomplete PRP was present in 48 patients (73.8%), whereas 17 patients (26.2%) had not undergone any PRP treatment before surgery. Therefore, all eyes included in the study had either incomplete or absent PRP at the time of vitrectomy.

Preoperative management

All patients received an intravitreal anti-VEGF injection (bevacizumab 1.25 mg/0.05 ml) 5 days prior to surgery as part of a standardized protocol aimed at reducing intraoperative bleeding and facilitating surgical dissection.

Surgical technique

All surgeries were performed using a 25-gauge pars plana vitrectomy system by a single experienced vitreoretinal surgeon (EC). The surgical procedure included: Core vitrectomy followed by complete vitreous removal, meticulous peripheral vitreous shaving under scleral indentation, dissection and removal of fibrovascular membranes, endodiathermy to control hemorrhage, endolaser photocoagulation applied to ischemic retinal areas in all cases. No adjunctive intraoperative anti-VEGF injections were administered.

Tamponade

At the end of surgery, tamponade agents were selected based on intraoperative findings and surgeon preference. Air tamponade was used in 7 patients (10.8%), gas tamponade (C3F8) was used in 21 patients (32.3%), and silicone oil was used in 37 patients (56.9%).

Postoperative follow-up

All patients were followed for a minimum of 12 months postoperatively. Follow-up visits were scheduled at: postoperative day 1, week 1, month 1 and monthly thereafter. At each visit, patients underwent: visual acuity assessment, slit-lamp examination, fundus evaluation, OCT measurement.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome was occurrence of RVH, and the secondary outcomes were timing of RVH (early RVH was defined as occurring within 4 weeks, late RVH was defined as occurring after 4 weeks postoperatively), response to treatment and the need for repeat surgery.

Definition and management of RVH

Recurrent vitreous hemorrhage was defined as the presence of clinically significant postoperative vitreous hemorrhage impairing fundus visualization. Management followed a stepwise protocol: 1.Observation for spontaneous resolution, 2. Intravitreal anti-VEGF injection at postoperative week 1 in persistent cases, 3. Repeat PPV in cases with non-resolving hemorrhage at 1 month.

Intraoperative parameters

The following intraoperative variables were recorded: presence and severity of intraoperative bleeding (graded as mild, moderate, or severe), presence of TRD, surgical duration, occurrence of iatrogenic retinal tears, panretinal photocoagulation status (complete, incomplete, absent). Severe intraoperative bleeding was defined as persistent hemorrhage that interfered with surgical visualization, required repeated endodiathermy, prolonged the surgical procedure, or necessitated additional hemostatic interventions, such as temporary elevation of infusion pressure and/or the use of perfluorocarbon liquids (PFCL).

Statistical analysis

We performed a statistical analysis using the SPSS 20.0 statistics software package (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Categorical variables were expressed as number and percentage. Comparisons between RVH and non-RVH groups were performed using the independent samples t-test for continuous variables and Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Variables associated with RVH were identified through univariate analyses. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 65 patients (29 male, 36 female) were included in the study, all of whom completed 12 months of follow-up. The demographic and systemic characteristics of the study population reflected advanced diabetic disease with a long duration of diabetes and a high prevalence of systemic comorbidities. The mean age was 57 ± 4.3 years, and the mean HbA1c was 7.5 ± 0.6%. The mean duration of diabetes was 14 years (Table 1).

The mean preoperative visual acuity was approximately 20/200 (Snellen equivalent; ~1.0 logMAR), indicating substantial visual impairment at baseline.

Preoperative PRP status reflected advanced and inadequately treated proliferative diabetic retinopathy. No patient had completed PRP before surgery, while 48 patients (73.8%) had received incomplete PRP and 17 patients (26.2%) had not received PRP at all. Consequently, all eyes underwent surgery in the presence of incomplete or absent retinal photocoagulation.

Recurrent vitreous hemorrhage developed in 13 patients (20.0%). The majority of cases, 11 of 13 (84.6%) occurred within the first 4 weeks after surgery. The clinical course of RVH varied. Among patients with early RVH, 2 patients (18.2%) showed spontaneous resolution. Following intravitreal anti-VEGF injection, hemorrhage resolved in 5 patients (45.5%). However, 4 patients (36.4%) required repeat PPV because of persistent or non-resolving hemorrhage.

Severe intraoperative bleeding was significantly more frequent in the RVH group (38.5% vs. 0%, p < 0.001). All patients who experienced severe intraoperative bleeding subsequently developed RVH. This finding suggests a strong association between intraoperative bleeding and postoperative hemorrhage (Table 2).

Tractional retinal detachment was present in 64.6% of patients, reflecting the advanced stage of proliferative diabetic retinopathy in this cohort. Interestingly, TRD was not associated with an increased risk of RVH, despite being traditionally considered a marker of disease severity. No significant difference was observed for TRD (p = 0.78).

Systemic factors were also associated with postoperative hemorrhage. Anticoagulant use was significantly more common in patients who developed RVH compared with those who did not (53.8% vs. 25.0%, p = 0.04). Similarly, dialysis treatment was more frequent in the RVH group (38.5% vs. 9.6%, p = 0.01) (Table 2).

Silicone tamponade was used less frequently in patients who developed RVH (23.1% vs. 65.4%, p = 0.01).

Analysis of the temporal distribution of RVH demonstrated a predominance of early postoperative hemorrhage. This observation suggests that perioperative and early postoperative factors may contribute substantially to RVH development in this patient population. Late RVH was relatively uncommon during the follow-up period.

The response to treatment also provided important clinical insights. The majority of early RVH cases responded favorably to intravitreal anti-VEGF injection administered during the early postoperative period. This highlights the continued relevance of VEGF-mediated mechanisms even after surgical intervention. However, a subset of patients required repeat PPV, indicating that pharmacologic treatment alone may not be sufficient in all cases.

Interestingly, all patients with severe intraoperative bleeding developed RVH. This observation underscores the importance of meticulous intraoperative hemostasis and may indicate that intraoperative bleeding serves as a surrogate marker for underlying disease severity and vascular instability.

Univariate analyses demonstrated significant associations between RVH development and severe intraoperative bleeding, anticoagulant use, dialysis, and silicone oil tamponade status. In contrast, tractional retinal detachment was not significantly associated with postoperative RVH.

The observed associations between dialysis, anticoagulant use, and RVH suggest that systemic factors may contribute to the risk of postoperative hemorrhage in addition to ocular disease severity. Patients receiving dialysis or anticoagulant therapy may represent a subgroup requiring closer postoperative monitoring.

Discussion

The findings of this study provide important insights into the mechanisms underlying RVH in advanced PDR.

Previous studies have consistently reported that RVH remains a significant postoperative complication despite advances in vitreoretinal surgery. The incidence of RVH in our study (20%) is comparable to that reported in previous studies. Gupta et al. reported an RVH incidence of approximately 18–25%, which is comparable to our findings [8, 9]. Similarly, Steel et al. emphasized that postoperative hemorrhage continues to be a major challenge in the management of advanced PDR [10].

The relatively favorable preoperative visual acuity in our cohort may reflect the variable severity of vitreous hemorrhage. Although all patients had vitreous hemorrhage requiring surgery, some retained partial fundus visualization and residual visual function, resulting in better baseline visual acuity than might be expected in eyes with dense hemorrhage.

Our study demonstrated a strong association between intraoperative bleeding and RVH. While earlier reports have described intraoperative bleeding as a marker of surgical complexity, our results suggest that it may be associated with an increased likelihood of postoperative hemorrhage. This distinction is clinically important, as it highlights the need for proactive intraoperative strategies aimed at minimizing bleeding. Steel et al. [10] reported that increased surgical complexity is associated with higher complication rates, including postoperative hemorrhage. Our findings extend this observation by demonstrating a significant association between intraoperative bleeding severity and RVH.

From a pathophysiological perspective, intraoperative bleeding may reflect both mechanical and biological factors. Mechanically, it may indicate incomplete removal of fibrovascular tissue or disruption of fragile neovascular complexes. Biologically, it may reflect an underlying proangiogenic and proinflammatory state that persists despite surgical intervention [19, 20]. In the present study, severe intraoperative bleeding was identified according to predefined surgical criteria, including persistent hemorrhage interfering with surgical visualization, repeated endodiathermy, prolonged surgical time, and the need for additional hemostatic maneuvers such as temporary elevation of infusion pressure and/or the use of PFCL. Patients meeting these criteria demonstrated a higher frequency of postoperative hemorrhage. These findings suggest that severe intraoperative bleeding may serve not only as a marker of surgical complexity but also as an indicator of active fibrovascular disease and ongoing vascular instability associated with recurrent vitreous hemorrhage.

Systemic factors also emerged as important determinants of RVH. Anticoagulant use was significantly associated with RVH, suggesting that altered coagulation status may contribute to postoperative bleeding in line with previous studies demonstrating an increased risk of hemorrhagic complications in patients receiving systemic anticoagulation during vitreoretinal surgery [21–23]. This finding is clinically relevant, as anticoagulant therapy is increasingly common in patients with diabetes due to cardiovascular comorbidities [23]. This is consistent with the known effects of anticoagulant therapy on hemostasis and suggests that perioperative management of anticoagulation may be an important consideration in these patients.

Similarly, dialysis was significantly associated with RVH development. Patients undergoing dialysis often exhibit platelet dysfunction, endothelial abnormalities, chronic inflammation and vascular fragility, which may contribute to increased bleeding risk [23, 24]. This observation has not been extensively reported in previous studies and represents a contribution of our study.

In contrast to some previous studies, we did not find a significant association between TRD and RVH. This finding differs from some previous studies that have reported an association between TRD and postoperative complications [12, 25]. This discrepancy may be explained by differences in study populations. In our cohort, TRD was highly prevalent, which may have reduced its ability to distinguish between high- and low-risk patients.

The association between silicone oil tamponade and a lower frequency of RVH observed in our study also warrants further discussion. Silicone oil may provide a mechanical barrier that stabilizes the retina and reduces the likelihood of postoperative bleeding [17, 18]. Additionally, it may limit fluid currents within the vitreous cavity, thereby reducing mechanical stress on residual neovascular tissue. Although previous studies have reported mixed results, our findings suggest an association between silicone oil tamponade and a lower frequency of postoperative hemorrhage.

However, the use of silicone oil is not without drawbacks, including the need for subsequent removal and potential complications such as emulsification. Therefore, its use should be carefully considered in the context of individual patient risk profiles.

Another important observation is that preoperative anti-VEGF therapy, while beneficial, does not completely eliminate the risk of RVH. Although all patients received preoperative anti-VEGF therapy, RVH was not completely prevented. Because the present study did not include a control group, the effect of anti-VEGF treatment on RVH risk cannot be directly assessed. This finding is consistent with previous reports indicating that anti-VEGF therapy reduces but does not eliminate bleeding risk [18, 20, 25]. In advanced disease, structural factors such as fibrovascular proliferation may play a more dominant role. This finding underscores the multifactorial nature of postoperative hemorrhage and suggests that anti-VEGF therapy should be viewed as part of a broader management strategy rather than a standalone solution.

In addition to its structural impact, PDR is increasingly recognized as a chronic inflammatory and angiogenic disease characterized by complex interactions between hypoxia, oxidative stress, and inflammatory cytokines. Retinal ischemia leads to upregulation of VEGF, which plays a central role in neovascularization and vascular permeability. However, VEGF is not the only mediator involved. Inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) have also been implicated in the pathogenesis of diabetic retinopathy and may contribute to persistent neovascular activity even after surgical intervention [18, 26, 27].

These molecular mechanisms may partially explain why RVH continues to occur despite advances in surgical techniques and widespread use of anti-VEGF therapy. While anti-VEGF agents effectively suppress angiogenic signaling, they do not fully address the underlying inflammatory milieu or the structural complexity of fibrovascular membranes. As a result, residual vascular activity and microvascular instability may persist postoperatively ([26–27]).

Furthermore, surgical factors such as incomplete removal of peripheral vitreous cortex, residual fibrovascular tissue, and insufficient endolaser photocoagulation may contribute to postoperative bleeding. Even with meticulous surgical technique, complete elimination of all neovascular sources may not always be feasible, particularly in eyes with extensive fibrovascular proliferation.

Another important but often overlooked aspect is the role of systemic disease burden. Patients with advanced PDR frequently have long-standing diabetes and multiple systemic comorbidities, including hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease. These conditions may impair vascular integrity and coagulation balance, potentially increasing the risk of postoperative hemorrhage.

Despite the clinical importance of RVH, there remains a lack of consensus regarding its optimal management and prevention. Most existing studies have focused on surgical techniques or pharmacologic interventions in isolation, rather than adopting a comprehensive approach that considers both ocular and systemic factors.

Therefore, a more integrated understanding of RVH is needed. In this context, our study aims not only to evaluate traditional surgical risk factors but also to explore the contribution of systemic conditions, thereby providing a more holistic perspective on this challenging complication.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective and single-center design may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings. An additional limitation of the present study is the relatively small number of RVH events. Consequently, multivariable modeling was not considered sufficiently robust, and the findings should be interpreted as associations identified through univariate analyses rather than evidence of independent predictors. Additionally, the lack of variability in PRP and anti-VEGF use restricts comparative analysis.

Furthermore, the duration of vitreous hemorrhage before surgery and the number of anti-VEGF injections administered prior to referral were not consistently available in the medical records, limiting assessment of their potential impact on postoperative outcomes.

The absence of detailed imaging biomarkers, such as fluorescein angiography or optical coherence tomography-based parameters, precludes a more comprehensive evaluation of disease activity and its association with recurrent vitreous hemorrhage. Despite these limitations, the use of a standardized surgical protocol and homogeneous patient population strengthens the internal validity of the study.

Future prospective studies incorporating imaging biomarkers are warranted.

From a clinical standpoint, our findings have several important implications. Patients with severe intraoperative bleeding, anticoagulant use, or dialysis may represent a subgroup at increased risk of RVH and may benefit from closer postoperative monitoring. Additionally, intraoperative strategies aimed at minimizing bleeding may have a direct impact on reducing postoperative complications.

The strengths of this study include its standardized surgical protocol and comprehensive evaluation of both ocular and systemic factors. These features enhance the reliability and clinical relevance of the findings.

Conclusion

Recurrent vitreous hemorrhage remains a clinically significant complication following PPV in advanced PDR. Severe intraoperative bleeding and systemic factors, particularly anticoagulant use and dialysis, were significantly associated with RVH development. Silicone oil tamponade was associated with a lower frequency of postoperative hemorrhage in our cohort. These findings have important implications for surgical planning and postoperative management.
