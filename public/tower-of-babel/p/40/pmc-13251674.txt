Effects of mesenchymal stem cell secretome on osteoclast- and osteogenesis-related markers during orthodontic relapse
Authors: Adya Pramusita, Ida B. Narmada, Ervina R. Winoto, Alida Alida, Fitri D.A. Pratiwi, Labieb F. Zaky, Ari Triwardhani, I.G.A.W. Ardani, Wibi Riawan, Desy Novianti, Nurul A.R. Putranti, Ananda F. Putranto, Tengku N.E.B.T.A. Noor, Alexander P. Nugraha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) secretome has emerged as a promising cell-free approach for modulating bone remodeling, but its effects during the orthodontic relapse phase remain poorly understood. This study investigated the effects of the human umbilical cord-derived MSC (hUC-MSC) secretome on alveolar bone remodeling during orthodontic relapse.MethodsForty-one male Wistar rats were randomly assigned to three groups: negative control (C−), positive control (C+), and secretome-treated relapse group (S). S rats were subjected to submucosal injections of the hUC-MSC secretome, whereas the control groups received submucosal injection of phosphate-buffered saline. Orthodontic force was applied for 14 days to move the maxillary first molars mesially. Relapse began on day 14 after the appliance was removed. A retention device was placed only in C− rats. The rats were sacrificed on days 21 and 28, and the expression levels were assessed for cathepsin K (CTSK), tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP), osteoprotegerin, receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and osteocalcin (OCN) via immunohistochemistry.ResultsQuantitative analysis demonstrated that the TRAP and CTSK expression levels were significantly lower in S than C−, but remained comparable to those in C+. In addition, the RANKL/OPG ratio was consistently lowest in S at both time points compared with C− and C+. Furthermore, across both observation periods, the expression levels of ALP and OCN were significantly higher in S compared with C−.ConclusionThe hUC-MSC secretome may promote a favorable alveolar bone remodeling balance at the tension side during orthodontic relapse by suppressing osteoclastic activity and enhancing osteoblastic activity.

Introduction

Rising public interest in cosmetic dentistry has made orthodontic treatment one of the most frequently sought dental procedures. Orthodontic treatment aims to correct malocclusion and achieve dentofacial harmony.1 However, orthodontic relapse refers to the tendency of teeth to return toward their original positions after completing treatment, and it remains a major clinical problem.2, 3, 4 High relapse rates can occur despite accurate diagnosis, thorough treatment planning, and effective retention protocols.5

The underlying cause of orthodontic relapse is multifactorial and not fully elucidated. Alveolar bone remodeling is a key biological factor that contributes to relapse.6 In vivo studies have shown that the tissue and molecular responses following orthodontic appliance removal resemble those observed during orthodontic tooth movement but they occur in the opposite direction. Woven bone forms at the tension side during tooth movement but bone remodeling is not fully complete at the end of active orthodontic treatment.7 The newly formed bone has an immature structure with incomplete mineralization, making it susceptible to external forces and continued osteoclastic resorption.8 At the end of active treatment, osteoclasts are redistributed along the alveolar bone in the direction of relapse, and their number has been reported to correlate positively with relapse activity, gradually decreasing as relapse progresses.6,9,10

Over the past decade, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and MSC-based regenerative strategies have been widely studied to enhance bone regeneration.11 However, direct MSC implantation has several limitations, including potential immune rejection, unpredictable cell differentiation, and unresolved ethical concerns.12 Accumulating evidence suggests that the immunomodulatory and regenerative effects of MSCs are mainly modulated through paracrine mechanisms involving the release of bioactive factors collectively referred to as the MSC secretome.13

Previous studies have shown that the MSC secretome can modulate bone remodeling by influencing the balance between osteoblastic and osteoclastic activity, promoting osteogenic differentiation while suppressing osteoclastogenesis.14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 Despite the established role of alveolar bone remodeling in orthodontic relapse, the effects of the MSC secretome on changes in osteogenic and osteoclast-regulatory markers during the relapse phase remain poorly explored. In particular, studies have not fully characterized the effects of the MSC secretome on alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and osteocalcin (OCN) as indicators of osteoblastic differentiation and bone matrix maturation, receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) and osteoprotegerin (OPG) as critical regulators of osteoclastogenesis, and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) and cathepsin K (CTSK) as markers of osteoclast differentiation and resorptive activity at the alveolar bone tension side.20, 21, 22, 23 Therefore, the present study was designed to investigate the effects of the human umbilical cord-derived MSC (hUC-MSC) secretome on the expression of these markers at the alveolar bone tension side during orthodontic relapse.

Materials and Methods

Sample preparation

The experimental protocol for the animal study was approved by the Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments of the Faculty of Dental Medicine, Universitas Airlangga, Indonesia (approval No. 0236/HRECC.FODM/II/2025). In total, 41 male Wistar rats (Rattus norvegicus), weighing 120–150 g and aged 6–8 weeks were included in the study. The animals were housed at the Research Center for Stem Cell & Regenerative Medicine, Universitas Airlangga, and allowed to acclimate for 7 days prior to the experiments. The rats were kept under a dark/light cycle of 12/12 h at a constant temperature of 21 °C ± 2 °C and relative humidity of 50% ± 10%. Water and standard laboratory pellets were provided ad libitum.24

Establishment of rat orthodontic tooth movement model

The animals were anesthetized intraperitoneally with 50 mg/kg ketamine and 5 mg/kg xylazine before fixation of the orthodontic devices.25 Experimental tooth movement was initiated according to the method described by Alnajar and Al Groosh (2021)26 using a closed-coil spring (diameter, 6 mm; 10 g; TG Orthodontics, China) ligated to the left maxillary first molar with a 0.08-inch stainless steel ligature wire (Morelli, Brazil). The opposite end of the spring was ligated to the maxillary central incisors with a ligature wire. The surfaces of the maxillary incisors were etched with 37% acid etch gel (Any Etch; MD-CLUS, South Korea) and the anterior ligature wire was secured using a composite adhesive (Dx Bond; Single bond, Azdent) and flowable composite resin (Any-Com Flow; MD-CLUS, South Korea), according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The left maxillary first molar was moved mesially using a force of 10 g for 14 days. The spring appliances were removed at the end of the experimental period.

A preliminary study was conducted with three samples per group to estimate variability and effect size. The pilot data indicated a mean difference of 3 and standard deviation of 1.53. Using a significance level of 0.05 and statistical power of 80%, the minimum required sample size was four animals per group.27 To account for potential dropouts, eight animals were initially included in each group. The rats were then randomly assigned to three groups: negative control (C−), positive control (C+), and secretome-treated relapse group (S). Rats under S were treated with submucosal injections of hUC-MSC secretome (Stem Cell and Cancer Research, Semarang, Indonesia) at a concentration of 2.39 mg/mL (volume, 20 μL), administered on days 0, 3, and 6 following appliance removal.26,28 Injections were administered into the buccal attached gingiva adjacent to the interdental space between the maxillary left first and second molars during the orthodontic relapse phase using a microinjection apparatus (OneMed, Indonesia).7 By contrast, rats in the C− and C+ groups only received injections of phosphate-buffered saline. Retention was applied exclusively to the C+ group using a light-cured resin (Any-Com Flow; MD-CLUS) to maintain the interdental space between the maxillary left first and second molars. Under general anesthesia, the tooth surfaces were etched with 37% phosphoric acid (Any Etch; MD-CLUS), followed by the application of a bonding agent (Dx Bond; Azdent) and light curing for 5 s before placing the resin retainer.7 All animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation at either 7 or 14 days after appliance removal.29

The experimental subjects were designated as C−7 and C−14 (negative control without retention), C+7 and C+14 (positive control with retention), and S7 and S14 (secretome-treated relapse group) based on the treatment received and day of sacrifice. Several animals died during the experiment, resulting in unequal final sample sizes: C−7 (n = 7), C−14 (n = 7), C+7 (n = 6), C+14 (n = 8), S7 (n = 6), and S14 (n = 6). All remaining animals were included in the final analysis.

Tissue preparation and histological analysis

After sacrificing the animals, the maxillae were immediately dissected and immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at room temperature. The tissues were then rinsed with water and placed in 14% ethylene diamine tetra-acetate to demineralize for approximately 3 weeks at room temperature. The solution was replaced every day, and the bone texture and hardness were evaluated by probing with a needle prior to sectioning. Decalcified bone tissue was dehydrated using a graded series of ethanol concentrations, followed by paraffin embedding. Each sample was sagitally sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm and prepared for immunohistochemical staining.30

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated according to standard procedures. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubating the sections in 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in methanol for 15 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the maxillae were pre-treated in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 350 W for 10 min. After rinsing using Tris-buffered saline, the sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the following primary antibodies: mouse monoclonal anti-RANKL (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-52950; 1:100), rabbit polyclonal anti-OPG (ElabScience, E-AB-68342; 1:100), mouse monoclonal anti-TRAP (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-376875; 1:100), mouse monoclonal anti-CTSK (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-48353; 1:100), mouse monoclonal anti-ALP (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-365765; 1:100), and mouse monoclonal anti-OCN (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-365797; 1:100).31

After incubating with the primary antibodies, the sections were incubated with a biotinylated secondary antibody for 30 min at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine hydrochloride, before counterstaining with hematoxylin for 30 s. The slides were then dehydrated via an ethanol series and xylene, before sealing with a glass coverslip using Canadian balm (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Immunohistochemical controls were prepared by incubating tissue sections with normal non-immune serum instead of the primary antibody to evaluate nonspecific staining.21,22

Positive immunostaining was observed for ALP, OCN, RANKL, and OPG in osteoblasts, whereas positive staining for TRAP and CTSK was seen in osteoclasts. Immunohistochemical evaluation was performed in the tension area of the alveolar bone at the distal root surface of the maxillary left first molar, corresponding to the tension side during mesial tooth movement. Quantitative analysis was conducted by counting immunopositive cells within a defined circular region of interest measuring 265 μm in diameter by using a light inverted microscope (TE2000; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). In total, 20 high-power fields were examined per section, where observations were systematically initiated from the apical region, before progressing coronally toward the occlusal aspect of the tooth. Counting was performed at 400 × magnification, and 1000 × was used to verify staining and cellular morphology. Immunopositive cells were characterized by brown cytoplasmic staining and visible nuclei. The mean number of positive cells per field was calculated and used for statistical analysis. All evaluations were performed by an observer who was blinded to treatment allocation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graph preparation were performed using Prism software (version 10, GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). The results were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Normality was tested and confirmed by using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance, followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test, where p < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

Discussion

Orthodontic relapse is a complex biological process driven by an imbalance in alveolar bone remodeling following the cessation of orthodontic force. Bone at the tension side may remain vulnerable to external forces and sustained osteoclastic resorption, thereby contributing to post-treatment instability.7,8 Consequently, maintaining homeostasis between bone resorption and bone formation is essential for stability after orthodontic tooth movement.33

This study evaluated the expression levels of osteoclast-related markers TRAP and CTSK in the alveolar bone on the tension side during the orthodontic relapse phase. TRAP is a marker of osteoclast differentiation, whereas CTSK is a key proteolytic enzyme responsible for degrading the collagen matrix during bone resorption.21,22 Orthodontic relapse is characterized by persistent osteoclastic activity, especially on the tension side, which contributes to ongoing alveolar bone remodeling after the cessation of orthodontic force. In addition to TRAP and CTSK, the RANKL/OPG ratio was examined as a central regulatory pathway of osteoclastogenesis. Osteogenesis-related markers, including ALP and OCN, were also analyzed as indicators of osteoblastic differentiation and matrix maturation, reflecting bone formation.22,23,33,34

In the present study, hUC-MSC secretome administration was associated with reduced expression of TRAP and CTSK in alveolar bone on the tension side, indicating attenuation of osteoclast activity during relapse. In parallel, secretome treatment resulted in decreased RANKL expression, increased OPG expression, and a consistently lower RANKL/OPG ratio compared with the negative control treatment. Collectively, the results demonstrated a shift in the local microenvironment toward reduced osteoclastogenic signaling. This pattern is consistent with previous reports that the MSC-derived secretome inhibits RANKL-induced osteoclast differentiation and maturation, reduces the number of TRAP-positive multinucleated osteoclasts, and downregulates osteoclast-related genes such as NFATc1, CTSK, and TRAP.14, 15, 16, 17 Furthermore, previous studies have reported that inhibition of orthodontic relapse is characterized by decreased osteoclast activity, as evidenced by a reduced RANKL/OPG ratio, as well as decreased CTSK and TRAP expression.35, 36, 37, 38

An important mechanism that underlies the anti-osteoclastogenic effects of the hUC-MSC secretome involves the regulation of oxidative stress during osteoclastogenesis. RANKL stimulation induces the intracellular generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which act as secondary messengers to promote osteoclast differentiation and activation through redox-sensitive signaling pathways. Excessive ROS production increases NFATc1 activation and osteoclast-specific gene expression, sustaining bone resorption. Previous studies have shown that the MSC secretome can attenuate RANKL-induced ROS production in osteoclast precursors and upregulate endogenous antioxidant enzymes, such as heme oxygenase-1, superoxide dismutases (SOD1 and SOD2), and glutathione peroxidases (GPX1–3). Moreover, bioactive components within the secretome, including leukemia inhibitory factor, hepatocyte growth factor, and insulin-like growth factor-2 (IGF-2), have been reported to suppress oxidative stress and interfere with ROS-dependent osteoclastogenic signaling. The reduction of oxidative stress through these mechanisms may have contributed to the observed downregulation of TRAP, CTSK, and RANKL expression under secretome treatment in the present study.17

In addition to suppressing osteoclast-associated markers, hUC-MSC secretome administration was associated with increased ALP and OCN expression levels on the tension side, indicating enhanced osteogenic activity during relapse. ALP is an early marker of osteoblastic differentiation and plays a critical role in matrix mineralization, whereas OCN reflects osteoblast maturation and extracellular matrix calcification.32,39 The concurrent increases in both markers suggest coordinated activation of the early and late stages of bone formation, which is particularly relevant in orthodontic relapse, where insufficient bone maturation has been linked to post-treatment instability.40

The osteogenic effects observed in this study are in accordance with previous in vitro and in vivo demonstrations that the MSC secretome promotes osteogenic differentiation, enhances mineral deposition, and accelerates bone regeneration.18,41 The composition of the secretome was not characterized in the present study, but previous research indicates that the MSC secretome contains osteoinductive and pro-angiogenic mediators, including growth factors such as transforming growth factor-β, IGF-1, vascular endothelial growth factor, and platelet-derived growth factor.42, 43, 44 These factors act synergistically to promote osteoprogenitor migration, osteoblast differentiation, extracellular matrix production, and angiogenesis, which is an essential process for sustained osteogenesis.

In addition to direct osteoinductive actions, the hUC-MSC secretome exerts immunomodulatory effects that may indirectly promote osteogenesis while suppressing osteoclastogenesis. Inhibition of the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway by the MSC secretome has been reported to reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine production and shift macrophage polarization from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype toward an anti-inflammatory and reparative M2 phenotype. This immunological shift is related to decreased levels of pro-inflammatory mediators, including interferon-γ and interleukin-17 (IL-17), and downregulation of osteoclast-inducing factors, such as RANKL, tumor necrosis factor-α, IL-1β, IL-6, inducible nitric oxide synthase, and matrix metalloproteinases, as well as increased expression of M2-associated markers (e.g., CD206, arginase-1, and Fizz1). A microenvironment dominated by M2 macrophages facilitates the resolution of inflammation and growth factor secretion, creating conditions that support bone formation and balanced remodeling while limiting sustained osteoclastogenic activity during the orthodontic relapse phase.15,16,19,45

However, the present study had several limitations that should be acknowledged. Although several animals were lost during the experimental period, all remaining specimens were included in the final analysis. The initial overestimation of the sample size ensured that the statistical power was adequate despite the unequal final group sizes, enabling the detection of biologically relevant differences in molecular expression. This study did not quantify the magnitude or rate of orthodontic relapse, and the findings were limited regarding biomolecular and immunohistochemical outcomes. The observation periods may not have fully captured early rapid relapse or long-term remodeling dynamics. Furthermore, the analysis focused solely on the tension side, and including the pressure side would yield a more comprehensive understanding of alveolar bone remodeling as a biological unit. The absence of secretome compositional profiling limits further insights into specific mechanistic details. Thus, future studies are warranted that incorporate quantitative relapse measurements, extended observation periods, histomorphometric analyses, and proteomic or extracellular vesicle profiling of the secretome to further clarify the mechanisms involved, and to establish clinical relevance.

Conclusion

The results obtained in this study suggest that the simultaneous downregulation of TRAP, CTSK, and the RANKL/OPG ratio, and upregulation of ALP and OCN by the hUC-MSC secretome may help to restore a more favorable remodeling balance at the alveolar bone tension side during orthodontic relapse. Future studies should focus on elucidating the underlying mechanisms and evaluating the clinical effects of the secretome in preventing orthodontic relapse, rather than only conducting histological assessments.
