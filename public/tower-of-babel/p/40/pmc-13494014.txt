Single-nucleus analysis of the adult human olfactory epithelium uncovers shared neurogenesis programs with the brain
Authors: Liting Song, John F Fullard, Claire Coleman, Evelyn Hennigan, Clara Casey, Xinyi Wang, Ayako Kawatake-Kuno, Stathis Argyriou, Steven Kleopoulos, Samuel DeMaria, Jaroslav Bendl, Alfred Marc Iloreta, Pengfei Dong, Panos Roussos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeurogenesis, a critical process implicated in diverse brain disorders, is greatly diminished in the adult human brain, complicating direct investigations into its mechanistic role in disease. In the olfactory epithelium (OE), olfactory sensory neurons (OSNs) maintain homeostasis via continual neurogenesis throughout life, providing a niche to investigate neurogenesis in vivo. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying this process and its similarities to brain neurogenesis remain largely unknown. Here, we performed single-nucleus RNA-seq on specimens of human OE from 6 living adult donors, yielding high-quality transcriptomes representing 145,720 cells. Integrating with two independent OE single-cell transcriptomics datasets, different developmental stages of OSNs were identified, including neural precursor cells (globose basal cells, GBCs), as well as immature and mature OSNs. We inferred trajectories and assessed the transcriptional and regulatory dynamics of OSN development. Genes and transcription factors (TFs) involved in regulating neuronal differentiation and neurogenesis were highly expressed in GBCs and early immature OSNs, but were downregulated in mature neurons. OSNs and cortical excitatory neurons exhibited convergence during early developmental stages, including dynamically expressed genes, TFs, biological processes, and polygenic enrichment for psychiatric disorders. In addition, expression trajectory alignment between OSNs and cortical excitatory neurons (CENs) revealed that OSNs could partially track the expression dynamics of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) risk genes in CENs. Overall, cells in the neuronal lineage of the OE represent a potential proxy to study gene programs involved in neurogenesis in the human brain, providing an accessible model for investigating neurodevelopmental dysfunction in psychiatric disorders.Subject terms: Olfactory system, Genetics of the nervous system

Introduction

The polygenic enrichment observed across multiple neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric disorders in developing neurons highlights the roles of neurogenesis and neuronal development in disease onset and progression1–3. Disruptions in neurodevelopment during fetal and neonatal stages can impair neural circuits, leading to symptoms in childhood or early adulthood4,5. However, except for certain interneurons in the olfactory bulb and granule cells in the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus, most neurons in the mammalian nervous system are believed to be post-mitotic and have lost their ability to regenerate6,7. The transient nature of early neurodevelopment means that the initial stages of disease onset in current patients cannot be directly observed in vivo. Consequently, studying postmortem brain tissue, primarily composed of fully differentiated neurons, often misses early neurogenesis and neurodevelopmental dysfunctions that contribute to disease onset. Although induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-based disease models could be an alternative approach that might overcome these limitations, they erase disease-associated, development/aging-associated processes and epigenetic signatures8,9.

Olfactory sensory neuron (OSN) lineage, located in the olfactory epithelium (OE) of the superior-medial nasal cavity10, provides a unique opportunity to overcome these limitations, as they facilitate the investigation of neurodevelopmental processes that continue through adulthood. A distinctive feature of OSNs is their ability to regenerate throughout life via the proliferation and differentiation of globose basal cells (GBCs)11. Accordingly, OE-derived neuronal cells have been utilized to unveil the neuronal signatures of psychiatric and neurological disorders such as schizophrenia, depression, bipolar disorder, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), and Parkinson’s disease12–16. For instance, dysregulation of Wnt signaling, microRNA-124-3p, and histone methylations have been observed in schizophrenia patient OE-derived neuronal cells17–22. Similarly, neuroinflammatory changes in olfactory neurons and immune cells were detected even in pre-clinical AD subjects23. Moreover, olfactory dysfunction, e.g., hyposmia, has also been reported in multiple neuropsychiatric disorders24, indicating potential shared pathologic mechanisms between olfactory deficits and brain disorders. Taken together, these observations suggest that the adult OSN lineage could serve as a model to investigate fundamental aspects of neuronal development, regeneration, and plasticity that are disrupted in neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric disorders.

A recent single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) study has identified cell populations in the adult OE, representing various stages of OSN development, from stem and progenitor cells to immature and mature neurons25. Additionally, the developmental trajectories and gene expression transitions of olfactory stem cells (i.e., HBCs) have been characterized using murine models of OE following injury26,27. However, a comprehensive analysis of the neurodevelopmental trajectory in normal adult human OSNs, capturing underlying biological processes and regulators, remains lacking. Furthermore, direct comparison of the expression profiles and developmental trajectories of OSNs with those of cortical neurons has not been performed, limiting our understanding of the potential of OSNs to model neuronal dysfunctions relevant to brain disorders.

Here, we performed single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) on 36 olfactory epithelium biopsies from six living adult human donors. We identified distinct developmental stages of the OSN lineage, including GBCs, as well as immature and mature OSNs. Trajectory and gene-regulatory network analyses revealed OSN stage-specific genes, biological processes, and TFs. We further compared these developmental programs with those of cortical neurons, revealing shared neurogenesis and regulation processes between GBCs/early immature OSNs and fetal/neonatal cortical ENs (CENs). Moreover, polygenic enrichment analysis revealed a significant association between neuropsychiatric disorders and genes/TFs highly expressed in GBCs and immature OSNs. Such heritability enrichment and stage-specific similarities provide insights into the broader implications of OSN neurogenesis for understanding brain disorders.

Results

Distinct stages of OSNs in adult olfactory epithelium

To investigate the cell taxonomy of the OE, we performed snRNA-seq on samples from six living adult humans (aged 26 to 72 years), collected during endoscopic endonasal anterior skull base surgery, and consisting of the left and right superior turbinate (ST), middle turbinate (MT), and superior septum (SS) (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Table 1; referred to as the MSSM-OE data). After quality control filtering (“Methods”), a total of 145,720 single-nucleus transcriptomes were retained for downstream analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1). Based on Leiden clustering and marker expression (Supplementary Fig. 2)25,28–30, we annotated 16 cell clusters, encompassing olfactory, respiratory, vascular, and immune cell types. The cells were visualized in two dimensions using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP)31 (Fig. 1b, “Methods”). The OSN lineage (n = 381) formed a distinct and well-defined cluster, clearly separated from other cell types, despite the predominance of vascular, serous, and secretory cells resulting from the inclusion of underlying stromal tissue (Fig. 1b).

To refine the classification of OSN developmental stages, we performed Leiden clustering and re-annotated the OSN cluster. This represented the sequential stages of OSNs, including GBCs, immature olfactory sensory neurons (iOSNs), and mature olfactory sensory neurons (mOSNs) (Fig. 1c). Consistent with previous findings25, iOSNs accounted for a large proportion (54.3%) of OSNs, followed by mOSNs (39.4%) and GBCs (6.3%) (Fig. 1d). The majority of OSNs were derived from the SS and ST regions (Fig. 1d). To strengthen the robustness of our findings, we integrated our dataset with two normosmic control cohorts from Durante et al.25. and Oliva et al.32 (Fig. 1e-f, Supplementary Fig. 3). Within the combined dataset (n = 1064), consisting of 74 GBCs, 601 iOSNs, and 389 mOSNs, cells were predominantly clustered by developmental stage rather than data source. While subtle batch effects were observed between data sources, likely arising from different sequencing methodologies (snRNA-seq in MSSM-OE cohort vs. scRNA-seq in Durante et al.’s and Oliva et al.’s cohort, Supplementary Fig. 3), the consistent cell orders confirmed the robustness of the integration. Marker genes were highly expressed in their corresponding cell clusters (Fig. 1g), demonstrating a gradual transition from GBCs to iOSNs to mOSNs. GBCs exclusively express immediate neuronal precursor (GBCinp) markers, including NEUROD1 and NEUROG126, whose progeny exit the cell cycle and become OSNs. GBCs exhibited higher second gap/mitotic (G2/M) cell cycle phase scores (Fig. 1h, Supplementary Fig. 4a), indicating active proliferation. iOSNs can be further divided into two subsets, early and late, based on the expression of neuronal markers during different stages of differentiation and on olfactory receptor expression33(Fig. 1g, Supplementary Fig. 4b). Compared to late iOSNs (l_iOSNs), early iOSNs (e_iOSNs) showed higher expression of neural precursor markers (e.g., SOX4) (Fig. 1g). e_iOSN-specific markers were enriched in the mitotic cell cycle phase transition and regulation of developmental process (e.g., NFIB, LHX2, and LHX9), while l_iOSN-specific markers were associated with cell projection organization (e.g., EFHD1 and B3GNT2) (Supplementary Data 1). Moreover, consistent with receptor transformations during olfactory neurogenesis33, compared to e_iOSNs, l_iOSNs showed a gradual increase toward a singular olfactory receptor (Supplementary Fig. 4b), indicating the stabilization of receptor choice.

To explore whether the olfactory epithelium could serve as a proxy for studying neuronal dysfunction in the brain, we performed polygenic enrichment analyses of brain disorder risk variants in OSN lineages. The single-cell disease relevance score (scDRS) was evaluated based on the expression of disease-associated genes implicated by genome-wide association studies (GWASs)34. Similar to the enrichment of psychiatric disorders in prefrontal cortex neurons during development and maturation (Supplementary Fig. 5)1–3,35, both GBCs and e_iOSNs were associated with psychiatric disorders, while no significant association was observed in mOSNs (Fig. 1i, Supplementary Fig. 6). As negative controls, fibroblasts and pericytes did not show any associations with neuropsychiatric disorders. These findings suggest that early developmental stages of OSNs may share genetic risk architectures with cortical neurons, highlighting their potential utility for investigating the cellular mechanisms underlying neuropsychiatric disorders19.

Expression dynamics across OSN genesis and development

To characterize the transcriptional dynamics underlying OSN genesis and development, we inferred their developmental trajectory using monocle336–38 (Fig. 2a-b). The resulting pseudotime, representing the cell order along a temporal trajectory, increased progressively from GBCs to iOSNs and mOSNs, with differences observed across OSN stages but not between datasets (Fig. 2c). Moreover, the spatial autocorrelation-based Moran’s I test36–38 was applied to identify differentially expressed genes along trajectories (trajDEGs; false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.05 and Moran’s I > 0.05) with adjustment of covariants (age, sex, dataset, and read count). Moreover, we cross-validated our findings using Slingshot39 and tradeSeq40. The high correlation of pseudotime (Spearman’s rho = 0.99, p < 2.2e-16, Supplementary Fig. 7a) and the significant overlap of trajDEGs (hypergeometric distribution; p < 2.2e-16; Jaccard index = 0.492, Supplementary Fig. 7b) confirmed the stability of our inference. To further mitigate the effect of lifestyle factors (i.e., smoking status), we excluded trajDEGs with > 5% expression variance attributed to smoking, which were primarily enriched in protein modification and RNA metabolism (Supplementary Fig. 8). Finally, 2,140 genes were retained and aggregated into 7 clusters of 3 trend patterns: down, transiently up (trans_up), and up (Fig. 2d-e, Supplementary Data 2).

The dynamically expressed genes highlighted key processes at each stage of OSN development (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Figs. 9–11). The down-trending genes (e.g., SIX1, NEUROD1, and LHX2), predominantly expressed in GBCs and e_iOSN, and downregulated at later stages (Fig. 2d-e), were enriched in signatures associated with neurogenesis, neuron differentiation, regulation of translation, and regulation of cell differentiation (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 9), highlighting the processes active during early neurogenesis. Transiently upregulated genes (e.g., SLC7A1 and SNCA), primarily expressed across the iOSNs (Fig. 2d-e), were mainly associated with axon development and extension, synaptic signaling, and amino acid transport processes (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 10). Finally, up-trending genes (e.g., DRC1 and FOXJ1), highly expressed in mOSNs (Fig. 2d-e), play roles in cilium organization and movement (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 11), presumably to facilitate odorant interactions. Notably, ion transport was also enriched in up-trending genes, critical components of the signaling between mOSNs and mitral, periglomerular, and tufted cells in the olfactory bulb41.

Similarities and differences between OSNs and CENs

Having characterized the transcriptional dynamics of OSN development, we next examined the similarities and differences between OSNs and cortical neurons. We performed comparative analyses of transcriptomic profiles, developmental DEGs, and enriched molecular pathways, leveraging single-cell datasets spanning gestation to adulthood in the developing cortex35,42.

To assess transcriptional similarity between OSNs and brain cell types, we analyzed both global expression correlation and expression of cell-type-specific marker genes (“Methods”). mOSNs showed a stronger transcriptional correlation with mature CENs than with inhibitory neurons or non-neuronal cell types (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 12). Consistently, mOSNs exhibited higher expression of CEN marker genes (e.g., SLC17A6) compared to markers of other cell lineages (Supplementary Fig. 13), aligning with their excitatory identity and their role in forming glutamatergic synapses onto olfactory bulb glomeruli43. In contrast, GBCs and iOSNs displayed greater transcriptional similarity to developing neural populations, including developing excitatory neurons (EN_dev), as well as progenitors from the medial and caudal ganglionic eminences (MGE_dev and CGE_dev) (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 12a). Given the heterogeneity of these developing cortical neurons, we further assessed the similarity using an independent, brain derived, dataset35 with finer resolution of developmental neural subtypes. Module scores were quantified for OSNs at different stages based on brain cell type–specific marker genes, representing the average expression of these markers in the OSN lineage relative to a random reference gene set. GBCs were most similar to ventricular radial glia (RG-vRGs) and intermediate progenitor of EN cells (IPC-ENs), while iOSNs aligned more with IPC-ENs, newborn ENs, and immature neurons (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 12b). Together, these findings revealed a transient resemblance between the OSN lineage and CEN development.

To further investigate the developmental similarities between OSNs and CENs, we compared their trajDEGs and dynamic biological processes. trajDEG overlap was defined as genes that were dynamically expressed in both OSNs and CENs within the same trend category. Down-trending genes showed the greatest overlap (Fig. 3c, one tailed Fisher’s exact test), suggesting relatively greater similarity during earlier developmental stages, corresponding to the GBC-to-iOSN transition in OSNs and the fetal-to-infant stage in CENs. We next compared the significance of the top 10 enriched biological processes (See "Methods”), revealing three distinct patterns across different DEG trends (Fig. 3d-f and Supplementary Fig. 14). For down-trending genes (Fig. 3d), most processes, including neurogenesis, RNA metabolism regulation, and cell proliferation, were highly enriched in both OSNs and CENs. For trans_up genes (Fig. 3e), axon development and synapse organization occurred in both cell types. For up-trending genes (Fig. 3f), the processes exhibited cell type specificity associated with functional specialization. Specifically, OSNs were enriched in cilium organization and cilium movement, while CENs were associated with synaptic signaling. These findings suggest partial convergence of early developmental programs between OSNs and CENs, followed by increasing divergence during later stages of maturation.

Expression trajectory alignment of ASD risk genes between OSNs and CENs

ASD is a neurodevelopmental disorder with early onset. Genetic variants associated with ASD are implicated in gene expression regulation, neurogenesis, chromatin modification, and synaptic function44,45. To further validate OSNs’ ability to recapitulate neuronal developmental dysfunctions, we assessed the expression of ASD risk genes in OSNs. By analyzing highly confident ASD risk genes from the SFARI database46 (“Methods”), we observed that these genes were significantly enriched among the trans_up genes in OSNs as well as the down and trans_up genes in CENs, confirming the potential role of early neuronal development in ASD etiology (Fig. 4a).

We next compared the expression dynamics of ASD risk genes between OSNs and CENs using Genes2Genes47, a trajectory-alignment method derived from single-cell transcriptomics. The OSN and lifespan CEN trajectories were segmented into 14 sequential time bins to evaluate the alignment status of individual genes (Fig. 4b). Concordant with the sequential transcriptomic correlations between OSN lineage and distinct developmental periods of CENs (Supplementary Fig. 12c-d), the pseudotime segments of GBCs, iOSNs, and mOSNs aligned with fetal, fetal-to-infancy, and childhood-to-adult CENs, respectively. The gene-level alignment similarity was quantified based on the state concordance across pseudotime bins. To validate the robustness of this measure, we examined the impact of varying key parameters, including the number of pseudotime bins (n = 4–16) and dataset size (number of CENs = 500 to 10,000) (Supplementary Fig. 15). The high correlation between similarities from these varied conditions confirmed the reliability and stability of the alignment.

Genes were categorized into three patterns based on their alignment similarity scores along the trajectory, including match (similarity > 0.75), medium match (0.25–0.75), and mismatch (< 0.25) (Fig. 4c). Given the bimodal distribution of fold changes in the mismatch cluster (Supplementary Fig. 16a), these genes were further subdivided into CEN-up and OSN-up groups. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that OSN-up and CEN-up mismatched genes were primarily associated with sensory organ morphogenesis and synaptic signaling, respectively (Fig. 4d). For instance, SCN1A, which encodes a sodium channel alpha subunit, exhibited specific high expression in mature CENs but not in OSNs (Fig. 4e-f, Supplementary Fig. 16b). Notably, out of 371 overlapping genes between ASD risk genes and expressed genes in OSNs and CENs, matched or medium-matched genes accounted for the larger proportion (Fig. 4c), highlighting that OSNs could partially track the expression dynamics of ASD risk genes in CENs. For example, SLC45A1, a glucose transporter gene in the brain, displayed a concordant up-trend in both the CEN and OSN lineages (Fig. 4e-f, Supplementary Fig. 16c). Similarly, SHANK3, encoding a key postsynaptic density protein at glutamatergic synapses48, exhibited a more modest increase in expression in OSNs that partially mirrored the elevation observed in CENs (Fig. 4e-f, Supplementary Fig. 16d). Functional enrichment analysis for matched genes highlighted convergent biological processes (Fig. 4c-d), including transcription regulation, chromatin organization, and synaptic signaling. Validation against mouse fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) purified GBCs and OSN datasets confirmed this concordance, with 75.5% of down-regulated (p = 0.0051, Hypergeometric distribution) and 53.1% (p = 2.2e-4, Hypergeometric distribution) of up-regulated ASD risk genes in CENs exhibiting the same trend in OSNs (Supplementary Fig. 17). Collectively, these results support the utility of OSNs as a model system for studying neuronal dysfunction in neurodevelopmental disorders.

Regulatory dynamics of OSNs and their associations with neuropsychiatric disorders

The regulatory dynamics of OSN development provide an opportunity to elucidate shared and distinct regulatory processes between OSNs and CENs. To investigate this, we constructed gene regulatory networks (GRNs) of OSNs using SCENIC49, revealing TF regulons (i.e., TFs and their target genes) with higher activity specific to GBCs, iOSNs, and mOSNs (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Data 3). Because low cell numbers increase the risk of spurious signals (Supplementary Fig. 18), for downstream analysis, we prioritized highly confident regulons by retaining only TF regulons that were consistently detected in at least 40 of 50 independent runs (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Fig. 18). GBC, iOSN, and mOSN-specific TF regulons were mainly enriched in down, trans_up, and up trending trajDEGs (Fig. 5c), respectively.

Furthermore, we revealed OSN-specific TFs and TFs shared between OSN and CEN neurogenesis (Fig. 5d). For example, SIX1, a key regulator of sensory organ development50,51, was specifically expressed in GBCs (Supplementary Fig. 19a, c). In contrast, LHX2, a TF regulating progenitor proliferation and axon guidance52, was highly expressed in progenitor and immature cells of both OSN and CEN lineages. The similar expression dynamic patterns of shared TFs suggest conserved roles in OSN and CEN neurogenesis (Supplementary Fig. 19b, d). TFs shared with CENs were primarily identified in GBCs and e_iOSNs, but were rarely found in mOSNs or l_iOSNs, suggesting that regulatory mechanisms are more conserved during earlier neurogenesis stages than later mature stages (Fig. 5f).

Dysregulation of brain neuronal development is a well-established contributor to neuropsychiatric disorders53, and shared regulatory mechanisms may similarly influence the molecular profiles of OSNs. Notably, genetic variants of neuropsychiatric disorders (Fig. 5e) were mainly enriched in TF regulons highly expressed in GBCs (33.3%) and e_iOSNs (53.3%) (Fig. 5g). For instance, TCF4, a key regulator involved in neuronal differentiation and implicated in SCZ, ASD, and other neuropsychiatric conditions54, was highly expressed in GBCs and iOSNs but showed a gradual decrease in mOSNs (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Fig. 19e). The TCF4 regulon showed higher activity in iOSNs and was enriched in down and trans_up trajDEGs (Fig. 5a, i). The stage-specific enrichment of brain disorder risk genes in TF regulons highlights the potential application of GBCs and iOSNs in studying the molecular mechanisms underlying neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders.

Discussion

In this study, we leveraged biopsy specimens from living adults to generate a cellular taxonomy of the human olfactory epithelium. The presence of GBCs and iOSNs provided evidence for ongoing neurogenesis in adulthood, a process that is largely restricted in most regions of the human brain. Through systematic characterization of gene expression and regulatory dynamics across the developmental trajectory of the OSN lineage, we elucidated critical biological processes underlying neurogenesis and neuronal maturation, achieving a finer resolution than with bulk data10. The similarities between OSNs and CENs suggest that cells in the neuronal lineage of OE could serve as a readily accessible model for investigating fundamental aspects of cortical neurogenesis in both healthy and diseased states.

The molecular profiles of olfactory epithelium-derived progenitor neurons have been shown to resemble those of immature neurons in the human brain, clustering close to prenatal (mid-fetal) brain neurons19,55. However, their biological processes and regulatory mechanisms have never been systematically compared. Our comparative analysis of transcriptional and regulatory dynamics revealed both shared and distinct features between OSN-lineage cells and CENs. We found that key developmental processes, including neurogenesis, cell proliferation, and transcriptional regulation, were conserved between GBCs/early iOSNs and neurons of the developing brain. Notably, TFs implicated in the cortical neuronal differentiation were also highly expressed in GBCs and early iOSNs. Furthermore, shared developmental processes, including axon development and synaptic signaling, were observed in OSN-lineage cells and CENs during mid-to-late developmental stages. However, in contrast to the decades-long maturation period of brain neurons, OSNs exhibit a markedly shorter lifespan, typically lasting approximately three months10. In line with this, several CEN maturation-related processes, such as neuronal pruning and myelination, were absent in OSNs. Despite these differences, our findings underscore the potential of OSN lineage as a model system for investigating specific aspects of brain neurogenesis and development, particularly the utility of GBCs and early iOSNs in capturing early-stage neuronal processes.

Clinical phenotypes, such as smell deficits observed across multiple neuropsychiatric disorders, provide strong evidence for a connection between the olfactory system and neuropsychiatric disorders56. However, the extent of shared vulnerability and the underlying molecular mechanisms require further genetic support. Here, the polygenic enrichment of psychiatric disorders in GBCs and early iOSNs indicates higher expression of risk genes in early-stage OSN lineage, highlighting their potential to capture psychiatric-associated dysfunctions. The weaker association between OSNs and neurological and neurodegenerative disorders aligns with a primarily non-neuronal contribution to these conditions57–60. Moreover, GBC- and early iOSN-specific TF regulons were significantly enriched for psychiatric disorder risk variants, particularly those involved in CEN development. In addition, the matched expression dynamics of ASD risk genes between OSNs and CENs further reinforce this connection. Collectively, these results suggest that GBCs and early iOSNs represent promising cellular models for investigating the genetic underpinnings of neuropsychiatric disorders.

Although this work provides the systematic comparison of neurodevelopmental molecular programs between OSN lineage and CENs, it has several limitations. The relatively small number of OSN-lineage cells analyzed limited our ability to detect cell type-specific genes and TF regulons, especially in GBCs. To enhance detection sensitivity, future studies should incorporate larger cohorts and improve sampling strategies. Removing the deep stroma from the epithelium under a microscope, focusing only on the SS and ST regions, will improve the resolution of these analyses. While snRNA-seq data revealed stage-specific TFs, additional assays (including chromatin accessibility profiling via snATAC-seq) and functional experiments are needed to confirm the regulatory mechanisms. Moreover, cell-cell interactions between neuronal and non-neuronal cells (e.g., immune and vascular cells) are critical for proper neurogenesis, neural differentiation, and synaptic structure61,62. Comparing non-neuronal cells in the olfactory epithelium and the brain will help elucidate the potential role of the cellular microenvironment in early neurodevelopment and its implications for neuropsychiatric disorders. Additionally, the nasal cavity is highly affected by environmental stressors, including air pollution and smoking63–65. Although we mitigated these confounding effects by carefully selecting healthy controls and removing transcripts affected by smoking status, we cannot entirely rule out the impact of individual environmental exposures. Future studies incorporating larger cohorts and systematic histological validation will be essential to standardize the host response against external environmental variables. In addition, although we validated the expression dynamics of some critical TFs and ASD risk genes using mouse RNA-seq data, further systematic experimental validation, both at transcription and protein levels in human tissue contexts, would strengthen the robustness of our findings. Finally, while the use of OSNs to identify dysfunctions in psychiatric disorders shows promise, further validation using specimens from donors diagnosed with these psychiatric conditions is needed. Case-control studies, alongside comparative analyses of brain tissues and organoid models, will be critical to establish the reliability and generalizability of the applications of olfactory epithelium–derived neuronal cells in neuropsychiatric disorder research.

In conclusion, our study provides a perspective on the transcriptional dynamics of cells in the neuronal lineage of the OE, demonstrating their potential as a model system for investigating neurodevelopmental processes and disease-associated gene expression. By leveraging the unique accessibility and regenerative capacity of neuronal cells in the OE, future research may elucidate key molecular mechanisms underlying neuropsychiatric disorders and advance precision medicine approaches.

Methods

Sample collection

The study was approved by the Mount Sinai Institutional Review Board (STUDY-22-00614), and written informed consent was obtained from all individuals. Both left and right normal superior turbinate (ST), middle turbinate (MT), and superior septum (SS) were collected from 6 living adult patients at the Mount Sinai Hospital (Supplementary Table 1), referred to as MSSM-OE data. Donor sex was recorded based on the sample metadata provided at collection. Gender identity was not assessed and was not considered in the study design. To minimize the impact of nasal disease or prior surgery on the transcriptome, patients who had evidence or history of inflammatory sinus disease, prior surgery, or a history of anosmia were excluded. Samples were taken within the operative corridor for an approach to routine anterior cranial base tumor. Specimen harvest was performed under direct endoscopic visualization with an effort for en-bloc harvest. Minimal cautery was employed during the harvest to maintain sample integrity and minimize cautery artifacts. Samples were immediately organized and oriented following removal. Immediately following extraction, samples were snap-frozen and stored at −80 ˚C.

Nuclei isolation and snRNA-seq library preparation

Frozen nasal tissue was homogenized in a cold lysis buffer (0.32 M Sucrose, 5 mM CaCl2, 3 mM Magnesium acetate, 0.1 mM, EDTA, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH8, 1 mM DTT, 0.1% Triton X-100) with RNAse inhibitors (Takara). The homogenate was filtered through a 40 µm cell strainer, and the flow-through underlaid with sucrose solution (1.8 M Sucrose, 3 mM Magnesium acetate, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH8, 1 mM DTT) before centrifugation at 107,000 x g for 1 h at 4 °C. The resulting pellets were resuspended in PBS with 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Six samples were processed simultaneously, with up to 2 million nuclei per sample pelleted at 500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The nuclei were then resuspended in 100 µl staining buffer (2% BSA, 0.02% Tween-20, 10 mM Tris, 146 mM NaCl, 1 mM CaCl2 and 21 mM MgCl2) and incubated with 1 µg of a unique TotalSeq-A nuclear hashing antibody (BioLegend) for 30 min at 4 °C. Prior to fluorescence-activated nucleus sorting (FANS), volumes were adjusted to 250 µl with staining buffer, and 7-Aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD) was added according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 7-AAD positive nuclei were sorted into tubes pre-coated with 5% BSA using a FACSAria flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

After FANS, the nuclei were washed twice with 200 µl of staining buffer, resuspended in PBS and quantified (Countess II, Life Technologies). Equal numbers of differentially hash-tagged nuclei were combined. Using 10x Genomics single cell 3’ v3.1 reagents, 60,000 nuclei (10,000 per donor) were processed in each of two Chromium G chip (10x Genomics) lanes to create technical replicates. During cDNA amplification for library preparation, 1 µl of a 2 µm HTO cDNA PCR additive primer was included66. Both cDNA and HTO libraries were prepared according to manufacturer’s instructions (10x Genomics and BioLegend, respectively). Libraries were sequenced using the Novaseq platform (Illumina).

snRNA-seq data preprocessing and clustering

Paired-end snRNA-seq library reads were aligned to the hg38 reference genome using STARsolo67 and sample pools were demultiplexed using genotype matching via vireoSNP68. Donor identity for demultiplexed samples was assigned through comparison with SNP-array genotypes using the mbv module in QTLtools69.

Preprocessing and cell clustering were performed with Scanpy (v1.9.8)70. First, we filtered cells with low quality and kept cells with: cell counts in each tissue from each donor (> 150 cells), mitochondrial content (< 5%), total counts (< 50,000), and gene counts (1000-10,000). Candidate doublets were detected using Scrublet71, and cells with a doublet score > 0.15, or clusters expressing markers of multiple cell types were removed. Then, the gene counts were normalized to median total counts and logarithmized. The top 5,000 highly variable protein-coding genes (HVGs) on autosomal chromosomes were selected. PCA dimension reduction based on HVGs was performed. The batch effects (identity of donors and tissue sites) were corrected with Harmony72. We then computed the neighborhood graph and embedded it into two dimensions for visualization with UMAP31. Finally, the Leiden graph-clustering73 method was applied to cluster the neighborhood graph of cells (resolution = 1.5). Cell-type annotations were performed based on marker genes (Supplementary Fig. 2)25,28–30. To get the cell stages of OSNs, we re-annotated the OSN cluster. Specifically, we selected 700 HVGs on cells of the OSN cluster, performed PCA reduction using the top 30 PCs, corrected batch effects using Harmony72, and identified OSN subclusters with a resolution of 0.2.

SNP array genotyping workflow

Genomic DNA was isolated using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen) and quantified by averaging measurements from a Qubit fluorometer (Life Technologies) and a nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). A total of 400 ng of DNA from each sample was genotyped using the Infinium Global Screening Array-24 Kit (Illumina), following manufacturer’s instructions.

Genotype data underwent pre-imputation processing, which included quality control using the HRC-1000G-check-bim.pl script provided by the McCarthy Lab Group (https://www.well.ox.ac.uk/~wrayner/tools/). Following this, genotypes were phased and imputed through the Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine (TOPMed) Imputation Server74. Only variants with an imputation R2 greater than 0.3 were retained.

Data integration

To integrate our MSSM-OE data with those from Durante et al.25 and Oliva et al.32 anchor-based canonical correlation analysis (CCA) integration was applied using the IntegrateLayers function in Seurat (v5.0.2). After removing low-quality cells, a total of 1064 cells were retained. The top 30 PCs, the top 3000 HVGs, and 30 nearest neighbors were selected to integrate data and co-embed shared cell types across datasets and batches. To ensure the robustness of our cell taxonomy, we independently validated the integration using scvi75. Data source and donor identity were treated as batch variables during model training.

Trajectory inference and trajDEG identification

Monocle3 (v1.3.7) was applied to reconstruct the developmental trajectory for the integrated OSN cell lineage36–38. Pseudotime was inferred to measure developmental progress. Differentially expressed genes along the trajectory (trajDEGs) were then identified with a modified graph_test function76, using positive Moran’s I from spatial autocorrelation analysis to get genes that were expressed in a focal region of the UMAP space (Moran’s I > 0.05 and FDR < 0.05). Covariates, including sex, scaled age, batches, and log(total counts), were corrected in DEG analysis. To validate the pseudotime and trajDEGs, we performed an alternative trajectory inference analysis using the Slingshot39 package. Subsequently, trajDEGs were identified by fitting a generalized additive model (GAM) via tradeSeq40.

To control for the potential confounding effects of smoking status on gene expression, we employed a variance partitioning analysis using a linear mixed model framework implemented in the dreamlet package77. Genes with >5% of expression variance partitioned to smoking status were excluded from the trajDEG set to ensure robustness against this confounding factor.

To cluster trajDEGs along OSN development, we fitted natural cubic splines between gene expression and the pseudotime for each gene (expression ~ splines::ns(pseudotime, df = 3)). Then, gene expression was scaled to 0-1 with max-min normalization. We ordered cells by pseudotime and grouped the DEGs into 7 clusters by k-means clustering on the fitted normalized expressions. TrajDEGs of CENs were identified using the same pipeline1. To examine the overlap of trajDEGs between OSNs and CENs, Fisher’s exact test was applied.

Functional enrichment

Functional enrichment of GO biological processes was performed using gprofiler2 (v0.2.3)78. For trajDEGs of OSN and CEN, custom backgrounds of genes expressed in more than five OSNs and CENs, respectively, were applied. Multiple testing corrections were performed using FDR, with significant enrichments identified at FDR < 0.05. Terms with < 2000 genes were selected to focus on more informative and interpretable terms.

Enriched biological processes were ranked based on adjusted p-values (FDR). To facilitate comparison between the two cell types, we selected the top 10 significantly enriched biological processes (FDR < 0.05) for OSNs and CENs within each trend category. The rrvgo (v1.14.2) package was applied to reduce the redundancy of GO lists by grouping similar terms based on their semantic similarity79. Terms with the highest enrichment significance were picked to represent each group.

GRN network construction

pySCENIC (v0.12.1) was applied to reconstruct the single-cell gene regulatory network for OSNs49. First, coexpression modules between TFs and candidate target genes were inferred using GRNBoost2. Second, coexpression modules were subjected to cis-regulatory motif analysis using RcisTarget. This approach identified modules with significant motif enrichment of the upstream regulator and only retained direct targets. We refer to these processed modules as regulons. Third, the AUCell scored the activity of each regulon in each cell. To prioritize high-confidence TF regulons, we ran the whole pySCENIC procedure 50 times. Only TF regulons that were consistently recovered in ≥ 40 runs were retained for subsequent analysis. We used a Z score to find cell type-specific regulators (i.e., the average AUCell score for the cells of a given type is standardized using the overall average AUCell scores and the standard deviation). TFs involved in CEN development were derived from a sn-multi-omic atlas of the human developing neocortex35 based on AUCell scores > 0.1. TFs in the OSN lineage were then categorized as ‘shared’ or ‘OSN-specific’ based on their presence or absence in the CEN TF set.

To assess the robustness of the GRN network derived from a limited sample size, we performed a validation using an independent CEN dataset. We compared TF regulons identified from a subsampled CEN dataset (~1,000 CEN cells) against those derived from a well-powered dataset (10,000 CEN cells). Furthermore, we ran pySCENIC 10 times to confirm the advantage of the multi-run strategy in mitigating false positive regulon calls. To assess the robustness of our OSN findings, we performed a downsampling analysis (to 300, 500, and 700) and compared the resulting regulons with those identified from the full dataset.

Genetic enrichment

The enrichment of brain-associated disorder/trait risk variants across the regulons and genes of different trends was evaluated with MAGMA80 analysis. At the gene set level, MAGMA applied a generalized least squares regression model to test whether the genes in a gene set (e.g., regulons, trajDEGs, cell type-specific markers) are more strongly associated with the GWAS signal than other genes. The GWAS summary files for disorders/traits of interest are: ADHD81, ASD82, BD83, MDD84, SCZ85, AD86, ALS87, MS88, PD89, and stroke90.

scDRS was applied to explore the association between OSNs and neuropsychiatric disorders34, based on the relative expression of GWAS putative disease genes in a given cell compared to other genes with similar expression across all cells. First, the top 1000 MAGMA genes were selected as putative genes from GWAS summary statistics. The aggregated expression of these genes for each cell was qualified, weighted by their GWAS MAGMA z-score and gene-specific technical noise. Then, cell-level p values were determined by comparing the observed aggregate expression to an empirical distribution generated from 1000 Monte Carlo (MC) control gene sets. Each control gene set was matched to the putative disease genes in terms of gene set size (n = 1000), mean expression, and expression variance. Finally, group analysis was performed to assess the significance at the cell type level, with a positive z score indicating the association between cell types and disorders. Covariates, i.e., sex, scaled age, batches, and log total counts, were regressed out from the normalized data. As positive and negative controls, the scDRS association was also performed on cortex cells35 and other OE cells.

Gene expression concordance

To assess the expression correlation between OSN and cell types/stages in the brain42, the pseudobulk expression for each gene across cell types/stages was evaluated by the AggregateExpression function in Seurat. We then computed the Spearman correlation for the overlapping highly variable genes. To further validate the expressional resemblance, we also examined the module score of the top 50 marker genes for each brain cell type in OSNs, i.e, the average expression of a set of marker genes after subtraction by the average expression of a reference set of genes. The marker gene lists were obtained from Wang et al.’s data35.

Trajectory alignment

Genes2Genes (v0.2.0)47 was applied to compare the gene expression dynamics of ASD risk genes. The highly confident genes were obtained from SFARI in the syndromic category and category 146. Based on a Bayesian information-theoretic dynamic programming framework, sequential alignment states of individual genes were captured, including 1-to-1 match (M), warps (1-to-many expansion (V) or many-to-1 compression (W) match), and mismatch (insertion (I)/deletion (D)). We compared the genes differentially expressed across the OSN trajectory or CEN_L23 trajectory, where the CEN trajectory was derived from integrated lifespan data (psychAD & Herring et al.’s datasets)1,42. Taking CEN as a reference, the number of time points was determined by the opt-binning. After obtaining the alignment string for each gene along the trajectory, pairwise Hamming distances between genes were calculated. Hierarchical clustering was used to reveal the alignment patterns. To systematically assess the robustness of the gene alignment results, we evaluated the similarity scores under varying parameter conditions, including a wide range of pseudotime bin counts (from 4 to 16) and diverse CEN cell counts (ranging from 500 to 10,000 cells via subsampling).

Validation of gene expression

To validate the dynamics of these ASD risk genes and TFs, we analyzed an independent bulk RNA-seq dataset generated from cells isolated via FACS from the mouse olfactory epithelium during the critical developmental window (postnatal day P2 to P16)91. Specifically, mature OSNs were purified from Omp-GFP mice at postnatal stages P2, P7, and P16, while intermediate neuronal progenitors (GBC-INPs) were isolated from Neurog1-GFP mice at P3, P7, P9, and P16.
