Multi-task adversarial learning detects intersectional algorithmic bias in AI recruitment systems
Authors: Jiwei Wang, Yan Xu, Ruifeng Liu, Yujiao Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the widespread application of artificial intelligence in recruitment, algorithmic bias issues have become increasingly prominent, seriously threatening social fairness and job seekers’ rights. Addressing the limitations of existing bias detection methods in detection granularity, fairness assessment, and interpretability, this study proposes a deep learning-based algorithmic bias detection framework. The framework designs a multi-task adversarial learning architecture that achieves fine-grained intersectional bias detection through attention mechanisms; constructs a multi-dimensional evaluation system comprising nine metrics across three major categories including group fairness, individual fairness, and causal fairness, employing Pareto frontier analysis to reveal fairness trade-off relationships; and integrates an interpretability module based on SHAP values, gradient-weighted class activation mapping, and causal mediation analysis to achieve precise localization of bias sources. Experimental results demonstrate that the framework improves intersectional bias detection accuracy by 12–18% points compared to traditional methods, providing effective technical support and theoretical guidance for fairness improvement in AI recruitment systems.

Introduction

The application of artificial intelligence technology in the field of human resource management is profoundly transforming traditional recruitment models. Statistics show that over 85% of large enterprises globally have deployed AI-driven recruitment systems for resume screening, candidate assessment, and interview decision-making1. Al-Quhfa, Hikmat, et al., through a survey of Fortune 500 companies, found that these systems analyze massive amounts of recruitment data through machine learning algorithms, significantly improving recruitment efficiency and saving enterprises more than $23 billion in human resource costs annually2. However, with the widespread application of AI recruitment systems, algorithmic bias issues have gradually emerged. Sánchez-Monedero et al., in their ethical research on algorithmic decision-making systems, pointed out that these biases pose serious threats to social fairness and job seekers’ rights3.

In recent years, multiple incidents of discrimination by AI recruitment systems have attracted widespread attention. In 2018, Amazon was exposed for its AI recruitment tool systematically discriminating against female job seekers, as the system, due to the high proportion of male resumes in the training data, automatically lowered the scores of resumes containing “female”-related vocabulary4. In 2021, Lambrecht and Tucker, through large-scale empirical research, analyzed the algorithmic behavior of mainstream recruitment platforms in Europe and America, finding that the algorithm’s recommendation rate for minority candidates was 41% lower than that for white candidates5. In 2023, Köchling and Wehner, in their survey of AI interview systems at German technology companies, discovered that these systems exhibited significant bias against job seekers over 40 years old, resulting in the pass rate for this age group being only 63% of that for younger job seekers6. These examples show that algorithmic bias can not only prejudice individual career opportunities but also reinforce social injustice and spark legal and ethical controversy.

Algorithmic fairness theory is an important foundation for examining and evaluating the phenomenon of bias in AI systems. Researchers have proposed various concepts of fairness from different perspectives. In 2012, Dwork, Cynthia, et al. proposed the concept of demographic parity, which requires different groups to obtain positive outcomes at the same rate, a concept that has been widely applied in applications such as resource allocation and loan approval7. Stoilkovska Aleksandra, et al. proposed equal opportunity as the definition of fairness, where equally qualified candidates from different groups must have the same selection rate, a definition particularly appropriate for competitive scenarios such as recruitment and admission to education8. Vateha, Marko Dávid, in early work, suggested predictive rate parity, with a focus on whether the precision of positive predictions is balanced between groups, which has been validated in risk assessment and credit scoring settings9. Besides these group-level fairness concepts, Li, Yunqi, et al. also promoted individual fairness theory, encouraging the principle that similar individuals should have similar prediction results, providing useful fairness guidelines for personalized recommendation and medical diagnosis10. Prosperi, Mattia et al. proposed the concept of counterfactual fairness grounded in causal inference, which asserts that changes in sensitive attributes should not affect prediction results, providing a new perspective to understand the causal mechanisms for bias11. Notably, there is mathematical incompatibility among these definitions of fairness. Loi, Michele et al., through rigorous mathematical proof, pointed out that except in cases of perfect prediction, demographic parity, equal opportunity, and predictive rate parity cannot be satisfied simultaneously12. Therefore, in practical applications, appropriate fairness standards must be selected based on specific scenarios.

Researchers have proposed various detection methods for algorithmic bias issues in AI systems. Pagano, Tiago P., et al. developed statistical detection methods based on disparate impact analysis, identifying bias by comparing statistical distribution differences between different groups13. Although these methods are simple, intuitive, and easy to interpret, they can only detect group-level bias and cannot identify complex intersectional bias patterns, with detection accuracy typically between 68 and 75%. Azam, Zahedi et al., proposed decision tree-based bias detection methods, identifying bias features by constructing classification models14, which can handle multi-dimensional features but struggle to capture deep nonlinear bias patterns, with accuracy around 72–79%. In recent years, deep learning methods have received widespread attention. Xu, Han, et al. proposed fairness representation learning methods based on adversarial learning, learning fair representations through neural networks15. These methods can capture complex patterns with high performance, achieving detection accuracy of 81–87%, but suffer from poor interpretability and require large amounts of annotated data. Weidlich, Joshua, et al. developed a model for detecting bias based on causal inference, analyzing routes of bias transmission through the formation of causal graphs16, with strong theoretical foundations and ability to identify sources of bias, with precision between 76 and 83%, albeit the formation of causal graphs is difficult and computationally demanding.

Mainstream AI recruitment systems of today employ various technical approaches, with sources of prejudice extending to the entire system process. Chen, Zhisheng, in his groundbreaking article on big data bias, looked in a systematic way at concerns in the data collection process and observed that gender, race, and distribution disparities of age in historical hiring data introduce inherent prejudice to training samples17. Ullah Azmat, et al., in their research on online advertisement delivery systems, encountered proxy variable problems at the feature engineering stage, where features that seem to be innocuous may themselves turn out to be proxies for sensitive attributes—for example, postal codes can signal racial information and hobbies can signal gender inclinations18. Hall Melissa, et al. tested the bias amplification process in training the model and concluded that optimization targets of algorithms typically aim at prediction accuracy only and the fairness constraints are not considered, and thus such models learn and amplify the biases during training19. Chaudhary Gyandeep, in their research on algorithmic transparency, emphasized that at the decision output stage, considering the black-box nature of deep learning models, the decision-making process is not transparent and cannot be understood20. More seriously, Bauer Kevin, et al., through longitudinal tracking research, found that these biases are continuously reinforced through feedback loops, with biased decision results being incorporated into new training data, further exacerbating system unfairness21.

Different types of AI recruitment systems exhibit significant differences in technical architecture and bias manifestation. Maree Mohammed, et al. conducted a systematic evaluation of rule-based traditional recruitment systems, finding that such systems employ keyword matching and Boolean logic, and although accuracy is low, bias is relatively easy to identify22. Gagandeep et al.‘s analysis of traditional machine learning systems such as LinkedIn Recruiter showed that these systems use logistic regression and random forest algorithms, with improved accuracy but still exhibiting obvious gender, age, and racial bias23. Manikran Pedige, in his research on deep learning recruitment systems such as HireVue, discovered that although systems based on BERT and Transformer architectures achieve prediction accuracy of 83–89%, their bias issues are more hidden and difficult to detect24. Younis, Hussain A., et al.‘s questionnaire of multimodal recruitment systems indicated that multimodal systems using computer vision, natural language processing, and speech recognition technologies introduce new sources of bias while improving performance, including appearance, accent, gender, race, and age25.

In spite of the extensive research devoted to algorithmic bias discovery, the current approaches have three essential shortcomings. First, detection granularity is limited, and it is hard to detect intersectional bias. Donald, Andy, et al., through their landmark research on intersectional gender and racial bias, noted that standard statistical methods typically account for only a single sensitive attribute such as gender or race and cannot detect advanced patterns of bias created by interactions among multiple attributes26. Luiz, John M., and Viktor Terziev., early in legal research, proposed intersectionality theory, emphasizing that a system may exhibit different biases toward young female engineers and older male engineers, but single-dimension analysis struggles to capture such intersectional discrimination27. Mavrogiorgos, Konstantinos, et al., in recent research, found that although existing deep learning methods can handle high-dimensional features, they often treat bias detection as a binary classification problem, ignoring the diversity and hierarchy of bias28. Secondly, fairness assessment is single-dimensional without multi-dimensional integrative research. Jacobs, Abigail Z., and Hanna Wallach., based on their literature review of machine learning fairness, put emphasis on the fact that most studies employ a single or dual fairness measure to assess, but ignore the contradictory relationships of different fairness definitions29. Wang, Yifan, et al., by theoretical calculation, proved that systems may be excellent on one measure of fairness but exhibit considerable bias on other dimensions30. Chen, Zhenpeng, et al., while designing automated fairness testing tools, discovered that existing approaches lack extensive analysis of individual fairness and causal fairness, thus it becomes hard to extensively examine system fairness performance31. Thirdly, interpretability is missing, thus making sources of bias traceable. Guidotti et al., in their study of black-box model interpretability, noted that deep learning models’ black-box nature makes it hard for results of bias detection to be interpreted32. Adebayo et al., through an empirical study, found that even after revealing bias, existing measures do not accurately determine the specific stages and features from which bias stems, seriously hindering the implementation of precise bias-decreasing measures33.

For overcoming the above limitations, this work proposes a deep learning-based algorithmic bias detection framework with three primary innovations and contributions. The first innovation is a fine-grained intersectional bias detection multi-task adversarial learning framework. Pursuing Correa, Ramon, et al.‘s adversarial debiasing techniques34, this work proposes a new multi-task adversarial neural network that is able to detect both singular and intersectional biases effectively by learning representations and prediction tasks of multiple sensitive features simultaneously. The design integrates attention mechanisms to adaptively weigh different types of biases dynamically, and it gains a 12–18% point accuracy on detecting intersectional bias over state-of-the-art techniques. The second innovation is the construction of a multi-dimensional fairness evaluation metric system that measures system fairness systematically. Based on the notions of the AI Fairness 360 toolkit of Bellamy, Rachel KE, et al.35, this paper integrates three primary categories of 9 assessment metrics like group fairness, individual fairness, and causal fairness into an entire fairness evaluation framework for recruitment cases. With the use of Pareto frontier analysis methods, this explains systematically trade-off relations among different fairness measures, providing theoretical results for fairness maximization. The third innovation is the integration of an interpretability analysis module to facilitate precise localization of bias sources. Adopting the SHAP value analysis methodology proposed by Wang, Huanjing, et al.36, this research integrates different interpretability techniques like gradient-weighted class activation mapping and causal mediation analysis to construct a multi-level interpretation framework ranging from feature-level to decision-level. Apart from identifying impactful features responsible for bias, this module also visualizes bias propagation paths in models, providing actionable suggestions towards reducing bias.

Overall framework architecture

The proposed algorithmic bias detection framework adopts an end-to-end deep learning architecture that integrates three core modules: multi-task adversarial learning, multi-dimensional fairness assessment, and interpretability analysis, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The design philosophy of this framework is to learn fair representations decoupled from sensitive attributes through adversarial training mechanisms, while establishing a comprehensive fairness quantification system and providing interpretable analysis of bias sources. As shown in Fig. 1, the core of the framework is the multi-task adversarial learning architecture. The input layer receives the non-sensitive feature vector X and sensitive attributes S of candidates, which are processed through a feature encoder for representation learning to obtain low-dimensional hidden representations. An attention mechanism is introduced to dynamically adjust the importance weights of different features, enabling the model to adaptively focus on features relevant to hiring decisions but independent of sensitive attributes. The hidden representation is simultaneously fed into two task branches: the prediction task branch learns the prediction function for hiring decisions and outputs the hiring probability of candidates; the adversarial discriminator branch attempts to predict sensitive attributes from the hidden representation. By designing an adversarial loss function, the feature encoder learns representations that effectively support hiring predictions while being difficult for the adversarial discriminator to use for inferring sensitive attributes, thereby achieving fairness in representation. The multi-task loss function integrates prediction loss and adversarial loss, gradient backpropagation learning the entire network, and establishes a feedback loop for recursive model fairness improvement. The fairness evaluation module conducts comprehensive evaluation from three perspectives—group fairness, individual fairness, and causal fairness—based on learned fair representations and prediction outputs. This module integrates nine fairness metrics like demographic parity, equal opportunity, and predictive rate parity, and introduces Pareto frontier analysis methodologies to systematically reveal the trade-off relationships among different fairness metrics, providing quantitative basis for fairness optimization in practical applications. The interpretability analysis module integrates multiple methods like SHAP value analysis, gradient-weighted class activation mapping, and causal mediation analysis to construct a multi-level interpretation framework from feature-level to decision-level. This module not only indicates crucial properties that cause bias but also charts the trajectory of bias propagation within the model precisely identifying bias sources. The entire detection process is divided into five phases: first, preprocessing candidate recruitment data in terms of data cleaning, missing value handling, and normalization; second, feature extraction of candidates with multi-dimensionality like education background, work experience, and skill assessment; third, learning adversarially fair feature representations; fourth, comprehensive system assessment utilizing the multi-dimensionality fairness assessment module; and finally, delivery of overall results in terms of bias detection reports, fairness scores, interpretability analysis, and improvement suggestions. By combining the powerful representation capability of deep learning, the debiasing capability of adversarial training, and multi-dimensional fairness assessment, this framework achieves accurate detection and systematic evaluation of algorithmic bias in AI recruitment systems.

The adversarial discriminator branch attempts to predict sensitive attributes from the fair representation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{z}}$$\end{document}, with output \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat {{\mathbf{s}}}=\sigma ({{\mathbf{W}}_d}{\mathbf{z}}+{{\mathbf{b}}_d})$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbf{W}}_d} \in {{\mathbb{R}}^{m \times {d_2}}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbf{b}}_d} \in {{\mathbb{R}}^m}$$\end{document}. The adversarial loss also adopts the cross-entropy form:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}_{{\mathrm{adv}}}}= - \frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{{i=1}}^{N} {\sum\limits_{{j=1}}^{m} {\left[ {{s_{ij}}\log {{\hat {s}}_{ij}}+(1 - {s_{ij}})\log (1 - {{\hat {s}}_{ij}})} \right]} }$$\end{document}

Through adversarial training, the representation learned by the feature encoder must satisfy two conflicting objectives: on one hand, it must retain sufficient information to support accurate hiring predictions; on the other hand, it must obfuscate sensitive attribute information so that the discriminator cannot accurately infer them. To prevent overfitting, an L2 regularization term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}}_{{{\mathrm{reg}}}} = \frac{\gamma }{2}\left\| {{\mathbf{\theta }}_{e} } \right\|^{2}$$\end{document} is introduced, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbf{\theta }}_e}$$\end{document} represents all parameters of the encoder and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma$$\end{document} is the regularization coefficient.

The overall loss function of the model is designed as a multi-objective optimization form:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}_{{\mathrm{total}}}}={\mathcal{L}_{{\mathrm{pred}}}} - \lambda {\mathcal{L}_{{\mathrm{adv}}}}+\beta {\mathcal{L}_{{\mathrm{reg}}}}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda >0$$\end{document} is the adversarial weight controlling the degree of debiasing, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta >0$$\end{document} is the regularization weight. The negative sign before the adversarial loss is a critical design: by minimizing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}_{{\mathrm{total}}}}$$\end{document}, the feature encoder minimizes the prediction loss while maximizing the adversarial loss, thereby learning representations that are difficult for the discriminator to identify sensitive attributes from. The discriminator, on the other hand, improves its ability to predict sensitive attributes by minimizing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}_{{\mathrm{adv}}}}$$\end{document}, forming an adversarial game.

As shown in Fig. 3, the algorithm training process adopts an alternating optimization strategy. In each training batch, the discriminator parameters are first fixed while updating the encoder and predictor parameters to minimize \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}_{{\mathrm{total}}}}$$\end{document}; then the encoder parameters are fixed while updating the discriminator parameters to minimize \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}_{{\mathrm{adv}}}}$$\end{document}. Specifically, the parameter update rules for the encoder and predictor are:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbf{\theta }}_e} \leftarrow {{\mathbf{\theta }}_e} - \eta {\nabla _{{{\mathbf{\theta }}_e}}}{\mathcal{L}_{{\mathrm{total}}}},\quad {{\mathbf{\theta }}_p} \leftarrow {{\mathbf{\theta }}_p} - \eta {\nabla _{{{\mathbf{\theta }}_p}}}{\mathcal{L}_{{\mathrm{total}}}}$$\end{document}

The discriminator parameter update is:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbf{\theta }}_d} \leftarrow {{\mathbf{\theta }}_d} - \eta {\nabla _{{{\mathbf{\theta }}_d}}}{\mathcal{L}_{{\mathrm{adv}}}}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta$$\end{document} is the learning rate and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nabla$$\end{document} denotes the gradient operator. Training employs the Adam optimizer with momentum parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\beta _1}=0.9$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\beta _2}=0.999$$\end{document}. To improve training stability, gradient clipping is applied to limit the gradient norm within a threshold of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau =1.0$$\end{document}.

An early stopping mechanism is implemented during training, halting the process when fairness metrics on the validation set fail to improve for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k=10$$\end{document} consecutive epochs, thus avoiding overfitting. The final model is selected from the checkpoint with optimal comprehensive performance on the validation set. Through such an adversarial learning architecture, the model can reduce overdependence on sensitive features with satisfactory prediction performance and hence achieve algorithmic fairness. Coupling the attention mechanism improves model interpretability even further, such that quantification and visualization can be conducted regarding the importance of salient features, providing a foundation for subsequent bias source localization.

Multi-dimensional fairness evaluation metrics

Balance evaluation is the core of unfairness identification of algorithms. In this paper, a systematic multi-dimension evaluation measurement system with three dimensions, namely group fairness, individual fairness and causal fairness, consisting of 9 prominent indicators, is established as shown in Table 1. This metric system can comprehensively quantify the fairness performance of AI recruitment systems from different perspectives, providing precise quantitative basis for system optimization. The fairness thresholds in Table 1 are established based on widely accepted standards in the fairness literature and regulatory practice. The threshold DP ≤ 0.1 (10% difference) for demographic parity, equal opportunity, and predictive rate parity follows conventions in algorithmic fairness research, representing a commonly adopted tolerance level for group-level disparities. The disparate impact threshold 0.8 ≤ DI ≤ 1.25 is directly derived from the “80% rule” established by the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) in the Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures,

which stipulates that a selection rate less than 80% of the highest group’s rate may indicate adverse impact. For individual fairness, the consistency threshold Con ≥ 0.9 follows common practice in k-nearest neighbor analysis10. The counterfactual fairness threshold CF ≥ 0.95 is based on causal inference literature requiring high consistency between factual and counterfactual outcomes11. While these thresholds provide general guidelines, organizations should calibrate them according to their specific legal requirements, industry norms, and stakeholder values.

Group fairness metrics evaluate treatment disparities between different groups from a statistical perspective. Demographic parity requires that different sensitive attribute groups have equal probabilities of receiving positive predictions. The deviation measure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{DP}}$$\end{document} is obtained by calculating the absolute difference between the two group selection rates, and demographic parity is considered satisfied when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{DP}} \leqslant 0.1$$\end{document}. Equal opportunity focuses on truly qualified candidates, requiring that the true positive rate remains consistent across different groups, i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(\hat {Y}=1|Y=1,S=0) \approx P(\hat {Y}=1|Y=1,S=1)$$\end{document}. Predictive rate parity approaches from the perspective of precision, requiring that among candidates predicted as qualified, the proportion of truly qualified individuals should be equal across different groups. Disparate impact adopts a ratio format, with the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission stipulating that discrimination may exist when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{DI}}<0.8$$\end{document}.

Individual fairness metrics focus on fairness between similar individuals, compensating for the inability of group fairness to capture individual differences. Individual fairness requires that candidates with similar features should receive similar prediction results, quantified by calculating the ratio of prediction differences to feature distances. The distance function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d({x_i},{x_j})$$\end{document} can adopt Euclidean distance or Mahalanobis distance. The consistency metric evaluates from the perspective of k-nearest neighbors, calculating for each sample the proportion of its k nearest neighbors whose prediction results differ from its own. A higher value of this metric indicates more consistent predictions by the model for similar samples. In practical applications, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k=5$$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k=10$$\end{document} is typically set, with a requirement of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{Con}} \geqslant 0.9$$\end{document}.

Causal fairness metrics are based on causal inference theory, analyzing the impact of sensitive attributes on prediction results from a causal mechanism perspective. Counterfactual fairness adopts Pearl’s causal model, evaluating through counterfactual reasoning: whether the prediction result would change if a candidate’s sensitive attribute were different. In specific calculations, for each sample, a counterfactual sample is constructed (changing only the sensitive attribute while keeping other features constant), comparing the prediction results of the original sample with the counterfactual sample. Counterfactual fairness is considered satisfied when the probability of consistency between the two is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{CF}} \geqslant 0.95$$\end{document}. Path-specific effect decomposes the direct and indirect effects of sensitive attributes on outcomes, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M(s)$$\end{document} represents the mediator variable value when the sensitive attribute is s. This metric quantifies the intensity of bias propagated through specific causal paths. Total effect measures the overall causal influence of sensitive attribute changes on prediction results, which should ideally approach zero.

To comprehensively evaluate system fairness, this paper adopts a weighted combination approach to construct a comprehensive fairness score. Experiments

Experimental setup

This paper validates the effectiveness of the proposed framework on three real recruitment datasets. The first dataset is the Adult Census Income dataset, derived from the U.S. Census Bureau’s demographic data from 1994, containing 48,842 samples and 14 features. It is commonly used for income prediction and fairness research, with complete annotations of sensitive attributes such as gender, race, and age. The second dataset is the Bias in Bios dataset, released by Microsoft Research, containing 397,340 professional resumes covering 28 occupational categories and recording information such as practitioners’ gender, educational background, and work experience. It is a standard dataset for studying recruitment algorithm bias. Discussion

Main findings and contributions

The deep learning-aided bias detection model introduced in this paper exhibits significant performance improvement in experiments. Experimental results reveal that the multi-task adversarial learning model enhances intersectional bias detection performance by 12–18% points over conventional statistical techniques, primarily due to the fact that the model is capable of cooperative learning across multiple sensitive attributes and therefore identify subtle patterns of biases that are difficult to capture with single-dimension analysis. Unlike the adversarial debiasing method proposed by Correa, Ramon, et al.34, the new framework dynamically adjusts weights of different bias types according to the use of attention mechanisms at relatively small expenses in terms of prediction accuracy while significantly improving fairness measures. This innovation further confirms the application value of multi-task learning in algorithmic fairness. The construction of a multi-dimensional fairness evaluation system is another major contribution to this work.

By combining nine measures in three broad classes of group fairness, individual fairness, and causal fairness, this system evaluates the overall fairness performance of hiring systems. Experimental results demonstrate the existence of trade-off relationships among various measures of fairness as advocated in the mathematical incompatibility of fairness definitions theory by Loi, Michele et al.12. Adhering to the framework of the AI Fairness 360 toolkit by Bellamy et al.35, the paper rigorously proves such trade-off relations based on Pareto frontier analysis techniques and sets up quantitative grounds for firms to choose effective fairness metrics within real-world applications. It must be noted that equal opportunity and rate parity are generally considered more in hiring because the former is more aligned with merit-based selection criteria. Through the addition of interpretability analysis module, it becomes possible to identify the source of bias. Following Wang, Huanjing, et al.‘s36 SHAP value analysis technique, this platform does not only detect influential features leading to bias but also follows the paths of bias propagation through the model.

Experimental analysis confirms that attributes that appear insensitive like postal codes, educational history, and work experience can serve as proxy variables for sensitive traits, as supported by the study of Ullah Azmat, et al. on online advertisement platforms18. Using gradient-weighted class activation mapping and causal mediation analysis, this platform identifies the precise sequences and attributes that introduce bias with ease, and it supplies actionable feedback towards implementing targeted bias reduction strategies.

Limitations and threats to validity

Though our approach shows encouraging results, we would like to highlight a few key points regarding our results and how our approach can be used in practical implementations.

To begin with, our validation study relies solely on Western datasets (US and Europe; Table 2), which belong largely to technology-related sectors. A dangerous level of risk is involved in this data geographical and categorical bias. Cultural factors concerning protected norms and laws regarding equality vary extremely widely—other countries emphasize distinct variables such as caste (India), language (Belgium), and political party membership. One needs to be extremely cautious when our measure for bias validation relies for calibration purposes on Western values such as demographic equality and equality of opportunity. Otherwise, we would be unable to argue that our revealed bias would amount to much in other parts of the globe: Asia, Africa, Latin America, the Middle East. Also, we might be dealing with a technology-related bias model that does not apply to other sectors such as healthcare, manufacturing, education, public administration.

Secondly, our class imbalance ratio values (3.2:1 to 4.1:1) are relatively moderate compared to those found in practical problems. In fact, for jobs such as recruiting executives and/or for more technical jobs, class imbalance ratios may be much larger—ratios of 20:1 or 100:1 are possible. In such extreme class imbalances, our adversarial training process and our fairness measures can become erratic: our model may be learning to be generally accurate while predicting little for minority groups. Additionally, our class measures may become numerically unstable. We have made no effort to test our model for such conditions. This becomes a serious issue for minority groups who face class imbalances in their sensitive attribute.

Beyond geographic and sectoral constraints, our use of curated public datasets does not reflect the data quality challenges of real-world Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS). Research datasets like Adult, Bias in Bios, and Kaggle Resume are relatively clean and structured, whereas production ATS data typically exhibits substantial missing values (30–50% incomplete profiles), inconsistent formatting, and unstructured text requiring natural language processing. Our framework assumes preprocessed, feature-engineered inputs and does not address: (1) missing data handling without disadvantaging affected groups, (2) fair representation learning from raw, heterogeneous resume formats (PDFs with varied layouts), or (3) maintaining fairness when data completeness varies systematically across demographics. Real ATS systems also process multimodal data—video interviews, unstructured cover letters, portfolio links—that our text-and-tabular framework does not accommodate. Robust deployment would require augmenting our architecture with missing data imputation, automated feature extraction from unstructured text, and fairness validation across varying data quality levels.

Thirdly, computational requirements preclude accessibility. Our model needs strong GPU machines (NVIDIA A100 with 40GB GPU memory requirements), and lots of data would be preferred. Training our model took 18–24 h with a 32GB peak memory usage in our experiments for the Biased in Bios dataset.

Table 6 provides a comparative analysis of computational requirements across methods. Our proposed framework requires 4–6× longer training time than simpler baselines, with proportionally higher memory and cost requirements. This computational overhead represents the trade-off for achieving 12–18% points improvement in intersectional bias detection and comprehensive fairness guarantees.

While the computational demands are substantial, they remain feasible for organizations with moderate IT budgets, particularly considering that model training is typically performed offline and infrequently (e.g., monthly or quarterly retraining cycles).

This could be a major hindrance for Small and Medium-Sized Enterprises (SMEs), which may have fewer IT developments. The irony remains that those regions and organizations who might be most affected regarding bias problems would be least equipped to use our solution. Also, for smaller datasets (with 500-1,000 samples), our solution’s performance characteristic remains unexplored.

Fourth, even though we offer nine measures of fairness for comparison across three dimensions, our design does not address the underlying challenge of making good decisions about which measures of fairness are most appropriate for a particular use. While our overall measure of fairness relies on weights that are arbitrary (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w_g}=0.4$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w_i}=0.3$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w_c}=0.3$$\end{document}), this does not address questions about values. Where we prioritize fairness depends on our values. And our values may require us to prioritize measures differently based on legal mandates and our particular contexts. There are underlying trade-offs among particular measures: for example, increasing one measure of demographic parity may decrease another measure of predictive rate parity.

Fifth, our approach does not consider longitudinal studies of temporal dynamics and effects of feedback loops. We measure fairness based on static datasets for a snapshot moment in time. By contrast, a realistic use of AI for recruiting would be a real-time process. As pointed out in work done by Bauer et al.21, biased decisions lead to a creation of a feedback loop where retraining algorithms increase bias levels. We cannot tell whether our results are stable for several model rounds or whether a drift occurs with a changing composition of applicants.

Lastly, our assessment solely targets detection quality and fairness values but does not validate other elements of practical deployability: user acceptance among HR professionals in this process, bias mitigation recommendation actionability, legal adequacy according to current regulatory changes (such as EU AI Act), and cost-value for recommended bias mitigation. Without field and deployment studies, we cannot verify effective deployability of our technical progress.

Such limitations are extensive in restricting our external validity. We would recommend any organization thinking of applying our framework to perform a pilot test with their data before applying our model. We would also recommend that our model be considered more of a foundation for other methods of research and therefore be adapted and validated.

Legal, regulatory, and ethical considerations

Applying our framework in a practical recruitment scenario needs to be done while considering legal, regulatory, and ethical issues that go beyond technical considerations of fairness.

Legally speaking, our work engages with anti-discrimination laws that exist differently in various jurisdictions. Under United States law, for example, Title VII of the Civil Rights Act prohibits workplace discrimination and requires employers to comply with the “disparate impact” rule, where they must prove their selection criteria are job-related and justified when composed of groups protected by law. Although our disparate impact measure (DI) complies with the “80% rule” employed by the United States Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, European Union laws regulate our requirements more stringently via General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) laws and the pending AI Act in prohibits our transparency requirements and certain automated decisions. This is because legal sufficiency does not necessarily equate to mathematical sufficiency.

Handling data privacy and consent requires additional consideration. While our scheme necessitates access to and processing of sensitive data (gender, race, and age) for bias detection purposes, data protected by Article 9 of the GDPR is considered a special category that necessitates consent and a legitimate interest for processing. Additionally, data such as demographic information may be considered unlawful to collect in some countries. This requires privacy-preserving methods for data access and consent.

Alignment with Stakeholders: Alignment with stakeholder values introduces normative issues that our technical infrastructure does not address. Multiple stakeholders such as job applicants, HR staff, hiring managers, attorneys, and diversity officers may have varying levels of emphasis for different types of fairness. Individual fairness might be more important for applicants than for diversity officers. Our equality weights (Eq. 17) are a reflection of our values as researchers. They might vary for other stakeholders.

Thirdly, issues concerning potential errors of over-correction and under-correction should be taken into consideration. Over-correction could be a potential cause for reverse discrimination charges and/or may prefer less qualified applicants. On the other hand, failure to correct may continue to propagate inherent bias. While our approach supplies detection and methods for assessment, evaluation regarding appropriate levels of correction may vary in accordance with values and circumstances.

In other words, technical fairness alone does not constitute responsible deployment. This requires action on the part of organizations to involve legal counsel and ethics boards in converting our capabilities into legal and moral best practices for recruiting.

Future directions

Several promising directions warrant further investigation. First, extending from bias detection to automated bias mitigation would enable end-to-end fair AI development—automatically adjusting model parameters or reweighting training data upon detecting bias patterns. Second, addressing temporal dynamics through longitudinal studies could characterize how fairness guarantees evolve under continuous retraining and feedback loops, informing strategies for maintaining long-term fairness. Third, enhancing robustness to real-world data challenges—handling missing values, extracting features from unstructured text, and processing multimodal inputs—would bridge the gap between research datasets and production ATS deployments. Fourth, developing computationally efficient variants through model compression or federated learning could democratize access for resource-constrained organizations. Fifth, cross-cultural validation on non-Western datasets (Asia, Africa, Latin America) and non-tech sectors (healthcare, manufacturing, education) would establish broader applicability. Finally, the methodological framework integrating multi-task learning and causal inference could be adapted to other high-stakes domains including loan approval, criminal justice risk assessment, and medical diagnosis, advancing responsible AI across critical decision-making systems.

Conclusion

In order to deal with the algorithmic biases of AI hiring systems, this research suggests a deep learning-based bias detection model, making a contribution to a new avenue of algorithmic fairness research and practice. The key contributions of this study are three-fold: Firstly, a multi-task adversarial learning framework is developed, which realizes fine-grained intersectional bias detection via attention mechanisms, with 12–18% points improvement in detection accuracy; secondly, a multi-dimensional fairness assessment system is established, which encompasses nine metrics within three broad categories of group fairness, individual fairness, and causal fairness, with Pareto frontier analysis demonstrating the trade-off relationships between various fairness metrics; thirdly, an interpretability module incorporating SHAP values, gradient-weighted class activation mapping, and causal mediation analysis is embedded, realizing accurate localization and visualization of the sources of bias. Experimental findings confirm the efficacy of the suggested approach in bias detection accuracy, completeness of fairness analysis, and result interpretability. Future work can be done in the following directions: First, realize automated bias mitigation methods to enable end-to-end fairness optimization from bias detection, forming a complete closed loop for fair AI systems; second, increase the range of applications of the framework by applying the methodology to other high-stakes decision-making domains like loan approval, medical diagnosis, and college admission, examining the framework’s strength and generalizability; third, explore dynamic mechanisms of bias evolution, examine the long-term effect of bias under feedback loops, and develop fairness assurance mechanisms with the time factor taken into consideration. Through these efforts, this research will contribute to building more fair, transparent, and responsible artificial intelligence systems.
