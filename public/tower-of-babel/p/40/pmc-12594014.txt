Factors impacting medical students’ behavioral intentions and use behavior to use mobile applications for health care escort services (MA-HCES) in Chengdu, China
Authors: Min Shen, Yu Zhao, Ziyi Qin, Xu Jia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMobile applications for health care escort services (MA-HCES) have emerged as an innovative solution to assist patients during hospital visits and improve service delivery efficiency. Despite their growing presence, their adoption among specific populations such as medical students remains understudied. This research aimed to evaluate the effects of perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, attitudes, facilitating conditions and perceived behavioral control on behavioral intention and the utilization of MA-HCES among medical college students.MethodsEmploying a quantitative research design, data were collected through questionnaires distributed to the target population of medical college students. The data were analyzed and the research hypotheses were tested via the item-objective congruence (IOC) index, pilot test, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), and structural equation modeling (SEM) methods.ResultsFindings indicated that perceived usefulness exerted the most significant influence on the behavioral intention to utilize MA-HCES. Additionally, perceived ease of use, attitudes, facilitating conditions and perceived behavioral control had positive effects on college students’ behavioral intention to use MA-HCES. Consequently, behavioral intention was found to significantly influence actual use behavior.ConclusionsA survey was conducted among 511 medical college students to collect data, and a SEM was used to test the research model. The findings suggest that perceived ease of use, attitudes, facilitating conditions, and perceived behavioral control positively influence college students’ intention to utilize MA-HCES. Therefore, behavioral intention has a substantial effect on use behavior. This study introduces a novel model that can accurately forecast the behavioral intentions of medical college students to use MA-HCES, making valuable contributions to both practical application and theoretical understanding.Keywords: Mobile applications for health care escort services (MA-HCES), use behavior, behavioral intention

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study examined factors influencing medical students’ behavioral intentions toward mobile applications for healthcare escort services (MA-HCES) among 511 students at Chengdu Medical College. Structural equation modeling revealed that perceived usefulness was the strongest predictor of behavioral intention (β=0.282), followed by attitude (β=0.257), perceived ease of use (β=0.212), facilitating conditions (β=0.190), and perceived behavioral control (β=0.183). Behavioral intention significantly influenced actual use behavior (β=0.384).

What is known and what is new?

• Mobile health technologies can reduce healthcare costs and improve service quality. The technology acceptance model (TAM) and theory of planned behavior (TPB) are established frameworks for understanding technology adoption.

• This is the first comprehensive study examining MA-HCES adoption among medical students in China. The research uniquely integrates five predictors within a single framework tailored to healthcare escort services, providing empirical evidence that perceived usefulness is the most critical adoption factor.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• MA-HCES developers should prioritize system functionality that demonstrates clear utility and time-saving benefits. Educational institutions should implement training programs emphasizing practical MA-HCES benefits while addressing usability concerns. Healthcare organizations must establish supportive infrastructures and technical support systems. Policymakers should consider integrating MA-HCES into medical education curricula and establish quality assurance guidelines for mobile healthcare escort services to facilitate widespread adoption among future healthcare professionals.

Introduction

Mobile applications for healthcare escort services (MA-HCES) represent a novel approach to the delivery of healthcare services, utilizing a multitude of mobile devices as conduits (1,2). The objective is to improve the quality of patient care by providing companionship during medical appointments and facilitating the completion of tasks during medical visits (3,4). The MA-HCES represents a distinctive appointment service that offers a comprehensive range of medical services during a patient visit. These include home delivery, face-to-face consultation, registration, medication and report collection, and waiting for treatment. Recent data indicate that the global digital health market reached $549 billion in 2028—a clear sign of rapid growth and public interest in mobile health interventions (5). A number of private enterprises in China have recently initiated the provision of MA-HCES in response to the increasing demand for medical care workers. The initial MA-HCES was the Ipeizhen app, which was launched in 2015, followed by the Enjoy Companion app, the Youjia app, the Xiaoju app, the Chengyi app, and the Anxin app. Furthermore, internet medical applications such as Ping An Good Doctor (https://www.yjdjk.com/) have incorporated medical support services into their functionality.

MA-HCES offers innovative solutions to the obstacles individuals face in obtaining healthcare services. As reported, the deployment of mobile technologies in healthcare systems can result in a notable reduction in expenditure associated with healthcare operations and activities while simultaneously enhancing the quality of healthcare services (6). The advent of mobile technology has profoundly impacted the manner in which communication is conducted within healthcare settings, leading to notable improvements in service delivery, enhanced interoperability, cost reductions, more effective emergency response, quality assurance, and streamlined processes (7,8). Despite the anticipated advantages of MA-HCES, their market penetration remains relatively modest, largely due to restrictions in service registration. The obstacles that MA-HCES face in the market suggest that a number of factors impede the desire and intention to utilize them. These include limited access to technical support, concerns about strict confidentiality and protection of personal health information, and the inherently strict nature of healthcare processes.

Governments in developing countries are confronted with financial constraints and face challenges in providing adequate healthcare services to their citizens because of inadequate infrastructure (8,9). However, the healthcare service delivery process still needs to mature and evolve. It is recommended that further empirical studies be conducted to enhance the understanding of mHealth innovations. The findings of this research may facilitate the development of strategies to increase the utilization of mobile-based technologies by clinical patients and professionals.

Theories used in the study

The conceptual framework of this study was based on the following fundamental principles. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) was developed by Deng et al. (10). According to the TAM, researchers can infer a user’s feelings during product use and quantify the user’s intention to use the product based on the user’s acceptance of that product. Wang et al. held the belief that trust is made up of certain entities, such as individuals, systems, and institutions. According to trustworthiness theory (TT), networked information systems necessitate elevated degrees of trust (11). According to Santos et al. users may forego certain benefits in networked information systems when performing actions such as downloading, uploading, saving, and transferring information (12). Yang and Green introduced the concept of structural equation modeling (SEM). Many experts in the field of market research generally use SEMs for related research (13). Marcoulides and Falk proposed that the testing of SEMs is a validation process in which each variable and the corresponding hypothesis in the study must successfully meet various required criteria (14).

Introduction of variables

Facilitating conditions

Bouznif posited that supportive conditions entail learners’ conviction in the technology infrastructure’s capacity to furnish an environment conducive to e-health (15). Rahmani et al. defined the facilitation state as an environment supported by technology and organizational infrastructure that enables undergraduates to utilize e-health (16). Contextual factors were found to influence the perceived difficulty of the task. Venkatesh et al. defined the facilitation condition as the perceived utilization of organizational and technological infrastructure to support the implementation of new systems (17). Cronin and Taylor posited that resource conceptions are interconnected elements influencing external control of perception (18).

As evidenced by Selim’s 2003 research, a significant number of academics emphasized the pivotal role of support in the acceptance paradigm, encompassing assistance, accessibility, and the reliability of online resources (19). In addition, technical components such as fully equipped classroom computer laboratories were introduced to facilitate the learning process. Furthermore, the study examined additional variables related to the process, including the necessity of sufficient computer resources and the dependability of various elements. The absence of facilitating conditions may present obstacles for users in adopting new technologies. The assessment of facilitating conditions has been identified as a significant factor influencing the decision to adopt a system (20,21).

Consistent with these findings, Fleddermann et al. [2024] reported that organizational-level supports—including staff training, technical assistance, and telehealth champions—were among the most critical factors for maintaining and promoting telehealth use in behavioral healthcare settings (22). Recent empirical research from developing healthcare contexts underscores the pivotal role of facilitating conditions in shaping technology acceptance. For instance, Hailu et al. [2025] reported that facilitating conditions exerted a significant positive influence (β=0.249, P<0.001) on healthcare professionals’ adoption of telemedicine services, emphasizing the essential contribution of both supportive infrastructure and organizational resources (23) Extending this perspective, Alasmari et al. [2024] argued that in the context of digital healthcare transformation, facilitating conditions are no longer limited to basic technical infrastructure but also include organizational readiness, institutional policy backing, and targeted educational interventions (24). Reinforcing these insights, a study on medical faculty members revealed that facilitating conditions—together with other UTAUT constructs—directly and significantly influenced e-learning adoption, thereby highlighting the integral role of institutional support in successful educational technology implementation (25).

Perceived ease of use

In their seminal work, Davis et al. defined perceived ease of use as a user’s perception of how easy a system is to use. This concept illustrates the degree of compatibility between a user and the electronic equipment they are using (26). Venkatesh et al. [2003] established that perceived ease of use is a consequence of user self-efficacy in comprehending and utilizing new information systems (27). Furthermore, Curran and Meuter proposed that perceived ease of use is reflected in the feedback users receive from their experience with a device (28). This feedback information encompasses the user’s subjective assessment of the complexity of the device.

In a nationwide study of over 1,000 participants, the study found that perceived ease of use was significantly associated with a higher likelihood of seeking telemedicine consultation (β=0.118, P=0.01), confirming its role as a key determinant in healthcare technology adoption (29).

A study by Kim demonstrated that the perceived utility and simplicity of a product or service have a significant effect on the attitudes and behavioral intentions of consumers in the health sector (30). The attitudes and behavioral intentions of health consumers were found to be indirectly influenced by factors such as subjective norms, health status, health concerns, health information technology (HIT) characteristics, and HIT self-efficacy. Mostafa Al-Emran and Khaled Shaalan determined that users prefer to employ systems or software that provide a positive perceived ease of use experience (31). Research by Tuzovic and Kuppelwieser [2016] indicated that participants were more likely to adopt and utilize electronic devices if they found them to be straightforward to use (32). Recent empirical research in healthcare settings has demonstrated the critical mediating role of perceived ease of use in technology adoption. A comprehensive study among healthcare nurses revealed that perceived ease of use significantly mediates the relationship between technology characteristics and adoption intention, with users showing greater willingness to adopt systems perceived as straightforward and performance-enhancing (33). Li et al. evaluated the impact of positive perceived ease of use on customers’ experiences with new software and systems, affecting client satisfaction (34). A study revealed that information system self-efficacy has a significant effect on the perceived ease of use of information systems. The perceived ease of use had a significant effect on individuals experiencing anxiety surrounding change (35).

Koenig-Lewis et al. reported that previous studies concentrated on elucidating the reasons behind users’ resistance to novel information platforms (36). The focus of their research was on the perceived ease of use of new information platforms by users. In a study which the ease of use of new information platforms by consumers was assessed (37). Chen et al. reported that consumers’ perception of the ease of use of a new information system was a significant factor influencing their resistance behavior (38).

Recent qualitative research in healthcare settings has expanded the conceptualization of perceived ease of use to include system integration and support accessibility. Studies in specialized healthcare contexts, such as palliative care, demonstrate that ease of use encompasses not only interface simplicity but also seamless integration with existing IT infrastructure and availability of technical support resources (39). Large-scale empirical evidence from healthcare institutions confirms the significant positive role of perceived ease of use in health information system adoption. A study involving 602 healthcare workers across 20 hospitals demonstrated that perceived ease of use, alongside perceived usefulness, serves as a fundamental determinant of adoption decisions in complex healthcare environments (40).

Attitude

In their 1989 study, Davis and colleagues defined attitude as a comprehensive evaluation of a user’s positive or negative subjective intentions following the utilization of a product (26). Bohner and Dickel [2011] conducted research in the field of computers and information technology (41). In accordance with the conceptualization proposed that an individual’s attitude can be defined as their perspective on a system and their evaluation of its usefulness (25). In other words, attitude can be viewed as a user’s evaluation of the suitability of utilizing a specific information system application. Tao et al. identified a correlation between an individual’s attitude and their level of interest and concern regarding the future utilization of the system (42). The authors asserted that attitudes are subjective appraisals of users. User assessments are contingent upon their knowledge of computer systems and information technology. The focus of research conducted by Lam et al. was the information systems utilized by organizations within the service sector (43). They posited that an individual’s attitude toward a system is contingent upon their preparedness to utilize it, taking into account factors such as the system’s functional capabilities and its alignment with their specific requirements. An individual’s attitude can be defined as their opinion regarding the efficacy of their actions in a specific context (44,45). In accordance with the definition proposed by Spark and Willis [2014], attitudes may also be defined as the level of commitment to utilizing a specific object or action (46). An individual’s attitude can be defined as their opinion on the efficacy of their actions in a given situation (47).

Wang et al. [2022] emphasized the considerable impact of the perception of the technology’s utility and ease of use on attitudes and the intention to use the technology in question (48). Hsu et al. reported that perceived ease of use significantly influences individual attitudes (49).

Or et al. posited that individuals are more inclined to utilize attitudes as a means of elucidating their sentiments pertaining to themselves and others than other variables, such as satisfaction, because customer attitudes are influenced by their emotions, which identified as one of the primary factors affecting customer behavioral intention (50). Hsu and Lin posited that an individual’s attitude can be used to predict their assessment of the importance of adopting new products, technologies, and techniques, as well as the extent of assistance they perceive as helpful (51).

In their study, investigated the influence of users’ intentions and attitudes toward computer utilization on their learning outcomes. An optimistic disposition positively influences the capacity of users to transcend their emotional responses when novel information systems are utilized (52). Mao et al. investigated the motivation and attitudes of users with respect to the adoption of new technologies. It was established that user perspectives exert a considerable influence on the acceptance of new technology (53).

Perceived usefulness

Davis [1989] posited that the perceived usefulness of a given innovation, such as new technology, is contingent upon the user’s belief that it will facilitate the completion of tasks more expediently or provide timely information that will enhance their work performance (26). Krebs and Duncan [2015] suggested that a specific strategy could be employed to improve the quality of one’s work (54). The perceived ease of use was identified as a significant component in the determination and measurement of user satisfaction with the utilization of information technology. Cigdem and Topcu defined perceived usefulness as a subjective assessment by users of the utility of using information technology (55). The behavior of users in utilizing information technology was influenced by their perception of its usefulness (56). The findings indicate that an individual’s perceived usefulness exerts considerable influence on their attitudes and behavioral intentions (57). Arbaugh JB demonstrated that consumers’ intention to use and subsequent behavior are influenced by their perception of the utility of the product or service in question. The perceived usefulness of the information network learning environment affected the level of immersion experienced by customers (58). Akinnuwesi posited that perceived utility exerts a direct influence on customer satisfaction when customers access online information platforms (59).

Arbaugh investigated the relationship between a range of variables and customer satisfaction. The research findings indicated that perceived usefulness has a significant effect on usersatisfaction (58). The perceived ease of use was found to be reflected in the depth, trustworthiness, and professionalism of users’ experience when accessing online information and data platforms. Liang et al. identified a correlation between perceived usefulness and user pleasure (60).

H4: perceived usefulness significantly affects university students’ behavioral intention to use the MA-HCES.

Perceived behavioral control

The concept of “perceived behavioral control” refers to an individual’s perception of the extent to which they are able to control the resources required to perform a specific action (61). Perceived behavioral control is a construct associated with the facilitating conditions in the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), and it reflects an individual’s perceived control over their own behavior. This study sought to gain insight into patients’ perspectives on the internal and external limitations of utilizing HIT in relation to perceived behavioral control (39). Ajzen [1991] proposed that an individual’s behavior is shaped by their behavioral intention, which is in turn informed by their attitude, subjective norms and perceived behavioral control with respect to the behavior in question (62). Perceived behavioral control examines the factors that may facilitate or impede behavioral performance. This study examines the relationships between perceived behavioral control and confidence and certainty in completing a specific task (63).

The extant literature on rational behavior indicates that attitudes and perceived behavioral control exert a direct influence on an individual’s behavioral intentions. Nevertheless, even if individuals hold favorable attitudes and cognitive norms regarding a given behavior but lack the self-efficacy to engage in that behavior, they are unlikely to do so (53,64). Perceived behavioral control may act as a moderator between behavioral intention and normative and attitudinal influences. When a significant correlation is observed between attitudes, norms, and intentions, it can be inferred that individuals’ intentions are likely to be greater when they perceive themselves to have greater perceived behavioral control (65). Empirical evidence from healthcare behavior research demonstrates the critical role of perceived behavioral control in patient self-care management. Recent studies in chronic disease contexts show that patients’ perceived control over health-related behaviors significantly predicts both intention and actual behavior, with substantial effect sizes (b =1.283±0.095, P<0.001). Importantly, research indicates that perceived behavioral control may have differential effects across demographic groups, with particular importance for vulnerable populations such as elderly patients and women (b =0.885±0.442, P=0.045) (66). This finding that norms and attitudes better explains an individual’s intention when the action is perceived to be under their control— is consistent with previous research (67).

Recent reviews of theory of planned behavior (TPB) research underscore that perceived behavioral control is shaped not only by internal psychological capacities (e.g., self-efficacy, motivation) but also by external sociostructural constraints such as healthcare infrastructure, digital access, and institutional support. This multidimensional perspective reflects an expanded understanding of control beliefs, particularly in healthcare technology contexts (68).

H5: perceived behavioral control significantly affects university students’ behavioral intentions to use the MA-HCES.

Behavioral intention

Fishbein and Ajzen proposed that behavioral intention represents the initial factor in determining the actual adoption of new technology by users (47). In their 2021 study, Tomczyk and colleagues defined behavioral intention as the user’s subjective intention. This subjective intention can prompt a specific behavioral response from the user. For example, TPB and UTAUT models have explained up to 63% of variance in adoption intentions, demonstrating the predictive strength of behavioral intention in health app usage (69). Behavioral intention is considered a foundational concept and is widely used in numerous theoretical frameworks, including TRA, TAM, and UTAUT. The term is defined as the probability that a user will engage in a specific activity. A substantial body of research has demonstrated that behavioral intention is a reliable predictor of an individual’s actual conduct (70). Zhang and Liu investigated users’ intentions to utilize new information systems from a behavioral perspective. Researchers have established that factors such as the user’s perception of the system’s ease of use and usability can influence the user’s propensity to engage in specific behaviors (67).

Use behavior

As posited by Lewi PJ, the behavioral aspect is of paramount importance in evaluating the dependability and honesty of collaborative relationships, typically based on qualities such as durability, equity, neutrality, amiability, and willingness. The behavioral aspect was a pivotal element in the construction of the model (71). Furthermore, commercial digital health tools have been incorporated into evidence-based interventions precisely due to their demonstrated ability to facilitate behavior change techniques, underscoring the relevance of actual use behavior in MA-HCES contexts (72). Hsieh et al. [2008] proposed that use behavior is the extent to which individuals acknowledge and feel emotionally safe about a particular thing (73). The use of behavioral conduct in collaborative relationships has been demonstrated to influence the satisfaction of partners with the collaborative process itself. Elwalda et al. studied digital platforms and regarded them as sociotechnical systems (STSs) composed of mutually reinforcing enterprises, dominant entities, and end customers (74). The fundamental rationale was based on the premise of information exchange, contingent on the behavioral characteristics of the parties involved. Shankar posited that use behavior plays a pivotal role in facilitating connections between customers and vendors Customer satisfaction and loyalty in online and offline environments (75). Researchers have evaluated the socioeconomic variables that influence users’ confidence in the platform. Use behavior is a pivotal factor in facilitating business transactions between dominant corporations, complementary firms, and farmers. In a study published in 2018, Tao and colleagues investigated the role of renewable energy service platforms (42).

The term “use behavior in a mHealth environment’ refers to an individual’s perception that mHealth technology has the attributes needed to perform as expected under specific conditions (76). The concept of user behavior has been incorporated into a multitude of healthcare research studies, demonstrating a significant influence on the uptake of e-healthcare (10).

H6: behavioral intention significantly affects use behavior among university students using MA-HCES.

Methods

Research framework

The research framework was constructed using four previous theoretical frameworks as a basis for its development. The initial theoretical framework was developed by Xu et al. (77), in which two factors were identified as influential: facilitating conditions and behavioral intentions. The second theoretical framework was proposed by Abdekhoda, who identified three variables as significant: perceived ease of use, use behavior and perceived utility (25). Wang et al. subsequently developed a third theoretical framework, which the present study adopts by incorporating five factors—attitude, perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, behavioral intention, and perceived behavioral control—to elucidates the relationships among these variables (11). The fourth theoretical framework was derived from a study conducted by Faqih (78) and employed four variables. The aforementioned theoretical frameworks were based on the following four variables: perceived ease of use, use behavior, perceived usefulness and behavioral intention. Methodology

The objective of this study was to examine the behavioral intentions and factors that influence the utilization of MA-HCES-assisted medical care among medical students at Chengdu Medical University (Hangzhou Lianke Meixing Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd; Dingxiangyisheng, 11.31.0.). The survey was conducted among unmarried college students residing in three universities in Chengdu, China. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics and Research Committee of Chengdu Medical College (No. 23052). All participants agreed to participate and provided their informed consent. The survey process commenced with creating an online questionnaire on the WeChat platform, which was then distributed to a selected group. The responses were subsequently exported to an Excel spreadsheet via WeChat forms. The collected data were then subjected to TAM testing as proposed by Kim (56). Similarly, the data must be aligned with the Technology Intention Model (TIM) proposed by Davis and the Trust Theory put forth by DeLone and McLean (26). SEM is employed to analyze and validate variables, including perceived ease of use, use behavior, perceived usefulness, attitudes, facilitating conditions, and perceived behavioral control.

This study employed a quantitative methodology, with data collected via the distribution of online questionnaires to the target population. The questionnaire comprised three sections: screening questions; items on factors affecting medical college students’ intention to use MA-HCES and demographic questions. To measure the variables represented in the scale, a 5-point Likert scale was employed, with the following responses: 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

Prior to the distribution of the online questionnaires, the researcher conducted an item-objective congruence (IOC) test, sending the scale items for all seven variables to three experts who were familiar with the research topics (Table S1). The purpose of this test was to assess the content validity of the research. Additionally, the researcher conducted a pilot test by distributing questionnaires to 30 members of the target population to assess the reliability of the research, utilizing Cronbach’s alpha measurement. Once reliability and content validity were confirmed, the researcher distributed the questionnaire to the target population and collected of 511 valid responses for analysis. The data were analyzed via the statistical software packages SPSS and AMOS, which were employed to process the results of the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and SEM. During SEM analysis, the researcher conducted separate examinations of the predicted factor structure, model fit indices, and theoretical coherence to ensure both statistical robustness and conceptual alignment.

Target population and sample size

Medical students from four majors at Chengdu Medical College were selected to participate in the MA-HCES program. Nursing, School of Nursing; Clinical Medicine, school of clinical medicine; Pharmacy, School of Pharmaceutical Sciences; Medical laboratory technique, College of laboratory medicine. The sample size of students in these four majors is adequate for this investigation. The researchers distributed questionnaires to students enrolled in each of the four academic majors under investigation. The data collected indicate the factors that influence the intention of Chengdu medical students to utilize MA-HCES. The four specialties are nursing, clinical medicine, pharmacy and medical laboratory technology. Chengdu Medical College (CMC) is located in the Xindu District of Chengdu City, Sichuan Province, China. It is a full-time medical college offering higher education.

Sekaran and Bougie defined ’sample size’ as a representative subset of the target population used to conduct a study (79). Given that there were 7 latent variables and 27 observed variables, the calculator devised by Pourmand indicated that the minimum sample size should be 425. To obtain a valid sample, the researcher distributed 600 questionnaires and used 511 completed questionnaires for analysis (66).

Sampling procedures

The target population for this study was defined as medical college students at Chengdu Medical College in Sichuan Province who had used MA-HCES. Consequently, the researcher employed purposive and convenience sampling techniques to select and reach the target sample. Purposive sampling is the process of selecting a target population that is capable of satisfying the research requirements and fulfilling the role of a research object (44). The initial stage of the study involved the selection of students from four different majors at Chengdu Medical College. Stratified sampling was subsequently employed to collect data proportionately on the basis of the size of the four majors. Data analysis

Data analysis employed IBM SPSS (descriptive statistics, reliability testing) and AMOS (SEM). The process prioritized data quality validation, model testing, and hypothesis verification.

Preliminary data screening and descriptive statistics

Invalid questionnaires (incomplete responses, uniform answers) were excluded first. Descriptive statistics summarized two key aspects:

Sample demographic profiling: categorical variables (gender, major, academic year) were analyzed via frequencies/percentages; continuous variables (MA-HCES usage duration/frequency) via mean/standard deviation.

Variable distribution and normality test: latent variables (e.g., PEOU, BI) and indicators were summarized with mean/standard deviation. Normality—critical for parametric SEM—was assessed via skewness (−1 to 1) and kurtosis (−2 to 2).

SEM

SEM tested the conceptual framework via two-step approach: measurement model validation (CFA) followed by structural model testing.

Structural model analysis: the model tested six hypotheses (e.g., FC→BI, BI→UB):

❖ Paths were specified to reflect hypotheses; AMOS calculated standardized path coefficients (positive/negative for association direction); significance was tested via P values (α=0.05) and CR ≥1.96.

❖ Paths were specified to reflect hypotheses; AMOS calculated standardized path coefficients (positive/negative for association direction); significance was tested via P values (α=0.05) and CR ≥1.96.

Model fit assessment: fit was evaluated via absolute and incremental indices: absolute: χ2/df <5, GFI >0.85, AGFI >0.80, RMSEA <0.08; incremental: NFI >0.80, CFI >0.80, TLI >0.80. Poorly fitting models were modified (e.g., removing low-loading items) per theory and AMOS indices.

Hypothesis testing

Hypotheses (H1–H6) were tested via path significance: Supported: P<0.05 (meaningful relationship); Rejected: P≥0.05 (no relationship). Results included path coefficients, standard errors, CR, and P values for transparency.

Discussion

Pourmand [2006] posited that the objective of CFA is to ascertain the relevance of observed variables to latent variables. Confirmation of convergent validity (factor loading, composite reliability, average variance extracted) and discriminant validity may be achieved through the utilization of CFA (66). In accordance with the methodology proposed by Fornell and Larcker, discriminant validity was evaluated by computing the square root of each AVE and comparing it with the factor correlations (76).

Yang defined a SEM as a statistical approach for evaluating relationships within a set of structural equations (13). The assessment of SEM encompasses the evaluation of the model’s fit and the examination of the influence exerted by one variable on another. From a statistical perspective, the SEM assesses and approximates causality through the use of transverse analysis and comprehensive factor analysis (81,82). As Pourmand G stated, SEM is employed to observe the research model and to ascertain the discriminant validity and internal consistency of the test variables (66).

According to the findings, the perceived usefulness of MA-HCES was the factor that had the greatest impact on the behavioral intention to use these applications. It was found that college students’ behavioral intention to use MA-HCES was positively influenced by perceived ease of use, attitudes, facilitating conditions, and perceived behavioral control. Consequently, behavioral intention has a significant influence on use behavior.

Limitations and further study

This study presents a report on the utilization of MA-HCES by medical college students. However, it is important to acknowledge that several limitations require additional exploration. This study’s survey sample consisted exclusively of current medical students, which may not represent the broader and more diverse user base of MA-HCES. During the questionnaire study conducted with MA-HCES customers, a portion of young individuals utilized the service to provide companionship to their middle-aged and elderly parents. Investigating the underlying motivations of adult children who use MA-HCES while accompanying their parents could be a promising area of study for future research.

Furthermore, the majority of participants in this study had a limited understanding of MA-HCES. Moreover, the research focused solely on their behavioral intentions with respect to the utilization of MA-HCES, which may only partially mirror the actual usage by students. Hence, future research should focus on actual users who have a deep understanding of and extensive experience with MA-HCES, and examine their behavior to obtain more precise results. Furthermore, the study failed to account for other possible variables that may influence MA-HCES behavioral intentions, such as the platform’s pricing and individuals’ self-perception of their health. Future studies should utilize alternative technology acceptance models and variables to examine the correlation between those variables and the inclination to use MA-HCES.

While this study achieved a high response rate (92.3%), the possibility of non-responder bias cannot be entirely ruled out. Non-participants might systematically differ from respondents in key characteristics (e.g., lower technology adoption, stronger privacy concerns, or reduced interest in MA-HCES). This could limit the generalizability of findings, particularly regarding the strength of relationships between constructs like perceived usefulness and behavioral intention. Future studies could employ targeted follow-ups with non-respondents to assess potential systematic differences.

Conclusions

The researcher aimed to investigate the factors influencing college students’ intention to use MA-HCES apps. The study’s sampling units consisted of students from four different majors at Chengdu Medical College in Sichuan Province, China. The study examined seven variables and six hypotheses to explore how factors such as perceived ease of use, facilitating conditions, perceived usefulness, attitude, and perceived behavioral control influence behavioral intention and use behavior in the context of MA-HCES. This study employed a quantitative research design, utilizing a questionnaire as the primary tool for data collection. The content validity and reliability of the proposed conceptual framework were assessed via IOC, a pilot test, CFA, and SEM.

The following is a condensed version of the conclusions derived from the statistical analysis: the findings demonstrate that facilitating conditions, perceived ease of use, attitudes, perceived usefulness, and perceived behavioral control significantly influence students’ behavioral intentions to use MA-HCES. These results align with the TPB and prior research in the field of mHealth. Importantly, perceived usefulness had the strongest positive impact on behavioral intention. Attitude was a significant factor in shaping behavioral intentions, underscoring the need to uphold a favorable outlook toward MA-HCES, as it greatly enhances the probability of student utilization. In addition, perceived behavioral control was positively correlated with behavioral intention.

Finally, the study hypothesized that a strong behavioral intention would lead to greater actual use of the applications, and the results confirmed this expectations.

Implications

Based on the findings of this investigation, the following are some theoretical implications. In the first place, the results of this research contribute to a deeper comprehension of the processes that are involved in the operation of MA-HCES. Kim came up with the idea for the TAM (30. Venkatesh proposed two theories: Information System Success Theory (ISST) and TT (27). This paper analyzed and validated relevant variables using SEM, including Chinese university medical students’ attitudes toward using the MA-HCES, their use behavioral, attitude, perceived ease of use, facilitating conditions, perceived usefulness, and perceived behavioral control.

The TAM was initially presented by Fred D. Davis and colleagues in 1989 (Perceived Usefulness, Perceived Ease of Use, and User Acceptance of Information Technology) (26). According to TAM, one can infer a user’s feelings about using a product and quantify their intention to use it, based on the user’s acceptance of the product. A study that was based on the TAM model was carried out by Wixom and colleagues in the year 2005 (A Theoretical Integration of User Satisfaction and Technology Acceptance) (83). They studied the relationship between the acceptance of technology by users and the level of satisfaction experienced by users.

This paper paid special attention to the Facilitating conditions by medical students when using the MA-HCES. The concept known as TIM was utilized in their research. The research found that facilitating conditions had a substantial impact on individuals’ behavioral intentions to adopt the technology. In fact, facilitating conditions proved to be a crucial factor in whether users would embrace a new technology.

Secondly, the results demonstrated that when university medical students used MA-HCES, perceived ease of use had a significant impact on their behavioral intentions. The researcher’s quantification of the role of mediators in this operational mechanism had significant theoretical implications. This study employed SEM to conduct factor-based and regression analysis. Elwalda contended that the applications of SEM in research within the management sector are only partially utilized. He conducted a comprehensive review of 11 highly regarded management publications, covering the period up until the end of 2013 (74). Researchers commonly employ SEM as a research tool, as indicated by the results. This study specifically examined the use of SEM with regards to data analysis, model characteristics, and the evaluation and reporting of models.

Furthermore, the findings of this study would offer fresh perspectives on the factors that influence the behavioral intentions of medical students who utilize the MA-HCES at Universities in Chengdu, China. This study employed a quantitative approach to measure the factors outlined in the conceptual framework. The study’s findings demonstrated the correlation between the parameters. Constructing the model confirmed the presence of an internal operational mechanism. By adjusted the relevant factors, the researchers were able to successfully influence certain elements within this mechanism. According to McCubrey JA, the ISST has been extensively utilized in electronic commerce research (70).

Fourth, in their research, Nguyen examined the influence of user behavior on online information systems (39). Marcoulides discovered that there is a direct influence of Behavior Intention on Use behavior. Behavior intention played a crucial role in determining how users interacted with mobile information sites (14). Santos BD argued that a transparent and customer-oriented approach has an impact on the creation of web-based information systems (12). Behavior Intention emerged as a robust predictor of web-based information systems. This research specifically examines the management implications of the system, using TT as its basis. The primary purpose of the MA-HCES was to revolutionize communication in healthcare settings by strengthening service delivery, promoting collaboration, decreasing expenses, adjusting to emergencies, assuring high standards, and streamlining operations.
