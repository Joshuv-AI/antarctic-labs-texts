Rapid food desensitization supported by omalizumab, with adjunctive dupilumab for type 2 comorbidities: A pediatric case series☆
Authors: Pedro Giavina-Bianchi, Bruna Giavina-Bianchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe therapeutic landscape of IgE-mediated food allergy is expanding, and Rapid Food Desensitization (RFD) has been proposed as an optional and innovative procedural strategy to provide rapid protection in selected high-risk patients. However, real-world evidence integrating RFD with biologic therapy remains scarce, and its optimal role within contemporary management strategies is still evolving.ObjectiveTo describe a real-world pediatric case series of RFD supported by targeted biologic therapy in children with severe IgE-mediated food allergy, including those with additional Type 2 inflammatory multimorbidities.MethodsWe conducted a prospective real-world case series of 3 consecutive pediatric cases managed under a standardized institutional RFD protocol. Clinical data were collected prospectively at each visit and during RFD using a standardized clinical report form within REDCap. All patients received omalizumab prior to RFD, and those with prominent Type 2–driven diseases also received dupilumab. Clinical evolution, desensitization outcomes, safety, and maintenance of daily ingestion following transition to oral immunotherapy (OIT) were evaluated.ResultsAll 3 patients had a history of severe food-induced anaphylaxis, and 2 had clinically relevant Type 2 multimorbidities (atopic dermatitis or eosinophilic esophagitis). Omalizumab enabled RFD to be performed safely by suppressing the immediate IgE-mediated response, while dupilumab was used reactively in selected cases to control persistent Type 2 inflammation. All patients achieved successful desensitization without severe breakthrough reactions and subsequently transitioned to OIT. At latest follow-up, all maintained daily ingestion of desensitized foods, with only mild, manageable symptoms in 1 case.ConclusionThis real-world pediatric case series suggests that RFD, when supported by omalizumab, is a feasible, safe, and innovative—yet optional—procedural approach for carefully selected high-risk patients. Omalizumab facilitated rapid protection, and targeted use of dupilumab may provide additional benefit in patients with overlapping Type 2 inflammatory pathways. Given the very small sample size and absence of a control group, these findings are hypothesis-generating. Larger, rigorously designed prospective studies are needed to refine patient selection, timing of biologics, and long-term outcomes of this evolving strategy.Keywords: Anaphylaxis, Rapid food desensitization, Food allergy, Omalizumab, Dupilumab, Immunomodulation, Monoclonal antibody, IgE

Introduction

The prevalence and clinical impact of food allergy have risen steadily over recent decades, increasing the need for individualized and safer management strategies. Traditionally, food allergy has been managed with strict elimination diets; however, this approach carries a substantial burden in terms of quality of life, anxiety related to accidental exposures, and nutritional restrictions. Over the past years, oral immunotherapy (OIT) and biologic therapies—particularly omalizumab—have emerged as alternative or complementary approaches in selected patients.1,2

Food allergy encompasses a broad spectrum of clinical phenotypes, ranging from mild symptoms to severe, life-threatening anaphylaxis. This heterogeneity requires careful patient selection when considering active interventions such as desensitization or biologic therapy. In addition to clinical efficacy, issues of safety, feasibility, and accessibility must be considered when proposing novel therapeutic strategies.1,2

Food allergy is characterized by both immediate hypersensitivity IgE-mediated reactions, mediated by mast cells and basophils, and delayed Type 2 inflammatory responses involving Th2 cells and eosinophils. These pathways contribute not only to acute reactions but also to chronic Type 2–driven multimorbidities such as atopic dermatitis and eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE), forming part of the broader Type 2 syndrome that we have recently defined.3 Understanding this dual mechanism provides a rationale for exploring approaches that target both IgE-mediated reactivity and downstream Type 2 inflammation.1,3 Recognizing this network clarifies the mechanistic underpinnings the Type 2 syndrome and provides a rational basis for innovative interventions, such as rapid food desensitization (RFD) in combination with IgE- and Type 2–targeted biologics, offering a strategy to control both acute and chronic allergic inflammation.4

Rapid drug desensitization (RDD) is a well-established procedure in clinical allergy that induces a temporary state of tolerance to a previously reactive medication through controlled, stepwise dose escalation under close medical supervision. The goal is to allow safe administration of a necessary drug while minimizing the risk of severe reactions. The mechanisms underlying RDD are multifaceted but center on the temporary inhibition of mast cells and basophils—distinct from allergen immunotherapy (aeroallergens or oral food immunotherapy), in which regulatory T-cell (Treg) induction plays a key role5, 6, 7. More recently, similar principles have been adapted to food allergy in the form of RFD, performed in specialized settings.4

Omalizumab, an anti-IgE monoclonal antibody, reduces circulating free IgE and downregulates FcεRI expression on mast cells and basophils, thereby decreasing their reactivity to allergens.8,9 Its role as an adjunct to food desensitization and OIT has been increasingly investigated, and it is now approved in some countries to reduce the risk of reactions to accidental food exposure.10 Additionally, omalizumab has emerged as a valuable adjunct to allergen immunotherapy—improving safety, reducing systemic reactions, and potentially enhancing treatment efficacy.4

Dupilumab is a fully human monoclonal antibody developed to suppress the Type 2 inflammatory cascade by blocking the shared IL-4Rα receptor subunit, thereby inhibiting downstream signaling of both interleukin-4 (IL-4) and interleukin-13 (IL-13) 2 central, redundant cytokines that orchestrate Type 2 immunity. By inhibiting these pathways, dupilumab attenuates essential immunologic processes, including IgE class switching, eosinophil recruitment, epithelial barrier dysfunction, and mucus hypersecretion. This broad and coordinated blockade has established dupilumab as a transformative therapy for multiple Type 2–driven diseases, such as atopic dermatitis and eosinophilic esophagitis, where it has led to substantial improvements in disease control and patient quality of life.11,12 Its role in food allergy remains exploratory, but it may be beneficial in patients with significant Type 2 multimorbidities.

In this study, we report a small pediatric case series describing the use of RFD supported by omalizumab in 3 high-risk patients, with adjunctive dupilumab used in 2 cases for clear clinical indications related to Type 2 multimorbidities. Our aim is to share our real-world clinical experience, recognizing that these observations are descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

Methods

We conducted a prospective observational case series involving 3 pediatric patients with severe IgE-mediated food allergy, all managed according to a clinical protocol developed by our group.4 The study was reviewed and approved by our institutional ethics committee. Patients receiving care in our tertiary allergy center were invited to participate, and those whose parents or legal guardians agreed were included after signing the informed consent form.

Patient selection was based on individualized clinical assessment rather than predefined experimental criteria. Patients were considered “high risk” when they presented 1 or more of the following features: (i) a documented history of severe anaphylaxis, particularly with respiratory involvement and/or hemodynamic instability (present in Cases 1 and 3); (ii) multiple prior systemic reactions or a high perceived risk of accidental exposure; (iii) clinically relevant Type 2 comorbidities, when applicable; and (iv) multidisciplinary evaluation in a specialized tertiary care setting.

Before referral to our center, all 3 patients had been managed exclusively with strict dietary avoidance. Conventional OIT was discussed as an alternative; however, given the severity of prior reactions and the anticipated risks of standard up-dosing protocols, RFD supported by omalizumab was considered more appropriate in these selected cases.

The RFD protocol was conducted in a hospital setting with continuous clinical monitoring and the presence of trained medical staff with immediate access to emergency treatment if needed. The procedure followed a stepwise dose-escalation approach over a short period (typically 24–48 h), based on our previously published protocol.4 Using cow's milk as an illustrative example, initial doses ranged from approximately 0.05–0.1 mL, with subsequent steps generally doubling the administered amount. Shorter intervals of about 15 min were used between the earliest steps, while longer intervals of up to 2 h were applied between later steps as doses increased. The target final dose ranged from approximately 30 to 100 mL, depending on the clinical context and patient tolerance.

As per protocol, all participants were initiated on omalizumab and became eligible to undergo RFD only after completing at least 4 months of continuous biological treatment. Following RFD, all patients systematically transitioned to oral immunotherapy (OIT). Patients who achieved successful desensitization were instructed to maintain unrestricted intake of the involved food(s), with guaranteed daily ingestion to induce long-term immunotolerance.

Clinical assessments included baseline characterization, monitoring of immediate and delayed hypersensitivity reactions, procedural safety surveillance during RFD and OIT, and longitudinal follow-up once sustained ingestion was established. Prospective data were systematically collected at each clinical visit and throughout the RFD procedure using a standardized case report form specifically designed for this protocol and managed via the REDCap electronic data capture system.

Results

A total of 3 pediatric patients with severe IgE-mediated food allergy were prospectively followed as part of a descriptive case series under our real-world clinical protocol combining omalizumab to enable rapid food desensitization (RFD), followed by oral immunotherapy (OIT) and daily maintenance ingestion. All patients completed the planned clinical approach without treatment-limiting adverse events. The main demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Desensitization outcomes

All 3 patients underwent successful RFD to their respective culprit foods after at least 4 months of omalizumab therapy in a closely monitored hospital setting (Table 2). The first patient, previously reported,4 completed milk RFD and sustained daily intake of 250 mL of milk. The second patient underwent RFD to 3 foods (milk, egg, and fish) without severe breakthrough reactions. The third patient successfully underwent sequential RFD to milk, egg, and peanut over a 48-h period, with only mild, transient symptoms during the protocol.

Following RFD, all patients transitioned to OIT and continued daily ingestion of their desensitized foods. No systemic reactions requiring epinephrine were observed during the RFD or early OIT phases.

Adverse events and safety profile

Mild and transient symptoms were observed in 2 cases during RFD (Table 2). In Case 1, the patient experienced mild oropharyngeal pruritus that resolved spontaneously. In Case 3, mild oropharyngeal pruritus occurred during the early steps of milk desensitization, and mild eyelid angioedema was observed at the end of peanut desensitization; all symptoms resolved spontaneously or with oral antihistamines. During follow-up after RFD, the patient in Case 1 developed new-onset dysphagia and retrosternal pain 7 months after achieving daily milk ingestion and was subsequently diagnosed with eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE). This occurred despite ongoing omalizumab therapy, suggesting persistent Type 2–driven esophageal inflammation that was not fully controlled by IgE blockade alone.

No patient experienced systemic reactions requiring escalation of care, treatment discontinuation, or clear adverse events directly attributable to biologic therapy in this limited cohort.

Impact of combined biologic therapy

Together, these real-world observations highlight the potential value of combined IgE and Type 2 pathway inhibition in managing complex food allergy phenotypes. Two patients (Cases 1 and 2) received combined biologic therapy with omalizumab and dupilumab, the later for individualized clinical indications, rather than as a predefined component of the RFD protocol. In Case 1, dupilumab was introduced reactively after the diagnosis of EoE, initially in combination with topical budesonide, resulting in complete endoscopic and histologic remission while unrestricted milk ingestion was maintained. In Case 2, dupilumab was initiated to manage moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis that remained uncontrolled despite optimized conventional therapy; subsequently, the patient underwent RFD to 3 foods and maintained regular ingestion, with sustained improvement of skin disease.

Long-term maintenance and tolerance

At the latest follow-up, all 3 patients continued daily ingestion of their desensitized foods, with no recurrence of anaphylaxis. Case 1 remained in complete EoE remission under dupilumab while tapering omalizumab, which was initiated 6 months after RFD. Case 2 remained clinically stable with sustained control of atopic dermatitis. Case 3 reported occasional mild symptoms with peanut ingestion, which were managed with antihistamines as needed, while maintaining daily peanut intake limited to no more than 10 peanuts per ingestion.

All patients have initiated tapering of omalizumab (beginning at 6 months after RFD) and, to date, this has not been associated with any change in their clinical course, as evidenced by the absence of new episodes of anaphylaxis. Given the small sample size and variable follow-up periods, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of long-term tolerance or safety.

Discussion

In a descriptive pediatric case series, these 3 cases suggest the potential benefit of adding omalizumab to RFD to facilitate the procedure in carefully selected patients, rather than demonstrating definitive efficacy or safety at a population level. Moreover, the combined use of omalizumab and dupilumab in selected patients with overlapping IgE- and cell-mediated Type 2 mechanisms may offer broader immunologic modulation, but this remains speculative and requires formal evaluation in controlled studies. Omalizumab neutralizes circulating IgE and downregulates FcεRI expression on mast cells and basophils, thereby attenuating the immediate phase of Type I hypersensitivity, whereas dupilumab inhibits IL-4 and IL-13 signaling, reducing downstream Type 2 inflammation. Together, these agents could provide more comprehensive modulation of both the immediate and late phases of allergic inflammation (Fig. 1), although our data do not allow causal inferences.

In our first case, the patient had completely avoided cow's milk because of recurrent anaphylaxis. After initiating omalizumab and achieving effective suppression of the immediate IgE-mediated Type 2 response, it became possible to reintroduce milk during RFD within a closely monitored hospital setting. The biologic blockade likely contributed to stabilization of mast cells and basophils facilitating tolerance of escalating doses during RFD and subsequent daily ingestion. However, this observation should be interpreted as procedural feasibility rather than proof of safety or efficacy. Although the expectation was that subsequent oral immunotherapy might promote long-term immunotolerance, the unblocked cellular arm of the Type 2 response instead drove the development of EoE—a complication reported in a minority of OIT-treated patients.13 In this case, rather than inducing Treg pathways, the immune system maintained a pathogenic, cell-mediated Type 2 response. Dupilumab was introduced reactively after the diagnosis of EoE, in association with topical budesonide, and led to complete endoscopic and histologic remission while unrestricted milk ingestion was maintained. It is not yet fully understood why some patients develop Treg-mediated tolerance while others persist with Type 2 cellular inflammation; whether dupilumab can meaningfully alter this trajectory remains hypothetical and requires prospective investigation.

In Case 2, the patient initially presented with cow's milk allergy and subsequently developed additional food allergies alongside atopic dermatitis, progressing toward a multisystem Type 2 inflammatory phenotype. Because she was rapidly accumulating new food allergies and showing signs of escalating Type 2 inflammation, dupilumab was introduced before omalizumab in an attempt to modulate the progression of the Type 2 Syndrome by suppressing downstream IL-4/IL-13 signaling. The rationale was to stabilize the Type 2 milieu and possibly reduce further sensitization. We acknowledge that this was an exploratory, clinically driven decision rather than evidence-based prevention of multisensitization. Recent data—such as the multicenter Phase II study evaluating dupilumab in children with peanut allergy—suggest that dupilumab does not necessarily facilitate tolerance induction on its own.14 These findings highlight that while dupilumab may modulate the inflammatory environment and improve comorbid Type 2 diseases, its isolated use may not drive true oral tolerance. This underscores the need for combined strategies, such as pairing dupilumab with omalizumab and RFD, particularly in children exhibiting aggressive progression of food allergy and other Type 2 multimorbidities.

Overall, our experience supports the concept that omalizumab can facilitate the procedural performance of RFD in carefully selected, high-risk patients, but this should not be interpreted as generalizable real-world evidence of safety or effectiveness. Moreover, in 2 of the 3 patients, adjunctive dupilumab was used for clear medical indications related to Type 2 multimorbidities (EoE in Case 1 and moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis in Case 2), rather than as a standardized component of desensitization. Any potential synergistic effect of dual biologic therapy therefore remains hypothesis-generating.

While rapid food desensitization (RFD) supported by omalizumab may shorten the time to full-dose ingestion compared with prolonged conventional OIT protocols and may reduce the risk of reactions during up-dosing, this case series was not designed to assess cost-effectiveness. Dual biologic therapy remains associated with very high costs and limited accessibility, which currently restricts its use to specialized centers and to carefully evaluated patients in whom the individual risk–benefit profile is favorable.15 Moreover, assessing the true economic impact of different management strategies in food allergy is inherently complex, as it must consider not only direct drug costs but also the burden of strict dietary avoidance, quality-of-life impairment, anxiety related to accidental exposures, the cumulative demands of prolonged OIT protocols, and the societal and personal impact of rare but potentially fatal anaphylaxis. These factors underscore the need for future prospective studies that integrate clinical outcomes with formal health-economic evaluations.

In conclusion, this descriptive pediatric case series demonstrates the feasibility of rapid food desensitization (RFD) supported by omalizumab in 3 carefully selected high-risk patients, positioning RFD as an optional yet innovative procedural approach within the evolving therapeutic landscape of food allergy. Our observations encompass a clinical spectrum ranging from isolated food-induced anaphylaxis to anaphylaxis associated with Type 2 multimorbidities such as EoE and atopic dermatitis, and document the targeted use of dupilumab in these cases. Although these findings suggest that combined modulation of IgE and IL-4/IL-13 pathways may have clinical utility in selected situations where acute and chronic Type 2 inflammation coexist, our data do not establish that dual biologic therapy is necessary, superior, or broadly safe, and should not be interpreted as such. Rigorous, adequately powered studies are therefore required to better define patient selection, optimal timing and sequencing of RFD and biologic therapies, and long-term outcomes in food allergy complicated by recurrent anaphylaxis and persistent Type 2–driven disease.

Finally, although novel approaches such as bispecific nanobodies targeting IgE and IL-4/IL-13Rα are conceptually attractive, their clinical role remains speculative. Larger, rigorously designed, controlled studies with standardized endpoints, longer follow-up, and predefined safety monitoring are required before broader implementation of RFD combined with biologics can be considered.
