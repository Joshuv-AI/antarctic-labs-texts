The effect of resistance training for older adults with cognitive frailty: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: TingTing Wu, XiaoXia Wang, Yifei Xu, Chufan Zhang, MeiZhen Huang, Hong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCognitive decline and the progression to dementia are hastened in individuals with cognitive frailty. Exercise interventions benefit cognitive function in patients with Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) or frailty. While the optimal implementation of resistance training for cognitive frailty is still being explored. The aims of this study were to develop and evaluate the effect of a novel, hybrid online-offline resistance training regimen for the cognitive frailty cohort.MethodsIn this single-blind, randomized controlled trial, 68 individuals with MCI and frailty or pre-frailty were enrolled and allocated to either a resistance training program or a standard senior fitness exercise regimen. The resistance training, designed based on the intervention mapping framework, comprised a 12-week hybrid intervention, blending online and offline sessions, conducted three times a week. The primary outcomes measured were global cognitive function and physical function. Data analysis was conducted meticulously using covariance analysis, with a commitment to the intention-to-treat principle.ResultsThe hybrid online-offline resistance training regimen was both safe and feasible. Participants in the resistance training group exhibited significantly greater improvements in global cognitive function as measured by MoCA (F = 6.289, p = 0.011) and 4-meter walk time (F = 10.951, P = 0.002), with least squares mean difference of 1.299 (95% CI: 0.306 to 2.291) and − 0.576 (95% CI: -0.924 to -0.228), and with effect size of 0.095, 1.114, respectively. Furthermore, resistance training proved more effective in boosting attention and enhancing quality of life. Between the groups, no significant differences were noted in executive, language, and memory functions, as well as in measures of anxiety, depression, and self-efficacy, among other aspects.ConclusionsOur study demonstrated that a hybrid online-offline resistance training model is safe and effective for older people with cognitive frailty, providing modest benefits over traditional exercises in enhancing global cognitive function, attention, physical function, and quality of life.Trial registrationChiCTR2100043826 was registered on 4 March 2021.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06311-y.

Introduction

With the rapid expansion of the aging population, health challenges among older adults have become increasingly pronounced. Frailty, in particular, is a key determinant of vulnerability in this demographic [1]. Defined as an age-related clinical condition, frailty is characterized by cumulative declines in multiple physiological systems, leading to heightened susceptibility to adverse outcomes [2]. It is commonly classified into three subtypes: physical, cognitive, and social frailty. Cognitive frailty is defined as the coexistence of physical frailty or pre-frailty along with cognitive impairment, excluding any form of dementia [3]. In China, the prevalence of cognitive frailty among older adults is estimated to be approximately 15%, with a rising trend in recent years [4]. Cognitive frailty is a strong predictor of dementia and functional decline in older adults [5, 6], with a 5.36-fold increased risk of dementia compared to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) alone [7]. It is also associated with higher disability and mortality rates [8, 9]. Despite these adverse outcomes, recent evidence suggests that early intervention may halt or even reverse the progression of cognitive frailty [10].

Physical activity, as a non-pharmacological intervention, represents a promising strategy for promoting healthy aging. Targeted exercise programs have been shown to significantly improve memory function and slow cognitive decline [11], while also helping to delay or prevent frailty onset [12, 13]. A proposed mechanism is that physical exercise enhances hippocampal neuroplasticity by elevating circulating neurotrophic factors and reducing pro-inflammatory markers [14–16]. While various exercise modalities offer these benefits, evidence suggests that resistance training (RT) may lead to more substantial changes in neurotrophic and inflammatory biomarkers compared to other forms of exercise [17–19]. RT aimed at enhancing muscle strength, utilizes techniques such as fixed apparatus and free weights, often incorporating dumbbells and resistance bands. It has fewer contraindications and causes only minimal fluctuations in heart rate, making it particularly suitable for older adults. Substantial research has explored its effects on individuals with MCI [20–23] or frailty [24], with increasing attention on those with cognitive frailty, including both institutionalized [13] and community-dwelling older adults [25, 26]. These studies underscore the potential of RT to improve both cognitive and physical function in these high-risk populations.

The heterogeneity of exercise intervention protocols is evident in the variation of targeted muscle groups, such as the lower limbs [13] or both upper and lower limbs [25, 26], and in the diverse outcomes measured, including inflammatory biomarkers, physical function [26], or combined cognitive and physical metrics [13, 25]. The effectiveness of these interventions is influenced by key variables such as exercise intensity, frequency, and type, which can result in differing outcomes [20]. Therefore, developing structured, targeted, and appropriately dosed exercise programs is crucial. Bartholomew et al. [27] introduced the Intervention Mapping (IM) framework, a six-step process: Needs Assessment, Identifying Objectives, Intervention Design, Intervention Production, Implementation, and Evaluation. This evidence-based framework guides the systematic development of health promotion strategies and is widely used to design behavior change interventions and optimize intervention efficacy. Current offline, group-based interventions [13, 25, 26], while effective, are resource-intensive and lack scalability. Mobile health technologies offer promising alternatives [28], yet fully online methods encounter significant barriers such as reduced engagement and technological challenges [29], particularly in older adults with cognitive frailty. Hybrid models, which integrate online and offline components, address these limitations by enhancing adherence, reducing costs, and increasing flexibility, all while ensuring safety [30]. For individuals with cognitive frailty, the dual challenges of impaired cognitive function and physical limitations hinder participation in purely online or offline programs. Hybrid models provide a balanced approach, offering real-time guidance to support cognitive function and reducing physical demands through remote participation.

Thus, the goal of this research was to develop and evaluate the effect of a scientifically based hybrid online-offline RT regimen, guided by Intervention Mapping theory, for older adults with cognitive frailty. We hypothesized that the hybrid RT intervention would lead to greater improvements in both physical and cognitive functions compared to traditional senior fitness exercises. To test this, we conducted a parallel-arm randomized controlled trial (RCT) to compare the effects of 12 weeks online-offline RT regimen versus senior fitness exercises in older adults with cognitive frailty.

Methods

Study design

This study was a parallel RCT with a 1:1 ratio between the experimental and active control groups. The RCT was registered with the Clinical Trials Registry on 4 March 2021 (ChiCTR2100043826) and received approval from the Fujian Provincial Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (K2021-02-003). All procedures adhered to ethical standards for human experimentation, in accordance with the 1975 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants

Participants were recruited from Fujian Provincial Hospital and community health clinics between January 2021 and the achievement of the target sample size. Recruitment methods included billboards, posters, leaflets, and outreach by community health workers. Eligible older adults residing in the community were identified through these channels and underwent preliminary screening based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The study’s objectives, significance, and procedures were fully explained to participants to ensure comprehension, and informed consent was obtained.

Inclusion criteria for participants were as follows

(1) Age ≥ 60 years; (2) Diagnosis of MCI is based on a Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score < 26 [31], along with normal Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores to rule out dementia, with cutoffs adjusted for education level: illiterate group > 17, primary school > 20, and secondary education or higher > 24 [32]. MoCA and MMSE both assess global cognitive function, covering areas such as memory, executive function, attention, language, visuospatial abilities, and orientation [31, 32]. (3) Barthel Index > 60 (4) Individuals at both pre-frail and frailty stages were included, based on the Fried frailty phenotype. Participants with one or two of the five physical characteristics were classified as prefrail, while those with three or more were classified as frail [33]. (5) Unimpaired verbal communication. (6) Residence in the community for the next six months. (7) Access to internet-capable smart devices.

Participants were excluded from the study if they met any of the following criteria

(1) Poorly controlled chronic diseases; (2) Severe anxiety or depression; (3) History of psychiatric illness, severe physical impairment (including stroke-induced hemiplegia), end-stage diseases (like terminal cancer), or hearing impairment; (4) Inability to walk 10 m independently; (5) Concurrent participation in another study.

Randomisation and allocation concealment

The study coordinator used an automated sequence (www.random.org) to produce a total of 68 random numbers. After that, random numbers were concealed during allocation by being placed in opaque, sealed envelopes. In order to ensure fair group assignment, researchers opened envelopes one at a time after recruiting individuals and gathering baseline data.

Blinding

Data collectors and analysts in this study were blinded, meaning they were not informed of the group allocations or the interventions. Assessments were performed by master’s level nursing students who were not involved in the intervention and were blinded to the groupings. To ensure neutrality and maintain research integrity, group labels “A” and “B” were used for data entry. These labels were only unmasked after the completion of the statistical analysis to prevent any bias during data handling.

Sample size

In this study, the MoCA and the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) were employed as primary outcome measures. Using an independent two-sample t-test via PASS software, we set the significance level (α) at 0.05 and the power (1-β) at 0.90, and used a two-tailed test. Based on Song et al. [34] MoCA scores (23.66 ± 1.92 and 21.40 ± 2.27), the minimum sample size was determined to be 20 per group. Factoring in a 20% dropout rate, a minimum of 50 participants was established. Further, following Haritz et al. [35] SPPB scores (7.9 ± 3.1 and 4.9 ± 2.8 in post-intervention frail older people), at least 22 participants per group were required, increasing to a minimum of 56 participants when accounting for dropout rates. Thus, the study’s total minimum sample size was established at 56.

Three phases comprised the 12-week resistance training intervention: initial (1–6 weeks), mid-term (7–10 weeks), and final (11–12 weeks). The intervention targeted the shoulders, back, chest, upper limbs, glutes, thighs, and calves. In the first phase, each session targeted two to three muscle groups (upper and lower body), rising to four in the middle phase, and all seven in the end. The curriculum adhered to the FITT principle [36]: Frequency: Three times weekly for a duration of 12 weeks, totaling 36 sessions. Intensity: with a Borg exertion rating of 10–14 on the Borg 6–20 scale [37], two sets of 10 repetitions during the first and mid-term phases and two sets of 12 repetitions during the final phase. Time: Sessions lasted 30–50 min, including a 5 min warm-up, 20–40 min of exercise (20 min in the initial, 30 min in mid-term, 40 min in final phase), and a 5-minute cooldown. Type: Resistance training. Intervention delivery was structured through an offline-online hybrid model, beginning with group-based training (1–6 weeks), moving to online training via Tencent meeting which offers real-time video conferencing capabilities (7–12 weeks), with elastic bands started from week three, with support from exercise videos, fitness wristbands, and instruction manuals. Active control group

The control group participated in senior fitness exercises, specifically routines developed by the Shanghai Institute of Physical Education, designed for the older adults. Instruction and practice of these exercises were conducted individually from weeks 1 to 6, followed by combined routines in weeks 7 to 12. Senior fitness exercises were based on the FITT principle: Frequency: three sessions a week for 12 weeks. Intensity: with a Borg exertion rating of 10–14. Time: Each session included 5 min of warm-up, 30 min of exercise, 5 min of cooldown, and 10 min of health education. Type: These routines comprised finger exercises, clapping exercises, and stretching exercises. Examples of senior fitness exercise movements were included in the supplementary file. Intervention delivery was structured through group-based training in the first 6 weeks, followed by home-based exercises in the last 6 weeks, with support from exercise videos, fitness wristbands, and instruction manuals. Outcome measures and data collection

Cognitive function

The Changsha version of Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [38] and Mini-Mental Status Examination (MMSE) [32] were widely utilized in China for global cognitive function evaluation, demonstrates robust reliability and validity. Memory function was assessed using the Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT) [39], encompassing immediate recall, delayed recall, and recognition. The Digit Span Test (DST) was employed for attention assessment [40]. Language function was gauged using the Verbal Fluency Tests (VFT) [41], and executive function was appraised with the Trail Making Test (TMT) [42].

Physical function

The Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) [43] was utilized for physical function evaluation, encompassing a standing balance test, 4-meter walking speed test, and five chair sit-to-stand tests. The Fried Frailty phenotype comprises five criteria: unintentional weight loss, self-reported exhaustion, weakness (grip strength), slow walking speed, and low physical activity [33]; patients exhibiting abnormalities in these areas are classified as frail. Grip strength, a key frailty indicator, was measured using the CAMRY-EH101 dynamometer on the dominant hand of participants, who exerted maximum force for 3 s while standing; the highest of two trials was recorded. The Barthel Index [44] assesses daily living abilities in older adults.

Additional assessment instruments

The Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) [45] and the Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA) [46] were utilized for self-reported assessment of depression and anxiety levels. The General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) [47] assessed individuals’ belief in their ability to handle challenges adaptively. Quality of Life (QoL) was measured using the 13-item, self-rated Quality of Life in Alzheimer’s Disease scale (QoL-AD) [48], which offered response options of poor, fair, good, and excellent. Compliance was monitored through detailed attendance logs for each session. Safety was monitored by recording physiological parameters, including heart rate, blood pressure, fatigue, and other relevant measures. Satisfaction was evaluated using a custom-developed questionnaire across several aspects, rated on a Likert 5-point scale: Curriculum Design, Teaching Methodology, Facility Provision, Task Difficulty, Professional Demeanor, Schedule Coordination, Course Mastery, and Overall Assessment.

All outcome measures were assessed by experienced staff members, blinded to group allocation, at pre- and post-intervention during the study. RPE and adverse events were closely monitored by intervention personnel throughout the entire intervention process.

Primary outcomes

The score of MoCA and SPPB were measured post-intervention.

Secondary outcomes

Post-intervention assessments included the Fried frailty phenotype, 4-meter walk speed, grip strength, MMSE (global cognition), AVLT (memory), DST (attention), VFT (language), TMT (executive function), and scores for GDS-15, HAMA, GSES, and QOL-AD. Compliance, satisfaction, and safety were also evaluated.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 was used to analyze the data for this investigation. A two-tailed α of 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance at p < 0.05. Means ± standard deviation was used for quantitative data with a normal distribution; medians (P25, P75) were reported for non-normal data. Independent sample t-tests were employed for group comparisons in normally distributed data, while the Mann-Whitney U test was utilized in non-normally distributed data. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests or paired t-tests were used for within-group comparisons. Frequencies, percentages, and, if necessary, Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used in the analysis of categorical data. Appropriate methods were applied for between- and within-group comparisons before and after the intervention, based on the data type. To ascertain inter-group outcome differences, primary and secondary outcomes were assessed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with baseline scores acting as covariates. The least squares (LS) mean difference was used to determine the adjusted mean differences between the two groups, while η² (Eta-squared) was reported to represent the effect size. Based on established guidelines, effect sizes can be interpreted as follows: small (η² = 0.01), medium (η² = 0.06), and large (η² = 0.14) effects [49]. The study used an intention-to-treat (ITT) approach, with missing data handled by the last observation carried forward (LOCF) method, carrying baseline values forward when post-intervention data were unavailable. This preserved dataset integrity and ensured a conservative estimate of the intervention’s effect.

Results

Participant characteristics

From January to May 2021, 248 older adults were screened at Fujian Provincial Hospital and associated community centers, as outlined in Fig. 2. Of these, 68 individuals were randomized into the RT group (n = 34) and the active control group (n = 34). All participants initially completed baseline data collection. In the RT group, one participant withdrew in week 6 due to a family emergency, and in the control group, two withdrew in weeks 1 and 3 due to extended travel. Of the initial cohort, 65 completed the post-intervention assessment. The dropout rate was 8.8%, with 2.9% in the RT group and 5.9% in the control group. According to ITT principles, all 68 participants were included in the final analysis, with missing data replaced by the most recent data.

Procedure evaluation

In the RT group, single absences due to back pain and dizziness occurred during group and home exercise sessions. The control group saw two dizziness-related absences in training, with another participant missing home exercises due to travel. Exercise-related adverse events were not reported. Physiological parameters, including heart rate, blood pressure, and fatigue, remained within safe limits, as outlined in Table S10. Post-intervention satisfaction in the RT group, documented in Table S11, showed 93.94% for schedule coordination and 87.88% for course mastery, with full satisfaction in other evaluated domains.

Cognitive function

Covariance analysis indicated that the RT group showed greater improvement than the control group in MoCA scores (F = 6.289, p = 0.011), DST-Total Score (F = 4.850, p = 0.031), and DST-Forward (F = 5.264, p = 0.025), with LS mean difference of 1.299 (95% CI: 0.306 to 2.291), 0.758 (95% CI: 0.071 to 1.445), and 0.639 (95% CI: 0.083 to 1.195) respectively, with an effect size of 0.095, 0.069 and 0.075 respectively, as detailed in Table 2. No significant differences were found between groups for other cognitive function measures.

Discussion

This is the first RCT to develop and evaluate a hybrid RT program based on the Intervention Mapping framework, combining online and offline modalities. Over the 12-week period, the program showed modest improvements in cognitive function, with RT demonstrating a slight advantage over traditional senior fitness exercises, particularly in MoCA scores and DST. RT also significantly improved 4-Meter Walk Speed, with a notable effect size of 0.144, underscoring its potential impact on physical function. However, the absence of follow-up data also limits conclusions about the long-term effects. Larger-scale studies are needed to confirm these findings and assess their potential for integration into exercise guidelines for individuals with cognitive frailty.

Informed by qualitative research on exercise needs and influenced by the COVID-19 pandemic, which necessitated reduced physical gatherings, we pioneered a hybrid exercise intervention for the cognitive frailty population, combining online and offline elements. This approach aligns with our intervention program, which was designed based on Intervention Mapping theory to assess patient needs, identifying objectives, intervention design, and was validated for its scientific robustness and feasibility through expert panel and pilot testing. The resistance exercises were designed following general guidelines to ensure appropriate intensity and frequency for older adults with cognitive frailty. The flexible hybrid online-offline intervention allowed for adaptation to different needs, making the program both feasible and acceptable. The exercise regimen is conducted in both standing and seated positions, encompassing a sequential training of various muscle groups, including shoulders, back, chest, upper limbs, glutes, thighs, and calves, to ensure comprehensive muscular engagement across the body. Resistance training with elastic bands is employed as a cost-effective and user-friendly tool, particularly suitable for individuals to practice at home. The training approach integrates both online and offline modalities, addressing the cognitive challenges faced by older people with MCI, such as diminished memory and executive function, while simultaneously catering to their needs and fostering social interaction. Previous scholars have also implemented a blended online-offline intervention in community groups [50], and through group focus interviews, identified that maintaining independence and social connections were key motivations for participating in weekly offline training sessions [51]. Feedback indicated that participants not only valued offline training for social interaction but also appreciated the convenience of remote access. Hence, our mixed-modality intervention was highly feasible and acceptable among the older adults with cognitive frailty, fulfilling their need for social interaction and the convenience of telemedicine.

A systematic review has demonstrated that physical activity exerts a notable influence on overall cognitive function, memory, and executive functions, with varying degrees of impact depending on the exercise type [52]. This 12-week hybrid resistance training program yielded modest cognitive improvements, with greater gains observed in MoCA scores compared to conventional senior fitness. Notably, no significant changes were detected in MMSE. The divergence may reflect differences in instrument sensitivity: MoCA is more responsive to subtle cognitive changes, particularly in executive function and attention, whereas MMSE is less sensitive to early impairments [53, 54]. Thus, MoCA may more accurately capture intervention-related cognitive gains, while MMSE may underestimate such effects. RT showed some improvement in DST scores, indicating potential benefits for attention, while traditional senior fitness exercises appeared to enhance immediate memory as measured by the AVLT. Although the differences were modest and accompanied by small effect sizes, the variation in outcomes across different cognitive domains underscores the need for further research to understand the mechanisms through which these interventions impact cognitive function. Similar studies, such as Yoon et al.‘s 16-week high-intensity RT study [25], focusing on individuals with cognitive frailty, which confirmed the significant impact of RT on global cognitive and executive functions. In a comparative study by Fiatarone and colleagues [55] involving 100 MCI individuals, progressive RT was observed to significantly enhance both global cognitive function and executive function, as opposed to computerized cognitive training. Although numerous studies have underscored the efficacy of RT in improving executive functions among individuals with MCI, our results present a marginal deviation. This could be attributed to the relatively higher baseline cognitive function of our subjects, where the post-intervention improvement failed to reach statistical significance. Such an outcome may be linked to the nature of our intervention, characterized by its low intensity and medium-short duration. Additionally, although the intensity of resistance training was gradually increased during the intervention by increasing repetitions and exercise duration, the RT for each individual exercise was not progressively increased based on their repetition-maximum (RM) strength.

In this investigation, the RT group demonstrated a statistically significant decrease in the time required to complete a 4-meter walk, with an average reduction of 0.576s (95% CI: −0.924 to −0.228), when compared to the control group. Notwithstanding, no significant disparities were observed between the groups in Fried Frailty Criteria, SPPB scores, or grip strength. Several factors may elucidate the absence of marked differences in these measures: First, although frailty levels decreased in both the RT and control groups, no significant differences were found between groups. This suggests that both interventions may help reduce frailty, though neither showed clear superiority over the other. Second, the analysis of SPPB scores did not reveal significant changes between groups, potentially due to the elevated baseline levels of participants (SPPB ≥ 9) and the intervention’s intensity. Previous research, such as that by Englund et al. [56], restricted their 12-week progressive RT study to participants with SPPB scores ≤ 9. Similarly, Sahin et al.‘s study contrasting high- and low-intensity strength training over eight weeks in a frail cohort [24] found significant SPPB score improvements exclusively in the high-intensity group, implying that the impact of short-duration, low-intensity RT on SPPB scores may be insufficient. Lastly, although post-intervention grip strength significantly increased in both the RT and control groups, RT did not exhibit a definitive advantage. This suggests that the intensity or duration of the intervention may not have been sufficient to produce a more pronounced effect on physical strength. This study employed the RPE to monitor RT intensity, which might lead participants, particularly those with cognitive frailty, to overestimate their exertion levels due to diminished physical capabilities. Prior studies have effectively managed RT intensity using 50-75% of one repetition-maximum strength (1-RM) [21–23]. While 1-RM provides a more objective and quantifiable measure of intensity, requiring professional, one-on-one supervision, its applicability is limited in online interventions. Consequently, our study utilized RPE for intensity regulation, aligning with the methodologies of comparable research designs [57, 58], to ensure both practicality and fidelity to the intervention protocol.

This study acknowledges several limitations. First, although RT demonstrated greater improvements in cognitive and physical function compared to traditional senior fitness exercises, the modest effect sizes suggest that longer or more intense interventions may be necessary to achieve clinically meaningful outcomes. Second, the study design included only two time points (pre- and post-intervention), making it difficult to fully account for time effects, such as natural recovery. Future research should incorporate multiple follow-up assessments or use time-series analyses to better control for and differentiate time effects from the intervention’s actual impact. Lastly, while elastic bands were used due to their cost-effectiveness and versatility, they present challenges in precisely controlling resistance. Future studies could improve the accuracy of intensity control by using machine-based RT with repetition-maximum (RM) measurements.

Conclusions

Our study has successfully demonstrated that a hybrid model, integrating online and offline modalities of RT, is both safe and feasible for older adults with cognitive frailty. Notably, RT was found to be more effective than conventional fitness exercises in improving global cognitive function, attention, 4-meter walk speed, and overall quality of life. The implications of these findings are profound, offering pivotal insights for the enhancement of physical activity guidelines specifically tailored for individuals with MCI and frailty. Future research should involve larger-scale RCTs to further validate the efficacy of this intervention and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.
