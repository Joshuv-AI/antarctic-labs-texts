‘…It Makes It Possible for Me to Have a Life…’: Microtransformative Helpline Communication with Frequent Users
Authors: Trine Natasja Sindahl, Carsten Stage, Matilde Nisbeth Brøgger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFrequent use of anonymous helplines is often framed as problematic from a service perspective. This study aims to explore how frequent users themselves perceive the helpfulness of repeated helpline contact and to examine tensions between user and staff understandings of effective helpline communication. The study draws on qualitative interviews with 31 frequent users across ten Danish helplines, covering telephone‐, chat‐, and SMS‐based services. The analysis is conducted in dialogue with prior findings on helpline staff's communicative ideals, using a framework distinguishing between transformative, relational, and respectful communication. Frequent users experience helpline interactions as micro‐transformative, providing immediate emotional regulation, relief from intrusive thoughts, and temporary containment of loneliness. Relational presence is experienced as meaningful change in the here‐and‐now. Frequent helpline use is best understood as a situated, relational community practice rather than an individual deficit, with important implications for community‐based mental health support.Keywords: communication, community mental health services, counselling, help‐seeking behaviour, hotlines, mental health services, qualitative research

Introduction

Helplines exist worldwide as low‐threshold access points to psychosocial support, offering help in the here‐and‐now to people seeking crisis assistance, advice, referrals, or a listening ear (Iversen and Westerlund 2024; Mazzer et al. 2021; Sindahl 2019; Vattø et al. 2020). As informal support services, helplines operate in contexts shaped by stigma, power imbalances, and uneven access to formal systems of care (Foucault and Howard 2003; Hellström et al. 2023; Kirkbride et al. 2024).

Most helplines are familiar with the phenomenon of frequent use, where a relatively small group of users account for a substantial proportion of contacts (Middleton et al. 2014; Mishara et al. 2023; Pirkis et al. 2016). In both research and practice, frequent users are often framed as a challenge or problem for services (Erlangsen et al. 2025; Pirkis et al. 2016; Skogevall et al. 2022). Such framings risk obscuring how repeated use may reflect unmet needs and limited access to alternative forms of support.

The current article explores how frequent users of anonymous helplines perceive the helpfulness of their interactions. By placing the perspectives of frequent users in dialogue with those of helpline staff, the article examines the mutual and communicative struggles that emerge in these encounters. Rather than understanding frequent use as an individual deficit, the article approaches it as a meaningful and situated practice shaped by users’ social circumstances and available support options.

Frequent Use of Helplines

Frequent use has been acknowledged and described since the inception of anonymous helplines. In an article from 1965 concerning an American suicide‐prevention telephone service, 'repeaters' were described as: '…often confused, alcoholic, or psychotic, as well as chronically suicidal. They could, if permitted, absorb unlimited amounts of the telephone workers’ time' (Litman 1965, 110). A few years later, the term 'chronic caller' was introduced to describe individuals who repeatedly contacted suicide helplines (Lester and Brockopp 1970). According to the authors, in many respects chronic callers resembled other callers; however, a small group '…seem[s] to want someone merely to listen to them talk' and 'arouse a great deal of resentment on the part of the staff', who were frustrated by these users’ apparent lack of motivation for change (Lester and Brockopp 1970, 250). Subsequently, a number of articles referred to 'chronics' or 'chronic callers' as people at high risk of suicide (Greer 1976), while noting that the acute crisis support offered by helplines was insufficient in addressing their chronic difficulties (McKenna et al. 1975).

Several studies focus on describing the characteristics of frequent helpline users, identifying them as people with problems such as substance abuse or a high risk of suicide (McKenna et al. 1975; Sawyer and Jameton 1979; Spittal et al. 2015; Xu et al. 2024), social isolation (Mao et al. 2023; Pirkis et al. 2016; Spittal et al. 2015), high levels of anxiety (Bassilios et al. 2015; Burgess et al. 2008; Middleton et al. 2016) and limited personal resources and ineffective coping strategies (Imboden 1980). Other sources focus on topologizing frequent users into several different categories based on their calling patterns or characteristics (Erlangsen et al. 2025; Mao et al. 2023; Middleton et al. 2016; Middleton et al. 2017; Sanders 2007). A different line of research highlights the type of help frequent users seek, reporting that it primarily involves venting feelings (Barmann 1980; Middleton et al. 2017) or taking a pause from loneliness (Vattø et al. 2019) while other studies find that frequent users contact helplines to discuss treatment options or to compensate for inadequate treatment (Iversen and Westerlund 2024).

Frequent use is widely experienced by helplines due to easy access and anonymity, yet it poses a challenge as most helplines are not designed to meet the long‐term needs of these users (Imboden 1980). Moreover, frequent users are described as inept at recognising and articulating their needs (DeCarli 1988), but at the same time, helplines cannot refuse to engage with them, as this would conflict with their core principle of 'anyone, at any time' (Hall and Schlosar 1995, 67) and might trigger suicidal behaviour (Hall and Schlosar 1995).

Lastly, the literature includes a number of intervention studies (Brunet et al. 1994; Hall and Schlosar 1995; Leuthe and O'Connor 1980; Mao et al. 2023; Mishara et al. 2023; Pirkis et al. 2016; Sawyer and Jameton 1979; Schon 2019; Vivekananda et al. 2021; Vivekananda et al. 2021), suggesting or opposing various ways of addressing frequent use and users.

Most existing studies focus on a single helpline and primarily draw on quantitative data or qualitative data from helpline staff. A notable exception is the study by Middleton et al. (2016) in which 19 frequent users of the Australian Lifeline were interviewed by telephone following interactions with helpline responders. The study identified three main reasons for frequent helpline contact: (1) a reactive response to triggering events seeking support to regulate distress; (2) a need for human contact in everyday life; and (3) habitual use.

The current study extends previous research by: (1) examining frequent users across 10 helplines and media (telephone‐, chat‐ and SMS‐services); (2) basing the analysis on qualitative interviews with 31 frequent users; (3) supplementing brief on‐site interviews with in‐depth interviews conducted outside the helpline context; and (4) incorporating a second‐order analysis in which the findings were validated and discussed with five frequent users involved as co‐researchers as well as a group of helpline practitioners.

Communicative Ideals and Challenges in Helpline Work

As previously stated, this article is structured through a dialogue with a prior article focusing on the perspectives of helpline workers. In that earlier article, we explored the communicative expectations and notions of successful interaction held by staff and responders from 10 Danish helplines (Stage et al. Forthcoming). By taking an interest in these expectations, we argued that frequent use should not be understood solely as individual behaviour, but rather as a communicative practice that emerges and is problematised within the specific organisational and technological context of the helpline.

Furthermore, we argued that helpline staff navigate between transformative, relational and respect‐oriented forms of helpline communication (see Figure 1). Transformative communication was the form most consistently associated by helpline responders with successful helpline interactions, as it centred on facilitating emotional, practical and/or cognitive change. Relational communication represented a less preferred, yet widely accepted, form of interaction that offered users human contact and companionship. Respectful communication constituted a more basic requirement, emphasising that helpline interactions should at the least be non‐confrontational and honest.

Helpline staff's communicative ideals and how these are challenged by frequent users.

Within this framework helpline staff problematised frequent use in three distinct ways, linked to these communicative ideals: (1) a perceived lack of change or the presence of negative change; (2) the sheer volume or frequency of contact; and (3) communication that was perceived as disrespectful or dishonest. The latter two problematisations—excessive contact volume and disrespectful communication—tended to elicit more explicit measures aimed at altering the interaction, either by limiting contact or by meta‐communicating the interactional norms of the helpline. This also implied that frequent use—which was neither perceived as numerically excessive nor confrontational—was often accepted by helplines and responders.

Research Questions

The above led us to the following research questions, as we analytically seek a dialogue between the findings from interviews with helpline staff and the interviews with frequent users of the same helplines:

RQ: How are helpline communication perceived by frequent users, when applying the differentiation between transformative, relational and respectful communication?

Viewing frequent use as a relational phenomenon that develops between users and helpline services, this article contributes by exploring how helpline interactions are experienced on both sides of the line.

Methods

As this study builds on previous analysis of helpline staff experiences, the analysis adopts a more deductive approach, creating a dialogue between helpline workers and frequent users in order to highlight mismatches and tensions between these perspectives on helpline communication and frequent use.

The study examines frequent use across 10 Danish anonymous helplines (see Table 1 for a list of participating helplines). The helplines were selected to capture variation in size, scope, target population, thematic focus, organisational approach, and the characteristics of helpline responders. The current study comprises interviews with 31 frequent users of the same 10 helplines (as well as other helplines), and seeks to approximate a first‐person perspective (Bøttcher et al. 2018) by unfolding what frequent users are engaged in and oriented towards in their interactions with the helplines they regularly use.

All authors hold research positions at the university, and none are currently affiliated with any of the participating helplines. The first author is a clinical psychologist with 30+ years of experience working with helplines as a supervisor, trainer, helpline responder, consultant, and researcher. This insider knowledge proved valuable in establishing access to the field and in building trust with frequent users. At the same time, the first author's familiarity with helpline practice required ongoing reflexive attention to avoid reproducing the field's own self‐understandings in the interpretation of user experiences. The two remaining authors brought extensive experience as qualitative researchers within health communication and lifeline communication, but were outsiders to the helpline context. This insider/outsider dynamic was treated as an analytical resource throughout the research process, complemented by participant involvement in the second‐order analysis.

Frequent Use at the Participating Helplines

All participating helplines were asked to share data extracted from their telephone and chat systems, including the number of calls, unique users, and related metrics. In almost all cases, these data could be accessed without identifying specific users. In one case, the telephone company granted formal researcher access to telephone log data, and the first author could compile the data without linking it to specific users. Due to technical constraints, we were unable to obtain reliable data from all platforms at every participating helpline. However, all helplines were able to provide at least some data to contribute to the puzzle.

On a monthly basis, the 10 participating services received an average of 48,153 contact attempts, of which 31% were answered. Based on an overview of the very diverse definitions of frequent use applied in previous research (Burgess et al. 2008; Greer 1976; Hall and Schlosar 1995; Johnson and Barry 1978; Lester and Brockopp 1970; Middleton et al. 2014; Mishara et al. 2023) and in dialogue with the helplines operating with very diverse opening hours, frequent use was defined as a user having a session with the helpline lasting more than 5 min on average at least every second opening day for a period exceeding 3 months. The 5 min threshold may be debated, as some frequent users engage in hanging up/logging off repeatedly, or helpline responders might be instructed to end the sessions immediately with certain users. However, such practices are also used by many other users, and we chose not to include these, focusing on sessions that have some form of development. Using this definition, frequent users constituted 1.0% of unique users but accounted for 16% of helpline sessions lasting longer than 5 min.

We identified 70 frequent users across the participating helplines and platforms. As it was not possible to identify unique users across helplines or platforms, the actual number was likely lower, as some frequent users at one helpline or platform may also be frequent users at another helpline and/or platform. On a monthly basis, these at most 70 frequent users would account for least 1523 helpline sessions lasting longer than 5 min.

Due to anonymity, it was not possible to link individual, identifiable users to these figures. However, the data suggests that the 31 frequent users participating in this qualitative study, constitutes a substantial proportion of the frequent user population.

Interviews

Participants were primarily recruited at the end of helpline sessions, after being identified as frequent users by the helpline responder. In most cases, identification was based on the responder's prior experience with the user. In some cases, a supervisor would recognise the user and inform the responder accordingly. In two cases, supervisors could flag frequent users directly in the chat system, thereby alerting the helpline responder. The interviewer (first author) was present at the helpline offices during data collection. Prior to each shift, the researcher consulted with helpline staff regarding which frequent users might be relevant to approach for participation in a research interview.

For ethical reasons, frequent users were excluded if they were under the age of 15, were considered by the helpline responder to be in acute crisis at the end of the session, or it was determined that participation would place an excessive burden on the user. Apart from these criteria, efforts were made to ensure fair and inclusive recruitment (Carlsson et al. 2017). The initial interviews were conducted using the helpline technology—telephone or chat—in order to preserve participants' anonymity.

During data collection, two users were excluded for being underage and one for declining to disclose her age. Additional young users contacted the helplines during the period but were not referred to the researcher, as helpline responders assessed that they did not meet the inclusion criteria. Four users were excluded due to high suicidality. In one case, the helpline responder judged the user too vulnerable to be approached for participation.

During approximately 200 h spent at helpline sites, most identified frequent users were invited to participate, and the majority agreed. At the conclusion of the initial interview, some participants were invited to take part in a follow‐up interview conducted outside the helpline context. As this required disclosure of identity, only adult users without custodial responsibility for a child were invited. Invitations were further limited to participants who expressed having additional perspectives to share. Thirteen frequent users participated in these in‐depth interviews, conducted either by telephone (n = 3) or face to face (n = 10). In a small number of cases, helplines facilitated contact outside an active session, in which case, the in‐depth interview constituted the sole interview. Three participants declined the invitation to participate in a follow‐up interview.

Informed consent was obtained both at the beginning and at the end of each interview (recorded orally for telephone and face‐to‐face interviews and in writing for chat or SMS interviews). Prior to commencing the actual interview, participants were informed about the purpose and expected length of the interview and were assured that their identity would not be disclosed in any dissemination of findings. At the end of the interview, consent was reaffirmed to ensure participants remained comfortable with their contributions being used now knowing what information they had provided. All participants were informed of their right to withdraw consent without consequence and were provided a link to a webpage containing detailed information about the research project, data storage procedures, and instructions for withdrawing consent. The webpage also offered participants the option to receive newsletters about the study as it progressed.

The initial interviews were collected during the period of January to July 2025, and each interview lasted between 15 and 30 min. The in‐depth interviews were collected from February to September 2025 and lasted between 45 and 90 min. All interviews were conducted by the first author who has a background in clinical psychology. Telephone and face‐to‐face interviews were audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim, while chat interviews were saved following completion.

The initial interviews focused on: (1) participants’ experiences of the current session; (2) their general patterns of helpline use; and (3) their understanding of their own needs. The in‐depth interviews additionally addressed: (4) personal histories and everyday life contexts; (5) changes in helpline use over time; (6) perceived advantages and disadvantages of helpline use; (7) experiences of limit‐setting and other regulatory interventions by helplines, as well as participants’ own 'theories of change' (i.e., how they understand their difficulties, their origins and maintenance, and what they perceive as helpful); (8) perceptions of life fulfilment and unmet needs; (9) current and previous sources of support; and (10) anticipated reasons for potentially discontinuing helpline use. Translated interview guides are available in Appendix 1 and 2.

No participants received incentives or reimbursement.

Data collection was conducted with informed consent, approved by the Research Ethics Committee at Aarhus University, and complied with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).

Artificial intelligence (AI) has assisted the authors in improving the language and clarity of the manuscript.

Qualitative Analysis

The research design is constructivist, aiming to generate knowledge about participants’ own interpretations of their engagement with helplines (Creswell and Creswell 2023). Interview transcripts were uploaded to and coded in NVivo 15 (QSR International Pty Ltd., 2024). As the present article is in dialogue with a previous study based on helpline staff perspectives (Stage et al. Forthcoming), a deductive coding strategy was employed (Fife and Gossner 2024), guided by the three communicative themes identified in the earlier analysis.

Re‐interviewing participants enabled deeper exploration of key themes and served as a means of participant validation (Corbin and Strauss 2015; Holzkamp 1983). This process was further extended through a participatory approach informed by critical psychology, including the involvement of co‐researchers (Holzkamp 2005; Teo 2014), and by Luhmann's concept of second‐order observation (Luhmann 1993; Åkerstrøm Andersen 1999). The first‐order analysis was discussed with representatives from the helpline sector ('SolutionLab') and with five frequent users involved as co‐researchers. This participatory process aimed to validate, refine, supplement, and test the relevance of the analytical interpretations, seeking knowledge that is socially robust (Nowotny 2003).

The SRQR (Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research) were consulted throughout the writing of this article (O'Brien et al. 2014).

Analysis

Participants reported engagement with anonymous helplines ranging from a few months to over 40 years. Most participants contacted helplines multiple times per week, and in several cases, multiple times per day. While a small number of participants primarily used a single helpline, most accessed multiple services with varying frequency. Collectively, participants reported regular use of at least 21 different helplines. One participant in her twenties described periods during which she spent more than 30 h per week writing and speaking with anonymous helplines. Another participant in her fifties reported daily contact with helplines since adolescence.

The sample included participants with intellectual disabilities, schizophrenia, and other severe mental health conditions. These conditions were not used as exclusion criteria, as doing so would have substantially limited the representativeness of the sample. Individuals with disabilities may experience structural discrimination, and their voices may be neglected in research due to paternalistic assumptions (Witham et al. 2015). As such, some participants may be considered 'more‐than‐ordinarily vulnerable' (Sellman 2005). The study aims to foreground otherwise marginalised and anonymous voices (Gough and Parker 2015), mainly heard by a large number of helpline responders.

Ensuring anonymity posed a particular challenge, as frequent users are often well known to helpline staff. To protect participants’ identities, quotations are attributed using self‐identified gender and age brackets. All participants identified as binary: 25 women (F) and six men (M), aged between 15 and 73. All quotations have been translated from Danish into English.

While the previous study of helpline staff's perspectives on frequent use focused on when and how frequent use becomes a problem for helplines (Stage et al. Forthcoming), the current analysis examines how frequent users understand helplines’ ability to help them and how they make sense of their continuous interactions with these services.

Transformative Communication

Previous analysis showed that helpline staff's ideals of successful interaction involve engaging with a problem or challenge presented by the user and facilitating some form of positive emotional, cognitive, or behavioural change (Stage et al. Forthcoming).

Although the frequent user participants constituted a diverse group, the majority—if not all—had been diagnosed with one or more mental health conditions. Many also lived with physical disabilities that contributed to social vulnerability and restricted opportunities for self‐realisation. Several participants further described adverse childhood experiences marked by neglect and/or severe bullying.

None of the participants interviewed expected helplines to provide advice or long‐term solutions. Most demonstrated a clear awareness of the complexity and persistence of their difficulties and acknowledged the need for sustained, long‐term support. As a young woman with a history of severe neglect explained:

… I would say that my trauma is so complex that I need both forms of support. Because of course I need a long‐term therapeutic process that enables me to move forward with my life. But what do I do in specific situations where I'm really upset?

(F16, 20–29 y)

In most cases, participants were already receiving extensive support outside the helpline context, including psychological or psychiatric treatment, medication, peer support groups, municipal services, social support workers, residential care, volunteer visitors, and various therapeutic interventions. Several participants also reported daily contact with family members, although most had very limited friendship networks—if any. At the same time, several participants expressed a lack of sufficient help, particularly access to trauma‐focused or talking therapies.

Many participants, unprompted, described helpline responders' need for transformative communication. They explained how the helpline responders would be eager to suggest what they could do to change the immediate or the long‐term crises in their lives. While a few found such interventions supportive, most explained that they already knew what would be considered ‘the right thing to do’. Suggestions were often experienced as pressure to change, as invalidating, or as a form of rejection (i.e., the helpline responder's efforts to end the session), sometimes triggering emotional outbursts. As one participant noted: ‘If I tell them I want to travel, they almost order the tickets there and then’ (M21, 30–39 y).

The participants described helplines as a mediated form of support that offers assistance in moments of immediate emotional need. Rather than expecting long‐term solutions, they turned to these services for help with managing (for them) everyday challenges—such as regulating difficult emotions or coping with intrusive or overwhelming thoughts. For many of them, the value of the helpline was its ability to provide compassionate, real‐time support. While they recognised the need for more sustained, long‐term assistance—often sought through other healthcare and social services—the helplines fulfilled for them an important role in helping to navigate the present moment.

When thoughts are racing uncontrollably, get stuck in repetitive rumination, or are too difficult to manage alone, it can be helpful—here and now—to ‘get them out’, thereby facilitating the continuation of daily life. Users described a sense of something ‘building up’ that needs to be placed with another person to restore a sense of calm: ‘[…] I think I use the chat often, to “air out”… in the hope that it can help me handle the difficult afternoons/evenings.’ (F3, 30–39 y)

When emotions become overwhelming, grief intolerable, or anxiety pervasive, contact with a helpline can facilitate emotional downregulation or provide distraction, thereby offering a sense of relief and enabling the user to continue their day—or night. 'Because I talk to them every day. And because it makes it possible for me to have a life where I can handle my suicidal thoughts and the illness that I have'. (F7, 40–49 y)

This said, for some participants, helpline contact had developed into a habitual routine structuring everyday life. When asked why they had contacted the helpline, some responded simply: 'I call them every day' (F13, 50–59 y) indicating that no other reason was needed.

While several frequent helpline users reported having daily or regular contact with family members or friends, some described an extensive and ongoing need for interpersonal communication. They explained that reaching out to helplines allowed them to protect and preserve personal relationships that might otherwise become strained or overwhelmed by their frequent need for emotional support. Additionally, they noted that the complexity of their issues was often difficult for relatives to fully understand, whereas helpline responders generally were perceived as knowledgeable, empathetic, and better equipped to address their specific needs. And even though the participants saw themselves as having a form of relationship with the helplines and the helpline responders, they perceived these relationships as less vulnerable than those of friends and families, and thus less was at stake if the relationship with the helpline responder would collapse.

Finally, a few of the participants described finding it difficult to uphold the reciprocity typically expected in everyday conversations:

Participant: 'I am not good at talking with people other than helplines, because here it is all about me'

Interviewer: 'And when you talk to friends, how is that different?'

Participant: 'Then you have to listen to them instead of talking about yourself all the time, and that is a bit hard for me'.

(F4, 50–59 y)

In summary, most participants experienced some degree of change during helpline interactions (perhaps not observable to the helpline responder). While the theme of the conversation was seen as less important, the underlying function was typically emotional regulation, relief from intrusive thoughts or distraction from voice‐hearing, or alleviation of loneliness.

For many participants, speaking with helpline responders offered a brief transformation, enabling them to continue with their day—until the need for contact reemerged. This also implies that the boundary between transformative communication and relational communication becomes a lot more blurred from a user perspective, as informal interactions were also experienced as producing meaningful, short‐term change in the here‐and‐now.

Relational Communication

Although helpline staff preferred transformative communication, relational communication was accepted when transformation seemed unattainable (Stage et al. Forthcoming). Relational communication means focusing on presence, listening, and offering human connection.

The participants were acutely aware that the complexity and persistence of their difficulties exceeded what helplines could resolve. They referenced these challenges in their interactions with helpline responders—not to seek solutions, but to signal distress in order to capture the responder's attention or to release emotional pressure. Ultimately, the primary aim was immediate emotional regulation and a brief sense of connection.

Frequent users generally have more seniority and experience communicating with anonymous helplines than any of the helpline responders themselves. This accumulated knowledge was actively applied to navigate interactions in ways that maximised the likelihood of receiving the support they were seeking.

Most frequent user participants engaged with multiple helpline services and were able to articulate differences between various helpline approaches or models. For many, the choice of which helpline to contact depended more on availability and access than on the theme or formal purpose of the service. They described how gaining access to a conversation often depended on how one presented oneself and demonstrated awareness of the different expectations attached to different services. Some helplines were perceived as requiring the user to present 'a serious problem' or 'a specific question', while others were more open to casual or ongoing conversations. This underlines frequent users’ communicative competences and how they navigate the different themes and affordances of helplines to obtain the support they feel in need of.

One user explained, with shame, how she would sometimes change her identity to that of a younger person experiencing severe abuse by one or both parents in order to receive the attention and care she was seeking. Another explained how changing her identity and story felt necessary to gain access to support at a specific helpline.

One participant described how, over time, he had actively shaped the interaction by guiding responders on how best to support him. Another reflected on how her emotional intensity as a new user prompted responders to question the usefulness of the interaction, leading her to adopt a more focused and contained narrative:

So, when I call in, I always try to be very specific. I try to focus on what I am experiencing and feeling right now. And then I try to describe my past in as few sentences as possible. Because otherwise I end up having to repeat a whole lot, which becomes too overwhelming.

(F2; 50–59 y)

This highlights how frequent users, over time, become aware of the communicative affordances and limitations of (specific) helplines and develop strategies to use them in ways they find helpful.

Just as helpline responders may become frustrated by the repetitive nature of conversations with frequent users (Stage et al. Forthcoming), the frequent user participants themselves also expressed frustration. Many explained how exhausting it was to repeat the same stories again and again. In fact, this was the most common response when participants were asked whether they could identify any downsides to their continuous helpline use.

Several participants reported that being recognised by helpline responders was a comforting experience. Many felt welcomed and reassured by responders who expressed care and concern ('… most of the helpline responders know me and they often tell me that they care about me a lot', F10, 20–29 y). For these users, it was meaningful to speak with someone familiar with their history, as this eliminated the need to repeatedly recount their story.

Many participants described having developed relationships with specific helpline responders, often expressing strong positive feelings towards some, while noting discomfort with others. In this context, continuity was not framed negatively as a lack of progress, but rather as fostering a sense of intimacy and closeness with the helpline service.

(Dis)Respectful Communication

Helpline staff agreed that, at a minimum, helpline users need to be sincere, authentic, and respectful in their interactions, and to follow basic unwritten rules governing how helpline communication should be conducted (Stage et al. Forthcoming). Most helpline staff also agreed that while relational communication was accepted, it had to remain within limits—not too often and not too long (Stage et al. Forthcoming).

Several participants were aware that helplines might perceive their frequent use as problematic and something that needed to be restricted. Some referred to a 'rule' allowing only one session per day—a rule that some had strategies to circumvent, while others managed by contacting several helplines daily. Beyond this, participants found it difficult to articulate the boundaries or framework of helpline services, reporting that these appeared to vary from helpline to helpline, responder to responder, day to day, or even time of day:

Participant: 'Some write an hour, and some write half an hour, and some write: ‘We have plenty of time”.'

Interviewer: 'So it is a bit random? Or is it more, that you know that at this helpline it is like this, and at this one it is like this? Or is it that you don't really know what the boundaries are?'

Participant: 'Well, I definitely haven't figured out their system. But I assume that there are pressure points, when people get off work or something, when a lot of people reach out. Or late in the evening, when people are going to bed, or …'

(F5; 18–29 y)

When asked about potential drawbacks of frequent use, some participants described feelings of shame associated with overusing services and exposing deep emotional problems to 'strangers'. However, most did not identify clear negative consequences, although some had the impression that helpline responders could become frustrated with their repeated contact:

No, well, the downsides are if they're kind of on my case a bit, or they get a little annoyed with me. Or they kind of… I sometimes feel like maybe they think: ‘Oh, why is she calling again now?’ […] And sometimes you can also feel like they don't really want to talk to you at all.

(F7, 40‐49 y)

Others were unaware that their way of using helplines differed from that of the average user:

Interviewer: '[…] how often do you talk to them?'

Participant: 'It is not that often I call them. There are many who are feeling much worse than I. I call about two to three times a day'.

(M6, 60–69 y)

Helpline responders reported that some frequent users acted aggressively during sessions (Stage et al. Forthcoming). A few user participants acknowledged having engaged in such behaviour, attributing it to frustration at not feeling understood or to long waiting times. The perception that a responder was attempting to end a session could be extremely provoking for those users who were highly sensitive to cues of rejection. As one participant explained: 'I would rather push people away than have them leave me' (F24, 18–29 y).

Several participants explained that they had learned that if a responder did not meet their immediate needs, it was often more effective to end the call or log off than to attempt to redirect the conversation. What, from a responder's perspective, may appear as a confusing or unpleasant practice (hanging up or disconnecting) was, from a user perspective, an attempt to create a communicative situation more likely to have a positive effect on their immediate challenges. Users described helpline sessions as highly dependent on the individual responder and explained that one sometimes had to ‘go fishing’ until finding ‘the right one’. This strategy would, of course, result in more repeated contacts.

A few participants described measures taken by helplines to limit their contact, such as restrictions on the number or length of weekly sessions, encouragements to contact someone else, or—in some cases—blocking the user or reporting them to the police for harassment. Participants reported that these interventions either had no effect on their overall use of the helpline or led them to increase contact with other helplines instead. A few respondents, particularly those sensitive to rejection, described increasing their contact with a helpline that attempted to restrict access, in an effort to seek validation from the very service rejecting them.

Among participants who reported a reduction in helpline use, this change was typically attributed to improvements in mental health or engagement in therapy, where they were consciously focusing their efforts. Others described taking breaks from helpline use during periods of deteriorating mental health, often coinciding with hospitalisation.

Second Order Analysis

The above findings were presented to representatives from both the practice field ('SolutionLab') and frequent users (co‐researchers). Both practitioners and frequent users agreed that the presented analysis resonated with and reflected their own experiences and perspectives, while also opening up the well‐known dilemmas in new and useful ways.

Practitioners largely recognised the distinction between transformative, relational and micro‐transformative communication and found the framework helpful in articulating practice dilemmas. Micro‐transformative communication was described as a potential 'meeting place' where sessions could feel meaningful to both responder and user. At the same time, the micro‐transformative element was perceived as potentially problematic, as it may reinforce dependency and obscure users’ underlying needs, particularly in cases involving underaged users. Practitioners also came to recognise that interventions aimed at reducing frequent use often merely shifted users between services rather than altering overall help‐seeking patterns.

The frequent user co‐researchers emphasised relational communication as the primary value of helpline contact, due to its immediate effects on emotional regulation and the calming of racing thoughts, and questioned expectations of major transformation within helpline settings. They agreed that the transformation they were seeking would best be served through relational communication. As one of the co‐researchers put it: 'If you want a huge transformation, then you would go somewhere else'. Major change was generally associated with psychological or psychiatric treatment. They recognised that if helpline responders did not acknowledge the function that the contact had in frequent users’ lives, it made sense that responders would experience the sessions as meaningless and attempt to keep them short.

Users described tensions related to anonymity versus recognition, experiences of rejection, and the limited visibility of slow, externally occurring change. They recognised that helpers could feel helpless in contact with them. While acknowledging the short‐term regulatory benefits of helpline contact, some co‐researchers reflected critically on habitual use and highlighted the need for emotion regulation skills and access to longer‐term support.

Across both groups, the data point to a central tension between immediate emotional containment and longer‐term change, as well as between organisational expectations and users’ relational needs.

Discussion

Previous research has shown that helpline staff's notion of successful communication prioritises that users are transformed or change their perspective as a consequence of their engagement with the helpline (Stage et al. Forthcoming). They regard excessive contact as a challenge because it signals a lack of actual change and/or takes up too much time (Stage et al. Forthcoming). This confirms existing research on responders’ sense of efficacy and responses to frequent use (Gilat and Rosenau 2011; Kinzel and Nanson 2000; Vattø et al. 2019; Vivekananda et al. 2021). Helpline staff thus often frame interactions with frequent users as less impactful due to their redundancy and lack of perceived progression (Stage et al. Forthcoming).

The current study demonstrates that frequent users of helplines find these interactions helpful and valuable in their lives and everyday routines precisely because of the helplines’ continuous openness to ongoing contact. As shown by Laursen et al. (2025) in their study of frequent callers to the Danish emergency helpline (112/911), frequent users ‘struggle along’ as '… a way of being in the world in which life is lived from day to day' (p. 9). The frequent users interviewed in this study seek help in the here and now, when things are hard. Most of the frequent users in this study—consistent with previous research in the field (Evans et al. 2024; Middleton et al. 2016; Middleton et al. 2017)—seek emotional deregulation or a pause from intrusive thoughts. This is an ongoing need for them, rather than something that will disappear. They often do not seek advice on what to do, and some may even perceive such suggestions as unwelcomed pressure or critique, which in some cases provokes abusive behaviour towards the helpline responder.

Some might argue that the conflict or tension arises from a mismatch between the service models of the helplines and the needs of frequent users (Pollock et al. 2012). However, these service models are unclear to the users and are found to be applied inconsistently by the helpline responders. From the perspective of frequent users, it is difficult to envision a service that would better meet their needs than one that is always available, free and easily accessible from home.

From a coping theory perspective, frequent helpline use may be understood as emotion‐focused coping (Lazarus and Folkman 1984)—a rational response when the underlying stressor is perceived as beyond direct control. Rather than reflecting avoidance or inadequate help‐seeking, such coping reflects an adaptive use of available resources. Tensions arise when the helplines insist on applying a more problem‐focused approach (Lazarus and Folkman 1984) or perceive the need for micro‐transformative emotion regulation as outside the scope of the service.

A recurring theme concerned the tension between anonymity and recognition. Remaining anonymous required frequent users to repeatedly recount their experiences, while being recognised increased the perceived risk of rejection or stigmatisation as a ‘frequent user’. Experiences of rejection—whether explicit or implicit—were described as particularly distressing and, paradoxically, as drivers of repeated contact.

When combining the perspectives of helpline responders and frequent users across 10 Danish helplines, we identified at least four tensions in their perspectives on valuable helpline communication:

1.What helpline staff perceive as a failure to deliver sufficiently substantive assistance as a basis for long‐term transformation of the user's situation is experienced by frequent users as valuable short‐term support in response to ongoing needs.

2.What helpline staff regard merely as relational interaction is experienced by frequent users as helpful and micro‐transformative interaction providing emotional regulation or a pause from intrusive thoughts.

3.Both helpline staff and frequent users are burdened by the repetitive nature of helpline sessions maintained by user anonymity. The perspectives of frequent users presented in this article are limited to those users who could, would and were invited to participate. Frequent users constitute a diverse group, and not all perspectives are represented. Nevertheless, the interviews provide new knowledge about an often overlooked user perspective on frequent use, which, as demonstrated, can challenge helpline staff's understanding of the practice of frequent use.

The analysis seeks to create a dialogue between the perspectives of helpline staff and frequent users; however, the onset for this dialogue is an analysis of helpline staff perspectives. This limits the extent to which the frequent user perspective can stand in its own right. Future analyses will address this limitation.

The analysis leaves us with a dilemma regarding how to approach frequent use as a form of helpline communication that, on the one hand, leaves helpline responders with a feeling of not making a difference or not fully doing their job, and, on the other hand, is described by users as a valuable tool for meeting ongoing needs for short‐term emotional regulation, distraction and social connection.

Implications for Practice

As reflected by the practitioners when presented with the analysis, gaining insight into frequent users’ perspectives may reduce helpline staff's experiences of powerlessness and helplessness. A clearer understanding of the function of repeated helpline use may equip staff with better tools for making sessions meaningful for both users and responders. The analysis further highlights dynamic interactional factors in helpline communication that may contribute to disrespectful behaviour from frequent users. Such insights could inform the development of interventions aimed at fostering more constructive and sustainable relationships between helplines and frequent users. However, acquiring this understanding alone is unlikely to resolve the challenges associated with frequent use, either from the perspective of helplines—balancing limited resources while providing adequate support—or from the perspective of frequent users, whose difficulties often involve limited social support, emotional dysregulation, and intrusive thoughts.

The insights from the present analysis will inform subsequent research aimed at developing and testing practical interventions within helpline services to address these challenges.

Lastly, we address the finding that several participants reported limited access to trauma‐focused therapy. A history of trauma may help explain why helpline responders experience some frequent users as repeatedly retelling past events without achieving sustained relief (Moulds et al. 2020). Rumination is a common emotion regulation strategy among individuals with trauma histories (Seligowski et al. 2015) and is rarely a deliberate choice, but rather an automated process (Birrer and Michael 2011). As described by Judith Herman in Trauma and Recovery (1998), traumatic experiences may be recounted in a 'repetitious, stereotyped, and emotionless' manner, and when not integrated, such narratives may 'not develop or progress in time' (p. 97).

Taken together, these findings point to the need for further exploration of more clinically informed and individualised approaches to frequent use.

Conclusion

This study foregrounds frequent users’ perspectives on anonymous helpline communication and demonstrates how repeated helpline contact is experienced as meaningful and supportive rather than as a failure of intervention. Frequent users describe helpline interactions as micro‐transformative, providing immediate emotional regulation, relief from intrusive thoughts, and temporary containment of loneliness or distress. From a user perspective, relational presence itself is experienced as producing valuable effects in the here and now.

The findings highlight a set of tensions between helpline staff's orientation towards change and progression and frequent users’ ongoing need for situational, repeated support. What responders may perceive as stagnation or lack of development reflects, from the user perspective, chronic vulnerability and limited access to alternative forms of support. Anonymity further contributes to repetitive interactional patterns that are burdensome for both users and responders, shaping strategies such as moving between helplines or disconnecting to secure supportive encounters.

By centring users' lived experiences, the study reframes frequent use as communicative practice with its own logics and incentives rather than merely an organisational problem. This allows for recognising the micro‐transformative value of relational contact, while also acknowledging that both parties in the interaction are engaged in the ongoing struggle to make communication meaningful.
