Tocotrienol-rich vitamin E complex in CADASIL (T3CAD): a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial
Authors: Hugues Chabriat, Lucie Biard, Stéphanie Guey, Nassira Alili, Carla Machado, Aude Jabouley, Sonia Reyes, Jessica Lebenberg, Antoine Guillonnet, Estelle Dubus, Abbas Taleb, Fonsart Julien, Nathalie Dias-Gastellier, Fanny Fernandes, Eric Jouvent, Dominique Hervé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundCerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) is the most common monogenic small vessel disease, causing recurrent stroke and progressive cognitive decline, with no disease-modifying treatment available. Tocotrienols, members of the vitamin E family, have demonstrated neuroprotective effects in preclinical studies. We evaluated the efficacy and safety of a tocotrienol-rich vitamin E complex (HOV12020) in patients with CADASIL.MethodsIn this randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, 51 patients (median age (range): 58 years (43–73 y), male (n = 24, 47%)) with genetically confirmed CADASIL were assigned (1:1) to receive HOV12020 or placebo for 24 months. The primary endpoint was treatment failure, defined as any stroke occurrence, disability progression, or cognitive decline, and was analysed within a Bayesian framework to estimate the probability of treatment benefit. The secondary endpoints included the time to the first failure event, changes in clinical and cognitive scores, and safety. Exploratory MRI endpoints were analysed using quantile regression with Wilcoxon rank-sum sensitivity tests. EudraCT 2019-002867-23; ClinicalTrials.govNCT04658823.FindingsPatients were enrolled in the trial between 15/01/2021 and 11/02/2022. Treatment failure occurred in 68.0% of patients in the HOV12020 group and 61.5% in the placebo group, both exceeding the 40% placebo event rate that was initially expected. Bayesian analysis indicated a low posterior probability of benefit, meeting the predefined futility criteria. No significant differences were observed in the secondary endpoints or exploratory MRI measurements. HOV12020 was well tolerated, with an excellent safety profile and no excess of adverse events.InterpretationHOV12020 did not reduce clinical progression in CADASIL over 24 months. Bayesian analysis confirmed futility, suggesting that tocotrienol-rich vitamin E is unlikely to confer benefits in the advanced stages of the disease. This rigorously designed trial illustrated the potential of Bayesian randomised studies in a rare, slowly progressive cerebral small vessel disease as CADASIL and provides key information for the development of future preventive strategies in cerebral small vessel disease.FundingHovid Berhad (study sponsor).Keywords: CADASIL, Tocopherol, Cerebral small vessel disease, Neuroprotection, Vitamin E, Bayesian model, Clinical trial, MRI markers, Stroke, Cognition, Disability

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed (last search 31 October 2025) using the terms “CADASIL” AND “randomized controlled trial” without language restrictions. We retrieved three randomised controlled trials on CADASIL. To the best of our knowledge, no study has evaluated the clinical outcomes of a palm-derived tocotrienol-rich vitamin E complex (HOV12020). Prior interventional work focusing on antioxidant strategies has been limited and heterogeneous, and most imaging studies were observational or used methods not pre-specified as clinical endpoints.

Search platform: PubMed. Search terms: CADASIL AND (randomised controlled trial OR randomised clinical trial).

Date range: Inception to 31 October 2025. Filters: Article type “Randomised Controlled Trial/Randomised Clinical Trial”; no language limit.

Screening: Titles/abstracts screened for interventional trials in CADASIL; full texts were considered when eligible. This search identified three interventional RCTs in CADASIL. The first (PMID 18296124) tested donepezil over three months for cognitive improvement, the second (PMID 25184356) assessed sapropterin's effects (a tetrahydrobiopterin analogue) on vascular endothelial function, and the third (PMID 37863608) evaluated antihypertensive agents (amlodipine, losartan, atenolol) on cerebrovascular reactivity. While the sapropterin and antihypertensive trials explored mechanisms potentially relevant to disease progression (e.g., endothelial function and cerebrovascular flow reactivity), no significant effects were detected in either study at clinical level. Importantly, none was a preventive trial aimed to reduce the disease progression, and none investigated tocotrienol-rich vitamin E.

Added value of this study

This study represents the first randomised, placebo-controlled trial of a preventive intervention for CADASIL, addressing an unmet therapeutic need. This is also the first study to assess HOV12020 (a palm-derived tocotrienol-rich vitamin E complex) for clinical endpoints in cerebral small vessel disease, with safety and tolerability as primary objectives and treatment failure (stroke occurrence, disability progression, or cognitive decline) as the primary efficacy endpoint. The trial showed no effect on primary clinical endpoints over 24 months in CADASIL, while confirming excellent tolerability. Methodologically, it illustrates the feasibility of a Bayesian small-sample design at a relatively advanced disease stage in a rare, slowly progressive disorder with high follow-up completeness and blinded imaging measures.

Implications of all the available evidence

Taken together, the current evidence does not support a meaningful clinical benefit of tocotrienol-rich vitamin E compounds in the advanced stages of CADASIL. These findings, alongside the feasibility demonstrated here, suggest that future research should prioritise: (1) earlier disease stages in CADASIL; (2) replication in sporadic small vessel disease; and (3) evaluation of alternative therapeutic targets that can be efficiently assessed in small cohorts using Bayesian designs. Safety data remain reassuring and informative for the planning of subsequent trials.

Introduction

Cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) is the most common monogenic form of ischaemic cerebral small vessel diseases (SVD).1,2 The disease is caused by stereotyped mutations in the NOTCH3 gene, which encodes a transmembrane receptor expressed on the surface of vascular smooth muscle cells and pericytes.3,4 All pathogenic variants alter the number of cysteine residues within epidermal growth factor–like repeats in the extracellular domain, resulting in the accumulation of misfolded NOTCH3 protein in the vessel wall.5 This aggregation disrupts extracellular matrix homeostasis, promotes smooth muscle cell degeneration, and impairs the function of cerebral small vessels.5,6

Clinically, CADASIL manifests as migraine with aura, recurrent ischaemic strokes, mood disorders, and progressive cognitive and motor decline.1,7 Most clinical manifestations are related to the accumulation of different cerebral tissue lesions with aging. Disease onset can range from early adulthood to late middle age; in severe forms, patients become fully dependent before death8 in the sixth or seventh decade.8 No disease-modifying therapies are available. Current management is limited to symptomatic treatment, aggressive control of vascular risk factors, and supportive care.9 A single randomised trial of the cholinesterase inhibitor donepezil failed to improve cognitive performance in daily life,10 and no intervention has been shown to alter cerebral lesion accumulation. Therefore, there is an urgent need for treatments that target the underlying vascular pathology or protect the cerebral tissue.

Tocotrienols are natural compounds of the vitamin E family that have shown neuroprotective activity in different experimental models,11 acting through both antioxidant-independent mechanisms and modulation of key neurodegenerative signalling pathways.12,13 In a randomised trial of adults with cardiovascular risk factors and MRI-detected white matter lesions, tocotrienol supplementation over two years was associated with stability of lesion volume compared to progression in placebo recipients.14 CADASIL is also characterised by rapid accrual of white matter damage, often measurable within 18–24 months, and frequent incident lacunes over 2–3 years.15 Combined with its well-defined genetic cause and relatively homogeneous pathology, CADASIL represents a unique translational model for assessing candidate neuroprotective agents in SVD and potentially in cerebrovascular diseases more broadly.16

Therefore, we conducted a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial to assess the safety and potential efficacy of HOV12020, a palm-derived tocotrienol-rich vitamin E complex, in patients with CADASIL. The primary endpoint was treatment failure, defined as stroke occurrence, progression in disability, or cognitive decline, with additional monitoring for adverse events and MRI markers of disease progression.

Methods

Study design

This was a single-centre randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot/phase 2 trial to evaluate the safety and efficacy of 24 months of tocotrienol (HOV12020) supplementation in patients with CADASIL. Participants were recruited from the disease registry and outpatient neurology clinic at Lariboisière Hospital (Assistance Publique–Hôpitaux de Paris, France).

The study was conducted in accordance with the study protocol, the International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice (ICH-GCP) guidelines, and the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (October 2013). The protocol, amendments, and informed consent documents were approved by the French Independent Ethics Committee “Comité de Protection des Personnes Île-de-France 1” (IRB/IORG #IORG0009918; CPP IDF1 dossier number CPPIDF1-2020-ND28, category 1), which granted final approval on 16 April 2020, with an amendment approved on February 4th, 2021 (internal reference T3-CAD-01; national trial number 2019-002867-83). Prior to enrolment, all participants received detailed oral and written information about the study and provided written informed consent.

The trial was registered with the European Union Clinical Trials Register (EudraCT number 2019-002867-23) and ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier NCT04658823) and was sponsored by Hovid Berhad.

Participants

Inclusion criteria

Patients of both sexes were eligible for enrolment if they met all of the following criteria: (1) age 45–75 years inclusive at the time of informed consent; (2) confirmed diagnosis of CADASIL, defined by either (a) a pathogenic NOTCH3 mutation resulting in an odd number of cysteine residues, or (b) a positive skin biopsy showing typical granular osmiophilic material in the vascular wall of small vessels on electron microscopy; (3) presence of at least one prevalent lacune on MRI (3D T1 or FLAIR images); (4) presence of confluent white matter hyperintensities (Fazekas grade 2 or 3) on T2-weighted or FLAIR MRI; (5) Mini-Mental State Examination score ≥15; (6) modified Rankin Scale score 0–3; (7) for women of childbearing potential, not pregnant, not breastfeeding, and willing to use appropriate contraception during treatment and for 28 days thereafter; and (8) ability to provide signed informed consent. Historical data from the French CADASIL cohort at the National Referral Center (data collected between 2013 and 2016) were used to inform the definition of the study population by characterising short-term clinical progression in symptomatic CADASIL. These data guided the selection of eligibility criteria compatible with detecting clinically meaningful change over a 24-month follow-up. Age and MRI-based eligibility thresholds were determined a priori. Inclusion criteria — primarily those based on clinical assessments — were selected to enrich the study population with individuals at increased risk of disease progression within the planned follow-up, while preserving the representativeness of symptomatic CADASIL patients seen in clinical practice. Application of these criteria to the historical dataset showed that 119 of 310 patients (38.4%) were eligible for inclusion.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were not eligible if they had a stroke with persistent neurological deficit within 6 months before screening, any other potential neurodegenerative disorder such as Parkinson's disease or Alzheimer's disease, uncontrolled seizures, untreated hypertension, or any other significant haematological, cardiac, pulmonary, metabolic, neurological, or psychiatric disorder that, in the investigator's opinion, could interfere with the study participation. Additional clinical exclusion criteria included a recent episode of major depression (within the previous 6 months, but clinically stable minor depression was permitted), a history of suicide attempt within 6 months, or a positive response to items 4 or 5 of the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale17 at screening or baseline. Patients were also excluded if they had a myocardial infarction within 3 months before screening, active angina pectoris, symptomatic heart failure, haemophilia, or another disorder increasing the risk of bleeding; an indication for anticoagulant therapy; or a history of cancer within the previous 5 years, except for basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, or stage 1 prostate cancer. Other exclusion criteria were a history of drug or alcohol abuse or dependence, HIV infection, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, severe allergy or intolerance to vitamin E (tocopherols or tocotrienols), known sensitivity to polyoxyl castor oil, risk of allergy to soybean or peanuts, and any contraindication to MRI.

Laboratory-based exclusions were alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, amylase, or lipase levels more than twice the upper limit of normal (ULN), total bilirubin greater than 1.5 × ULN, creatinine clearance (Cockcroft–Gault) less than 60 mL/min, or any other clinically significant abnormality. Patients who used tocotrienol supplementation within 3 months before screening, vitamin E other than the study drug, or cognitive enhancers such as donepezil without a stable dose for at least 3 months were ineligible, as were those who had participated in another interventional clinical trial within 30 days before screening. Pregnant or breastfeeding women were also excluded from the study.

Randomisation and masking

Randomisation was performed centrally by an independent statistician at the contract research organisation (CRO) using permuted block randomisation (1:1), with undisclosed block size and no stratification. The randomisation list was generated independently using SAS software (version 9.4). Participants were enrolled by investigators at the study site, and treatment allocation was implemented centrally by the CRO using the pre-generated randomisation list, ensuring allocation concealment. The study was double-blind: participants, investigators, outcome assessors, and data analysts remained unaware of treatment assignment throughout the trial. The placebo was identical in appearance and administration schedule to the active treatment, thereby maintaining blinding throughout the study.

Procedure

The study consisted of a screening period of up to 28 days, followed by an active treatment phase in which patients were assigned in a 1:1 ratio to one of two groups: treatment with HOV12020, administered as one capsule twice daily (one after the morning meal and one after the evening meal), with each capsule containing 285 mg of total tocotrienols and tocopherols (corresponding to a total daily dose of 570 mg), or Placebo capsules (consisting of vitamin-E–stripped soybean oil with only trace or undetectable isoflavone and no evidence of biologically relevant activity). The detailed composition of HOV12020 and the placebo is provided in the Supplementary Appendix. After randomisation, the subjects were evaluated at baseline, Months 12 and 24 at the clinical site, and Months 3, 6, 9, 15, and 18 by telephone interview. A follow-up telephone call was performed within 28 days of the last study treatment administration. Patients were instructed to return all empty bottles and remaining capsules during each visit on site (M12 and M24). Capsule counting was performed by the study staff to determine patient compliance. Poor treatment compliance was defined as having less than 80% of the total doses taken.

Outcomes

The primary efficacy outcome was the occurrence of one failure event within 24 months after the baseline. A failure event was considered when at least one of the following manifestations was detected during the study period: 1) Stroke events defined as acute clinical neurological deficits consistent with stroke, centrally adjudicated by an independent blinded expert committee, with neuroimaging (MRI or CT) required in all suspected cases; imaging lesions without new neurological symptoms were not classified as stroke events; 2) increase in disability corresponding to an increase in the Modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of at least 1 point to result in a score of 2 or greater (a threshold widely used in clinical trials to reflect clinically meaningful disability)18,19 and judged, based on clinical assessment, to be attributable to CADASIL-related disease progression, and 3) cognitive decline corresponding to an increase of at least 4 points of the Vascular Dementia Assessment Scale Cognitive subscale (VADAS-Cog) score20,21 (a threshold pragmatically derived from ADAS-Cog–based trials).22 Adjudicated strokes were categorized by a blinded, independent Central Adjudication Committee (CEC) according to a predefined charter.

Secondary efficacy endpoints included time-to-event endpoints (the time to the first failure event, the time to the first stroke event, the time to the first increase in disability, and the time to the first occurrence of cognitive decline, as defined previously) and longitudinal change in different clinical scores (Clinical Dementia Rating Scale Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB),23 Mattis Dementia Rating Scale (MDRS),24 initiation/perseveration subscale of the MDRS (MDRS-I/P), Trail Making Test Part B (TMT-B) time, and Trail Making Test Part A (TMT-A) time25; Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) score26,27; and in patient-reported outcomes using the Short Form 3628 and Disability Assessment Dementia.29

Safety was assessed as a key secondary endpoint: number of serious adverse events (AEs), type of severe AEs during the study period, and total number of AEs. All reported AEs were monitored closely. An independent Data and Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) reviewed and monitored the AEs and serious adverse events (SAEs) occurring during the study based on a pre-defined DSMB charter. The following safety assessments were specifically obtained during the study at baseline, M12, and M24: physical examinations, vital sign assessments, ECGs, and suicidal risk monitoring (as a precautionary safety assessment in line with regulatory expectations for long-term trials involving treatments for diseases affecting the central nervous system).

Additional exploratory MRI and biological analyses (Details and references provided in the Appendix “for MRI data acquisition and analysis”)

MRI data were also collected at baseline and M24, as specified in the initial protocol, using a 3T Siemens scanner with predefined imaging sequences and fixed acquisition parameters. However, detailed analytic procedures for the quantitative evaluation of MRI markers were not pre-specified. MRI reading and processing were performed centrally and blinded to treatment allocation and the clinical status. Analyses were conducted to assess incident lacunes on T1-weighted images (defined herein as any new lacune appearing during the follow-up), changes in white matter hyperintensity (WMH) volume (absolute and normalised values relative to the intracranial cavity) on FLAIR images, number of focal hyperintense lesions on diffusion-weighted images (DWI), number of cerebral microbleeds (CMB) on susceptibility-weighted images (SWI), and brain volumes. WMH were segmented with the Brain Intensity AbNormality Classification Algorithm (BIANCA), implemented in the FMRIB Software Library (FSL, Oxford, UK) using centrally developed templates and intensity normalisation and manual correction by an experienced rater (intra- and inter-rater ICC ≥ 0.97). Lacunes were identified according to the STRIVE criteria by automated segmentation with expert review, and incident lacunes were detected by subtracting baseline from follow-up scans (intra-class correlation coefficients (ICC) = 0.96). CMBs were visually rated on susceptibility-weighted imaging with good reproducibility (intra-rater ICC = 0.93, inter-rater ICC = 0.82). The intracranial cavity and cerebrospinal fluid were segmented from T1-weighted MRI using an Advanced Normalisation Tools (ANTs) pipeline. The brain parenchymal fraction (BPF) was calculated as the brain tissue volume (intracranial cavity minus cerebrospinal fluid) divided by the intracranial volume, expressed as a percentage. The MRI analyses were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating and were performed post hoc. Diffusion tensor imaging was acquired in addition to the core MRI sequences on an optional basis, to avoid prolonging the examination and to prioritise the previous key structural measures. The peak width of skeletonized mean diffusivity (PSMD), a diffusion-derived marker of microstructural white matter integrity reflecting the dispersion of mean diffusivity values within the white matter skeleton, was computed.30 Longitudinal change in PSMD between baseline and follow-up was assessed in the subset of participants with available diffusion data.

At late stage of the trial, an institutional biobanking study including patients with CADASIL followed at the centre was initiated (study reference CD/EB 22-138), allowing the collection of blood samples in a subset of 31 participants under a dedicated ethical approval (Inserm Institutional Review Board: IRB00003888, IORG0003254, FWA00005831). Plasma tocopherol concentrations were measured in this subgroup at the time of the final clinical evaluation using a fully accredited method (ISO 15189:2022), based on liquid chromatography with UV detection and a six-point calibration, to document biological exposure to the study treatment.

Statistical analysis

Owing to the rarity of the disease and the need to draw inference from a limited number of participants, a Bayesian framework was adopted to analyse the primary endpoint. This approach allows treatment effects to be evaluated using probabilistic decision rules rather than conventional hypothesis testing. On the basis of historical data from 310 patients followed at the same centre between 2013 and 2016, the proportion of patients experiencing the primary outcome—defined as any stroke event, a ≥1-point increase in modified Rankin Scale score, or a ≥4-point decline in VADAS-Cog—was expected to be approximately 40% at 24 months in the placebo group.

Let πplacebo and πHOV12020 denote the probabilities of experiencing a failure event by 24 months in the placebo and HOV12020 groups, respectively, and let δ = πHOV12020 − πplacebo represent the between-group difference. Bayesian inference was used to update the posterior distribution of δ (the estimated treatment effect after analysing the trial results) using beta prior distributions (different simple starting assumptions about possible treatment benefit before the trial) reflecting non-informative, weakly informative, sceptical, and optimistic assumptions about treatment benefit.

Efficacy was concluded if the posterior probability that δ < 0 (indicating a lower risk of failure with HOV12020) exceeded 90% at 24 months.

Simulation studies with a planned sample size of 40 participants (20 per arm) showed a type I error between 8% and 14% and a 78–97% probability of concluding efficacy when the true event rates were 10% for the treatment and 40% for placebo. Posterior probabilities between 70% and 90% prompted recommendations for additional data or extended follow-up, whereas probabilities below 70% indicated futility. To account for non-treatment-related withdrawals, 50 participants were planned (25 per arm).

The secondary endpoints were the time to the first occurrence of any component of the primary composite endpoint and the time to each individual component, analysed using Cox proportional hazards models with hazard ratios (HR) and 95% CIs, supplemented by Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests.

For the other secondary endpoints (clinical, cognitive, and quality-of-life measures), outcomes were summarized descriptively by treatment group; no formal hypothesis testing was performed.

Primary and secondary efficacy analyses were performed in the full analysis set (FAS), comprising all randomised patients, analysed according to their assigned treatment (intent-to-treat), using all available data for the primary and key secondary endpoints. Additional analysis of data was obtained when deemed appropriate in the per-protocol set (PPS), which included FAS patients without major protocol deviations.

Safety was analysed in the safety set (SS), defined as all patients who received at least one dose of the investigational drug. Bleeding events were monitored as part of routine adverse event collection.

Statistical analyses of clinical data were conducted in accordance with the pre-specified statistical analysis plan (SAP) finalised on 13 October 2023 (v1). The medical analysis dataset was completed and approved on 6 June 2024, prior to database lock and before any unblinding. A revised SAP (v2; 24 July 2024) was subsequently issued to account for minor changes in biological reference ranges following implementation of ORBIS, the hospital-wide centralized electronic health record system. These updates concerned only the reporting framework for laboratory parameters and had no impact on the definition of endpoints, the analytical methods, or the results of the study.

All additional exploratory analyses were post-hoc and limited to participants who underwent MRI at both time points or or who had a blood sample collected for biobanking at end of the study. Imaging lesion quantification was performed independently and in a blinded manner. The primary imaging analysis evaluated the baseline-adjusted median difference between the 2-year and baseline measures, estimated using median quantile regression (τ = 0.5) with 95% CIs obtained from 1000 bootstrap resamples. Sensitivity was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test on unadjusted changes. For available diffusion data, group comparisons of PSMD change over 2 years between the HOV12020 and placebo were performed using a one-way analysis of variance, with effect size estimated using Cohen's d.

Hypothesis testing was conducted at a 5% (2-sided) significance level using SAS Enterprise Guide version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) for clinical data. Imaging data analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). P-values were rounded to four decimal places, with values < 0.0001 reported as such. For continuous variables, a two-sample t-test (normal distribution) or Mann–Whitney U test (non-normal) was used, and for categorical variables, the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test (if expected cell counts <5) was used.

Hovid Berhad acted as the legal sponsor of the study and fulfilled all regulatory sponsor responsibilities in accordance with applicable regulations and Good Clinical Practice. Results

A total of 55 patients were initially enrolled (Fig. 1) at the National Referral Centre for rare cerebrovascular diseases CERVCO (https://cervco.fr/) in Paris, France, between 15/01/2021 and 11/02/2022, follow-up of the last patient ended on the 26/02/2024. All participants had a genetically confirmed diagnosis of CADASIL. The initiation of the trial was delayed by the COVID-19 pandemic; although the study had been largely prepared beforehand, it could only be initiated after the end of the pandemic period, and once recruitment started, study conduct and follow-up were not materially affected. Four patients, who entered the screening period of up to 28 days, failed screening because predefined inclusion or exclusion criteria were not met and 51 patients were subsequently randomised (25 and 26 patients in the HOV12020 and placebo groups, respectively). A total of 46 patients completed the study, and 5 patients withdrew before study completion. A similar proportion of patients withdrew from the study in the HOV12020 group (3/25 patients) and the placebo group (2/26 patients). The demographics and baseline characteristics of the patients are summarised for the FAS in Table 1. Approximately half of the patients were male (28/55 patients), with a mean age of 58 years (range: 45–73 years) and mean BMI of 28 kg/m2 [SD: 5 kg/m2]. Baseline characteristics, including demographic data, clinical history, and prior medications did not differ between the HOV12020 and placebo groups (Table 1). The use of concomitant medications at baseline was limited and comparable across treatment arms: hypolipidemic agents (statins) were taken by 2 patients (10%) in the HOV12020 group versus 4 (20%) in the placebo group; antithrombotic/antiplatelet therapy by 2 (10%) versus 3 (15%); and antihypertensive therapy by 2 (10%) versus 1 (5%), respectively. These medical history details are summarised for the FAS in Appendix—Table A. Treatment compliance was 98%, with no difference between the HOV12020 and placebo groups.

Primary efficacy endpoint

In the FAS population, 33 patients (65%) had a failure event within 24 months, with a similar proportion of patients with such a failure event in the HOV12020 (17/25 patients [68%]) and placebo groups (16/26 patients [62%]). The posterior difference in the probabilities of a failure event within 24 months between HOV12020 and placebo (delta) was 6% (95% CI: −19 to +31%) (Table 2). No efficacy conclusion could be reached as the posterior probabilities of a negative difference in failure rates (delta <0) between the treatment and placebo arms were not >90% (Appendix—Table B).

Secondary efficacy endpoints

Time to failure events

No significant difference was observed between the HOV12020 and placebo groups in the time to first incident failure event (hazard ratio (HR): 1.29 (0.65, 2.55), p-value [log-rank- test]: 0.47) (Appendix–Figure).

In the FAS population, no significant difference was observed between the HOV12020 and placebo group in the time to the first stroke (5 events in each group, HR: 1.09 (0.32, 3.78), p value [log-rank test]: 0.88), in the time to the first incident increase of disability (5 events in the HOV12020 group and 7 in the placebo group, HR: 0.77 (0.24, 2.42), p value [log-rank test]: 0.64), in the time to the first incident event of cognitive decline (10 events in the HOV12020 group and 8 in the placebo group, HR: 1.54 (0.61, 3.91), p value [log-rank test]: 0.36). These results did not change when the analysis was restricted to the PPS population.

Change in cognitive scores

No difference in the mean CDR-SB change was detected between the two groups at Month 24 (Tables 3 and 4). Safety

A total of 172 treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAE) were reported for 46 patients (90.2%) during the study (Table 5), with TEAEs reported for a similar proportion of patients in the HOV12020 and placebo groups. Any TEAE reported during the study was considered to be related to the study treatment. The most common AE was cognitive disorder (18 patients [35.3%]), with a slightly higher proportion of patients reporting this type of event in the HOV12020 group than in the placebo group. No haemorrhagic or bleeding-related safety signal was observed during the trial.

No notable differences were detected between the HOV12020 and placebo groups in terms of vital signs, physical examinations, ECG data, and concomitant medications. During follow-up, one participant required initiation of anticoagulant treatment for venous thrombosis. This treatment was recorded as a concomitant medication and did not require modification of the assigned study treatment. No bleeding complications occurred in this context.

Exploratory MRI and biological measures

At baseline, all MRI markers were similar between the groups, except for the cerebral microbleed counts, which were slightly higher in the placebo group (Appendix–Tables C and D).

From baseline to month 24, brain parenchymal fraction (BPF) decreased while WMH volume (absolute and normalised values), and incident lacunes significantly increased in both treatment groups (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 0.0001 for all; data not shown). In contrast, within-group changes in cerebral microbleed (CMB) counts were not significant (median [range] 0 [–1 to 1] in each group).

At baseline, CMB were present in 17/24 patients in the placebo group (range: 1–154). After 24 months, their counts decreased in nine and increased in seven patients (n = 24). At baseline, CMB were present in 13/23 patients (range: 1–281) in the HOV12020 group. Their counts decreased in four and increased in nine patients in the HOV12020 group (n = 23; χ2 test–placebo versus HOV12020, p = 0.2974).

Median quantile regression (τ = 0.5) for BPF, WMH, and incident lacunes, with Wilcoxon rank-sum sensitivity analyses did not identify any treatment-related differences (Appendix–Table E).

PSMD longitudinal change was available in a subset of 34 participants (18 in the HOV12020 group and 16 in the placebo group). Mean PSMD change was 121 ± 102 10−4 mm2/s in the HOV12020 group and 92 ± 108 10−4 mm2/s in the placebo group, with a between-group difference of −30 (95% CI −333 to 274). No treatment-related effect was detected (ANOVA, p = 0.84; Cohen's d = −0.07).

At end of follow-up, mean blood tocopherol concentrations (μmol/L) measured in patients who were able to participate in the establishment of the local biobank were higher in the active group (n = 16) than in the placebo group (n = 15) (mean [95% CI] 43.9 [38.3–49.6] versus 33.5 [30.2–36.8]).

Discussion

In this randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in patients with CADASIL, treatment with a tocotrienol-rich vitamin E complex did not reduce the occurrence of the primary clinical endpoint over 24 months, and the posterior probability of experiencing a failure event over this period did not differ between the two groups.

The absence of treatment effect is unlikely to be attributable to insufficient statistical power, as the observed incidence of primary endpoint events was substantially higher than anticipated. Specifically, failure events occurred in 68% of patients assigned to active treatment and in 62% of those receiving placebo, compared with the 40% event rate assumed in the Bayesian design. This unexpectedly high event rate most plausibly reflects selection bias inherent to rare-disease trials, in which individuals with recent or clinically meaningful disease progression are more likely to be referred to, or volunteer for therapeutic studies conducted in expert centres—often requiring long-distance travel—than patients represented in historical cohorts. In addition, differences in event ascertainment are likely to have contributed to the elevated number of detected events. In the present trial, all cerebrovascular events were prospectively and systematically adjudicated using combined clinical and imaging criteria, capturing not only strokes with persistent neurological deficits but also transient neurological symptoms associated with acute ischaemic lesions on MRI that may have been classified differently or incompletely captured in historical datasets. Importantly, however, these factors would be expected to affect both treatment groups similarly and therefore do not account for the lack of separation between the groups. Consistent results in both the full analysis set and the per-protocol population further support this interpretation and strengthen confidence in the robustness of the overall findings. In this context, the composite endpoint combines outcomes that may differ in their clinical relevance. The component-specific Kaplan–Meier curves did not indicate a concentrated treatment effect within any single domain, and the limited number of events did not allow for adequately powered analyses of individual components.

Regarding safety and tolerability, comparison with the placebo group showed that HOV12020 was well tolerated, with an excellent safety profile and no evidence of adverse effects.

Secondary analyses, including exploratory MRI markers, did not reveal any evidence of treatment effects. Although the progression of different imaging measures, particularly WMH and lacunes, was confirmed over the study period in both groups, they were not pre-specified outcomes and should be interpreted cautiously. Variations in cerebral microbleed counts over time likely reflect methodological variability related to independent and non–side-by-side MRI reading at each time point, rather than true biological change, with no evidence of significant change. Diffusion tensor imaging–derived PSMD was available only in a two third of participants and showed no between-group differences; these findings do not alter the interpretation of the primary imaging results.

These results contrast with those of an earlier study performed in sporadic SVD, suggesting that palm vitamin E tocotrienols might attenuate white matter hyperintensities progression on T2-weighted MRI and, therefore, could benefit patients with CADASIL at clinical level. Our trial differed from this study in several important ways. First, it is the first study to assess clinical endpoints as primary outcomes with vitamin E compounds to prevent the progression of SVD. Second, patients with CADASIL were included at a relatively advanced disease stage. All were symptomatic and had at least one lacune and extensive WMH. Conversely, only healthy volunteers with no symptoms and WMH approximately 50 times smaller were included in the earlier trial. Differences in MRI quantification methods and the absence of translation from possible limited imaging effects to clinical benefits could also account for the different findings. It should also be noted that in our study, WMH volume changes were nearly tenfold greater than those previously reported and that, consequently, small treatment-effects might also have been masked given the pronounced disease progression in the present CADASIL population.

Biological factors may also account for the absence of an observed treatment effect. Although CADASIL is considered a model of severe, progressive small-vessel disease, the neuroprotective properties of vitamin E may be less effective in advanced stages, when irreversible tissue damage and accumulated lacunes are already present. Moreover, the pathophysiological mechanisms driving disease progression at this stage in CADASIL may not be adequately targeted by antioxidant therapy.

This study has several potential limitations.

First, the sample size was constrained by the rarity of the monogenic disease under investigation and by the deliberate focus on patients at a stage of rapid clinical progression, which necessarily restricted eligibility. This constraint was further compounded by organisational requirements of the study design to ensure consistency in clinical and imaging assessments, the trial was conducted at a single centre, requiring long-distance travel for some participants and imposing a substantial logistical burden. Nevertheless, these constraints were explicitly anticipated and incorporated a priori into the trial design. The planned sample size was selected to align with the Bayesian analytical framework, which is particularly well suited to rare diseases and small populations and allows estimation of both the probability and magnitude of a potential neuroprotective effect of vitamin E. Consequently, although the limited sample size may restrict generalisability, it does not undermine the internal validity of the study or the interpretation of the results.

A second limitation is that the study was powered for a composite primary endpoint based on predefined, discrete clinical events reflecting stepwise disease progression, rather than for detecting very small or incremental treatment effects on individual clinical outcomes. Consistent with this design, exploratory MRI analyses revealed large changes in lesion volume, reflecting substantial disease-related variability and limiting sensitivity to subtle treatment-related differences. Consequently, these negative findings may not be generalizable to other forms of small vessel disease or to earlier stages of CADASIL. Additionally, while the follow-up period was sufficient to capture disease progression, it may have been too short to detect longer-term treatment effects.

Finally, although the possibility of unreported vitamin E supplementation cannot be entirely excluded, several methodological safeguards were implemented to minimise this risk. The use of any vitamin or dietary supplement was an explicit exclusion criterion at enrolment, and participants were repeatedly counselled against initiating new supplements during the study. Concomitant treatments were systematically reviewed at each clinic visit and interim telephone contact, with no use of vitamin E or tocotrienol-rich supplements reported or identified. In addition, end-of-study plasma tocopherol measurements available in a subset of participants showed clear separation between treatment groups, supporting adequate biological exposure to the study treatment and making substantial placebo contamination unlikely. Together, these elements suggest that unreported supplementation did not materially influence the trial results. Baseline dietary intake and vitamin E levels were not assessed. Although randomisation is intended to distribute measured and unmeasured characteristics between groups, such balance in background nutritional status cannot be ensured in a trial of this size, and residual imbalance cannot be excluded.

The trial also has several important strengths. It was rigorously designed, with blinded outcome assessment, high completeness of follow-up, and centralised imaging analysis. The number of observed events was consistent with, and even exceeded, projections from the Bayesian design model, supporting the robustness of the power estimation. A further strength of this study is the explicit evaluation of the potential influence of NOTCH3 mutation location, a biologically plausible source of heterogeneity in CADASIL. For the primary outcome, sensitivity analyses adjusting for EGFr domain did not materially alter the main conclusions, supporting the robustness of the findings to this potential source of confounding.

In summary, this trial found no effect of a tocotrienol-rich vitamin E preparation on primary clinical endpoints in patients with CADASIL over 24 months, although the treatment was safe and well tolerated. The high incidence of failure events observed indicates that clinical progression in this population is frequent and difficult to modify within a two-year timeframe. Importantly, the present study does not provide evidence regarding the efficacy of the tocotrienol-rich vitamin E preparation at earlier stages of CADASIL, where lower event rates would be expected and detection of treatment effects would require different trial designs, larger sample sizes, or longer follow-up. Beyond the treatment-specific findings, this study primarily demonstrates the feasibility of evaluating alternative preventive strategies, including putative neuroprotective approaches, in CADASIL using reasonable and limited sample sizes in symptomatic patients who have already developed diffuse WMH and at least one lacune at cerebral level.
