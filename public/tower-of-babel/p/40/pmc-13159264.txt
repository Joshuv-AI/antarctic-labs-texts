Temperature-driven metabolic adaptation in thermophilic microbial communities of Western Sichuan hot springs
Authors: Sainan Luo, Xiuzhen Chen, Shunchao Guo, Songnian Hu, Zhiyang Dong, Jianing Geng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding microbial adaptation to extreme environments remains a key challenge in microbial ecology. Geothermal hot springs, characterized by temperature gradients and varying geochemical conditions, represent valuable natural laboratories for studying microbial diversity, adaptive strategies, and evolutionary mechanisms. However, despite many studies of hot spring communities, how temperature gradients shape key microbial adaptation strategies remains insufficiently understood, limiting our ability to explain survival and function in extreme environments.ResultsOur study investigated microbial community composition and functional profiles across a natural thermal gradient (50–93 °C) in six hot springs on the Western Sichuan Plateau using optimized contig- and MAG-based metagenomic strategies. Enhanced annotation approaches significantly improved taxonomic resolution in these extreme environments. Metagenomic analyses revealed distinct shifts in microbial communities along the thermal gradient: moderate-temperature springs (50–70 °C) were dominated by Pseudomonadota and Bacteroidota, exhibiting heterotrophic flexibility and utilizing the Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle and diverse nitrogen reduction pathways; high-temperature springs (70–90 °C) were enriched in Chloroflexota, which primarily employed the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway coupled with enhanced sulfur metabolism; and extreme-temperature springs (≥ 90 °C) were characterized by Aquificota and Thermoproteota, relying on specialized autotrophic pathways (rTCA, DH/HH cycles), streamlined nitrogen assimilation, and sulfur oxidation pathways. These thermophilic lineages showed genome streamlining, reduced regulatory complexity, and specialized metabolic strategies, reflecting narrower ecological niches and deeper phylogenetic branches.ConclusionsThis metagenomic investigation across a temperature gradient in western Sichuan hot springs highlights temperature as an essential driver of microbial community structure, genome evolution, and adaptive specialization. Thermophilic lineages in extreme-temperature environments exhibited streamlined genomes, specialized metabolic functions, and narrower ecological niches, consistent with adaptation to persistent thermal stress. The findings enhance understanding of microbial evolutionary strategies and underscore the ecological significance of temperature-driven adaptation in extreme environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04921-z.

Introduction

Geothermal hot springs, as representative high-temperature extreme ecosystems on Earth, are characterized by their abundance of reducing gases and variable geochemical conditions [1–3]. These factors have created unique and relatively stable microbial habitats [4, 5], making hot springs important natural laboratories for investigating the functional potential of thermophilic and hyperthermophilic microorganisms. Hot spring environments have been widely recognized for their strong resemblance to early Earth conditions [6], representing some of the most primitive lifestyles in prokaryotic communities and thus providing critical natural models for exploring the origin of early life [7]. Over long-term adaptation to high-temperature conditions, microbial communities are thought to have developed specialized metabolic strategies and molecular mechanisms for thermal adaptation [8–11]. Therefore, studying the composition and functions of hot spring microbiomes holds significant theoretical value for understanding how microorganisms expand their ecological niche and respond to environmental stress through adaptive evolution [12]. In addition, hot springs play indispensable roles in global biogeochemical cycles [13, 14]. Thermophilic and hyperthermophilic microorganisms, which are widely distributed in these environments, contribute unique ecological functions and adaptation strategies in the acquisition and transformation of key nutrients such as carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur [6, 15, 16]. A comprehensive understanding of microbial communities and their nutrient utilization strategies in hot springs can be achieved through metagenomic approaches, which allow for the identification of key functional genes driving biogeochemical cycles and the reconstruction of nutrient cycling pathways. Such efforts are essential for deepening our understanding of microbial contributions to element cycling in extreme ecosystems [17, 18].

In hot spring ecosystems, temperature is not only a key factor influencing microbial community structure but may also profoundly shape microbial genomic traits and ecological adaptation strategies [6, 19, 20]. Thermal gradients are typically associated with changes in resource availability and environmental stress: moderate-temperature environments often feature higher nutrient availability and lower levels of stress, whereas extreme high-temperature conditions tend to be resource-poor and highly stressful [21]. During long-term adaptation to such distinct ecological niches, microorganisms may evolve markedly different genome-based adaptive traits [22, 23]. Traits related to growth, stress tolerance, and resource acquisition form the basis of microbial strategies ranging from copiotrophic to oligotrophic lifestyles [24]. At the same time, microbial niche breadth—the ability to survive and reproduce across diverse environments—is closely linked to genomic functional diversity [25]. Taxa with broader ecological distributions tend to encode a greater variety of functional modules associated with growth, nutrient acquisition, and stress response, enabling survival across heterogeneous habitats [24]. In contrast, specialist microorganisms typically possess fewer functional traits and adopt more narrowly focused adaptive strategies. Therefore, as a key gradient shaping microbial niche and genome evolution, temperature warrants systematic investigation of its coupling with genomic complexity and niche breadth, particularly in extreme environments.

In recent years, substantial progress has been made in hot spring microbial research globally, particularly in regions such as America [26], Iceland [27], India, Japan [28, 29], and China. Studies conducted in Yellowstone National Park have revealed how thermophilic microorganisms utilize sulfide, nitrogen sources, and other extreme conditions for metabolism [30–32]. Investigations of Indian hot springs have mainly focused on the adaptive mechanisms driven by functional genes, highlighting how microbial survival and ecological fitness are enhanced through functional integration under extreme environmental stress [33, 34]. In the Tengchong hot springs of China, research has centered on microbial community diversity and metabolic potential, particularly the functional genomic features under high-temperature stress [35–37]. Among environmental factors, temperature has long been considered one of the most influential drivers shaping microbial community distribution in hot springs [38]. Despite the substantial body of study on community composition and metabolic potential, most existing studies have focused on single environmental conditions, and a systematic comparative analysis of functional diversity and adaptive strategies across temperature gradients remain lacking.

Located on the eastern edge of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, western Sichuan is characterized by significant geographic transitions, frequent tectonic activity, and diverse geothermal systems [39, 40]. These factors contribute to the formation of unique microbial habitats. The coexistence of biogeographic isolation and species migration in this region may have resulted in distinct evolutionary trajectories and functional features of microbial communities compared to other geothermal areas [19, 38, 41]. However, metagenomic and functional ecological studies of hot springs in western Sichuan are still in their infancy, with limited data and sparse sampling coverage. In this study, six representative hot springs located in western Sichuan Province, spanning a broad geographic distribution and a temperature range of 55 °C to 93 °C, were selected as a model system to investigate microbial dynamics in geothermal environments. Through metagenomic analyses, we systematically examined microbial community composition and functional responses associated with temperature variations. Key microbial lineages dominant at different thermal conditions were identified, and nine representative thermophilic phyla were selected for comparative genomic analysis to explore their adaptive features and metabolic strategies along the temperature gradient. This study aims to elucidate the mechanisms underlying microbial ecological niche expansion and functional adaptation in high-temperature extreme environments. Our findings address critical knowledge gaps concerning geothermal ecosystems on the eastern margin of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and offer novel insights into microbial adaptation mechanisms and evolutionary trajectories under thermal stress. Furthermore, this research underscores the significance of western Sichuan as a unique natural laboratory for exploring microbial evolution in extreme thermal settings.

Materials and methods

Sample collection, DNA extraction, and sequencing

Nine water samples for our study were collected from outlets of six hot springs located in Western Sichuan Province, China (N26°03′ ~ 34°19′, E97°21′ ~ 108°31′) (Fig. 1A). These sites were selected to capture a broad thermal gradient (50 °C to 93 °C) and geographic diversity across the region. All water samples were collected from the outflow point near the spring vent, where the water emerges and temperature is highest and most stable. This approach ensured that the sampled microbial communities reflect those adapted to the core thermal conditions of each spring. The temperature range measured of these six hot springs was between 50℃ and 93℃, and the pH range was between 6 and 6.5 (Fig. 1B). Microbial cells were collected by filtering approximately 1–2 L of hot spring water sequentially through sterile 0.45-µm and 0.22-µm pore-size filters (Millipore) using a vacuum-pressure pump in the field. Filters were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and transported on dry ice for downstream processing. Total genomic DNA was extracted from the trapped microbial cells using the Quick Soil DNAonly Isolation Kit (Huayueyang Biotech, Beijing, China). The concentration and purity of extracted DNA were assessed using a Nano-600 spectrophotometer (Jiapeng, China). Approximately 300 ng of DNA was loaded onto a 1% agarose gel to evaluate DNA integrity by electrophoresis. The metagenome library was prepared using MGIEasy Universal DNA Library Prep Kit V1.0, following the manufacturer’s protocols. Shotgun sequencing was performed using the sequencing platform of MGISEQ-2000 (BGI Genomics Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) utilizing a 150 bp paired-end approach with index.

Metagenome assembly with short reads and analysis

Raw metagenomic reads were quality trimmed using trim_galore (v0.6.10, parameters: -a AAGTCGGAGGCCAAGCGGTCTTAGGAAGACAA -a2 AAGTCGGATCGTAGCCATGTCGTTCTGTGAGCCAAGGAGTTG --quality 20 --stringency 10 -length 75 -e 0.1 --paired). The remaining reads from each sample were used to calculate the distances among the whole metagenomes using the Mashtree (v1.4.6, parameters: --reps 100, --, --min-depth 0). Samples D and E, as well as G, H, and I, exhibited extremely small pairwise distances and clustered tightly together in the Mashtree analysis, indicating high genomic similarity (Fig. 1B). Given their spatial proximity and nearly identical metagenomic profiles, the remaining reads were merged as DE and GHI, respectively, for downstream analysis. Nonpareil 3.0 [42] was used with default settings to estimate the coverage of the microbial community achieved by the sequencing effort applied. The trend line of Nd values was calculated and displayed using a linear regression algorithm implemented in R (v4.4.0).

Metagenome reads were assembled into contigs using Megahit (v1.2.9), with default parameters. Contigs with length ≥ 1000 bp were retained. Metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) were built and refined using MetaWRAP (v1.3.2) with default parameters, which integrated the binning results of metaBAT2 (v2.12.1), MaxBin2 (v2.2.6), and CONCOCT (v1.0.0). A thorough taxonomy-based post-binning refinement process was then implemented for each MAG meeting the criteria of completeness ≥ 50% and contamination < 10%. All recovered MAGs had a quality score value [defined as completeness – (5 × contamination)] of ≥ 50 [43, 44].

Microbial community composition

Reads and contigs, assembled from the reads, were initially annotated by Kraken2 with the GTDB V220 database (Release 220). For the MAGs, classification was conducted using the GTDB-Tk classify_wf workflow against the GTDB genome database (Release 220) [45]. We implemented four distinct approaches to enhance taxonomic profiling: (1) direct annotation of reads with Kraken2; (2) inference of taxonomic composition from reads mapped to annotated contigs; (3) inference from reads mapped to annotated MAGs; and (4) a comprehensive strategy involving initial mapping to annotated contigs, followed by alignment of unmapped reads to annotated MAGs, from which microbial community composition was deduced. The fourth method was selected to infer microbial community composition.

Phylogenetic and genomic feature analysis, gene annotation, and metabolic analysis

The dataset used in this analysis includes 217 MAGs, representing the following 9 phyla: Pseudomonadota (72), Actinomycetota (8), Bacteroidota (54), Deinococcota (13), Bacillota (4), Verrucomicrobiota (15), Chloroflexota (47), Aquificota (10), and Thermoproteota (21) (Table S1). The phylogenetic trees for bacterial and archaeal MAGs were constructed based on multiple sequence alignments of conserved proteins generated by the GTDB-Tk workflow, using IQ-TREE v2.0.3 with the parameters: -m JTT, -alrt 1000, and -bb 1000 [46]. The phylogenetic tree, rooted with Thermoproteota, was visualized and annotated using the online tree visualization tool iTOL (v7) [47]. Protein-coding sequences were predicted with Prodigal V2.6.0 [48]. The ribosomal RNA (rRNA) was annotationed by RNAmmer v1.2 and supplemented by barrnap v0.9 [49]. The tRNA genes were annotated by tRNAscan-SE v2.0.12 [50]. The amino acid frequency was calculated using the aa_usage module of the CompareM software. For further functional annotation, individual MAGs were analyzed using eggnog-mapper v2 [51] with default parameters and the RefSeq database from NCBI, utilizing DIAMOND v2.1.8. Cazymes were annotated by bCAN3 [52]. The key genes (Table S2) in metabolism pathways and criteria for metabolic pathway completeness were referenced, as demonstrated [53]. The data analysis results were visualized using Rscript.

Results

Composite mapping significantly improves annotation efficiency in hot spring metagenomes

A total of six metagenomes, representing pooled samples from each sampling site, were generated in this study. The metagenomes comprised between 62 million and 190 million reads per sample (22–62 Gbp), with an average read length of approximately 145 bp following quality trimming (Table S3). Coverage estimation using the Nonpareil method demonstrated that five of the samples (A, DE, C, F, GHI) achieved an average coverage exceeding 90%, with sample J exhibited a coverage of 60%, indicating sufficient sequencing depth for downstream analyses (Fig. 1C). Initial taxonomic annotation using Kraken2 showed read utilization rates ranging from 25.82% to 52% (Fig. 1D), suggesting suboptimal annotation efficiency for samples derived from extreme environments. To overcome this limitation, reads were mapped to annotated contigs and metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), substantially increasing annotation rates to 56.47%–95.58% and 54.65%–93.18%, respectively (Fig. 1D). To maximize species-level resolution and enhance the accuracy of microbial community profiling, a combined approach of mapping reads to both annotated contigs and MAGs was implemented. This integrative strategy achieved the highest annotation rates, ranging from 68.92% to 97.75% (Fig. 1D), offering a more comprehensive view of community structure.

Temperature-associated thermophilic microbial diversity and community structure

Microbial diversity was approximated by sequence diversity (Nd) derived from Nonpareil curves (Fig. 2A), which correlates well with traditional metrics such as the Shannon index [54]. A decreasing trend in species diversity with increasing temperature was observed through linear regression analysis. WPGMA clustering based on species-level profiles grouped the six hot spring samples into three community types: Moderate (samples A and J), High (samples DE and C), and Extreme (samples F and GHI) (Fig. 2B). The correlation between bacterial community structure and geochemical parameters was evaluated using the envfit function. The first two axes explained 22.77% and 19.71% of the variance, respectively (Fig. 2C). Temperature showed a strong correlation with community structure (R² = 0.7398), though not statistically significant (P = 0.1097).

Microbial communities in hot springs demonstrated clear temperature-dependent enrichment patterns, with distinct shifts toward specialized taxa as temperatures increased (Fig. 2D). Although several phyla were consistently detected across all sites, their relative abundances varied markedly along the thermal gradient (Table 1). Pseudomonadota dominated from 50 °C to 90 °C, reaching a peak of peaking at 63.19% at 85 °C and then plummeting to 6.76% at 93 °C, suggesting its thermal sensitivity to extreme temperatures. In contrast, Bacteroidota and Actinomycetota declined steadily with increasing temperatures, from 7.91% to 14.34% at 50 °C to 1.15% and 4.31% at 93 °C, respectively. Meanwhile, Bacillota showed gradual enrichment, rising from 1.85% at 50 °C to 6.44% at 93 °C, indicative of thermotolerance. At 65 °C, Verrucomicrobiota emerged as the second most abundant phylum (17.94%), yet it was nearly undetectable at other sites (Table 1). Chloroflexota dominated at 80 °C and 85 °C, with relative abundances of 16.27% and 11.27%, respectively, but declined to 1.59% at 93 °C, highlighting its preference for intermediate thermal conditions. In high- and extreme-temperature springs (80–93 °C), Deinococcota displayed increasing prominence, reaching 11.3% at 90 °C. Similarly, Thermoproteota rose from 11.24% at 90 °C to 13.81% at 93 °C, becoming the second most abundant phylum. Notably, Aquificota dominated exclusively at 93 °C, accounting for 39.89% of the community, underscoring its strong adaptation to extreme thermal environments.

Thermal specialization aligns with deep phylogenetic origin and narrower niche breadth

Bacterial lineages with higher thermotolerance were associated with deeper phylogenetic branches and narrower ecological distributions, whereas more recently diverged groups appear to have adapted to broader temperature ranges and more variable environments. Phylogenetic analysis rooted with Thermoproteota revealed a clear correlation between branch depth and thermal preference. The hyperthermophilic phylum Aquificota, dominant at 93 °C, occupied the deepest bacterial branch, followed sequentially by Deinococcota, Actinomycetota, Chloroflexota, Bacillota, Verrucomicrobiota, Pseudomonadota, and Bacteroidota (Fig. 3). This topology corresponded with temperature gradients: deep-branching phyla were predominantly enriched in high- and extreme-temperatures springs, whereas shallow-branching groups favored moderate- temperatures. Moreover, deeper branches exhibited narrow niche breadth; Aquificota and Thermoproteota were restricted to four and eight habitat types, respectively, indicating strong thermal specialization. In contrast, phyla occupying shallower branches displayed wider niche breadth, consistent with greater environmental adaptability (Table S4).

Thermoadaptive genome streamlining and functional trade-offs shape microbial life strategies across the thermal gradient

A comparative analysis of 217 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) from nine thermophilic phyla revealed genome streamlining in response to increasing thermotolerance. Hyperthermophilic Aquificota and Thermoproteota exhibited significantly smaller genome sizes (1.56 Mb and 1.25 Mb, respectively) compared to mesophilic Bacteroidota and Pseudomonadota. This streamlining was accompanied by a loss of regulatory genes and a reduction in functional complexity (Fig. 4A-B, Fig. S2A). Aquificota, found in extreme-temperature environments, had regulatory factors limited to six COG categories, while Bacteroidota and Pseudomonadota retained 18–20 categories. Analysis of carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) revealed that Aquificota and Thermoproteota, compared to mesophilic phyla, tended to retain core GH and GT enzymes.

Energy-conserving and efficiency-enhancing strategies underlying thermal adaptation of carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur metabolism in thermophilic lineages

Temperature-driven shifts in carbon fixation pathways

Thermophilic microbes displayed a clear transition from heterotrophic diversity in moderate temperatures to autotrophic specialization under extreme thermal conditions. In 50–90 °C hot springs, Pseudomonadota predominantly utilized partial Calvin-Benson-Bassham (CBB) and 3-Hydroxypropionate (3 H) cycles, found in 37/72 and 18/72 MAGs, respectively. Chloroflexota showed greater metabolic diversity, with 19/47 MAGs encoding a complete Wood-Ljungdahl (WL) pathway and 22/47 MAGs containing an incomplete 3 H cycle. At extreme temperatures, Aquificota employed complete reductive tricarboxylic acid (rTCA) cycles in 3/10 MAGs, while Thermoproteota mainly relied on the dicarboxylate-hydroxybutyrate (DH) and hydroxypropionate-hydroxybutyrate (HH) cycle for carbon fixation (Fig. 6A-B).

Streamlined nitrogen metabolism along the thermal gradient

Thermophilic lineages showed a progressive simplification of nitrogen metabolism with rising temperatures, shifting from diverse, loss-prone processes to efficient, nitrogen-retaining pathways. In moderate-to-high-temperature springs (50–90 °C), Bacteroidota, Pseudomonadota, Verrucomicrobiota, Chloroflexota, and Deinococcota exhibited diverse nitrogen pathways, including partial dissimilatory nitrate reduction (DNR) and denitrification. Verrucomicrobiota favored incomplete DNR and partial assimilatory nitrate reduction (ANR), while Chloroflexota combined partial DNR with some complete nitrogen fixation pathways (Fig. 6A). As temperatures increased, Verrucomicrobiota, Chloroflexota, and Deinococcota showed higher proportions of MAGs involved in nitrogen cycling (Fig. 6B). In extreme-temperature springs, Aquificota and Thermoproteota lacked DNR entirely; Aquificota relied on complete ANR, while Thermoproteota utilized an incomplete nitrification process.

Temperature-dependent sulfur oxidation and reduction

Sulfur metabolism was highly diverse across 217 MAGs. In moderate springs, Bacteroidota, Pseudomonadota, and Verrucomicrobiota dominated sulfur pathways. Verrucomicrobiota showed the highest proportion of complete assimilatory sulfate reduction (ASR), while Pseudomonadota exhibited complete dissimilatory sulfate reduction (DSR). In high-temperature environments, Chloroflexota contained the most MAGs with DSR genes, primarily in partial pathways. Deinococcota, common in both high- and extreme-temperatures, was enriched in thiosulfate disproportionation. At extreme temperatures, Aquificota and Thermoproteota dominated sulfur cycling. Aquificota encoded high levels of sulfide: quinone oxidoreductase (sqr) and pathways for sulfite and thiosulfate oxidation (Fig. 6B). In contrast, Thermoproteota showed limited sulfur oxidation capacity, relying mainly on sqr but demonstrating higher sulfur reduction potential.

Integrated nutrient acquisition in extreme hot springs

Microbial communities in extreme-temperature hot springs adopted integrated nutrient acquisition strategies. Sulfur oxidation, primarily driven by Aquificota and Thermoproteota, provided electrons for carbon fixation and ATP for nitrogen assimilation (Fig. 6C, red arrows). This sulfur-driven energy flow supported efficient autotrophic carbon fixation pathways, including rTCA cycle in Aquificota and DH/HH cycles in Thermoproteota, replacing less efficient heterotrophic strategies typical of lower temperatures. Nitrogen metabolism was streamlined, favoring ANR over energy-intensive DNR and denitrification. The coupling of sulfur, carbon, and nitrogen pathways minimized redundancy, enhanced energy conservation, and optimized survival under extreme conditions.

Discussion

For decades, ecologists have adhered to the concept proposed by Baas-Becking, “everything is everywhere, but the environment selects,” which has shaped our understanding of microbial biogeography and adaptation strategies [55, 56]. As temperature and pH emerge as key environmental factors influencing species evolution and ecological strategies, these gradients play critical roles in shaping microbial niches. While previous studies have primarily focused on microbial responses along pH gradients, temperature preferences are increasingly recognized for their influence on phylogenetic positioning, ecological niche breadth, and genome complexity [23, 57]. Temperature, in particular, is a fundamental driver of microbial community composition, metabolic adaptation, and genome evolution, which is further exemplified in extreme environments like hot springs [57]. Here, we integrate deep metagenomic sequencing data from six geochemically distinct hot springs across a wide thermal range to provide a high-resolution perspective on microbial diversity and genome-level adaptation. Unlike traditional 16 S rRNA-based surveys, our approach enables a direct comparison of functional genomic features and ecological strategies across taxa. By systematically comparing the genomes of nine representative thermophilic phyla, our study contributes to understanding of how niche breadth, functional traits, and adaptive strategies of microbiomes are shaped along a thermal gradient. These patterns align with ecological axes of resource availability and environmental stress, offering a framework to interpret microbial diversity and specialization in extreme environments (Fig. 7).

Temperature-driven ecological distribution and metabolic specialization

Our study demonstrates clear shifts in microbial community composition and metabolic strategies across the thermal gradient (50–93 °C) in Western Sichuan hot springs. In moderate temperatures (50–70 °C), Pseudomonadota and Bacteroidota dominated, primarily utilizing the Calvin-Benson-Bassham (CBB) cycle for carbon fixation. This pathway, coupled with heterotrophic flexibility, may allow these phyla to dynamically adjust to fluctuating organic carbon availability [58]. Nitrogen metabolism in these moderate environments is diverse, featuring dissimilatory nitrate reduction (DNR) and partial denitrification, enabling adaptation to variable redox conditions [59].

As temperatures increase to high-temperature ranges (70–90 °C), community composition shifts toward Chloroflexota, which predominantly employs the Wood-Ljungdahl (WL) pathway. This pathway is recognized for its high energy efficiency and thermal stability [58, 60], which could confer an advantage under energy-limited and high-temperature conditions. Additionally, these microbes including Deinococcota, employ direct nitrogen utilization strategies like ANR and engage in sulfur metabolism through thiosulfate disproportionation, optimizing their energy acquisition and adapting effectively to dynamic redox conditions [61, 62].

In extreme high-temperature springs (93 °C), microbial communities become increasingly streamlined, with Aquificota and Thermoproteota dominating. These phyla utilize specialized carbon fixation pathways, including the reductive tricarboxylic acid (rTCA) cycle, dicarboxylate-hydroxybutyrate (DH) cycle, and hydroxypropionate-hydroxybutyrate (HH) cycle, which are likely adapted to anaerobic and high-temperature conditions [63]. In contrast, the shift from extreme high-temperature, anoxic environments to cooler, oxygen- and nutrient-rich settings is marked by the increasing prevalence of oxygen-insensitive carbon fixation pathways like the 3 H cycle and nutrient-efficient routes such as the CBB cycle [64]. The nitrogen metabolism of Aquificota and Thermoproteota shifts entirely to assimilatory nitrate reduction (ANR), effectively minimizing nitrogen loss [61]. Furthermore, Aquificota is highly enriched in sulfide: quinone oxidoreductase (sqr) and pathways for sulfite and thiosulfate oxidation, supporting chemolithoautotrophic growth in oxygen-limited, high-temperature environments [65].

Genome streamlining and thermal resilience

Temperature not only reshapes community composition but also drives genome streamlining and metabolic optimization. In extreme thermophiles, Aquificota and Thermoproteota display significantly reduced genome sizes, elevated GC content, and fewer regulatory genes, minimizing metabolic redundancy and conserving energy [66, 67]. This reduction may enhance thermal stability and cellular efficiency, reflecting potential evolutionary adaptation to persistent high-temperature stress.

Evolutionary implications and niche expansion

The dominance of Aquificota and Thermoproteota in hyperthermal environments is consistent with the hypothesis that these lineages represent ancient evolutionary strategies adapted to early Earth’s volcanic and hydrothermal conditions [68]. Their metabolic specialization and genomic streamlining are consistent with survival in high-temperature, low-nutrient environments. In contrast, Pseudomonadota and Bacteroidota, which dominate in moderate-temperature springs, exhibit broader metabolic flexibility, suggesting a capacity for ecological expansion as global temperatures decreased over geological time. This temperature-driven distribution mirrors the evolutionary trajectory from thermophilic origins to mesophilic diversification.

Comparative microbial strategies across the temperature–resource quadrants

To complete the temperature–resource framework, we incorporated two additional environmental quadrants informed by established microbial strategies (Fig. 7). In moderate temperature/poor resource systems such as oligotrophic oceans, persistent nitrogen scarcity drives strong ecological specialization [69, 70]. Dominant picocyanobacteria (Prochlorococcus, Synechococcus) exhibit genome streamlining, reduced regulatory complexity, and biochemical features—including lower GC content, shortened proteins, and simplified metabolic repertoires—that collectively minimize nitrogen demand [71]. High-affinity uptake systems and photoautotrophy further enable survival under chronically low nutrient availability [72].

In extreme temperature/rich resource environments exemplified by deep-sea hydrothermal vents, severe thermal stress coincides with abundant reduced compounds (H₂S, H₂, CH₄, Fe²⁺) [73]. These habitats favor thermophilic chemolithoautotrophs, particularly Campylobacteria and sulfur-oxidizing Gammaproteobacteria, which rely on sulfur oxidation and other redox-driven pathways. Vent microorganisms retain non-streamlined genomes enriched in redox and stress-response modules and employ multiple carbon fixation routes [74]. High chemical energy availability supports metabolically demanding processes and flexible nitrogen transformations, alleviating constraints imposed by extreme temperatures [75].

Viewed across all four quadrants, consistent ecological and evolutionary patterns emerge from the interaction of temperature and resource supply. Resource scarcity promotes genome reduction, functional narrowing, and high-efficiency nutrient acquisition, yielding streamlined specialists optimized for elemental economy [76, 77]. In contrast, resource richness enables the retention or expansion of metabolic pathways and enhances biogeochemical coupling. Temperature further shapes these strategies by modulating the energetic and structural costs of cellular maintenance: extreme heat selects for elevated GC content, reinforced protein-folding systems, and thermally robust energy metabolisms, whereas moderate temperatures allow greater regulatory and metabolic diversity [78, 79]. Together, this expanded framework may provide a generalizable eco-evolutionary context for interpreting how microorganisms may balance metabolic economy, environmental stress, and resource availability across diverse habitats.

Enhanced metagenomic annotation for extreme environments

Accurate taxonomic annotation is fundamental for assessing the genetic potential of microbial communities in extreme environments. However, traditional annotation tools like Kraken2 and MetaPhlAn exhibit low annotation rates in extreme environments due to limited reference databases [80, 81]. Our approach, which mapped short reads to contigs and MAGs, significantly improved annotation rates from 25.82% to 52% to 56.47%–95.58%, providing deeper insights into the community structure and functional potential of thermophilic microbes. The improved annotation framework not only enhances taxonomic and functional resolution but also facilitates the discovery of novel thermophilic traits and adaptive pathways crucial for life in extreme environments.

Study limitations

While our study provides a comprehensive view of microbial adaptation along a thermal gradient, it has certain limitations. The sampling focused on six representative hot springs in Western Sichuan, which, although spanning a broad thermal and geographic range, may not capture the full diversity of geothermal ecosystems. Functional predictions were primarily based on gene presence and inferred pathway completeness, lacking direct experimental validation through transcriptomic or proteomic analyses. Future studies incorporating multi-omics data and broader environmental parameters (e.g., mineralogy, redox potential) would help refine and validate the observed adaptive patterns.
