Effect of age on postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain after radical resection of lung cancer-specific pain in the post-anaesthesia care unit: a single-centre retrospective cohort study
Authors: Shichao Wang, Haipeng Zhu, Qinyue Yuan, Bing Li, Jiaqiang Zhang, Wei Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo explore the relationship between age and postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain after radical resection of lung cancer and the specific effect of moderate-to-severe pain in the post-anaesthesia care unit (PACU) on this relationship.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingSingle medical centre.ParticipantsPatients ≥18 years having radical resection of lung cancer between 2018 and 2020.MeasurementsPostoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain.ResultsA total of 3764 patients were included in the analysis. The incidence of postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain was 28.3%. Age had a significant effect on the prediction model of postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain. Among the whole population and those without moderate-to-severe pain in the PACU, those who were younger than 58.5 years were prone to experience moderate-to-severe pain 24 hours after surgery, and in patients with moderate-to-severe pain in the PACU, the age threshold was 62.5 years.ConclusionFor patients who underwent elective radical resection for lung cancer, age was related to postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain, and moderate-to-severe pain in the PACU had a specific effect on this relationship. Patients among the whole population and those patients without moderate-to-severe pain in the PACU were more likely to experience postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain when they were younger than 58.5 years old, and in patients with moderate-to-severe pain in the PACU, the age threshold was 62.5 years old.

Introduction

Postoperative pain is a common postoperative complication that can lead to discomfort, anxiety, increased use of analgesics, delayed ambulation, prolonged hospital stays and increased medical costs.1 The earlier postoperative pain is treated, the better postoperative rehabilitation will be achieved. Although many studies have proposed methods for managing postoperative pain,24 postoperative 24-hour pain, especially moderate and severe pain, is still an important factor affecting postoperative rehabilitation. In the overall surgical population, 30%–55% of patients experienced moderate-to-severe pain 24 hours after surgery.1 5 6 Poorly managed postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain increases the risk of chronic pain and affects rehabilitation.7 Early identification of high-risk patients who are more prone to postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain is necessary, as this helps to achieve early identification of high-risk patients and individualised pain management.

Lung cancer is a disease with high morbidity and mortality worldwide that seriously threatens human life.8 At present, surgery is usually the first choice for treating lung cancer. However, thoracic surgery is often accompanied by severe postoperative pain, which is relatively more common during all surgical procedures.9 10 Surgical trauma caused by invasive surgery both increases the incidence of pain and can also easily lead to a series of complications, which may adversely affect patient prognosis.11 Many patients with lung cancer suffer moderate-to-severe pain after surgery.12 Based on the current postoperative pain treatment plan, it is necessary to further explore the possible risk factors for postoperative pain and identify them early.

Surgical pain management must focus on individualised treatment, and age is an extremely important factor, as it is closely related to postoperative pain.13 The intensity of postoperative pain in elderly patients is lower than that in young patients.14 15 With increasing age, the tolerance of pain in elderly patients increases. In contrast to the above research, several studies have shown that there is no difference in postoperative pain according to age.16 17 There are several other studies showing that the severity of pain detected differs according to age group.18 As shown in the above studies, there is no clear conclusion on the relationship between age and postoperative pain. To better implement individualised analgesic management, it is necessary to further explore the relationship between age and postoperative pain.

Postoperative pain in the post-anaesthesia care unit (PACU) refers to pain during the period from awakening to leaving the PACU, and it is usually the first pain experience experienced by most patients after surgery. Studies have shown that patients experience a peak of postoperative pain at 0–2 hours after surgery,19 and 20.2% of patients experience moderate or severe PACU pain.20 Effective PACU pain management is essential, and unrelieved PACU pain has serious immediate and long-term consequences. Unrelieved PACU pain is associated with respiratory, renal and cardiac dysfunction or failure; immunosuppression; and delirium. In the long run, unrelieved PACU pain is associated with functional impairment and the development of postoperative chronic pain.21 Therefore, PACU pain is considered to be closely related to patient adverse outcomes, and it is very important to determine whether PACU pain is effectively controlled.

In this study, we hypothesised that age has an impact on postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain (defined as moderate-to-severe pain during the period from the time the patient left the PACU until 24 hours after surgery) and that moderate-to-severe PACU pain correlates with the above relationship. Based on the above analysis, through this retrospective cohort study, we explored the relationship between age and postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain after radical resection of lung cancer. Through further analysis, we clarified the age threshold of postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain in such populations. Finally, we explored the interaction effect of PACU pain on these relationships. The aim of our study was to provide a basis for individualised pain management after radical resection of lung cancer.

Methods

This study was approved by the ethics committee of Henan Provincial People’s Hospital (approval no. 100) and was designed as a retrospective cohort study.

Study cohort and setting

This was a retrospective study. We collected patient data from patients who underwent elective radical resection of lung cancer at Henan Provincial People’s Hospital from January 2018 to May 2020. The patients’ baseline data, preoperative laboratory examination and intraoperative data were extracted from the information centre.

We included lung cancer patients who underwent elective radical resection under general anaesthesia.

The visual analogue scale (VAS) was used to assess the degree of pain in all patients. The pain intensity was divided into painless (VAS=0), mild (VAS=1–3), moderate (VAS=4–6) and severe (VAS=7–10) pain. The VAS score indicating PACU pain was assessed by an anesthesiologist working in the PACU, and the pain score at 24 hours postsurgery was assessed by the postoperative follow-up group. In this study, a VAS score≥4 in the PACU was defined as moderate-to-severe PACU pain. A VAS score≥4 in the period from leaving the PACU to the first 24 hours after surgery was defined as postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain. The patients were divided into two groups according to whether they experienced moderate-to-severe pain 24 hours after surgery.

Variables and outcomes

The exposure indicator of interest was age, and the covariates, including demographic data, preoperative laboratory examination, anaesthesia/surgery data and PACU pain, were selected based on clinical experience and previous studies.

The primary outcome of this study was to explore the relationship between age and postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain, and the secondary outcome was to explore the age threshold at which postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain is prone to occur and the effect of moderate-to-severe PACU pain on the age threshold.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using R (V.4.2.0). Since this was a retrospective database study, the number of eligible patients was fixed, so we estimated the statistical power rather than calculated the sample size. Continuous variables were analysed by independent sample t tests (normal distribution) or Mann‒Whitney U tests (nonnormal distribution). Categorical variables were analysed by χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. P<0.05 was indicated statistical significance. We incorporated variables with significant differences in baseline data into a stepwise regression model and then established a basic model based on the significant variables (p<0.05) identified in the regression model and clinical experience. The variables included in the basic model included female, body mass index (BMI), nerve block, preoperative haemoglobin, the preoperative Albumin–Globulin ratio (A/G), the duration of surgery, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), sufentanil consumption, minimally invasive surgery, the length of stay in the PACU and moderate-to-severe PACU pain. We subsequently incorporated the age factor into the model, and the bootstrap method was used to verify the two models internally. The mean area under the curve (AUC) and 95% CI of the two models were calculated, and the significance of the difference in AUC between the two models was compared.

The linear trend of age and risk of outcome was fitted by a restricted cubic spline plot. The Youden index (Youden index=sensitivity+specificity−1) was used to calculate the age thresholds for postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain in the whole population, patients with moderate-to-severe PACU pain and patients without moderate-to-severe PACU pain. The likelihood ratio test and the modified Akaike information criterion (AICc) and ΔAICc were used to compare the basic model and the model with different age thresholds to determine the optimal fitting model. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to determine the optimal decision threshold range.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

A total of 3922 patients who underwent elective radical resection of lung cancer were screened, 3764 of whom met the inclusion criteria. 40 patients were transferred to the intensive care unit after the operation; 1 patient was less than 18 years old, 18 patients had no postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain score and 99 patients were excluded due to incomplete baseline data. A total of 3764 patients were ultimately eligible for this analysis (figure 1). There were 1065 patients (28.3%) with postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain and 2699 patients (71.7%) without this pain (online supplemental table 1).

Age can affect postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain

We designed a basic model (AUC=0.6017, 95% CI=0.5815 to 0.6219) based on stepwise regression and clinical experience, and age was subsequently incorporated into the base model to obtain the base model+age (AUC=0.6126, 95% CI=0.5926 to 0.6326) (table 1). The difference in the AUC between the two models was compared by the bootstrap method, and the results showed that the AUC of the basic model+age was greater than that of the basic model (p=0.03) (figure 2A). Therefore, this study revealed that age had a significant effect on the prediction model for postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain.

Age can affect postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain. (A) Comparison of two models. There was a significant difference between the basic model and the base model+age. (B) The linear trend of age and risk of outcome. The dose–response relationship between age and postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain in the whole population, the risk of postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain decreased first and then stabilised after reaching a certain value, reflecting a certain saturation effect.

Next, after adjustment for female, BMI, nerve block, preoperative haemoglobin, preoperative A/G, duration of surgery, NLR, PLR, sufentanil dosage, minimally invasive surgery, length of stay in the PACU, and moderate-to-severe PACU pain, it was found that with increasing age, the risk of postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain decreased first and then stabilised after reaching a certain value, reflecting a certain saturation effect; that is, there was a piecewise linear relationship between them (figure 2B).

Moderate-to-severe PACU pain is specific to the effect of age on postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain

In this study, we found that moderate-to-severe PACU pain according to the basic model (OR=1.775, 95% CI=1.262 to 2.486, p<0.001) and the basic model+age (OR=1.766, 95% CI=1.255 to 2.474, p<0.001) were risk factors for postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain (table 1); therefore, we further studied the effect of moderate-to-severe PACU pain on the specific relationship between age and postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain.

By calculating the Youden index of the whole population and patients with and without moderate-to-severe PACU pain, we determined the age threshold of postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain and then constructed a probability density function diagram (figure 3A) and a cumulative distribution function diagram (figure 3B) to explore the distribution of age in the two groups. We found that the age threshold for postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain in patients with moderate-to-severe PACU pain was significantly different from that of the whole population, indicating that moderate-to-severe PACU pain is specific for the effect of age on postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain.

In this study, patients younger than 58.5 years in the whole population were prone to experience moderate-to-severe pain 24 hours after surgery (Youden Index=0.124) (figure 3C). Among the patients without moderate-to-severe PACU pain, patients younger than 58.5 years were prone to experience postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain (Youden Index=0.124) (figure 3D), while among the patients with moderate-to-severe PACU pain, patients younger than 62.5 years were prone to experience postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain (Youden Index=0.194) (figure 3E).

Comparison of models and goodness-of-fit test

We used the likelihood ratio test to compare the three models. Compared with the basic model (AUC=0.6017, 95% CI=0.5815 to 0.6219), the basic model+age threshold of the whole population (AUC=0.6079, 95% CI=0.5879 to 0.6280) had a better fitting effect (χ2=13.7129, p<0.001). The fitting degree of the basic model+moderate-to-severe PACU pain age threshold (AUC=0.6134, 95% CI=0.5934 to 0.6333) was also better than that of the basic model (χ2=25.6998, p<0.001). The degree to which the basic model+age threshold was fit for the whole population was lower than that of the basic model+moderate-to-severe PACU pain age threshold (χ2=11.9569, p<0.001) (online supplemental table 2). By comparing the three models, we found that the model fit was the best for the basic model+moderate-to-severe PACU pain age threshold.

We compared the three models by calculating the modified Akaike’s information criterion (AICc). The AICc of the basic model was 4407.439, the AICc of the basic model+age threshold of the whole population was 4395.741 and the AICc of the basic model+moderate-to-severe PACU pain age threshold was 4383.784 (online supplemental table 2). The AICc of the basic model+moderate-to-severe PACU pain age threshold was the smallest, indicating that the model had the best fit.

The optimal threshold range of the model

We performed DCA of the three models. When the decision threshold was 20%–33%, the multivariate model containing the age threshold of moderate-to-severe PACU pain yielded the greatest net benefit. When the decision threshold is lower than 20%, the net benefit of the three multivariate models is equivalent (figure 4).

Discussion

By screening the data of patients who underwent elective radical resection of lung cancer, to the best of our knowledge, we first clarified the relationship between age and postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain. In addition, we also found that moderate-to-severe PACU pain has a specific impact on this relationship. With increasing age, the risk of postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain first decreased, then reached a certain value and stabilised, reflecting a certain saturation effect. There is a piecewise linear relationship between them. Further analysis revealed that people younger than 58.5 years were more likely to experience postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain among the whole population. Patients without moderate-to-severe PACU pain were prone to experience postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain at less than 58.5 years of age. Patients with moderate-to-severe PACU pain were prone to experience postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain at less than 62.5 years of age.

Although multimodal analgesia has been deeply rooted in people’s hearts, a recent study showed that the incidence of moderate-to-severe pain on the first day after thoracic surgery is still high,22 and the management of postoperative acute pain is still not optimal. Identifying which patients are at high risk of postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain is the key to pain management. The results of this study showed that the incidence of postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain was 28.3%. The younger the patient was, the greater the incidence of postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain. These results are consistent with the results of the present study. Their results suggested that younger age is associated with increased acute pain,2326 and the reason may be related to the greater tolerance of pain in elderly patients, the decrease in the number and activity of NK cells and the relatively mild inflammatory response. With increasing age, neurotransmitter levels, such as acetylcholine and catecholamine, in the brain tissue of patients decrease, the density of neurons decreases and conduction function decreases.27 As far as we know, there is no clear age threshold for postoperative pain in the previous literature; therefore, we performed this study and found for the first time that patients younger than 58.5 years who underwent radical resection of lung cancer were more likely to suffer postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain. Our results showed that age can affect the occurrence of postoperative moderate-to-severe pain within 24 hours. The incidence of postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain in patients of different ages is not exactly the same. Therefore, age should receive increased attention in pain management. It is necessary for anaesthesiologists to carry out individualised perioperative pain management in patients of different ages.

Moderate-to-severe PACU pain is closely related to patient adverse outcomes, and poorly controlled moderate-to-severe PACU pain seriously affects patient early recovery. If moderate-to-severe PACU pain is not fully controlled in a timely and effective manner, it may progress to chronic pain, which may also progress to neuropathic pain or mixed pain.28 Reducing PACU pain, especially moderate and severe PACU pain, is helpful for improving perioperative pain management. Our study showed that moderate-to-severe PACU pain is a risk factor for postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain. Based on the importance of moderate-to-severe PACU pain and the findings of this study, we further analysed the specific effect of moderate-to-severe PACU pain on the relationship between age and postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain. We found that patients with moderate-to-severe PACU pain who were younger than 62.5 years were more likely to experience postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain, which significantly deviated from the age threshold of 58.5 years found in the whole population model. This difference indicates that if patients aged 58.5–62.5 years suffer moderate-to-severe pain in the PACU, the probability of suffering postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain increases; that is, whether postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain occurs is affected by moderate-to-severe PACU pain. Therefore, it is necessary for anaesthesiologists to pay more attention to this group of patients aged 58.5–62.5 years and develop individualised analgesic programmes to actively prevent and avoid moderate-to-severe PACU pain. The specific effect of moderate-to-severe PAUC pain on the relationship between age and postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain may be related to peripheral sensitisation, central sensitisation, microglial activation and other mechanisms after surgery.

Referring to the methods in previous study,29 we paid more attention to whether the AUC of the basic model could be significantly improved after age was included in the basic model instead of the absolute value of the model’s AUC. After including age in the basic model, there was a significant improvement in the basic model’s AUC. By calculating the Youden index of the whole population and patients with and without moderate-to-severe PACU pain, we determined the age threshold of postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain. Based on different age thresholds, we reclassified patients and constructed two models. We compared the goodness of fit of the three models (basic model, basic model+age threshold of the whole population, basic model+moderate-to-severe PACU pain age threshold) by using the likelihood ratio test and the modified Akaike’s information criterion. The two comparison methods supported the model of the basic model+moderate-to-severe PACU pain age threshold, which had the best fitting effect. We also clarified the optimal scope of application of this model by generating decision curves. When the incidence of postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain was 20%–33%, the multivariate model containing the age threshold of moderate-to-severe PACU pain obtained the highest net benefit. When the incidence was less than 20%, there was no significant difference among the three multivariate models.

This study has several limitations. First, since it was retrospective, we were unable to obtain detailed information, such as pre-existing pain conditions and psychological status, which are also known risk factors for postoperative pain. Second, this study included only patients who underwent radical resection of lung cancer. Whether the results of the study were applicable to other types of surgery requires further study. Third, our model can obtain the greatest net benefit only when the postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain incidence is 20%–33%. However, whether this approach is suitable for patients with an incidence lower than 20% or higher than 33% remains to be further explored. A recent study showed that 26%–38% of patients suffer severe postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain.18 This range is very close to the optimal scope of our model; therefore, we believe that our model has good general applicability.

In conclusion, for patients undergoing elective radical resection for lung cancer, patient age is related to the incidence of moderate-to-severe pain 24 hours after surgery. Moderate-to-severe PACU pain has a specific effect on this relationship. Patients younger than 58.5 years old were more likely to experience postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain in the whole population and those without moderate-to-severe PACU pain, and those who had moderate-to-severe PACU pain were more likely to experience postoperative 24-hour moderate-to-severe pain when younger than 62.5 years old.
