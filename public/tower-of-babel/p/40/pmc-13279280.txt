Assessing mining-related land footprints of global steel use with a global physical input–output model
Authors: Hanspeter Wieland, Dominik Wiedenhofer, Nina Eisenmenger, Takuma Watari, Stefan Giljum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe production of iron and steel poses numerous sustainability challenges, including the often-overlooked environmental impact of iron ore mining through its land use. Although the global area affected is small, iron ore mining involves intensive land exploitation, with sites typically located far from steel users, creating a disconnect between impacts and beneficiaries. This study overcomes this disconnect by applying a consumption-based footprint perspective to quantify land-related pressures and impacts of mining, providing quantitative evidence for debates about responsibility and impact mitigation. A multi-regional physical input–output model of global iron-steel supply chains is extended with geospatial data on biomes, mining-related land use, and associated impacts (human appropriation of net primary production). The results show that the majority of mining-related land impacts are attributable to steel use in China and Europe (48% and 16%, respectively). Per-capita indicators reveal that China ranks fourth in terms of steel use, but first in embodied land impacts, which is due to its high indirect iron ore requirement per unit of steel use (3.28 t/t). If China were to match Japan's indirect iron ore requirement (1.70 t/t), its mining-land impacts could decrease by 59%. Notably, 71% of China's embodied land impacts occur domestically, offering greater control over impact mitigation than other regions. Europe, the region with the largest share of tropical biomes in its embodied iron ore imports (39.5%), bears significant responsibility for mining-related impacts in these ecologically vulnerable regions. We highlight policy implications, especially for China and Europe, and conclude with a discussion of the modeling approach. INTRODUCTION

Iron and steel are crucial for any nation's economic development and industrialization in emerging economies, as well as efforts towards decarbonizing the economy (Creutzig et al., 2024; Deetman et al., 2021; Kalt et al., 2021; Müller et al., 2006; Pauliuk et al., 2013). Global extraction of crude metallic ores in 2022 amounted to approximately 10 Gt/year, of which iron ore (3.26 Gt) was the most mined metal (UNEP IRP, 2024). The processing of iron ore into 1.88 Gt/year of steel causes around 9% of global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions (Hertwich, 2021), and this impact is set to rise, as global steel demand is expected to increase by a factor of 2–2.6 till 2050 (Creutzig et al., 2024; Watari et al., 2020). A growing body of work focuses on steel cycles and material stocks (Nechifor et al., 2020; Pauliuk et al., 2013; Song et al., 2020) and how to mitigate their emissions (Watari et al., 2021). However, pressures and impacts on ecosystems due to iron ore mining have not been systematically and globally assessed so far, and it is unclear how these land-related pressures and impacts are “embodied” in internationally traded products and final demand around the world.

The existing case study literature shows that socio-ecological problems linked to iron ore mining are often localized in regions distant from the users of steel products (Kolie et al., 2024; Muhirwa et al., 2023). For example, Sonter et al. (2014) demonstrate that global steel demand drives significant land-use change in Brazil's “Iron Quadrangle.”1 Using remote sensing data on mining land use (Maus et al., 2020), Giljum et al. (2022) reveal that mining is a major global driver of deforestation in tropical/subtropical regions, which are vital for the biogenic carbon cycle and biodiversity. Although mining-related land use with around 100,000 km2 is relatively small compared to agriculture and forestry on a global scale, it can have devastating and long-lasting effects on local ecosystems and communities, which are difficult to quantify (Giljum et al., 2022; Maus & Werner, 2024). Mining activities involve intensive land exploitation where approximately 70% of global extraction is embedded within international supply chains (Giljum et al., 2025). This interconnectedness complicates the attribution of environmental impacts to socioeconomic drivers.

With steel demand projected to rise, it is concerning that about 50% of global iron ore extraction occurs within 15 km of protected areas (Luckeneder et al., 2021), and approximately 47% of global iron ore reserves are in areas with medium-to-high environmental and social risks (Lèbre et al., 2019). Producers and consumers of iron and steel must recognize their responsibility for the environmental impacts associated with mining activities.

Analyzing global supply chains and how resource extraction and associated environmental pressures and impacts are interlinked with final demand is increasingly done using global multi-regional monetary input–output tables extended with environmental data (GMIOT; Wiedmann & Lenzen, 2018). GMIOTs are integrated monetary accounts of production, trade, and consumption, which use monetary allocations2 to link production and consumption activities of numerous sectors and regions. They are widely used to calculate consumption-based “footprints” of various environmental pressures such as material extraction (Lenzen et al., 2021; Wiedmann et al., 2015) or GHG emissions (Kanemoto et al., 2016).

Only recently, scholars began to use GMIOTs to investigate supply chains and environmental issues related to mining. For example, Cabernard and Pfister (2022) use a GMIOT to show that electricity generation drives approximately 10% of global mining-related biodiversity loss in 2014, and that the impact of coal-fired electricity was 10 times higher than that of renewables per unit of electricity generated. A study by Nungesser and Pauliuk (2022) used a GMIOT to investigate the material footprint for copper ores and natural hazards regarding tailings dams. They find that Poland constitutes the hazard hotspot for Europe's copper supply. Nakajima et al. (2017) investigated how land use change associated with nickel mining is linked to international trade flows. Their analysis revealed that Japanese final demand accounted for 19.5% of nickel mining–related global land use change in 2005. While GMIOT assessments provide valuable insights into the environmental pressures linked to final demand, they have significant limitations when evaluating highly resource-intensive economic activities.

Due to the monetary allocation, GMIOT footprint indicators are often criticized for being biased toward economic activities with significant monetary value, thereby underestimating the role of activities with large material throughput of comparatively small value, such as extraction and processing of bulk materials (Liang & Zhang, 2013; Merciai & Heijungs, 2014; Schaffartzik et al., 2015; Weisz & Duchin, 2006). In certain cases, it has even been found that GMIOTs contradict the results of purely physical models (Hubacek & Feng, 2016; Kastner et al., 2014). To enable footprint assessments with mass-based instead of monetary allocation,3 scholars are developing global physical input–output tables (GPIOTs) (Bruckner et al., 2019; Towa et al., 2022; Wieland et al., 2022). GPIOTs depict economic activities in physical units (e.g., metric tons) to model material cycles and the actual material flows between extraction, processing, manufacturing processes, and final end-uses such as buildings, infrastructure, or machinery.

The biophysical description of economic activities in GPIOTs shifts the analytical focus from monetary flows to a mass balanced systems perspective, which depicts actual material flows between processes as well as the inputs from and outputs to the natural environment. The first ever GPIOT for iron and steel was constructed by Wieland et al. (2022), and it differentiates 32 regions, 39 products, and 30 processing steps from crude ore extraction to final end-uses. This iron and steel GPIOT captures the complete iron and steel cycle linked to global steel demand.

A significant advantage of this GPIOT over GMIOTs for iron ore material footprint (IO-MF) assessments is its higher sector detail and the explicit representation of steel scrap supply and utilization, which is often downcycled into construction activities (Wieland et al., 2022). The explicit modeling of scrap flows, which accounts for approximately 30% of the feedstock of global steelmaking, enables a more nuanced depiction of how different supply chains and end-uses vary in their reliance on iron ore mining (Watari et al., 2025), thereby providing greater granularity in tracking extracted raw materials and steel flows in a mass-balanced manner all the way up to the end-uses.

The methodological benefits offered by GPIOTs for assessing the environmental impacts of mining have not yet been fully exploited, representing a novel but challenging research frontier for the development of innovative footprint indicators that allow for a better assessment of environmental impacts and associated pressures.

The present work addresses this research gap by introducing the first GPIOT-based footprint assessment for pressures and impacts of global iron ore mining, specifically related to land use and ecosystems, thereby testing their feasibility. These indicators are derived from integrating the GPIOT with spatially-explicit datasets, allowing us to address three overarching research issues.

First, to investigate how a country's steel use relates to mining activities in different ecosystems, we present a new IO-MF that is disaggregated into the biomes where mining takes place (IO-MF-biome). In this context, we pay special attention to international trade relations to identify the most important importers of iron ore from key biomes, such as tropical/subtropical regions, which play a crucial role in the global biogenic carbon cycle and for biodiversity (Friedlingstein et al., 2022; Malhi, 2012; Pan et al., 2011). The IO-MF-biome indicator allows us to systematically assess how complex international trade relations are intertwined with global mining activities in tropical/subtropical regions.

Second, to assess the extent to which a country's steel use is driving global mining-related land pressures and impacts, we are modeling embodied land footprints (eLand-steel) and the embodied “human appropriation of net primary production” (eHANPP-steel) indicator, respectively. These indicators build on geospatial data on mining-related land use (Maus et al., 2020) and a global raster map on the net primary production (NPP) of the potential vegetation (NPP0; Haberl et al., 2007). HANPP (human appropriation of net primary production), which is part of the planetary boundary framework (Richardson et al., 2023; Running, 2012), is an indicator for land use intensity, which measures human impacts on the biosphere caused by removing trophic energy that is otherwise available to ecosystem processes. The NPP of the vegetation is deeply intertwined with various ecosystem functions, such as species richness (Semenchuk et al., 2022) and ecosystem stability (Canelas & Pereira, 2022). In the present context, we are particularly interested to what extent pressure (eLand-steel) and impact (eHANPP-steel) footprint indicators diverge. This would facilitate the identification of regional steel supply chains that exert similar land pressures but with diverging impacts. From a methodological standpoint, the key question is whether the relatively simple land pressure footprints can serve as a suitable proxy for more complex land impact footprints.

Third, we apply index decomposition analysis (IDA) to investigate the most important supply chain factors that explain the cross-regional differences observed in the embodied pressure (eLand-steel) and impact (eHANPP-steel) footprints. In this context, we are specifically interested in how regional differences in consumption levels (metric tons of steel use per capita), production structure (iron ore per metric ton of steel), or the land requirements (land area per metric ton of iron ore) affect the outcome in the overall footprint indicators. Based on these insights, we discuss intervention points for mitigating mining-related land impacts in the bva iron and steel supply chains of China, specifically, the largest steel producer in the world, which is facing deep structural changes in its steel sector (Song et al., 2020).

The analysis is performed for the year 2014, the most recent year for which all necessary data are available.

METHODS AND DATA

We combine several socio-ecological methods available to investigate society-nature interactions across different spatiotemporal scales (Haberl et al., 2019). First, we draw on economy-wide material flow accounting (ew-MFA) for the general system definition (Krausmann et al., 2017; Wiedenhofer et al., 2019). Second, we use input–output analysis and an iron-steel GPIOT to quantify footprint indicators (Weisz & Duchin, 2006; Wieland et al., 2022). Third, we apply methods from land use sciences to quantify land use and HANPP of iron ore mining and extraction in different biomes (Erb et al., 2016; Haberl et al., 2014). Finally, we apply IDA (Ang & Wang, 2015) to assess the factors that explain the cross-regional differences in footprint indicators. Table 1 provides an overview of the datasets used in this study.

ew-MFA indicators

Our analysis is grounded in the concept of socioeconomic metabolism (Haberl et al., 2019) and uses established system boundaries and indicators from ew-MFA, which systematically quantifies raw material extraction and trade around the world (Krausmann et al., 2017; United Nations Environment Programme, 2023).

Domestic extraction (DE) quantifies the total raw materials extracted from nature, which, in the case of metals, complies with the “run of mine” concept (United Nations Environment Programme, 2023). Gross additions to stocks (GAS) stand for the inflow of new materials into the use phase, where they accumulate as material stocks (MS) in the form of buildings, infrastructure, machinery, and other long-lived products, thereby mainly reflecting the physical dimension of manufactured capital (Wiedenhofer et al., 2019, 2024). Steel-GAS in the iron-steel GPIOT also encompasses short-lived products (e.g., packaging materials and disposable containers), which are assumed to have an average in-use lifetime of less than 1 year. These short-lived products differ from the concept of gross fixed capital formation (GFCF) as defined in the System of National Accounts (SNA). Steel-GAS is therefore more similar to SNA's final demand than to GFCF (Wiedenhofer et al., 2019). The IO-MF quantifies the amount of iron ore associated with the steel-GAS of a region, wherever in the world extraction takes place (Wiedmann & Lenzen, 2018; Wiedmann et al., 2015). IO-MF therefore measures the extraction embedded in international trade allocated to regions where steel is used (steel-GAS). Global IO-MF equals global DE.

Data on MS was sourced from the MISO2 database, a newly released dynamic ew-MFA model, which provides steel stock data for 177 countries (Wiedenhofer et al., 2024). Because the GPIOT was also used to inform steel flows in MISO2, both databases yield a highly consistent representation of regional steel cycles.4

DE, steel-GAS, and IO-MF can be calculated from the iron-steel GPIOT, because this GPIOT was constructed following the system boundaries of the ew-MFA framework. A summary of the footprint calculus, including how to compile the total requirement matrix with the GPIOTs, is contained in Section 1 of Supporting Information S2 and can also be found in the associated method paper (Wieland et al., 2022).

The GPIOT differentiates 32 regions, 30 processes, and 39 types of iron-containing products. The footprint calculations are always carried out using the full detail of regions, products, and processes to minimize the aggregation bias (Lenzen, 2011) and are only aggregated to 13 regions for analysis and discussion. Results in the 32-region classification can be found in Section 3 of Supporting Information S2. All codes of the present work, including the GPIOT itself and footprint calculus, are accessible through a GitHub repository (see Data Availability Statement).

The GPIOT extensions for the IO-MF-biome, eLand-steel, and eHANPP-steel

The biomes extension for IO-MF-biome depicts regional DE disaggregated into terrestrial biomes and is constructed in three steps following the approach of Luckeneder et al. (2021). First, we intersect the geo-referenced mine-level database (SNL, 2020) with maps on the terrestrial biomes from the ecoregion dataset (Resolve, 2017). Second, we filter for iron ore mines in the mine-level data, now disaggregated into biomes, and aggregate to the 32-region classification of the GPIOT to calculate region-wise allocation keys, that is, shares distributing extraction to 12 sub-biomes. Third, we multiply the region-wise allocation shares by DE from the GPIOT. This approach maintains full consistency with the DE of the GPIOT. For ease of interpretation, IO-MF-biome is in the following are aggregated into six biome groups.5 Full details of IO-MF-biome can be found in Supporting Information S3 and a biome concordance in Section 2 of Supporting Information S2.

The land extension of the GPIOT builds on a global geo-spatial dataset derived from satellite images (Maus et al., 2020). Because these data do not inform about the type of metal that is extracted, land use for iron ore mining is approximated through a geo-referenced mine-level database (SNL, 2020) in conjunction with a global-to-subnational geo-referenced region classification of 51,275 regions. Further information on the global-to-subnational region classification, which is based on global maps of administrative regions (GADM, 2022), is contained in Section 9 of Supporting Information S2. When merging mining-related land use with iron ore extraction data, it is assumed that mining land that falls within a subnational region where mine-level data reports extraction is associated with iron ore mining. Possible by-products of iron ore mining that may exist are not taken into account. Aggregating the thereby estimated land use of iron ore to the 32-region classification of the GPIOT yields the land extension of the GPIOT for the calculation of the eLand-steel indicator.

The HANPP extension quantifies the extent to which land use for mining affects flows of trophic energy in local ecosystems, namely NPP measured in grams of carbon per year (gC/year). The design of the HANPP extension is based on the assumption that mining areas have been completely cleared and that no carbon fixation is taking place and no biomass is growing or harvested on that land, that is, a situation equal or comparable to complete soil sealing. In this case, by definition, HANPP equals the NPP of the potential vegetation, that is, NPP0. The HANPP extension is constructed as follows. First, we intersect a global raster map of NPP0 intensities (gC/m2) taken from Haberl et al. (2007)6 with the mining land data from Maus et al. (2020). Subsequently, multiplying the NPP0 intensities by the mining land area associated with iron ore mining (recall the construction of the land extension above) yields the HANPP of iron ore mining and aggregation to the 32-region classification of the GPIOT gives the HANPP extension, which forms the input to the calculation of the eHANPP-steel indicator.

HANPP is quantified in units of grams of carbon per year and should not be confused with product carbon footprints, which measure life cycle-wide GHG emissions. The NPP of carbon measures the available energy in ecosystems, which can be interpreted as a proxy for the impacts on such ecosystem functions as species richness (Semenchuk et al., 2022) or ecosystem stability (Canelas & Pereira, 2022). No single indicator can fully represent the environmental impact of human activities on the natural environment, particularly in the long term. However, HANPP provides a practical indicator framework for approximating human influence on trophic energy flows, which is crucial for the biosphere (Richardson et al., 2023; Running, 2012).

RESULTS

Iron ore extraction (DE) by biome-group, mining-related land use, and HANPP

Global DE and associated pressures and impacts are concentrated in three countries (see Figure 1a). In 2014, China (50% of global DE), Australia (23%), and Brazil (11%) produced over 2578 Mt/year, accounting for approximately 80% of the global DE. Iron ore mining occupies about 5594 km2, with China (41%), Australia (18.5%), and Brazil (10%) being responsible for 70% of global mining-related land use. China also leads in global HANPP of iron ore mining (1,300 ktC/year, 43.5%), followed by Brazil (18.5%) and Australia (10.5%).

Iron ore mining is concentrated in the temperate biome-group (42%), deserts and xeric shrublands (27%), and tropical/subtropical biomes (24%), totaling 93% of global DE. This distribution for iron ore mining differs from that of the global land area,7 where the tropical biomes make up the largest share with approximately 35% (Dinerstein et al., 2017). Most of the DE in temperate biomes is situated in China (81%), while Australia leads in mining in deserts and xeric shrublands (81%). 44% of DE in tropical/subtropical biomes takes place in Brazil (see Figure 1b).

Brazil, with 100% of its DE in the tropical/subtropical biome-group, which is the biome-group with the highest average NPP intensity, has the highest HANPP/Land-intensity (970 gC/m2/year). With 158 gC/m2/year, we find a very small HANPP/land-intensities for Latin America (nec), which is only a fraction of Brazil, which can be explained by its biome composition. Two-thirds of DE in Latin America (nec) takes place in the tropical/subtropical biome-group, where the majority is in the sub-biome dry broadleaf forests, which have a lower overall NPP intensity with potentially much higher variability (Malhi et al., 2011) compared to the tropical moist forests sub-biome of Brazil. In general, regional HANPP/land-intensities vary by a factor of 10 (see Figure 1c).8

The comparison of land intensities of extraction shows significant regional differences, from 8 m2/t/year in Asia and Pacific (nec) to 1.2 m2/t/year in India (see Figure 1c). For Asia and Pacific (nec), we we find a relatively large difference of 7% when comparing its global shares of DE and mining land use (Figure 1a). Looking into the GPIOT and the underlying raw data, that is, IRP's extraction account (Schandl et al., 2018, 2024), we find that Kazakhstan is the predominant supplier in Asia and Pacific (nec), accounting for approximately 51 Mt of the total 62 Mt. We identified several reasons for the high land intensity in Kazakhstan. Kazakhstan has a mean iron ore grade of 39%, which is considerably lower than those reported for other prominent mining nations, namely Brazil (61.5%) and Australia (60%), thereby further increasing the area required for waste rock disposal. Moreover, mining and processing technologies utilized in Kazakhstan tend to be less efficient than those employed in other major iron ore-producing countries, potentially resulting in increased waste generation and land requirements for tailings (compare with ITA, 2022).9

China's IO-MF with 1748 Mt/year accounts for 54.5% of global IO-MF, followed by Europe with 403 Mt/year (12.5%) and the United States with 219.5 Mt/year (approx. 7%). Final demand in these three regions, which stand for 31.4% of the world population, is responsible for approximately 73.8% of global DE.

We also analyzed IO-MFs end-uses, which are discussed briefly in Section 6 of Supporting Information S2. The construction sector holds the highest share in total IO-MFs across regions. On the global level, steel use in construction accounts for 60.5% (1,947 Mt/year) of global IO-MF, followed by transport machinery (16.8% or 541 Mt/year), stationary machinery (16.7% or 535 Mt/year), and all other products (6% or 191.5 Mt/year).

The disaggregation of the IO-MF indicator into source regions (Figure 2b) reveals origin and associated trade patterns of embodied iron ores. We observe high import-dependencies for 10 of the 13 regions, that is, shares of foreign regions in the IO-MF larger than 50%. Only China (33%), India (34%), and Brazil (38.5%) have import shares smaller than 50%, which are major iron ore mining countries providing large volumes to global markets. Japan has the highest import share with 100%. Interestingly, for Australia, which accounts for 23% of global DE, we find a quite large import share of 66%, which is due to its heavy reliance on imported steel products.

1780 Mt/year or 55.5% of the global IO-MF is distributed through international trade. Distinct regional trade relations can be identified when we group the consuming regions according to their most important foreign source region. Australia is the number one foreign source region for China (approx. 23.5% of the IO-MF), Japan (65%), and Asia and Pacific (nec) (37.5%). Brazil represents the largest foreign source only for Europe (approx. 24%) and China for all other regions, that is, United States (30%), Canada (24.5%), India (12%), Africa (24.5%), Australia (31.5%), Russia (18%), Middle East (21.5%), Brazil (19%), and South America (nec) (24%).

eLand-steel and eHANPP-steel of steel use

The regional distribution of global eLand-steel (5593 km2) and eHANPP-steel (2979 MtC/year) strongly resembles IO-MF patterns. We find the same four regions to have the largest footprints, that is, China, Europe, the United States, and Asia and Pacific (nec). Comparing regional shares across the different pressure/impact indicators, we see only small differences (see Figure 3c). The top four regions combined are responsible for 78.6% of global eLand-steel and 78.5% of eHANPP-steel, which is slightly less than the 80.5% of global IO-MF. In the regional ranking for eLand-steel and eHANPP-steel, the same regions are to be found in the same order up to the 10th place. Only in the case of Brazil and Canada (ranking 11th and 12th in eLand-steel) is the order of regions reversed from eLand-steel to eHANPP-steel. Reasons for the higher eHANPP-steel of Brazil are the low import share in the IO-MF (38.4%) and its high territorial HANPP-intensity (961.2 gC/m2/year).

We also performed a sensitivity analysis using Monte Carlo simulations (MCS; Moran & Wood, 2014; Rodrigues et al., 2018) to assess the robustness of the indicators with regard to uncertainties stemming from the estimation of mining land associated with iron ores (see Section 2.2 and Section 4 of Supporting Information S2). In the absence of information on standard errors, we assumed a normal distribution for land intensities of extraction (m2/t/year), with a standard deviation of 20% and applying 100,000 iterations. The MCS shows that the interquartile range (IQR; ± 25% of all iterations) of eLand-steel and eHANPP-steel revolve around ± 5% to ± 10% of the regional mean value. We find that even when considering the entire maximum and minimum range, China consistently ranks first, followed by Europe. For other regions with more similar eLand-steel and eHANPP-steel, we sometimes find significant overlaps in the total range (e.g., the United States and Asia and Pacific (nec)), but for the IQR, mostly not. Further details and a discussion of the MCS results are provided in Section 4 of Supporting Information S2).

Per-capita consumption–based indicators

So far, we have analyzed results at scale, that is, for total flows on the regional level. This perspective on resource use ignores the fact that we are comparing regions with very different populations. In the following, we analyze steel-GAS, IO-MF, eLand-steel, and eHANPP-steel from a per-capita perspective.

Across all per-capita indicators, the same regions can be found among the top eight consumers (see Figure 4). China has the highest rate for eLand-steel (2.01 m2/cap), eHANPP-steel (1.05 kgC/cap/year), and IO-MF (1.28 t/cap/year). Per-capita steel-GAS is 0.69 t/cap/year highest in Japan, followed by Australia (0.47 t/cap/year), Canada (0.45 t/cap/year), and with 0.39 t/cap/year at fourth position China. With the exception of Russia's steel-GAS, all per-capita indicators of the top eight regions are larger than the respective global averages, while the other five regions are always below.

We find huge inequalities across regions when comparing the highest and the lowest per-capita figures. For steel-GAS, the highest per-capita value (Japan with 0.69 t/cap/year) is about 25.7 times larger than the lowest value (approx. 0.03 t/cap/year for Africa). For per-capita IO-MF and eLand-steel, disparities are similarly large, with factors of 24.1 (China vs. Africa) and 24.7 (China vs. India), respectively. For per-capita eHANPP-steel, the differences are with a factor 20.5 still large (China vs. India) but smaller when compared to steel-GAS, IO-MF, and eLand-steel (approx. 15%–20%).

Cross-regional IDA of per-capita eHANPP-steel of China

We use IDA to investigate the most important supply chain factors that explain the cross-regional differences observed in eHANPP-steel, focusing on China, which is the largest steel producer in the world. When comparing per-capita eHANPP-steel of China with regions that have a per-capita steel-GAS that is either higher or lower than the global average, different factors stand out. For regions with per-capita steel-GAS above the global average (see Figure 5), the IO-MF/steel-GAS intensity always had the largest effect on per-capita eHANPP-steel and was strongest for Japan. If China had the same IO-MF/steel-GAS intensity as Japan (1.7 t/t), China's eHANPP-steel would decrease by 59.5%. Because China has the highest IO-MF/steel-GAS intensity of all regions (3.28 t/t), the effect of this factor is always negative. A detailed discussion of the regional differences in IO-MF per steel-GAS, as well as eHANPP-steel per eLand-steel and eLand-steel per IO-MF intensities, is provided in Section 8 of Supporting Information S2.

For regions with per-capita steel-GAS below the global average, differences in per-capita steel-GAS had the largest effect on per-capita eHANPP-steel, which is strongest with regard to Africa. If China had the same per-capita steel-GAS as Africa (27 instead of 391 kg/cap/year), China's per-capita eHANPP-steel would decrease by 84%. The effect of the eLand-steel/IO-MF intensity plays a minor role but generally tends to increase China's eHANPP-steel, most pronounced for Russia, which would increase China's eHANPP-steel by approx. 47%. For both Europe's and the global average eHANPP results, we find that for 11 of the 13 decompositions, the effect of per-capita steel-GAS was most influential.

DISCUSSION

Europe's IO-MF-biome in tropical regions

The present study introduced the IO-MF-biome indicator, providing new insights into the relationship between steel-GAS and upstream iron ore mining activities across ecosystems. 24% of global DE occurs in environmentally vulnerable tropical/subtropical biomes (769 Mt/year). Given the importance of these regions to the global biogenic carbon cycle and biodiversity, supply chains originating in tropical regions are of particular interest.

The IO-MF-biome assessment shows that Europe has a significant responsibility for mining in foreign tropical regions, especially in Brazil. This is also reflected by Europe's high eHANPP-eLand intensity of 572 gC/m2/year (see Section 8 of Supporting Information S2), ranking just below Brazil and India. The negative environmental and social impacts of mining in Brazil, which have been documented extensively (Giljum et al., 2022; Siqueira-Gay et al., 2022; Sonter et al., 2017), should be further investigated through the lens of consumer responsibility, with a particular focus on international trade and the supply chains serving Europe's steel demand.

Europe's IO-MF-biome results are of relevance in the context of implementing new supply chain regulations, such as the “Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive” (CSDDD; European Parliament, 2024) and the “Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive” (CSRD; Hummel & Jobst, 2024). These directives aim to force European companies to assess environmental and social risks related to upstream production activities in the supplier countries, such as mining. European iron and steel trade patterns are likely to change dramatically in the near future. As the EU states in its “European Steel and Metals Action Plan” (European Parliament, 2025), Europe has become the global receiving market for steel imports, as a growing number of countries adopted measures to restrict steel imports. Vice versa, the EU is elaborating measures to protect its steel sector through trade restrictions. This trend will be accelerated by the EU's targets to increase the share of steel scrap in steelmaking as part of its efforts to move toward a circular economy (European Parliament, 2020). The modeling framework presented in this study can provide crucial information on the global environmental repercussions of European policy measures that are related to the iron and steel industry.

Methodological reflections on eLand-steel and eHANPP-steel

The comparison of eHANPP-steel and eLand-steel revealed that the same four regions have the largest footprints. The regional distribution of eHANPP-steel strongly resembles eLand-steel patterns. Only in the case of Brazil and Canada, the order of regions is reversed from eLand-steel to eHANPP-steel.

The overall similarity in the regional rankings of total eLand-steel and eHANPP-steel can be explained by two main factors. First, despite significant differences in production-based mining-land characteristics (HANPP/Land-intensities diverging by a factor of 6.1), such disparities are small compared to differences in population sizes (factor 40) or per-capita steel-GAS (factor 23). Second, because iron steel supply chains are relatively diverse in terms of source regions (only 3/13 regions have import shares below 50%), eHANPP-steel and eLand-steel tend to converge to the global average eHANPP-steel/eLand-steel intensity (533 gC/m2/year), resulting in similar regional rankings. Consumption-based eHANPP-steel/eLand-steel intensities (see Section 8 of Supporting Information S2) differ by a factor of 1.7, while production-based intensities (Figure 1c) differ by a factor of 6.1. A very similar pattern is observed for eLand/IO-MF-intensities. Impact footprints comprise flows from regions with varying HANPP per metric ton, which results in more similar region indicators compared to the production-based accounts. We therefore propose that for such a granular assessment at the national level, embodied land pressure indicators can be a reasonably good proxy for land impacts.

Regarding HANPP, it should be noted that such indicators are mainly used to analyze land use effects of biomass extraction (Haberl et al., 2014). To the best of our knowledge, the estimation of HANPP in the context of mining and steel supply chains represents a novelty. We are aware of the limitations of this indicator when it comes to the longer-term perspective. Mining sites will continue to impact the biosphere for many years after operations have ceased, which makes it difficult to link consumption with upstream production impacts. LCA impact indicators on resource depletion and scarcity, for example, address this challenge by linking resource extraction to the total availability of that resource (Klinglmair et al., 2014).11 The present study was the first to test the feasibility of using the HANPP indicator for metals and mining. It is evident that further development is necessary.

Options for mining impact mitigation for China

China's steel use is responsible for the largest shares in the global eLand-steel (49%) and eHANPP-steel (47%). At the same time, China is facing deep structural changes in its iron-steel supply chains (Song et al., 2020). We applied IDA to investigate the most important supply chain factors that explain the cross-regional differences observed for eHANPP-steel of China.

China's steel sector is transitioning from ore-based to scrap-based (electric arc furnace, EAF) steelmaking, driven by increasing availability of end-of-life steel scrap from its expanding infrastructure (Nechifor et al., 2020; Pauliuk et al., 2012). Per-capita steel stocks in China, currently about 4.5 t/cap (compare with Figure 2a), are expected to reach saturation (8–12 t/cap) after 2040 (Song et al., 2020). Our per-capita assessment revealed that, in 2014, China ranked fourth in steel-GAS but first in eLand-steel and eHANPP-steel, due to its high IO-MF per steel-GAS intensity (3.28 t/t).

The IDA results suggest, if China had the same IO-MF per steel-GAS intensity as Japan (1.7 t/t), its land impacts could drop by over 59%, which would require increased scrap usage through EAF. The Chinese government aims to increase EAF's share in steelmaking from 15% to at least 20% by 2030 (Global Energy Monitor, 2024; Steel Times International, 2024), and its steel scrap use to 320 Mt/year by 2025 (OECD, 2024). Wang et al. (2014) find untapped potentials for increasing scrap use in China and that the secondary production share is expected to grow sharply in the coming decades. China is indeed moving into the envisioned direction, and it seems likely that China's IO-MF per steel-GAS intensity will decrease strongly over time. Reduced embodied land impacts can be seen as a co-benefit of circular economy measures (e.g., prolonged product lifetimes and improved recycling). However, further analysis with more recent data is needed to explore how changes in China's iron-steel supply chains and stock dynamics may mitigate impacts from mining.

The decomposition of China's eHANPP-steel into source regions revealed that 71% of China's embodied land impacts occur domestically, which is in stark contrast to other regions like Europe (19%) or the United States (13%). Although China has the highest eHANPP-steel in absolute and per-capita terms, the land impacts are largely tied to its domestic mining sector, allowing China greater control over its iron-steel supply chain governance and responsibilities for mitigating mining-related impacts.

Mining impact mitigation in other regions

Region-specific strategies combined with global efforts to transition to scrap-based steel-making offer pathways to mitigate land-related mining impacts. For Brazil and other tropical countries, focusing on enforcing environmental regulations for the mining sector seems critical. For regions with high per-capita steel use (e.g., Japan, Canada), improving material efficiency could be an important lever to reduce mining-land impacts. In Asia and Pacific (nec), we detected high land-use intensities, which could be a result of processing inefficiencies. Upgrading technologies could therefore help to reduce land impacts. For the United States, which relies heavily on imported iron ore, enforcing responsible sourcing through better supply chain governance could help to address upstream mining impacts.

In general, we see many ambitious policies being implemented (such as the Paris agreement or the Sustainable Development Goals), which call for innovative institutional frameworks to enable tracking the responsible sourcing of minerals (Ali et al., 2017). The development of enhanced monitoring and indicator frameworks, such as that presented in this study, has the potential to facilitate the provision of information to stakeholders regarding the global environmental impacts associated with the activities of national and regional economies.

Validation, further methodological limitations, and next steps

Our estimation approach for the global land use of iron ore mining yields a global area of approximately 5593 km2, which is remarkably similar to what Cabernard and Pfister (2022) have estimated (5496 km2). Iron ore mining land use is rather small when viewed on a global scale. However, mining land is subjected to intensive exploitation (Giljum et al., 2022; Maus & Werner, 2024), and the industry is one of the fastest-growing sectors, where environmental impacts will significantly increase in the future (Creutzig et al., 2024; Watari et al., 2021).

Moreover, the indirect land use from roads and infrastructure that is necessary to operate iron ore mines, which are sometimes located in remote areas, is not accounted for here and might add a substantial amount of land. Estimating this additional/indirect land use of mining raises questions about meaningful system boundaries in land accounting for mining.

The comparison of land intensities of extraction shows significant regional differences (1.2–8 m2/t/year), which may stem from different mining technologies as pointed out for Kazakhstan. Moreover, the remote sensing approach by Maus et al. (2020) could overestimate the land requirements in certain regions, due to the difficulty in distinguishing abandoned sites from mines in operation. Our footprints are also subject to uncertainties due to the format of the land use data, which does not distinguish between types of metals. In our approximation for iron ore mining, we assume areas that fall within a certain subnational region where mine-level data report extraction, to correspond to iron ore, thereby neglecting other extractions within that region and possible co-production of companion metals. Additionally, geo-referenced mine-level data can sometimes inaccurately report locations, creating further uncertainties.

Our MCS suggests that the potential variability in land intensities of extraction (assumed standard deviation of 20%) has a moderate but consistent impact on footprint indicators (5%–10%), which could influence the overall ranking of regions that have similar footprints, but not for China and Europe. Please note the detailed discussion of the MCS results provided in Section 4 of Supporting Information S2.

A limitation of this study is that the footprints pertain to the year 2014. The GPIOT assessment represents a methodological innovation, and the development of such models is a time-intensive process, where updates are implemented only with significant time delays. The most recent global ew-MFA indicators from Schandl et al. (2024) show that from 2014 to 2024, global iron ore extraction has increased by approximately 0.5%, and international trade of ferrous materials has grown by 18%. From this, we can infer that pressures/impacts of mining have likely not decreased between 2014 and 2024, and that burden shifting between countries through global trade has further intensified.

A major advantage of the GPIOT in assessing mining-related impact footprints is its mass-balanced representation of processes and the explicit modeling of primary (mining) and secondary (recycling) materials. This enables a more accurate depiction of how regional supply chains differ in their reliance on mining. The influence of this feature is reflected in the IDA results, where the IO-MF-per-steel-GAS-intensity emerged as a major factor explaining cross-regional variations in footprints. However, a limitation of the GPIOT is its exclusion of service sectors, which are difficult to model in physical units.12 Addressing these inter-sectoral relations would be more suitable in a hybrid IO framework using mixed units.

Another methodological consideration for future enhancements to improve robustness of the indicators involves the fact that the GPIOT does not account for subnational trade patterns. The GPIOT allocates mining impacts uniformly, assuming identical trade shares for all mines in a country, which is particularly problematic for larger heterogeneous countries where mining is situated in very different ecosystems.13 While subnational trade data are available from shipping data sets like Panjiva or the Global Trade Atlas (REF; S&P Global Market Intelligence, 2024, 2025), its integration is challenging due to misaligned classifications and incomplete information.

Regarding the policy relevance of the present work, it is important to emphasize that information about the company-level supply chain-wide impacts all the way back to the mining sector is currently almost completely missing. Stakeholders are struggling to obtain such information. GPIOTs that integrate subnational information on local impacts should be further developed as a key source of such information for various stakeholders in the future.
