The OsCPK4–OsCNGC7 phosphorylation-centered feedback loop promotes Ca2+ influx and confers salt tolerance in rice
Authors: Chenghang Zhan, Hao Chen, Nian Liu, Jiamei Sun, Chengfeng Qu, Zizhao Xie, Ning Jin, Xinyue Gu, Tian Qin, Junli Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soil salinity severely threatens plant growth and crop production. Although elevated cytosolic Ca2+ levels in response to osmotic stress are essential for plant responses to salt stress, the mechanisms underlying the generation and regulation of these Ca2+ signals remain largely unknown. Here, we report that rice cyclic nucleotide-gated channel 7 (OsCNGC7) forms a regulatory module with Ca2+-dependent protein kinase 4 (OsCPK4) to fine-tune salt-stress responses. OsCNGC7 promotes salt tolerance by mediating Ca²⁺ influx, whereas loss of OsCNGC7function increases salt sensitivity and impairs salt-induced Ca2+ influx. Mechanistic analyses revealed that, during the early phase of salt stress, enhanced OsCPK4 kinase activity promotes the phosphorylation of OsCNGC7, thereby increasing its channel activity and protein stability. The resulting Ca2+ influx rapidly activates downstream salt-stress responses. During prolonged salt stress, OsCPK4 kinase activity is suppressed, reducing the phosphorylation and protein abundance of OsCNGC7 and thereby inhibiting Ca2+-mediated salt-stress responses. OsCPK4-mediated phosphorylation thus fine-tunes OsCNGC7 channel activity and stability, enabling the rapid activation of salt-stress responses followed by their timely attenuation to facilitate growth recovery. Our findings reveal that the OsCPK4–OsCNGC7 module provides an auto-regulatory mechanism for salt-stress adaptation in rice that dynamically modulates Ca2+ influx to balance stress tolerance with growth.

Introduction

Soil salinization is a global agricultural and ecological crisis that poses a grave threat to food security (Qadir et al., 2014; Mao et al., 2023). Rice (Oryza sativa L.), a vital staple crop, is highly sensitive to salt stress, with salinity-related annual yield losses of up to 20%–30% worldwide (Islam et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022). Plants have evolved sophisticated mechanisms to combat salt toxicity, in which Ca2+ serves as a central second messenger (Ma et al., 2015; Zhu, 2016; Dong et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2025). Salt stress triggers a transient increase in the cytosolic free Ca2+ concentration ([Ca2+]cyt) (Li et al., 2017; Pei et al., 2024). Specifically, Na+ binds to glycosyl inositol phosphoryl ceramide (GIPC) sphingolipids in the plasma membrane, altering the membrane potential to activate plasma membrane Ca2+ channels and thus induce Ca2+ influx (Jiang et al., 2019). Increased [Ca2+]cyt is sensed by the Ca2+-binding protein SOS3/SCaBP8, which interacts with and activates the serine/threonine protein kinase SOS2; the SOS2–SOS3 complex then activates the Na+/H+ antiporter SOS1 to enable Na+ efflux, thereby enhancing salt tolerance (El Mahi et al., 2019; Jiang et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2023b, 2023c; Lu et al., 2023). This SOS pathway is recognized as a core regulatory mechanism for the control of ion homeostasis and mitigation of Na+ toxicity, playing a pivotal role in salt-stress adaptation (Lou et al., 2020b; Ma et al., 2025). Although salt-induced increases in [Ca2+]cyt and downstream adaptive mechanisms have been well characterized (Jiang et al., 2019; Ma et al., 2019; Bellandi et al., 2022; Pei et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2025), less is known about the molecular identities of key Ca2+-permeable channels and the regulatory components involved in salt-induced Ca2+ influx.

Cyclic nucleotide-gated channels (CNGCs), a class of Ca2+-permeable non-selective cation channels found throughout eukaryotes, mediate extracellular Ca2+ influx to regulate plant growth, development, and stress adaptation in response to various intracellular and extracellular signals (Moeder et al., 2011; Pan et al., 2019; Tian et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2021; Cook et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). Functional characterization of CNGC family members in Arabidopsis thaliana has revealed their multifaceted roles: CNGC7/8 and CNGC18 orchestrate apical Ca2+ gradient–dependent pollen tube growth, and CNGC2/4, CNGC11/12, and CNGC19/20 positively regulate immunity through Ca2+-mediated defense responses (Yoshioka et al., 2006; Chin et al., 2013; Pan et al., 2019; Kundu et al., 2025). Although a transient influx of Ca2+ triggers protective responses, prolonged influx can result in cytotoxic Ca2+ overload (Zheng et al., 2021; Du et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2025). Therefore, fine regulation of the activity and stability of Ca2+ channels is crucial for tight control of Ca2+ influx. Phosphorylation is an important mechanism for regulating the activity and protein stability of Ca2+ channels, including CNGCs (Yu et al., 2023; Peng et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2021a; Yang et al., 2024; Ming et al., 2025). OPEN STOMATA 1 (OST1/SnRK2.6) activates CNGC5/6/9/12 via phosphorylation, triggering Ca2+ signaling to promote ABA-induced stomatal closure in Arabidopsis (Yang et al., 2024). CNGC2/4 are activated by BOTRYTIS-INDUCED KINASE1 (BIK1)-mediated phosphorylation, increasing [Ca2+]cyt levels and thus initiating innate immunity in Arabidopsis (Tian et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2021). OsCNGC9 is activated via phosphorylation by SAPK8, a SnRK2 protein kinase, thereby inducing Ca2+-mediated chilling tolerance in rice (Wang et al., 2021a). In Arabidopsis, the receptor-like kinase PSY1R activates CNGC20 via phosphorylation to promote Ca2+-mediated cold acclimation during early cold stress, whereas prolonged chilling induces the phosphorylation of CNGC20 by the receptor-like cytoplasmic kinase CRPK1 and subsequent CNGC20 degradation, attenuating cold responses and establishing a negative feedback loop for precise Ca2+-mediated cold adaptation (Peng et al., 2024). A recent study showed that the SCF ubiquitin E3 ligase SKIP31 mediates ubiquitination of CNGC3 for degradation in the absence of pathogens, whereas BIK1 phosphorylates SKIP31 to inhibit its ubiquitin ligase activity and phosphorylates CNGC3 to enhance its Ca2+ channel activity upon pathogen infection, triggering Ca2+-mediated plant immunity (Yi et al., 2025). BIK1 thus plays a dual role, promoting both the channel activity and stability of CNGC. The channel activity of CNGCs is thus regulated by a variety of kinases, some of which promote both channel activity and protein stability. Despite recent progress, the underlying mechanisms by which phosphorylation modifications regulate the stability of CNGCs are largely unknown.

Ca2+ signals are decoded by various Ca2+-binding proteins. These include the Ca2+-dependent protein kinases (CDPKs/CPKs), a unique plant-specific family of serine/threonine protein kinases that function as both Ca2+ sensors and signal transducers, inducing downstream transcriptional/translational reprogramming through phosphorylation of their target proteins in response to environmental challenges (Loranger et al., 2021; Fan et al., 2023; Jia et al., 2025). After activation by salt-induced Ca2+, CPK3 phosphorylates vacuolar two-pore K+ channel 1 (TPK1), enabling its interaction with 14-3-3 proteins and promoting potassium (K+) efflux to maintain cytosolic K+ homeostasis, thus enhancing salt tolerance (Latz et al., 2013). In addition to their typical roles in decoding complex and stimulus-specific Ca2+ signals, emerging evidence in Arabidopsis shows that CPKs are involved in shaping the primary Ca2+ signature through phosphorylation of Ca2+ channels. CPK1 phosphorylates and activates CNGC5/6/9 to establish a Ca2+ gradient that promotes root hair growth, and CPK32 specifically activates CNGC18 to increase Ca2+ influx in the pollen tube tip (Zhou et al., 2014; Zhu et al., 2025). In response to gradual cooling, CPK3 activates CNGC5/6 to shape a specific Ca2+ signature for acclimation to natural cold stress (Ming et al., 2025). Nonetheless, how CPKs regulate primary Ca2+ signals and whether they modulate salt-induced Ca2+ signals through phosphorylation of CNGCs remain to be determined. More importantly, the feedback mechanism by which CPKs attenuate Ca2+-mediated protective responses after prolonged stress is largely unknown.

In this study, we demonstrate that the OsCPK4–OsCNGC7 regulatory module fine-tunes salt-stress responses in rice. During the early stage of salt stress, the salt-responsive Ca2+-dependent protein kinase OsCPK4 enhances the channel activity and protein stability of OsCNGC7 via phosphorylation, mediating rapid Ca2+ influx to trigger the downstream salt-adaptive response. After prolonged salt stress, the kinase activity of OsCPK4 is suppressed, thus reducing the phosphorylation and stability of OsCNGC7 and impairing its channel function. As a result, the Ca2+-mediated stress response is attenuated, promoting a rapid transition from the salt-stress response to plant growth recovery. Our study reveals an autoregulatory mechanism that fine-tunes Ca2+ signaling for salt-stress adaptation in rice through OsCPK4-mediated phosphorylation of OsCNGC7, providing novel targets for resolving growth–defense trade-offs during crop improvement.

Results

OsCNGC7 is a positive regulator of salt tolerance

Extracellular Ca2+ influx mediated by CNGCs has been shown to play essential roles in response to various abiotic stresses, including chilling, heat, and drought (Wang et al., 2021a; Peng et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2025), but less is known about the roles and Ca2+-encoding mechanisms of CNGCs under salt stress. Examination of the expression of 16 CNGC genes in rice revealed that OsCNGC7 was strongly induced by salt treatment (Supplemental Figure 1), suggesting its potential involvement in the salt-stress response.

To explore whether OsCNGC7 regulates the response to salt in rice, we generated two CRISPR-Cas9 loss-of-function mutants in the Nipponbare (NIP) background; oscngc7-cr1 and oscngc7-cr2 harbored 1-bp and 7-bp deletions in the second exon of OsCNGC7, respectively, resulting in premature termination of the predicted protein (Supplemental Figure 2A). When 2-week-old seedlings of different genotypes were subjected to a 12-day salt treatment followed by a 9-day recovery period, the survival rates of oscngc7-cr1 and oscngc7-cr2 were significantly lower than those of NIP (Figures 1A and 1D; Supplemental Figure 2B), suggesting that OsCNGC7 positively regulates rice salt tolerance. To confirm the role of OsCNGC7 in salt tolerance, we introduced OsCNGC7pro:OsCNGC7 into the oscngc7-cr1 and oscngc7-cr2 backgrounds to generate two independent complementation lines (Com #1 and Com #2). After salt treatment and recovery, the survival rates of the complementation lines were comparable to those of NIP (Figures 1B and 1E; Supplemental Figure 2C), confirming that OsCNGC7 fully rescued the salt-sensitive phenotypes of the mutants. To further assess the contribution of OsCNGC7 to salt tolerance, we subjected the OsCNGC7 overexpression lines 35S:OsCNGC7-GFP #1, #2, and #3 to salt-tolerance assays and observed that they exhibited significantly higher survival rates than NIP (Figures 1C and 1F; Supplemental Figures 2D–2F). Salt stress damages plants in two ways: osmotic stress and Na+ toxicity (Deinlein et al., 2014; Ma et al., 2025). To determine whether OsCNGC7 influences the osmotic stress response, the Na+ toxicity response, or both, we included 180 mM mannitol (isotonic to 100 mM NaCl) (Katsuhara et al., 2014) in the stress-tolerance assays. The OsCNGC7 overexpression lines exhibited a significantly higher survival rate (82.37%) than NIP (56.74%) under mannitol-induced osmotic stress, and NaCl caused a more pronounced decline in survival rate in NIP (23.61%) than in 35S:OsCNGC7-GFP #1 (70.83%) (Supplemental Figure 3). These results indicate that OsCNGC7 regulates rice responses to both osmotic stress and ion toxicity and may play a more prominent role in regulating the response to Na+ toxicity. Na+ efflux is a key physiological mechanism by which plants remove excessive intracellular Na+ to reduce cytoplasmic ion toxicity, ultimately enhancing plant salt tolerance (Gámez-Arjona et al., 2024). We first examined the expression of key genes involved in Na+ efflux or compartmentalization, including OsSOS1, OsSOS2, OsHKT1;5, and OsNHX1 (Soni et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2023a; Liu et al., 2025a), in different OsCNGC7 genotypes. Transcript levels of these genes were significantly lower in oscngc7-cr2 but higher in 35S:OsCNGC7-GFP #1 compared with NIP (Figure 1G). We next measured Na+ efflux at the root tip using non-invasive micro-test technology (NMT). After salt treatment, net Na+ efflux was significantly higher in the roots of 35S:OsCNGC7-GFP #1 but lower in those of oscngc7-cr2 compared with NIP (Figures 1H and 1I). Consistent with these results, SOS2 phosphorylation was substantially higher in 35S:OsCNGC7-GFP #1 than in wild-type plants under salt stress (Supplemental Figure 4). In addition, the oscngc7 mutants exhibited significantly higher malondialdehyde (MDA) levels but lower chlorophyll content and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity under salt stress than NIP (Supplemental Figure 5), indicating that OsCNGC7 plays a role in minimizing the oxidative damage imposed by salt stress and providing further evidence that OsCNGC7 positively regulates salt tolerance in rice.

OsCNGC7 functions as an active channel to mediate extracellular Ca2+ influx under salt stress

Some plasma membrane-localized CNGCs mediate extracellular Ca2+ influx in response to various environmental stimuli (Wang et al., 2021a, 2021b). Subcellular localization assays in rice protoplasts showed that OsCNGC7 was mainly localized at the plasma membrane (Figure 2A), suggesting that OsCNGC7 may function as a Ca2+-permeable channel. Although the Xenopus laevis oocyte system is widely used to study Ca2+channels, the Ca2+-activated chloride channels present in these oocytes generate interfering currents (Peng et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2025). To address this issue, we used barium (Ba2+) solutions rather than Ca2+ solutions, as Ba2+ and Ca2+ are divalent cations with similar charges and radii, and Ba2+ can pass through Ca2+ channels and generate comparable currents (Verhage et al., 1995; Peng et al., 2024). To investigate whether OsCNGC7 functions as an active Ca2+ channel, we injected cRNA of OsCNGC7 into Xenopus oocytes for transient expression for 2–3 days (Supplemental Figure 6), with injection of AtCNGC20 cRNA as a positive control (Peng et al., 2024). Channel currents were monitored using the two-electrode voltage clamp (TEVC) technique. Under step-voltage stimulation ranging from +20 mV to −160 mV, oocytes injected with water (negative controls) showed no readily detectable channel currents, whereas oocytes expressing OsCNGC7 exhibited a large inward current in a bath solution containing 30 mM BaCl2 (Figures 2B and 2C). Notably, inward currents in the same oocyte mediated by OsCNGC7 were abolished by application of LaCl3, a typical Ca2+ channel blocker (Figures 2B and 2C). We also used 30 mM CaCl2 with 500 μM 4,4′-diisothiocyanostilbene-2,2′ disulfonic acid (DIDS), a typical chloride channel blocker (Pan et al., 2019), as the bath solution for TEVC assays, and similar results were obtained (Supplemental Figure 7). To further characterize the function of OsCNGC7 as a Ca2+ channel, we performed cytosolic Ca2+ imaging assays on HEK293T cells transiently expressing OsCNGC7, with cells expressing OsCNGC9 serving as a positive control (Wang et al., 2021a). After application of 3 mM Ca2+, we observed a significant increase in cytosolic Ca2+ in HEK293T cells expressing OsCNGC7 but not in control cells expressing the empty vector (Figures 2D and 2E; Supplemental Figure 8). We next used NMT to investigate whether OsCNGC7 is involved in salt-induced extracellular Ca2+ influx in living rice roots (Yu et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2024). Upon salt stimulation, the OsCNGC7 overexpression line (35S:OsCNGC7-GFP #1) showed a more rapid and sharp Ca2+ influx than NIP, and this influx was completely abolished by the addition of LaCl3. By contrast, the salt-induced Ca2+ influx was lower in the oscngc7-cr1 mutant than in NIP (Figure 2F). As expected, Ca2+ influx was restored to wild-type levels in the complementation line (Com #1) under salt stress (Figure 2F). Consistent with these findings, the average maximum Ca2+ influx was significantly higher in 35S:OsCNGC7-GFP #1 but lower in oscngc7-cr1 than in the wild type, and there was no significant difference between Com #1 and NIP (Figure 2G).

To further confirm that the OsCNGC7-mediated Ca2+ influx led to an increase in [Ca2+]cyt in response to salt stress, we monitored changes in [Ca2+]cyt by transiently expressing GCaMP3 (a fluorescent protein–based [Ca2+]cyt indicator) in rice protoplasts. As shown in Figures 2H and 2I, the increase in [Ca2+]cyt upon salt stimulation was greater in 35S:OsCNGC7-GFP #1 than in NIP but less pronounced in oscngc7-cr1. Taken together, these results convincingly demonstrate that OsCNGC7 functions as a Ca2+-permeable channel that positively regulates salt-induced extracellular Ca2+ influx, thereby increasing [Ca2+]cyt.

OsCPK4 interacts with and phosphorylates OsCNGC7

To reveal the molecular mechanism by which the OsCNGC7 channel is activated under salt stimulation, we performed a split-ubiquitin membrane yeast two-hybrid library screening. Among the OsCNGC7-interacting proteins, the Ca2+-dependent protein kinase OsCPK4 was indentified(Figure 3A). To determine whether OsCPK4 and OsCNGC7 interacted in planta, we performed bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) experiments in Nicotiana benthamiana, using the OsCPK4–OsRLCK176 interaction as a positive control (Wang et al., 2018b). As shown in Figure 3B, a clear YFP fluorescence signal was observed when OsCNGC7-cYFP and OsCPK4-nYFP were transiently co-expressed in N. benthamiana leaves. Likewise, split-luciferase complementation (SLC) assays revealed robust luminescence when cLUC-OsCNGC7 was co-expressed with OsCPK4-nLUC (Figure 3C). To confirm the specificity of the OsCPK4–OsCNGC7 interaction, we included representative members from four groups of CPKs (OsCPK13 and OsCPK24 from Group I, OsCPK11 from Group II, OsCPK21 from Group III, and OsCPK4 and OsCPK18 from Group IV) (Asano et al., 2005) in the SLC assays. CNGCs typically consist of six transmembrane domains, with both the N terminus and the C terminus oriented toward the cytoplasm (Cook et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). To identify the specific domain(s) of OsCNGC7 involved in the interaction with OsCPK4, we subjected the N terminus (1–113 aa, OsCNGC7-N), transmembrane (TM) domain (114–416 aa, OsCNGC7-TM), and C terminus (417–702 aa, OsCNGC7-C) (Supplemental Figure 10A) to SLC assays. Strong interaction was observed only when OsCPK4-nLUC was co-expressed with cLUC-OsCNGC7-C, but not with cLUC-OsCNGC7-N or cLUC-OsCNGC7-TM (Supplemental Figure 10B), suggesting that only the C terminus of OsCNGC7 interacts with OsCPK4. Next, glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assays showed that a substantial amount of His-OsCPK4 was pulled down when incubated with GST-OsCNGC7-C, but not with GST alone (Figure 3D), confirming that OsCPK4 directly interacts with OsCNGC7 in vitro. Together, these results demonstrate that OsCNGC7 interacts with OsCPK4 both in vitro and in vivo.

The physical interaction between OsCPK4 and OsCNGC7 prompted us to investigate whether OsCNGC7 is a substrate for phosphorylation by OsCPK4. We examined whether OsCPK4 could phosphorylate the C terminus of OsCNGC7 by performing in vitro phosphorylation assays, considering only the interaction of OsCNGC7-C with OsCPK4 (Supplemental Figure 10B). Phos-tag biotin detection revealed that GST-OsCNGC7-C was clearly phosphorylated by His-OsCPK4 but not by its kinase-dead variant (OsCPK4-dead, OsCPK4K88E/D185A) (Figure 3E) containing mutations of Lys88 and Asp185, which are critical for its kinase activity (Wang et al., 2018a; Tao et al., 2025). To determine whether OsCNGC7 is phosphorylated in planta and how salt stress affects its phosphorylation, we analyzed the phosphorylation of OsCNGC7-GFP immunoprecipitated from 35S:OsCNGC7-GFP #1 plants subjected to salt stress. The results showed that OsCNGC7 phosphorylation was substantially increased by salt stress (Figure 3F). To investigate whether OsCNGC7 is phosphorylated in an OsCPK4-dependent manner during salt stress, we examined the phosphorylation of OsCNGC7 in the oscpk4-cr1 mutant, a CRISPR-Cas9 loss-of-function mutant in the ZH11 background (Li et al., 2022a). Salt-induced phosphorylation of OsCNGC7-GFP was substantially lower in oscpk4-cr1 than in ZH11 (Figure 3G; Supplemental Figure 11), indicating that phosphorylation of OsCNGC7 during salt stress is dependent on OsCPK4. Consistent with this finding, co-immunoprecipitation (coIP) and SLC assays indicated that the interaction between OsCPK4 and OsCNGC7 was markedly enhanced by salt treatment (Supplemental Figure 12). Together, these results demonstrate that OsCNGC7 is a substrate of OsCPK4, which promotes OsCNGC7 phosphorylation in response to salt stress.

CPKs have been shown to phosphorylate Ser/Thr residues in the conserved R-X-X-S/T motif of their substrates (Zhu et al., 2025). Four putative CPK-recognized phosphorylation motifs, containing Ser (S)599, S634, S658, and S682, were identified in the C terminus of OsCNGC7 (Supplemental Figure 13A). To determine whether these potential phosphorylation sites are specifically targeted by OsCPK4, we used site-directed mutagenesis to individually substitute these four Ser residues with alanine (Ala), thus blocking their phosphorylation by OsCPK4. Single-site substitutions (OsCNGC7-CS599A, -CS634A, -CS658A, and -CS682A) did not substantially reduce the phosphorylation of OsCNGC7 by OsCPK4 (Supplemental Figure 13B). However, replacement of two or three Ser residues (i.e., OsCNGC7-CS599A/S634A, OsCNGC7-CS658A/S682A, OsCNGC7-CS599A/S634A/S658A, and OsCNGC7-CS599A/S658A/S682A) partially reduced OsCNGC7 phosphorylation, and complete mutation of all four Ser residues (OsCNGC7-CS599A/S634A/S658A/S682A, OsCNGC7-C4A) nearly abolished the phosphorylation signal (Figure 3H), suggesting that these four Ser residues in OsCNGC7 are phosphorylated by OsCPK4 in vitro.

We then measured whole-cell currents using the TEVC assay (Supplemental Figure 14A). After applying voltage steps from +20 mV to −160 mV, we observed a pronounced inward current in oocytes expressing OsCNGC7. OsCPK4-mediated phosphorylation enhances the protein stability of OsCNGC7

Phosphorylation regulates CNGCs through two mechanisms, modulating both their channel activity and protein stability (Wang et al., 2021a; Yu et al., 2023; Peng et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Ming et al., 2025). We therefore performed a cell-free protein degradation assay to examine whether OsCPK4-mediated phosphorylation affects the protein stability of OsCNGC7. When recombinant GST-OsCNGC7-C was incubated with total protein extracts from ZH11 (wild type) and the oscpk4-cr1 mutant, GST-OsCNGC7-C degradation was much faster in oscpk4-cr1 extracts than in ZH11 extracts, and the degradation rate of GST-OsCNGC7-C4A was faster than that of GST-OsCNGC7-C (Figure 4D; Supplemental Figure 15), confirming that OsCPK4 stabilizes the OsCNGC7 protein via phosphorylation. Addition of MG132 markedly delayed the degradation of GST-OsCNGC7-C (Figure 4D), suggesting involvement of the 26S proteasome in OsCNGC7 degradation.

We next investigated the effects of OsCPK4-mediated phosphorylation on OsCNGC7 stability under salt stress in planta. To this end, we generated transgenic plants overexpressing 3×FLAG-fused OsCNGC7, OsCNGC74A, and OsCNGC74D (35S:OsCNGC7-3×FLAG #1, 35S:OsCNGC74A-3×FLAG #4, and 35S:OsCNGC74D-3×FLAG #5), which displayed comparable transcript levels of OsCNGC7 (Supplemental Figure 16A). We then subjected the transgenic plants to salt stress in the presence of cycloheximide (CHX), an inhibitor of protein biosynthesis. Assessment of protein levels in these plants showed that OsCNGC74A was degraded much faster than OsCNGC7, whereas OsCNGC74D was more stable (Figure 4E). Accordingly, when rice protoplasts transiently expressing OsCNGC7-GFP were subjected to salt stress with CHX, OsCNGC7 degradation was substantially faster in oscpk4-cr1 than in ZH11 protoplasts (Supplemental Figure 16B). Taken together, these observations demonstrate that OsCPK4-mediated phosphorylation stabilizes OsCNGC7 during salt stress.

Phosphorylation of OsCNGC7 by OsCPK4 confers salt tolerance in rice

To evaluate the role of OsCPK4-mediated OsCNGC7 phosphorylation in rice salt response, we investigated the salt tolerance of the 35S:OsCNGC7-3×FLAG #1, 35S:OsCNGC74A-3×FLAG #4, and 35S:OsCNGC74D-3×FLAG #5 lines. As shown in Figures 4F and 4G, 35S:OsCNGC74A-3×FLAG #4 exhibited salt tolerance comparable to that of NIP but was more sensitive to salt stress than 35S:OsCNGC7-3×FLAG #1, indicating that phosphorylation of OsCNGC7 plays an essential role in rice salt tolerance. 35S:OsCNGC74D-3×FLAG #5 showed greater salt tolerance than NIP but was still less tolerant than 35S:OsCNGC7-3×FLAG #1 (Figures 4H and 4I). This result suggested that constitutive phosphorylation of OsCNGC7 might give rise to a prolonged Ca2+ influx, leading to Ca2+ overload and cytotoxicity. To assess this possibility, we monitored cell death in 35S:OsCNGC7-3×FLAG #1 and 35S:OsCNGC74D-3×FLAG #5 plants under salt stress by Trypan blue staining and found that cell death was more severe in 35S:OsCNGC74D-3×FLAG #5 than in 35S:OsCNGC7-3×FLAG #1 (Supplemental Figure 17). Consistent with the salt-tolerance assays, NMT analysis revealed that, after salt treatment, net root Na+ efflux in 35S:OsCNGC74A-3×FLAG #4 was comparable to that in NIP but lower than that in 35S:OsCNGC7-3×FLAG #1, whereas Na+ efflux was lower in 35S:OsCNGC74D-3×FLAG #5 than in 35S:OsCNGC7-3×FLAG #1 but still higher than that in NIP (Supplemental Figure 18).

To determine whether the phosphorylation status of OsCNGC7 affects its Ca2+ channel activity in planta, we examined the salt-induced Ca2+ influx in 35S:OsCNGC7-3×FLAG #1, 35S:OsCNGC74A-3×FLAG #4, and 35S:OsCNGC74D-3×FLAG #5 by NMT. Compared with the rapid and sharp Ca2+ influx observed in 35S:OsCNGC7-3×FLAG #1 upon salt stimulation, the salt-triggered Ca2+ influx was less pronounced in 35S:OsCNGC74A-3×FLAG #4 but more robust in 35S:OsCNGC74D-3×FLAG #5 (Figures 4J and 4K), demonstrating that phosphorylation of OsCNGC7 at S599, S634, S658, and S682 by OsCPK4 promotes its Ca2+ channel activity in response to salt stress in rice. Taken together, these data provide compelling evidence that OsCPK4-mediated phosphorylation enhances Ca2+ channel activity and protein stability of OsCNGC7 under salt stress, thereby improving rice salt tolerance.

Building on our finding that OsCPK4 phosphorylates OsCNGC7 and modulates its protein stability (Figures 4D and 4E), we next investigated the dynamics of OsCPK4 kinase activity in 35S:OsCPK4-3×FLAG #1 plants during salt stress. Immunoprecipitated OsCPK4 exhibited low basal activity before salt treatment; its activity was markedly increased under short-term salt stress and then declined under prolonged salt stress (Figure 6C), consistent with the dynamics of OsCNGC7 phosphorylation (Figure 6A). These observations indicated that OsCPK4 activity is enhanced under short-term salt stress but suppressed under prolonged stress. To characterize the regulatory mechanism underlying OsCPK4 kinase activity, we examined whether the increase in its activity required Ca2+. To this end, we used the Ca2+ chelator EGTA, which effectively suppresses the transient increase in [Ca2+]cyt induced by salt stress (Schmollinger et al., 2013). When plants were subjected to salt stress in the presence of 10 mM EGTA, the salt-induced kinase activity of OsCPK4 was greatly reduced (Figure 6D), indicating that Ca2+ is required for the increase in OsCPK4 kinase activity during short-term salt stress. Consistent with these findings, surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assays demonstrated that OsCPK4 can directly bind Ca2+ in vitro (Supplemental Figure 21). Together, these results demonstrate that OsCPK4 is a salt stress–responsive Ca2+-dependent protein kinase.

Upon NaCl stimulation, [Ca2+]cyt in plant cells increases rapidly from 100–200 nM under resting conditions to 300–750 nM (Kaplan et al., 2006; Li et al., 2025). Because the salt-induced kinase activity of OsCPK4 requires Ca2+, we next investigated whether the activity of recombinant OsCPK4 responds to the physiological range of Ca2+ concentrations in vitro. To this end, we established a defined Ca2+ concentration gradient (0–100 μM) by adding 5 mM EGTA to various concentrations of CaCl2 (Hu et al., 2024). In vitro phosphorylation assays showed that recombinant OsCPK4 had low basal activity in the absence of Ca2+ and that its activity increased substantially at Ca2+ concentrations within the physiological range (about 200–750 nM). Notably, EGTA inhibited the Ca2+-dependent enhancement of OsCPK4 kinase activity (Supplemental Figure 22A). We next performed in vitro phosphorylation assays to determine whether OsCPK4-mediated phosphorylation of OsCNGC7 was also enhanced under physiological Ca2+ concentrations. Consistent with the dynamics of OsCPK4 activity, OsCPK4-mediated phosphorylation of OsCNGC7 was strongly increased at physiological Ca2+ concentrations (Figure 6F; Supplemental Figure 22B), indicating that these concentrations enhance OsCPK4 kinase activity, thereby promoting OsCNGC7 phosphorylation. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that short-term salt stress increases OsCPK4 kinase activity, which in turn enhances OsCNGC7 phosphorylation; however, prolonged salt exposure suppresses OsCPK4 activity, attenuating OsCNGC7 phosphorylation.

Discussion

Under environmental stress, plants typically prioritize stress defense at the expense of growth and yield, a phenomenon known as the growth–defense trade-off (Zhu, 2016; Zhang et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2025). Understanding the molecular mechanisms that govern the trade-off between growth and salt tolerance is therefore important for enhancing crop productivity under saline conditions. Here, we demonstrated that the OsCPK4–OsCNGC7 module fine-tunes the balance between salt tolerance and plant growth in rice (Figure 7). Under normal conditions, [Ca2+]cyt remains at a low, steady-state level appropriate for plant growth. Upon salt stress, the kinase activity of OsCPK4 is enhanced; it phosphorylates OsCNGC7, increasing OsCNGC7 channel activity and protein stability to enable rapid extracellular Ca2+ influx, thus amplifying Ca2+ signals to trigger a stress response. After prolonged salt exposure, the activity of OsCPK4 is suppressed, reducing OsCNGC7 phosphorylation and protein abundance to attenuate the Ca2+-mediated stress response. Plants then shift from stress response to the growth recovery phase (Figure 7). Our findings reveal an auto-regulatory module that fine-tunes Ca2+ signals in fluctuating environments to optimize salt-stress responses, balancing growth and salt response according to stress duration and intensity. A proposed working model showing how the OsCPK4–OsCNGC7 module fine-tunes salt-stress adaptation in rice.

Under normal conditions, the cytosolic Ca2+ concentration ([Ca2+]cyt) remains at a low, steady-state level. Upon salt stress, the kinase activity of OsCPK4 is increased, and OsCPK4 phosphorylates OsCNGC7 to enhance its channel activity and protein stability. This enables rapid extracellular Ca2+ influx, amplifying Ca2+ signals to trigger the salt-stress response. By contrast, prolonged salt exposure suppresses the kinase activity of OsCPK4. This reduces OsCNGC7 phosphorylation, decreasing its activity and stability and thus reducing the Ca2+-mediated stress response. Plants then shift from the stress response to the growth-recovery phase.

As a second messenger, Ca2+ plays essential roles in plant responses to environmental challenges (Hashimoto and Kudla, 2011; Lu et al., 2025). The mechanisms that shape specific Ca2+ signatures in response to environmental stresses have been studied extensively (Sanders et al., 1999; Zhu, 2016), but how stress-induced Ca2+ signals are scaled back during prolonged stress or growth recovery has received less attention. Precise regulation of stress-induced Ca2+ signals is critical for optimizing stress responses, enabling a balance between plant growth and stress tolerance (Lu et al., 2025). Here, we describe a newly identified mechanism that fine-tunes Ca2+ signals to optimize the salt-stress response, ensuring rapid activation of protective responses while preventing overactivation of the energy-depleting stress response, thus optimizing the growth–salt tolerance balance. Specifically, OsCPK4 activity increases rapidly during early salt stress, activating OsCNGC7 via phosphorylation and thereby triggering the Ca2+-mediated salt response (Figures 1, 2, 3 and 4). By contrast, prolonged salt exposure suppresses OsCPK4 activity, reducing OsCNGC7 channel activity and promoting degradation of the OsCNGC7 protein (Figure 6) to attenuate the stress response. Our results suggest that Ca2+ influx slows after prolonged salt exposure, owing to reduced phosphorylation and protein levels of OsCNGC7 (Figures 6A and 6B). This notion is supported by several lines of evidence. First, TEVC assays in Xenopus oocytes showed that OsCNGC74A produces smaller currents than OsCNGC7, showing its reduced capacity for Ca2+ transport (Figures 4A–4C). Second, NMT assays in living roots demonstrated that the salt-triggered Ca2+ influx was substantially smaller in OsCNGC74A-overexpression lines than in OsCNGC7-overexpression lines (Figures 4J and 4K). Finally, biochemical evidence indicated that the stability of OsCNGC74A was markedly reduced in plants during salt stress (Figure 4E). Together, our findings delineate an auto-regulatory mechanism that precisely regulates Ca2+ signaling in fluctuating environments, advancing our understanding of the trade-off between plant growth and stress responses.

In future work, it would be worthwhile to examine the mechanisms underlying the reduction in OsCPK4 kinase activity after prolonged salt exposure, as this reduction is essential for the precise regulation of Ca2+ signals by the OsCPK4–OsCNGC7 module. We propose that prolonged Ca2+ influx may be one of the factors that reduce the kinase activity of OsCPK4, a possibility that is supported by in vitro phosphorylation assays (Figures 6E and 6F). OsCPK4 activity was substantially enhanced across the physiological range of Ca2+ concentrations (about 200–750 nM) but was suppressed at higher Ca2+ concentrations, suggesting an auto-regulatory feedback mechanism for orchestrating the Ca2+ influx and shaping the Ca2+ signal in fluctuating environments. Similar Ca2+-dependent feedback regulation has been reported previously in plants (Hu et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025b). Arabidopsis ANN4 mediates the salt-induced Ca2+ influx to activate the SOS pathway, whereas the Ca2+-binding SCaBP8–SOS2 complex suppresses ANN4 function to prevent excessive Ca2+ influx (Ma et al., 2019), revealing a negative feedback loop that fine-tunes Ca2+ signals and optimizes plant salt response. Similarly, CPK3 rapidly activates CNGC5/6 via phosphorylation to promote Ca2+ influx upon cold stress; however, during prolonged cold exposure, the Ca2+-binding protein CaM2 inhibits CNGC5/6 activity to dampen Ca2+ signals (Ming et al., 2025). Consistent with our findings, free Ca2+ concentrations in the physiological range promote OsCPK9 kinase activity and phosphorylation of its substrate OsSAPK8, but high Ca2+ concentrations (>1,000 nM) inhibit OsCPK9 activity and substrate phosphorylation (Liu et al., 2025b). These findings suggest a general model by which Ca2+-dependent feedback regulation of Ca2+ channel activity optimizes stress responses in plants.

The role of CDPKs/CPKs as Ca2+ sensors and decoders in plants is well recognized (Wernimont et al., 2010). Ca2+ binding induces conformational changes in CPKs, relieving autoinhibition and enabling them to relay Ca2+ signals to downstream pathways through substrate phosphorylation (Ormancey et al., 2017). Recent studies have demonstrated that CPKs can also contribute to shaping Ca2+ signatures by regulating the activity of Ca2+-permeable channels. In Arabidopsis, CPK3 activates CNGC5 and CNGC6 to promote cold-induced Ca2+ influx, and CPK1 activates CNGC5/6/9 to establish tip-focused Ca2+ gradients during root hair growth (Ming et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). Here, our results suggest that OsCPK4 regulates salt-induced Ca2+ signaling by modulating OsCNGC7 activity. Therefore, OsCPK4 may function as a Ca2+-dependent regulator that amplifies Ca2+ influx through OsCNGC7. Nevertheless, the mechanism responsible for the initial activation of OsCPK4 during early salt stress remains unclear. On the basis of previous studies, we propose that salt stress may trigger the initial Ca2+ influx through early salt- or osmotic-stress-sensing pathways. For example, early Ca2+ influx may be initiated by Na+ perception through membrane-associated GIPC sphingolipids, which have been linked to salt-induced membrane depolarization and Ca2+ influx (Jiang et al., 2019), or by osmotic-stress-mediated activation of OSCA channels and other Ca2+-permeable channels (Pei et al., 2024). The initial increase in Ca2+ levels may then activate OsCPK4, which phosphorylates and activates OsCNGC7, further enhancing Ca2+ influx and strengthening salt-stress responses.

The SOS pathway is known to play a core regulatory role in plant salt tolerance (Lou et al., 2020b; Ma et al., 2025). We explored whether the OsCPK4–OsCNGC7 module interacts with the SOS pathway directly, but SLC assays showed that OsCPK4 does not interact with OsSOS1 or OsSOS2 (Supplemental Figure 23). Interestingly, OsSOS2 phosphorylation was substantially increased in 35S:OsCNGC7-GFP #1 lines compared with wild-type plants (Supplemental Figure 4), and OsCNGC7 promotes Na+ efflux in plants, as indicated by NMT assays (Figures 1H and 1I). These observations support a functional connection between OsCPK4–OsCNGC7-mediated Ca2+ signaling and activation of the SOS pathway .

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Rice knockout mutants (oscngc7-cr1 and oscngc7-cr2) were generated in the NIP background (O. sativa spp. japonica) using the CRISPR-Cas9 system. For overexpression lines, the CDS of OsCNGC7 or its variants (OsCNGC74A and OsCNGC74D) fused with GFP or 3×FLAG under the 35S promoter was cloned into pCAMBIA1301, and the resulting construct was introduced into NIP by Agrobacterium tumefaciens (GV3101)-mediated transformation. For complementation lines, the OsCNGC7 CDS driven by its native promoter (2000 bp) was cloned into pCAMBIA1301, and the construct was transformed into the oscngc7-cr1 and oscngc7-cr2 backgrounds. The oscpk4-cr1 and oscpk4-cr2 knockout mutants generated by the CRISPR-Cas9 system have been described previously (Li et al., 2022a). The T-DNA insertion lines oscpk4-1 (04Z11DJ31) and oscpk4-2 (03Z11AF12) were obtained from a T-DNA insertion population in O. sativa ssp. japonica cv. ZH11 (Zhang et al., 2006). Primers used for vector construction and identification of mutants or transgenic plants are listed in Supplemental Data 1.

Rice plants were cultivated in the experimental paddy fields of the Chongqing University Experimental Station in Chongqing, China, during the rice growing season. For seedling-stage salt-tolerance tests, seedlings were grown hydroponically in Yoshida nutrient solution (Coolaber) in a controlled greenhouse (14-h light/10-h dark cycle, 28°C/25°C, 60% humidity, and 200 μmol/m2/s light from white fluorescent tubes).

Evaluation of salt tolerance

For salt tolerance assays, 2-week-old rice seedlings were transferred to a nutrient solution supplemented with 100 mM NaCl, exposed to this salt stress for 12–16 days, and allowed to recover for 9–15 days. Seedlings that had green leaves or generated new visible shoots upon recovery were considered to be viable. The number of surviving plants for each genotype was recorded, and the survival rate was calculated. At least three independent experiments were performed, with at least 24 seedlings per genotype in each experiment. Measurements of total chlorophyll content, MDA content, and SOD activity were performed on 2-week-old seedlings that had been exposed to 100 mM NaCl for 3 days, in accordance with previously published protocols (Al-Aghabary et al., 2004).

RNA extraction and RT–qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from 2-week-old rice seedlings using TRIzol reagent (Takara, 9109) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Reverse transcription was performed using reverse transcriptase (Takara, 2690A), and quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green reagent (Takara, RR820A), with OsUBQ5 (LOC_Os01g22490) as the internal control (Soni et al., 2021). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with three biological replicates (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). Primer sequences for specific genes are provided in Supplemental Data 1.

Subcellular localization

Subcellular localization assays in rice protoplasts were performed as described previously (Guo et al., 2006). The full-length CDS of OsCNGC7 fused with GFP was cloned into the pRTVC vector to generate pRTVC-OsCNGC7-GFP, and the plasma membrane marker OsPIP2;1 fused with RFP was cloned into the pRTVC vector to generate pRTVC-OsPIP2;1-RFP (for primer sequences, see Supplemental Data 1). OsCNGC7-GFP was transiently co-expressed with OsPIP2;1-RFP in rice protoplasts by PEG-mediated transfection as described previously (Guo et al., 2006; Zhang et al., 2011). In brief, leaves from 2-week-old rice seedlings were cut into 0.5–1 mm strips and digested in enzyme solution (1.5% cellulase R10, 0.75% macerozyme R10, 0.6 M mannitol, 10 mM MES [pH 5.7], 10 mM CaCl2, 0.1% BSA) in the dark for 4–5 h. After filtration through a 200-mesh sieve and centrifugation at 800 rpm for 3 min, protoplasts were washed twice with W5 solution (154 mM NaCl, 125 mM CaCl2, 5 mM KCl, 2 mM MES [pH 5.7]) and adjusted to 2×105 cells/ml. A mixture of 10 μg pRTVC-OsCNGC7-GFP and 10 μg pRTVC-OsPIP2;1-RFP plasmids was introduced into 100 μl of protoplasts by PEG-mediated transfection. After incubation in the dark at 25°C for 8–12 h, fluorescence signals were visualized using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope with excitation/emission settings of 488/500–530 nm for GFP and 561/580–630 nm for RFP.

Split-ubiquitin membrane yeast two-hybrid library screening

The membrane yeast two-hybrid library screening was performed using the DUALhunter system (Dualsystems Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, the bait plasmid pBT3-N-OsCNGC7 carrying the full-length CDS of OsCNGC7 was transformed into yeast strain NMY51, which harbors a rice cDNA library in the prey plasmid pPR3-N. Transformants were plated on quadruple dropout medium (SD/−Trp/−Leu/−His/−Ade) and incubated at 28°C for 3–7 days. Positive colonies were isolated, and library plasmids were rescued by transformation into E. coli DH5α, followed by selection on plates supplemented with kanamycin. Insert sizes were confirmed by DNA sequencing.

To confirm interactions, pBT3-N-OsCNGC7 was co-transformed with pPR3-N-OsCPK4 into yeast, together with controls. The yeast was then plated on synthetic defined medium (SD/−Ade/−His/−Leu/−Trp), and growth was assessed after 3 days at 30°C. All experiments were repeated at least three times. Primers used are listed in Supplemental Data 1.

BiFC assay

BiFC assays were performed according to previously established protocols (Kim et al., 2008). In brief, the CDSs of OsCNGC7 and OsRLCK176 were individually inserted into the pFGC-cYFP vector to generate cYFP fusion constructs, and the CDS of OsCPK4 was cloned into the pFGC-nYFP vector to generate an nYFP fusion construct. The resulting constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. Agrobacterium cultures harboring the indicated plasmids were adjusted to an OD₆₀₀ of 0.8–1.0 and then mixed 1:1 with 150 μM acetosyringone, incubated for 3 h at room temperature, and co-infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. After 48–72 h of incubation at 22–25°C, YFP fluorescence was visualized using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope with the following parameters: zoom 4.86, HyD detector gain 100 for fluorescence signals, PMT transmission gain 180 for brightfield, frame average 1, line average 1, GFP channel (excitation 488 nm, emission 542 nm), and RFP channel (excitation 552 nm, emission 628 nm). Primers used for vector construction are listed in Supplemental Data 1.

SLC assay

The SLC assay was performed using the JW772 (cLUC) and JW771 (nLUC) vectors, following a protocol similar to that described previously (Gou et al., 2011). In brief, the full-length CDSs of OsCNGC7, OsSOS1, and OsSOS2 were separately cloned into the JW772 vector, and the full-length CDSs of OsCPK4, OsCPK11, OsCPK13, OsCPK18, OsCPK21, and OsCPK24 were cloned into the JW771 vector (for primer sequences, see Supplemental Data 1). After agroinfiltration and 48–72 h of transient expression, 1 mM luciferin potassium salt solution was evenly applied to the infiltrated leaves, and the leaves were placed in the dark for 5 min. Luminescence signals were then captured using a Uvitec Alliance Q9 CCD imaging system.

coIP assay

coIP assays were performed as described previously (Sun et al., 2024), with minor modifications. In brief, FLAG-tagged OsCPK4 and GFP-tagged OsCNGC7-C or the empty GFP vector were transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaves by Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. After 48 h, the leaves were pretreated with 50 μM MG132 and then inoculated with 100 mM NaCl or ddH2O (as a control) for 1 h before sampling. Proteins were extracted, and the protein mixture (1 ml) was incubated with 100 μl anti-GFP antibody-coupled agarose beads (Abmart) for 3 h at 4°C. The beads were washed five times with PBS buffer (containing 0.1% NP40), and the immunoprecipitated proteins were detected using anti-FLAG antibody (1:2500; Abmart, M20008) and anti-GFP antibody (1:2500; Abmart, M20004).

To examine the salt-induced interaction between OsCPK4 and OsCNGC7 in rice protoplasts, OsCPK4-3×FLAG was transiently expressed in protoplasts from OsCNGC7-overexpressing plants. After treatment with or without NaCl for 1 h, proteins were extracted and used for coIP assays. The immunoprecipitated proteins were analyzed by immunoblotting to assess the interaction between OsCPK4 and OsCNGC7.

In vitro pull-down assay

The full-length CDSs of GST-OsCNGC7-C, GST-OsCNGC7-C4A, and GST-OsCNGC7-C4D were individually cloned into pGEX-4T-1, and the resulting constructs were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) to produce GST-tagged recombinant proteins. The CDS of OsCPK4 was cloned into pET32a, and the resulting construct was transformed into BL21 to produce His-tagged recombinant proteins. The GST pull-down assay was performed as described previously (Wu et al., 2022). In brief, purified GST-tagged proteins were incubated with glutathione agarose resin (Yeasen, 60342ES) in pull-down buffer (1× PBS [pH 7.4], 0.1% [v/v] NP-40, 1 mM DTT) at 4°C for 1 h, then washed 5 times with the same buffer (5 min each) and incubated with His-OsCPK4 protein for 2 h at 4°C. After final washes, immunoblotting was performed using anti-GST (Proteintech, 66001-2-Ig) and anti-His (Proteintech, 66005-1-Ig) antibodies. Primers used for vector construction are listed in Supplemental Data 1.

In vitro phosphorylation assay

The in vitro phosphorylation assay was performed as described previously (Wu et al., 2022). In brief, 10 μg of GST-OsCNGC7-C, GST-OsCNGC7-CS599A, GST-OsCNGC7-CS634A, GST-OsCNGC7-CS658A, GST-OsCNGC7-CS682A, GST-OsCNGC7-CS599A/S634A, GST-OsCNGC7-CS658A/S682A, GST-OsCNGC7-CS599A/S634A/S658A, GST-OsCNGC7-CS599A/S658A/S682A, or GST-OsCNGC7-C4A was incubated with 2 μg of His-OsCPK4 in 20 μl of kinase reaction buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 50 μM ATP) at 30°C for 30 min. Reactions were terminated by adding 5× SDS loading buffer, boiled at 95°C for 5 min, and separated by 10% SDS–PAGE. Phosphorylated proteins were detected using Phos-tag biotin (APExBIO Technology, F4001), with an equal amount of protein stained by Coomassie brilliant blue (CBB) as a loading control.

For the Ca2+-dependent kinase assay, a series of defined Ca2+ concentrations was established by adding 5 mM ethylene glycol tetraacetic acid (EGTA) to various concentrations of CaCl2: 5 mM EGTA alone for 0 nM, 5 mM EGTA + 1.306 mM CaCl2 for 20 nM, 5 mM EGTA + 3 mM CaCl2 for 100 nM, 5 mM EGTA + 3.55 mM CaCl2 for 200 nM, 5 mM EGTA + 4.28 mM CaCl2 for 500 nM, 5 mM EGTA + 4.40 mM CaCl2 for 750 nM, 5 mM EGTA + 4.61 mM CaCl2 for 1000 nM, 5 mM EGTA + 4.865 mM CaCl2 for 3000 nM, 5 mM EGTA + 4.921 mM CaCl2 for 5000 nM, 5 mM EGTA + 4.967 mM CaCl2 for 10 000 nM, and 5 mM EGTA + 5.095 mM CaCl2 for 100 000 nM. The exact amounts of EGTA and CaCl2 were calculated using Ca-Mg-ATP-EGTA Calculator v1.0 (https://somapp.ucdmc.ucdavis.edu/pharmacology/bers/maxchelator/CaMgATPEGTA-NIST.htm) (Hu et al., 2024).

Assays for protein stability and phosphorylation in vivo

Protein stability and phosphorylation in vivo were assessed as described previously (Meng et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2022). In brief, OsCNGC7-GFP, OsCNGC7 (OsCNGC74A, OsCNGC74D)-3×FLAG, and OsCPK4-3×FLAG transgenic plants were treated with 100 mM NaCl for the specified durations. Total proteins were extracted using a buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 10 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), 0.5 M sucrose, 1 mM MgCl2, and 5 mM DTT. Immunoprecipitation was carried out using anti-GFP (Abmart, M20004) or anti-FLAG (Abmart, M20008) antibodies. Protein abundance was detected using the same antibodies, and phosphorylated proteins were detected using Phos-tag biotin (APExBIO Technology, F4001). HSP70 served as a loading control.

Phosphorylation and protein stability assays in rice protoplasts

Phosphorylation and protein stability assays in protoplasts were performed as described previously (Wu et al., 2022). In brief, rice protoplasts were transfected with the indicated plasmids and exposed to salt stress in the presence or absence of CHX. Total proteins were extracted using the protein extraction buffer described above. Immunoprecipitation was performed using an anti-GFP antibody (Abmart, M20004). Protein abundance was detected using an anti-GFP antibody, and phosphorylated proteins were detected using Phos-tag biotin (APExBIO Technology, F4001). HSP70 served as a loading control.

Cell-free degradation assay

Cell-free degradation assays were performed as described previously (Xie et al., 2024). In brief, total proteins were extracted from 2-week-old rice seedlings using the protein extraction buffer described above. The protein extracts were incubated with GST-OsCNGC7-C, GST-OsCNGC7-C4A, or GST-OsCNGC7-C4D recombinant proteins at 30°C for various durations in the presence of 10 mM ATP, with or without 50 μM MG132. After incubation, the samples were separated by SDS–PAGE and immunoblotted using an anti-GST antibody (Proteintech, 66001-2-Ig) to detect the recombinant proteins.

Na+ and Ca2+ flux measurements by NMT

Intracellular Na+ efflux was monitored using NMT as described previously (Lou et al., 2020a), with minor modifications. Three-day-old seedlings were exposed to 100 mM NaCl for 24 h, and Na+ efflux in the primary root meristem zone (approximately 20 μm from the root tip) was measured by NMT. First, a pre-pulled and salinized microelectrode (4.5 ± 0.5 μm in diameter) was prepared and injected with backfill solution (250 mM NaCl) to a length of 1.0 cm; the tip of the microelectrode was then filled with 50–60 μm of liquid ion-exchange cocktail (XY-SJ-Na type). The microelectrode was calibrated in calibration solution (0.1 mM CaCl2, 0.1 mM KCl, and 0.3 mM MES [pH 6.0], with 0.5 mM or 5.0 mM NaCl). Roots were incubated in measurement solution (calibration solution with 0.5 mM NaCl) for 10 min, and net Na+ flux was measured. Calculations were performed using JCal v3.3 software (Younger, USA).

Extracellular Ca2+ influx was monitored using NMT as described previously (Yu et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2024). In brief, seeds were germinated on filter paper saturated with a test solution (0.1 mM KCl, 0.1 mM CaCl2, 0.1 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM NaCl, 0.3 mM MES, and 0.2 mM Na2SO4; pH 6.0) for 3 days, then transferred into the same test solution and allowed to equilibrate for 10 min to reach a physiological steady state. Ca2+ fluxes in rice root cells were recorded continuously for 3 min. Next, 100 mM NaCl was rapidly added to the solution surrounding the roots, and Ca2+ fluxes were immediately monitored for at least another 3 min to capture the dynamic changes in response to salt stimulation.

All measurements were performed under the same conditions, and each treatment was replicated three times.

Measurement of [Ca2+]cyt in rice protoplasts using GCaMP3

Rice protoplasts were prepared as described above. To measure [Ca2+]cyt on the basis of GCaMP3 fluorescence (Maintz et al., 2014), GCaMP3 was transiently expressed in rice protoplasts by PEG-mediated transformation as described above, and protoplasts were adjusted to 2.5 × 105 cells/ml. Fluorescence signals were detected using a Hitachi F-4500 fluorescence spectrophotometer (excitation: 488 nm, emission: 507 nm). In brief, the protoplast suspension was added to a quartz cuvette (Sangon Biotech, No. F511019), and fluorescence was captured for 3 min (F0). Then, 100 mM NaCl was rapidly added, and signals were continuously captured for another 6 min (F). Relative fluorescence intensity ΔF/F0 = (F − F0)/F0 was calculated, and dynamic curves of [Ca2+]cyt were plotted. Three independent experiments were performed.

Cytosolic Ca2+ imaging assays in HEK293T cells

The Ca2+ imaging assays in HEK293T cells were performed as described previously (Wang et al., 2021). In brief, the full-length CDSs of OsCNGC7 and OsCNGC9 were cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector to generate pcDNA3.1-OsCNGC7 and pcDNA3.1-OsCNGC9, respectively. HEK293T cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. Cells were seeded onto glass-bottom dishes and transfected with pcDNA3.1-OsCNGC7, pcDNA3.1-OsCNGC9, or the empty pcDNA3.1 vector when they reached 60%–80% confluence. After transfection, cells were cultured for 24–48 h. Before imaging, cells were washed with Ca2+-free test buffer (130 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 0.6 mM MgCl2, 1.2 mM NaHCO3, 10 mM glucose, 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.2) and then loaded with 2 μM Fluo-8 AM at 37°C in the dark for 30 min. The cells were washed with the Ca2+-free test buffer again for complete de-esterification of the dye.

Fluorescence signals were acquired every 3 s using a 3D Cell Culture live-cell imaging system (Leica Thunder), with cells maintained at 37°C during imaging. Basal fluorescence was first recorded in Ca2+-free test buffer; CaCl2 was then added to a final concentration of 3 mM, and Fluo-8 fluorescence was recorded continuously. Images were analyzed using CellProfiler. The average fluorescence intensity during the stable baseline period before CaCl2 stimulation was defined as F0, and the fluorescence intensity at each time point was defined as F. The relative fluorescence change was calculated as ΔF/F0 = (F − F0)/F0. Cells transfected with the empty pcDNA3.1 vector were used as the negative control.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay

A sensor chip was prepared by immobilizing OsCPK4 onto a CM5 chip (Biosensor AB, Uppsala, Sweden) according to the standard amine-coupling protocol. The SPR analysis was performed as described previously (Pan et al., 2019). In brief, the CM5 chip with immobilized OsCPK4 was mounted onto a BIAcore X100 system (GE Healthcare, USA). Analytes were injected into the channel at a flow rate of 10 μl/min in different buffers (containing Ca2+ or EGTA) prior to recording the response curves. Sensorgrams were analyzed using BIAevaluation software.

Trypan blue staining

Trypan blue staining was performed as described previously (Xie et al., 2024). Rice leaf samples were completely immersed in Trypan blue solution at room temperature for 12 h, then washed three times with PBS to remove excess stain and immersed in 95% ethanol for at least 12 h. The destained leaves were photographed to record the distribution of dead cells.

TEVC assay in Xenopus laevis oocytes

TEVC recordings in Xenopus laevis oocytes were obtained as described previously (Peng et al., 2024). In brief, the CDS of the target gene fused with GFP or RFP was cloned into the pGEM-HE vector. The resulting construct was linearized for in vitro transcription of cRNA using the T7 In Vitro Transcription Kit (ZOMANBIO, ZT602), and the purity and concentration of the cRNA were determined. Mature Xenopus eggs were rinsed with Ca2+-free ND96 solution (96 mM NaCl, 2 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2·6H2O, 100 μg/ml streptomycin sulfate, 5 mM HEPES–Tris [pH 7.5]) until the suspension was clear, then transferred to the same buffer supplemented with 1 mg/ml collagenase for digestion until complete dissociation. Stage V/VI oocytes with distinct black-and-white pigmentation and intact morphology were selected for subsequent experiments. Oocytes were injected with 50 ng of cRNA or DEPC water (negative control) using a microinjector, then incubated in ND96 solution supplemented with 1.8 mM CaCl2 at 18°C for 48 h.

Protein expression in oocytes was detected using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope. GFP was excited at 488 nm (30% laser intensity), with emission collected at 530–575 nm (gain 30 V); RFP was excited at 552 nm (40% intensity), with emission collected at 592–618 nm (gain 30 V). Voltage-gated currents were recorded using an Axon Axoclamp 900A microelectrode amplifier and a Digidata 1322A analog–digital converter, then recorded using pClamp 10.6 software. The bath solution, which contained 30 mM BaCl2 (or 30 mM CaCl2, with or without 500 μM DIDS) and 5 mM MES–Tris (pH 5.6), was adjusted to 220 mOsm/l with mannitol. The pipette solution was 3 M KCl. Channel currents were recorded with hyperpolarized pulses consisting of a 0.1-s prepulse at −30 mV, followed by voltage steps from +20 to −160 mV in −10-mV decrements (2-s duration), and a 2-s deactivation at −30 mV, with cell currents recorded synchronously. Data were analyzed using Clampfit 10.6 and Origin 2024 software to fit the I–V relationship. TEVC assays were repeated with three independent batches of oocytes.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.0 was used for statistical analysis. Student’s t-test was used to assess the significance of differences between two groups (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001; ns, not significant). One-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test was used for pairwise comparisons, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All data underlying the statistical analyses are provided in Supplemental Data 2.
