Single-cell and molecular insights into the immunopathogenic mechanisms of oral mucous membrane pemphigoid
Authors: Wenjing Kuang, Hao Cui, Qionghua Li, Shumin Duan, Dan Liu, Tiannan Liu, Jiongke Wang, Wei Li, Qianming Chen, Jing Li, Xin Zeng, Taiwen Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMucous membrane pemphigoid (MMP) is a chronic autoimmune bullous disorder, characterized pathologically by subepidermal blistering inflammation primarily affecting mucosal surfaces. The immunopathogenesis of MMP involves a complex interplay between cytokines, epithelial components, and immune cells, yet the specific cell subsets driving disease progression, especially in oral mucosal lesions, remain poorly defined. To address this gap in knowledge, we performed integrated single-cell transcriptomic and immune repertoire profiling of paired oral mucosal lesions and peripheral blood samples from MMP patients prior to therapeutic intervention. Our analyses uncover a multifaceted pathogenic signature, including aberrant WNT4 signaling, dysfunctional Th17 cell responses, TNF-α-producing macrophages, expanded T cell clones enriched in the TRBV20–1 gene segment, and dysregulated fibroblast subsets. Collectively, these findings provide new insights into the key immunopathogenic mechanisms underlying MMP and establish a molecular framework for the development of targeted immunotherapies for this condition.Keywords: Mucous membrane pemphigoid, Autoimmune blistering disorder, WNT pathway, T cell receptor, Single-cell transcriptome sequencing

Introduction

Mucous membrane pemphigoid (MMP) is a rare, chronic autoimmune blistering disorder characterized by inflammation and subepithelial blister formation predominantly affecting mucosal surfaces. The documented annual incidence of MMP falls within the range of 13–20 cases per million inhabitants [1]. Among the various mucosal sites, the oral cavity is the most frequently involved and often represents the initial site of disease manifestation. Clinically, MMP is categorized into “low-risk” and “high-risk” subtypes based on the anatomical distribution of lesions. Low-risk MMP refers to cases in which lesions are confined to the oral mucosa, with or without concurrent skin involvement, whereas high-risk MMP is characterized by lesions involving critical mucosal sites such as the ocular, genital, nasopharyngeal, esophageal, or laryngeal epithelium [2]. Although low-risk MMP generally lacks the scarring and severe complications seen in the high-risk form, its recurrent episodes of erythema, blistering, and mucosal erosion present a chronic burden, leading to significant impairment in oral function and overall quality of life. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the molecular and cellular mechanisms driving low-risk MMP is essential, as these aspects remain poorly characterized.

The pathogenesis of MMP remains incompletely understood. Current evidence suggests that the formation of subepithelial blisters primarily results from the deposition of circulating autoantibodies targeting the basement membrane zone (BMZ) of the skin and mucosa. Identified autoantigens include BP180 (collagen XVII), laminin 332 (formerly laminin 5), BP230, type VII collagen, and integrin α6β4. These pathogenic autoantibodies are predominantly IgG and complement C3, although a subset of cases exhibits IgA or IgM [3]. Previous study has indicated that microbiota residing in local lesions may contribute to the pathogenesis of the disease through mechanisms such as molecular mimicry or augmented antigen exposure [4]. Advancements in microbiome research methods will help further elucidate the microbial mechanisms underlying MMP pathogenesis. [5], [6], [7]. Moreover, accumulating data suggest that various immune cells and cytokine networks contribute significantly to disease pathogenesis [8]. For example, regulatory T cells (Tregs) have been demonstrated to modulate the myeloid cell-mediated inflammatory in pemphigoid diseases [9]. Furthermore, in comparison to healthy controls, lesional tissues from MMP patients exhibit increased infiltration of pro-inflammatory and fibrogenic cytokines such as interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), interleukin-4 (IL-4), and transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1), all of which have been implicated in promoting tissue damage and scarring [10], [11], [12], [13]. Notably, the majority of these findings are derived from peripheral blood (PB) samples or murine models, with limited investigation of the immune landscape within human lesional tissues. Analysis of immune cell composition and gene expression profiles in lesional sites-particularly oral mucosal lesions, which are frequently involved and readily accessible for sampling, may yield critical insights into the immunopathogenic mechanisms underlying low-risk MMP.

Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have enabled unprecedented resolution in deciphering cellular heterogeneity and intercellular communication in various oral mucosa [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21] and skin disorders [22], [23], [24], [25]. For example, Williams et al. [16] constructed a single-cell atlas of oral mucosa, and demonstrated that activated stromal cells in periodontitis recruit immune cells, particularly neutrophils, to drive inflammation. Wu et al. [25] employed scRNA-seq to analyze immune changes in psoriatic skin before and after IL-23 blockade, revealing that clinical responders exhibited reduced psoriasis-related signatures in skin-resident memory T cells, while non-responders showed persistent Th17 activation. Similarly, Zheng et al. [24] applied scRNA-seq to skin samples from patients with discoid and cutaneous lupus erythematosus, identifying enhanced intercellular communication among fibroblasts, macrophages, and dendritic cells within lesional sites.

Building upon these advances, we employed an integrated approach using scRNA-seq in combination with T cell receptor (TCR) sequencing. This strategy enabled high-resolution characterization of cellular phenotypes and intercellular interactions within the MMP lesional microenvironment. Our analysis revealed distinct immune activation signatures and clonal expansion patterns of lymphocytes, offering novel insights into the immunopathogenic landscape of MMP. Collectively, these results advance current understanding of the immune microenvironment driving disease progression and provide a valuable framework for the development of targeted immunotherapeutic strategies in low-risk MMP.

Results

Single-cell profiling and unbiased clustering of cells in buccal tissues of MMP samples

We isolated single cells from buccal mucosal tissues (n = 6) and peripheral blood (PB, n = 4) obtained from patients with low-risk MMP and healthy volunteers (Healthy) for scRNA-seq and TCR sequencing (Fig. 1A). For two MMP patients, both buccal mucosa and PB samples were collected simultaneously, whereas for the another two patients, only buccal mucosa tissues were obtained. Matched buccal and PB samples from healthy volunteers were obtained during jaw corrective surgery. In total, we included four buccal mucosal samples from MMP patients, two from healthy volunteers (n = 6), and PB samples from two MMP patients and two healthy volunteers (n = 4) for the sequencing analysis. The clinical features, representative clinical manifestations, pathological hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining, and direct immunofluorescence (DIF) results are summarized in Table S1 and Fig. 1B.

We initially performed scRNA-seq on all viable cells isolated from six buccal mucosal tissue samples (Figure S1A). After batch effect correction using the “RunHarmony” function within the Harmony package, a total of 62,014 cells were successfully integrated across all samples, comprising 11,423 cells from healthy tissues and 50,591 cells from MMP tissues. Following integration, cells from all six buccal mucosal samples clustered cohesively, indicating effective batch correction (Figure S1B). Unsupervised clustering and marker gene expression analyses identified ten distinct cell subtypes (Fig. 1C), which were further categorized into two major groups: immune and non-immune cells (Fig. 1D,E; Figure S2). The immune compartment consisted primarily of T/NK cells (69 %), followed by myeloid cells (22 %), plasma cells (5 %), and B cells (4 %) (Fig. 1F). The non-immune compartment included epithelial cells (58 %), fibroblasts (23 %), myofibroblasts (9 %), endothelial cells (8 %), myocytes (2 %), and melanocytes (1 %), with epithelial cells representing the predominant population (Fig. 1G). In addition to differences in cellular composition, differential expression analysis revealed that MMP tissues displayed distinct transcriptional signatures compared with healthy controls, characterized by the upregulation of multiple inflammation-related genes, including CCL5, TNF, and IL1B (Fig. 1H). These results highlight a robust inflammatory microenvironment in MMP lesions, consistent with the disease’s autoimmune and immune-mediated pathology.

Activation of the WNT pathway in MMP samples

MMP is characterized by the production of autoantibodies targeting epithelial-subepithelial junctional proteins, leading to the formation of subepithelial blisters [2]. Given this pathogenic feature, the epithelial compartment in MMP lesions was a major focus of our investigation. Clinically, both pemphigus vulgaris (PV) and MMP can present with erythematous and erosive oral lesions, which often complicates differential diagnosis based solely on clinical appearance. Therefore, we sought to determine whether distinct transcriptional signatures exist among epithelial subsets in MMP and PV, with the aim of identifying molecular features that may facilitate more accurate disease differentiation.

Epithelial cells were extracted from the single-cell atlas, along with epithelial subsets from two previously generated PV samples obtained concurrently by our group, followed by reclustering analysis (Figure S3A). After correction for batch effects (Figure S3B), five distinct epithelial clusters were identified, designated C0-C4 (Fig. 2A). These subpopulations were annotated based on established epithelial marker genes [26], [27]. Cluster C0 exhibited high expression of the COL17A1 gene, which encodes type XVII collagen (BP180/BPAG2)—a transmembrane component of the basement membrane zone critical for epithelial–basement membrane adhesion—and was therefore classified as the Basal cell subset. Cluster C1 expresses early differentiation markers such as KRT1 and KRT10 (Fig. 2A-B), corresponding to the Spinous layer, whereas cluster C2 showed strong expression of proliferation markers TOP2A and MKI67, and was enriched in DNA replication-related pathways such as “Cell Cycle DNA Replication Initiation” (Fig. 2C), defining it as the Proliferating subset. Cluster C3 expressed late differentiation markers IVL and TGM3, representing the Granular layer. Cluster C4, in contrast, did not align with previously characterized epithelial subsets but exhibited specific expression of CCL2 and enrichment in immune-related pathways (Fig. 2B-C). Accordingly, this cluster was designated CCL2⁺ epithelial cells (CCL2⁺Epi), which may participate in immune modulation, though its functional role requires further investigation (Fig. 2C-D).

Quantitative analysis of epithelial subcluster proportions revealed a reduced percentage of Basal cells in MMP compared to both PV and healthy controls (Fig. 2E), consistent with the pathological manifestation of basement membrane disruption in MMP. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of upregulated genes in MMP identified activation of the WNT signaling pathway within the Basal subset (Fig. 2G). Further examination showed that WNT4 expression was markedly elevated in MMP samples compared with PV and healthy groups (Fig. 2F). This observation was further supported by IHC validation, which confirmed increased WNT4 protein expression in MMP epithelial tissues (Fig. 2H-I).

Quantitative analysis revealed a moderate increase in the Th17 cell proportion in MMP samples (Fig. 3C), consistent with previous reports highlighting Th17 involvement in pemphigoid pathogenesis [28]. Furthermore, PDCD1 (encoding PD-1) expression was significantly downregulated in Th17 cells from MMP compared with controls (Fig. 3F), suggesting reduced inhibitory signaling and potential hyperactivation of these cells.

To assess the migratory potential of T/NK subsets, we analyzed the “leukocyte migration” gene set (GO:0050900) in both MMP and healthy tissues. Each T/NK subset in MMP exhibited enhanced leukocyte migration signatures relative to corresponding subsets in healthy controls (Fig. 3E), indicating globally elevated immune cell trafficking activity within the MMP microenvironment.

Cell-cell communication analysis further demonstrated that interactions between Basal epithelial cells and T cell subtypes were enriched for several immune-inflammatory regulatory pathways, particularly MIF-(CD74 +CXCR4) and MIF-(CD74 +CD44) signaling axes (Fig. 3G). The expansion of these basal cell–T cell interactions may represent a key pathogenic mechanism driving local immune activation and tissue injury in MMP. Moreover, transcription factor analysis revealed significant upregulation of pathways associated with myeloid cell differentiation, T cell differentiation, leukocyte activation, and Th17 cell differentiation in MMP samples (Fig. 3H). Notably, STAT6 and NFKB1, two central regulators of inflammatory signaling, were markedly elevated (Fig. 3I-J), further underscoring the enhanced proinflammatory transcriptional activity of T cells in the MMP milieu.

Subpopulations of myeloid cells exhibit pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic activity in MMP

Myeloid cells were further subdivided into monocytes, macrophages, conventional dendritic cells (cDCs), and mitotic cells based on canonical marker expression (Fig. 4A-4D). The proportions of these subpopulations are presented in Fig. 4E. Previous studies have demonstrated that myeloid cells play a critical role in the pathogenesis of organ fibrosis [29]. Given that patients with MMP often exhibit varying degrees of fibrosis and scarring, we sought to determine whether specific myeloid cell subsets contribute to pro-fibrotic processes in oral MMP lesions.

To elucidate intercellular communication between myeloid cells and fibroblasts, we applied CellChat analysis. Comparison of signaling pathway information flow between healthy oral mucosa and MMP tissues revealed a predominant activation of the TNF signaling pathway in MMP (Fig. 4F). Further analysis of TNF, ICAM, and MIF signaling networks demonstrated markedly enhanced crosstalk between macrophages and fibroblasts as well as between cDCs and fibroblasts in MMP compared with healthy tissues (Figure S4A-C). Subsequent examination of ligand–receptor interactions between myeloid subsets and fibroblasts (Fig. 4G) identified several upregulated pairs in MMP tissues, including MIF-ACKR3 (Fig. 4H), TNF-TNFRSF1A (Fig. 4I), and ITGB2-ICAM1 (Fig. 4J). These enhanced interactions suggest that myeloid cells may contribute to both pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic signaling in the buccal mucosa of MMP patients, potentially promoting fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix remodeling.

Disease-specific V-J pairing and clonal expansion patterns in MMP

After data filtering, we obtained 45,133 cells from four peripheral blood samples. Based on canonical marker gene expression, these cells were categorized into five major subgroups: T/NK cells, B cells, plasma cells, myeloid cells, and platelets (Figure S5A). Reclustering of the T/NK cell population revealed twelve transcriptionally distinct subclusters (Figure S5B).

To systematically characterize T cell clonal dynamics and V(D)J recombination patterns across different sample sources, we merged Seurat objects containing T cells from four peripheral blood and six buccal mucosal tissue samples (Fig. 5A), and jointly analyzed TCR sequences with scRNA-seq data. Clonotype analysis revealed that expanded clonotypes were predominantly localized within effector memory T cell (Temra) subsets—consistent with their expected high clonal activity—and were not uniquely associated with MMP (Fig. 5B). Distinct clonal expansion patterns were observed between peripheral blood and tissue-derived T cells, with specific distribution profiles summarized in Figure S5C−F.

Cross-individual comparison of CDR3 sequence characteristics indicated a trend toward greater CDR3 sharing among MMP samples compared with healthy controls (Fig. 5C). However, given the limited sample size and interindividual variability in clonal counts, this observation should be considered preliminary. Interestingly, we also detected a notable number of shared CDR3 clonotypes between the MMP01 and Healthy03/04 samples. To further investigate the origin of these shared clones, we exported the clonotype list (Supplementary data1) and compared them with reference sequences in the most recent TCR database [30]. The results suggest that this overlap may be related to COVID-19 infection or vaccination, a hypothesis that warrants validation in larger cohorts.

Notably, several TCRα CDR3 sequences—CAASNDYKLSF, CAATGGGNKLTF, CAESFTGGGNKLTF, CALSDPGNQFYF, CAVAPYGNNRLAF, CAVEKAAGNKLTF, CAVGDYKLSF, CAVRGSGGSYIPTF and CAVSLNSGYSTLTF—were exclusively shared among MMP samples (Fig. 5D, Supplementary data 2). After aligning these sequences against the VDJdb database [31] revealed that none have been previously reported to be associated with known diseases, suggesting they may represent MMP-specific TCR clonotypes.

Analysis of V-J gene usage and pairing preferences further revealed distinct patterns between MMP and healthy controls. In MMP tissue samples, the top three preferentially used V-region genes of the α chain were TRAV21, TRAV13–1, and TRAV9–2, while the most frequently used J-region genes were TRAJ42, TRAJ40, and TRAJ49. The dominant V-J pair was TRAV8–3/TRAJ10 (Figures S5G, S6A-C). For the β chain, the most highly expressed V and J genes included TRBV20–1, TRBV28, TRBV5–1, TRBJ2–7, TRBJ2–1, and TRBJ2–3, with TRBV20–1/TRBJ2–7 representing the predominant pair (Fig. 5E, G).

In MMP PBMCs, the top α-chain V genes were TRAV26–1, TRAV1–2, and TRAV12–2, while the top J genes were TRAJ45, TRAJ33, and TRAJ31, with TRAV26–1/TRAJ45 being the most frequent V-J pair (Figures S5H, S6D-F). For the β chain, the most highly expressed V and J genes were TRBV20–1, TRBV5–1, TRBV19, TRBJ2–3, TRBJ2–1, and TRBJ2–7, with TRBV20–1/TRBJ2–3 as the dominant V-J combination (Fig. 5F, H).

Collectively, these findings reveal distinct TCR repertoire characteristics in MMP, including unique V–J pairing preferences and potential disease-associated clonotypes, underscoring the contribution of antigen-driven T cell responses to MMP pathogenesis.

Discussion

MMP is a rare autoimmune blistering disorder that primarily affects mucosal tissues. Although previous studies have elucidated the role of autoantibodies in disrupting epithelial-subepithelial adhesion [32], the broader immunopathogenic mechanisms of MMP remain insufficiently characterized. Most existing research has concentrated on ocular MMP—a high-risk subtype marked by fibrosis and scarring—often utilizing peripheral blood samples or murine models [28], [33], [34]. In contrast, low-risk MMP, which is typically confined to the oral mucosa and lacks severe fibrotic sequelae, has received comparatively limited molecular investigation.

Clinically, MMP shares features with pemphigus vulgaris (PV), including mucosal erythema, blistering, and erosions. However, the two diseases are distinguished by their pathophysiological mechanisms: MMP involves subepithelial blistering due to autoantibody deposition against BMZ antigens such as BP180, laminin 332, and type VII collagen [3], whereas PV is characterized by intraepithelial acantholysis mediated by anti-desmoglein (Dsg1/Dsg3) antibodies [35]. Our single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed fundamental differences in epithelial subpopulation composition and gene expression between these disorders, providing molecular insights that may facilitate differential diagnosis.

A major finding of this study is the activation of WNT signaling, particularly involving WNT4, in MMP lesions. The WNT pathway is known to participate in the regulation of immune homeostasis, inflammation, and fibrosis across several autoimmune diseases. For instance, Wnt7b and Wnt5a promote inflammatory cytokine production in rheumatoid arthritis [36], while aberrant WNT activation drives pathological bone formation in ankylosing spondylitis [37] and collagen deposition in systemic sclerosis [38]. Our data suggest that WNT4-mediated β-catenin activation in oral MMP may enhance pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and impair epithelial differentiation and BMZ integrity, thereby contributing to subepithelial blister formation. These findings position WNT4 as a potential pathogenic mediator and therapeutic target in MMP.

T cell dysregulation also emerged as a key pathogenic feature. We observed an elevated proportion of Th17 cells and concurrent downregulation of PDCD1 expression, consistent with a hyperactivated T cell state. Th17 cells have been implicated in autoimmune tissue injury through the release of IL-17, GM-CSF, and other cytokines [39], [40], [41], [42] Moreover, reports of PD-1 inhibitor-induced MMP further support a causal link between PD-1 dysregulation and disease onset [8], [43]. Our data also revealed enhanced interactions between epithelial basal cells and multiple T cell subsets, mediated by inflammatory signaling pathways, alongside increased expression of transcription factors such as STAT6 and NFKB1, which are central regulators of inflammatory gene expression.

At the myeloid cell level, our findings highlight macrophage- and dendritic cell–derived TNF-α signaling as a major contributor to the inflammatory and fibrotic milieu in oral MMP. TNF-α, a pro-inflammatory cytokine typically absent in healthy tissue, is markedly upregulated during autoimmune inflammation [44], [45], [46], [47]. It mediates leukocyte recruitment and vascular permeability via TNFR1/TNFR2 activation [48]. Prior work has demonstrated TNF-α–induced ICAM-1 upregulation on conjunctival fibroblasts, promoting leukocyte adhesion and fibrosis [49]. Consistent with these mechanisms, our single-cell communication analysis identified enriched TNF–TNFRSF1A and ITGB2–ICAM1 ligand–receptor pairs between myeloid cells and fibroblasts in MMP tissues. Clinically, anti-TNF therapy has shown promising outcomes in refractory MMP cases, including complete lesion resolution following infliximab treatment and sustained improvement with etanercept [50], [51]. Together, these findings underscore TNF-α as a central driver of MMP pathogenesis, integrating early immune activation and late fibrotic remodeling, and support TNF-targeted therapy as a rational treatment approach.

Analysis of TCR repertoires further revealed disease-specific immune features. The predominance of TRBV20–1 in both lesional and peripheral T cells suggests its potential involvement in autoreactive clonal expansion. TRBV20–1 has previously been associated with chronic inflammatory and age-related immune responses [52], [53], [54], and its enrichment in MMP lesions supports a model of antigen-driven T cell activation. Distinct V-J recombination patterns between peripheral and tissue-infiltrating T cells further suggest local antigenic selection or inflammation-driven clonal expansion. Notably, several TCRα CDR3 sequences (e.g., CAASNDYKLSF, CAVRGSGGSYIPTF) were shared exclusively among MMP samples and are not documented in current databases, raising the possibility that they represent MMP-specific clonotypes. These findings warrant further exploration, as identifying such public or disease-restricted clonotypes could provide a foundation for TCR-based diagnostic markers and precision immunotherapies in MMP.

Despite the novel insights provided by our study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the limited sample size may reduce statistical power and increase the likelihood of type I error in differential gene and clonotype analyses, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings. Second, the restricted number of samples may underestimate the diversity of low-frequency TCR clonotypes. Finally, the absence of validated in vitro or in vivo models for MMP precluded direct functional verification of the observed pathways. Future work incorporating larger, multicenter cohorts and mechanistic validation using patient-derived cell lines, animal models, or mucosal organoids will be essential to confirm and expand these findings.

In summary, this study provides the first integrated single-cell transcriptomic and TCR repertoire characterization of oral MMP. We reveal that WNT4 signaling activation, Th17 cell dysregulation, TNF-α–mediated macrophage-fibroblast interactions, and TRBV20–1-restricted clonal T cell expansion collectively shape the immunopathogenic landscape of MMP. Although the small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings, these results not only advance understanding of the cellular and molecular mechanisms driving oral MMP but also highlight potential therapeutic targets—including WNT signaling modulators, TNF-α inhibitors, and TCR-directed strategies. Continued investigation with larger cohorts and functional studies will be critical to translate these insights into targeted, precision-based therapies for patients with MMP.

Materials and methods

Recruitment for patients and acquisition of samples

The diagnoses of low-risk MMP were conﬁrmed with clinical manifestations, histological evaluation and direct immunofluorescence assay [55]. All enrolled patients presented with oral mucosa-limited lesions at initial diagnosis, and no extra-oral involvement (e.g., ocular, genital mucosal, or cutaneous lesions) was documented during a minimum follow-up duration of 12 months. Biopsy specimens from the buccal mucosa (n = 4) and PB samples (n = 2) were obtained from MMP patients without any treatment. The control group comprised healthy volunteers undergoing orthognathic surgery, from whom matched buccal mucosa (n = 2) and PB samples (n = 2) were acquired.

Single-cell suspension preparation

The protocols for single-cell suspension preparation and PBMCs isolation were consistent with established methodologies described in previous studies [20].

Library generation and high-throughput sequencing

The scRNA and TCR libraries were generated employing the Chromium Single Cell V(D)J platform (10X Genomics, v2 Chemistry) according to standardized protocols. High-throughput sequencing was performed via Illumina NovaSeq 6000, with subsequent GRCh38 genome alignment and feature quantification executed through CellRanger pipeline (version 6.1.1), yielding both transcriptional profiles and TCR clonotype datasets.

Quantification and normalization of single-cell data

For the analysis of single-cell gene expression data, the R package Seurat (version 4.4.1) was employed. To address ambient mRNA contamination, the package “SoupX” was utilized. Doublet prediction and filtration were performed using the “DoubletFinder” package. Data integration was performed by merging individual Seurat objects derived from the same sample type. During the quality control process, datasets were filtered based on specific criteria: mitochondrial gene transcript percentage (<20 %), the UMI count (>1000), and detected gene count (>300). Normalization of the filtered Seurat objects was carried out using the NormalizeData function within Seurat. Linear transformation of the data was achieved through the ScaleData function, ensuring standardized gene expression levels across cells. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to reduce dimensionality. Batch effects were mitigated using the Harmony package when required. Cell clusters were identified and visualized using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP).

Differential gene expression and functional enrichment analysis

In this study, differential gene expression analysis was performed using the FindMarkers function with default parameter settings. Subsequently, functional enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler package to further explore the results of the aforementioned differential expression analysis. With reference to the definitions of the Gene Ontology Resource, this study evaluated the enrichment of gene lists derived from the differential expression analysis in biological process (BP) terms.

Gene signature score

To evaluate the functional activity of gene signatures, we utilized the "AddModuleScore" function available in the Seurat package. Gene sets used for calculating gene signatures in the T/NK subset were retrieved from the MsigDB database (GO:0050900).

Analysis of intercellular communication

The CellChat package was used to analyze and visualize interactions between cell types, using its default parameters for the investigation.

Analysis of TCR clonotypes

The R package scRepertoire was utilized for the analysis of TCR clonotypes. TCR clonotypes were identified by combining the VDJ genes of the TCR α and β paired chains with the nucleotide sequences of their CDR3 regions. The UpSetR package was utilized to generate the UpSet plot.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed following established protocols from prior studies(). Tissue slides were imaged using a high-resolution scanner (Aperio VERSA, Leica). The following antibody was used:WNT4A(proteintech;14371–1-AP;1:300).

Statistical analysis

We used software R (version 4.4.1) to conduct all statistical analyses. The detailed descriptions of statistical methods can be found in the figure captions.

Study approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan University West China Hospital of Stomatology (WCHSIRB-D-2020–331). Written informed consent was obtained from each patient.
