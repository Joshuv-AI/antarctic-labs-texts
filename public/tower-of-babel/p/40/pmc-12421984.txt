Patient-derived medullary thyroid cancer organoids: a potential model for mechanistic studies on diagnostics and therapy
Authors: Eline C Jager, Luc H J Sondorp, Rufina Maturi, Inês F Antunes, Bettien M van Hemel, Uilke Brouwer, Liesbeth Jansen, Wouter T Zandee, Adrienne H Brouwers, Thera P Links, Rob P Coppes, Schelto Kruijff
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveMedullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) is a rare neuroendocrine thyroid tumor, with only 30 new patients annually in the Netherlands. PET imaging provides information on distant metastases, after which tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) may be initiated. The rarity of the disease impedes large controlled trials, and therefore the challenge of selecting the best TKI and PET tracer for individual patients persists. To explore whether an in vitro model could be developed to guide the selection of appropriate PET tracers or TKI therapies in the future, we aimed to establish an MTC organoid model for the first time.MethodsDispersed cells from MTC biopsies were suspended in Matrigel, allowing organoid formation. The self-renewal potential was tested by dissociation and re-plating cells and determining organoid-forming efficiency. MTC-specific gene and protein expression were characterized by qPCR and immunofluorescent staining. Moreover, MTC-organoids (MTOs) were exposed to TKIs and PET tracers in proof-of-principle experiments.ResultsTen MTC biopsies were processed and successfully cultured as MTOs. MTC-derived cells showed self-renewal potency for several passages, indicating the presence of putative stem cells. Gene and protein expression of MTC-specific markers in tissue and MTOs, and function measurements showed the production of calcitonin and CEA. Interpretation of the preliminary experiments with TKIs and PET tracers was limited by sample size but demonstrates their future potential.ConclusionWe were able to culture MTC organoids that resemble the original tissue in gene expression, protein expression, and functionality. However, international, multi-center studies are required to meet the standards for future clinical applications.

Introduction

Medullary thyroid cancer (MTC) is a type of thyroid cancer derived from the parafollicular C-cells, located between the thyroid hormone-producing follicles of the thyroid gland (1). MTC is a rare neuroendocrine tumor that constitutes only 5% of thyroid cancers and occurs sporadically (65–75%) or hereditarily (25–35%) (2, 3, 4).

Genetic mutations in the Rearranged during Transfection (RET) gene play a vital role in the development of all hereditary MTCs and 65–100% of sporadically occurring MTCs (5, 6, 7, 8). RET encodes a tyrosine kinase that activates numerous intracellular proteins through phosphorylation. However, mutations in RET can cause tyrosine kinase phosphorylation with enhanced signal transduction of downstream pathways PI3K-AKT and RAS-MAPK and cancer cell proliferation (9, 10).

Once MTC has become clinically apparent, most patients undergo a total thyroidectomy and lymph node dissection. The extent of the primary lymph node dissections depends on preoperative serum tumor markers (calcitonin and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA)) and the presence of lymph node metastases on preoperative imaging (11).

Advances in molecular imaging techniques have improved staging of MTC patients. 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) and 18F-dihydroxyphenylalanine (18F-DOPA) PET are used in clinical practice to assess the extent of disease. Whereas 18F-FDG has a patient-based sensitivity of 17–62% in MTC patients, 18F-DOPA PET has a slightly higher sensitivity of 44–95% for MTC and allows better detection of indolently growing MTCs (12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19). However, neither PET tracer reliably detects all MTC lesions in each patient. It is challenging to perform large prospective studies with such a rare disease, making it difficult to determine the best PET tracer for each patient. Therefore, establishing preclinical models to screen, test, and validate (new) tracers is an unmet need.

Furthermore, when extensive distant metastatic disease is apparent on imaging, systemic therapy may be considered to prolong progression-free survival. There are currently four systemic options for patients with MTC; all are tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) that inhibit intracellular signal transduction pathways by blocking tyrosine kinases (9, 20). Selpercatinib and pralsetinib are relatively new RET-specific inhibitors and have shown promising results in phase I and II clinical trials (21, 22). Although the development of new TKIs has enlarged the treatment reservoir, it has also challenged determining individual patients’ most adequate therapy.

A patient-derived organoid model may guide patient-specific imaging and treatment. Cancer stem cells are believed to be a subpopulation within malignant tumors responsible for cell growth and dedifferentiation (23). After a tissue biopsy, patient-specific cancer stem cells can be cultured to form in vitro, three-dimensional structures. These organoids mimic the in vivo situation. As such, they are ideal for studying metastatic potential, tumor heterogeneity, and treatment sensitivity (24, 25, 26). This study aims to culture MTC organoids (MTOs) for the first time and evaluate their potential as an in vitro model for mechanistic studies on diagnostics and therapy.

Materials and methods

Study approval

Written informed consent of participants was obtained before participation. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee Groningen (approval no. 2015/101).

Patient material

Biopsies were obtained from MTC patients undergoing surgery from December 2020 to January 2023 in the UMCG, where 20–25% of all MTC diagnoses in the Netherlands are treated.

MTC thyrosphere cultures

MTC biopsies were mechanically dispersed using the gentleMACS dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec, The Netherlands) and subjected to digestion in 5 mL HBSS/1% BSA buffer containing 80 mg/mL dispase (Sigma, Germany), 1.2 mg/mL collagenase type II (Gibco, USA), and calcium chloride (Sigma) at a final concentration of 6.25 mM. This was followed by two periods of 15 min in a 37°C shaking water bath. Thereafter, the isolate was collected by centrifugation, washed in HBSS/1% BSA solution, and passed through a 100 μm cell strainer (BD Biosciences, USA). The pellet was collected by centrifugation and resuspended in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium:F12 medium (DMEM:F12) containing Pen/Strep (Invitrogen, USA) and GlutaMAX (Invitrogen). The cell solution was combined with Basement Membrane Matrigel (BD Biosciences) in a 1:2 ratio, on ice, and plated. Following solidification of the gel (30 min incubation), 500 μL of complete growth medium (MTC-medium) consisting of 50% conditioned Wnt3a medium, 10% conditioned R-Spondin medium, and 40% DMEM:F12 containing B27 (Gibco, 0.5×), HEPES (Gibco, 10 mM), ROCK inhibitor Y-27632 (10 μM, Abcam, UK), Nicotinamide (100 μM, Sigma), Noggin (25 ng/mL, Peprotech, USA), EGF (20 ng/mL, Sigma), FGF-2 (20 ng/mL, Peprotech), TGF-β inhibitor A 83-01 (5 μM, Tocris Bioscience, UK), Fungin (10 μg/mL, Invivogen, France), and VEGF-121 (100 ng/mL, Immunotools) was added to each well and replaced weekly. Some samples were stored at −140°C directly after isolation and thawed later to restart culturing. After filtration, the cell pellet was collected in CS10 (Stemcell Technologies, USA/Canada) with 0.5% ROCK inhibitor Y-27632. All obtained MTC biopsies were successfully cultured as MTC-thyrospheres.

Self-renewal

Organoids were dissociated and re-plated to study the long-term self-renewing potential (passaging). Before passaging, Matrigel was dissolved by incubation with dispase enzyme (1 mg/mL for 30 min at 37°C; Sigma), and organoids with a size >50 μm were counted. Initial cultures of 7 days old (d) were dissociated to single cells using 0.05% trypsin‐EDTA (Invitrogen), counted using Trypan blue, and cell concentration was adjusted to 2 × 106 cells per ml. The cell solution was combined with Basement Membrane Matrigel in a 1:2 ratio, on ice, and plated. After solidifying the Matrigel (30 min at 37°C), 500 μL of medium was added (defined above) and renewed weekly. This self-renewal procedure was repeated every 3 weeks and up to four passages.

Cell culture

The human medullary thyroid cancer cell line MZ-CRC-1 was cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (Thermo Scientific, USA) and Pen/Strep (Invitrogen).

Calcitonin and CEA secretion

Media samples were collected and stored at −80°C until use. Calcitonin concentration in the collected media was measured by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) using an Elecsys Calcitonin kit (Roche, Switzerland). CEA concentration was measured by chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassay (CMIA) using an Alinity CEA kit (Abbott Ireland Diagnostics).

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA from patient material (n = 3) and organoids (n = 3) was extracted (RNeasy™ Mini Kit, Qiagen). To obtain cDNA, 500 ng of total RNA was reverse transcribed using 1 μL 10 mM dNTP Mix, 100 ng random primers, 5× First-strand Buffer, 0.1 M DTT, 40 units of RNase OUT, and 200 units of M-MLV RT, in a volume of 20 μL for each reaction (all Invitrogen). qPCR was performed using Bio-Rad iQ SYBR Green Supermix according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For each sample, PCR buffer was mixed with 100 ng cDNA, SYBR Green, and forward and reverse primers for the targeted genes in a volume of 13 μL. Oligo sequences of primers used were: CALCA (fwd): 5′-GGCTTCCAAAAGTTCTCCCCC-3′; CALCA (rev): 5′-CCAGCCGATGAGTCACACAG-3′; CEACAM5 (fwd): 5′-AAGAAATGACGCAAGAGCCTATG-3′; CEACAM5 (rev): 5′-CCCGAAAGGTAAGACGAGTCTG-3′; SST (fwd): 5′-ACCCAACCAGACGGAGAATGA-3′; SST (rev): 5′-GCCGGGTTTGAGTTAGCAGA-3′; TTF1 (fwd): 5′-ATGTACCGGGACGACTTGGAA-3′; TTF1 (rev): 5′-CAATGCCTGTCAGGGCTAGAA-3′, CHGA (fwd): 5′-GGTTCTTGAGAACCAGAGCAGC-3′; CHGA (rev): 5′-GCTTCACCACTTTTCTCTGCCTC-3′; SYP (fwd): 5′-CTCGGCTTTGTGAAGGTGCT-3′; SYP (rev): 5′-CTGAGGTCACTCTCGGTCTTG-3′; NANOG (fwd): 5′-CCCCAGCCTTTACTCTTCCTA-3′; NANOG (rev): 5′-CCAGGTTGAATTGTTCCAGGTC-3′; SALL4 (fwd): 5′-CCCGGCAGTAAGGACTGTC-3′; SALL4 (rev): 5′-TCTCTGTCTTTAGGTACACCACA-3′, and YWHAZ (fwd): 5′-GATCCCCAATGCTTCACAAG-3′; YWHAZ (rev): 5′-TGCTTGTTGTGACTGATCGAC-3′.

Immunostaining

For immunostaining, antibodies to calcitonin (1:100, AB_2335678), CEA (1:100, AB_2244691), TTF1 (1:100, AB_1310784), SYP (1:100, AB_2271365), CHGA (1:100, AB_2918358), and SST (1:100, AB_2195910) were used. In the case of the organoids, Matrigel was dissolved by incubation with dispase enzyme, followed by subsequent washes with PBS/0.2% BSA and centrifuged at 400 g for 5 min. The resulting pellet was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min, RT) and washed with PBS. Next, the organoids were embedded in HistoGel (Richard-Allan Scientific/Thermo Scientific), subjected to dehydration, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned (5 μm). Patient material was fixed in 4% formaldehyde. After dehydration, the tissue was paraffin-embedded and sectioned (5 μm). Sections were deparaffinized, and Tris-EDTA antigen retrieval was subsequently performed (pH 9.0). This was followed by washing, blocking, and incubation with primary antibodies overnight. Next, the slides were incubated with secondary antibodies, stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), and mounted with aqueous mounting medium (DAKO) for tissue sections or ProLong Glass Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher) for organoid sections.

Microscope image acquisition

Fluorescent microscopy was performed at room temperature with a Leica Thunder widefield imaging system equipped with a K8 Camera. Images were acquired with 63x oil HC PL APO 63x/NA: 1.40–0.60 oil. Large computational clearing (LVCC) and deconvolution were applied within the LAS X/thunder acquisition. As fluorochromes, the following were used: goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 633 (Invitrogen), and goat anti-mouse IgG (H+L), Superclonal™ Recombinant Secondary Antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 633 (Invitrogen). The images were analyzed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health).

RET mutation analysis

To assess retention of the RET mutation in the cultured organoids, a small part of the RET gene was amplified (Phusion® High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase, NEB) using the following primers: RET (fwd): 5′-CTG​GCT​ATG​GCA​CCT​GCA​A-3′; RET (rev): 5′-CCC​ACA​GCA​GGA​CAC​CAA​A-3′, resulting in a product of 1,017 bp, which was purified (NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-up, Bioké) and sent for Sanger sequencing (Mix2Seq Kit, Eurofins).

Tyrosine kinase inhibitor screening

Vandetanib (Selleck Chemicals, USA), cabozantinib (LKT Laboratories Inc., USA.), selpercatinib (Selleck Chemicals), and pralsetinib (Selleck Chemicals) were added to the medium of the MZ-CRC-1 MTC cell-line derived spheroids, cultured in an ultra-low attachment U-bottom plate, in a logarithmic dose de-escalation per TKI, 1 week after start of culture. Incucyte images obtained 72 h after TKI administration were used to analyze cytotoxicity induced by the TKI. The Largest Brightfield Object Area (um2) metric was used to determine the IC50 and optimal dose for TKI screening in MTC organoids (Supplemental Figure 1 (see section on Supplementary materials given at the end of the article)). The final dose of 1.0 μM of vandetanib and 0.1 μM of cabozantinib, selpercatinib, and pralsetinib were added to the medium of MTC organoids at the end of passage one. After 72 h, medium was collected to measure the concentration of secreted calcitonin and CEA.

PET tracer validation

18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG), 18F-L-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine (18F-DOPA), and 18F-Prostate Specific Membrane Antigen (18F-PSMA) were administered to assess uptake of the tracer by the MTC organoids (passage 1). Specific uptake was determined after saturation with 5% glucose solution, a cold dose of 0.76 mM DOPA, or a cold dose of 0.38 mM PSMA. Before the experiments, the Matrigel was dissolved by incubation with dispase, followed by three washes with 1,000 μL of HBSS/1% BSA. Approximately 10,000 organoids in 450 μL HBSS/1% BSA were incubated with 1 MBq (in 50 μL) tracer solution at 37°C for 60 min. Afterward, the organoids were collected with a 5-micron filter needle and washed thrice through the filter needle with 1,000 μL ice-cold HBSS/1% BSA. Activity in the organoids was measured in a gamma counter (Wizard2 2480, SW 2.1, PerkinElmer). Tracer uptake was corrected for the number of organoids by organoid counting before the experiment. Uptake was expressed as the percentage of organoid-associated radioactivity per 10,000 organoids. Uptake values were corrected for background radiation.

Statisticsal analysis

All data represent the mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses and graphs were produced using GraphPad Prism, version 8. The statistical methods relevant to each figure are described in the figure legends. Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test or Student’s t-test (depending on normal distribution). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Ten patients with an MTC diagnosis were included after informed consent. The median age at the time of inclusion was 47 years (IQR: 20–69), and four patients were female (Table 1). Germline RET analysis was performed in nine patients as part of clinical care, establishing the hereditary Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia type 2A (MEN2A) syndrome in two patients and MEN type 2B (MEN2B) in one patient. No germline RET analysis was available in one patient, and the disease was classified as sporadic MTC based on a negative family history and the absence of other MEN2-related diseases. In two patients with sporadic disease, the presence of a somatic RET mutation was assessed to evaluate eligibility for systemic therapy. All patients were surgically treated; two patients received a TKI additionally, and in two patients, postoperative radiotherapy was applied.

Organoid formation and in vitro self-renewal potential of medullary thyroid carcinoma stem cells

Ten MTC biopsies were dissociated, cultured in Matrigel with defined medium, and formed spheroid structures (Fig. 1A and B, passage 0). To determine the presence of potential stem cells, self-renewal potential of single cells was assessed. Organoid formation efficiency (OFE) was assessed using nine patient cultures, with all reaching passage 1 (p1), 100% reaching p2, and 78% reaching p3. A maximum OFE of 6.3 ± 0.81% in p1, 6.9 ± 0.83% in p2, and 9.4 ± 1.17% in p3 was reached (Fig. 1B and C), showing self-renewal potential. These spheroids continued to secrete calcitonin and CEA in all passages, indicative of MTC-derived organoids (MTOs), although a decline in concentration was seen from p1 to p2 and p3. This may be due to a gradual decrease in the number of fully differentiated cells (Fig. 1D).

(A) Schematic of medullary thyroid cancer tissue isolation, primary tissue culture, and the self-renewal assay. (B) Primary MTC-thyrospheres 7 days in culture (p0) and MTOs at the end of p1, p2, and p3. Scale bars, 100 μm. (C) Self-renewal potential of MTOs during passaging (n = 9 patients; error bars represent range). No apparent difference between primary tumor MTOs and metastatic MTOs was observed in this preliminary model. Generally, the generation of organoids was subject to interpatient variability. The number of experiments per MTO culture depended on the number of cells in the surgical biopsy and the yielded OFE. Of the ten MTO cultures, nine were used to determine OFE, five were used for characterization, four for TKI experiments, and three for PET tracer experiments.

Characterization of MTOs

To characterize the MTOs, we selected six MTC-specific genes: Calcitonin Related Polypeptide Alpha (CALCA), Carcinoembryonic Antigen-related Cell Adhesion Molecule (CEACAM), Chromogranin A (CHGA), Synaptophysin (SYP), Somatostatin (SST), and Thyroid Transcription Factor 1 (TTF1). qPCR analysis showed that all genes were found in the originating tissue and derived MTOs, although with reduced expression levels in MTOs, as expected in less differentiated cells (Fig. 2A). This concurs with other (para)thyroid-derived organoid models developed by our group (25, 27). Immunostaining confirmed MTC-specific protein expression in tissue and MTOs (Fig. 2B). When comparing CALCA and CEACAM expression patterns in tissue and MTOs, a widely dispersed expression pattern is seen in tissue. In contrast, the MTOs show intensive expression along the outer edges for CALCA and confluent cytoplasmic expression of CEACAM. In addition, CHGA, SYP, and SST (markers for neuroendocrine tumors) show an almost clustered pattern in the tissue sections. In the MTOs, their expression is quite dispersed, possibly indicative of the excretory nature of CHGA and SST and variable membrane expression of SYP. TTF1 is a nuclear marker crucial for thyroid differentiation and morphogenesis (28). TTF1 immunostaining shows nuclear expression in tissue and MTOs, likely indicative of an active proliferative state. Altogether, the MTOs show expression of all factors needed to confirm MTC origin.

In addition, Sanger sequencing was performed to determine whether the RET mutation identified in the original tumor material (standard clinical care) was retained in the organoids. Organoids (passage 1) from three patients were selected. Sanger sequencing showed that two out of three patients had a c.2753T>C, p.(Met918Thr) mutation, which was retained in the organoids. The other patient did not have a RET mutation, which corresponded with its organoids (Supplemental Figure 1).

Drug screening

To evaluate the future potential of MTOs in patient-tailored TKI treatment selection, TKIs were administered as a proof-of-concept experiment. The effect on the secretion of calcitonin and CEA was assessed through media measurements (Fig. 3A).

Effect of TKIs on calcitonin and CEA concentrations in culture medium from MTOs. (A) Schematic of MTC tissue isolation, primary tissue culture, and the drug screening experiment. MTOs exposed to (B) vandetanib, (C) cabozantinib, (D) selpercatinib, and (E) pralsetinib. All; n = 4 patients; error bars represent SEM, normalized to DMSO controls; dotted lines *P < 0.05.

To select the appropriate dose for this proof-of-concept drug screening, spheroids were cultured using the MTC cell line MZ-CRC-1. These spheroids were treated with a logarithmic dose de-escalation of vandetanib, cabozantinib, selpercatinib, and pralsetinib. The Incucyte live-cell analysis system was used to determine the IC50 value of each TKI (Supplemental Figure 2). The final dose of 1.0 μM of vandetanib and 0.1 μM of cabozantinib, selpercatinib, and pralsetinib were added to the medium of MTC organoids at the end of passage 1. After 72 h, medium was collected to measure the concentration of calcitonin and CEA.

We observed a significantly decreased calcitonin concentration in the medium of organoids cultured from the two patients with a Cys634Arg RET mutation when exposed to either of the four TKIs tested and compared with the DMSO control (average reduction of 37 ± 8.6% when exposed to vandetanib, 36.5 ± 9.1% when exposed to cabozantinib, 14.5 ± 3.1% when exposed to selpercatinib, and 40 ± 10.5% when exposed to pralsetinib) (Fig. 3B, C, D, E). A similar effect was observed in the secretion of CEA in the same MTOs (average reduction of 45 ± 8.6% when exposed to vandetanib, 30 ± 9.2% when exposed to cabozantinib, 20.5 ± 3.2% when exposed to selpercatinib, and 37 ± 10.5% when exposed to pralsetinib). The organoids obtained from the patient with the somatic Met918Thr RET mutation showed a significant reduction (30 ± 13.5%) in calcitonin secretion when exposed to pralsetinib (Fig. 3E).

The two patients (both Cys634Arg RET mutation) were treated with a TKI in clinical practice (1× pralsetinib, 1× selpercatinib). Both developed a partial response on treatment, as defined by RECIST criteria (29), and a profound reduction of serum calcitonin and CEA.

While interpretation of the results is limited by the small sample size, the experiments were performed to demonstrate the potential of MTC organoids in patient-tailored drug screening. To allow the application of MTOs in clinical practice in the future, extensive additional research is required.

Positron emission tomography (PET) tracer validation

To demonstrate the potential future utility of MTOs to validate PET tracers, 18F-FDG, 18F-DOPA, and 18F-PSMA were used in proof-of-concept experiments (Fig. 4A). In contrast to 18F-FDG and 18F-DOPA, 18F-PSMA PET has no role in MTC detection in clinical practice. However, a few case reports have illustrated the detection of MTC using 68Ga-PSMA (30, 31, 32). Moreover, in our center, we use 18F-PSMA instead of 68Ga-PSMA. Both target the prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA), which is therefore a useful diagnostic and, possibly, therapeutic target.

PET experiment of MTOs with radioactive tracers. (A) Schematics of MTC tissue isolation, primary tissue culture, and the PET/SPECT experiment. (B) Percentage of 18F-FDG cell-associated uptake compared with the percentage of cell-associated uptake after saturation using 0.5% glucose (n = 3 patients, Tukey boxplot). (C) Percentage of 18F-DOPA cell-associated uptake compared with the percentage of cell-associated uptake after saturation using cold DOPA (n = 3 patients, Tukey boxplot). (D) Percentage of 18F-PSMA cell-associated uptake compared with the percentage of cell-associated uptake after saturation using cold PSMA (n = 3 patients, Tukey boxplot).

We found that the MTOs (passage 1) showed cell-associated uptake of 18F-FDG (0.028 ± 0.009%), 18F-DOPA (0.078 ± 0.008%), and 18F-PSMA (0.039 ± 0.011%) (Fig. 4B, C, D). 18F-FDG showed a significantly reduced uptake when MTOs were first saturated with 0.5% glucose (0.028 ± 0.009% versus 0.011 ± 0.004%), indicating specific uptake (Fig. 4B). Similarly, a significant reduction in the uptake of 18F-DOPA was observed when MTOs were saturated with an excess of cold DOPA (0.078 ± 0.008% versus 0.055 ± 0.010%, P = 0.032). This also indicates specific uptake of 18F-DOPA (Fig. 4C). However, when we saturated the uptake of 18F-PSMA with an excess of cold PSMA, we observed no significant difference (0.039 ± 0.011% versus 0.051 ± 0.014%).

Our results indicate cellular uptake of 18F-FDG and 18F-DOPA by the MTOs. Accumulation of 18F-FDG in the MTOs suggests a higher glucose metabolism, commonly found in tumor cells (33). 18F-DOPA binds to the LAT-1 transporter (amino acid transporter) widely found on neuroendocrine cells, allowing internalization of the tracer (34). MTC is derived from neural crest cells and, therefore, of endocrine origin, which could clarify the specific uptake of the MTOs. On the contrary, 18F-PSMA binds to PSMA, which is only found on the neo-vasculature of MTCs (35). Therefore, the absence of vascularization in MTOs could explain the non-specific uptake of 18F-PSMA. Altogether, our preliminary results show the potential of MTOs to understand PET tracer uptake mechanisms. Moreover, our results suggest a potential place for MTOs in PET tracer development, provided that our results are confirmed in further studies.

Discussion

MTC is a rare type of thyroid cancer, with unique biological characteristics, and persisting diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in everyday clinical practice. It has been shown that a patient-specific approach is fundamental to prolong survival (36). In the present study, we show the establishment and self-renewing capacity of MTOs and illustrate potential applications. Characterization of MTOs and their originating tissues has revealed similarities regarding MTC-specific markers on both gene and protein levels. In addition, we note that MTOs mimic the originating tissue in functionality by producing calcitonin and CEA, and showing a functional response to TKIs. Moreover, our results show specific uptake of PET tracers by the MTOs, showing representative and functional MTOs with potential for further studies and future clinical applications.

With the present study, we aimed to establish an appropriate preclinical and patient-derived model, and evaluate the potential of this model for patient-tailored imaging and treatment for patients with this rare cancer in the future. The model had to be robust, reliable, and able to allow high-throughput screening methods. In addition, the in vitro model maintained the 3D cell-to-cell interactions, as with in vivo MTCs, to allow an accurate comparison to the in vivo situation. After successfully generating and characterizing MTOs, we exposed them to various TKIs in proof-of-principle experiments. The results suggest that these organoids can mimic MTC functionality and may be of potential value for further drug studies. We also demonstrated that the MTOs revealed specific uptake of the PET tracers 18F-FDG and 18F-DOPA. This further validates their origin from MTC cells and indicates that we can culture patient-specific MTOs to use them as a PET tracer testing platform. By assessing the response to systemic therapy in MTOs before patient treatment initiation, ineffective treatments and unnecessary adverse events could ultimately be prevented. Moreover, MTO cultures could guide developing and testing of effective MTC imaging tracers. By determining the PET tracer with the highest uptake in a patient-derived preclinical model, PET imaging may become personalized, with improved disease staging at diagnosis and in the follow-up, and treatment adapted accordingly.

This study is the first to develop a patient-derived organoid culture of MTC tissue. Organoids are self-organizing 3D culture systems that are highly similar to human organs, including increased exposure to circulating molecules, neighboring cells, and the extracellular matrix (Matrigel) (37, 38). Organoids from several endocrine organs and cancers have been developed, such as thyroid organoids (39, 40), papillary thyroid cancer organoids (25), and parathyroid organoids (27). Moreover, numerous papers have shown the value of organoids for discovering potential novel therapeutic targets while also opening opportunities for future imaging tracer testing and targeted treatment screening (24, 27).

The limitations of our study include the absence of the original microenvironment. This consists of, among others, fluctuating concentrations of extracellular signals and the presence of blood vessels. This may be the reason why our MTOs did not reveal 18F-PSMA uptake. Several case reports showed the uptake of PSMA-binding tracers in MTC, which led to our experiment. However, histological examination of MTC tissue shows PSMA expression only in the neo-vasculature of the tumor, which is not incorporated in the MTOs and may explain the absence of 18F-PSMA uptake (35). Although a more complex organoid model may improve the resemblance to in vivo tissue, bioengineers have suggested that robust models, with the specific characteristics relevant to the pathophysiological functions of interest only, may be sufficient for studying mechanisms and functions (41).

In addition, a persisting challenge in the development of thyroid-derived organoids is the lack of a specific stem cell marker, limiting evidence for stem cell-ness to the data on the organoid forming efficiency (39, 40, 42). As NANOG and SALL4 are embryonic stem cell markers, the absence of their expression is therefore unsurprising and highlights the need for further research to uncover an ideal thyroid stem cell marker (43).

Based on this proof-of-concept study, we cannot draw solid and definitive conclusions with the primary aim of demonstrating the generation of MTOs, their functionality, and future potential in drug and PET tracer studies. Due to the rarity of the disease, further development of MTOs in multi-center, international collaborations are required to create MTOs on a large scale, study different media, produce significant results, and validate their use in drug and PET tracer screening. Even then, it will be difficult to directly compare the results of the drug and tracer experiments to patients. In contrast to patients who take daily oral doses of a TKI, we administered only a single dose of each TKI to the MTOs and measured the tumor marker concentrations 72 h later (27). It is also difficult to extrapolate TKI and PET tracer doses to patient doses. Pharmacokinetic characteristics, possible drug–drug interactions, and renal or hepatic impairment in patients influence how much of the administered TKI or tracer finally reaches the tumor cells (22). In addition, some suggest that decreases in tumor markers do not adequately reflect the response to TKIs (44). Therefore, CT imaging measurements are the gold standard for assessing tumor response in clinical practice. However, growth rate is complicated to determine in organoids. One passage takes 3 weeks, and no growth rate could be determined within 72 h of TKI treatment. Measuring differences in ATP levels or cell proliferation markers during TKI administration could potentially provide more information on the metabolic activity of the TKI-exposed organoids in future studies. Finally, the limited window in which experiments were performed may have influenced the results, since passage 1 organoids may be biologically different from those observed in further passages. However, as we were purely interested in showing proof-of-principle, we hope our methodology will initiate other people to generate more MTOs, further refine the medium components, and expand the evidence for clinical applicability.

In conclusion, we present the successful generation of a patient-derived MTO culture that recapitulates the originating tissue regarding gene and protein expression and functionality. This medullary thyroid cancer culture may be able to serve as a novel in vitro model to perform physiology studies and discover potential novel therapeutic targets, opening opportunities for future tracer testing and drug screening.
