A multifunctional g-C3N4@WO3 hybrid nanocomposite for integrated SPE-UPLC-DAD determination and photocatalytic degradation of tetracycline hydrochloride†
Authors: Ömer Papak, Gökhan Sarp, Uğur Şahin, Erkan Yılmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The removal of pharmaceutically active compounds and synthetic dyes entering aquatic systems due to environmental pollution is of great importance. Furthermore, the reliable determination of these substances in various matrices is essential. In this study, we focused on the removal and trace-level determination of environmental pollutants by synthesizing a multifunctional hybrid nanomaterial. This study proposes a g-C3N4@WO3 multifunctional hybrid nanocomposite (MHNC) synthesized simply and cost-effectively according to a specific approach. The structure and morphology of the material were characterised by X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, and field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM). The MHNC was developed for dual applications in solid-phase extraction (SPE) and photocatalytic degradation of tetracycline hydrochloride (TC-HCl), a widely used antibiotic. Quantitative analyses were carried out using an ultra-performance liquid chromatography system equipped with a diode-array detector (UPLC-DAD) and UV-vis spectrophotometry. The systematic optimisation of critical analytical parameters affecting SPE performance was conducted, including the sample solution pH, sample volume, type and volume of the elution solvent and sorbent amount. The newly developed SPE-UPLC-DAD method, after optimisation, was successfully applied to wastewater and synthetic urine samples, yielding recovery values in the range of 93.0–95.0%. The developed method was facile, fast, and inexpensive and also exhibited satisfactory precision and accuracy. The limits of detection (LODs) were determined to be 2.60 ng mL−1 for tetracycline HCl using UPLC-DAD and 0.69 ng mL−1 for methylene blue using UV-vis spectroscopy; the corresponding limits of quantification (LOQs) were calculated as 7.67 and 2.09 ng mL−1, respectively. Furthermore, under UV irradiation, the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC achieved almost complete removal of tetracycline HCl within 150 minutes and that of methylene blue within 210 minutes. The g-C3N4@WO3 nanocomposite is reported, for the first time, as a dual-function platform for both SPE-UPLC-DAD determination and photocatalytic degradation. Unlike conventional approaches, the proposed system enables both trace-level determination and removal of organic contaminants from aqueous systems using a multifunctional hybrid nanocomposite.

Introduction

The increasing use of pharmaceuticals, particularly antibiotics, has resulted in their frequent detection in aquatic environments, raising growing environmental concerns.1–4 Among these compounds, tetracycline hydrochloride (TC-HCl) is widely used due to its broad-spectrum antibacterial activity and relatively low cost.1–3,5 A significant portion of TC-HCl is not fully metabolized and is released into wastewater systems after excretion. Even at low concentrations, its presence in natural waters may contribute to antibiotic resistance and negatively affect aquatic ecosystems.2 For this reason, the development of reliable analytical methods for trace-level detection, together with effective removal strategies, is of great importance in environmental chemistry.

Additionally, synthetic dyes, such as methylene blue (MB), mainly originating from the textile and printing industries, represent another important group of hazardous pollutants. These dyes reduce light penetration in water bodies, limiting photosynthetic activity and having toxic effects on aquatic organisms.6 The coexistence of antibiotics and dyes in wastewater highlights the need for integrated treatment approaches capable of handling chemically diverse contaminants.

Conventional wastewater treatment methods are often insufficient for the simultaneous removal of pharmaceutical residues and organic dyes. Therefore, increasing attention has been given to multifunctional materials that combine analytical detection with remediation processes.7–10 Solid-phase extraction (SPE) is commonly used for the preconcentration and selective isolation of trace analytes from complex matrices.11–14 Semiconductor-based photocatalysis is an effective method for degrading persistent organic pollutants through the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS).9,15 Integrating these two functions within a single material system offers a promising strategy for both detection and removal.

Graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) has attracted considerable interest in recent years due to its two-dimensional layered structure, chemical stability, and visible-light activity.16–19 Nevertheless, its performance is often limited by the rapid recombination of photogenerated charge carriers and its relatively low surface area. To overcome these limitations, coupling g-C3N4 with transition metal oxides such as tungsten trioxide (WO3) has been proposed as an effective approach.20,21

WO3 possesses a narrow band gap and excellent stability. When combined with g-C3N4, it forms a heterojunction that significantly enhances charge separation and increases the number of active sites for adsorption and catalytic reactions. Although g-C3N4-based materials have been investigated for various applications, their use as both an SPE sorbent for trace-level pharmaceutical analysis and a high-performance photocatalyst has not been sufficiently explored. Notably, the g-C3N4@WO3 heterojunction synergistically generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) under UV irradiation to degrade dyes and organic pollutants while providing selective π–π interactions and electrostatic attractions to enrich TC-HCl, offering a unique ‘detect and eliminate’ platform.20,22–29 Therefore, the development of a multifunctional material capable of integrating these two functionalities within a single platform is of considerable scientific and practical interest.28,29

In this study, a novel g-C3N4@WO3 multifunctional hybrid nanocomposite (MHNC) is synthesised through a strategic combination of calcination and hydrothermal methods. The synthesised multifunctional hybrid nanomaterial is utilised as an adsorbent in the SPE method and as a photocatalyst in photocatalytic applications, exhibiting dual functionalities. The structural and morphological integrity of the material is comprehensively characterised using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), and X-ray diffraction (XRD). The analytical usefulness of the multifunctional hybrid nanomaterial is determined through the SPE-UPLC-DAD assay of TC-HCl in complex matrices, achieving superior sensitivity and recovery. Furthermore, the efficacy of the multifunctional hybrid nanomaterial in environmental applications is investigated by monitoring the photocatalytic degradation performance of MB and TC-HCl under 400-W UV radiation (365 nm) using UV-vis spectrophotometry. In this way, it is demonstrated that the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC represents a pioneering approach for integrated tasks such as trace antibiotic determination and organic pollutant removal and can offer a versatile solution for modern environmental challenges.

Despite the considerable progress reported in the literature, most existing studies primarily focus on pollutant removal, rather than integrating both functionalities within a single material platform. This separation often limits practical applicability in real environmental systems, where simultaneous detection and remediation are required. In this context, the present study introduces a multifunctional g-C3N4@WO3 hybrid nanocomposite designed to bridge this gap by combining solid-phase extraction capability with photocatalytic activity. Unlike conventional approaches, the proposed system enables both trace-level determination and efficient degradation of contaminants using a single material. This integrated functionality not only simplifies the overall process but also enhances its potential for real environmental applications. Therefore, the developed material represents a meaningful contribution toward the design of next-generation multifunctional platforms for environmental monitoring and remediation.

Experimental

Reagents and materials

The following reagents were utilized in the experiment: sodium hydroxide (NaOH, Merck, Germany), phosphoric acid (Merck, Germany), ethylene glycol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), hydrochloric acid (HCl, Merck, Germany), ethyl alcohol (Merck, Germany), acetonitrile (Merck, Germany), methanol (Merck, Germany), 2-propanol (Merck, Germany), acetone (Merck, Germany), and glacial acetic acid (Merck, Germany). These reagents were used as received. The following chemicals and reagents were utilized: tetracycline hydrochloride (purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, USA), urea (purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, USA), cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, USA), sodium dihydrogen phosphate (purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, USA), sodium chloride (NaCl, purchased from Merck, Germany), methylene blue (purchased from Merck Millipore Corp., Singapore), sodium acetate trihydrate (purchased from Merck, Germany), and sodium tungstate dihydrate (purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, USA). All reagents were of analytical grade. Chromatographic separations were performed using a UPLC C18 column (150 mm × 3.0 mm × 2.7 µm, 1000 bar; Agilent, Poroshell 120).

Preparation of standard solutions and real sample solutions

The main stock solutions of the active ingredients were prepared at a concentration of 1000 mg L−1 and stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C. Intermediate stock solutions at different concentrations were prepared from these stock solutions and used throughout the study. The tetracycline hydrochloride stock solution in ethanol and intermediate solutions in deionized water were prepared.

The wastewater used as the real sample was diluted in a 1 : 1 ratio with deionized water and then passed through a 0.22-µm filter before being used for the analyses. Lastly, the artificial urine model solution was used in the recovery studies.14,30

Instrumentation

The UPLC system used was an Agilent 1290 Infinity series (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and the Agilent Poroshell 120 column (150 mm × 3.0 mm × 2.7 µm, 1000 bar) was used for separation in the study. A PerkinElmer Lambda 25 model UV-vis spectrometer was utilized for photocatalytic studies. A photocatalytic reactor equipped with a 400-W UV lamp manufactured by Uniterm (Turkey) was employed for the photocatalytic degradation experiments. An FT-IR 400 spectrometer equipped with an ATR accessory (Waltham, MA, USA), an X-ray diffraction (XRD) instrument (PANalytical Empyrean Apparatus, Malvern, UK) and a Gemini-500 field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, Zeiss) were used for the characterization of the synthesized nanomaterials. An ultrasonic mixer (Bandelin Sonorex, Germany) was employed as a stirrer to dislodge and disperse the residual gas bubbles that had formed in the mobile phase. During the experimental phase, a centrifuge (Hettich Universal 320, Germany) was employed to effectively separate the solid phase from the liquid phase. An oven, manufactured by Memmert (Germany), was used for the drying process of the synthesized nanomaterials. To weigh all of the samples, an analytical balance (Precisa LS 220 A SCS, Switzerland) was utilized.

Development of the UPLC-DAD method

A UPLC-DAD detection system was utilized for the analysis of tetracycline HCl. The mobile phase was composed of 20 mM phosphate buffer (pH = 3.0) (A) and acetonitrile (B) in a ratio of 67 : 33 (A : B, v/v). The chromatographic separation of tetracycline HCl was conducted on an Agilent Poroshell 120 column (150 mm × 3.0 mm × 2.7 µm, 1000 bar). Following the optimization process, the flow rate of the method was set to 0.4 mL min−1 and the wavelength to 270 nm. These values were utilized for analysis at all stages of the study.

Results and discussion

Synthesis of g-C3N4, WO3 and the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC

The first step was to synthesise g-C3N4 using a controlled thermal calcination method. Briefly, 15 g of urea was weighed and transferred to a lidded porcelain crucible. An equal volume of distilled water was then added. The mixture was first homogenized in an ultrasonic bath for 15 min. The same procedure was then performed in a second crucible to confirm reproducibility. To avoid overflow and to promote more uniform heat transfer, the volume of the mixture was kept below half of the crucible height (h/2). Calcination was carried out in a Protherm muffle furnace (Turkey) using a stepwise heating program, reaching a final temperature of 550 °C. The temperature was initially increased from room temperature at a rate of 10 °C min−1 until the first stage was reached, followed by a holding period of 45 min. The second heating step was then applied at the same rate, and the final calcination stage was maintained for 120 min. The total duration of the process, including cooling, was approximately 270 min (4.5 h). The resulting yellowish g-C3N4 product was ground into a fine powder using an agate mortar to ensure homogeneity and then stored in a desiccator to prevent moisture absorption.

In the second step, WO3 nanoparticles were synthesised via a nitrogen-assisted hydrothermal method. In brief, 0.3 g of Na2WO4·2H2O and 0.05 g of NaCl were dissolved separately in 10 mL of glacial acetic acid. Both solutions were then heated to 40–50 °C and ultrasonicated for 30 min. The solutions were then combined and subjected to further ultrasonication for 1 h to ensure homogeneous dispersion. Subsequently, the pH of the mixture was adjusted to 2.0 using 0.1 M HCl under constant magnetic stirring. Thereafter, the resulting suspension was transferred into a 50-mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave. Subsequently, the system was pressurized with N2 gas (1.5–3.0 bar) and heated from 30 °C to 180 °C over 60 min. Subsequently, the hydrothermal reaction was sustained at a temperature of 180 °C for 24 h. Following the natural cooling process of the autoclave to ambient temperature, the precipitate was collected and thoroughly washed with ethanol twice and with acetone once. This was accomplished via a centrifugation process at a speed of 4000 rpm for 15 min. The resulting yellowish powder was dried at 70 °C for 10 h, ground in an agate mortar and stored for further characterization.

In the third step, the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC was synthesised via a hydrothermal assembly process at a mass ratio of 2 : 1. Specifically, 0.5 g of presynthesised g-C3N4 and 0.25 g of WO3 NPs were weighed in a beaker and homogenised in an ultrasonic bath for 15 min. Then, a solvent mixture consisting of 65% ethylene glycol and 35% distilled water was added. The dispersion was then subjected to magnetic stirring for a further 30 min, followed by an additional 30 min of ultrasonication to ensure complete homogenisation. To facilitate surface interaction and stability, 75 mg of cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) was added as a surfactant, and the mixture was ultrasonicated for a further 30 min. The resulting suspension was transferred to a hydrothermal reactor and maintained at 180 °C for 16 h.

After the reaction, the product was collected and washed once with ultrapure water and twice with methanol to remove residual reagents. Subsequently, the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC was exposed to a drying process at a temperature of 70 °C. Thereafter, the material was ground into a fine powder using an agate mortar. The resulting yellowish-green-grey powder was stored for use in subsequent solid-phase extraction, photocatalytic applications and further characterisation.

The analytical performance of the developed method was evaluated according to internationally accepted and validation guidelines, specifically the ICH Q2 (R1)31 guideline and its updated version, ICH Q2 (R2),32 which provide the standard framework for analytical method validation.

Real sample analysis

For real sample analysis, recovery studies were conducted on wastewater and artificial urine. Samples were taken from two different points in the sewage system of Erciyes University. Two liters of the samples were collected simultaneously from each of these two designated points (Fig. 1).30 The samples were stored in a reservoir adjusted to +4 °C until the SPE method was applied. Before undergoing the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC-based SPE-UPLC procedure, the samples were filtered through a 0.22-µm filter. This procedure was implemented with the objective of minimizing the matrix loading and reducing the necessity of filling work.

Characterization techniques

Characterization of the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC

FTIR analysis of g-C3N4, WO3 and the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC

FTIR analyses were performed in the 4000–450 cm−1 wavenumber range to elucidate the functional groups of the synthesised nanomaterials (Fig. 2). An examination of the obtained FTIR spectrum of g-C3N4 reveals a maximum peak at 3198.4 cm−1, which is attributed to N–H asymmetric stretching bands. The band observed in the 1200–1650 cm−1 region is characteristic of g-C3N4 nanomaterials and corresponds to the typical stretching modes of C–N heterocycles. Furthermore, the FTIR spectrum of the WO3 structure shows hydroxyl group stretching vibrations at 3300–3100 cm−1. The characteristic peaks of the structure are observed at 1100–850 cm−1, corresponding to OH bending vibrations, as well as in the fingerprint region at 700–400 cm−1, where M–O (metal oxide) peaks are predominantly seen (WO3). In the final nanomaterial, the characteristic asymmetric stretching and bending peaks of both materials are observed.

FTIR spectra of the g-C3N4 NPs, WO3 NPs, and g-C3N4@WO3 MHNCs.

XRD analysis of g-C3N4, WO3 and the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC

XRD spectra were obtained for the synthesized nanomaterials. The distinctive peaks of the g-C3N4, WO3, and g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC structures are clearly discernible (Fig. 3). A thorough examination of the XRD spectrum of the final synthesized material reveals that the characteristic peaks of some species are suppressed and that new crystalline phases emerge following the synthesis. A total of two distinct diffraction peaks are identified in the examined samples. These peaks can be indexed to the hexagonal phase of g-C3N4. The most intense XRD peak, located at 27.38°, is attributed to the stacking of the conjugated aromatic system. This peak is typically observed in graphite materials and corresponds well to the interlayer d-spacing of g-C3N4 (0.336 nm). The intensity of the diffraction peak at 13.04° decreases with increasing heating temperature, indicating that the (100) crystal plane of g-C3N4 is damaged by processing at high temperatures, resulting in structural defects.

XRD patterns of the g-C3N4 NPs, WO3 NPs, and g-C3N4@WO3 MHNCs.

The diffraction peaks observed at 2θ values of 16°, 23°, 26°, 29°, 34°, 35°, 38°, 49°, 52°, 56°, and 62° can be assigned to the characteristic reflections of monoclinic WO3, in agreement with the standard JCPDS card no. 01-072-1465. Among these, the reflection at 2θ = 23° suggests the presence of a substoichiometric tungsten oxide phase (WO2.95). Notably, the most intense diffraction signal corresponds to the (111) crystallographic plane of WO3, observed at 2θ = 26°, indicating its dominant crystalline orientation. In the final synthesised material (g-C3N4@WO3), the peaks of both g-C3N4 and WO3 are observed. Upon the completion of the synthesis process, both materials have been successfully synthesized.

FE-SEM analysis of g-C3N4, WO3 and the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC

The FE-SEM images of g-C3N4 NPs, WO3 NPs, and g-C3N4@WO3 MHNCs recorded at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV and at different magnifications (30 k) are presented in Fig. 4. The images of g-C3N4 reveal characteristic leaf-like layered structures, which are clearly observed at both magnifications. For WO3 NPs, rod-like structures with diameters of approximately 100 nm are distinctly visible. In the case of the final nanocomposite (g-C3N4@WO3 MHNCs), the WO3 nanorods are well distributed within and partially embedded into the layered g-C3N4 matrix, indicating successful heterostructure formation. When examining the surface morphologies of the final nanomaterial before (g-C3N4@WO3-a) and after (g-C3N4@WO3-b) the SPE-photocatalytic processes, visible changes on the surface are observed in the images at similar magnifications. It is clearly seen that the structure undergoes deformation compared to its initial form as a result of swelling and agglomeration in the structures.

FE-SEM images of the g-C3N4 NPs, WO3 NPs, g-C3N4@WO3-a (before SPE-photocatalytic) MHNCs and g-C3N4@WO3-b (after SPE-photocatalytic) MHNCs.

EDX analysis was performed to characterize the elemental changes in the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC before and after its application in the SPE-photocatalytic procedure

To investigate the elemental distribution and structural stability of the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNCs, EDX analyses were performed for both the pristine material and after its fifth recycling run (Fig. 5). A comparative evaluation of the EDX spectra revealed a significant decrease in the weight percentages of N (nitrogen) and W (tungsten) elements following the repeated SPE-photocatalytic processes. These elements are of paramount importance as they constitute the active sites of the heterojunction: the nitrogen atoms in the g-C3N4 framework are responsible for light harvesting and electron generation, while the tungsten centers in WO3 are essential for the effective separation of photogenerated holes and the subsequent oxidation reactions. The leaching or loss of these key elements directly impairs the charge transfer efficiency between the conduction and valence bands, thereby leading to the observed decline in photocatalytic performance after the fifth cycle. This elemental depletion provides clear evidence for the structural degradation and resulting loss of catalytic activity over time.

Comparative EDX spectra and elemental mapping of the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNCs before (A) and after (B) the SPE-photocatalytic process (after the 5th cycle), illustrating the depletion of the N and W active sites.

Optimization of the SPE procedure and UPLC-DAD

Effect of the sample solution pH on the extraction efficiency of tetracycline HCl

The pH of the sample solution plays a critical role in determining the adsorption behavior of analyte molecules. This is mainly because the extracted fractions of the species present in the solution are influenced by the pH conditions. Tetracycline HCl is an amphoteric and polar nutrient containing ionizable functional groups such as phenolic, enolic, and amine groups. Therefore, it can exist in cationic, zwitterionic, neutral, or anionic forms depending on the pH of the solution. The direct alteration of tetracycline ionization by pH, and thus its interaction with the adsorbent surface, is well documented. Because tetracycline has multiple pKa values (approximately 3.3, 7.7, and 9.7), the dominant ionic species vary according to the solution pH, and this is considered when interpreting its adsorption. Therefore, the effect of the solution pH on the adsorption of tetracycline HCl was investigated in the pH range of 2.0–10.0 (Fig. 6). It was ensured that the pH of the adsorption solution remained constant between 2.0 and 4.0; the maximum tetracycline HCl adsorption reached is approximately 95% at pH 4.0. Beyond this point, adsorption processes are reduced, and a more pronounced decrease in adsorption efficiency is observed under alkaline conditions (pH ≥ 8.0). At low pH values, the protonated form of tetracycline becomes dominant, promoting stronger electrostatic attraction and increasing bonds with adsorbent surfaces, thus increasing the adsorption rate.

Effect of the sample solution pH on the extraction efficiency of tetracycline HCl (N = 3).

The significant signal change observed around pH 7.0 is likely driven by the speciation of tetracycline ionic species. Near this pH, the molecule approaches its second dissociation constant (pKa2 ≈ 7.7), where the dominant species shifts from the zwitterionic form to the anionic species. This transition leads to a sudden change in the electrostatic interactions and adsorption performance between tetracycline and the adsorbent surface. Specifically, the decrease in the contribution of hydrogen bonding and the repulsion between anionic species may explain the sharp signal change observed at pH 7.0. With a further increase in the pH, tetracycline progressively transforms into negatively charged species, leading to decreased adsorption due to possible electrostatic repulsion between the adsorbent surface and anionic tetracycline molecules. Overall, the findings suggest that the optimum pH for tetracycline HCl adsorption under the studied degradation conditions is 4.0.

Effect of the adsorbent amount on the extraction efficiency of tetracycline HCl

The influence of the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC dosage on the adsorption efficiency of tetracycline HCl was evaluated within the range of 5–150 mg (Fig. 7). The results indicate that, at a fixed analyte concentration, increasing the adsorbent amount significantly improves the adsorption efficiency. This enhancement can be explained by the increased availability of active adsorption sites at higher dosages. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the adsorption efficiency progressively increases with increasing adsorbent amount, reaching a maximum value of approximately 95% at 125 mg. After this point, the adsorption efficiency shows almost no further increase. It appears that equilibrium is already established at this stage. Therefore, adding more adsorbent does not significantly contribute to the additional binding of tetracycline molecules. For this reason, 125 mg of the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC is selected as the optimum amount and used in the subsequent experiments.

Effect of the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC amount on the SPE efficiency of tetracycline HCl (N = 3).

Effect of the eluent type and volume on the extraction efficiency of tetracycline HCl

In solid-phase extraction, the desorption step plays an important role before instrumental analysis. Efficient removal of the analyte from the adsorbent is necessary to obtain satisfactory recovery and sensitivity. Because tetracycline HCl is an organic pharmaceutical compound, various organic solvents were used as eluents for its desorption from the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC adsorbent. In order to achieve this objective, a series of tests was conducted using 2-propanol, methanol, ethanol, acetonitrile, an ethanol–methanol mixture, an acetonitrile-water mixture, and a mobile phase consisting of 67% pH 3 buffer solution and 33% acetonitrile. These tests were conducted during the desorption stage. Following elution, the concentration of tetracycline HCl in the final phase was determined using UPLC-DAD analysis with appropriate calibration standards prepared using each eluent. As demonstrated in Fig. 8, moderate desorption efficiencies were obtained with alcohol-based solvents and acetonitrile. However, it was found that the highest desorption efficiency was achieved when the mobile phase composed of 67% pH 3 phosphate buffer solution and 33% acetonitrile was utilised as the eluent. Consequently, this mobile phase was selected as the optimum eluent for subsequent desorption experiments.

Effect of the eluent type on the solid-phase extraction efficiency of tetracycline HCl (N = 3).

In addition to the type of eluent, the volume of the eluent plays a critical role in achieving a high enrichment factor and a low detection limit while minimising organic solvent consumption. Consequently, various volumes of the selected eluent (0.75–2.0 mL) were examined for the desorption of tetracycline HCl adsorbed on the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC. As demonstrated in Fig. 9, quantitative desorption was achieved with an eluent volume of 2.0 mL (Fig. 9A), while a decrease in the eluent volume resulted in a corresponding reduction in desorption efficiency. Next, the sequential elution method was implemented. In the sequential elution procedure, the total eluent volume was divided into three equal parts, and the elution process was performed three times separately for each part. Next, the resulting fractions were amalgamated within a singular vial, and UPLC analyses were conducted once more.

Effect of the eluent volume without sequential elution (A) and with sequential elution (B) on the solid-phase extraction efficiency of tetracycline HCl (N = 3).

Consequently, the evaluation conducted to ascertain the lowest eluent volume yielded a recovery value of 90.79% for 1.0 mL (Fig. 9B). Because a high recovery rate was observed, it was determined that 1.0 mL would serve as the optimal eluent volume.

Effect of the sample volume on the extraction efficiency of tetracycline HCl

Optimization studies were conducted by using different sample volumes in the developed solid-phase extraction procedure. The sample volume is a critical parameter affecting analytical performance characteristics, particularly the enrichment factor, limit of detection, and limit of quantification. In order to evaluate the effect of the sample volume, the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC-based SPE method was applied to tetracycline HCl solutions with volumes ranging from 10 to 50 mL under optimised experimental conditions (Fig. 10). As demonstrated in Fig. 10, the desorption efficiency remained consistently above 97% for sample volumes between 10 and 40 mL, indicating stable and quantitative extraction within this range. An increase in the sample volume to 50 mL resulted in a slight decrease in recovery (95.71%), indicating the emergence of capacity limitations for the adsorbent material. Nevertheless, satisfactory quantitative extraction was attained. Following a thorough evaluation of quantitative recovery and method robustness, it was ascertained that a sample volume of 50 mL offered both a high enrichment factor and high extraction efficiency. Consequently, 50 mL was selected as the optimum sample volume for subsequent studies.

Effect of the sample volume on the solid-phase extraction efficiency of tetracycline HCl (N = 3).

Analytical performance of the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC-based SPE/UPLC procedure

In order to facilitate the analytical management or quality control of the SPE-UPLC procedure developed using the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC, a number of factors were evaluated independently, including linearity, specificity, precision, accuracy, and reproducibility. The calibration curve for tetracycline HCl was obtained using the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC-based SPE-UPLC procedure with 50 mL of standard solutions with increasing tetracycline HCl concentrations. The LOD was calculated by multiplying the standard deviation of the signals obtained at the lowest concentration of the analyte by 3 times the slope of the calibration curve, while the LOQ was calculated by multiplying the standard deviation of the signals obtained at the lowest concentration of the analyte by 10 times the slope of the calibration curve. The sensitivity of the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC-based SPE-UPLC procedure was expressed as the intraday RSD% calculated using the procedure for 50 mL of 10 standard solutions containing 25 µg L−1 tetracycline HCl. The concentration recoverability values were determined using 50 mL of 10 standard solutions containing 25 µg L−1 tetracycline HCl using the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC-based SPE-UPLC procedure. In this SPE-UPLC procedure, a sequential elution method was applied with 1.0 mL of the mobile phase solution to desorb tetracycline HCl molecules from g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC lesions. Reusability and stability of the adsorbent

The reusability and stability of the developed hybrid material are critical aspects of the extraction procedure from both economic and environmental perspectives. Adsorption and desorption experiments were conducted using the optimized analytical method to investigate the ability to regenerate and reuse the synthesized adsorbent. The reusability of the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC as an SPE sorbent was rigorously evaluated throughout the optimization studies (n = 12). For this purpose, the adsorbent was synthesized once and successfully reused by applying a simple washing procedure involving methanol and deionized water (twice). Subsequently, the adsorbent was rinsed with ultrapure water and dried at 60 °C overnight. After drying, the adsorbent was reused for the subsequent adsorption and desorption cycles. Based on the obtained results, the adsorption performance of the material remained remarkably stable, with recoveries exceeding 89% even after >10 cycles of tetracycline hydrochloride removal (RSD < 3%). These results indicate that the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC has great potential for repeated use in sample preparation procedures.

Photocatalytic mechanism and charge transfer pathway

The charge transfer mechanism between g-C3N4 and WO3 can be interpreted through two main pathways: the conventional type-II heterojunction and the Z-scheme system. In a type-II heterojunction, the migration of charge carriers leads to a reduction in the overall redox potential of the composite. However, the Z-scheme mechanism allows the recombination of lower-energy electrons from the CB of WO3 with the lower-energy holes in the VB of g-C3N4. This configuration preserves the high-energy electrons in the CB of g-C3N4 and the high-energy holes in the VB of WO3, maximizing the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). The following band energy values, derived from the authoritative literature, support the proposed mechanism:g-C3N4: Eg = 2.70 eV, ECB = −1.12 V, EVB = +1.58 V vs. NHE.38WO3: Eg = 2.80 eV, ECB = +0.41 V, EVB = +3.21 V vs. NHE.39

Based on these potentials, the CB of g-C3N4 is sufficiently negative to facilitate the reduction of adsorbed oxygen into superoxide radicals (˙O2−) (E0 = −0.33 V vs. NHE). Simultaneously, the deep VB of WO3 provides a strong driving force for the oxidation of H2O molecules into hydroxyl radicals (˙OH) (E0 = +2.40 V vs. NHE). The synergy between these species, along with direct hole (h+) oxidation, explains the near-complete degradation of TC-HCl and MB observed in this study, pointing towards Z-scheme charge transfer as the dominant pathway, as a type-II mechanism would significantly lower the redox potentials and limit the production of these key reactive species.

Additionally, a schematic diagram illustrating the proposed Z-scheme photocatalytic mechanism is provided in Fig. 12. This figure visually demonstrates the charge transfer pathway and the generation of reactive oxygen species (˙O2− and ˙OH) based on the calculated band edge potentials, providing a clearer understanding of the synergetic degradation process of TC-HCl and MB.

Conclusion

The presence of pharmaceutical and industry-derived trace pollutants in environmental matrices necessitates the integrated consideration of analytical determination and remediation processes. In this study, the applicability of the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC was systematically evaluated both as a solid-phase extraction (SPE) sorbent and as an active photocatalyst. The model contaminants used in this study were tetracycline hydrochloride (TC-HCl) and methylene blue (MB). The two-dimensional structure and visible-light responsiveness of the g-C3N4 matrix were effectively enhanced through the formation of a heterojunction with WO3, resulting in improved charge carrier separation, an increased number of active sites, and promoted generation of reactive oxygen species.

From an analytical standpoint, the developed SPE-UPLC-DAD method allowed for the reliable and sensitive determination of TC-HCl in complex matrices, including wastewater and synthetic urine. Notably, the method exhibited superior analytical sensitivity with a detection limit (LOD) of 2.60 ng mL−1, outperforming the methods reported in several recent studies in the literature that utilize more complex magnetic nanocomposites or conventional extraction phases. Furthermore, a high enrichment factor (EF) of 50 was achieved, which is significantly higher than that reported for many carbon-based analogs, providing a robust advantage for trace-level residue monitoring.

In addition to its analytical capability, the material also exhibited effective photocatalytic activity toward both TC-HCl and MB under 365-nm UV irradiation. The g-C3N4@WO3 system demonstrated near-complete degradation (∼100%) of both pollutants, surpassing the removal efficiencies of various binary and ternary photocatalytic systems reported in recent years, even those employing higher-power light sources. These findings suggest that the g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC can function as a single platform that integrates both detection and removal processes. Such a dual-functional system could be advantageous for studies focused on environmental monitoring and pollutant degradation.

The recovery values obtained for TC-HCl were consistent, falling within the range of 93.00–95.00%. This consistency indicates that the sorbent preserves stable interaction sites across different sample matrices. This stability is particularly significant, as it demonstrates that the material can operate not only under controlled laboratory conditions but also in real environmental samples. Compared to the existing methods reported in the literature, the proposed method provides a more precise and reproducible alternative, as evidenced by the low relative standard deviation (RSD) of 2.90%.

Overall, the proposed g-C3N4@WO3 MHNC represents a promising platform that links analytical determination and pollutant removal within a single material system. By achieving both superior detection sensitivity and exceptional photocatalytic efficiency, this dual-functional approach contributes to the development of more integrated and sustainable strategies for environmental monitoring and remediation applications.
