Lateral mass intrapedicular screw fixation: A radiological evaluation of screw pullout strength
Authors: Kota Kojima, Hiroaki Kanbe, Kenichiro Fukui, Takahiro Endo, Yasuyuki Fukui, Masayuki Ishikawa, Shunji Asamoto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Lateral mass intrapedicular screw (LMIS) fixation was introduced in 2021 as a safe and simple alternative method for the fixation of the subaxial cervical spine in the treatment of various cervical spine diseases. The purpose of this study was to evaluate and compare the screw pullout strength of this new technique to the trajectories of two other major methods, the transpedicular screw (TPS) and lateral mass screw (LMS) methods.Methods:Two hundred and three patients who underwent a cervical computed tomography (CT) scan at our institution were included in the study. A region of interest for each of the three trajectories was selected using axial slices of the cervical vertebra from C3 through C6, and the CT number (Hounsfield unit) was calculated.Results:A total of 4872 data points were collected. The average age of the patients was 59.5 years. The male-to-female ratio was 135:68. The number of patients over the age of 65 was 93. The average CT numbers were 511.9 for TPS, 473.1 for LMS, and 598.5 for LMIS. There was a significant difference in the CT number among the three trajectories, even when adjusted for sex and age.Conclusion:LMIS is a promising alternative to the common methods used for subaxial cervical spine fixation that has a significant pullout strength. This approach is worth considering in many patients.

INTRODUCTION

The two most popular surgical methods for performing posterior fixation of the subaxial cervical spine (C3–C6) are the lateral mass screw (LMS) method and the transpedicular screw (TPS) method. In 2021, we introduced a novel method, the lateral mass intrapedicular screw (LMIS) method, as a relatively strong and safe third option with certain merits over the two other popular methods.[1] In brief, the purpose of LMIS is to place the tip of the screw within the cortex-rich pedicle to increase the pullout strength of the screws while minimizing the risk of vertebral artery (VA) injury by not advancing the screws beyond the posterior border of the vertebrae. The entry point of the screw is the midpoint of the lateral mass [Figure 1]. The screw is angled 20°–25° against the spinous process in the axial plane [Figure 2]. In the sagittal plane, under fluoroscopic guidance, the screw is directed toward the rostral quarter (zone 1) of the vertebral body [Figure 3]. The screw is not advanced beyond the posterior border of the vertebral body to minimize VA injury [Figure 4]. This trajectory allows us to maximize the length of the screws while reducing the incidence of screw pulling out. Since the first paper was published, we have performed this method in more than 30 patients, and the intra- and postoperative results have been excellent[2] [Figure 5].

Computed tomography (CT) has become increasingly important in the field of orthopedic research for estimating bone strength, and its application in diagnosing and assessing osteoporosis-related fractures is being investigated. The CT number, expressed in Hounsfield units (HU), can be used to measure bone density and estimate bone strength.[3] The pullout strength of screws is believed to increase with thread depth, screw diameter, screw pitch, and bone strength.[4] In other words, the screw-to-cortical screw bone contact can be used to estimate the pullout strength, andtherefore, the strength of the fixation.

The aim of this study was to compare the average CT number within the screw placement regions for all three methods (TPS, LMS, and LMIS) to provide an objective explanation for the significant pullout strength that we observed intraoperatively.

METHODS

Two hundred and three patients who underwent a cervical CT scan between April 2020 and December 2021 at our institution were included in the study. All patients who underwent a cervical CT scan were included in the study. The exclusion criterion was obvious deformities that made evaluation difficult. The average age was 59.5 years (±19.5, range 15–95). The male-to-female ratio was 135:68. The number of patients over the age of 65 years was 93, with a male-to-female ratio of 51:42. All patients underwent a CT scan using the same machine (GE Healthcare, Revolution Frontier), and vertebral bodies C3 to C6 were selected. A region of interest (ROI) was selected using axial slices of the patient’s cervical vertebrae for all three methods. The ROI was purposefully placed within the outer cortex so that the window did not protrude, which would produce a significantly lower CT number. A total of 4872 CT numbers were calculated (1624 data points for each of the three methods).

For TPS, the axial CT images were sliced on a plane horizontal to the trajectory [Figures 6 and 7]. The entry point was below the facet joint at the midpoint between the medial and lateral margins of the lateral mass. The ROI was fixed at 3 mm × 18 mm, representing the screw used in this method, and was directed from the entry point toward the midline of the pedicle.

For LMS, the axial CT images were sliced on a plane horizontal to the trajectory [Figures 8 and 9]. The entry point was slightly inferomedial to the midpoint of the lateral mass. The cranio-caudal angulation was set by connecting the entry point and the facet joint. The ROI was fixed at 3 mm × 14 mm and was directed 25° laterally in the axial plane.

For LMIS, the axial CT images were sliced on a plane horizontal to the trajectory described above [Figures 10 and 11]. The ROI was fixed at 3 mm × 14 mm.

For each patient, the average CT number was calculated for all mid-cervical vertebrae (C3 to C6) for all three methods (TPS, LMS, and LMIS).

Statistical analysis

All the data are presented as the means ± standard deviations. A two-sample t-test was performed to compare the CT numbers among the three methods. The significance level was set at P < 0.05. Microsoft Excel for Mac (ver. 16.63.1) and SPSS Statistics (ver.24, IBM, USA) were used for all calculations.

RESULTS

Transpedicular screw

The mean CT number (HU) for the TPS method for all the patients was 511.9 ± 207.2 HU (C3: 494.0 ± 177.7, C4: 556.2 ± 241.9, C5: 515.1 ± 203.6, C6: 482 ± 193.1). The mean CT number for male patients was 524.8 ± 204.0 HU, and the mean CT number for female patients was 486.4 ± 211.2 HU. The mean CT number for patients under age 65 was 575.6 ± 205.1 HU, and the mean CT number for patients over age 65 was 436.6 ± 184.4 HU.

Lateral mass screw

The mean CT number (HU) for the LMS method for all the patients was 473.1 ± 163.2 HU (C3: 476.4 ± 163.0, C4: 512.0 ± 170.1, C5: 464.2 ± 153.8, C6: 439.9 ± 157.8). The mean CT number for male patients was 486.4 ± 166.4 HU, and the mean CT number for female patients was 446.7 ± 153.4 HU. The mean CT number for patients under age 65 was 523.9 ± 164.2 HU, and the mean CT number for patients over age 65 was 413.1 ± 140.1 HU.

Lateral mass intrapedicular screw

The mean CT number (HU) for the LMIS method for all the patients was 598.5 ± 227.9 HU (C3: 525.5 ± 207.4, C4: 614.9 ± 235.9, C5: 605.3 ± 217.3, C6: 648.4 ± 232.8). The mean CT number for male patients was 614.2 ± 234.0 HU, and the mean CT number for female patients was 567.3 ± 207.7 HU. The mean CT number for patients under age 65 was 670.2 ± 231.9 HU, and the mean CT number for patients over age 65 was 513.7 ± 187.1 HU.

There were significant differences in the CT numbers among all three methods for all age groups, sex groups, and patients over/under the age of 65 years [Graph 1 and Tables 1-5].

DISCUSSION

The purpose of LMIS is to place the screw in an area that will allow for maximum cortical bone-to-screw contact while minimizing the risk of VA injury. In the lumbar spine, the cortical bone trajectory is known to provide pullout strength similar to that of TPSs, even with shorter screws,[56] which is believed to occur because the screws are placed in an area that is rich in cortical bone.[7] In this study, we demonstrated that the area in which the screws are placed in the LMIS method is significantly richer in cortical bone than that in the TPS and LMS methods. Without detailed cadaveric and biomechanical experimentation, it is difficult to measure the exact pullout strength of LMISs. However, we believe that the cortical bone-to-screw contact is responsible for the significant pullout strength that is observed intraoperatively. Although the safety of this method is slightly beyond the scope of this paper, the fact that this trajectory provides significant pullout strength while minimizing the risk of VA injury suggests that it could become the first option for many surgeons performing posterior cervical fixation. It should be emphasized that a preoperative CT scan must be performed to rule out any anatomical anomalies within the path of the VA. Other benefits of this technique include decreased muscle stripping and the combined use of laminoplasty given that the trajectory is directed mediolaterally.[12]

CONCLUSION

LMIS fixation is a safe and relatively simple technique that can provide significant pullout strength while minimizing VA injury. This study has shown that, in this technique, the screws are placed in an area that is rich in cortical bone, which is responsible for the pullout strength observed intraoperatively. This technique could become the first choice for many patients requiring posterior cervical fixation.
