Association Between Red Blood Cell Distribution Width‐To‐Albumin Ratio and Breast, Colorectal, Lung, and Prostate Cancers
Authors: Parham Habibzadeh, John Sorkin, Dennis Hsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionCancer remains a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality, underscoring the need for accessible biomarkers for early detection and risk stratification. Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and early detection and risk stratification remain critical priorities. Biomarkers derived from routine laboratory tests are appealing because of their ubiquity, low cost, and ease of implementation. Composite biomarkers derived from routine blood parameters, such as inflammation‐based hematologic markers and albumin‐related indices, have demonstrated prognostic relevance in colorectal cancer [1, 2].

The red blood cell distribution width (RDW), a routine parameter in complete blood counts that quantifies variability in red cell corpuscular volume, has emerged as a biomarker of interest. High RDW values are associated with inflammatory states and poor nutritional status, conditions frequently encountered in patients with malignant disorders [3, 4]. Similarly, serum albumin, a negative acute‐phase reactant, is a well‐known index of nutritional health and systemic inflammation [5, 6, 7]. A relative increase in RDW in the context of lower albumin may reflect a milieu of heightened inflammatory stress and diminished resilience, a situation commonly observed in patients with malignancies. The combination of these two markers into the RDW‐to‐albumin ratio (RAR) may thus offer a more robust prognostic biomarker than either marker alone [8, 9, 10].

Previous research has shown that increased levels of RAR are associated with all‐cause mortality among patients with cancer and in general populations [10, 11]. For instance, a historical cohort study based on the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III database shows that an RAR ≥ 5.51% dL/g is independently associated with increased all‐cause mortality at 30 days, 90 days, and 1 year in patients with cancer [12]. After adjusting for confounders, the high‐RAR group had a 74% higher risk of 30‐day mortality than the low‐RAR group (HR = 1.74; 95% CI, 1.48–2.04). Another study revealed that RAR is a prognostic factor in patients with metastatic renal cell carcinoma [13]. However, data remain sparse on whether RAR is differentially associated with the presence of specific cancer types, particularly common malignancies such as breast, colorectal, lung, and prostate cancer, in representative population cohorts. To address this gap, we conducted this cross‐sectional study of NHANES data to evaluate the association between RAR and the recent presence of breast, colorectal, lung and prostate cancers, the most prevailing cancers in the world. We hypothesized that a higher RAR would be positively associated with a recent diagnosis of the studied cancers.

Materials and Methods

Study Population and Data Source

Data from seven consecutive 2‐year cycles (2005–2018) of the NHANES were analyzed. Conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), NHANES includes interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory tests [14]. The analysis was restricted to adult participants aged 18 years or older.

Variable Definitions and Data Processing

The exposure variable, RAR, was calculated as RDW divided by serum albumin. RDW and albumin were measured from standard laboratory samples as part of the NHANES protocol [14]. The outcome variable was a history of cancer. Participants were identified as having cancer based on a positive response to the question “[h]as a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had cancer or a malignancy of any kind?” The analysis was restricted to individuals reporting only a single cancer to avoid confounding from multiple primary malignancies. The study participants were limited to those with a recent cancer diagnosis (defined as ≤ 1 year since diagnosis) to enhance the temporal link between the exposure (RAR) and the outcome and to better capture potential tumor‐related systemic inflammation. The time since diagnosis was derived from the difference between the participant's age at the survey and their reported age at first diagnosis. A one‐unit increase in RAR represents an absolute increase of 1% dL/g in the ratio, a change that can reflect clinically meaningful shifts in underlying inflammation or nutritional status.

Statistical Analysis

R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) was used for data analysis.

Propensity Score Matching

For each cancer type found to be eligible, a matched comparison group was constructed from the pool of participants with no history of cancer. Propensity score matching was employed to control for potential confounding by age, sex and race. While this addresses major demographic confounders, residual confounding by unmeasured variables (e.g., smoking, comorbidities, cancer stage) may persist. Matching was performed using the R package MatchIt, with the “full” matching method [15]. This technique forms matched sets that minimize the total within‐set distance across the entire sample, offering a robust alternative to fixed‐ratio matching. Exact matching was enforced for sex and race to ensure perfect balance on these categorical variables, while age was matched on the propensity score derived from a logistic regression model. No prespecified matching ratios were imposed, allowing the algorithm to determine the optimal pairing structure. The balanced of matching process was also evaluated by measuring standardized mean difference (SMD); the goal was to achieve an SMD for all matched variables of < 0.1.

Logistic Regression Analysis

Following matching, separate weighted binary logistic regression analyses for each cancer type were performed using the matched datasets and weights. The dependent variable was the presence vs. absence of cancer (case/comparison status). The primary independent variable was RAR. The regression models were adjusted for the matching variables (age, sex, and race) for robustness. For breast and prostate cancers, which are sex‐specific, the models were adjusted for age and race. Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), representing the change in odds of having the specific cancer per one‐unit (1% dL/g) increase in RAR.

Results

Study Population

The initial pooled NHANES sample from 2005 to 2018 comprised 70,190 participants. After excluding participants younger than 18 years, those with missing RAR data, those with a history of multiple cancers, and those with cancer but missing or unknown cancer type, the final analysis dataset included 37,373 participants (Figure 1). From this pool, we identified individuals with a recent diagnosis (≤ 1 year) of a single primary breast, colorectal, lung, or prostate cancer to serve as cases for their respective matched analyses. This yielded 48 breast, 31 colorectal, 19 lung, and 77 prostate cancer cases.

Association Between RAR and Cancer

The mean RAR was higher in cases than in comparisons for breast (3.53% vs. 3.34% dL/g), colorectal (3.56 vs. 3.38), and lung (3.67 vs. 3.30) cancers, but similar for prostate cancer (3.32 vs. 3.31) (Table 2). In the weighted logistic regression models, a 1% dL/g increase in RAR was associated with significantly higher odds of having a recent diagnosis of breast cancer (adjusted OR, 1.62 [95% CI, 1.17–2.24]), colorectal cancer (adjusted OR, 1.56 [95% CI, 1.01–2.40]), and lung cancer (adjusted OR, 1.95 [95% CI, 1.44–2.66]). The association for prostate cancer was not statistically significant (adjusted OR, 1.05 [95% CI, 0.69–1.59]) (Table 2). Discussion

We found that a higher RAR is significantly associated with the presence of breast, colorectal, and lung cancers, but not prostate cancer. Given the cross‐sectional nature of the study, the observed associations likely reflect the systemic inflammatory and nutritional burden associated with these cancers rather than a prediagnostic risk signal. After rigorous propensity‐score matching and adjustment in weighted regression models, RAR remained an informative marker for distinguishing individuals with these malignancies. A 1% dL/g increase in RAR was associated with a near twofold increase in the odds of lung cancer and approximately a 1.6‐fold increase in the odds of breast or colorectal cancer (Table 2). In contrast, the association for prostate cancer was nonsignificant, suggesting that the diagnostic relevance of RAR is not uniform across all tumor types but may be specific to cancers with a pronounced systemic inflammatory component.

The findings align with a growing body of evidence supporting the utility of composite inflammation‐ and nutrition‐based biomarkers in oncology. For example, other indices derived from routine blood parameters have shown prognostic value in colorectal cancer [1, 2]. Our study extends this rationale by specifically evaluating the RAR across multiple common cancers in a population‐based setting.

The observed differential associations provide valuable insights into cancer‐specific pathophysiology. The robust association with lung cancer likely reflects the well‐documented tendency of this malignancy to induce systemic inflammation and cachexia, processes that directly elevate RDW through impaired erythropoiesis and lower albumin levels through a combination of reduced hepatic synthesis and increased catabolism [16, 17, 18]. Similarly, colorectal cancer may elevate RAR through mechanisms involving chronic mucosal inflammation, occult gastrointestinal bleeding leading to iron‐deficiency anemia, and nutritional compromise [16, 19]. The significant, though somewhat milder, association of RAR with breast cancer is consistent with its more variable systemic inflammatory profile, which can be present in advanced or aggressive subtypes [16, 20, 21]. The lack of an association with prostate cancer may be explained by the typically indolent and localized nature of many cases at diagnosis, which often fail to provoke a significant systemic inflammatory or nutritional response that would be captured by RAR. This is consistent with the understanding that biomarkers reflecting systemic stress may be less sensitive for detecting localized, early‐stage disease, such as that common in prostate cancer at initial diagnosis.

The biological rationale for RAR as a composite biomarker is compelling. An elevated RDW signifies impaired red blood cell production and increased anisocytosis, often driven by the effects of pro‐inflammatory cytokines like interleukin‐6 and tumor necrosis factor‐α on erythroid maturation in the bone marrow [18, 22, 23]. Concurrently, hypoalbuminemia in this context is not merely a marker of malnutrition but also a direct consequence of the systemic inflammatory response, which reprioritizes hepatic protein synthesis away from albumin and toward acute‐phase reactants [5, 16]. Therefore, RAR integrates two synergistic pathways of cancer‐associated physiological disruption—chronic inflammation and nutritional decline—potentially amplifying its sensitivity as a harbinger of underlying malignancy. This perspective is supported by a growing literature that emphasizes the prognostic value of integrated, inflammation‐based scores, such as the Glasgow Prognostic Score, which also combines albumin with another inflammatory marker, the C‐reactive protein [17, 24].

The key strengths of our study include the use of a large, nationally representative sample, the application of robust propensity‐score matching to minimize confounding by key demographic variables, and the focus on simple, inexpensive biomarkers that are readily available in most clinical settings. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross‐sectional nature of the design precludes any inference of causality or predictive utility between RAR and cancer diagnosis. The elevated RAR is likely a consequence of the cancer's physiological impact rather than a predisposing factor. Second, the number of recent cases for certain cancers, particularly lung cancer (n = 19), is small. While propensity score matching efficiently uses available data, these small sample sizes can limit the precision of estimates and increase the susceptibility of effect sizes to inflation; our results for these cancers should therefore be interpreted with caution and require replication in larger cohorts. Third, while we matched on age, sex, and race, residual confounding by unmeasured variables such as disease stage, specific tumor characteristics, comorbidities, smoking history, or recent treatment exposure could persist and influence RAR levels differentially across cancer types. Fourth, restricting the analysis to cancers diagnosed within 1 year, while methodologically intended to strengthen the temporal link, may introduce selection bias by excluding individuals with longer‐standing diagnoses and different disease states. Future research with larger, prospective cohort studies is essential to validate these associations and elucidate the precise biological mechanisms linking the RAR to oncogenesis and progression.

Conclusion

RAR shows meaningful and differential associations with a recent diagnosis of several common cancers in a representative population. Its strong relationship with malignancies characterized by a significant inflammatory and cachectic burden, such as lung and colorectal cancer, suggests its potential utility as an adjunct, low‐cost biomarker reflecting disease‐associated physiology. It could potentially aid in risk stratification or serve as a flag for further diagnostic investigation in specific clinical settings. Prospective longitudinal studies are strongly warranted to clarify the temporal relationship between RAR and cancer development and to rigorously assess its predictive and clinical value.
