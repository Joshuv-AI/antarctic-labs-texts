Determinants of Health‐Related Quality of Life in Older Adults With Rheumatoid Arthritis: A Cross‐Sectional Study in Northern Iran
Authors: Amene Beigmohammadi, Roya Mansour‐Ghanaei, Parand Pourghane, Bahare Gholami Chaboki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsWith increasing life expectancy, noncommunicable diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA) are major contributors to reduced health‐related quality of life (HRQOL) among older adults. This study aimed to identify demographic and clinical factors associated with HRQOL in older adults with RA in northern Iran.MethodsIn this cross‐sectional analytical study, 94 RA patients aged ≥ 60 years were recruited from the only government rheumatology clinic in Rasht, northern Iran. Disease activity was evaluated using the Disease Activity Score in 28 joints (DAS28), which incorporates the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR). HRQOL was measured with the EuroQol‐5 Dimensions‐5 Levels (EQ‐5D‐5L) questionnaire (range: −0.594 to 1.000). Body mass index (BMI) was categorized as normal (≤ 25), overweight (> 25), or obese (> 30). Demographic and health‐related data were also collected.ResultsModerate RA severity predominated (58.51%). The mean DAS28 and EQ‐5D‐5L index scores were 3.98 ± 1.1 and 0.583 ± 0.253, respectively. HRQOL was significantly associated with age (H = 10.512, p = 0.005), education (H = 14.306, p = 0.003), urban residence (U = 703.500, p = 0.006), and occupation (H = 7.833, p = 0.030). Disease activity severity was significantly inversely correlated with HRQOL (r = −0.566, p < 0.001). Multiple regression confirmed disease activity severity (β = −0.101, 95% CI: −0.137 to −0.065; p < 0.001) and urban residence (β = 0.086, 95% CI: 0.011 to 0.161; p = 0.025) as key predictors.ConclusionIdentifying and addressing modifiable factors such as disease activity and geographic disparities may guide interventions to improve quality of life in older adults with RA.

Introduction

Population aging, a significant social upheaval of the twenty‐first century, requires nations to restructure their social and health systems and address broad economic and societal repercussions [1]. Advancements in public health policies, disease prevention, and access to quality healthcare have contributed to the increase in life expectancy among older adults [2]. From a demographic perspective in Iran, old age is generally considered to begin between 60 and 65 years [3]. Understanding the factors influencing aging‐related issues is crucial. Aging is a natural process that affects all individuals, leading to physiological and psychological changes in the body [4]. As individuals age, the likelihood of developing chronic diseases and disabilities also increases. Recent studies indicate that 80% of the elderly population suffers from at least one chronic disease, and these conditions contribute to greater vulnerability to disability and mortality. Approximately 40% of older adults experience limitations related to chronic diseases [5].

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA), a progressive chronic disease [6], affects approximately 2% of adults globally. While it is most prevalent at ages 35–50, its incidence increases with age, and 30% of patients are ≥ 60 years [7, 8, 9]. Women are affected nearly three times more frequently than men are affected [10]. The etiology of RA remains unknown but involves genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors [11], including obesity [12]; overweight, particularly among women (specifically, a high body mass index [BMI]) [13]; high intake of sugar‐sweetened beverages [14]; excess sodium intake in smokers [15]; low physical activity; alcohol use [16]; and exposure to environmental pollutants such as air pollution [17].

RA is characterized by chronic inflammation of the joints and other body systems [18]. As the disease progresses, it leads to joint damage and restrictions in daily activities [9]. The spread of RA not only contributes to increased mortality but also escalates related healthcare costs. The disease's effects significantly impact the patient's overall quality of life, encompassing physical, mental, psychological, social, economic, family, and sexual domains [19]. RA imposes considerable challenges across all aspects of a patient's life, with nearly two‐thirds of affected individuals requiring assistance from family members or friends [20].

Health is a fundamental determinant of quality of life and plays a pivotal role in healthcare systems. Accordingly, the concept of health is often examined in relation to quality of life, particularly in the context of health‐related quality of life (HRQOL). HRQOL is a critical area of research and is regarded as one of the key indicators in the management of chronic diseases [5]. The goals of RA management in addition to early detection, timely intervention, disease control, and pain relief to also encompass maximizing HRQOL and preserving physical function [21].

Attention to improving the quality of life in older adults has become a priority [22]. Previous studies have identified several factors, including social and demographic variables, that significantly influence HRQOL in this population. These factors include sex, physical activity, body mass index [23], diet, age, alcohol consumption in men [24], education level, and lifestyle [25]. In addition, disease‐related symptoms such as back pain, knee stiffness, and lower back discomfort, which lead to activity limitations, are strongly associated with a decline in HRQOL [22].

Although Guilan Province has the highest proportion of older adults (13.87%) in Iran [26], few studies have examined the factors influencing HRQOL in elderly patients with RA in this region. Considering the progressive nature of RA, its disabling complications, and its substantial treatment costs, there is a clear need to identify the demographic and clinical factors that influence HRQOL in this population. This study was therefore designed to assess the associations of demographic characteristics, BMI, and disease activity severity with HRQOL among older adults with RA attending the Rheumatology Clinic in Rasht, northern Iran, to provide a solution based on findings to help improve the HRQOL of these patients.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

In this cross‐sectional analytical study, which was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines [27], 94 older adults with RA were consecutively recruited using convenience sampling from the only government Rheumatology Clinic in Rasht, the capital of Guilan Province in northern Iran, between January to May 2021. This clinic is the only public facility of its kind in the provincial capital, serving patients from various cities across the region. Convenience sampling was employed due to COVID‐19‐related constraints on patient flow and clinic access.

Participants

The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: aged 60 years or older, possessed adequate mental and cognitive function, and provided informed consent to participate, had a confirmed diagnosis of RA according to the 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria [28], had a minimum disease duration of 6 months, and had no vision or hearing impairments that would interfere with communication. The exclusion criterion included the inability to continue completing the questionnaire.

The sample size for this study was determined to be 94 participants, on the basis of the relevant study by Paek (2016) [22], which utilized PASS software and multiple regression analysis. The calculation considered an effect size of r = 0.3, a significance level of 0.05, a power of 0.9, and an anticipated dropout rate of 20%.

Measures

Data, including age, sex, education, place of residence, occupation, income, marital status, smoking habits, and BMI, were collected via a personal‒social information questionnaire. The occupation categories included housewives, farmers, employees (salaried), and workers (manual labor). Income status was based on patients’ self‐reports, and participants categorized themselves as having low, middle, or high income according to their perception of their household financial situation. This self‐report method was used because of the sensitivity of collecting exact income data in the Iranian context. To determine BMI, the researcher measured participants’ weight via a digital scale with an accuracy of 0.1 kg and assessed height via a tape measure. BMI was calculated via the following formula: BMI = weight (kg)/height² (m). The participants were categorized as follows: a BMI ≤ 25 was considered normal, a BMI > 25 was classified as overweight, and a BMI > 30 was classified as obese. Health‐related information, including the duration of RA and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, gastrointestinal disorders, and thyroid disorders) was obtained from the patients’ medical records. The disease activity score (DAS28‐ESR) was calculated via DAS28 online software, a widely utilized clinical tool for assessing RA disease activity. To calculate this scale, a series of variables were examined in patients, including 28 swollen joints and 28 painful joints; the examined joints included 2 shoulders, 2 elbows, 2 wrists, 10 metacarpophalangeal joints, 10 proximal interphalangeal joints, and 2 knee joints. The DAS28 score also incorporates the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) or C‐reactive protein (CRP) value and the patient's self‐reported Global Health score. The analysis and calculation of the disease activity score were performed via online software. Patients with DAS28‐ESR scores less than 2.6 were classified as having remission. Scores between 2.6 and 3.1, 3.1 and 5.0, and above 5.0 were classified as indicating mild, moderate, and severe disease activity, respectively.

To assess health‐related quality of life (HRQOL), the Persian version of the EQ‐5D‐5L questionnaire was utilized. This five‐dimensional instrument was developed by the European Quality of Life Organization to measure HRQOL. Linde and colleagues validated the EQ‐5D in a sample of 200 patients with RA, demonstrating that the EQ‐5D is a reliable tool for assessing HRQOL in RA patients [29]. The questionnaire includes five dimensions: mobility, self‐care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. For each dimension, participants are asked to rate their health on a five‐level scale: 1 (no problems), 2 (slight problems), 3 (moderate problems), 4 (severe problems), and 5 (extreme problems). The health states are represented on a scale ranging from 11111 (best health state) to 55555 (worst health state) [30]. The EQ‐5D‐5L includes a visual analog scale (EQ‐VAS), where participants rate their current health status on a vertical scale from 0 to 100 [31]. The EQ‐5D‐5L system provides 3,125 possible health states on the basis of the five dimensions. To calculate the HRQOL score, the data were entered into an Excel table, and the index value calculator program was used to evaluate the overall score. For the EQ‐5D‐5L, scores range from −0.594 to 1.000, with values lower than 0 indicating poorer HRQOL [32].

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages) were used to summarize the demographic data. The normality of the data distribution was assessed via the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test. As the HRQOL score was not normally distributed, nonparametric tests were employed. Differences in HRQOL across categorical variables were assessed via the Mann‒Whitney U test (for two groups) or the Kruskal‒Wallis H test (for more than two groups). The correlation between disease activity (DAS28) and HRQOL was evaluated via Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. Similar statistical methods were used to analyze the EQ‐VAS scores. Simple and multiple linear regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of HRQOL, with the results reported as unstandardized beta (β) coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All analyses were prespecified. A significance level of p < 0.05 was applied for all the statistical tests.

Ethical Approval

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guilan University of Medical Sciences (Code: IR. GUMS. REC.1399.085). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and the Helsinki declaration. Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 94 older adults with RA were included in this study. The participants had a mean age of 63.57 ± 5.14 years (range: 60–86) and a mean BMI of 26.71 ± 4.35 years (range: 18.61–41.77). The majority of participants were female (81.91%), had a moderate level of disease activity (58.51%), and had lived with RA for over 10 years (59.6%). Health‐Related Quality of Life, Disease Activity, and Dimension Analysis

The mean HRQOL score, as measured by the EQ‐5D‐5L index, was 0.583 ± 0.253 (range: −0.331 to 1.000). The mean EQ‐VAS score was 55.51 ± 19.89. The mean DAS28 score was 3.98 ± 1.1 (range: 1.20–6.66), indicating a moderate level of disease activity for the group.

An analysis of the five dimensions of the EQ‐5D‐5L revealed that pain/discomfort was the most affected dimension, with 92 patients (97.9%) reporting any problem (slight to extreme) and 73 patients (77.7%) reporting moderate to extreme problems. Factors Associated With the EQ‐VAS

Bivariate analyses indicated that several demographic factors were significantly associated with EQ‐VAS scores. These included place of residence (U = 768.500, p = 0.025), and duration of RA (H = 6.041, p = 0.049). The participants were urban residents, and those with a disease duration < 5 years reported the highest EQ‐VAS scores. The findings revealed a statistically significant relationship between disease activity severity (measured by the DAS28) and the EQ‐VAS score (H = 29.512, p < 0.001), with patients in remission reporting the highest mean EQ‐VAS score (66.18 ± 18.87) and those with severe activity reporting the lowest score (35.27 ± 18.02) (Table 3).

Multivariate Regression Analysis

A multiple linear regression model was constructed to identify key predictors of HRQOL while controlling for confounding variables. The severity of RA disease activity was the strongest predictor (β = −0.101, 95% CI: −0.137 to −0.065; p < 0.001), indicating that for every one‐unit increase in the DAS28 score, the HRQOL score decreased by 0.101. Additionally, patient age (β = 0.007, 95% CI: −0.014 to 0.000; p = 0.059) and place of residence (urban vs. rural) (β = 0.086, 95% CI: 0.011 to 0.161; p = 0.025) were also found to be associated with HRQOL. Specifically, HRQOL decreased with increasing age, and patients living in urban areas had higher HRQOL scores than did those living in rural areas. Discussion

This study aimed to identify the key determinants of HRQOL in older adults with RA in northern Iran. Our findings demonstrate that HRQOL is multifactorial and significantly influenced by both disease activity severity and sociodemographic characteristics. The strongest predictor of poorer HRQOL was higher disease activity severity, followed by rural residence and older age within this elderly group. A detailed analysis of the EQ‐5D‐5L dimensions identified pain/discomfort as the most prevalent impairment, followed by limitations in mobility, underscoring the central role of pain control in this population.

Regarding demographic factors, our findings are in line with those of the study by Al‐Jabi et al. (2021), which identified age, place of residence, income, education, and employment status as significant predictors of HRQOL. Similar to our study, they also demonstrated the influence of socioeconomic factors, although they reported somewhat different effects for sex and BMI [21]. Jalali Farahani et al. (2017) likewise reported that demographic factors such as age, sex, and occupation are associated with HRQOL, supporting the current results [33]. In our study, although the vast majority of participants were women (81.91%) who reported lower mean HRQOL scores than men did, this difference did not reach statistical significance. Rather than a biological prioritization, a more plausible explanation lies in socioeconomic disparities. Many women in our study were housewives without independent income, potentially leading to financial dependency and barriers to accessing healthcare, which could consequently depress their HRQOL. This interpretation is bolstered by a review by Haridoss et al. (2021), which corroborated the significant role of female sex and older age in reducing HRQOL [34]. In contrast, Karimi Moghadam et al. (2017) reported no significant association between age, occupation with HRQOL [35], ‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬ a discrepancy that may be explained by the younger age of their participants compared with our elderly group. ‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬‬

In the present study, the mean EQ‐5D‐5L index was 0.58, and the mean EQ‐VAS score was 55.5. These values are lower than those reported in other countries, including Denmark, Spain, Japan, and Korea, where EQ‐5D scores ranged from 0.63 to 0.77 [36, 37, 38, 39]. The lower scores in our study are likely attributable to the older age of the participants, higher prevalence of comorbidities, and possible cultural differences in self‐rated health. On the other hand, our results are comparable to those of Al‐Jabi et al. (2021), who reported a median EQ‐5D‐5L score of 0.56, highlighting the variability in HRQOL across different populations [21].

In the present study, a significant strong inverse correlation was observed between disease activity (DAS28‐ESR) and HRQOL. Specifically, patients with severe arthritis disease activity presented a lower mean HRQOL score. This finding is clearly in line with many previous studies [9, 20, 34, 40, 41, 42], confirming that disease activity is an important clinical factor determining functional capacity, HRQOL, and overall well‐being in patients with RA. Gouda et al. (2023) reported that depression and disability are positively correlated with disease activity (DAS28), underscoring the interplay between clinical status and psychosocial health in patients with RA [43]. This finding is consistent with the earlier work of Liu et al. (2017), which indicated that heightened disease activity adversely affects both physical and mental health domains [44]. These findings complement our results, as pain/discomfort was identified as the most common EQ‐5D dimension, and anxiety/depression was reported by almost one‐third of patients. Taken together, these data suggest that mental health is an important mediator of HRQOL and may exacerbate the impact of disease activity on functional outcomes.

Overall, HRQOL is a multidimensional construct, shaped by a variety of variables. The evidence indicates that social and demographic factors, as well as cultural and societal differences substantially affect HRQOL [33]. As efforts to reduce RA disease activity continue, it is imperative to identify mediators and potential moderators for improving HRQOL through targeted interventions. However, the contribution of these factors to HRQOL in RA patients remains inconsistently defined in the literature.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its cross‐sectional design restricts the ability to establish causal relationships between the variables. Second, the focus on older adults limits the generalizability of the findings to other age groups. Third, the lack of a control group (e.g., younger RA patients or healthy older adults) limits the comparative analysis of the findings. Fourth, the ongoing COVID‐19 pandemic has resulted in a reduced patient flow, prolonging the data collection period and potentially introducing selection bias. Finally, the lack of longitudinal follow‐up prevents us from examining changes in HRQOL over time or the long‐term effects of disease activity. Future studies with diverse age groups, longitudinal designs, and larger sample sizes across multiple regions would help to validate and expand upon the findings of this study.

Conclusion

Our findings highlight the importance of reducing disease activity as a primary strategy for improving HRQOL in older adults with RA. In addition, sociodemographic disparities, particularly those related to age, gender, and rural residence, must be addressed. Expanding access to rheumatology care in rural areas, integrating telemedicine, and providing targeted social support for women and low‐income patients could help mitigate these disparities. The incorporation of HRQOL measures into routine clinical assessments can further guide individualized treatment strategies.
