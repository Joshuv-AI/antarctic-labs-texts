Mandibular Osteomas: Report of Two Cases With Surgical Management
Authors: Tito Molina, Mark W. Gonzalez, Haeseong Lee, Bonifacio Rivera Virella, Lidia M. Guerrero Rodriguez, Parish P. Sedghizadeh, Nader Salib
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSolitary peripheral osteomas of the mandible can cause facial deformity and are best evaluated using CT scans, which reveal well‐circumscribed, hyperdense masses. Importantly, osteomas warrant prompt systematic evaluation to rule out Gardner's syndrome, especially when numerous lesions are present. Surgical excision is an effective treatment with no recurrence observed in these cases, highlighting the importance of early diagnosis and intervention for optimal functional and esthetic outcomes.

Introduction

Osteomas are benign osteogenic tumors characterized by the proliferation of compact or cancellous bone. They can be classified as central, peripheral, or extraskeletal. Central osteomas arise from the endosteum, peripheral osteomas from the periosteum, and extraskeletal soft tissue osteomas develop within a muscle [1]. Clinically, peripheral osteomas are typically asymptomatic but may cause swelling and facial asymmetry. These lesions generally exhibit slow growth [2].

According to Sayan, peripheral osteomas most commonly occur in the frontal, ethmoid, and maxillary sinuses [2]. Although rare in the jawbones, when present, they are more frequently found in the mandible than in the maxilla [3]. Osteomas are more prevalent in men and usually manifest in patients over 30 years old [4].

Additionally, patients diagnosed with osteomas should be evaluated for Gardner's syndrome (GS), an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by gastrointestinal polyps, multiple osteomas, skin and soft tissue tumors, as well as multiple impacted or supernumerary teeth. The intestinal polyps in GS are predominantly adenomas, with a high potential for malignant transformation [5].

This article presents the typical features of both small and large mandibular peripheral osteomas, with a focus on their surgical management.

Case 1: History/Examination

A 57‐year‐old Hispanic female, with a medical history significant for well‐controlled hypertension and an uneventful cesarean section, presented to the University of Puerto Rico (UPR) Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery (OMFS) outpatient clinic. She denied any history of smoking or substance abuse. Her chief complaint was: “I want this excess bone removed.”

The patient reported that the lesion on the right side of her mandible had been present since she was 27 years old. Despite its large size, the lesion remained asymptomatic, and she denied any pain or numbness. A previous attempt to have it removed at another institution was canceled due to administrative reasons. Encouraged by her children, she sought professional evaluation and treatment.

On physical examination, extraoral fullness of the right cheek was noted Figure 1b. Regional lymph nodes were non‐palpable. Intraorally, a firm, non‐mobile, bony mass covered by smooth, regular pink mucosa was palpated, as shown in Figure 1a. The lesion extended outward from the body of the mandible, distal to the lower right second premolar (#29), and protruded into the mucosa of the right cheek (Figure 1a). The area was partially edentulous, with the absence of the lower right first molar (#30). All remaining posterior teeth were vital with normal pulp, responding positively to cold testing without lingering pain.

Case 1: Differential Diagnosis

A CT scan revealed a large, unilateral, hyperdense, pedunculated, mushroom‐shaped bony growth arising from the buccal cortex of the left mandibular body, measuring approximately 5.5 × 4 × 2.5 cm (Figure 2). 3D reconstruction of maxillofacial CT provided improved visualization of the lesion's size, shape, and anatomical relationship in addition to enhanced surgical planning to minimize damage to adjacent tissues (Figure 3). These clinical and radiologic findings supported a working diagnosis of peripheral osteoma. There were no indications of Gardner's syndrome, which was subsequently ruled out. After discussing the risks, benefits, and alternative treatment options, the patient elected to undergo tumor resection via a transoral approach under IV sedation [6].

Case 1: Treatment

Surgical removal was performed under local anesthesia and IV sedation. The osteoma was completely excised using a reciprocating saw, mallet, bone chisels, and a rotary instrument with a bone bur (Figure 4). A Seldin retractor was consistently used to protect the soft tissues. Postoperative care included cold pack application, systemic antibiotics, NSAIDs, chlorhexidine gluconate 0.12% rinses for 7 days, and a soft diet.

(a) Fully exposed lesion via transoral approach. (b) Clinical view of alveolar bone and soft tissues immediately after excision of the lesion. Photograph courtesy of Dr. Uzkategui.

Case 1: Outcome and Follow‐Up

At the one‐week follow‐up visit, the patient exhibited normal healing without complications, as shown in Figure 5. The excised specimen was sent for histopathological analysis. The pathology report described a white‐tan, cauliflower‐shaped bony mass with an inserted screw, weighing 38 g and measuring 5.5 × 4 × 2.5 (Figure 6). Sectioning revealed a tan, solid, bony structure. An additional cortical bone segment, measuring 2 × 1.5 × 0.5 cm, was also submitted for decalcification and histological examination (RS5; 1: smaller bone segment, 2–5: largest lesion) (Figure 6).

Histopathological analysis confirmed the diagnosis of peripheral mandibular osteoma. Microscopic examination revealed a dense bony mass with minimal marrow tissue (Figure 7).

Case 2: History/Examination

A 58‐year‐old Caucasian male with an unremarkable medical history presented with a hard, spherical mass on the right mandible. The lesion was initially detected by the patient's general dentist during a tooth extraction and was subsequently referred for further evaluation. The mass was asymptomatic, not associated with the patient's chief complaint, and partially obscured by facial hair.

Case 2: Differential Diagnosis

A 3D reconstruction of the patient's skull using CT scans revealed a well‐defined, spherical, bone‐density lesion measuring approximately 1.6 cm in diameter (Figure 8). The radiological characteristics and physical presentation were consistent with an osteoma. Due to the absence of additional osteomas, gastrointestinal polyps, or other associated features, Gardner's syndrome was considered unlikely. After discussing treatment options, surgical removal was recommended, and the patient consented to the procedure.

3‐D reconstruction of maxillofacial CT from a (a) coronal viewpoint, (b) coronal‐sagittal, and (c) submental viewpoint.

Case 2: Treatment and Outcome

The surgery was performed under IV sedation and local anesthesia. A mucosal incision was made to expose the lesion (Figure 9a), which was then carefully excised without complications (Figure 9b). The incision was closed using interrupted sutures, and the excised mass was preserved in a 10% formaldehyde solution for histopathological evaluation. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of an osteoma (Figure 10). The patient tolerated the procedure well, and no postoperative complications were reported.

Discussion

The prevalence and exact cause of osteomas remain unknown. However, peripheral osteomas of the jawbones are considered rare. Søndergaard et al. [7] reported a 4% prevalence of osteomas in patients with ulcerative colitis compared to 2% in a control group. Some studies suggest no clear sex predilection for osteomas [8, 9]. These lesions occur more frequently in the mandible than in the maxilla. Sayan et al. [2] reported that 22.85% of cases were found in the mandible and 14.28% in the maxilla, while Kaplan et al. [10] observed 81.3% of cases in the mandible. Similarly, Chaurasia and Balan [9] reported 83%, and Woldenberg et al. [8] found 64% of cases occurring in the mandible. The most common sites for mandibular osteomas are the lingual surface and the lower border of the mandibular body [8, 11, 12]. However, in rare instances—such as in our case—the lesion may develop on the buccal aspect of the mandibular body [13].

The etiology and pathogenesis of peripheral osteomas also remain uncertain. Both neoplastic and reactive processes have been proposed as potential causes. Kaplan et al. [10, 14] and Woldenberg et al. [8] suggested that some peripheral osteomas may be reactive rather than neoplastic, possibly triggered by trauma. In this case, the extraction of tooth #30 may have been a contributing factor.

Clinically, peripheral osteomas present as unilateral, well‐circumscribed masses ranging from 1 to 4 cm in diameter [8, 9]. These lesions are usually asymptomatic and are often discovered incidentally during routine clinical or radiographic examinations. However, depending on their size and location, they may cause swelling, facial asymmetry, or functional impairment [8, 9, 11, 12, 15, 16]. In most cases, swelling is painless. In our patient, the lesion had grown to a considerable size, resulting in facial asymmetry without other obvious clinical symptoms.

Panoramic radiographs and computed tomography (CT) are commonly used for imaging osteomas. However, as demonstrated in this case, CT provides superior visualization, allowing for precise determination of the lesion's location and extent. Peripheral osteomas are generally easy to identify due to their distinct radiologic features. On imaging, they typically appear as well‐circumscribed, round or oval, mushroom‐shaped radiopaque masses with defined borders. When pedunculated, a narrow contact area can be seen between the lesion and the underlying compact bone. In our case, CT imaging confirmed the lesion as a dense, uniformly opaque compact bone mass with a well‐defined narrow pedicle.

The differential diagnosis of peripheral osteomas includes exostoses, osteoblastoma, osteoid osteoma, late‐stage central ossifying fibroma, and complex odontoma. Biopsy and histopathological analysis are essential for accurate diagnosis. Asymptomatic peripheral osteomas do not typically require removal. Surgical excision is indicated when the lesion grows large enough to cause facial asymmetry or functional impairment [1, 7, 9, 16, 17]. In well‐defined pedunculated peripheral osteomas, surgical removal is usually straightforward. For mandibular osteomas, a transoral surgical approach is preferred over an extraoral approach for cosmetic reasons, as was the case in our patient.

These cases demonstrate that mandibular osteomas, while rare, can be reliably treated with surgical excision. Recognition of their defining features and distinguishing them from other fibro‐osseous lesions, as outlined in the 5th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Head and Neck Tumor, is integral for accurate diagnosis and appropriate intervention [18].

Conclusion

Peripheral osteomas are rare, benign bone tumors that often remain unnoticed until they cause facial asymmetry or functional impairment. CT imaging is essential for accurate diagnosis, and surgical removal is recommended for symptomatic cases. A transoral approach provides effective treatment with minimal cosmetic impact. This case underscores the importance of early detection and timely surgical intervention to prevent potential complications.
