High-performance caffeic acid-based superhydrophilic membranes for efficient removal of microplastics-oil co-contaminants
Authors: Qin Chen, Riri Liu, Wenyi Zhang, Jiale Du, Zijian Yu, Jinhan Chen, Quan Chen, Mengni Ge, Yue Wang, Dong Han Seo, Ming Xie, Raf Dewil, Wenyuan Ye, Jiuyang Lin, Bart Van der Bruggen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microplastic-oil co-contaminants have raised high environmental concerns due to their persistence and synergistic toxicity. However, conventional separation membranes, typically designed for single-pollutant removal, are ineffective against such complex systems. Moreover, the strong interfacial interactions between microplastics and oil promote aggregate formation, leading to severe membrane fouling and compromised separation performance. Herein, we present a robust, antifouling, and superhydrophilic membrane fabricated via sodium periodate-assisted co-deposition of caffeic acid and ε-polylysine for simultaneously removing both oil droplets and microplastic particles from contaminated water. The resulting caffeic acid/ε-polylysine composite superhydrophilic membrane achieves an impressive separation efficacy for various oil-in-water emulsions and polystyrene microplastics of different sizes. Notably, it exhibits superior separation performance for challenging microplastic-oil co-contaminants, with rejection of 99.89% for oil and 96.99% for 100-nm polystyrene microplastics over 300-min continuous filtration. It also sustains a high flux recovery, reflecting robust antifouling properties and thus highlighting a great potential for practical application in complex wastewater treatment. Additionally, the membrane shows a remarkable antibacterial activity even after 30 days of immersion. This study demonstrates a scalable and sustainable strategy for fabricating high-performance polyphenol-based antifouling membranes, offering an effective solution to manage these emerging co-contaminants.

Introduction

The ocean, covering more than 70% of the Earth’s surface, plays an essential role in regulating global hydrological and ecological systems1–3. However, intensified human activities and increasing resource consumption have progressively turned marine environments into terminal sinks for diverse pollutants4–6. Notably, the interaction of different pollutants under tidal and wave forces have led to the emergence of complex co-contaminant systems, such as microplastic-oil mixtures7–9. Microplastics, defined as plastic particles smaller than 5 mm, originate from the breakdown of larger plastic debris or direct production and are now ubiquitous in marine environments10–12. Concurrently, oil pollution, mainly resulting from maritime transportation and spill incidents, represents another pervasive contaminant in the ocean13–15. Although chemical dispersants are widely used to break floating oil into fine droplets for improved dispersion, they also enhance interactions between oil and hydrophobic microplastics16. This unintended effect facilitates the formation of stable microplastic-oil co-contaminants, which exhibit an increased environmental persistence and synergistic toxicity, thereby amplifying ecological risks17–19.

Conventional remediation techniques for oil or microplastics, such as adsorption, oxidation, chemical coagulation, and flocculation, are often energy-intensive, chemically demanding, and prone to generating secondary pollution20–22. Moreover, these approaches typically target single contaminants and lack the flexibility to address complex co-contaminant systems. In comparison, membrane separation technologies offer a high efficiency, low energy consumption, and operational simplicity, making them attractive for the treatment of emerging pollutants23–27. Nevertheless, the accumulation of oil droplets or microplastics onto the membrane surface often leads to severe membrane fouling, markedly reducing permeability and compromising long-term separation performance. In particular, this challenge is exacerbated in the aqueous systems involving microplastic-oil co-contaminants, where hydrophobic microplastics can act as vectors that facilitate oil adhesion onto the membrane surface and even penetration into internal pores, resulting in irreversible membrane fouling28,29. Thus, developing advanced membranes with durable antifouling capabilities is essential for addressing such emerging challenges.

Surface hydrophilization has proven effective in improving membrane antifouling function by forming the hydration layers that repel hydrophobic pollutants30–32. Several approaches, such as functional surface coatings, nanomaterial incorporation, and interfacial engineering, have been developed to improve membrane hydrophilicity for fouling resistance. For example, superhydrophilic ZnO nanoparticle-based sol-gel coatings, MXene-TiO2 composite layers, and hydrophilic MOF-doped membranes have demonstrated an impressive rejection against pollutants with self-cleaning capability33–35. However, these methods often involve multi-step synthesis, nanomaterial handling, or toxic solvents, making them complex, costly and difficult to scale up.

Polyphenol-based co-deposition strategies stand out as a scalable and sustainable approach for effective membrane surface engineering36,37. Specifically, caffeic acid, a naturally derived polyphenol with strong hydrophilicity and biocompatibility, shows an ideal alternative to other plant-derived polyphenol species (e.g., tannic acid, phytic acid, gallic acid), as a sustainable coating precursor38. Its catechol and carboxyl groups enable oxidative self-polymerization under alkaline conditions, forming hydrophilic networks similar to polydopamine-based coatings39. However, its slow oxidative polymerization under mild conditions and insufficient coating stability hinder practical membrane applications38,40. Recent studies suggest that combining polyphenols with amine-rich molecules can enhance coating stability through covalent cross-linking via Michael addition or Schiff base reactions41,42. Moreover, the addition of oxidants can accelerate polymerization kinetics and improve the coating efficiency.

Herein, we present a sodium periodate-triggered co-deposition strategy using caffeic acid and ε-polylysine to fabricate robust antifouling membranes for efficient removal of microplastic-oil co-contaminants. As a natural antibacterial polypeptide rich in hydrophilic amino groups, ε-polylysine serves as an ideal co-deposition monomer to promote the formation of stable, hydrophilic and antibacterial cross-linking networks43,44. Benefiting from the abundant hydrophilic groups and capillary pore structure on the surface, the fabricated membrane firmly fixes water molecules and forms a strong hydration layer that effectively resists the adhesion of oil droplets and microplastics. To validate this antifouling potential, we first evaluated the separation performance of the proposed membrane using oil-in-water emulsions and microplastics suspensions (microplastics size < 500 nm), and subsequently investigated its efficacy in removing microplastic-oil co-contaminants. In addition, its long-term operational stability and antibacterial activity were comprehensively assessed. This study offers a sustainable surface engineering approach to address the challenges posed by emerging complicated co-contaminants and provides fundamental insights into the design of next-generation antifouling membranes for versatile environmental application purposes.

Results

Reaction kinetics of caffeic acid-based solutions

ε-Polylysine, rich in primary amino groups, can interact with catechol moieties in caffeic acid through covalent/non-covalent interactions (Fig. 1a)45,46. Generally, under ambient conditions, the reaction kinetics between polyphenols and amines are typically slow47. As observed in Fig. 1b, the pure CA/ε-Pls solution without sodium periodate (NaIO4) displayed a minimal color change during a 100 min reaction, consistent with its nearly flat UV-Vis absorbance curve (Fig. 1d, e). The slight increase in absorbance at the wavelength of 357 nm is due to the reaction between spontaneously oxidized caffeic acid and ε-polylysine in the presence of trace dissolved oxygen. In contrast, the addition of NaIO4 oxidant as trigger induced a rapid color pattern shift from pale yellow to dark brown (Fig. 1c), as reflected by the increasing absorbance at the wavelengths of 357 nm and 403 nm (Fig. 1d, e), indicating accelerated oxidative polymerization kinetics between caffeic acid and ε-polylysine48,49. Such an enhancement in polymerization kinetics is mainly attributed to the high oxidative potential of NaIO4, which rapidly converts catechol groups into electrophilic o-quinone and semiquinone intermediates even under weakly alkaline conditions50,51. These intermediates undergo Michael addition and Schiff base reactions with ε-polylysine, leading to fast dynamic covalent crosslinking52,53. Accordingly, a slight decrease in absorbance at the wavelength of 403 nm was observed in the later stage of the reaction (Fig. 1e), indicating the gradual consumption of quinone intermediates by amine groups and further confirming the progression of the coupling reaction. Membrane characterization

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to visualize the membrane surface morphology. The pristine polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) substrate exhibited a highly porous structure, which is suboptimal for retaining microplastic-oil co-contaminants (Fig. 2a). After co-deposition of caffeic acid and ε-polylysine, the membrane surface experienced a clear morphological transformation, with progressively narrowed pores observed as co-deposition duration increased (Fig. 2a). A concurrent color shift from white to dark brown further confirmed the evolution of the coating layer on the PVDF substrate (Fig. 2b). Quantitative image analysis revealed that the apparent porosity of the CA/ε-Pls-coated membrane surface decreased sharply from 40.8 ± 2.4% to 3.3 ± 0.9% after 100 min co-deposition (Supplementary Fig. 1), demonstrating that surface structure can be finely tuned by adjusting the co-deposition duration. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) further elucidates the morphology evolution of membrane surface at nanoscale during the co-deposition process (Supplementary Fig. 2). Compared with the rough and porous pristine PVDF substrate, the CA/ε-Pls composite membranes exhibited a smoother and more uniform surface due to continuous coating formation, accompanied by a pronounced reduction in average roughness (Ra) from 110.0 nm for pristine PVDF substrate to 47.6 nm for CA/ε-Pls-1 membrane. Notably, the CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane exhibited a decreased Ra of 36.1 nm. Continuing to extend the deposition time, no significant changes in surface roughness appeared on the CA/ε-Pls-5 membrane surface (Ra = 35.2 nm), indicating the formation of a uniform and cross-linked CA/ε-Pls layer covering the membrane surface, consistent with the SEM observations. The morphological transformation observed in the membrane surface motivated further investigation into its surface chemistry. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was employed to characterize the surface chemical composition (Fig. 2c). The pristine PVDF substrate exhibited intrinsically characteristic peaks at the wavenumbers of 1183 cm−1 and 1403 cm−1, corresponding to the -CF2 stretching and -CH2 scissoring vibrations, respectively56. After the co-deposition of caffeic acid and ε-polylysine, the CA/ε-Pls composite membrane showed three characteristic absorption bands at the wavenumbers of 1640, 1626, and 1538 cm−1, which are attributed to the stretching vibrations of C = O, C = N, and N-H/C-N bonds57–61, respectively. In addition, a new peak at the wavenumber of 2938 cm−1 was observed, designated to the asymmetric stretching vibration of methylene (-CH2) groups in ε-polylysine62, further indicating successful incorporation of the polypeptide component. Broad absorption bands at the wavenumbers range of 3686–3150 cm−1, associated with O-H and N-H stretching, reflected the presence of hydrophilic functional groups on the membrane surface63. Notably, the intensity of O-H and N-H stretching vibrations was enhanced with increasing co-deposition duration, which was consistent with the evolution in surface coating observed by SEM. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to detect surface elemental composition. Compared to the pristine PVDF substrate, the CA/ε-Pls-coated surface exhibited a distinct peak of N 1 s with a diminishing peak of F 1 s, indicating effective coverage by the nitrogen-rich coating (Supplementary Fig. 5). High-resolution C 1 s spectra revealed two additional peaks at the binding energy of 285.4 eV and 287.8 eV (Fig. 2d−i), which were derived from C-N and O-C = O/C = N groups, further validating covalent cross-linking between caffeic acid and ε-polylysine64. The concurrent attenuation of the C-F2 group and intensification of nitrogen-related peaks confirmed gradual coverage by the polyphenol-polypeptide crosslinking network with extended co-deposition.

The membrane surface wettability was evaluated by measuring the water contact angle (WCA) and underwater oil contact angle (UOCA). The pristine PVDF substrate displayed moderate hydrophobicity with a WCA of 94.7 ± 1.2°. Such poor water wetting behavior accordingly causes complete underwater oil-wetting occurred on the surface (UOCA = 0°; Supplementary Movie 1), which is not conducive to oil retention. After the co-deposition of caffeic acid and ε-polylysine, the CA/ε-Pls-coated membranes exhibited markedly enhanced hydrophilicity and underwater superoleophobicity (Fig. 3a–c). For instance, after 20-min co-deposition, a water droplet was fully absorbed within 11.5 s, and the UOCA increased to 152.2 ± 2.1° for the fabricated CA/ε-Pls-1 membrane. Extending the co-deposition duration progressively enhanced membrane wettability due to the continuous introduction of hydrophilic groups. Meanwhile, AFM analysis reveals the reduced surface roughness of the CA/ε-Pls composite membranes, which can potentially suppress interfacial air entrapment and increase the effective solid-liquid contact area for facilitating rapid water spreading on the membrane surface. Notably, with an 80 min co-deposition decoration, the WCA of the fabricated membrane (i.e., CA/ε-Pls-4) dropped from 19.3 ± 0.5° to 0° within 2.1 s, yielding an ultrahigh UOCA of 166.1 ± 1.9°. Membrane separation performance for single-component contaminants

Pure water flux measurements under gravity-driven filtration (ca. 10 cm height) showed that increasing the co-deposition duration from 20 to 100 min led to a gradual decline in water flux from 1262.6 ± 26.4 to 590.4 ± 22.2 L·m−2·h−1 (Supplementary Fig. 6). Such a decline was primarily attributed to narrowed water transport pathways and increased hydraulic resistance induced by decreasing pore size after the co-deposition. Despite this, the membrane with a co-deposition duration of 80 min (i.e., CA/ε-Pls-4) maintained a satisfactory pure water flux (i.e., 682.3 ± 27.7 L·m−2·h−1). Collectively, the CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane represents the optimal balance between pore structure, wettability, and water permeability, and was therefore employed for subsequent separation tests. In addition, zeta potential measurements revealed that the CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane was negatively charged under neutral conditions (Supplementary Fig. 7). Likewise, both emulsified oil and polystyrene (PS) microplastics presented negative surface charges in aqueous solution (Fig. 4a). The separation performance of the CA/ε-Pls-coated membranes was evaluated using both oil-in-water emulsions (i.e., surfactant-free and surfactant-stabilized emulsions) and microplastic suspensions (microplastic size < 500 nm). The CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane exhibited 99.99% oil rejection for surfactant-free oil-in-water emulsions (Supplementary Fig. 8), which can be attributed to the continuous coalescence of oil droplets that enhances the steric exclusion effect during filtration. In contrast, in the presence of surfactants, oil droplets are dispersed into smaller and more stable entities due to surfactant adsorption at the oil-water interface (Supplementary Fig. 9). Despite this increased stability, the CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane still achieved > 99.92% oil rejection when filtrating SDS-stabilized oil-in-water emulsions (Fig. 4b). Optical microscopy and dynamic light scattering analysis (Supplementary Fig. 9) revealed a complete absence of visible oil droplets in the permeate, confirming effective oil removal. The CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane also delivered a high flux of 585.1 ± 15.6 L·m−2·h−1 for petroleum ether-in-water emulsions during gravity-driven filtration, demonstrating its promising potential for energy-efficient oil/water separation. Long-term filtration tests revealed impressive separation stability, with negligible reduction in oil rejection from 99.92% to 99.90% and consistently stable permeation flux of 558.6 ± 17.8 L·m−2·h−1 (Fig. 4c). Furthermore, a flux recovery of 93.95% for the CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane was yielded after water rinsing (Fig. 4i). To elucidate the underlying separation mechanism, a wetting model based on the breakthrough pressure (ΔP) was employed (Fig. 4d)65. ΔP defines the minimum pressure required for a liquid to penetrate membrane pores and depends on surface tension, contact angle, and pore radius66,67. As previously analyzed, the CA/ε-Pls-coated membranes exhibit superhydrophilicity in the air, resulting in a negative ΔP for water, thereby enabling spontaneous infiltration. In contrast, the UOCA exceeded 150°, corresponding to a positive ΔP that prevents oil penetration without external pressure. Furthermore, the gradual reduction in pore size with increasing co-deposition duration enhanced the capillary effect (Supplementary Fig. 1), intensifying selective water transport and thus effective oil repellency. In addition, electrostatic repulsion occurred between the membrane surface and the emulsified oil droplets facilitates efficient oil separation.

The separation performance of the CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane toward microplastics was assessed using polystyrene (PS) microsphere suspensions (particle size of 100 nm, 200 nm, and 500 nm). As illustrated in Fig. 4e, f, the membrane displayed high rejection rates of 92.43, 94.96, and 96.13%, with corresponding fluxes of 416.2 ± 18.2, 480.4 ± 19.7 and 591.7 ± 18.8 L·m−2·h−1 for 100, 200 and 500 nm PS microspheres, respectively. These results reflect a synergistic microplastic separation mechanism: (i) The narrowed pore structure physically blocks microsphere from penetration, while the superhydrophilic coating forms a hydration layer that inhibits PS microsphere from adhesion and accumulation35,68; (ii) both the CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane and the microplastics exhibit negative surface charges (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 7), inducing electrostatic repulsion that further reduces particle deposition and enhances separation efficiency. During the continuous filtration, a slight reduction in permeation flux with increasing rejection efficiency was observed (Fig. 4e, f), attributed to the progressive accumulation of microspheres on the membrane surface, which remarkably reduced the effective pore channels and increased mass transfer resistance. Notably, the rejection of 500 nm PS microspheres approached 100% after 60-min filtration, indicating dynamic enhancement of the sieving effect.

To evaluate the membrane antifouling performance, flux recovery test of the CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane was performed after water rinsing (Fig. 4i). The CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane achieved appreciable flux recovery values of 58.95, 68.29, and 81.82% for 100, 200, and 500 nm PS microspheres, respectively. The increasing trend in flux recovery with particle size suggests that larger particles were more easily removed from the membrane surface, while smaller ones were more likely to enter and clog internal pores, hindering effective cleaning (Fig. 4g, h)69–71. Such an inference was further evinced by SEM observations of the membrane surface after water rinsing, which revealed that 100 nm PS microspheres were deeply embedded into the membrane surface, whereas larger PS microspheres were removed to a large extent (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Compared to single-component PS suspensions, the membrane exhibited enhanced rejection efficiencies of 93.92% and 97.32% for 100 nm and 500 nm PS microspheres in PS-oil co-contaminants, respectively (Fig. 5b). Competitive adsorption of microplastics and oil droplets for surfactant molecules likely induced emulsion destabilization, after which hydrophobic interactions overcame electrostatic repulsion and promoted aggregation. The resulting aggregates exhibited increased apparent particle sizes and more negative surface charges (Fig. 4a), thereby enhancing retention through size exclusion, hydrophilic defense and electrostatic repulsion mechanisms (Fig. 5g). Dynamic light scattering measurement confirmed this aggregation behavior, with particle size increasing from ~ 103 nm in pure PS suspensions to ~243 nm in the PS-oil mixture (Fig. 5c)72,73. In addition to efficient PS removal, the membranes maintained > 99.71% oil rejection efficiency with stable permeation flux (> 393.7 ± 15.1 L·m−2·h−1) throughout the filtration process (Fig. 5a). Flux recovery tests were further conducted to assess the antifouling performance of the CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane in the PS-oil co-contaminants systems (Fig. 5d). Compared to pure PS suspensions, the CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane showed slightly lower flux recovery after filtration of the 500-nm PS-oil co-contaminants system, possibly due to partial irreversible fouling caused by surface-adhered clusters. In contrast, the filtration of the 100 nm PS-oil co-contaminants system resulted in an elevated flux recovery of 74.82% (Fig. 5d), attributed to the enlarged aggregate size that reduced deep pore intrusion and enabled efficient removal during cleaning. In addition, the antifouling capability of the membrane was further evaluated using two typical foulants: humic acid and bovine serum albumin (BSA), respectively. The CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane exhibited high flux recoveries of 89.36% and 80.29% for humic acid and BSA, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 11), demonstrating effective resistance to organic fouling caused by natural organic matters and protein.

Long-term separation performance was assessed through filtration of the 100 nm PS-oil co-contaminant system (Fig. 5e). After 300 min continuous filtration, the CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane maintained the rejections of 96.99% and 99.89% for 100-nm PS microspheres and oil, respectively (Fig. 5f). It also exhibited a stable permeation flux, decreasing slightly from 456.9 ± 14.4 to 390.6 ± 19.1 L·m−2·h−1, with a flux recovery ratio of 72.63% (Fig. 5d), implying a remarkable operational durability. The chemical stability of the fabricated CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane was assessed under varied pH and salinity conditions. After 72 h exposure, the CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane retained comparable separation performance in all tested environments (Supplementary Fig. 12). A moderate decline in 100 nm PS rejection was observed under acidic conditions (Supplementary Fig. 12), which is attributed to acid-induced hydrolysis of Schiff base bonds and partial delamination of the CA/ε-Pls coating layer74–76. In contrast, oil rejection remained largely unaffected, because of oil separation being dominated by size exclusion and hydration-layer-induced underwater superoleophobicity, which are less sensitive to minor coating degradation (Supplementary Fig. 12). Generally, in realistic wastewater scenarios, where the pH typically ranges from neutral to slightly alkaline77, the membrane demonstrated a robust structural integrity and consistently high separation efficiency, ensuring effective retention of nanosized plastic particles. In addition, the durability of the CA/ε-Pls composite membrane under prolonged exposure in organic solvents was evaluated (Supplementary Fig. 13). After immersion in petroleum ether, n-hexane, and kerosene for 6 days, the membrane retained its superhydrophilicity and superior underwater oleophobicity, with underwater oil contact angles remaining around 165° (Supplementary Fig. 13). These results indicate that no pronounced swelling, dissolution, or interfacial reconstruction occurs in the CA/ε-Pls coating during extended organic solvent exposure, further verifying the structural integrity and chemical stability of the crosslinked CA/ε-Pls network.

In addition to chemical resistance, the CA/ε-Pls composite membrane was evaluated for its mechanical durability. The membrane maintained stable permeation flux and high rejection against oil and 100-nm PS microsphere after 30 min of water flushing at a flow rate of 1.38 m·s−1 (Supplementary Fig. 14). Mechanical deformation tests further showed that the membrane retained high separation performance, with oil rejection remaining above 99.80% after repeated bending, indicating that the CA/ε-Pls composite coating withstands substantial mechanical deformation without pronounced cracking or delamination from the PVDF substrate. The CA/ε-Pls composite membrane also preserved impressive separation performance after ultrasonication at an intensity of 80 W for 30 min, further demonstrating the robustness of the crosslinked coating against severe mechanical perturbations. Furthermore, the CA/ε-Pls composite membrane exhibited robust separation performance at different temperatures (10, 25 and 50 °C) and salinities (0.0 and 3.6 wt.% NaCl), with consistently high oil rejection and only a modest reduction in 100-nm PS rejection under saline conditions (Supplementary Fig. 15). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the CA/ε-Pls coating endows the membrane with robust mechanical durability and environmental tolerance, showing a great potential in a practical water treatment application scenario.

To further assess the scalability of this surface modification strategy, a scaled-up membrane with a dimension of 33.0 cm × 20.0 cm was fabricated using the same co-deposition protocol (Fig. 5h). The scaled-up membrane (effective area of 660.00 cm2) exhibited a permeation flux of 447.2 ± 25.8 L·m−2·h−1 and high rejection efficiencies (93.95% for PS microspheres and 99.80% for oil droplets) (Fig. 5i), which matches with the filtration performance of the CA/ε-Pls-4 composite membrane (effective area of 12.56 cm2). The consistently superior separation performance of the scaled-up CA/ε-Pls composite membrane demonstrates the practicability of the CA/ε-Pls co-deposition strategy for fabricating defect-free, high-performance superhydrophilic membranes on a large scale.

Antibacterial performance

The antibacterial performance of the fabricated CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane was evaluated using Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) as representative Gram-negative and Gram-positive strains, respectively (Fig. 6). The CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane exhibited remarkably enhanced the bactericidal capability, achieving an antibacterial efficiency of above 93.4% compared with the control (30.0%) (Fig. 6a). An equivalent antibacterial efficacy was observed against S. aureus, with an inhibition above 97.0% (Fig. 6b). Notably, this remarkable antibacterial efficacy of the membrane toward both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria strains remained stable even after 30-day water immersion, demonstrating a superior long-term durability. Beyond contact-killing capacity, the CA/ε-Pls-4 composite membrane also showed a superior rejection of bacteria. After filtration of E. coli and S. aureus suspensions, no detectable colonies in the permeate were observed, achieving the bacterial rejections of ca. 100% (Supplementary Fig. 16) and thus confirming the effectiveness of the CA/ε-Pls composite membrane in physically blocking the penetration of bacteria. Such strong bacteria inactivation and separation efficacy of the membrane were associated with the synergistic functionality of caffeic acid and ε-polylysine within the coating layer (Fig. 6c). Caffeic acid has a broad-spectrum antibacterial activity due to its phenolic hydroxyl groups and conjugated aromatic structure, which can disrupt bacterial metabolism and integrity44,78. Meanwhile, ε-polylysine electrostatically interacts with bacterial cell membranes, initiating a “contact-killing” behavior on the membrane surface39. Upon bacterial attachment, the cross-linked CA/ε-Pls composite network disrupts cell membrane integrity, induces cytoplasmic leakage, and inhibits enzyme activity, ultimately leading to cell death79. In addition to the contact-killing effect, the structural attributes of the CA/ε-Pls composite membrane further enhance its antibacterial performance. The narrowed pore structure enables efficient steric exclusion of bacterial cells during filtration, thereby limiting their transmembrane transport. Simultaneously, the hydration layer associated with the superhydrophilic surface suppresses bacterial adhesion and subsequent biofilm formation. Discussion

Microplastic-oil co-contaminants pose a substantial threat to marine ecosystems because of their increasing prevalence and synergistic toxicity17–19. Moreover, the strong interfacial affinity between microplastics and oil leads to severe membrane fouling, adversely affecting the membrane separation performance. In this study, we developed a robust antifouling superhydrophilic membrane that is capable of simultaneously gravity-driven separation of oil droplets and microplastics. This high-performance membrane was fabricated via a sodium periodate-triggered co-deposition strategy using caffeic acid and ε-polylysine as functional precursors. The implementation of sodium periodate facilitated rapid oxidative crosslinking between caffeic acid and ε-polylysine, enabling efficient membrane fabrication under mild conditions.

The resulting caffeic acid/ε-polylysine composite superhydrophilic membrane (i.e., CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane) exhibited efficient separation performance for diverse contaminated systems. For single-component systems, it achieved > 99.92% oil rejection for various oil-in-water emulsions and efficiently retained PS microplastics with varying particle sizes. In microplastic-oil co-contaminant systems, the CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane experienced an oil rejection of 99.89% and 100 nm PS rejection of 96.99% after extended filtration. Furthermore, the CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane demonstrated impressive antifouling properties with high flux recovery for microplastic-oil co-contaminants. These results were attributed to the synergistic size exclusion, protective hydration layer and electrostatic repulsion, which collectively facilitated selective water permeation while resisting oil intrusion and microplastic adhesion. Moreover, benefiting from the synergistic antibacterial effects of caffeic acid and ε-polylysine, as well as the anti-adhesive hydrophilic layer, the membrane showed potent and durable antibacterial activity (> 93.4%) against E. coli after 30 days of immersion. Compared with previously reported superhydrophilic composite membranes that are typically designed for single-component pollutants, the CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane exhibits a distinct advantage in versatility. It effectively separates multiple types of contaminants, including oil-in-water emulsions, microplastic suspensions, and microplastic-oil co-contaminants, while maintaining a high separation efficiency, satisfactory permeability, and good antifouling properties (Supplementary Table 1), highlighting the separation efficacy of the CA/ε-Pls composite coating layer.

Beyond separation performance, the caffeic acid/ε-polylysine co-deposition strategy also proved highly versatile, enabling successful superhydrophilization of various membrane substrates with distinct wettability and underwater superoleophobicity (Supplementary Fig. 17). From an environmental sustainability perspective, however, the choice of substrate requires prudent consideration. In this work, PVDF was selected to ensure robustness and to facilitate comparison with most reported superhydrophilic composite membranes that employ PVDF as the substrate. Nevertheless, as a fluoropolymer with high bond energy, PVDF is resistant to biodegradation, raising concerns for long-term sustainability80,81. Future work will therefore focus on developing environmentally friendly substrates to achieve sustainable membrane fabrication. From a practical application perspective, the present study focuses on controlled model systems, and the performance of the CA/ε-Pls membrane under more realistic conditions, such as in the presence of highly viscous or aged oils and complex particle-rich water matrices, remains to be explored. In addition, microplastics in natural water matrices are highly heterogeneous in size, morphology, and surface properties, which may further influence separation and fouling behavior. Therefore, future work will be conducted under conditions involving real wastewater or environmental water samples to assess long-term fouling behavior and operational stability of the superhydrophilic CA/ε-Pls membrane. In parallel, integration into existing filtration modules or hybrid treatment systems may further enable decentralized or in situ water remediation. Overall, this study establishes a robust antifouling membrane for tackling emerging co-contaminant challenges in aquatic environments.

Methods

Materials

Non-woven polyester fabrics (Freudenberg, Germany) were used as the supporting substrate. Poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) polymer (Solef® 1015, purity ≥ 99%, Mn = 238, 000 Da) was obtained from Solvay (Germany). Caffeic acid (CA, purity ≥ 99%), N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP), n-hexane (purity ≥ 99%), kerosene (purity ≥ 99%), petroleum ether (purity ≥ 99%), sodium periodate (NaIO4, purity ≥ 99%), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, purity ≥ 99%), and tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane (Tris) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Belgium). ε-Polylysine (ε-Pls, purity ≥ 95%) was supplied by Handary S.A. (Belgium). Bovine serum albumin (BSA, purity ≥ 98%, lyophilized powder) purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (China) was used as a representative protein foulant. Humic acid (purity ≥ 90%) purchased from Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co., Ltd. (China) was employed as a model natural organic foulant. Commercial peanut oil was acquired from a local supermarket. Polystyrene (PS) microspheres with diameters of 100, 200 and 500 nm were purchased from Taoyu International Trading Co., Ltd. (China). In addition, commercial PVDF, polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) and polysulfone (PSF) membranes were employed as substrates to assess the universality of the coating (Supplementary Methods 1).

Fabrication of the caffeic acid/ε-polylysine (CA/ε-Pls) composite membranes

CA/ε-Pls composite membranes were fabricated via a one-step co-deposition method at room temperature (Fig. 1a). Pristine PVDF substrates were first prepared using a non-solvent phase inversion method. Briefly, 3.42 g of PVDF powder was dissolved in 80 mL of NMP and continuously stirred at 80 °C until a clear casting solution was obtained. The solution was uniformly cast onto a nonwoven fabric substrate and spread using a casting knife with a thickness of 300 μm. Subsequently, the coated substrate was immediately immersed in a nonsolvent coagulation bath to induce phase separation, yielding the pristine PVDF substrate. These PVDF substrates were then fixed in a custom-designed circular mould for surface modification. To prepare the modification solution, caffeic acid (3.0 g·L−1), ε-polylysine (1.5 g·L−1) and sodium periodate (1.25 g·L−1) were dissolved in a Tris-HCl buffer solution (pH 8.5). The solution was then transferred to the mould to initiate the co-deposition of caffeic acid and ε-polylysine onto the PVDF substrate surface. After a designated co-deposition duration, the surface-coated membranes were taken out and thoroughly rinsed with deionized water. Based on the co-deposition duration (i.e., 20, 40, 60, 80 and 100 min), the resulting membranes were denoted as CA/ε-Pls-1, CA/ε-Pls-2, CA/ε-Pls-3, CA/ε-Pls-4 and CA/ε-Pls-5, respectively. Furthermore, to assess the scalability of this surface modification strategy, a composite membrane with a large area (i.e., 660.00 cm2) was fabricated using the same co-deposition protocol. All membrane performance tests in this study were conducted three times.

Membrane characterization

The surface morphology of the CA/ε-Pls composite membranes was detected using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, XL30 FEG, Netherlands) operated at an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV. Average pore size and apparent porosity were quantified via image analysis using Image J software. The surface topography and roughness of the membranes were further examined by atomic force microscopy (AFM, Bruker Dimension Icon, Germany), which was operated in tapping mode over a scan area of 5 µm × 5 µm. The acquired images with the arithmetic mean roughness (Ra) and average pore depth were processed using NanoScope Analysis 2.0 software. Surface chemical functional groups were characterized by attenuated total reflectance Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR, PerkinElmer Spectrum 100, Germany) at the spectral range of 650–4000 cm−1. Surface elemental compositions were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα source. Prior to FTIR and XPS analysis, the membrane samples were dried at room temperature. The zeta potential of the CA/ε-Pls membrane was determined using an electrokinetic analyzer (SurPASS 30, Anton Paar, Austria) in 0.001 mol·L−1 potassium chloride solution at different pH values. Surface wettability was evaluated by measuring the static water contact angle (WCA) and underwater oil contact angle (UOCA) using a dynamic contact angle goniometer (OCA 20, Dataphysics Instruments, Germany). Oil adhesion resistance was evaluated via a spray test using Sudan III-stained petroleum ether and by observing the adhesion and detachment behavior of dichloromethane droplets on the membrane surface. The size distribution of dispersed contaminants, including oil droplets, PS microspheres, and their aggregates, was determined using a dynamic light scattering (DLS) analyzer (DLS13-320, Beckman Coulter, USA).

Preparation of synthetic wastewater

Oil-in-water emulsions were prepared by mixing oil and deionized water at a volume ratio of 1:99 (v/v) in the presence of SDS (0.02 g·L−1) as a surfactant. The mixtures were homogenized using a high-speed agitator at 10,000 rpm for 30 min to ensure emulsion stability. Four representative oil species, including n-hexane, petroleum ether, kerosene, and peanut oil, were selected to evaluate the separation performance of the CA/ε-Pls composite membranes during the filtration of surfactant-stabilized oil-in-water emulsions. For separation performance comparison, a surfactant-free petroleum-ether-in-water emulsion prepared under the same conditions was employed as the feed. Microplastic-contaminated wastewater was prepared by dispersing PS microspheres of different particle sizes (100 nm, 200 nm, and 500 nm) in deionized water at a concentration of 0.01 g·L−1. The suspensions were sonicated for 30 min to achieve uniform dispersion. For the preparation of microplastic-oil co-contaminant mixtures, 5 mL of petroleum ether, 5 mL of PS suspension (100 nm PS, 1.0 g·L−1), and 0.01 g of SDS were added into 490 mL of deionized water and homogenized at 10,000 rpm for 30 min. In addition, 100-nm PS-oil co-contaminant mixtures were prepared at different temperatures (10, 25 and 50 °C) and salinities (0.0 and 3.6 wt.% NaCl) conditions using the same protocol.

In addition, humic acid and bovine serum albumin (BSA) were employed as model natural organic matter and protein foulants to evaluate the antifouling performance of the CA/ε-Pls composite membrane. A stock humic acid solution was prepared by dissolving humic acid powder in deionized water under alkaline conditions (pH ≈ 10-11, adjusted with NaOH), followed by neutralization with HCl and diluted to 50 mg·L−1. The BSA solution was prepared by dissolving protease-free bovine serum albumin (purity ≥ 98%) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), which was diluted to a concentration of 500 mg·L−1. All solutions were stored at 4 °C prior to antifouling tests.

Long-term stability and chemical durability tests

The long-term separation stability of the CA/ε-Pls composite membranes was assessed using different feed solutions, including SDS-stabilized petroleum ether-in-water emulsions, PS microplastic suspensions of varying particle sizes (100, 200, and 500 nm), and microplastic-oil co-contaminant mixtures. After each separation cycle, the membrane was briefly rinsed with deionized water to remove residual foulants.

The chemical durability of the membranes under harsh environments was also investigated. Specifically, the membranes were immersed in acidic (pH 3), alkaline (pH 10), and saline (4 wt.% and 10 wt.% NaCl) solutions at room temperature for 72 h. After exposure, the membranes were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water to remove residual chemicals. The separation performance of the treated membranes was then evaluated following the same protocol, as described for the co-contaminant systems. In addition, the CA/ε-Pls-4 membrane was immersed in petroleum ether, n-hexane, and kerosene for 6 days at room temperature to examine its stability in organic solvents. After immersion, the membrane was thoroughly rinsed with deionized water and air-dried, and the wettability was subsequently characterized.

Antibacterial performance test

The antibacterial activity of the CA/ε-Pls membrane was evaluated using both Escherichia coli (E. coli) DH5α (a representative Gram-negative strain) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) ATCC 25923 (a representative Gram-positive strain). E. coli was first cultured in Lennox lysogeny broth (LB) at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 10 h, and the resulting bacterial suspension was diluted to a concentration of 6.3 × 107 CFU·mL−1 for subsequent testing. S. aureus was cultured at the same conditions and diluted to 3.7 × 107 CFU·mL−1 for testing. Membrane samples (a dimension of 2 cm × 3 cm) were sterilized by ultraviolet irradiation for 1 h prior to use. For the antibacterial performance test, 5 ml of LB medium containing 100 μL of the diluted bacterial suspension was transferred into individual sterile glass tubes. A membrane sample was carefully placed into each tube, ensuring full contact with the bacterial suspension. The tubes were then incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 4 h to allow sufficient interaction between the membrane surface and bacterial cells. After incubation, 100 μL of the suspension was withdrawn, serially diluted with fresh LB medium and plated on LB agar. After overnight incubation at 37 °C, colony-forming units (CFUs) were counted to assess bacterial viability.

Statistics and reproducibility
