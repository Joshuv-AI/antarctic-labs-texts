Simulation modeling of neighborhood-level differences in life course health outcomes: a proof of concept
Authors: Jarrod E Dalton, Paul R Gunsalus, Adam T Perzynski, Lyla Mourany, Elizabeth R Pfoh, Madeleine M Blazel, Douglas D Gunzler, Kathryn A Martinez, Glen B Taksler, Michelle Putnam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesAdults in low-resource neighborhoods face greater burden of preventable diseases than the general population. Methodologies to measure the impact of population-level efforts to reduce neighborhood-level disparities in health outcomes are lacking. We introduce a simulation modeling approach for incident disease and mortality risk over the life course and apply the model to assess the impact of a hypothetical population health initiative to reduce neighborhood-level disparities.Research Design and MethodsUsing electronic health records, we constructed a probabilistic dynamic systems model that simulated the life course of each patient in a large primary care population, taking into account specific relationships by sex and Area Deprivation Index (ADI) quintile. The model predicted long-term incidence of 10 chronic conditions and all-cause mortality.ResultsThe model was reliable, with strong discrimination (C-statistic for 10-year mortality: 0.871) and calibration. Predicted median life expectancy was nearly a decade lower for patients ages 40-45 who resided in the highest (ADI quintile 5) vs the lowest (quintile 1) deprivation neighborhoods (difference [95% CI]; women, 8.6 [8.2, 9.0] years; men, 10.0 [9.6, 10.3]. A hypothetical initiative to reduce the number of smokers by 10% had minimal effect on disparities in life expectancy when implemented in the general patient population (women, −0.4 years; men, −0.2), but meaningfully narrowed disparities when focused on high-deprivation neighborhoods (women, −0.8 years; men, −0.6).Discussion and ImplicationsIn a broad patient population, it was feasible to measure socioeconomic disparities in health outcomes and life expectancy and evaluate interventions to improve health equity.Keywords: Population health, Social drivers of health, Longitudinal analysis, Life expectancy, Electronic health records

Modeling life course health outcomes is complicated due to complex and co-evolving relationships impacting multiple incident diseases and mortality pressures over time, which manifest heterogeneously within and among populations. We present a methodology, based on probabilistic system dynamics modeling approaches, to characterize coevolutionary incident disease and mortality risk trajectories in heterogeneous populations. We apply the developed model to demonstrate how counterfactual outcomes of population-focused interventions in mid-life can be evaluated.

Introduction

In the United States, the poorest 1% of individuals have a 14-year shorter life expectancy than the richest 1% of individuals.1 It is widely recognized that socioeconomically disadvantaged individuals are disproportionately affected by smoking, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, type 2 diabetes, and other chronic diseases compared to the general population.2–7 From 1999 to 2020, the prevalence of cigarette use in low-income adults was stagnant (33%) but fell from 19% to 12% in high-income adults.8 Similarly, just half of low-income adults with diabetes achieved glycemic control, as compared with three-quarters of high-income adults.8 Studies have also found that uninsured adults have poorer hypertension control than those with insurance.9 Often, efforts to control such health conditions are implemented at a clinical level. However, although clinicians deliver evidence-based interventions to specific patients based on their risk factors, many patients with social drivers of health (SDOH)-related risks may also benefit from broader population health initiatives, such as enhanced care coordination or group nutrition and exercise classes. Often, these programs are provided by health systems as a complement to existing clinical care. Yet, little is known about their effect on long-term health outcomes.

A mathematical model to reproduce disparities in health and life expectancy (LE) across neighborhoods would improve understanding of whether population-level interventions are likely to achieve their stated goals.10 It may also improve identification of patients subject to SDOH-related risks using electronic health records (EHRs).11

We developed and demonstrated the application of a methodological approach, leveraging probabilistic system dynamics modeling techniques, to begin to address this gap. We aimed to identify subpopulations at the highest risk of SDOH-related health outcomes, quantify relationships between SDOH and chronic disease development over the life course, and evaluate resulting disparities in LE. We then modeled a hypothetical population-based health initiative to understand the potential differential impact by patient SDOH level.

Methods

Data sources and inclusion criteria

We obtained EHR data for a cohort of 558,055 individuals who were seen at Cleveland Clinic Health System (CCHS). CCHS has a large academic medical center, 13 regional hospitals, 21 family health centers, and >75 outpatient locations. About 95% of primary care patients reside in the local area, and many receive a majority of their healthcare at CCHS facilities. For this study, we included patients ≥40 years who had an internal or family medicine visit in Ohio, and also lived in Ohio, between January 1, 2007, and September 24, 2021. The first visit was designated as the study baseline. To limit our study to the patients who were most likely to have complete EHR data, we focused on patients with ongoing primary care,12 defined as ≥2 internal/family medicine encounters at CCHS within a 2-year window. The first of these encounters was considered the baseline date. The final encounter was considered the latest date without any subsequent encounter within 2 years. We excluded patients with missing self-reported race/ethnicity and, to maintain adequate sample sizes, restricted analyses to non-Hispanic White (“White”), non-Hispanic Black (“Black”), non-Hispanic Asian (“Asian”), and Hispanic (95.2% of patients).

To externally validate our results, we also obtained EHR data for a cohort of 74,421 individuals who were seen at a different health system, which is headquartered 8 miles from CCHS’ main campus. Specifically, MetroHealth System (MHS) is a public health system that operates 4 hospitals, >20 health centers, and 40 other locations. In contrast to CCHS’ environment where most primary care patients are commercially insured, approximately two-thirds of MHS patients are uninsured or covered by Medicaid. Eligibility criteria for the external validation cohort were the same as above. The study was approved by the Cleveland Clinic’s Institutional Review Board (#22-896), with reliant approval by MHS.

Study variables

We modeled incident disease of 10 prevalent conditions: hypertension (HTN), hyperlipidemia (HLD), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), myocardial infarction (MI), stroke, congestive heart failure (CHF), chronic kidney disease (CKD), cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD). Diseases were classified by ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes, which were harmonized using the National Library of Medicine’s Unified Medical Language System ontology.13 All-cause mortality was obtained from EHRs, the Ohio Department of Health, and the Social Security Death Master File. Other covariates included sex, race/ethnicity, US Census block group-level Area Deprivation Index (ADI) quintile14 (1 = lowest neighborhood deprivation; 5 = highest), partner status (current, former, single, widowed, unknown), smoking status (current, former, never, unknown), and previous diagnosis with each of the 10 conditions. The ADI is a composite measure of neighborhood characteristics, including income, education, employment, and housing quality.10 We defined quintile cutpoints based on ADI for all census block groups in Ohio, via the sociome R package and the American Community Survey 2011-2015.15,16

Analytic methods

We used R version 4.3.1 for all analyses. First, we randomly allocated CCHS patients to training and internal validation cohorts (80%/20% ratio), stratified by sex and baseline age group (5-year increments, 40-44 to 85-89 years; ≥90 years).

Competing risk models of disease incidence

To characterize the 5-year probability of incident disease for each of the 10 conditions (conditional on survival), we estimated stratified, cause-specific Cox regression models by condition, sex, and age group. Covariates in each regression were determined based on the number of observed outcomes (for a given condition, within a particular sub-cohort of patients of a given sex and age category at baseline) available within the CCHS training cohort within 5 years of baseline. Supplementary Figure 1 provides details. Incident diagnoses were not modeled for patients ≥90 years. For each patient, data were right-censored after 2 years with no primary care encounters (beginning of the period for incident disease, end of the period for deaths), to reduce bias in underestimating risk due to attrition. The resulting probabilistic models were incorporated as individual components in our life course simulation model, described below.

Life course simulation model development

We developed a probabilistic dynamic systems model, called a dynamic Bayesian network,17 to characterize variation in incident disease and mortality risk across the life course (see Author Note 1). Supplementary Figure 2 provides a depiction of the overall model structure. The model was stratified by sex and age group. Within each strata and condition, the model applied EHR-derived variables to predict new-onset disease and mortality.

To estimate all-cause mortality risk, we estimated Cox regression models for the 5-year probability of death in each sex and age group. Covariates for these models included ADI quintile and previous diagnosis with each of the 10 conditions (yes/no). There were no exclusions based on prior diagnosis. For patients ≥90 years, we estimated a parametric Weibull survival model with sex, race/ethnicity, ADI quintile, partner status, smoking status, and total number of previous diagnoses with the 10 conditions as predictors.

The probabilistic dynamic system17,18 simulated the risk of chronic disease diagnosis and mortality over the remaining life course in 2 separate cohorts: the 20% internal validation cohort of CCHS patients and the external validation cohort of MHS patients. The 2-step model alternated between simulating new diagnoses of disease over the next 5 years (from the cause-specific Cox regression models described above) and applying the accrued disease diagnoses to predict the next 5 years of annual survival (from the Cox regression models for mortality), until age 85-89 years. Thus, the model dynamically adapted survival risk predictions to account for the likelihood of developing chronic disease throughout the life course. Predicted survival ≥90 years was obtained from the parametric Weibull model. Thus, for each patient, we obtained conditional health outcome and survival estimates for each 5-year time period, from baseline age until death (maximum age, 115 years).

Composite survival was measured as the cumulative product of conditional age-specific survival curves, and predicted LE was obtained as the integral of the composite survival curve. To account for uncertainty in predictions of chronic disease patterns over patients’ remaining life course, we employed 30 simulations per patient in a validation cohort and averaged the results.

Model validation

Using data from the internal and external validation cohorts, we estimated time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values associated with predictions of 5-year incident disease diagnosis for each of the ten conditions and with predictions of 10-year composite survival.19,20 Model calibration was assessed graphically.

Scenario analysis: predicted impacts of neighborhood smoking reduction initiatives

Smoking is an example of a health indicator that a) has major impacts on the risk of chronic disease development over the life course and b) is disproportionately prevalent among neighborhoods across the socioeconomic spectrum. We applied 2 hypothetical population health initiatives to the CCHS cohort. The first initiative sought to reduce the number of smokers aged 40-49 years by 10% in the general patient population. The second initiative had the same goal but was limited to patients residing in census block groups with the highest quintile of ADI. For each initiative, we randomly selected 10% of smokers aged 40-49 years to quit, stratified by sex and (for the first initiative) quintile of ADI.

Results

We included 605,990 patients. CCHS training and internal validation cohorts contained 425,198 and 106,371 patients, respectively, and the MHS external validation cohort contained 74,421 patients.

The overall CCHS cohort was 56.4% female with a median baseline age of 57 (interquartile range, 49-67) years. In both sexes, the higher deprivation neighborhoods had more patients identifying as Black or Hispanic and as current smokers (Table 1). Across ADI quintiles, men had a higher prevalence of CHF, CKD, HLD, MI, and T2DM than women. A similar trend was observed for HTN in ADI quintiles 1 (least deprivation)-4. The MHS cohort generally had lower neighborhood socioeconomic position, as reflected by the ADI quintile distribution, and relatively higher prevalence of HF, COPD, MI, and stroke at study baseline than CCHS patients (Supplementary Table 1).

Within the CCHS internal validation cohort, composite survival estimates at 10 years based on the average of 30 simulation runs for each patient exhibited good overall discrimination (AUC (95% confidence interval) of 0.872 (0.871-0.872). Discrimination performance for 10-year mortality risk predictions was consistently good across subgroups by sex, ADI quintile, race/ethnicity, partner status, and smoking status (Table 2). AUC estimates for each of these subgroups were ≥0.815. AUC estimates within subgroups defined according to decade of age at baseline were lower (e.g., 0.746 for ages 81-90 years and 0.813 for 40-50 years), reflecting the fact that individuals of similar age have more homogeneous mortality risk based on their risk factors than individuals of varying age. Applying the model to the MHS external validation cohort resulted in an overall AUC (95% CI) estimate of 0.811 [0.809-0.812], which was comparable to the observed discrimination performance with CCHS patients from ADI quintile 5 neighborhoods. Subgroup-specific AUC estimates for the MHS cohort (Supplementary Table 2) ranged from 0.735 (among current smokers) to 0.866 (among those widowed), with similarly reduced AUC estimates within those defined according to decade of age at baseline. Therefore, the model was able to accurately rank patients by risk. Additionally, calibration of 10-year composite survival estimates was excellent for both women and men in the CCHS internal validation cohort. The model tended to overestimate the magnitude of risk for a subset of high-risk women and men in the MHS external validation cohort (Supplementary Figure 4).

Figure 3 shows the distribution of LE and 10-year survival by age, sex, and ADI quintile, for the CCHS internal validation cohort. Life expectancy was shorter in high- vs low-deprivation neighborhoods. Predicted median (IQR) LE for women aged 40-45 years was 75.3 (71.1-77.8) and 84.6 (83.1-86.3) years in high- and low-deprivation neighborhoods, respectively. The disparity was 9.3 years, with 75% of women from high-deprivation neighborhoods expected to die before 2% of women from low-deprivation neighborhoods. Similarly, predicted median (IQR) LE for men aged 40-45 years was 71.0 (67.3-74.0) and 81.7 (79.9-83.5) years in high- and low-deprivation neighborhoods. The disparity was 10.7 years, with 75% of men from high-deprivation neighborhoods expected to die before 4% of men from low-deprivation neighborhoods. Disparities in remaining LE gradually decreased in successive age cohorts. The model estimated substantially higher disparities in 10-year survival and LE by ADI than by race (Supplementary Figure 5).

Results of a hypothetical population health intervention to reduce the number of smokers by 10% are shown in Supplementary Table 3. When implemented in the general population, there was little effect on SDOH-related disparities. Specifically, the difference in LE between high (ADI quintile 5) and low (ADI quintile 1) deprivation neighborhoods was reduced from 9.9 years to 9.6 years (disparity reduction of 0.3 years) in women and from 10.7 years to 10.6 years (disparity reduction of 0.1 years) in men. However, when only implemented in high-deprivation neighborhoods, SDOH-related disparities in LE were estimated to decline by 0.8 years in women and 0.6 years in men.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated a technique that employed the ADI for each patient’s census block group of residence and classified patients’ SDOH-related risk. The technique enables modelers to explain mechanisms that lead to socioeconomic disparities and to measure potential effects of population-level interventions to reduce disparities. In our analysis of regional electronic health records, we found that SDOH-related disparities in disease incidence (based on ADI) exceeded sex-based differences for 3 conditions: COPD, HLD, and T2DM. We found that a population health initiative to control their only shared modifiable risk factor, smoking, through a 10% reduction in the number of smokers aged 40-49 years in the highest-deprivation areas would reduce overall SDOH-related disparities in LE by 0.8 years (8%) in women and 0.6 years (6%) in men. For context, in the entire decade 2010-2019, the disparity in Black-White race LE declined by 0.3 years.21

It is well-known that poor Americans have lower life expectancies than wealthier individuals.1,22 This pattern holds for all income percentiles in the US population; it is amplified in neighborhoods where people are poorer, with less education.21 Additionally, it is well-documented that socioeconomically disadvantaged adults are more likely to have risk factors for cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and cancer, along with higher disease incidence, than wealthier individuals.23–26 Yet, the relative impact of SDOH disparities in each risk factor on disease incidence and mortality is poorly understood. There are also a few, if any, clear processes for health systems to identify the highest-risk patients for SDOH-related health outcomes. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services recently developed a health-related social needs screening tool, which benefits from patient-specific data. However, at 26 questions, the measure is onerous and challenging to translate into actionable population health management strategies.27 A better way to evaluate SDOH-related health disparities is needed.

To our knowledge, our model is the first to provide health systems (and policymakers) the ability to quantify the benefits of potential interventions to reduce SDOH-related disparities. Although imperfect, by applying area-level rather than patient-specific SDOH and by reducing calibration performance for certain high-risk patients within a public health system’s population, we were able to proxy the subgroup of individuals at the highest risk and then identify those who would most benefit from a hypothetical tobacco cessation initiative. Compared with an initiative to reduce the number of smokers by 10% in the general population, focusing on the highest-deprivation neighborhoods would result in a greater LE gain for both men and women.

The 3 conditions most contributing to SDOH-related disparities (COPD, HLD, and T2DM) only shared a single common risk factor: smoking. Other highly prevalent risk factors, such as overweight/obesity, unhealthy diet, and lack of physical activity, impact only 2 of the 3 conditions (HLD and T2DM, but not COPD). Should our framework eventually result in a tool for adoption in practice, one could envision that results would vary substantially by geography and setting (eg, public health systems), based on the prevalence of evidence-based risk factors in a particular population.

We envision 3 potential extensions of our framework. First, by adding clinical data to our model, it would be possible to repeat our process with a series of potential interventions based on modifiable risk factors (control of obesity, hypertension, glycemia, etc.), yielding population-specific priorities for health equity improvement. This model would not need to use all-cause survival as an outcome to be nonetheless useful. Second, although we focused on socioeconomic disparities, a similar tool could be used to address disparities in health outcomes by race, sex, or sexual orientation. Third, our model was derived from 2 health systems’ EHR data. Future work may consider methods to develop tools specific to an end-user health system.

Limitations

First, to maintain adequate power across subgroups, we utilized diagnoses rather than clinical data, which prevented measurement of risk factor control. Second, we did not consider the type of cancer. Third, the SDOH of specific patients may have differed from that of their neighborhoods, although much of this effect should average out at the population level. In analyses of electronic health records, the breadth and depth of data capture on individual SDOH can vary substantially among institutions. Fourth, our characterization of neighborhood socioeconomic position relied on a static assessment of residential locations at study baseline and a representation of socioeconomic position based on a specific 5-year American Community Survey data epoch. This approach, while practical for the analyses of electronic health records that lack detail on residential location history, captured neither heterogeneity in lived experience at the neighborhood level nor the nature of changes in neighborhood condition over time. Fifth, we had no data on healthcare received outside the health systems. However, we only included patients with a subsequent primary care encounter ≤2 years after the baseline visit, increasing the likelihood of complete diagnosis data.12 Sixth, because Cleveland, Ohio, is the third-poorest city in the United States,28 the potential to reduce SDOH-related disparities may differ in other communities. Lastly, our analyses are not spatial in nature, relying on an overall measure of neighborhood socioeconomic position; future work that incorporates novel approaches29,30 for incorporating spatial data elements could further enhance modeling capabilities.

Conclusions

We developed a framework to assess social drivers of health disparities in LE for a patient population and used it to model the potential impact of a population-focused health intervention on specific groups of patients. This approach can be used by health systems and/or policy makers to identify strategies to attenuate health disparities.
