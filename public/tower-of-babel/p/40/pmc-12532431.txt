From load monitoring to training decisions: a practical approach using drop jump metrics in semi-professional soccer
Authors: A Sierra-Casas, J A Rodríguez-Marroyo, D Castillo, J Gutiérrez-Arroyo, A Rodríguez-Fernández
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSoccer performance involves frequent high-intensity efforts and often insufficient recovery, making neuromuscular fatigue a critical factor in player monitoring. The drop jump (DJ) test, particularly the reactive strength index (RSI), has gained attention as a practical tool for assessing neuromuscular readiness. This study examined the relationship between accumulated external training load and neuromuscular status, assessed via DJ-derived metrics, in semi-professional soccer players.MethodsNineteen semi-professional outfield players (20.7 ± 1.4 years) were monitored over a 30-week competitive season, including 144 training sessions and 30 official matches. External load was tracked using 10 Hz Global Positioning System (GPS) devices. Neuromuscular performance was assessed weekly via the DJ test, measuring RSI, contact time (CT), and jump height (JH) with Optojump. Repeated-measures ANOVA evaluated longitudinal changes, and individual correlations were calculated across acute (7-day), chronic (28-day), and acute: chronic workload ratio periods.ResultsExternal load metrics varied significantly across microcycles, whereas DJ performance metrics remained stable at the group level. However, individual-level analyses revealed significant associations between external load and DJ-derived metrics (particularly between accelerations, decelerations, and high metabolic load distance with CT and JH) in both acute and chronic windows.ConclusionNeuromuscular responses to external training load appear highly individual among semi-professional soccer players. RSI and its components (CT and JH) should be interpreted separately to enhance decision-making regarding training and recovery. A multi-metric, individualized monitoring approach is recommended to optimize performance and reduce injury risk.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Neuromuscular readiness, Reactive strength index, Training load, Soccer, Drop jump, GPS monitoring

Introduction

Soccer is an intermittent sport characterized by alternating bouts of high-intensity activity and periods of low to moderate intensity [1, 2]. Throughout matches and training sessions, players frequently perform explosive actions such as sprinting, jumping, accelerating, decelerating, and changing direction, making fatigue an unavoidable outcome. This exercise-induced fatigue can negatively affect in-game performance and may persist for up to 72 h post-exercise [3]. Specifically, reductions in countermovement jump height and sprint performance have been observed 72 h after match play in male soccer players [4]. Furthermore, recent evidence suggests that a 72-hour recovery window may be insufficient for complete hamstring recovery following competition [5]. This is particularly relevant since the hamstrings play a decisive role in high-speed running, acceleration, and deceleration actions that are critical in soccer [6]. Incomplete recovery of these muscles may not only impair performance in subsequent training sessions or matches, but also predispose players to a higher risk of strain-type injuries, which are among the most frequent and recurrent in soccer. Therefore, understanding individual recovery kinetics and adjusting training loads accordingly becomes essential to safeguard both performance and player availability. These findings highlight the need for reliable tools to assess player readiness and guide informed decisions regarding training load management and match availability [7].

Due to the short recovery periods between matches, coaches often structure the training week to include high-load acquisition sessions (strength, endurance and speed sessions) in the mid-turnaround phase. This phase typically refers to the middle days between two consecutive matches (e.g., match day + 3 or + 4 in a standard seven-day microcycle), when players have partially recovered from the physiological and neuromuscular demands of the previous match but still have adequate time to recover before the upcoming one [8–10]. This scheduling strategy aims to sustain performance while balancing the competing demands of physical development and recovery. Considering that both training and competition impose substantial physical and psychological stress, monitoring athletes’ readiness (their capacity to train and compete effectively) [11, 12] is essential to determine when to increase training demands and when to prioritize recovery [13–15]. Therefore, tracking player readiness facilitates better decision-making around training intensity and contributes to injury risk reduction and performance optimization [13].

Although fatigue plays a critical role in driving physiological adaptation [16], its impact on readiness must be understood to avoid overtraining and underperformance. To this end, researchers have proposed various objective and subjective methods to monitor player status. Objective assessments typically include off-field (e.g., jump tests, heart rate variability) and on-field tests (e.g., sprints, submaximal drills, or small-sided games), while subjective tools rely on self-reported perceptions of fatigue and readiness [13]. Moreover, monitoring strategies can assess fatigue at a systemic or tissue-specific level [17]. Given the sport’s high neuromuscular demands (including frequent accelerations, decelerations, and sprinting) neuromuscular fatigue has received considerable attention due to its link to reductions in force and power output [3, 18–20]. To capture these dynamics, workload monitoring frameworks such as the acute: chronic workload ratio (ACWR), which compares short-term (3–7 days) to longer-term (21–28 days) load exposure, have been proposed as indicators of athlete preparedness and injury risk [21–24]. In practice, neuromuscular fatigue is frequently tracked through performance decrements in physical tests [4].

Among the available tests, the countermovement jump has traditionally been favored by strength and conditioning professionals for its practicality in assessing neuromuscular status [7, 25–28]. However, recent findings suggest that jump height alone may be insufficient to detect subtle signs of neuromuscular fatigue [29, 30]. Fatigued athletes may adjust their movement strategies to preserve flight time, thereby masking performance impairments [31]. To overcome this limitation, time-based metrics such as the reactive strength index modified (RSI-mod) derived from the CMJ (calculated as the ratio of flight time to contraction time) and the reactive strength index (RSI) derived from the drop jump (DJ) test (calculated as the ratio of flight time to ground contact time) has emerged as a promising alternative. The RSI, derived from the DJ, is particularly relevant to soccer because it relies on a short ground contact time and thus reflects the fast stretch-shortening cycle (SSC). This makes it distinct from the CMJ, which primarily involves a slow SSC due to longer contraction times. Whereas the CMJ provides insight into maximal power output and lower-limb force production, the DJ and its associated RSI are more sensitive to performance qualities linked to sprinting and rapid changes of direction, both of which are highly dependent on the fast SSC and are particularly susceptible to neuromuscular fatigue [32]. To date, RSI has been investigated as a marker of neuromuscular fatigue in soccer post-exercise [32, 33] and in response to training over a seven-week period in rugby [16].

Given the scarcity of research exploring the dose–response relationship between training and match loads and neuromuscular readiness in soccer, this study aimed to examine the association between accumulated external training load and neuromuscular status, assessed via DJ-derived metrics measured with Optojump, in semi-professional soccer players over a competitive season. Specifically, the study investigated whether acute (7-day), chronic (28-day), and acute-to-chronic workload ratios were associated with changes in jump height (JH), contact time (CT) and RSI.

Methods

Study design

This observational and correlational study was conducted during the 2023–2024 competitive season, which spanned from September to May and encompassed 30 microcycles (144 training sessions and 30 official matches). External training load for all sessions and matches was recorded using 10 Hz GPS devices with 100 Hz accelerometers (STATSports, Newry, Northern Ireland). Only microcycles that followed a standardized 7-day structure were considered for analysis, beginning with a recovery or compensatory session (MD + 1), followed by a rest day (MD + 2). On MD + 1, starters typically engaged in low-intensity recovery activities (e.g., mobility, stretching, and light aerobic exercise), whereas non-starters and players with reduced playing time completed compensatory work involving small-sided games, running drills, and strength exercises to match the load of the starters. This approach ensured that all players entered the week with a relatively similar baseline level of accumulated load. Acquisition training sessions were held on MD-4 and MD-3, while tapering sessions occurred on MD-2 and MD-1, in line with previous literature [9, 34]. Neuromuscular assessments were conducted prior to the first acquisition session (MD-4), approximately 48 h post-match, between 9:30 and 10:30 a.m. Each player completed two DJ trials using the Optojump™ system (Microgate, Bolzano, Italy). The study examined whether DJ performance metrics were associated with external load accumulated over 7-day (acute), 28-day (chronic), and ACWR. Participants

Nineteen semi-professional outfield soccer players from a Tier 3 national-level team (3ª RFEF, Spain) [35] participated in this study (age: 20.7 ± 1.4 years; height: 172.2 ± 11.1 cm; body mass: 72.8 ± 7.1 kg). The team finished among the top four in the league during the 2023–2024 season. Players trained 4–6 times per week and completed two weekly gym sessions focused on upper and lower body strength. During the preseason period (6 weeks), athletes were exposed to higher training loads and participated in preparatory matches. As data collection was part of routine daily assessments and club operations, institutional ethics approval was not required [36]. However, the study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee (ETICA-ULE-004-2021), and all procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Procedures

Neuromuscular performance was evaluated using a double-leg DJ from a 0.3 m box, performed prior to the first acquisition session (MD-4) and before any gym or on-field training. After a standardized 10-minute dynamic warm-up and five minutes of submaximal jumping, players performed two maximal DJ attempts with 2-minute recovery intervals. The trial with the highest RSI was retained for analysis.

To eliminate arm swing influence, athletes kept their hands on their hips throughout the movement and were instructed to jump as high as possible while minimizing ground contact time. They were instructed to keep their legs extended during flight and land in the same posture as take-off. The Optojump™ system (Microgate, Bolzano, Italy), composed of two parallel bars connected to analysis software (version 1.13.17.0), was used to measure ground contact time (CT) and flight time (FT). The transmitter unit emitted infrared signals 3 mm above ground level, and timing was activated when foot contact interrupted the beam. DJ performance metrics included CT, FT, JH, and the reactive strength index (RSI), calculated as the ratio of FT to CT [37].

External training load data were collected using portable 10 Hz GPS units with integrated 100 Hz accelerometers (STATSports, Newry, Northern Ireland), validated for accuracy with < 5% error for peak speed and distance [38], and widely used in soccer research [39]. Devices were activated 15 min before each session to ensure adequate satellite reception and positioned between the scapulae using custom vests. Each player consistently used the same device to minimize inter-unit variability [40].

The following locomotor variables were analyzed: total distance (TD), high-speed distance (HSD, >19.8 km·h⁻1), sprint distance (SPD, >25.2 km·h⁻1), high metabolic load distance (HMLD, >25.5 W·kg⁻1), number of high-intensity accelerations (ACC, >3 m·s⁻2), and decelerations (DCC, <−3 m·s⁻2) [41]. Data were processed using the manufacturer’s software (Viper Statsports v4.3.8) and exported to Microsoft Excel. Acute load was defined as the cumulative total over the most recent 7 days, while chronic load was calculated as the cumulative total over the previous 28 days (4 microcycles). The ACWR was computed by dividing the cumulative acute load by the 28-day average for each metric [42]. This summation-based approach has been widely used in applied sports science to monitor training loads in team sports, including soccer, and allows for practical comparisons across microcycles [43, 44]. By using this approach, we were able to capture the total external load accumulated by each player in both short- (acute) and medium-term (chronic) periods, providing a practical framework for analyzing associations with neuromuscular performance.

Statistical analysis

A repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted to assess changes in DJ metrics (FT, CT, JH, RSI) and external load metrics (i.e., TD, HSD, SPD, ACC, DCC, HMLD) across the 30 microcycles. Partial eta-squared (ηp2) was used to estimate effect sizes, interpreted as small (< 0.06), moderate (0.06–0.13), or large (≥ 0.14) [45]. Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were computed individually for each player to examine the relationships between external load variables (acute, chronic, and ACWR) and DJ metrics (FT, CT, JH, RSI). The purpose of these individual-level analyses was to capture inter-individual variability that may be obscured by group-level statistics. Corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. We summarized these findings by reporting the mean, minimum, and maximum correlation values across players, reflecting both typical and extreme individual responses. Correlation magnitudes (r) were classified as trivial (< 0.1), small (0.1–0.3), moderate (0.3–0.5), large (0.5–0.7), very large (0.7–0.9), nearly perfect (>0.9), or perfect (= 1.0) [46]. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Changes in performance were expressed as percent variation (Δ% = [(post − pre)/pre] × 100). All analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

The analysis of external load metrics across the 30 monitored microcycles revealed significant interaction effects for TD (F = 5.485; p < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.379), HSR (F = 3.757; p < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.294), SPR (F = 2.640; p = 0.037; ηp2 = 0.227), ACC (F = 6.893; p < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.434), DCC (F = 5.757; p < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.390), and HMLD (F = 6.659; p < 0.001; ηp2 = 0.425) (Fig. 2).

In contrast, analysis of DJ performance metrics across the same 30 microcycles revealed no significant interaction effects at the group level for JH (F = 2.401; p = 0.365; ηp2 = 0.706), CT (F = 5.711; p = 0.252; ηp2 = 0.851), or RSI (F = 7.421; p = 0.224; ηp2 = 0.881) (Fig. 3).

At the group level, no significant associations were observed within the 7-day (acute) training window between changes in DJ metrics and external load variables. However, the individual-level analysis revealed meaningful associations that were otherwise obscured in the group data. Specifically, some players showed significant correlations between DJ metrics and external load variables: CT was significantly associated with ACC, DCC, and HMLD; FT with TD, ACC, DCC, and HMLD; and JH with TD, ACC, DCC, and HMLD. Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the relationship between acute, chronic, and acute: chronic workload ratios and neuromuscular performance, assessed through DJ metrics in semi-professional soccer players. While the interaction effects found in external load metrics underscore the dynamic nature of training demands across a season, no such effects were observed for DJ-derived metrics variables at the group level. Despite the DJ test being recognized as a tool to assess neuromuscular fatigue in soccer [32], no significant associations emerged between DJ metrics and external load when analyzed at the group level across the 7-day, 28-day, or ACWR. However, when adopting an individual-level approach, relevant associations were observed between DJ metrics and several external load variables (e.g., TD, ACC, DCC, HMLD). These findings highlight substantial interindividual variability and reinforce the practical importance of individualized monitoring, as players may respond differently to similar workloads. Nevertheless, such correlations should be interpreted with caution, since analyses at the single-player level are inherently limited by sample size and may be influenced by short-term variability.

Short-term (acute, 7-day) training windows are widely used in soccer for planning and adjusting training load [34, 47–49]. The external loads observed in our cohort are consistent with previous reports in similar populations [34, 50], although slightly lower than those observed in professional teams [51]. The significant variation in external load across microcycles reinforces the importance of continuous monitoring to guide individualized training and recovery strategies and prevent maladaptation or excessive fatigue [13, 52, 53]. At the group level, no associations were found between changes in DJ metrics and external load variables within the acute window. However, individual-level analyses revealed meaningful relationships in some players, particularly between DJ performance and ACC, DCC, and HMLD, supporting the relevance of accelerative and decelerative actions as sensitive markers of neuromuscular stress [54]. Since strength and conditioning professionals often use these metrics to prescribe and adjust training loads [47], monitoring their dose–response relationship with DJ outcomes may enhance load individualization. Additionally, considering that neuromuscular impairments (e.g., decreased jump performance) can persist for up to 72 h post-exercise [4, 55], it is advisable to interpret acute GPS-derived metrics in the context of the athlete’s recovery status before prescribing demanding acquisition sessions (e.g., MD-4) that emphasize ACC and DCC [9]. Notably, in certain players, changes in RSI were associated with all external load variables, possibly because it integrates both JH and CT, thus offering a more comprehensive assessment of stretch-shortening cycle efficiency and force production than isolated measures. Still, given the limited sample size and the exploratory nature of individual correlations, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, and longitudinal replication in larger cohorts is warranted.

From a long-term perspective, chronic load accumulation (28-day) has been associated with performance gains, as sustained exposure to load can enhance physical capacity [56]. Conversely, low chronic loads have been linked to increased injury risk [57]. Our findings, although no group-level effects were detected, at the individual level all DJ metrics correlated with all external load metrics. This suggests that neuromuscular performance may reflect the cumulative impact of training and competition over time, offering practitioners an additional tool to evaluate adaptation or maladaptation in specific players. Yet, it should be acknowledged that single-player correlations, while practically informative, are more vulnerable to noise, emphasizing the need for cautious interpretation. Well-managed chronic loads may foster neuromuscular adaptations (e.g., improved jump efficiency and power output), but excessive exposure could also signal accumulated fatigue. These observations are in line with the concept of load tolerance and suggest that a balanced chronic exposure promotes both performance and resilience. Interestingly, contrary to common expectations, JH occasionally showed greater sensitivity to external load than time-based metrics (e.g., CT or FT) or composite indices like RSI [58, 59].

The ACWR is frequently used to balance load management and injury prevention, with optimal ratios typically falling between 0.8 and 1.3 [60, 61]. While high chronic loads have been shown to reduce injury risk [24, 62], the utility of ACWR has been questioned due to inconsistent predictive value in some studies [63, 64]. Despite this, it remains a commonly monitored metric, particularly concerning sprint exposure, as including maximal velocity stimuli across the training week is recommended [10, 65].

Our findings on the ACWR revealed similar individual-level trends as the acute load window, with significant associations observed between changes in DJ-derived metrics and ACC, DCC, and HMLD in some players. These results underline the practical utility of accelerative and decelerative actions as indicators of neuromuscular strain at the player level, even when group-level effects are absent [47, 54]. However, the limited sample per player and the variability of match exposure restrict the generalizability of these correlations. Additionally, we observed that the ACWR for SPD was significantly correlated with JH and FT in certain players. This indicates that SPD, beyond its acute effects, may influence neuromuscular performance when viewed from a cumulative exposure perspective. Prior studies have proposed chronic sprint exposure as a protective factor for injury, contributing to improved neuromuscular function and tissue resilience [66]. Therefore, practitioners should be cautious of abrupt fluctuations in SPD across microcycles. As sprint exposure follows a U-shaped relationship with injury risk [42], positive correlations between SPD and changes in DJ-derived metrics may reflect a beneficial adaptation in previously under-stimulated players. Thus, while our individual findings reinforce the applied value of considering player-specific load–response patterns, they should be interpreted as exploratory and require validation in larger cohorts.

Contextual factors, such as player position, training drill type, and match participation status, also influence training loads [67, 68], potentially explaining interindividual variability in the load–response relationship. Furthermore, players with higher neuromuscular capacity may exhibit greater sensitivity to training load due to their ability to produce higher mechanical outputs [69, 70]. In our analysis of percentage changes, significant associations were found between RSI and volume/intensity-based GPS metrics (TD, ACC, DCC, SPD), indicating the potential of these markers to guide individualized load adjustments.

Ultimately, future research should continue to explore dose–response relationships on an individual level. The present study suggests that examining player-specific associations between load and neuromuscular markers can offer applied insights for training prescription. Nonetheless, such analyses should be viewed as complementary to group-based approaches rather than a substitute, due to their inherent methodological limitations. We hypothesize that some players may be more responsive to certain load characteristics (e.g., sprinting vs. accelerative efforts), and that their neuromuscular status may be more accurately captured by specific DJ metrics (e.g., JH vs. CT or RSI). Personalizing load monitoring strategies based on these differences could improve the precision of training prescription. In addition, new testing approaches that combine different stretch–shortening cycle characteristics within the same protocol, such as rebound-based jumps that integrate CMJ and DJ actions (CMJRJ), may represent a time-efficient alternative and provide a more comprehensive picture of both long-slow and short-fast SSC function.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the sensitivity of DJ-derived metrics (proposed as practical tools for neuromuscular monitoring) varies substantially between individuals in response to external training loads. While group-level associations were not observed, individual-level analyses revealed meaningful relationships, with changes in RSI and its individual components (JH and CT) showing distinct associations with key GPS-derived metrics, including ACC, DCC, SPD, and HMLD. These findings support the use of a multi-metric monitoring strategy that considers individual variability when assessing neuromuscular readiness. However, given the exploratory nature of these individual correlations and the limited sample size, the results should be interpreted with caution and warrant replication in larger cohorts. Nevertheless, this approach may help optimize training load management, enhance athletic performance, and reduce injury risk throughout the competitive season.
