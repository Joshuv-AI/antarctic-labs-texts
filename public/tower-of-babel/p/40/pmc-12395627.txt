Regarding effect of different doses of intravenous dexmedetomidine as an adjunct to the serratus anterior plane block in patients undergoing modified radical mastectomy: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Fu-Shan Xue, Dan-Feng Wang, Zhi-Bin Huang, Yong-Bao Lin
Published: 2025

To the editor

A recent study by Huang et al. [1] demonstrated that intravenous addition of dexmedetomidine 1.0 µg/kg as an adjunct to the serratus anterior plane block significantly decreased postoperative pain intensity and improved postoperative recovery quality assessed by the QoR-15 score in patients undergoing modified radical mastectomy. However, there are several issues in the methodology and results of this study that require further discussion and clarification.

First, all participants in this study were breast cancer patients undergoing radical mastectomy, but Huang et al. did not provide the information on preoperative pain severity, psychological and sleep disorders, use of chemoradiotherapy, extent and type of surgery, and postoperative complications. The available literature indicates that preoperative distress, anxiety, depression, poor sleep quality, pain catastrophizing, chemotherapy, axillary dissection, number of axillary lymph node resections, and postoperative urinary and gastrointestinal complications are associated with increased severity of acute pain after breast cancer surgery, whereas preoperative radiotherapy is associated with reduced severity of postoperative acute pain [2,3]. We are concerned that any significant difference in these unknown factors among the three groups would have biased the results of postoperative acute pain.

Second, the postoperative analgesia target of this study was to maintain a pain score of <3, that is, mild pain. According to the data in Figure 2 of Huang et al.’s article, the median rest and movement pain scores at all time points postoperatively in the three groups were 3.5 or less, with interquartile ranges of about 1 or less. Furthermore, the highest median pain scores in the three groups were 3. These results indicate that most patients have only mild postoperative pain. It was unclear why the incidence of moderate-to-severe postoperative pain with a pain score >4 within 24 h was up 22.3-46.4% in the three groups. In addition, the 24-h area under the curves of rest and movement pain scores were significantly reduced in the two dexmedetomidine groups compared with the control group, but net differences among the three groups in the mean 24-h area under the curves of pain scores were less than the clinically important differences reported in the available literature, that is, a relative change >30% or an absolute change >24 [4]. This means that the differences in cumulative postoperative pain intensities between the control and dexmedetomidine groups were statistically significant, but clinically insignificant.

Finally, the QoR-15 score at 24 h postoperatively significantly improved in the two dexmedetomidine groups. It has been reported that the QoR-15 score measured 24 h postoperatively cannot provide a true evaluation of postoperative recovery quality. Thus, a repeated measurement of QoR-15 score at 48 h postoperatively is required to enable adequate evaluation of postoperative recovery quality [5]. Most importantly, Huang et al. [1] did not assess other outcome variables of current enhanced recovery after breast surgery, such as the time to first ambulation, time to first flatus, patient satisfaction, length of stay, and overall complication rate. In these cases, it is a dilemma for the readers to determine whether decreased postoperative pain intensity and improved postoperative recovery quality with intravenous addition of dexmedetomidine 1.0 µg/kg to a regional block may transform the real clinical benefits of patients undergoing radical mastectomy.
