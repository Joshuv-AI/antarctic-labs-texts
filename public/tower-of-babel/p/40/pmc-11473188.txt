Ramucirumab‐induced ascites with endothelial growth factor receptor mutation‐positive non‐small cell lung cancer: Two case reports
Authors: Keisuke Shiraha, Tomoki Tamura, Taisaku Koyanagi, Takahiro Umeno, Kazuya Nishii, Shoichi Kuyama
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRamucirumab (RAM) has been approved for the treatment of non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Here, we report two cases of RAM‐induced ascites with epidermal growth factor receptor‐mutant NSCLC. Patient 1, a 72‐year‐old man, developed ascites 20 months after erlotinib (ERL) and RAM administration, which resolved after their discontinuation and performing paracentesis. Patient 2, an 83‐year‐old woman, developed ascites 9 months after ERL and RAM administration, which resolved after RAM discontinuation and furosemide administration. Ramucirumab administration can cause ascites due to increased hepatic sinusoidal pressure. Clinicians should be aware of RAM‐induced ascites in patients with NSCLC and should appropriately manage it.Keywords: ascites, epidermal growth factor receptor, erlotinib, non‐small cell lung cancer, ramucirumab

INTRODUCTION

Ramucirumab (RAM) is a human immunoglobulin G1 monoclonal antibody receptor antagonist against vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) 2 that has been approved for the treatment of several cancers, including non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). 1 , 2 , 3

Ascites accumulation with ramucirumab has been reported as an adverse event in HCC2 but is extremely rare in NSCLC. Herein, we report two cases of ascites induced by RAM administration in patients with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)‐mutant NSCLC.

CASE REPORTS

Patient 1 was a 72‐year‐old man diagnosed with stage IVB (cT1bN3M1c) lung adenocarcinoma with multiple lymph node metastases. The tumour was an epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) exon 19 deletion‐positive adenocarcinoma. The Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status score was 1, and the patient was treated with afatinib. Eleven months later, magnetic resonance imaging revealed brain metastasis, which was consistent with progressive disease, and he was treated with gamma knife radiosurgery. Subsequently, the patient received two courses of chemotherapy comprising carboplatin, paclitaxel, bevacizumab, and atezolizumab; one, docetaxel and RAM; and eight pemetrexed. The patient underwent combined chemotherapy comprising erlotinib (ERL) (150 mg/day, daily) and RAM (10 mg/kg, every 2 weeks) administrations as fifth‐line chemotherapy. Twenty months later, abdominal computed tomography (CT) revealed a small amount of ascites (Figure 1A). Further, 24 months after the first ERL + RAM chemotherapy session, abdominal CT revealed massive ascites (Figure 1B). Laboratory examinations indicated a white blood cell count of 4260 cells/μL, with 44.4% neutrophils; C‐reactive protein concentration, 0.11 mg/dL; aspartate transaminase (AST) level, 71 U/L; alanine transaminase (ALT) level, 50 U/L; lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level, 377 U/L; total protein level, 7.8 g/dL; albumin (ALB) level, 3.5 g/dL. The ascites was clear and pale yellow in appearance and contained a serum ascites albumin gradient (SAAG) of 2.5 g/dL (Table 1). Cytological examination and cell culture of the ascites revealed no definitive evidence of infection or malignancy. Therefore, the patient was diagnosed with transudative ascites, possibly induced by ERL and RAM administrations, which were then stopped. He was originally taking azosemide 30 mg/day and spironolactone 25 mg/day for chronic heart failure. We increased the azosemide dose to 60 mg/day; however, the ascites did not improve. The ascites was aspirated five times (1700, 2000, 5700, 3000, and 4580 mL), of which two aspirations were followed by cell‐free and concentrated ascites reinfusion therapy (CART). Two months after his last RAM chemotherapy session, the ascites decreased (Figure 1C), and 4 months after his last RAM chemotherapy session, the ascites was resolved (Figure 1D), and ERL administration was resumed. The patient's subsequent clinical course was good, with a partial response lasting for more than 4 months.

Patient 2 was an 83‐year‐old woman who was diagnosed with stage I (cT1bN0M0) lung adenocarcinoma and underwent left lower lobectomy and hilar and mediastinal lymph node dissections. The postoperative pathological diagnosis was stage IIIA (pT1bN2M0) lung adenocarcinoma. She underwent postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy comprising carboplatin and vinorelbine administrations. However, 2 years post‐surgery, chest CT revealed a nodule in the left upper lobe and right hilar lymphadenopathy, which was diagnosed as postoperative recurrence. The tumour was an EGFR exon 19 deletion‐positive adenocarcinoma. The ECOG performance status score was 1, and the patient was treated with combined chemotherapy with ERL (150 mg/day, daily) and RAM (10 mg/kg, every 2 weeks) as first‐line chemotherapy. Nine months later, abdominal CT revealed a small amount of ascites (Figure 2A). Thirteen months after the first ERL and RAM chemotherapy, she gradually experienced abdominal fullness, and abdominal CT revealed massive ascites (Figure 2B). Laboratory examination indicated a white blood cell count of 3240 cells/μL with 54.4% neutrophils; C‐reactive protein concentration, 0.04 mg/dL; AST level, 32 U/L; ALT level, 18 U/L; LDH level, 238 U/L; total protein level, 6.6 g/dL; and ALB level, 4.1 g/dL. The ascites was clear and pale yellow in appearance and showed an SAAG of 2.8 g/dL (Table 2). Cytological examination and cell culture of the ascites revealed no definitive evidence of infection or malignancy. Therefore, the patient was diagnosed with transudative ascites possibly induced by RAM; hence, we stopped RAM administration, continued ERL administration, and initiated oral furosemide administration at 20 mg/day. Two months after the last RAM chemotherapy, her ascites decreased (Figure 2C), and furosemide administration was discontinued. The ascites resolved 3 months after her last RAM chemotherapy session (Figure 2D). The patient continued to exhibit stable disease for 5 months with no recurrence of ascites.

DISCUSSION

Ramucirumab is a human immunoglobulin G1 monoclonal antibody VEGFR‐2 antagonist that has proven to be effective for EGFR mutation‐positive NSCLC as a combination therapy with an EGFR‐TKI and angiogenesis inhibitor in the RELAY trial. 3 Ramucirumab administration is associated with various adverse events such as hypertension, proteinuria, haemorrhage, and deep vein thrombosis. It inhibits ligand activation of VEGFR‐2, which promotes blood vessel permeability 4 and is supposed to inhibit the development of severe ascites. Ramucirumab‐induced ascites is a common adverse event in patients with HCC. Although fluid retention, such as pleural effusion and peripheral edema, has been reported in NSCLC, ascites has rarely been reported. 5 , 6 In patients with HCC treated with second‐line therapy, the incidence of treatment‐emergent ascites was higher in the RAM group (20.9%) than that in the placebo group (14.8%) and was predominantly grade 1 or 2 according to the common terminology criteria for adverse events in a previous study. 7 In a Japanese cohort of patients with HCC treated with RAM, grade 3 or higher ascites was observed in only 3% of patients. 8

RAM‐induced ascites can be difficult to distinguish from ascites of other etiologies such as portal hypertension, malignancy, and infection. 9 In the two present cases, cell culture and cytological examination of the ascites gave negative results for infection and malignancy, and there was a low probability of infection or peritoneal dissemination of lung cancer. In addition, the liver function in the two present cases was normal. Ramucirumab administration can cause ascites even in patients with normal liver function. 10 The ascites did not increase in volume again after discontinuation of RAM administration, suggesting that the ascites might have been caused by RAM administration.

Ramucirumab‐induced ascites is treated by withdrawal of the drug as well as furosemide and spironolactone administration and paracentesis. 7 Patient 1 was treated with azosemide and CART, and patient 2 was treated with furosemide. As Patient 1 showed no improvement with azosemide administration, and furosemide administration was started in Patient 2 when RAM was withdrawn, the effect of diuretic administration is not clear. In these cases, the ascites decreased after diuretic discontinuation, and ascites did not reaccumulate after RAM withdrawal. The decrease in ascites may have been due to RAM withdrawal rather than diuretics.

However, the mechanism underlying RAM‐induced ascites remains unclear. One mechanistic hypothesis is that the strong inhibitory effect of RAM on VEGFR‐2 leads to increased hepatic sinusoidal pressure due to the upregulation of vasoconstricting factors and downregulation of vasodilating factors, resulting in portal hypertension and transudative ascites. 11 Based on these mechanisms, RAM administration may have been a confounding factor for the ascites in these cases.

The reported median time of ascites onset in patients with HCC is 43 days (range: 1–589 days) after RAM treatment initiation. 7 In our cases, ascites onset was relatively late, occurring approximately 20 months for patient 1 and 9 months for patient 2 after the first dose of RAM. Notably, HCC is often associated with portal hypertension; therefore, the absence of portal hypertension at the time of treatment initiation in our cases could have been a reason for the delayed onset of ascites.

In conclusion, we present recent cases of RAM‐induced ascites in patients with NSCLC. With the increased use of RAM in the treatment of NSCLC, caution should be exercised regarding the presence of ascites.
