The GLIDE Score: One-Year Outcomes Following Tricuspid Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair
Authors: Felix Rudolph, Akhil Narang, Maria I. Körber, Kai P. Friedrichs, Johannes Kirchner, Maria Ivannikova, Carolina Esperanca Clara Göttsche, Jan Althoff, Paul Cremer, Peter Luedike, Tanja K. ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe GLIDE score is a 5-point imaging-based tool to predict residual tricuspid regurgitation (TR) after tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER). Lower residual TR is associated with better prognosis, but long-term associations between GLIDE score and outcomes are uncertain.MethodsWe performed an analysis of a multinational registry of patients undergoing T-TEER for severe or greater TR from January 2017 to November 2022. GLIDE scores were assessed using preprocedural transesophageal echocardiography. The primary endpoint was a composite of all-cause mortality or heart failure hospitalization (HFH) at 1 year, and the secondary endpoints included TR grade of moderate or less at 1 year.ResultsIn 336 patients (81 [interquartile range 77–84] years; 44.6% male, TRI-SCORE 5 [interquartile range 4–6]), survival and HFH follow-up at 1 year was available in 90.5%. One-year mortality was 15.8%. The GLIDE score was significantly associated with the combined primary endpoint (hazard ratio: 1.20; 95% CI 1.03–1.40; p = 0.019), even after adjustment for TRI-SCORE. Freedom from death or HFH was significantly higher among patients with GLIDE scores ≤1 compared to those ≥2 (p = 0.032). Of the 251 1-year survivors, follow-up echocardiography was available in 170 (68%). The GLIDE score predicted 1-year TR severity (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve: 0.73; 95% CI 0.65–0.81), with moderate or less residual TR achieved in 80.6%, 54.9%, and 18.8% of patients with GLIDE scores of 0–1, 2–3, and ≥4, respectively.ConclusionsThe GLIDE score is associated with 1-year survival and HFH after T-TEER as well as ≤ moderate TR.

Introduction

Severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) with heart failure symptoms is strongly associated with mortality and worsening quality of life. Transcatheter tricuspid edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER) has emerged as a safe and effective treatment option for patients at high surgical risk and has been shown to improve functional status compared to optimal medical therapy alone.1 Observational data suggest an association between lower residual TR and improved survival.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 A persistent clinical challenge is the optimal selection of candidates who are most likely to benefit from T-TEER, especially as transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement emerges as a viable alternative treatment that virtually eliminates TR.8

The GLIDE score is an anatomical scoring system incorporating gap size (G), jet location (L), image quality (I), chordal structure density (D), and en-face TR jet morphology (E) on a scale of 0–5 points and is a validated tool for predicting residual TR following T-TEER.9 It was designed to predict moderate TR, less-residual TR, or an absolute reduction by 2 or more grades, and it was further demonstrated to predict mild residual TR.10

The TRI-SCORE predicts in-hospital mortality and is included in the TR treatment decision algorithm of the 2025 European Society of Cardiology guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease.11, 12, 13 However, this score was created to predict surgical outcomes and demonstrated to overestimate mortality in transcatheter tricuspid valve interventions.14,15

To date, the predictive performance of the GLIDE score has been evaluated for immediate procedural outcomes, but its ability to predict long-term clinical outcomes is unknown. There is an established association between residual TR and 1-year prognosis following T-TEER.2, 3, 4 Whether the preprocedure GLIDE score provides incremental benefit for longitudinal risk stratification is unknown. The goal of the current study is to evaluate the GLIDE score as a predictor of the composite endpoint of mortality and heart failure hospitalizations (HFHs). A secondary analysis was performed to evaluate GLIDE score prediction of residual TR at 1 year.

Methods

Study Design

A multicenter international registry from 5 tertiary care centers (4 in Germany and 1 in Switzerland) was used to evaluate 336 consecutive patients who underwent T-TEER for severe or greater TR between January 2017 and November 2022. All patients underwent preprocedural transthoracic echocardiography and transesophageal echocardiography. The baseline GLIDE score was assessed based on transesophageal echocardiography imaging at the Northwestern University core lab, and it was blinded to the procedural results, as previously described.9 TR severity was graded on a 5-point scale.16 T-TEER device selection and treatment approach were determined by the local heart team. Further selection details have been described previously.9

Intraprocedural success was defined according to the Tricuspid Valve Academic Research Consortium consensus: successful delivery, deployment, and positioning of the device; absence of procedural mortality; and freedom from emergency surgery related to the device (except for device performance).17,18 Procedural success was defined as TR reduction to moderate or less or by 2 or more grades.

The primary endpoint was a composite of all-cause mortality and HFH at 1 year. Secondary endpoints were the individual components of the primary endpoint (all-cause mortality and HFH) and site-reported TR moderate or less after 1 year (defined as 365 ± 30 days postprocedure). For group comparisons, patients were stratified based on a GLIDE score cutoff ≥2, (i.e., 0–1 = low risk, ≥2 = intermediate-to-high risk). This threshold previously demonstrated significant discrimination for acute procedural results.9,10

Follow-up data were obtained through a combination of in-person visits, structured telephone interviews, queries at the German registration authorities, and physician office contact.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R in RStudio (version 2023.06, Posit PBC, Boston, Massachusetts). Continuous variables were reported as mean ± SD if normally distributed and as median with interquartile range (IQR) if not normally distributed. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to determine if the data were normally distributed. Group comparisons for continuous variables were conducted using the Wilcoxon test or Student’s t-test, as appropriate. Categorical group comparisons were calculated using Fisher exact or Chi-square tests. The association between the GLIDE score and time-to-event outcomes was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression with additional analyses adjusted for TRI-SCORE and the type of device implanted (PASCAL [Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, California] or TriClip [Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, Illinois] system).11,12 Discriminatory performance of the GLIDE score and TRI-SCORE was assessed using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) from the Cox proportional hazards models, with differences compared between correlated C-indices. Time-to-event analyses were censored at 365 days. Logistic regression analysis was applied to assess the association between GLIDE score and TR severity at 1 year. The goodness-of-fit of the logistic regression model was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. In addition, a likelihood ratio test was performed to compare the full model with an intercept-only model. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted employing the “pROC” package (V 1.18.5) in R. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No data were imputed.

Survival and HFH status at 1 year were available for 304 patients (90.5%). One-year mortality was 15.8%. Of the 251 survivors, 170 patients (67.7%) had available follow-up transthoracic echocardiograms. There was no significant difference in clinical characteristics, procedural details, and GLIDE score components between the overall study population and those with available clinical follow-up, except for a higher proportion of patients in NYHA class ≥III in the subgroup (Supplementary Table 1).

Procedural Details

The median procedure time was 101 minutes (IQR 71–132), and a median of 2 (IQR 1-2) devices were implanted. TR reduction to ≤ moderate was achieved in 73.2% and by ≥2 grades in 22.0% of all patients. The rate of periprocedural adverse events was 6.8%. The PASCAL system (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, California) was used in 60.1%, whereas the TriClip system (Abbott Cardiovascular, Indianapolis, Indiana) was used in 39.9%. When stratified by GLIDE score, patients with GLIDE ≥2 had significantly longer procedure times and lower rates of device and technical success.10

Outcomes

The 1-year primary endpoint of mortality or HFH occurred in 83 patients (24.7%). The median follow-up time was 365 days (IQR 154–365). Cox regression revealed a significant association between the GLIDE score and the primary endpoint (hazard ratio [HR]: 1.19; 95% CI 1.02–1.40; p = 0.029). This association remained significant after adjustment for baseline TRI-SCORE and T-TEER device type (Figure 1). The C-index was 0.58 ± 0.03 for the GLIDE score and 0.63 ± 0.03 for the TRI-SCORE (ΔC-index: 0.05; p = 0.17). Secondary Endpoints

In a paired analysis of the 170 (67.7%) survivors with available long-term clinical and echocardiographic follow-up, 95 patients (56.4%) had massive or torrential TR at baseline, whereas 131 (77.1%) had moderate or less after the procedure (p < 0.001). This improvement was sustained until 1-year follow-up, where 114 patients (67.0%) remained at a TR grade of moderate or less (p = 0.57). When stratified by GLIDE score, moderate or less TR at 1 year was noted in 83 patients (80.6%) with a low-risk score (i.e., 0 or 1), 28 (54.9%) with an intermediate-risk score (i.e., 2 or 3), and 3 (18.8%) with a high-risk score (i.e., ≥4) (all p = 0.001) (Figure 6). Residual mild or less TR at 1 year was observed in 41 (39.8%), 8 (15.7%), and 0 patients (0.0%) with GLIDE scores 0–1, 2–3, and ≥4, respectively (p = 0.001 for 0–1 vs. ≥4) (Figure 7). In this subset, the overall GLIDE score was significantly associated with TR ≤ moderate at 1 year with an odds ratio (OR) of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.82–0.90; p < 0.001), which remained significant after adjustment for baseline TR severity (OR: 0.88; 95% CI: 0.83–0.93; p < 0.001). ROC analysis showed that the GLIDE score had modest discriminatory capacity for predicting a TR grade of moderate or lower at 1 year with an area under the ROC curve of 0.73 (95% CI): 0.65–0.81 (Figure 8). The optimal threshold was calculated to be 0.7. At this threshold, the diagnostic metrics were as follows: sensitivity, 0.73; specificity, 0.64; positive predictive value, 0.81; and negative predictive value, 0.54. The model demonstrated good calibration, as assessed by the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p = 0.55). A likelihood ratio test comparing the full model with the GLIDE score to an intercept-only model showed a significant improvement in model fit (χ2 = 30.3; df = 1; p < 0.001). Discussion

The GLIDE score was originally developed to assess immediate procedural success, defined as achieving moderate or less TR or an absolute reduction of at least 2 grades. In the present study, we extend the clinical prediction capability of the GLIDE score by demonstrating that it is associated with clinical outcomes at 1 year following T-TEER. Specifically, higher GLIDE scores were associated with an increased risk of the composite endpoint of all-cause mortality or HFH (with the association primarily driven by HFH). Although its discriminatory performance was modest with a C-index of 0.58 ± 0.03, this association remained significant after adjustment for TRI-SCORE, suggesting that anatomical complexity captured by the GLIDE score provides incremental prognostic information beyond clinical risk stratification (Graphical Abstract). This likely reflects the reported relationship between residual TR and prognosis after T-TEER, as there was no significant difference in the primary endpoint based on low and intermediate-to-high risk GLIDE score categories after stratification according to residual TR (Figure 5).2, 3, 4, 5,7 However, the key clinical challenge is not estimating prognosis after the procedure but refining preprocedural patient selection. Therefore, the incremental value of an anatomical score in addition to TRI-SCORE may provide insights for preprocedural risk assessment. There was also a signal that T-TEER device type was associated with outcome favoring PASCAL. As this was not a randomized comparison, further study is needed. Long-term follow-up was not available when the original GLIDE study was reported. By incorporating both clinical endpoints and TR severity at 1 year, our study addresses this gap and adds new evidence regarding the GLIDE score’s relevance for predicting treatment success at 1 year.

We found that the GLIDE score retained modest predictive power for achieving moderate or less TR at 1 year but not for an absolute TR reduction by 2 or more grades. This discrepancy may reflect a baseline imbalance, as patients with higher GLIDE scores suffered from more significant TR, which inherently offers greater potential for numerical improvement. In addition, patients with more significant TR and higher anatomical complexity may have poorer survival, as evidenced by the significantly higher rates of death and HFH in the higher GLIDE score group. As was reported from various registry analyses, this perception may also reflect on a shift in perspective away from the relative improvement in TR severity toward the actual postprocedural TR grade associated with clinical outcome.2, 3, 4,6 The update of the EuroTR registry incorporates data from >2500 T-TEER patients. It suggests additional prognostic benefit of achieving mild rather than moderate residual TR with respect to 2-year survival and HFH-free survival.7 Favorable outcomes are still possible in anatomically challenging cases, but the probability of sustained TR reduction decreases with increasing GLIDE scores. Alternative therapies, such as transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement, should be carefully considered in those with high GLIDE scores, especially ≥4 points. However, with these additional options available, decision-making is challenging in the intermediate scores between 2–3, where only about half the patients attain moderate or lower residual TR that will need further investigation.

Although functional TR remained the predominant etiology in our cohort and most T-TEER studies, pathoanatomy is an important factor in treatment planning. CIED-associated TR is now commonly recognized as a distinct clinical entity and often complicates procedural success due to lead interference with leaflet grasping.19,20 Although the presence of CIED leads was not identified as an independent predictor in the creation of the GLIDE score, this is likely due to selection bias and underrepresentation in this cohort. T-TEER’s more frequent use in CIED-associated TR should be considered in future efforts to further enhance the GLIDE score.

Among the 5 components of the GLIDE score, chordal structure density and image quality may be susceptible to interobserver variability. Nevertheless, all 5 parameters remain clinically intuitive, reproducible, and relevant to procedural complexity.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. The retrospective observational design limits causal inference and introduces potential confounders. Also, incomplete echocardiographic follow-up among 1-year survivors (67.7%) could introduce selection bias and limit generalizability of our findings regarding TR severity at 1 year. However, this does not impact the GLIDE score hard outcome (obtained in 90.5%) predictability of mortality and HFH. Also, this rate is comparable to or even higher than that reported in several other real-world registries.2,21 Although baseline TR was core-lab adjudicated, the assessment of TR severity at 1 year by site investigators could lead to interpretation bias. Finally, the sample size may have been insufficient to detect independent associations with all-cause mortality alone. In addition, multiple subgroup and exploratory analyses were performed without formal adjustment for multiple comparisons. Accordingly, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

Optimal patient selection remains a key clinical challenge in transcatheter tricuspid valve interventions, as suboptimal procedural results are associated with worse long-term prognosis. The GLIDE score is currently the only validated tool to predict procedural outcomes following T-TEER. In this study, we extended its utility by demonstrating that the GLIDE score is also associated with a composite endpoint of all-cause mortality and HFH at 1 year, even after adjustment for TRI-SCORE, and predicts sustained TR reduction at 1 year. These findings support incremental value of the GLIDE score as a practical, imaging-based tool for both procedural planning and long-term risk stratification.
