RePo index: a multidimensional framework to quantify genetic resilience in data-limited amphibian faunas
Authors: Marcela A Vidal, Felipe Barraza-Acuña, Cristian Torres-Díaz, Ian S Acuña-Rodríguez, Gabriel Ballesteros, Moisés A Valladares
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores the resilience of Chilean amphibians to environmental disturbances through an integrative approach that combines ecological, demographic, bibliometric, and molecular information. A total of 58 species distributed across 14 genera and 9 families were evaluated via the resilience potential (RePo) index, which incorporates eleven criteria grouped into five dimensions: distribution, population trends, emerging diseases, evolutionary history, and genetic records. The results revealed high ecological vulnerability: 83% of the species were classified as non resilient (45% with no resilience and 38% with low resilience), and none reached the high-resilience category. At the family level, Telmatobiidae presented the lowest resilience values, whereas Leptodactylidae presented the highest. At the genus level, Insuetophrynus was identified as the most vulnerable taxon, with no molecular records and an extremely restricted distribution. In contrast, species such as Rhinella spinulosa and Pleurodema thaul presented moderate resilience, suggesting greater adaptive potential and relevance for functional studies. From a bibliometric perspective, a bias toward classical research topics (distribution, physiology, and taxonomy) was detected, with limited representation of integrative approaches such as genetic conservation or climate change. The conceptual modularity in the literature was low (Q = 0.1328), indicating weak thematic differentiation and little integration of omics tools. Most species lack transcriptomic and genomic data, severely limiting the assessment of their adaptive mechanisms. In this context, the RePo index has emerged as an integrative tool useful for operationalizing concepts such as evolutionarily significant units (ESUs) and management units (MUs), which are essential for evidence-based conservation. Finally, this study highlights the need to incorporate high-throughput sequencing (HTS) technologies and to participate in international initiatives, such as the Amphibian Genomics Consortium, as a strategic path forward for advancing adaptive conservation of Chilean amphibians.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12686-y.Keywords: Bibliometric, Genomic, Transcriptomic, Sequences

Introduction

Amphibians represent one of the most threatened groups of vertebrates on the planet, with more than 40% of their species at risk of extinction [1]. Their high sensitivity to environmental changes, dependence on water bodies for reproduction, and complex life cycles make them excellent ecological indicators of ecosystem disturbances [2]; however, these features also contribute to their heightened sensitivity to ecosystem alterations. Consequently, this group has been disproportionately affected in recent decades by factors such as habitat loss, invasive alien species, pollution, climate change, and emerging diseases such as chytridiomycosis caused by the fungus Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis [3]. These threats not only compromise their short-term survival but also challenge their long-term adaptive capacity, particularly in the absence of functional genetic diversity and applied genomic tools [4].

Amid growing environmental pressures, the concept of resilience—understood as the capacity of a population or species to resist, adapt, and recover from disturbances—has become central to conservation biology [5, 6]. Traditionally, resilience has been addressed from eco-physiological or demographic perspectives, emphasizing traits such as thermal tolerance, fecundity, or dispersal ability, which help species persist through environmental fluctuations [7]. However, a complementary and increasingly recognized dimension is genetic resilience, which refers to the ability of a population to maintain its genetic variability over time. This variability is critical for enabling adaptive responses to new or intensified environmental pressures [4, 8], particularly in small or isolated populations, where genetic drift and inbreeding can quickly erode adaptive potential.

A striking example of genetic resilience reducing extinction risk can be found in Spea hammondii, a threatened amphibian from southern California, where genomic studies revealed unexpected genetic connectivity among fragmented populations. This connectivity is instrumental in maintaining genetic diversity and supporting species recovery plans [9]. These cases highlight the need to integrate genetic parameters into conservation strategies, especially under rapid environmental change.

Genomics and transcriptomics tools have revolutionized the way we understand the adaptive capacity of species. These technologies allow the identification of relevant genetic variations associated with any phenotypic trait, such as thermal tolerance, pathogen resistance, or phenotypic plasticity, among other critical aspects of biological resilience [10–12]. Through the study of gene expression profiles, it is possible to evaluate in real time how organisms respond to environmental stress or infections [13]. However, despite their potential for conservation biology, the use of these tools in South American amphibians is still incipient, which is related to a limited representation in molecular databases and a lack of integration between functional genetics and conservation strategies [14]. While the biological significance of these data depends on their results, their mere presence, or absence, can itself be considered an informative metric. Therefore, the scarcity of genomic and transcriptomic resources indicates that these taxa remain underrepresented, despite being listed as at risk of extinction. This underrepresentation likely reflects structural and economic constraints that shape research capacity and data generation in the region, rather than a lack of scientific interest. In this sense, evaluating the availability of such data can provide a complementary perspective on existing knowledge gaps and help inform research priorities within conservation science.

The lack of molecular information limits the identification of evolutionarily significant units (ESUs) and management units (MUs), which are essential for prioritizing at-risk populations and guiding evidence-based conservation actions [15–17]. However, it has been proposed that management strategies should incorporate not only ecological variables but also genetic metrics that anticipate vulnerabilities and promote evolutionarily functional connectivity. For example, the use of molecular markers would allow the identification of limited genetic connectivity among isolated populations, which is considered one of the main threats to long-term resilience in multiple species, as it reduces gene flow, increases inbreeding, and limits adaptive capacity [18, 19].

Chile harbors a unique diversity of amphibians, with a high degree of endemism (60%) resulting from its biogeographic configuration and climatic heterogeneity [14, 20]. From deserts in northern Chile to temperate forests and sub-Antarctic ecosystems in southern Chile, Chilean amphibian species have evolved under extreme and varied environmental pressures, making them ideal models for exploring mechanisms of genetic resilience [21]. Nevertheless, many of these species are threatened by habitat fragmentation, invasive exotic species, changes in hydrological regimes, and extreme climatic events [22]. Despite their evolutionary and ecological value, Chilean amphibians remain largely understudied from a genetic standpoint, with limited availability of transcriptomes, whole genomes, or comprehensive genetic diversity assessments. Importantly, the absence of such information is not only a knowledge gap but also a signal of structural asymmetries in research efforts and conservation planning.

In this context, the lack of genomic and transcriptomic data is not only a current knowledge gap but also a research opportunity. The high level of amphibian endemism in Chile, combined with its wide range of environmental gradients and well-documented threats [14], constitutes an optimal setting for implementing and expanding the use of molecular tools in amphibian research. Even when genetic data are scarce or incomplete, documenting their presence or absence can help identify neglected taxa and support prioritization schemes. These approaches not only fill critical knowledge gaps but also position Chilean amphibians as novel model systems for understanding resilience mechanisms in extreme and changing environments.

This study aims to evaluate how the availability of genetic and molecular information informs amphibian conservation in Chile within the broader framework of resilience under environmental change. By adopting a multicriteria perspective that integrates genetic, evolutionary, demographic, and ecological dimensions, we address limitations arising from the uneven availability of molecular data across Chilean amphibian taxa. Specifically, this work examines patterns of data availability and absence and explores how these patterns may influence assessments of adaptive potential and the prioritization of conservation actions. In doing so, we propose a conceptual framework for incorporating genetic resilience into conservation planning, highlighting the value of multidimensional approaches for guiding future research and management strategies.

Materials and methods

Fifty-eight amphibian species belonging to 14 genera were evaluated in Chile. The species included in this study correspond to those recognized as valid on the basis of the most comprehensive and updated taxonomic information available (Table 1). Taxonomic determinations followed a wide range of sources, including Cuevas & Formas [23], Correa et al. [24], Vidal & Díaz-Páez [25], Lobos [26], Correa et al. [27], Fibla et al. All available taxonomic evidence was critically reviewed to ensure that each species evaluated reflects the current consensus in Chilean herpetology.

Data sources and bibliographic search strategies

A comprehensive review of the literature on the variables that constitute genetic resilience [31, 32] was conducted. For this purpose, bibliographic databases with extensive coverage of scientific literature, such as Scopus (Elsevier) and Web of Science Core Collection-WoS (Clarivate Analytics), were consulted [33]. The collection of bibliographic data was carried out via Boolean operators within the respective search interfaces. To optimize the retrieval of relevant information, keywords with truncation wildcards (*) were used to encompass terms related to the field of research. These included the following terms: distribution area and geographic presence (distribut* = distribution, distributions, distributed; habitat; occurrence* = occurrence, occurrences); population trends and main threats (populat* = population, populations; threat* = threat, threatened); evolutionary history from taxonomy (taxon* = taxon, taxonomic); genomics (genom* = genome, genomic, genomes) and transcriptomics (transcriptome* = transcriptome, transcriptomic, transcriptomics); emerging diseases (disease* = disease, diseases); and genetic information and sequences (gene* = gene, genes, genetic; sequenc* = sequences, sequencing).

To construct precise query parameters, each species was cross-referenced with each keyword (i.e., species × concept × database). These combinations were searched via the TITLE-ABS-KEY operator in Scopus and the TS operator in WoS, both of which cover the title, abstract, and keyword fields of each publication [34]. The extracted articles included metadata such as the article title, author names, author keywords, automatic keywords (kws plus for WOS; index kws for Scopus), publication year, abstract, journal name, DOI, ISSN, and database source. To evaluate eligibility and remove duplicates, the raw dataset was processed and exported to Microsoft Excel. The final filtering was based on unique DOIs.

To explore the conceptual structure of the filtered articles, we conducted a frequency analysis of abstracts and automatic keywords, as well as a co-occurrence network based exclusively on terms extracted from abstracts [35, 36]. To ensure thematic specificity in the conceptual structure, a customized stopword list was applied, which included general language terms (e.g., the, and, in, its) and frequently used technical-scientific terms (e.g., data, analysis, method, study) to exclude linguistic elements of low semantic value [37, 38]. Absolute term frequencies were calculated for each term in both abstracts and automatic keywords. The 40 most frequent terms in each section were then identified and visualized via horizontal bar charts to highlight the dominant concepts and thematic emphases present across the entire set of evaluated articles. For the construction of the term co-occurrence network, defined as the simultaneous appearance of two terms, the 120 most frequent words from the set of abstracts were selected to represent the corpus [33, 39]. All analytical procedures were implemented within a unified computational workflow in Python. Only connections with a pre-established co-occurrence weight (threshold > 5) were retained to avoid network distortion [40].

Thematic clustering was performed via the “greedy modularity communities” algorithm [41–43], and the spatial arrangement of nodes was achieved via the “spring layout” algorithm (stiffness k = 0.4; seed = 42) for visual interpretation [44, 45]. The following metrics were computed: i) network structure: number of nodes (N), number of edges (E), density and modularity (Q) as general indicators of network size and connectivity [42, 46–48]; ii) node-level centrality: degree (proportion of direct connections per node), weighted degree (total co-occurrence frequency with neighboring nodes), clustering coefficient (local cohesion among directly connected nodes), betweenness centrality (indicator of structural connectivity through nodes acting as semantic bridges), and closeness centrality (approximation of the average shortest path from a node to all others in the network) [36, 49–52]; and iii) community structure: number of clusters and nodes per cluster [33, 53].

All conceptual structure analyses were implemented via Python (3.12.3) with the following libraries: pandas 2.2.3 (data handling), matplotlib 3.10.1 and seaborn 0.13.2 (data visualization and plotting), networkx 3.4.2 (network modeling, analyses and visualization), built-in modules re (regular expressions), collections (frequency counting) and itertools (term combinations) [54–57]. Additionally, datasets obtained from citizen science initiatives (e.g., iNaturalist, GBIF), as well as from species conservation plans and government programs corresponding to the period 2017–2024 [58], were incorporated. On the basis of these occurrence records, maps were generated to assess the extent of the geographical distribution ranges of each species; evidence from the bibliographic search was also considered in the development of several criteria (next section).

Criteria for resilience potential

The evaluation of potential resilience was structured around five dimensions, with 11 quantitative assessment criteria (Table 2): distribution (which includes breadth of habitat, area of occupancy, and extent of occurrence); population trend (which includes threat level and population trend); emerging diseases (which includes levels of prevalence of emerging diseases); evolutionary history (which considers taxonomic uniqueness, genomic knowledge, and transcriptomic knowledge); and genetic records (which includes general genetic knowledge and sample GenBank records). Several criteria have been proposed by various authors to create potential resilience functions for amphibians and serve as a guide to assess the status of the populations of each species [14, 27–29, 59–62]. Breadth of habitat (BREHA): This criterion considers the ability of species to adapt to different types of habitats [63]. Species with a restricted range of habitats, owing to their specialized nature, should exhibit low ecological flexibility, making them more susceptible to local extinctions when their habitats are altered [21, 64–66]. From the resiliency perspective, species with a greater habitat range are expected to accumulate greater genetic diversity because of the great (micro) environmental heterogeneity that they are supposed to face [67]. This variability allows these species to respond better to abrupt environmental changes, in contrast to those with small ranges, which are frequently related to reduced genetic variability [68, 69], and hence, low response potentials against environmental changes. In Chile, restricted range has been identified as a limiting factor for amphibian conservation, as species with small areas are at a relatively high risk of extinction [70].

Area of occupancy (AOO): AOO is a standardized measure expressed in square kilometers that quantifies the surface area occupied by a given species. In this study, AOO values correspond to those reported in official IUCN assessments and national species classification regulations, following IUCN subcriterion B2 guidelines. AOO is calculated by counting the number of occupied grid cells, allowing assessment of how extinction risk is distributed among occupied fragments [71]. For Chilean microendemic amphibian species with highly restricted distributions, a grid resolution of 1 km2 was applied, consistent with IUCN recommendations. When a species occupies a very limited area, the probability that a localized threat will affect a significant portion of its distribution increases, thereby elevating extinction risk [72, 73], particularly in taxa with low resilience to disturbance, such as those with limited dispersal capacity [68].

Extent of occurrence (EOO): EOO is a standardized metric representing the geographical area encompassing all known, inferred, or projected occurrences of a species. EOO values used in this study were obtained from existing IUCN assessments and national classification frameworks, in accordance with IUCN subcriterion B1. EOO was delineated using the minimum convex polygon method, which defines the smallest polygon containing all known occurrence records of a taxon [1]. This measure enables evaluation of the spatial extent over which threats may act across a species’ geographic range, thereby helping to quantify the magnitude and distribution of extinction risk [73]. See also [14].

Level of Conservation Threat (LET): This is a standardized metric employed to classify the conservation status of a taxon. In Chile, species are categorized in accordance with the species classification regulation (RCE, in Spanish), which was instituted by Decree Nº 29 of 2011 [74]. The conservation status assigned to each species in this study was obtained from official Chilean classification processes conducted under this regulatory framework. Conservation classifications include the following: Extinct (EX), Extinct in the Wild (EW), Critically Endangered (CR), Endangered (EN), Vulnerable (VU), Near Threatened (NT), Least Concern (LC), and Data Deficient (DD).Although the RCE constitutes an independent national classification system, its assessment criteria are largely consistent with those established by the IUCN Red List. Consequently, conservation categories derived from the Chilean system are broadly comparable to IUCN categories. The categorization assigned to each species denotes its level of threat and is used here as a proxy for species-level resilience. Species classified as critically endangered or endangered generally demonstrate diminished resilience, as their capacity to adjust to environmental changes is constrained by reduced population sizes and narrow distribution ranges [75]. Thus, these evaluations enable the prioritization of conservation efforts by identifying taxa most vulnerable to environmental challenges [76].

Population trend (POPT): This criterion assesses temporal changes in population size within natural populations, considering demographic parameters such as mortality, birth rate, migration, and immigration [71]. Population reduction was quantified as the percentage decrease in the number of mature individuals over a defined temporal window, following IUCN subcriterion-based approaches [72, 73]. This metric provides a standardized proxy for evaluating population-level dynamics relevant to species persistence and comparative assessments within the index [68, 75]. Environmental context and population size thresholds were considered as underlying conditions influencing these trends [8].

Prevalence of emerging diseases (PREV): This criterion accounts for the occurrence and prevalence of emerging infectious diseases affecting amphibian populations, particularly those associated with documented population declines. The criterion was defined based on the reported presence of pathogens and disease-related impacts at the species level, as documented in the scientific literature [77]. Disease prevalence was treated as a categorical indicator reflecting differential exposure and response patterns among taxa, following established amphibian disease frameworks [78–80].

This classification was based on taxonomic status and phylogenetic information available in the literature, recognizing the relevance of genetic distinctiveness in comparative conservation assessments [82–84]. Conceptual foundations for incorporating phylogenetic considerations follow established conservation genetics frameworks [8].

Genomic knowledge (GENOM): This criterion is directly linked to genetic resilience, as genomic information enables the identification of adaptive variants, the estimation of genomic diversity, and the detection of signatures of natural selection associated with environmental factors [4, 8, 85]. The assessment of this criterion considered the availability of genomic and transcriptomic resources for each species, including whole-genome data, reduced-representation sequencing, or functional genomic information reported in the literature [12, 32, 86, 87]. The existence of such data supports evaluations of genomic diversity, population structure, and adaptive potential within comparative frameworks. Genomic datasets further allow the evaluation of demographic and genetic processes such as population bottlenecks, levels of inbreeding, and functional connectivity among populations, which are relevant for species management, particularly in taxa with small or fragmented populations [19, 88–90]. Transcriptomic knowledge (TRANS): This criterion captures the availability of transcriptomic information relevant for evaluating functional genomic responses at the species level. Transcriptomic data enable the characterization of gene expression patterns associated with environmental factors such as temperature, salinity, or desiccation, providing insight into context-dependent biological responses [99, 100].Unlike genomic data, transcriptomes reflect dynamic regulatory processes and therefore represent sensitive indicators of functional responses to environmental variation [99, 101, 102]. The assessment of this criterion considered the presence of high-quality transcriptomic resources, including differential gene expression studies and functional pathway analyses reported in the literature [103, 104]. Transcriptomic datasets support the identification of regulatory genes, metabolic pathways, and cellular processes associated with tolerance mechanisms, including detoxification, immunoregulation, and protein stability [103, 104]. The absence of transcriptomic information constrains the interpretation of genomic functionality in ecological contexts and limits the evaluation of rapid or plastic responses to emerging environmental stressors [100]. The presence or absence of transcriptomic data was therefore treated as an indicator of functional knowledge availability within the RePo index, recognizing its relevance for comparative assessments of adaptive potential and resilience [105–108].

Transcriptomic knowledge (TRANS): This criterion captures the availability of transcriptomic information relevant for evaluating functional genomic responses at the species level. Transcriptomic data enable the characterization of gene expression patterns associated with environmental factors such as temperature, salinity, or desiccation, providing insight into context-dependent biological responses [99, 100].

Unlike genomic data, transcriptomes reflect dynamic regulatory processes and therefore represent sensitive indicators of functional responses to environmental variation [99, 101, 102]. The assessment of this criterion considered the presence of high-quality transcriptomic resources, including differential gene expression studies and functional pathway analyses reported in the literature [103, 104]. General genetic knowledge (GENET): This criterion reflects the availability of genetic information derived from the characterization of specific loci, coding regions, or partial DNA sequences for each species. Such data provide a basis for assessing genetic variability, population structure, and historical or recent demographic processes relevant to comparative resilience assessments [19, 109, 110]. Genetic datasets allow the estimation of intraspecific genetic diversity, the detection of bottlenecks, and the identification of alleles potentially associated with local adaptation [111, 112]. The presence of functional genetic variants, particularly in genes related to stress responses, has been documented across multiple taxa and represents a relevant dimension for evaluating adaptive potential within disturbed or heterogeneous environments [113–115].The assessment of this criterion considered whether genetic markers or sequence-based data were available for each species, including mitochondrial genes, nuclear loci, or microsatellite markers, as reported in the literature [116–118]. Metrics such as genetic differentiation, population structuring, and expected heterozygosity provide information on population genetic status and were therefore treated as indicators of genetic knowledge availability rather than direct measures of resilience [19, 119, 120]. Conversely, the absence of genetic data or metrics limits the ability to infer adaptive responses or extinction risk, constraining comparative evaluations within the index [121, 122].

Biobanks store genetic material, tissues, and whole organisms that can be accessed for conservation-related research and management purposes [123, 124]. Digital repositories provide access to genetic sequences and associated metadata, supporting the documentation of genetic and evolutionary information across taxa [12, 125]. The presence or absence of records in these systems was recorded to characterize data availability relevant to conservation assessments [126].

Therefore, to assess the availability of genetic sequences for each species, an exhaustive search was conducted in GenBank, the nucleotide database of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) [127]. Each species was queried by its scientific name within the “nucleotide” field of GenBank [128]. Additionally, four filters and their associated terms were applied to capture different types of sequences: (i) “genom*”, “complete genome”, “genomic DNA”, and “genomic assembly”; (ii) “trans*”, “transcriptome”, “mRNA”, and “RNA-seq”; (iii) “epigen*”: “epigenome”, “epigenetic”, “DNA methylation”, and “histone modification”; and (iv) “genet*”, “genetic marker”, “microsatellite”, “SNP (single-nucleotide polymorphism)”, “COI”, and “16S”. A virtual environment was configured with Biopython v1.81 [129] and Pandas v1.5.0 [130]. To optimize record retrieval, the parameter usehistory = “y” from the Entrez module of Biopython was employed, which enabled the retrieval of large volumes of data in batches [128]. Each batch was subsequently processed through the SeqIO module of Biopython [129]. To evaluate data representativeness, the metadata were stored in Microsoft Excel. Each filter was coded as presence = 1 and absence = 0, on the basis of the availability of corresponding sequences. All sequence queries were conducted in Python 3.12.3 and executed in a macOS terminal 2.14.

Evaluation of resilience potential

Five dimensions were evaluated with eleven criteria on a scale of 0–4, on the basis of the proposal by Vidal et al. [14] and the references therein (see Table 2). The criteria BREHA, AOO, EOO, and LET were rated on a scale of 0–3, whereas POPT, PREV, TAXU, GENOM, TRANS, GENET, and SGBR were evaluated on a scale of 0–4. Four resilience categories were defined for the evaluation: no resilience, low resilience, medium resilience, and high resilience. Each category was established on the basis of the frequency distribution of resilience potential (RePo) values, according to the quartile intervals Q1, Q2 and Q3. SDMs under the current climate for species with extreme resilience potential

Species distribution models (SDMs) were calibrated for two species with contrasting potential resilience values by applying the random forest algorithm in R version 4.4.3 [131, 132]. Nineteen bioclimatic variables from the WorldClim 2.1 dataset at a resolution of 30 arc-seconds were used, along with georeferenced presence records from mainland Chile [133–135]. Only records with spatial uncertainty ≤ 1 km were retained to match the resolutions of the environmental predictors [136]. To reduce spatial pseudoreplication, one record from each pair located within 1 km was excluded on the basis of a geodetic distance matrix [137, 138]. The environmental layers were cropped to a shared geographic extent and masked with the administrative boundary of Chile [139, 140]. Multicollinearity among bioclimatic predictors was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF), retaining only variables with VIF < 10 [141, 142]. Following this procedure, seven non-collinear predictors were retained for model calibration, while highly correlated variables were excluded. Fifty independent model replicates were performed per species to capture variation due to data partitioning [143]. For each replicate, pseudoabsences were randomly generated at a 1:10 ratio relative to the presence records [144, 145]. The occurrence data were randomly split into 70% for training and 30% for validation [146]. Each model was fitted with 500 trees [147], and performance was evaluated via the area under the ROC curve (AUC) [148, 149]. The resulting predictions from all the replicates were averaged to generate a continuous map of climatic suitability [140, 150–152]. The relative importance of predictors was estimated as the mean decrease Gini (MDG) index across replicates [147, 153]. The area (km2) with high climatic suitability was calculated as the sum of raster cell surfaces with occurrence probabilities above 0.75 [140, 154], a conservative threshold selected to delineate core suitable habitat and to reduce commission errors in suitability-based spatial analyses. To evaluate habitat configuration, the SDMs were binarized using the same threshold (> 0.75), ensuring consistency between suitability estimation and subsequent spatial metrics. Fragmentation metrics, including the number of patches (np), mean patch area, and clumpiness index, which reflect habitat extent and spatial aggregation under current climatic conditions, were calculated following Hesselbarth et al. [155, 156].

At the species level, 17% of the evaluated taxa were classified as having medium resilience, according to the resilience potential (RePo) categories defined in the Methods section. This group includes Rhinella spinulosa, Pleurodema thaul, Pleurodema bufoninum, Eupsophus calcaratus, Batrachyla antartandica, Batrachyla leptopus, Batrachyla taeniata, Eupsophus emiliopugini, Hylorina sylvatica, and Atelognathus nitoi (Table 5). In contrast, the remaining 83% of species were classified as nonresilient.

Based on the quartile-based classification of RePo values (see Methods and Fig. 1), most species fell within the no-resilience and low-resilience categories, whereas no taxa reached the high-resilience category.

A comparison of criterion scores between the species with the highest (Rhinella spinulosa) and lowest (Alsodes vittatus) RePo values revealed significant differences (W = 0.0, p = 0.004; Table 5). Alsodes vittatus showed lower scores for several criteria, including breadth of habitat (BREHA), area of occupancy (AOO), and extent of occurrence (EOO), while also presenting higher conservation threat levels (LET) and lower population trend values (POPT) (Fig. 6). Spatial differences in resilience potential were also evident in the species distribution models (SDMs; Fig. 7), which showed high predictive performance for both species (AUC = 0.93 for R. spinulosa; AUC = 0.97 for A. vittatus). Based on areas with high occurrence probabilities (> 0.75), R. spinulosa (n = 120) exhibited a projected suitable area of 2,448.1 km2 within Chile, whereas A. vittatus (n = 5) showed a substantially smaller projected extent of 200.71 km2 (Fig. 7B).

Habitat configuration metrics further differed between species. Rhinella spinulosa presented a more continuous spatial structure, characterized by 75 discrete patches, a mean patch area of 34.14 km2, and a clumpiness index of 0.78. In contrast, A. vittatus was associated with 34 smaller patches, a mean patch area of 5.93 km2, and a clumpiness index of 0.80. For the BREHAcriterion, no families presented critically low values; the highest scores were recorded for Batrachylidae, Bufonidae, and Leptodactylidae. No extreme values were observed for AOO or EOO; however, the most restricted ranges were found in Telmatobiidae (both criteria) and Rhinodermatidae (EOO). For the LET and POPT criteria, low values were recorded across Alsodidae, Calyptocephalellidae, Rhinodermatidae, Bufonidae, Leptodactylidae, Telmatobiidae, and Ceratophryidae. With respect to PREV, Leptodactylidae recorded the highest scores. In contrast, Alsodidae, Calyptocephalellidae, Bufonidae, Telmatobiidae, and Ceratophryidae presented intermediate values with a negative influence. For TAXU, lower values were observed in Batrachylidae, Calyptocephalellidae, and Rhinodermatidae. The GENOM, TRANS, and SGBR criteria showed consistently limited values across all families. In terms of GENET, lower values were detected in Calyptocephalellidae, Rhinodermatidae, and Ceratophryidae. On the basis of all the parameters comprising the resilience potential index, Telmatobiidae was identified as the family with the lowest resilience.

At the genus level (Fig. 5), specific patterns were observed among taxa. For BREHA, Insuetophrynus presented the most restrictive value. Similarly, Insuetophrynus and Telmatobius presented low AOO and EOO values, which is consistent with highly restricted distributions. For LET, the highest values were found in Batrachyla, Hylorina, and Nannophryne, all of which included a majority of species categorized as least concern.

For POPT, only Atelognathus and Telmatobufo presented relatively high values.

For PREV, Chaltenobatrachus and Nannophryne presented lower scores.

With respect to TAXU, high values were recorded in Alsodes, Atelognathus, Eupsophus, Pleurodema, Rhinella, and Telmatobius.

For the GENOM, only Calyptocephalella presented a greater value than the other taxa did. This genus, along with Rhinella, was the only one with TRANS values. The GENET criterion returned a limited value only for Nannophryne. Finally, in SGBR, molecular data remain unavailable for Atelognathus and Chaltenobatrachus. On the basis of the complete set of parameters that integrate the resilience potential index, Insuetophrynus was identified as the genus with no resilience (see Fig. 5).

Discussion

A review of the scientific literature on Chilean amphibians revealed a predominance of biogeographic and physiological research, reflected in the high frequency of terms such as species, Chile, populations, and distribution [201–204]. Significant interest in behavioral and bioacoustic aspects was also evident, as indicated by terms such as calls, presence, individuals, and genera, along with a growing emphasis on species distribution modeling [202, 205–207]. In parallel, the presence of terms such as variation, size, body, and southern point to an active line of research focused on phenotypic variability [208–210]. However, the low representation of terms such as genetic, ecosystem, priority, conservation, and climate [107, 211], highlights an underrepresentation of critical topics related to genetic resilience, conservation biology, and climate change. Notably, the clustering analysis conducted in this study points toward a conceptual network with limited thematic differentiation and high overlap among research lines [212]. This observation is reinforced when the organization patterns of the currently available literature, which are mostly focused on traditional approaches centered on natural history, taxonomy, and species distribution, are examined [213, 214]. These research patterns also reflect a very scarce integration of omics tools, such as genomics and transcriptomics, thereby limiting the advancement of contemporary conceptual frameworks for understanding resilience in Chilean amphibians.

The empirical findings reinforce this trend, as most species analyzed lack transcriptomic information and data on emerging diseases, restricting the assessment of their potential adaptive response mechanisms [14]. Importantly, however, the RePo index does not evaluate the specific content or results of omic studies but rather their existence or absence for each species. In this sense, the lack of molecular studies is, by itself, a significant indicator of informational vulnerability, limiting the capacity to monitor, interpret, and respond to environmental changes. The molecular representation of available sequences is uneven across genera; while Eupsophus and Batrachyla are relatively well represented, genera such as Atelognathus and Chaltenobatrachus remain absent from sequence databases. This informational gap, highlighted by the RePo index, reflects asymmetries in research coverage that may arise from multiple factors, including logistical, ethical, and legal constraints associated with sampling rare or threatened taxa. Importantly, while limited molecular data can restrict the integration of genetic perspectives into conservation planning, conservation decisions are typically informed by a combination of genetic, ecological, behavioural, and demographic evidence, with genomic information representing one complementary—though increasingly valuable—component of integrative conservation strategies.

The absence of species classified as highly resilient according to the RePo index (Fig. 1) suggests that, within the evaluated taxa, resilience is generally constrained by a combination of ecological, demographic, and historical factors rather than reflecting a single underlying cause. Families such as Telmatobiidae and genera such as Insuetophrynus exhibit particularly low resilience values, which are associated with restricted geographic ranges, small population sizes, and limited availability of molecular information. In contrast, species such as Rhinella spinulosa and Pleurodema thaul, which display intermediate levels of resilience, may serve as informative comparative taxa for exploring how adaptive mechanisms operate in amphibians. Their ecological breadth and relative data availability make them useful reference points for examining patterns of adaptive capacity that can inform conservation-oriented research and management strategies, without implying their status as classical experimental model organisms.

A particularly notable case is Alsodes vittatus, which was recently rediscovered after 130 years without records [215]. Its high LET, low POPT, and lack of molecular data position this species as one of the most vulnerable in Chile. Compared with R. spinulosa, which has greater environmental tolerance, the predictive model for A. vittatus is strongly dependent on specific climatic variables and has a very limited potential distribution [204, 215, 216]. Therefore, this microhabitat dependence may have contributed to its low adaptive capacity (Fig. 6).

The marked differences in resilience potential observed between Rhinella spinulosa and Alsodes vittatus likely reflect contrasting ecological and biological profiles. Species with broader habitat use, larger geographic ranges, and higher spatial connectivity tend to exhibit higher resilience potential, whereas narrowly distributed species with fragmented habitats may be more vulnerable to environmental disturbances. In addition, the limited availability of molecular data for A. vittatus constrains the assessment of its long-term adaptive capacity, highlighting the importance of integrating genetic and genomic information into resilience evaluations. At a broader scale, the analysis of resilience criteria by family suggests that shared life-history traits, degrees of endemism, and ecological constraints may influence resilience patterns across taxonomic groups (Fig. 4). Such family-level similarities may partly explain consistent differences in resilience potential among lineages and underscore the relevance of incorporating evolutionary and ecological context into conservation prioritization frameworks.

At the family level, the high BREHA values in Batrachylidae, Bufonidae, and Leptodactylidae could be associated with transboundary endemism, which facilitates broader ecological breadth [14, 217, 218]. In contrast, the low AOO and EOO values in Telmatobiidae and Rhinodermatidae reveal restricted and isolated distributions [22, 219, 220], whereas the low LET and POPT scores across multiple families indicate a predominance of fragmented or declining populations [14], especially in Rhinodermatidae [22, 66, 221, 222].

High PREV scores in Leptodactylidae may reflect lower exposure to aquatic pathogens because of their association with dry habitats [60, 223]. In contrast, intermediate scores in Alsodidae, Calyptocephalellidae, Bufonidae, Telmatobiidae, and Ceratophryidae indicate greater susceptibility to Bd or Rv [224]. Low TAXU scores in Batrachylidae, Calyptocephalellidae, and Rhinodermatidae may be due to ancient lineages or recent taxonomic rearrangements [225–227]. In addition, there is a generalized technical limitation in obtaining GENOM, TRANS, and SGBR data across nearly all families [32]. The case of GENET is particularly critical in Calyptocephalellidae, Rhinodermatidae, and Ceratophryidae, where low genetic diversity may reflect evolutionary conservatism [228].

In the genus-level analysis, Insuetophrynus stands out because of its extremely low BREHA, which aligns with its specialization in a single aquatic environment [229, 230]. Its low resilience is further supported by its minimum AOO and EOO values, similar to those reported in Telmatobius [231, 232]. In contrast, genera such as Batrachyla, Hylorina, and Nannophryne have high LET scores, which are associated with low extinction risk [233]. The high POPT values in Atelognathus and Telmatobufo may be due to recent records of new populations [20, 234, 235]. The low PREV scores in Chaltenobatrachus and Nannophryne may be linked to ephemeral and periglacial habitats that limit pathogen persistence [236, 237].

High TAXU values in Alsodes, Atelognathus, Eupsophus, Pleurodema, Rhinella, and Telmatobius reflect deep divergences or well-resolved phylogenies [238–240]. Calyptocephalella is the only genus with relatively high GENOM values, possibly due to its early divergence within Neobatrachia. In addition to Rhinella, it was also one of the only genera with available TRANS data, likely due to prioritization in sequencing programs [12, 211, 228]. In Nannophryne, the low GENET value may reflect sampling limitations across its wide range [241], whereas the absence of SGBR data in Atelognathus and Chaltenobatrachus precludes any robust genetic assessment.

Reductions in population size have direct implications for genetic resilience, as declining populations tend to exhibit lower genetic variability, which constrains adaptive capacity under environmental change [68, 75]. Such demographic contractions are often driven by external stressors, reinforcing the dependence of population persistence on environmental conditions that shape survival and resilience [8]. Emerging infectious diseases represent an additional layer of vulnerability, particularly in amphibians, where pathogen outbreaks have been associated with elevated mortality rates and severe range contractions [77]. While some taxa exhibit partial tolerance or reduced impacts, widespread disease prevalence remains a major factor influencing population trajectories and resilience outcomes [78–80]. Taxonomic and phylogenetic uniqueness further modulate conservation relevance. The loss of species representing isolated or monotypic lineages implies a disproportionate erosion of evolutionary history and genetic diversity [81, 82]. Preserving phylogenetically distinct taxa is therefore critical for maintaining evolutionary potential and long-term adaptive capacity, especially in regions facing rapid environmental change [83, 84]. In Chile, emblematic endemic amphibians such as Calyptocephalella gayi and Insuetophrynus acarpicus exemplify this vulnerability, having been prioritized for conservation due to the convergence of multiple threats affecting their populations [242]. As emphasized by Shaffer et al. [8], safeguarding phylogenetically distinct lineages is essential for conserving both evolutionary legacy and future resilience.

According to the RePo index, eighty-three percent of the evaluated species were classified as nonresilient species (38% with low resilience and 45% with no resilience), suggesting a high level of ecological vulnerability. This situation is associated with high levels of ecological specialization and the low representation of omic data in public databases [4]. A key aspect of genetic resilience is the identification of evolutionarily significant units (ESUs) and management units (MUs) on the basis of genetic and ecological criteria [15, 16]. These units allow for the planning of future conservation strategies that consider evolutionary history, population genetic structure, and adaptive capacity [16, 243, 244]. Indeed, these parameters have already proven to be useful in prioritizing at-risk populations, as in the case of Rana luteiventris [89].

From a methodological perspective, the RePo index developed in this study constitutes an integrative tool to assess resilience across multiple dimensions, complementing existing prioritization indices [14]. Its novelty lies in its ability to identify whether critical types of information, such as genomic, transcriptomic, or demographic data, are available for each species, regardless of their content or quality. This agnostic yet structured approach allows for the identification of priority gaps in knowledge generation while facilitating comparisons across taxa. The RePo framework aligns with recent advances in conservation genomics and helps operationalize concepts such as ESUs and MUs [17, 89].

In ecological terms, resilience refers to the ability of a system to absorb disturbances without losing its essential functions and structure [6]. This concept can be represented as the depth and breadth of a “stability basin,” where more resilient ecosystems can withstand greater perturbations before shifting into an alternative state [5, 245, 246]. Disruption of feedback mechanisms may be irreversible, leading to significant functional or biodiversity loss [247, 248]. Functional diversity and ecological redundancy are key to maintaining ecosystem resilience, enabling multiple species to fulfill similar roles across spatial and temporal scales [249–252].

Genomic information provides critical insights into the mechanisms underlying adaptive resilience, as it enables the identification of functional variants, genomic diversity patterns, and signatures of selection linked to environmental gradients [4, 8, 85]. In species with well-characterized genomes, such as Xenopus laevis and Rhinella marina, genomic analyses have identified functional regions associated with immune responses, thermal tolerance, and adaptation to contrasting environments, allowing a more detailed understanding of adaptive capacity [32, 86, 87].

In Chile, endemic amphibian species such as Alsodes gargola and Batrachyla leptopus have been prioritized for genomic sequencing due to their potential to reveal adaptive mechanisms associated with high-altitude environments [12]. Genomic data also enable the evaluation of bottlenecks, inbreeding levels, and functional connectivity among populations, which are particularly relevant for the management of species with small or fragmented distributions [19, 88–90]. However, the limited availability of genomic information restricts the capacity to assess adaptive responses and hampers the integration of many species into evolutionarily sustainable conservation strategies [91, 92]. This limitation is especially pronounced in neotropical amphibians, where genomic representation remains uneven [12, 32]. Beyond genomic variation, epigenetic mechanisms provide a complementary regulatory layer influencing gene expression in response to environmental stimuli [93]. Processes such as DNA methylation and histone remodeling have been identified as key regulators of chromatin structure, genomic stability, and transcriptional activity [94, 95]. These reversible modifications contribute to phenotypic plasticity and may enhance short-term tolerance to environmental disturbances, thereby playing a role in resilience under rapidly changing conditions [96–98].

Transcriptomic approaches provide a dynamic perspective on adaptive resilience by revealing gene expression responses to environmental variation, including temperature, hydric stress, salinity, and desiccation [99, 100]. Because transcriptomes reflect context-dependent regulatory activity, they offer sensitive indicators of functional adaptation that complement static genomic information [99, 101, 102]. In amphibians, transcriptomic analyses have elucidated molecular responses associated with survival and phenotypic plasticity under extreme environmental conditions. For example, studies on Rana kukunoris and R. arvalis have identified expression profiles linked to tolerance to thermal and hydric stress, highlighting the role of regulatory pathways in mediating resilience [105, 106]. Similarly, in Rhinella spinulosa, transcriptomic analyses detected signals of positive selection in transcription factors associated with thermal variables, suggesting their involvement in local adaptation to extreme environments [107].

Beyond species-specific examples, transcriptomic data facilitate the identification of regulatory genes, metabolic pathways, and cellular processes involved in tolerance mechanisms such as detoxification, immunoregulation, and protein stability [103, 104]. However, the absence of transcriptomic information severely limits the capacity to interpret genomic function in ecological contexts and constrains the assessment of rapid responses to emerging threats [100]. As such, transcriptomic knowledge provides a mechanistic framework for anticipating how species may respond to future environmental change, including climate change scenarios, and represents a key component for interpreting short-term resilience and adaptive capacity [105, 108].

The absence of transcriptomic data limits our ability to detect key adaptive responses such as gene regulation under thermal stress, especially in endemic species restricted to montane microhabitats [12, 103]. Studies on Rana arvalis have shown that certain phenotypes can re-emerge in restored habitats if lineages with sufficient genetic diversity persist [10], whereas Rana temporaria displays phenotypic differentiation associated with environmental gradients and predator presence [11]. Furthermore, genetic connectivity—fundamental for spatial and demographic resilience—depends on species ecology and tolerance to hydric variability [18, 253]. The low dispersal capacity of many species has been corroborated by genetic studies showing strong population structure [19, 254].

General genetic knowledge represents a foundational component for interpreting species’ adaptive potential, as information derived from specific loci, coding regions, or partial DNA sequences enables the assessment of genetic variability, population structure, and demographic history [19, 109, 110]. Genetic differentiation patterns, levels of heterozygosity, and evidence of historical or recent bottlenecks provide valuable insights into population genetic health and adaptive capacity under rapidly changing environments [111, 112, 119, 120]. Functional genetic variants, particularly in genes involved in responses to environmental stressors, have been shown to confer selective advantages in contaminated, fragmented, or thermally stressed habitats [113–115]. Such genetic signals may translate into phenotypic differences relevant for survival, as documented in several amphibian taxa [116]. In Chile, studies on species such as Pleurodema thaul, Rhinella arunco, and R. atacamensis have revealed significant genetic structuring and low connectivity among populations, suggesting local differentiation associated with environmental gradients [117, 118].

The availability of genetic sequences in biobanks and digital repositories further expands conservation options by facilitating access to genetic material, comparative analyses, and long-term data preservation [12, 123–126]. Conversely, limited representation in repositories such as GenBank constrains the capacity to integrate species into evolutionarily informed conservation strategies and reduces opportunities for assisted management or restoration actions [121, 122, 126]. Together, general genetic knowledge and the presence of curated genetic records constitute critical enabling conditions for resilience-based conservation planning, particularly for threatened amphibian species facing accelerating environmental change.

In light of these findings, and if the goal is to position Chilean amphibians as model systems for studying genomic adaptation in extreme environments [12], then it would be highly advisable to integrate sequencing techniques into subsequent amphibian research, as well as increase participation in initiatives such as the Amphibian Genomics Consortium.
