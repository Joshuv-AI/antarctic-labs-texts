Harnessing local chemical order in high-entropy ceramics for broadband electromagnetic absorption
Authors: Yanhui Chu, Fangchao Gu, Lei Zhuang, Ziyu Feng, Wu Wang, Hulei Yu, Bo Xu, Zibin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electromagnetic pollution from the rapid expansion of wireless and electronic systems demands advanced absorber materials. High-entropy ceramics offer a promising platform for broadband electromagnetic wave attenuation, yet their rational design remains underdeveloped. Here, we introduce a mechanism-driven strategy to enhance absorption by tailoring local chemical order via simple thermal processing. Applied to high-entropy carbides, this local chemical order-engineering approach delivers a record effective absorption bandwidth of 11.2 GHz. Atomic-resolution chemical mapping paired with theoretical simulations reveals that local chemical order, rather than atomic vacancies, governs the dominant electromagnetic wave absorption mechanisms. We further demonstrate the universality of this principle across diverse high-entropy ceramic families (borides, oxides, sulfides, silicides, and selenides), each exhibiting substantial performance gains. This work establishes a clear, structure-guided framework for designing high-performance single-phase absorbers and offers a versatile route to mitigate electromagnetic pollution through targeted control of atomic-scale chemical order.

Introduction

The proliferation of electromagnetic (EM) technologies, from global 5 G/6 G communications and the Internet of Things (IoT) to advanced medical diagnostics and defense systems, has led to increasingly severe EM pollution, which degrades device performance, risks sensitive data leakage, and raises health concerns1–4. To counteract this “electronic smog,” high-performance electromagnetic wave (EMW) absorbers that irreversibly convert EM energy into heat or other forms are essential. Research on EMW absorbers has bifurcated into multi-phase composites (e.g., FexN@graphite5 and Fe/Fe3O4@C6) and single-phase materials (e.g., MAXs/MXenes7,8, graphene9, and ceramics10,11). While composites offer design flexibility, single-phase absorbers are favored for their structural simplicity and stability. Yet, the inherently fixed EM parameters of conventional single-phase absorbers often lead to poor impedance matching and narrow effective absorption bandwidths (EABs), typically below 5 GHz, which is insufficient for next-generation applications.

Early attempts to overcome these limitations focused on heteroatom doping, a strategy to modulate electronic structure and induce localized polarization. While this yielded modest gains in materials like N/S/P/B-doped graphene12 and Sc-doped MAX13, the impact is often marginal due to low dopant concentrations, with EABs rarely exceeding 6 GHz. More radical advances emerged with the high-entropy design concept14–18, yielding single-phase high-entropy ceramics such as carbides (HECs)19, MAXs20, sulfides (HESs)21, and oxides (HEOs)22,23. By incorporating multiple principal elements into one lattice, these materials leverage severe lattice distortion and tunable electronic structures to enhance EMW absorption. Notably, our recent work on lattice-distortion engineering in high-entropy diborides (HEBs) achieved a promising EAB of 7.2 GHz24. Despite this progress, a critical mechanistic puzzle has hindered further advancement: the complex interplay between different defect types, specifically vacancies and local chemical order (LCO), which represents non-random, short-range correlations in elemental occupancy at the atomic scale25–27, makes their individual contributions to EMW absorption ambiguous. This lack of fundamental understanding has impeded the rational design of superior high-entropy absorbers.

In this work, we resolve this mechanistic ambiguity and establish a universally applicable design paradigm for high-entropy EMW absorbers. We employ a facile and scalable thermal-processing strategy to precisely tailor LCO within the crystal lattice. Through a synergy of atomic-resolution chemical mapping along with predictive molecular dynamics and Monte Carlo (MD/MC) simulations, we demonstrate conclusively that LCO, rather than structural vacancies, is the dominant factor in EM energy dissipation. Harnessing this LCO, which significantly amplifies atomic-interface polarization in the prototype HEC system, yields an ultrabroad EAB of 11.2 GHz and a minimum reflection loss (RLmin) of −67.4 dB, outperforming virtually all reported single-phase absorbers. Critically, we confirm the universality of LCO tailoring across diverse high-entropy families (borides, oxides, sulfides, silicides, and selenides). This work therefore provides not only a key mechanistic insight but also a generalizable framework for the design of next-generation absorbers in telecommunications and stealth applications.

Results and discussion

The HEC family with a pure face-centered cubic (FCC) structure was selected as our model system. Impedance matching and EMW attenuation are the key factors affecting the EMW absorption performance53. Given the comparable impedance matching (Supplementary Fig. 5) and the marginal contribution of magnetic loss to the EMW attenuation—as evidenced by the minimal fluctuations of magnetic parameters μ′ and μ″ (indicators of magnetic storage and dissipation capabilities, respectively) around 1 and 0 (Supplementary Fig. 6)—the enhanced absorption performance can be primarily attributed to dielectric loss. Notably, the elemental intensity fluctuation in current thermally optimized 9HEC (0.36–0.54; see Supplementary Fig. 9) is markedly higher than that of the prior pristine nine-cation high-entropy diboride (0.16–0.34)24. We therefore hypothesized that LCO—manipulated and intensified via thermal processing—governs the exceptional EMW absorption through enhancing atomic-interface polarization loss (Fig. 1g).

The initial findings point toward LCO-induced polarization as the dominant EMW absorption mechanism, amplified by thermal processing. Since LCO, the non-random arrangement of multiple elements at atomic sites, physically manifests as collective lattice distortion, we hypothesized that systematic control of this distortion would allow us to directly probe LCO’s impact on EMW absorption. Accordingly, we designed a series of single-phase, FCC HECs with increasing principal-element count—from four to nine (4–9HECs; Supplementary Table 3)—to serve as a tunable platform for lattice distortion. First, we carried out MD simulations to compute the atomic strain energy (U).55 These metrics provide an energetic quantification of lattice distortion by accounting for both the deviation of atoms from their intrinsic lattice sites and the interactions among different atomic species. As shown in Fig. 2a, U increases monotonically with the number of elements, confirming that compositional complexity can effectively tune lattice distortion. Experimentally, the 4−9HEC series were synthesized at 2000 °C (see “Methods”) and verified as pure, single-phase solid solutions via X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 10) and EDS analyses (Supplementary Fig. 11). Through Williamson−Hall analysis56,57, we calculated the experimental microstrain (ε) from XRD peak broadening and revealed an increasing trend with elemental complexity (Supplementary Fig. 12), which is in excellent agreement with our MD predictions (Fig. 2a). Next, we evaluated the EMW absorption performance across the 4–9HEC series. Reflection-loss (RL) spectra from the samples (Fig. 2c) reveal a progressive broadening of the EAB with increasing elemental complexity (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 13), indicating that greater lattice distortion directly enhances absorption performance. This observation is corroborated by a monotonic increase in the attenuation constant (α) across the 4−9HEC series (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 14, derived from Supplementary Fig. 15). Note that another key factor influencing EMW absorption performance—impedance matching—shows significant improvement from 4- to 7HEC, but remains at a comparable level from 7- to 9HEC (Supplementary Fig. 16). This plateau contrasts with the continuously increasing trend observed across 4−9HECs, and thus the EAB expansion in the HEC series can be principally ascribed to enhanced EMW attenuation induced by greater lattice distortion. In principle, dielectric loss and magnetic loss are both critical attenuation mechanisms for EMW absorbers. However, our HECs are typical dielectric materials and exhibit marginal magnetic loss, as indicated by the values of μ′ being close to 1 and μ″ near 0. To illustrate this point more clearly, the dielectric and magnetic dissipation parameters, ε″ and μ″, respectively, for the 4–9HEC series were averaged and summarized in Supplementary Fig. 17. The data distinctly show that μ″ remains negligible, while ε″ increases markedly with increasing constituent elements—a trend that aligns well with the broadening of EAB observed in Fig. 2d. These results collectively verify that dielectric loss is the dominant attenuation mechanism in these HECs.

To deconvolve the underlying dielectric attenuation mechanisms, we first distinguished between polarization and conduction losses. Overall, the dielectric parameters ε′ and ε″ both increase with greater elemental complexity, as summarized in Supplementary Fig. 18, indicating concurrent enhancement in dielectric storage and dissipation capabilities. Cole-Cole diagrams show that the number of semicircles, each corresponding to a Debye-type polarization relaxation29, increases with the number of constituent elements (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Fig. 19), signifying enhanced polarization loss. In contrast, conduction-related loss diminishes as compositional complexity rises: the trailing tails characteristic of free-carrier conduction in 4−5HECs vanish in 6−9HECs, suggesting a negligible role for conduction loss. As summarized in Supplementary Fig. 20 (specific values can be found in Supplementary Table 4), the ratio \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varepsilon }_{p}^{{\prime} {\prime} }/{{\rm{\varepsilon }}}^{{\prime} {\prime}}$$\end{document}εp″/ε″ substantially increases from 67.7% to 94.8% for 4–9HECs, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varepsilon }_{c}^{{\prime} {\prime} }/{{\rm{\varepsilon }}}^{{\prime} {\prime}}$$\end{document}εc″/ε″ correspondingly decreases from 32.3% to 5.2%. These trends confirm that conduction loss, while still contributing in HECs, becomes progressively negligible as elemental complexity increases. In contrast, polarization loss increasingly dominates the dielectric dissipation. To substantiate this observation, we measured the electrical conductivity (σ) of the HEC series (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Table 5), revealing a marked decrease from 1.15 S−1·m−1 (4HEC) to 0.62 S−1·m−1 (9HEC). This trend is consistent with increased electron scattering resulting from lattice distortion, suggesting that lattice distortion plays a dominant role over vacancies that typically enhance electrical conductivity58. Such lattice distortion-governed conductivity mechanism has been further corroborated by the studies on fully dense HEC bulk samples (Supplementary Fig. 21, Supplementary Table 6). The inverse relationship between σ and EAB across the HEC series thus rules out conduction loss as a driver of the EMW attenuation mechanism. Polarization loss is therefore the essential attenuation mechanism amplified by increasing lattice distortion.

To further differentiate between dipole polarization loss from vacancies and atomic-interface polarization loss arising from LCO, we performed high-temperature annealing at 2300 °C, a process known to modify atomic configurations59. Despite the absence of phase transformations (Supplementary Figs. 22, 23) and significant impedance changes (Supplementary Fig. 24) after annealing, universal reductions in EAB, average α, and Cole-Cole semicircle count (Fig. 2d−f, Supplementary Figs. 25−27) were observed in all HEC samples (detailed EM parameters are shown in Supplementary Fig. 28). Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy, however, confirms that carbon vacancy (g = 2.00360) concentrations remained essentially unchanged (Fig. 2h, Supplementary Fig. 29). This decoupling unambiguously identifies LCO-induced atomic-interface polarization as the dominant loss mechanism, rather than dipole polarization induced by vacancies. To directly link these structural changes back to the initial performance enhancements, we confirmed that this LCO modification manifests as a change in lattice distortion. XRD analyses of peak full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) consistently decrease after the 2300 °C anneal, indicating reduced distortion (Fig. 2i, Supplementary Fig. 30). Intriguingly, the optimized 1500 °C anneal has the opposite effect, dramatically increasing the FWHM of the 9HEC sample (red sphere, Fig. 2i). Together, these results demonstrate that LCO, and the collective lattice distortion it imparts, can be precisely tuned via thermal processing to either suppress or enhance EMW absorption.

Having established a clear link between lattice distortion, LCO, and EMW absorption, we sought definitive, atomic-scale evidence to directly visualize this relationship. We employed HAADF-STEM to examine the 9HEC: as-synthesized (2000 °C, Fig. 3a), high-temperature annealed (2300 °C, Fig. 3b). The performance-optimized 9HEC (1500 °C) is shown in Fig. 1f. The atomic-resolution HAADF-STEM images (Figs. 3a1−b1, 1f1) reveal that all three samples maintain a pristine, single-crystal FCC lattice, confirming that annealing does not induce phase changes. We then quantified the lattice distortion by directly measuring atomic displacements from these images, using the displacement separation method61. The mean atomic displacement in the as-synthesized sample was 5.4 pm (Fig. 3a2). After annealing at 2300 °C, this value decreases to 4.9 pm (Fig. 3b2), while the 1500 °C annealing increases it to a maximum of 6.4 pm (Fig. 1f2). It should be noted that the atomic displacements measured by STEM primarily reflect static, chemically induced lattice distortions associated with LCO, rather than thermal vibrational amplitudes. Stronger LCO can therefore produce larger correlated atomic displacements and strain heterogeneity, whereas high-temperature annealing (2300 °C) suppresses LCO, leading to reduced lattice distortion and sharper XRD peaks. We further performed atomic-resolution EDS mappings to directly visualize the chemical arrangements (Figs. 3a3−b3, 1f3). All three samples display periodic enrichment and depletion of different elements within the atomic columns. Since the intensity of individual spots is approximately proportional to the concentration of a specific element within an atomic column, a higher concentration of the element implies a relative deficiency of others, given a comparable number of atoms at each atomic column. This creates atomic-scale interfaces with sharp contrasts in elemental composition between adjacent regions (as circled in different colors62). Quantitative analysis of the EDS intensity fluctuations provides the ultimate proof for our hypothesis. As summarized in Fig. 3c, the elemental intensity fluctuations significantly decreased after annealing at 2300 °C but were markedly enhanced after the 1500 °C anneal (Supplementary Figs. 9, 31, 32). This provides incontrovertible evidence that our thermal processing approach directly manipulates the degree of LCO. This tunable LCO is therefore the underlying structural origin of the tunable lattice distortion and, consequently, the extraordinary EMW absorption in HECs.

To evaluate the generality of our LCO-tailoring strategy, we subjected a diverse suite of high-entropy ceramics to the identical annealing treatment (1500 °C for 4 h). This portfolio includes borides (diborides, hexaborides), oxides (perovskites, zirconates, ferrites), sulfides, silicides, and selenides (Fig. 4c, with specific compositions listed in Supplementary Table 10). XRD patterns confirm that both the as-synthesized and post-annealed samples are single-phase and possess the same crystal structure (Supplementary Fig. 35), eliminating the potential influence of phase transformation on EMW absorption performance. Further quantitative analysis of the XRD data reveals a universal increase in FWHM, indicating intensified lattice distortion induced by LCO (Supplementary Fig. 36). To more directly confirm the link between lattice distortion and LCO, we performed atomic-resolution STEM analysis (Supplementary Fig. 37). HAADF-STEM imaging for distortion quantification demonstrates that the mean atomic displacement of the exemplary five-cation high-entropy diboride sample increases from 3.4–4.0 pm, corroborating the enhancement in lattice distortion observed in the XRD analysis. Corresponding EDS mapping provides further visual, robust evidence for significantly enhanced LCO—the elemental intensity fluctuation range considerably increases from 0.14–0.29 to 0.21–0.39. Subsequent EWM absorption measurements show that all thermally processed high-entropy ceramic samples have marked performance gains (Supplementary Fig. 38). For instance, the EAB of a five-cation high-entropy ferrite was improved from 4.6–7.0 GHz, while a five-cation high-entropy zirconate was transformed from an almost non-absorbent material (~0.1 GHz EAB) into an effective absorber with a 2.2 GHz EAB. Cole-Cole patterns exhibit a general upward trend of semicircle number after annealing the samples, signifying enhanced polarization loss induced by LCO (Supplementary Fig. 39). The consistency of these enhancements across multiple ceramic families confirms that thermally driven LCO modulation is a broadly applicable and powerful design principle for advanced EMW absorbers.

In summary, we have demonstrated a simple yet effective thermal-processing strategy to rationally tailor LCO in high-entropy ceramics, achieving an exceptional EAB of 11.2 GHz and an ultralow RLmin of −67.4 dB in a prototype HEC—far surpassing current single-phase benchmarks. Through atomic-resolution microscopy and MD/MC simulations, we establish that atomic-interface polarization driven by tunable LCO, rather than vacancy-induced dipolar effects, dominates EM dissipation. The universality of this strategy is validated across high-entropy borides, oxides, sulfides, silicides, and selenides, each showing marked performance gains. Our findings furnish both a fundamental framework for atomic-scale polarization phenomena in high-entropy systems and a scalable design paradigm for next-generation EMW absorbers. Beyond single-phase absorbers, this LCO-tailoring principle may be extended to composite systems—to realize absorbers with even greater functionality and tunability.

Methods

Sample fabrication and thermal processing

The HEC samples were synthesized from commercially available high-purity oxide powders (HfO2, Ta2O5, Nb2O5, ZrO2, TiO2, V2O5, MoO3, WO3, Cr2O3; 99.9% purity and 1−3 μm in particle size, McLean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., China) and carbon powders (99.95% purity, particle size ≥ 100 mesh, Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology Co., Ltd., China). Powders were weighted according to the target molar ratios for each HEC composition and were uniformly mixed via ball milling for over 24 h. The resulting mixtures were then pressed into pellets at 10 MPa with a holding duration of 3 min. The pressed pellets were synthesized into HEC bulks using a custom-built UHTS apparatus in an argon atmosphere. A rapid heating rate of 50 °C·s−1 was used to reach 2000 °C, where the samples were held for 60 s before natural cooling. The resulting HEC bulks were ground and sieved to obtain a fine powder (<300 mesh).

The as-prepared HEC powders were subjected to two different annealing protocols under Ar protection. For high-temperature annealing, powders were heated to 2300 °C with a heating rate of 50 °C·s−1 using the UHTS apparatus with a 60-s holding time. For low-temperature optimization, powders were annealed in a commercial furnace at 1300 and 1400 °C for 1 h, and at 1500 °C for 1 to 4 h, with a heating rate of 2 °C·min−1. The universality of the annealing strategy was verified by treating all other high-entropy ceramics at 1500 °C for 4 h. To measure EM properties, the HEC powders were mixed with paraffin wax at a 7:3 weight ratio. The mixture was ultrasonically blended (at 85 °C for 30 min) and pressed into toroidal rings with an outer diameter of 7.00 mm, an inner diameter of 3.04 mm, and a thickness of 3 mm. Note that grain size is not a key factor affecting EMW absorption performance, as validated in Supplementary Figs. 40, 41.

High-resolution atomic structural analysis was performed on a double Cs-corrected TEM (Spectra 300, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with a Super-X EDS detector operated at 300 kV. Atomic structures were investigated by HAADF imaging in STEM mode, with a probe convergence angle of 25 mrad and a collection angle of 61−200 mrad. The drift distortions of high-resolution HAADF-STEM images were corrected by orthogonal image pairs in order to accurately determine the atomic displacement of lattice structures in HEC samples64. The center of atomic sites (with a number of approximately 600–700) in the STEM images was determined by a 2D Gaussian fitting algorithm in CalAtom software65. The atomic displacement was analyzed by the method of displacement separation analysis, which could directly separate the atomic displacements from the lattice structure without prior knowledge, based on Fourier space filtering61. Atomic-resolution EDS maps (400 × 400 pixels) of HEC samples were acquired with a dwell time of 8 μs and a pixel size of 18.13 pm. The total acquisition time was around 40 min. The beam current was ~50 pA for both HAADF images and EDS maps in STEM measurement. The raw EDS spectrum images were generated in Velox software (version 3.11) with the characteristic X-ray signals of each element (Ti Kα, V Kα, Cr Kα, Zr Kα, Nb Kα, Mn Kα, Hf Lα, Ta Lα, and W Lα). Then, these raw EDS maps were denoised using the method of non-local principal component analysis (the detailed denoised procedure was described in the literature)66, which enabled the accurate determination of atomic position and intensity in denoised EDS images. The intensity of atomic sites (with a number of ~600) in denoised EDS images was determined by CalAtom software with the same fitting algorithm used in atomic-resolution STEM images. The intensity variation of elemental maps in denoised EDS spectrum images was quantified to reflect elemental fluctuations, which are related to local chemical order. Specifically, for each elemental image, the intensity values from all atomic columns were collected, and the resulting intensity histogram was fitted to a Gaussian distribution. The elemental fluctuation was then defined by the FWHM, with the image intensity normalized by the mean value in the statistics.

The conductivity of the HEC samples was tested by a four-probe resistance meter (280SI, Four Dimensions, USA). For each sample, the conductivity measurement was repeated five times at its different regions. The EM parameters were measured using a vector network analyzer (E5071C, Agilent Technologies, USA) in the range of 2−18 GHz.
