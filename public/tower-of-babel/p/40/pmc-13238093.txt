Multi-technique characterization of gold nanoparticle-based DNA biosensor with smartphone-enabled photometry for accelerated detection of Campylobacter jejuni
Authors: Anthony James Franco, Finnian James, Leah Wilson, Woubit Abebe, Tina Conklin, Roger Stearns, Mollie Woods, Evangelyn Alocilja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCampylobacter is one of the most infectious foodborne pathogens, often associated with consuming contaminated poultry products. Many Campylobacter infections are not reported until the symptoms become severe, making it necessary to detect them rapidly in food production systems as part of a preventative intervention strategy. In this study, a gold nanoparticle (GNP)-based DNA biosensor that utilizes digital photometric signals from smartphone images was developed to rapidly detect Campylobacter jejuni in drainage water and poultry rinse samples.ResultsSpectrophotometric measurements, hydrodynamic size, zeta potential, and dark-field microscopy confirm that hybrids were successfully formed between GNP, oligonucleotide probe, and C. jejuni DNA using an in-situ approach. Triggering GNP aggregation upon hybridization, the nano-biosensor signals obtained through smartphone-enabled digital photometry were strongly correlated with spectrophotometry. Based on the smartphone-enabled signal, the nano-biosensor demonstrated a high degree of selectivity for C. jejuni and detected 103 and 106 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL in unenriched drainage water and poultry rinse samples, respectively.ConclusionsThis work demonstrates a GNP-based DNA biosensor employing cadF-targeted hybridization and smartphone-enabled digital photometry for the detection of Campylobacter jejuni in real samples. Diffusion-limited aggregation of unhybridized GNPs yields distinct optical responses, enabling the detection of target DNA in drainage water and poultry rinse. These findings provide a foundation for future validation studies in samples critical to monitoring foodborne pathogens across various food production and processing systems. Moreover, they highlight the potential of the smartphone-aided nano-biosensing system as a cost-effective, field-deployable means of pathogen surveillance.

Background

Campylobacter are Gram-negative, mostly microaerophilic bacteria recognized as one of the most infectious foodborne pathogens worldwide [1, 2]. In the U.S., the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimates that 1.5 million people get infected by Campylobacter every year, with several cases potentially undiagnosed or unreported [3]. While most infections are sporadic and self-limiting [2], high-risk populations, including young children, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals, can suffer severe symptoms [3]. Beyond gastroenteritis, infections can lead to serious sequelae such as reactive arthritis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, bacteremia, and meningitis [1]. Among various Campylobacter species, Campylobacter jejuni is the most associated with human infection [4].

Poultry is one of the primary sources of Campylobacter [5, 6]. It is estimated that the financial impact of Campylobacter infections from poultry amounts to $6.9 billion in medical expenses and decreased quality of life [7]. To mitigate its impact on food safety, Campylobacter is monitored in poultry production and processing facilities using both culturing and culture-independent detection techniques (CIDT). Culturing remains the “gold standard” procedure as it confirms viable cells in positive samples and enables downstream characterization. Meanwhile, CIDT, such as PCR, can be used to rapidly screen samples [8]. Despite their widespread use, these methods have drawbacks that limit their effectiveness. Culturing Campylobacter requires maintaining microaerobic conditions and specialized growth media supplemented with various antimicrobials, and takes up to 72 h before presumptive detection [8, 9]. On the other hand, PCR is typically performed after 24 h of enrichment [8], requires special equipment and sensitive reagents, and is affected by inhibitors present in the sample. Both methods demand careful handling by highly skilled personnel.

Biosensors have been explored to address several limitations of culturing and PCR-based detection. They are gaining interest due to their potential for high sensitivity and specificity under optimal conditions, real-time monitoring, and low-cost fabrication. Integrating nanoparticles, such as gold nanoparticles, in biosensors enhances their sensitivity, portability, stability, and selectivity [10]. Gold nanoparticles exhibit size-dependent optical properties that have been demonstrated to be effective for signal generation, and they can be easily functionalized with DNA [11], which provides a high degree of specificity in detecting biological targets like bacterial pathogens such as Salmonella, E. coli, Listeria monocytogenes, and Campylobacter [4, 12–15].

Advances in smartphone technology enabled the collection and analysis of high-quality images at low cost, thanks to integrated image analysis software. This enables smartphones to be used for digital photometry, which can then be integrated into biosensing systems [16]. In this study, a gold nanoparticle-based DNA biosensor that uses digital photometry signals derived from smartphone images was developed to rapidly detect Campylobacter jejuni. The developed nano-biosensor was tested on drainage water and poultry rinse. The findings in this study contribute to the broader goal of developing a cost-effective, field-deployable means of monitoring foodborne pathogens.

Methods

Reagents and materials

CHROMagar™ Campylobacter (CAC) was purchased from DRG International (Springfield, NJ, USA). Tryptic soy broth (TSB) was purchased from MilliporeSigma (Burlington, MA, USA). Phosphate-buffered saline, pH 7.4 (PBS) powder and Difco™ Buffered Peptone Water (BPW) were purchased from VWR International (Radnor, PA, USA). 1X Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 8.0) (TE) buffer was purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc. (Coralville, IA, USA). Hydrochloric acid (ACS reagent, 37%), 11-mercaptoundecanoic acid (MUDA, HS(CH2)10CO2H), and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, C12H25NaO4S) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

The stock of gold nanoparticles (GNPs) was synthesized following a previously described procedure [17]. The stock GNP was incubated with SDS for 30 min, followed by incubation with MUDA for 30 min. Incubation was at ambient temperature (approximately 21 °C) with constant, gentle mixing. The final concentrations of SDS and MUDA were 1 µM each. The resulting suspension was centrifuged at 10,000×g for 10 min to remove the supernatant. The recovered GNP-MUDA was resuspended in 0.1 M borate buffer (pH 7.4) to an absorbance at 520 nm of 15 absorbance units. The GNP-MUDA were used directly in the nano-biosensor assay without further modification and stored at 4 °C when not in use.

Bacterial culture

Cultures of Campylobacter jejuni (ATCC 33560), Salmonella Typhimurium (ATCC 29630), Escherichia coli (ATCC 15597), Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 12600), and Listeria monocytogenes (ATCC BAA-679) were used in this study. The C. jejuni working culture is prepared by first plating the − 80 °C stock culture on CAC and then incubating at 42 °C under microaerophilic conditions for 18–24 h. The bacterial growth from the overnight culture was collected using an inoculation loop and then resuspended in 1mL of 1X PBS. 10 µL of this suspension was then spread on fresh CAC and incubated at 42 °C under microaerophilic conditions for 18–24 h. The microaerophilic condition is achieved using an airtight chamber equipped with a Pouch-Micro Aero microaerophilic gas-generating system (Mitsubishi Gas Chemical America, Inc., New York, NY, USA) of appropriate size. The bacterial growth was collected using a sterile inoculation loop and resuspended in 1 mL PBS. For the other bacterial species, stock cultures were prepared by transferring a single colony into 9 mL of TSB and incubating at 37 °C for 18–24 h. One milliliter of the stock culture was transferred to fresh 9 mL of TSB and incubated at 37 °C for 3–4 h to produce the working cultures (except for C. jejuni). Working cultures were serially diluted to the desired concentration using 1X PBS.

Genomic DNA extraction

Genomic DNA from the bacteria in pure cultures and in the samples was extracted by boiling. A 1-mL aliquot of the bacterial culture was placed in boil-resistant centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 6000×rcf for 5 min. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended completely in 0.2 mL of TE buffer. The tube was then placed in a heat block set at 99 °C for 20 min. After heating, the tube was placed in a -20 °C freezer for 5 min, then taken out to reach ambient temperature (approximately 21 °C). The tube was centrifuged at 21,000×rcf for 2 min, after which the top 0.1 mL of the supernatant was collected and used as the DNA extract in subsequent steps.

Gold nanoparticle-based DNA biosensor assay

To help in understanding the assay, a diagram is shown in Fig. 1. The assay solution, comprising 5 µL of the GNP-MUDA suspension, 5 µL of 25 µM of the probe, and 10 µL of the genomic DNA extract, was prepared in a PCR tube (Step 1). The 40-nt aminated oligonucleotide probe (/5-AmMC6/ ACC TTC TTT AGT GTC ATT GCT TGA GCG AGG ATT ATC TTG A) was purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc. (Coralville, IA, USA) in lyophilized form and was reconstituted using TE buffer to produce a 25 µM probe solution. The probe was designed based on the cadF gene sequence of Campylobacter jejuni (Accession number AL111168). The assay solution was heated to 95 °C for 5 min, then at 55 °C for 10 min, and then cooled to ambient temperature (approximately 21 °C) (Step 2). Detection was achieved by adding 0.1 M HCl to induce GNP aggregation (Step 3). Spectrometric measurements were performed using NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) 15 min after adding the acid (Step 4). At the same time, tube images were captured with a smartphone for analysis. A region of interest (ROI) in the tube image was selected by the user where the assay solution was visible. Within this ROI, pixels were sampled randomly. Sampled pixels were analyzed rather than the entire ROI to minimize noise that could result from image artifacts. The size of the ROI and the number of sampled pixels were kept consistent across all processed images. The RGB values from each sampled pixel were averaged, and the R/G ratio was calculated. The blank used for all nano-biosensor assays was 1X TE buffer. The normalized spectrometric signal and normalized R/G were calculated using Eqs. 1 and 2, respectively.

Instrumentation

The hydrodynamic size, electrophoretic mobility, and zeta potential were determined using Zetasizer Ultra (Malvern Pananalytical Ltd., Malvern, UK). Samples were prepared by performing the nano-biosensor assay, but instead of adding the acid, the hybridization products were collected by centrifugation at 10,000×g for 10 min. A total of 10 tubes per sample were prepared to ensure sufficient product remained for measurement. After centrifugation, the supernatant was removed, and the recovered products were resuspended in 1 mL of 1X TE buffer. The resuspended products were combined and then placed in a Folded Capillary Zeta Cell (DTS1070, Malvern Pananalytical Ltd., Malvern, UK).

Dark-field microscopy was performed using an Olympus BX41 microscope equipped with a dark-field condenser (numerical aperture 1.1; Evident Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Samples were prepared by placing a 3 µL droplet on a clean glass slide, then a coverslip was placed on top. The micrographs were obtained using a 6.4MP color CMOS camera (Evident Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) through a 40X objective with a numerical aperture of 0.65.

Analysis of samples

A chicken rinse sample was prepared by submerging a store-bought whole chicken in BPW, mixing occasionally for 1 h. The chicken was removed, and the rinse was placed in a sterile container. A water sample was obtained from a field drainage brought directly to the laboratory. Both samples were stored in a -20 °C freezer and thawed slowly to ambient temperature before use. Samples were artificially contaminated with various concentrations of C. jejuni. Uninoculated samples were analyzed simultaneously with the inoculated samples. A 1-mL aliquot of each sample was obtained and centrifuged at 10,000× g for 3 min. After removing the supernatant, the DNA of the residue was extracted by boiling. The extracts were assayed using the nano-biosensor. The analyses were carried out in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Statistical tests were performed using an online statistical tool [18]. The nano-biosensor assay results were compared against the uninoculated samples using Welch’s test at α = 0.05. The validation of random sampling of pixels was performed using Kruskal-Wallis test at α = 0.01.

Results

The nano-biosensor uses a 40-nt oligonucleotide probe to target the cadF gene of Campylobacter. The probe used in this study was designed using Primer-BLAST [19]. The Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) [20] was used to evaluate in silico the range of Campylobacter strains that the designed probe can detect. Based on the BLAST results (Fig. 2), at least 114 Campylobacter strains in the database have cadF gene sequences that are 100% homologous to the designed probe. The majority of these strains were isolated from humans (58/114) and chickens (28/114), indicating a strong association between Campylobacter-related human infections and consumption of contaminated chickens. Regarding isolation location, the strains originated from every continent except Antarctica and are mainly represented by those from the US (26/114) and South Korea (26/114). The sources of most strains were not reported (60/114); however, feces (25/114) and meat (7/114) were the most common sources when identified. These results demonstrate the potential of the designed probe for detecting Campylobacter across diverse samples and geographic locations.

The formation of the GNP-oligo-DNA hybrid is central to the assay. Assay solutions of the blank, and those with C. jejuni DNA and E. coli DNA (henceforth referred to as positive control and negative control, respectively) were prepared as outlined in the methods section. Instead of adding acid, the hybridization products were collected by centrifugation, and the amount of double-stranded (ds) DNA bound to the collected GNP was determined (Fig. 3a). It is shown that the amount of C. jejuni dsDNA bound to the GNP is significantly greater than that of non-target E. coli dsDNA. This suggests that hybrids were formed more successfully in positive control than in the negative control. The oligonucleotide probe enabled C. jejuni DNA and GNP to hybridize, resulting in a greater amount of GNP-bound dsDNA. On the other hand, the GNP-bound E. coli dsDNA could result from loose and non-specific binding between GNP and the DNA strands, but could be aggregated by adding an acid, as previously shown in earlier studies [21, 22].

The hydrodynamic size, electrophoretic mobility, and zeta potential of the collected hybridization products were further investigated. The hydrodynamic size of the collected products from the blank and negative control does not differ, but both are smaller than that of the positive control (Fig. 3b). This suggests that, while there may be some non-specific binding between E. coli dsDNA and the GNP, as demonstrated by the amount of GNP-bound E. coli dsDNA in the negative control, the GNP is loosely bound and behaves similarly to the blank in solution. This is supported by their similar electrophoretic mobility (Fig. 3c). The zeta potential of the products in the blank and the negative control is more negative than that of the positive control (Fig. 3d). One possible reason is that the C. jejuni DNA in the positive control could surround the GNP after hybridization, allowing more positively charged counterions to accumulate around it due to the exposed negatively charged deoxyribose-phosphate moiety of the DNA. This leads to charge screening around the GNP, partially neutralizing the negative charges and resulting in a less negative zeta potential. This is consistent with a previous report where the calculated zeta potential of GNP-DNA conjugates was less negative than that of the GNP [23]. The results of GNP-bound DNA determination and hybridization product characterization indicate that hybrids were successfully formed between GNP and C. jejuni DNA using the in-situ hybridization approach.

The nano-biosensor assay requires adding acid after in situ hybridization to trigger gold nanoparticle aggregation and generate a signal. The size-dependent spectrophotometric properties of gold nanoparticles have been extensively studied, in which as gold nanoparticles increase in size, their localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) peak shifts to longer wavelengths [24, 25]. The physical significance of this shift is that the color of the GNP in solution changes as they aggregate, and the manner of the color change could depend on the morphology of the emerging aggregate. The GNP aggregation was monitored using UV-Vis spectrometry within 15 min of adding acid to investigate changes in the spectral properties of the solution upon triggering GNP aggregation. For the blank, the resonance peak shifted rapidly and levelled off at approximately 620 nm (Fig. 4d). The peak shift in the negative control occurred similarly, with the peak levelling off at approximately 600 nm (Fig. 4f). On the other hand, the peak shift is less pronounced in the positive control, where the shift levelled off at approximately 550 nm (Fig. 4e). Additionally, the resonance peaks broaden more in both the blank and the negative control than in the positive control (Fig. 4a-c). The rapid shift and broadening of the resonance peak in both the blank and negative control suggest that the GNP aggregation is diffusion-limited [26]. The spectrometric signal can be simplified by taking the absorbance ratio at 520 nm to 620 nm, analogous to how the extent of GNP aggregation is reported based on spectrophotometric measurements [27]. Moreover, the absorbance ratios of the samples can be normalized with respect to the blank (Eq. 1) to minimize inter-replicate variation. Using the normalized signal enables quantitative comparison, which can then be used to interpret positive or negative results (Fig. 4g). Additionally, the tube images show differences in the color of the blank, the positive control, and the negative control, where the blank and the negative control turned purple/gray 15 min after triggering GNP aggregation, whereas the positive control remained red.

As shown in Fig. 4g, the color of the blank solution differs from that of the negative control, consistent with the difference observed in the resulting resonance peak 15 min after adding the acid. Dark-field microscopy (DFM) was performed to further investigate the aggregation of GNP in solution, especially to account for the difference in the optical properties of the blank and the negative control. DFM has been reported to visualize GNP in solution [28–30], allowing visualization without an electron microscope. In DFM, smaller GNP scatter less light, thus appearing dim, while large GNP scatter more light, appearing bright. Additionally, small spherical GNP appear green while larger spherical GNP appear orange to yellow [31, 32]. This enables real-time observation of GNP aggregates without drying the sample, which could alter their morphology and distribution. The DFM images show that the GNP aggregated in both the blank and the negative control, as indicated by the presence of large bright orange particles (Fig. 5a and c). This aligns with the previous finding that the resonance peaks of the blank and the negative control shifted to longer wavelengths. The GNP in the positive control appears green to yellow-green and dim, despite brightness adjustments, suggesting minimal GNP aggregation (Fig. 5b). Interestingly, elongated GNP-containing structures are prominently observed in negative control (Fig. 5c). This is a possible reason for the shift in the resonance peak and the appearance of the assay solution in the negative control, as the size and morphology of the GNP affect its spectral properties [25]. The formation of these elongated structures could result from the initial nonspecific binding of DNA to the GNP. Since the DNA is initially loosely bound to the GNP, the aggregation of the GNP could be sterically hindered, limiting the position where GNP-to-GNP binding could occur. After the loosely bound DNA separates from the GNP due to the action of the acid, the GNP aggregate has already become anisotropic. Several studies on nanoparticle aggregation show that anisotropic nanoparticle seeds promote anisotropic aggregation [33–35].

The appearance of the assay solution 15 min after adding the acid (Fig. 4g) can be used to generate a digital photometric signal by obtaining the color channels of the image of the tubes (i.e., RGB values). Random pixels were sampled within the user-selected region of interest (ROI). To evaluate the impact of random pixel sampling on RGB values, the sampling algorithm was executed 20 times within the same ROI of the image. Results show that the RGB values obtained with different numbers of sampled pixels are not significantly different from one another or from those obtained from the full ROI. The same was observed for both the red and purple solutions, indicating that solution color does not introduce bias in the RGB values obtained (Fig. 6). Using this information, the normalized R/G ratio, similar to the absorbance ratio, was obtained from spectrophotometric measurements (Eq. 2). There is a strong linear correlation (R2 = 0.85) between the normalized R/G value and the normalized A520 nm/A620 nm (Fig. 7). This demonstrates the potential of digital photometry as an alternative to spectrophotometry. Based on this, the digital photometric signal from the nano-biosensor assay was used moving forward.

The nano-biosensor was tested against different C. jejuni dsDNA concentration to determine whether the digital photometric response of the nano-biosensor responds to C. jejuni DNA. The normalized R/G ratio correlates strongly with C. jejuni dsDNA concentration (R2 = 0.96; Fig. 8a). Additionally, the nano-biosensor was tested against various non-target organisms typically associated with poultry production and processing. Results show that the nano-biosensor response to C. jejuni is significantly greater than that of the non-targets tested, indicating high specificity of the nano-biosensor (Fig. 8b). It should be noted that a single representative strain of the target organism was used to evaluate biosensor selectivity, consistent with early‑stage biosensor development in the literature [4, 15, 36, 37]. This serves as a proof of concept demonstrating that the nano-biosensor can differentiate Campylobacter from other potential interfering organisms.

The nano-biosensor was tested on farm drainage water and poultry rinse samples, and both normalized R/G and spectral signals were obtained. Using the normalized R/G value, the nano-biosensor detected C. jejuni as low as 3 log CFU/mL in the drainage water, and 6 log CFU/mL in the poultry rinse (Fig. 9a and b). Meanwhile, processing the spectrophotometric signal showed the same detection limit for the drainage water sample and 5 log CFU/mL in the poultry rinse (Fig. 9c and d). The normalized R/G signals for drainage water and poultry rinse samples inoculated with 5 log CFU/mL were not statistically different from those of the uninoculated samples, despite average signals that were visibly higher. One main reason is the relatively high signal variance in these samples, as indicated by their standard errors, which renders the difference statistically insignificant. The variance could be due to uneven distribution of extraneous substances across replicate samples, leading to variation in DNA extract quality. Interfering substances, such as fats and proteins, can be present in high amounts in the poultry rinse. These substances could have affected the assay in two ways. First, they increase the viscosity of the sample, leading to less efficient centrifugation of cells at lower concentrations. This, in turn, results in lower DNA yield, reducing the probability of detection. Second, these substances interfere with the hybridization between the GNP and DNA due to their potential to competitively adsorb onto the GNP [36, 37]. This causes less effective detection of the target DNA. In both cases, the detection limit of the nano-biosensor is compromised. This complexity in the sample could also have caused the unusually low digital photometric signal in the drainage water sample inoculated with 7 log CFU/mL. Slight inconsistencies in imaging conditions, such as lighting, shadows, and glare from surrounding light, could have also contributed to the degree of variation in the digital photometry signal. These results show the potential of digital photometry signals obtained with smartphones for integration into colorimetric nano-biosensor platforms.

Discussion

Rapid detection of Campylobacter in food production and processing systems is critical to ensuring consumer safety; however, current detection systems severely limit facilities’ monitoring capacity because they are either too slow, prohibitively expensive, or overly complex. This study presents nano-biosensing, coupled with smartphone digital photometry, as a tool to complement existing monitoring strategies to help facility managers make data-driven decisions at a fraction of the time and cost.

The nano-biosensor presented in this study uses gold nanoparticles (GNPs) as reporter molecules. The GNP hybridizes with Campylobacter DNA through an oligonucleotide probe. The probe confers selectivity during hybridization, allowing preferential formation of hybrids with Campylobacter DNA despite the presence of non-target DNA. Hybridization is carried out in situ via a series of thermal processes that denature the DNA in the assay solution, followed by the formation of the GNP-probe-target DNA hybrid. Afterwards, the signal is generated by triggering GNP aggregation via acid addition. The signal is typically measured by spectrophotometry; however, in this study, smartphone-enabled digital photometry was implemented as an alternative way of obtaining an interpretable signal (Fig. 1).

Detection using the nano-biosensor is performed by targeting the cadF gene of Campylobacter. Targeting the cadF gene serves two purposes. First, it increases the selectivity of detection because this gene has previously been shown to be conserved across a diverse group of Campylobacter isolates [38, 39], making cadF gene a suitable target for the development of DNA-based detection techniques such as PCR [40]. Second, it provides insight into the potential virulence of the Campylobacter strain present in the samples. The cadF gene is one of the markers that determines Campylobacter virulence. This gene encodes an adhesin that enables Campylobacter to bind and, later, infect mammalian cells [39]. By targeting the cadF gene, the presumptive presence and virulence of Campylobacter can be assessed simultaneously. The results of the in-silico evaluation of probe selectivity show that the probe sequence matches those of the cadF genes of Campylobacter isolated from different samples, host species, and geographic locations. This is critical, as genomic variations in bacteria can be triggered by exposure to different environmental conditions [41]. Nucleic acid-based methods, such as PCR and this nano-biosensor, rely on the homology between the probe and the target gene, since a single-nucleotide difference, especially near or at the 3’-end, could diminish annealing between the nucleic acid strands [42, 43], as it can affect the probability of binding between the probe and the target sequence [44]. This demonstrates the potential of the designed probe for detecting Campylobacter across diverse samples and geographic locations.

In-situ hybridization between the GNP, probe, and C. jejuni dsDNA is one of the key features of this nano-biosensor. Unlike other GNP-based DNA biosensors, this nano-biosensor does not require pre-functionalizing the GNP with the probe. This saves time and reduces the complexity of detection, since functionalizing the GNP with oligonucleotide probes can require days, multiple steps, and uncommon reagents [45]. Additionally, adopting an in-situ approach makes the assay convenient to perform even for minimally trained personnel. This paves the way for the development of other in situ hybridization-based GNP biosensors.

Characterizations performed after in situ hybridization provided evidence that hybrids between GNP and C. jejuni DNA were formed. Hybridization enabled the detection of C. jejuni DNA in the sample by triggering the aggregation of GNP. Based on the rate of resonance peak shift, the broadening of the resonance peak, and the microscopic observations, it was determined that the aggregation of GNP in the assay is diffusion-limited. A diffusion-limited GNP aggregation implies that the rate-controlling step is the rate at which particles move through the solution and interact with other GNP [26]. The GNP in the blank and the negative control move faster than in the positive control, as indicated by their measured electrophoretic mobilities, resulting in faster aggregation kinetics. Additionally, dark-field microscopy revealed differences in aggregate morphology between the blank and the negative control. It was determined that the interaction between the GNP and DNA may influence the GNP’s aggregation behavior, leading to a deviation in the optical properties of the negative control from those of the blank. This is critical when establishing a reference point from which assay results could be compared to minimize the rate of false-positive results, especially in highly complex and contaminated samples. Further investigations into the mechanism of hybridization and the impact of excess non-target DNA on the hybridization efficiency of the GNP and the target DNA are recommended to further optimize hybridization and improve target DNA detection using the nano-biosensor.

This study shows that nano-biosensor signals captured in smartphone images can serve as an alternative to spectrophotometry, as evidenced by the high correlation between the two approaches. This is despite using only the basic color channels, such as RGB, rather than advanced algorithms or complex mathematical calculations to process the image. C. jejuni concentrations as low as 103 CFU/mL of C. jejuni in drainage water samples and 106 CFU/mL in poultry rinsate samples were detected based on smartphone-enabled digital photometric signals of the nano-biosensor. This level of sensitivity of the nano-biosensor falls within the published detection limits of biosensors and molecular-based techniques for C. jejuni detection (Table 1). Despite this, the Campylobacter contamination level in retail raw chicken can be as low as 102 to 104 CFU/g, limiting its potential as a monitoring method. However, the nano-biosensor can still be advantageous given its current sensitivity in cases where Campylobacter loads are typically high, especially when used to complement standard monitoring procedures. On-site analysis of boot-sock rinsates from farms can be performed to identify and screen highly colonized flocks, thereby informing control strategies. In processing plants, the nano-biosensor can be used on samples obtained from early process operations, such as scald water, defeathering rinses, and swabs of contact surfaces. Analysis results can be used to verify sanitation efficacy and guide adjustments to sanitation practices. This can also provide timely alarms to adjust downstream interventions or slow down the line to catch up on sanitation. The nano-biosensor can also be used to assess gross loss of contamination control by analyzing drains, floors, and walls near evisceration and defeathering lines, as well as biofilm formation-prone sites such as processing surfaces and conveyor belts. It should be noted that the detection was performed directly on unenriched samples and unamplified DNA targets, and that the entire assay, from sample preparation to detection, took only 3 hours. Further, it was previously estimated that the assay cost is only $2/test under prevailing conditions [55]. The assay’s cost-effectiveness, due to its rapid, low-cost nature, makes it more accessible, especially in low-resource settings where cost can be a deciding factor in adopting technologies.

Detection limits of various methods developed to detect Campylobacter in real samples

Integrating smartphone-based digital photometry and biosensor platforms for Campylobacter detection has been previously reported. A biosensing platform was developed using aptamers as recognition elements to enrich Campylobacter in 96-well plates and detect them via a chromogenic reaction [56]. The color change was monitored by analyzing the color channels of smartphone images of the wells, and the biosensor detected 101 CFU/100 g of Campylobacter in artificially contaminated oysters at 40% recovery. In another study, Campylobacter coli was detected using loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), and the results were analyzed based on the hue-saturation-intensity color space of smartphone images of the wells [57]. Meat samples were analyzed using this scheme, and C. coli was detected in agreement with traditional identification and PCR methods. However, these approaches required complex sample processing and the preparation of the detection platform. Furthermore, they require a laboratory facility. Compared to these examples, the nano-biosensor offers several advantages. These include the straightforward preparation of GNP, which remains stable when refrigerated. Additionally, the probes can be used as-is, without needing further modification. Furthermore, the hybridization reaction can be conducted in a single tube and does not require any specialized reagents. Combined with smartphone-enabled digital photometry, these highlight the simplicity and convenience of the nano-biosensor presented in this study, which is critical for analyzing many samples under sub-optimal conditions on-site. The complementarity between biosensing and smartphone technology is expected to flourish as advancements continue and the demand for rapid, cost-effective pathogen monitoring at the point of use rises.

Limitations and further work

Improving the detection limit of the nano-biosensor is essential for increasing its practicality and is a primary focus of future research. This can be achieved by concentrating Campylobacter cells from the samples using magnetic nanoparticles, as previously demonstrated in various foodborne pathogens [15, 58–61]. Another potential approach is to incubate the recovered Campylobacter cells for additional hours to increase their concentration, at the cost of longer analysis time. These can be coupled with a washing step to minimize carryover of interfering substances into the DNA extracts. These interventions constitute robust sample-preparation techniques to enhance nano-biosensor performance, enabling their implementation across a wider range of food safety applications.

The study evaluated the performance of the nano-biosensor using artificially contaminated liquid samples. In the future, the nano-biosensor will be validated on naturally contaminated samples of varying complexity to better assess its ability to detect C. jejuni. Additionally, broadening strain coverage for selectivity testing to include multiple serotypes and field isolates will be incorporated into future validation studies to increase confidence in the nano-biosensor’s selectivity. Ways to improve the consistency of capturing tube images, such as fabricating a simple studio box to control lighting around the tube, will also be explored.

One of the main challenges in Campylobacter monitoring is distinguishing its physiological state, whether it is live, dead, or viable but not culturable. Complex molecular methods have been developed to achieve this [62]. These methods are highly complex and often require sophisticated instrumentation and substantial technical expertise from the analyst, limiting their usability in the field. The nano-biosensor presented in this study is designed to detect Campylobacter by targeting its cadF gene, but it does not differentiate between its physiological states. Differentiating these states represents a promising direction for future research, as it is crucial for enhancing the utility of the nano-biosensor in food safety assessment.

Conclusions

The demand for rapid, cost-effective detection methods to monitor critical foodborne pathogens, such as Campylobacter jejuni, remains high due to their impact on human health. This study presents a gold nanoparticle (GNP)-based DNA biosensor that uses signals derived from smartphone images. The detection is based on the GNP’s ability to form hybrids specifically with C. jejuni DNA. The hybrids are formed in situ using an oligonucleotide probe targeting the cadF gene of C. jejuni. The GNP aggregation in the absence of C jejuni DNA is determined to be diffusion-limited, which plays a crucial role in the difference in the signals of samples that contain C. jejuni DNA from those that do not. The smartphone-based digital photometry signal correlates strongly with the spectral signal, enabling the detection of at least 103 CFU/mL and 106 CFU/mL of C. jejuni in drainage water and poultry rinse samples, respectively. This study shows the potential for field operability of the nano-biosensor in tandem with smartphone digital photometry.
