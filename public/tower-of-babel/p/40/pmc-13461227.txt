Understanding Attention in Schizophrenia: The Enduring Influence of Barbara Cornblatt
Authors: Eric A Reavis, Samuel D Klein, Michael F Green
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this review, we highlight Barbara Cornblatt’s transformative contributions to the study and understanding of attention in psychosis. We begin by summarizing Cornblatt’s work on attention starting in the late 1970s, including her work showing that impaired vigilance is a heritable risk factor and predictor of psychosis, and her key role in the creation of the gold-standard Continuous Performance Test—Identical Pairs. We then summarize major developments in the understanding of attention in psychosis since that time, all of which have been influenced by the work and insights of Cornblatt. Finally, we describe recent research in this area and discuss ways that emerging ideas and methods could transform attention research going forward, building upon Cornblatt’s foundational work.

Introduction: Altered Attention as a Key Feature of Schizophrenia

Altered attention has been featured prominently in phenomenological descriptions and characterizations of schizophrenia since the earliest descriptions of the illness. Indeed, both Bleuler and Kraepelin considered attentional deficits to be central to the pathogenesis of schizophrenia (termed dementia praecox at that time).1,2 Subsequent reports from psychiatrists who listened carefully to patients as they described the lived experience of schizophrenia highlighted various ways in which attention was impaired by the illness.3 For example, James Chapman interviewed people with early-stage schizophrenia about their perceptual experiences in the 1960s. One interviewee, a machinist, described the ease with which irrelevant stimuli tended to capture his attention: “When I was still at work, my eyes got drawn away by things. I used to look at the metal shavings on the bench. Things like that could take all my attention because they looked as fascinating and priceless as the Mona Lisa.”4 Another person described the challenge of trying to divide his attention: “I don’t like dividing my attention at any time because it leads to confusion and I don’t know where I am or who I am. When this starts I just go into a trance and I just turn off all my senses and I don’t see anything and I don’t hear anything.”5

By the latter part of the 20th century, experimental studies of attention in schizophrenia began to supplant purely descriptive accounts, in a shift that coincided with a growing emphasis on cognition and information processing more broadly in psychopathology research, paralleling the growing influence of cognitive science in psychology. Importantly, experimental studies launched during this period aimed not only to measure attentional deficits precisely but also to determine what those deficits might tell us about vulnerability and risk for schizophrenia.6,7 A leading force in this merger of attention and schizophrenia risk research was Barbara Cornblatt.8,9 Her pioneering work transformed the study of attention in schizophrenia in ways that continue to strongly influence the field today.

In this article, we present a selective review, tracing major developments in the study of attentional impairments in schizophrenia with an emphasis on the foundational contributions of Barbara Cornblatt. Our review is not intended to be comprehensive. Rather, the goal is to summarize how concepts and research paradigms related to attention in psychosis—especially visual attention—have evolved over approximately the last 35 years, with an eye toward the ways that Barbara Cornblatt’s work has shaped and directed this evolution.

Indeed, the investigation and understanding of attention in psychosis has been strongly influenced by Cornblatt’s pioneering work. Beginning in the 1970s, her groundbreaking contributions led the way to a substantial expansion of research in this area, including dramatic diversification of the paradigms, populations, and techniques represented in the literature. This article aims to highlight some of the most salient concepts and findings that emerged from Cornblatt’s work and the research that followed from it.

We begin by describing the field of research on attention in schizophrenia and related at-risk groups that Cornblatt effectively founded in the late 20th century. A series of landmark studies during that era firmly established the value of attentional vigilance paradigms, which seek to measure a person’s ability to sustain alertness and avoid distraction for an extended period. Next, we describe how the conceptualization and measurement of attention in psychosis evolved during the early 2000s, as illustrated by 2 influential consensus initiatives sponsored by the US National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH). We then highlight novel ideas and methods for attention research that have further changed the field over the last 15 years. Finally, we close with a discussion of emerging approaches that might be positioned to further advance our understanding of attention in psychosis going forward.

Barbara Cornblatt and the Attention Revolution in Psychosis Research

Beginning in the late 1970s, Barbara Cornblatt spearheaded 2 major advances in schizophrenia research. During her doctoral training, Cornblatt established an enduring and highly successful program of developmental research on children at high risk for developing psychosis. In parallel, she demonstrated the value of attentional research in developmental cohorts and created a refined measure of attentional vigilance that remains in use today.

Alongside Niki Erlenmeyer-Kimling, Cornblatt helped establish one of the first and most influential developmental studies of schizophrenia: the New York High-Risk Project (NYHRP).10,11 The NYHRP, which Erlenmyer-Kimling launched in 1971, enrolled a sample of children with genetic high risk for a future schizophrenia diagnosis (ie, at least 1 parent with schizophrenia) as well as 2 comparison groups.8 Thus, the NYHRP was distinct from other projects that focused on clinical high-risk (ie, individuals with prodromal symptoms of psychosis who might or might not have a family history of mental illness). A major goal of the NYHRP was to assess children to identify characteristics that differentiated between those at genetic high risk from those with 2 healthy parents (or parents with an affective diagnosis), even many years before the appearance of any psychiatric symptoms.8 The assumption was that such differences in cognition or other markers would be attributable to genetic liability for schizophrenia.8

In her earliest publications, Cornblatt and her colleagues found that the children at genetic high-risk exhibited deficits in attentional vigilance compared to those at low risk.10,11 They measured attentional vigilance using an early version of the Continuous Performance Task (CPT), a type of monitoring task developed in the 1950s, in which participants view a continuous series of stimuli (eg, letters or images) over an extended period and are instructed to either make or withhold a response to rare target stimuli embedded in long strings of distractors. These early studies were groundbreaking because they established that deficits in sustained attention could be markers of genetic liability for schizophrenia, not merely sequelae or symptoms of the disorder. Indeed, much later family studies replicated and extended Cornblatt’s early findings by identifying elevated CPT deficits in unaffected first-degree relatives.12–17

Other studies beginning in the 1970s found corroborating evidence of attentional deficits as stable vulnerability markers of psychosis. For example, several early studies found that schizophrenia samples showed persistent deficits even during clinical remission.18,19 Similarly, other studies found that attention deficits persisted both in and out of psychotic episodes, consistent with trait-like impairments that remained regardless of symptom state.6,7 Together, these converging lines of evidence established attention deficits as enduring individual characteristics rather than state-dependent illness manifestations.

Later, Cornblatt and others further demonstrated that childhood attention deficits predicted schizophrenia spectrum disorders. Children from the NYHRP (a genetic high-risk cohort) who showed early deficits on CPT tasks had significantly elevated risk of developing psychosis later in life.20 Indeed, this predictive value of attentional vigilance measures was confirmed in a 25-year follow-up study that found impaired CPT performance in childhood predicted later development of schizophrenia but not mood or anxiety disorders.21 Later studies from the NYHRP confirmed that at-risk individuals who exhibited CPT performance deficits in childhood had markedly elevated risk for developing schizophrenia-spectrum disorders in adulthood.22,23 This type of research was a major throughline in Cornblatt’s career. Indeed, work published by Cornblatt and her colleagues just last year from a newer clinical high-risk cohort (the North American Prodrome Longitudinal Study; NAPLS2) found that CPT performance predicted role functioning (ie, level of independence in the community) assessed 2 years later.24

In parallel with these groundbreaking longitudinal studies, Cornblatt made another major contribution to the study of attention in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders by developing a gold-standard version of the CPT. During the early days of attention research, CPT tasks often suffered from ceiling effects and other major psychometric issues. To address these limitations, Cornblatt and her colleagues developed an improved version of the CPT. The new CPT-Identical Pairs (CPT-IP) significantly increased attentional and working memory demands relative to earlier versions of the task by requiring participants to respond only when consecutive identical stimuli appeared.25 Through comparative analyses, Cornblatt and her colleagues demonstrated that although both schizophrenia and depression groups had reduced performance on the CPT-IP relative to healthy controls, the schizophrenia group was unique in displaying impaired target-detection alongside increased false-alarm responses.26

Collectively, research by Barbara Cornblatt and her contemporaries between the late 1970s and the early 2000s showed that deficits in sustained attention were heritable and not limited to the active phase of illness in psychosis. Thus, Cornblatt’s research played a major role in establishing sustained attention deficits as candidate endophenotypes of schizophrenia. In addition, Cornblatt led the way in developing a gold-standard test to measure such deficits in attentional vigilance: the CPT-IP. Together, these foundational contributions laid the groundwork for the next major era of attention research in psychosis.

Evolving Concepts of Attention and Standardization: MATRICS and CNTRICS

Attentional deficits in schizophrenia figured prominently in 2 large consensus initiatives sponsored by NIMH in the early 2000s: Measurement and Treatment Research to Improve Cognition in Schizophrenia (MATRICS) and Cognitive Neuroscience Treatment Research to Improve Cognition in Schizophrenia (CNTRICS).

The first of these, MATRICS, was launched to address a fundamental problem in drug approval for schizophrenia. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and NIMH recognized that the cognitive impairment in schizophrenia was a critical unmet need, and that a process was needed to facilitate development of interventions in this area. At that time, there were no standardized procedures for evaluating new agents for cognition enhancement in schizophrenia, which was limiting development in this area. Given the general uncertainty in the field, the FDA was unwilling to approve any drug for improving cognition in schizophrenia without consensus on cognitive domains, measurement of cognition, and study design.27,28 Thus, NIMH launched MATRICS as a process to develop a consensus among experts from academia, NIMH, the pharmaceutical industry, and consumer advocates on research designs and cognitive measurement procedures. The MATIRICS Initiative yielded several deliverables including study design recommendations and an evaluation of promising neuropharmacological targets.29,30

One key contribution of the MATRICS Initiative was identification of separable dimensions of cognition in schizophrenia and development of a consensus measurement instrument to detect cognitive improvement in these domains in clinical trials (ie, The MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery, MCCB).31–33 The most relevant cognitive domains were identified by integrating data from the published literature and a survey of experts.32 The consensus decision was to include seven domains in a cognitive battery that could assess cognitive improvement in clinical trials of schizophrenia: speed of processing, attention/vigilance, working memory, verbal learning, visual learning, reasoning and problem-solving, and social cognition.

Attention presented a challenge for consensus because it could be defined in many different ways. For the development of the MCCB, the construct of attention was defined as “attention / vigilance.” This decision was based on a review of factor analyses of cognitive domains in schizophrenia in which attention was most often assessed using versions of the CPT, which Cornblatt and others had refined for use in schizophrenia and popularized.25,34,35 Two candidate measures of attention/vigilance were evaluated in a data-based psychometric study that included assessment of test-retreat reliability, utility as a repeated measure, and other practical and validity considerations.33 Both were versions of the CPT. The version that was selected based on this psychometric study was the CPT-IP developed by Cornblatt.25 It was selected because it had very high test–retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.84) and no ceiling effect. Thus, Cornblatt’s CPT-IP is the sole assessment of attention/vigilance included in the MCCB, which has been widely used ever since and translated into over 40 languages.

One challenge in selecting measures for the MCCB was the need to produce a set of consensus cognitive measures in a short period of time. Thus, the MATRICS selection process focused on tasks with well-established psychometric properties, along with demonstrated construct validity. Although measures derived from cognitive neuroscience were considered, few had established measurement properties that would have allowed them to be evaluated on an accelerated timeframe.

The CNTRICS project grew out of the final MATRICS meeting, at which the potential benefits of using tasks from cognitive neuroscience were discussed. These included the use of fine grained tasks that measure discrete cognitive processes, the ability to design tasks that distinguish between specific cognitive deficits and generalized poor performance, and the ability to link cognitive deficits to specific neural systems.36 Based on surveys and consensus meetings, CNTRICS arrived at a definition of attention that differed from that adopted by MATRICS.37 Specifically, the CNTRICS consensus was to emphasize input selection processes as central to the definition of attention.38 Studies at the time suggested that voluntary attentional control (ie, the ability to direct attention willfully toward a particular location, object, or visual feature in response to an abstract cue) might be impaired in schizophrenia, but not stimulus-driven attention (ie, automatic, involuntary shifts of attention towards sudden, salient changes in the visual environment, like the appearance of a new stimulus). Thus, CNTRICS emphasized control of attention as the core construct for evaluating paradigms for the study of schizophrenia.37 Control of attention was defined as “the ability to guide and/or change the focus of attention in response to internal representations.”38

Hence, 2 major consensus initiatives in the early 2000s, MATRICS and CNTRICS, both used empirical and consensus-based processes to select tests, but they arrived at different definitions of attention and different representative measures. In the decades since, both types of measures and both definitions of attention have remained in use across different areas of the psychosis literature. The existence of these parallel definitions, which capture notably different aspects of attention, illustrates the breadth of the attentional construct and the importance of precision in identifying the type of attention under investigation in any particular study. Indeed, research in the post-CNTRICS era further illustrates the complexity and multifaceted nature of attention.

Recent Topics in Attention Research (ca. 2010-Present)

Broadly, studies of attention in schizophrenia over the last 15 years have been exemplified by a renewed focus on different subtypes of attention and associated neural mechanisms. In parallel, new theoretical frameworks for thinking about attentional impairments in schizophrenia have exerted a growing influence on the field. While others have reviewed the recent literature in this area more comprehensively,39 we highlight some representative new developments below.

Rethinking Attentional Control and Selection: Hyperfocusing and Beyond

As articulated by CNTRICS, there is now broad agreement that attention often involves an active process of selection, which in many cases can be controlled voluntarily. Studies of voluntary attentional control in schizophrenia have identified expected deficits, but also a few surprising conditions in which schizophrenia samples tend to outperform controls. Perhaps the most influential recent framework for understanding attention in schizophrenia grew out of attempts to reconcile these seemingly contradictory findings. Specifically, Luck, Gold, and others have proposed that attentional hyperfocusing could help explain the complex pattern of attentional selection findings that have emerged in schizophrenia.40–45

In essence, the hyperfocusing hypothesis posits that individuals with schizophrenia tend to have an atypically narrow yet atypically intense attentional focus.42,44 This framework would explain why people with schizophrenia tend to outperform controls on certain kinds of tasks that require a highly directed attentional focus on a single location (eg, performing a highly demanding visual discrimination task at the point of fixation, while ignoring distractor stimuli in the periphery).41,44,46,47 At the same time, it would account for findings that schizophrenia samples tend to underperform relative to controls on tasks that require a broad attentional focus (eg, performing demanding visual detection tasks where targets can appear in multiple targets in the periphery, away from fixation). Although the hyperfocusing hypothesis remains just that—a hypothesis—it nevertheless represents a major conceptual advance that provides a parsimonious and mechanistic framework for thinking about attentional selection in schizophrenia. Indeed, testable predictions follow from the hyperfocusing framework (eg, that schizophrenia samples will outperform controls under testing conditions that benefit from an intense attentional focus and reduced susceptibility to certain types of distractors), which will be helpful for future studies of attention in schizophrenia.

Social Attention

Another recent development involves a gradual blurring of traditional distinctions between social and nonsocial cognitive processes, including attention. Even relatively recently, attention tended to be categorized firmly in the domain of non-social cognition.48 Yet, there is growing recognition that social stimuli can influence attention in specific ways, and that socially driven attention could be aberrant in schizophrenia.

Studies of social attention in schizophrenia to date have tended to take 1 of 2 forms. One type includes studies that use eye-tracking to assess the amount of time people spend looking at social stimuli. Studies of this kind have shown, for example, that people with schizophrenia tend to spend less time than controls looking at faces and informative facial features (eg, eyes), during free-viewing,49,50 but more time looking at threatening scenes.51 However, schizophrenia participants’ gaze more closely resembles controls’ when participants are given explicit instructions to extract specific social information during the viewing period (eg, to identify the expression, age, or gender of a face).50 Effects found in such eye-tracking studies may not be entirely attributable to attentional influences, however, since eye-movements can be influenced by many non-attentional factors. Nevertheless, these studies suggest that the way people with schizophrenia view social stimuli tends to differ from controls, particularly under non-directed conditions.

The second common type of social attention study uses a social stimulus as a cue, then measures attention using a probe stimulus, much like typical nonsocial attention-cueing paradigms. For example, various studies have compared face-stimuli to symbols as a way of cueing spatial attention in schizophrenia (eg, eyes vs. arrows pointing to a particular location). Results from such studies have been mixed. Some have found that eye-gaze stimuli tend to orient observers’ spatial attention to a similar degree in schizophrenia as in controls,52,53 but others have found impaired gaze-directed attentional cueing in schizophrenia.54–56 The reason for these discrepant results is not yet entirely clear, but one possibility is that some gaze stimuli (eg, photos) are better able to direct attention in schizophrenia than others (eg, drawings).53

Studies of social attention have also begun to investigate different temporal stages of attentional processing for social stimuli in schizophrenia (ie, initial orienting vs. sustained attention and executive control). Two recent studies in this area have addressed this issue using ERP-based methods. The first study tested a medication-naïve schizophrenia sample and found ERP effects congruent with altered orienting to faces (reduced P1 ERPs) as well as altered executive control (reduced P3 amplitude).57 Another study in a sample of medicated chronic schizophrenia found ERP effects congruent with relatively intact orienting (N1pc) as well as enhanced sustained attention and executive control (P300) in the clinical group compared to controls.58

Overall, the small but growing social attention literature suggests there are likely differences in the way that social information influences attentional orienting in schizophrenia. However, the details of exactly how these differences manifest remain murky, due to conflicting findings, and there are still too few studies to provide a clear view of social attention in psychosis. However, the data so far suggest—very tentatively—that group differences in social attention may be more pronounced for naturalistic stimuli (eg, photos, videos) than for simplified drawings. Medication status may play a role in social attention too, but this possibility is also speculative given the limited evidence at hand. Clearly, social attention remains an important topic for future study, to help reconcile the conflicting findings in psychosis that have emerged so far.

Spatiotemporal Dynamics of Attention

Finally, several recent visual attention studies have emphasized the spatiotemporal complexity of attentional orienting in schizophrenia, and the degree to which abnormal rhythmic brain activity might influence performance on attention tasks in schizophrenia. The growth of research in this area parallels a shift in the non-clinical literature, which acknowledges allocation of attention in time and space to be more dynamic than was once believed. Traditionally, the “spotlight” of visual attention was assumed to be relatively stable once it was trained on a spatial location or object. However, more recent work suggests that the spatial extent of the attentional spotlight can morph gradually over time (eg, to conform to the contours of visual objects).59–64 Moreover, the intensity of the attentional spotlight has been shown to flicker rapidly over time, in concert with ongoing rhythmic brain activity at corresponding frequencies, with shifts in the attentional focus tending to occur in conjunction with the phase of oscillations.65–67 A small but growing body of clinical research suggests that such attentional dynamics may be altered in schizophrenia.

Naturally, research in this area tends to utilize paradigms with high temporal resolution. In practice, this often means EEG. Broadly, 2 main types of EEG paradigms exist for this type of research: those that seek to tie intrinsic temporal rhythms of the brain (eg, alpha oscillations) to attentional performance, and those that seek to measure relationships between the timing of brain activity and performance on attentional tasks (eg, whether ability to detect attended targets depends on brain activity having certain characteristics shortly before the appearance of the stimulus). In addition to EEG paradigms, research in this area has also utilized clever performance-based tasks that rely on the presentation of many trials at various millisecond-precise timing intervals (eg, to determine how target-detection ability fluctuates as a function of time).

Much of the clinical research in this emerging research area has focused on understanding the potential influence of ongoing alpha-band (~8-12 Hz) EEG activity on visual attention. Within the alpha range, the exact frequency at which neural activity tends to oscillate varies considerably across individuals, but it remains relatively constant within-person. It has long been known that schizophrenia samples tend to have slower alpha oscillations than controls on average.68 More intriguingly, though, recent evidence from 2 studies in schizophrenia have found that individual differences in peak alpha frequency are linked to individual differences in attention. Specifically, people with schizophrenia who have slower resting alpha oscillations tend to perform worse on attentional tasks (including the CPT-IP).69,70 One interpretation of this result is that ongoing alpha-band activity in the brain may reflect a sort of “refresh rate” of attention, where faster cycling corresponds to an ability to shift the focus of attention more rapidly among locations or features.

Other recent work suggests the trial-wise dynamics of alpha activity during attention tasks may be aberrant in schizophrenia. One such study found that inter-trial phase coherence and event-related desynchronization of alpha activity during a CPT task were both diminished in schizophrenia.71 In other words, the rhythmicity of alpha oscillations was more variable in schizophrenia, and less malleable in response to task demands. In addition, that study found similar effects at beta (13-20 Hz) and theta (4-7 Hz) frequencies. Another recent study that used a totally different type of attention task (Posner cueing) also found abnormal inter-trial coherence in the alpha- and theta-bands for schizophrenia participants.72 Collectively, results like these suggest that the ability to modulate ongoing rhythmic brain activity in response to attentional task demands may be impaired in schizophrenia.

In a final example of research in this emerging area, a recent non-EEG study by our group used a performance-based paradigm to assess the spatiotemporal dynamics of attention in schizophrenia.73 Previous studies in healthy samples have identified rapid, periodic fluctuations in target detection accuracy during cued attention tasks at frequencies congruent with alpha- and theta-band oscillations.67,74–77 In other words, there is an accuracy-based signature of the flickering attentional “spotlight” that can be measured by repeatedly assessing performance at various densely sampled timepoints, spaced milliseconds apart, relative to the time of an attentional cue. In our study, we used this performance-based effect to interrogate attentional allocation to visual objects. We found that these rapid fluctuations in accuracy are present in schizophrenia during an attentional task, as in controls. Interestingly, though, the study revealed that in schizophrenia, the attentional spotlight appeared to get stuck in the cued location, and did not spread outward to the boundaries of the object as in controls.59–64 This effect is broadly congruent with the hyperfocusing hypothesis, and suggests that object-based attention may be disrupted in schizophrenia. More generally, it could also imply that the capacity of the attentional spotlight to morph to the optimal size and shape for a particular stimulus may be impaired in schizophrenia.

Attention Research in Schizophrenia: Looking Back and Looking Forward

Despite the incredible progress the field has made over the last 35 years, thanks in large part to Barbara Cornblatt’s contributions, there remains much we do not know about attention in schizophrenia. In some respects, the field seems as though it may be on the cusp of another transformation in the way attention is studied and understood in schizophrenia. In this final section, we consider recent developments that stand to contribute to another resurgence of attention research similar the one that Cornblatt and her contemporaries initiated.

Investigating Understudied Types of Attention

The widespread adoption of standardized tests of attentional vigilance (eg, the MATRICS CPT-IP) and attentional control has moved the field forward in many respects. At the same time, there are myriad other kinds of attention that could be clinically relevant which have hardly been investigated in schizophrenia. Thus, a sizeable knowledge-gap remains as to how other kinds of attention might be altered in psychosis.

There is a simple and obvious way to address this gap in knowledge. We believe it would benefit the field for psychosis researchers to utilize a broader array of tasks that measure more diverse types of attention alongside widely-used consensus paradigms. Indeed, non-clinical researchers have developed extensive taxonomies of attention, based on more than a century of research. While no particular categorization scheme is universally accepted, examination of any schema illustrates the fact that many (if not most) subtypes of attention have received little research emphasis in the psychosis literature. A Greater Focus on Specific Neural Mechanisms

In another development that has not entirely filtered down to the psychosis literature, there has been a growing shift in the conceptualization of attention among basic researchers. Specifically, there is general agreement that attention is not one thing. In other words, various phenomena labeled “attention” could result from changes in neural processing within neural circuits that may not overlap at all, even if they share a similar architecture.79–82 All these effects are “attention” in the sense that they reflect dynamic changes in information processing, but in some cases the commonalities may end there. In addition, nonclinical researchers have lately tended to reconceptualize attention as less of a cause and more an effect.83 In other words, rather than thinking of attention as the driving force behind dynamic shifts in information processing, there is a growing movement to recognize attention as the effect of such shifts in neural activity.

An important implication for psychosis researchers of these shifts in thinking is that although the functioning of some neural mechanisms of attention is clearly altered in schizophrenia, other mechanisms may be unaffected. Thus, it may be worthwhile to focus more resources on understanding the operation of particular neural mechanisms underlying attention more directly, not just the measuring the downstream effects of dynamic changes in those neural circuits (eg, changes in accuracy or reaction time). Ultimately, downstream effects could be the same even when they are attributable to wholly different neural causes. By more precisely delineating affected versus unaffected neural mechanisms, we stand to achieve a better understanding of exactly what types of attentional deficits exist in psychosis. If we can achieve this, it would be a major step toward developing targeted interventions to address particular types of dysfunction.

Fortunately, a wealth of methods exist in the basic science literature that clinical researchers can adapt to elucidate neural mechanisms of attention in schizophrenia. These range from direct measures of neural activity (eg, EEG), to indirect measures (eg, fMRI), to carefully designed performance-based tasks that can shed light on likely mechanisms (eg, perceptual psychophysics). Although some such methods can be demanding for both researchers and participants, others are quite practical and tolerable, making them good candidates for more widespread adoption, but even demanding methods can often be adapted to make them tractable in clinical populations.

Toward New Interventions and Advances in Clinical Practice

Another promising extension of the work on attention in schizophrenia raises the possibility of developing new interventions. An established body of research has shown that individuals’ capacity for attentional allocation can be improved via cognitive remediation.84 Although different versions of cognitive remediation exist, controlled trials often incorporate computerized cognitive training (ie, a set of game-like tasks that target specific cognitive domains) in conjunction with psychosocial interventions (eg, coaching, skills training or discussion groups).85 Cognitive remediation methods have been used and studied extensively in schizophrenia, and considerable evidence suggests that they can improve attentional vigilance in the illness.86

Despite longstanding interest in cognitive remediation for schizophrenia, a number of open questions remain. Most studies have used the CPT-IP or similar tasks to measure attentional vigilance, so less is known about the extent to which cognitive remediation can improve performance for other kinds of attention. In addition, relatively few studies have examined cognitive remediation effects in clinical high-risk populations.87 However, efforts to investigate the potential utility of this approach in high-risk youth are currently underway, including a study recently initiated by Barbara Cornblatt and her colleagues.88

Apart from cognitive remediation, other potential pathways toward modifying attention are emerging in the non-clinical literature. Perhaps the most intriguing development has been a growing consensus that playing action video games (ie, commercial, mass-market video games that require strong concentration and precise motor reactions to game stimuli) can improve individuals’ ability to control their attention.89 The exact nature of the learning effects associated with video games may vary depending on the demands of the particular game, but most studies have found that video games can improve players’ ability to direct their attention to space, time, or objects outside of the game.89

So far, only a handful of studies have investigated the effects of commercial video games on cognition in schizophrenia, and findings have been mixed.90,91 This could be in part because few studies used action games with high attentional demands; most studies in schizophrenia have tested other video game genres that may not require the same degree or kind of attention as action games. Another limitation of the current literature is that very few studies in schizophrenia have directly measured the effect of games on attention; most have focused on broader cognitive measures or other outcomes such as symptoms or functioning. However, at least one recent clinical trial found a significant improvement in attentional vigilance in schizophrenia with video game training.92 If such findings are replicated and extended, then naturally rewarding video games that engage the attentional system could present a basis for the development of novel interventions to improve specific types of attention in psychosis.

Conclusion

Thanks in large part to the pioneering work of Barbara Cornblatt, the last few decades have witnessed an explosion of research on attention in psychosis and a clear demonstration of the importance of attention for understanding clinical conditions such as schizophrenia. Indeed, without Cornblatt’s pioneering contributions, our understanding of attention in schizophrenia would not be what it is today. In particular, Cornblatt’s outsized contributions to genetic high-risk research and her development of the CPT-IP as a gold-standard measure of attentional vigilance continue to shape the field in profound ways. Beyond the influence that Cornblatt’s research has had on major national initiatives (eg, MATRICS, CNTRICS), her work has also shaped the work of countless individual laboratories.

Overall, the attention revolution sparked by Cornblatt and her colleagues in the late 1970s remains alive and well. There continues to be strong interest in the construct of attention among schizophrenia researchers. CPT-IP paradigms are still commonly deployed across a wide range of studies, often in standardized forms such as the MCCB. Results obtained with such methods have proven to be highly robust, consistent, and reproducible. Individuals with schizophrenia tend to show deficits in vigilance that are linked to outcomes. Other ongoing lines of research in schizophrenia continue to pursue questions related to other types of attention (eg, studies of voluntary attentional control and social attention).

Nevertheless, attention constitutes a broad and elusive set of phenomena. Although the field has made great strides in understanding some types of attention, much work remains to be done in order to reach a comprehensive understanding of attention in psychosis. We are hopeful that psychosis researchers today will follow Cornblatt’s example by pursuing novel lines of inquiry and developing innovative new methods. If we are lucky, such efforts might just spark a second revolution in understanding schizophrenia, similar to the one kindled by Barbara Cornblatt some 35 years ago.
