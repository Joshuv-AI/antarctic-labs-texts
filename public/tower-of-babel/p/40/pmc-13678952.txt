LC–MS/MS-based phytochemical profiling and preliminary in vitro anticancer-related bioactivity of Euphorbia petrophila ethanol extract
Authors: Mehmet Ali Güzel, İrem Nur Menevşe, Turgay Kolaç, Şengül Yüksel, Birol Mutlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEuphorbia species contain diverse secondary metabolites with reported biological activities, but Euphorbia petrophila remains pharmacologically underexplored. This study aimed to characterize the phytochemical profile of E. petrophila ethanol extract and evaluate its preliminary in vitro anticancer-related activity in lung and breast cancer cell models.MethodsAerial parts of E. petrophila were extracted with ethanol using ultrasonic-assisted extraction. The extract was profiled by LC–MS/MS. Cytotoxicity was assessed by MTT assay in A549 and MDA-MB-231 cancer cells and in BEAS-2B and MCF10A non-tumorigenic epithelial cells. Apoptosis and cell-cycle distribution were analyzed by flow cytometry. Migration and invasion were evaluated using Transwell assays, and selected gene-expression changes were measured by RT-qPCR. Data were analyzed using appropriate analysis of variance and post hoc comparisons.ResultsLC–MS/MS analysis identified phenolic acids and flavonoids, with gallic acid, quinic acid, ellagic acid, and isoquercitrin among the most abundant detected constituents. The extract reduced IC₅₀ value of in all tested cell lines, with the strongest cytotoxic effect observed in A549 cells. It increased apoptotic cell populations and promoted G0/G1 cell-cycle accumulation, particularly in A549 cells. The extract also markedly suppressed migration and invasion in A549 and MDA-MB-231 cells. These functional effects were accompanied by changes in TP53 and CDKN1A mRNA expression.ConclusionsE. petrophila ethanol extract shows preliminary antiproliferative, pro-apoptotic, anti-migratory, and anti-invasive activity in selected cancer cell models. However, because the findings are based on crude extract testing and transcript-level analyses, the results should be interpreted as preliminary and correlative. Further work should include extract standardization, LC–MS/MS method validation, broader normal-cell toxicity profiling, active-compound isolation, protein-level mechanistic assays, and in vivo safety and efficacy studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-026-05470-8.

Introduction

Cancer represents a leading cause of mortality globally, characterized by uncontrolled cellular proliferation, genetic instability, and metastatic potential [1]. Breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed malignancy among women, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases and over 670,000 deaths reported in 2022 alone [2]. Likewise, lung cancer remains the foremost cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. Among its histological subtypes, adenocarcinoma accounts for approximately 40% of all cases, whereas non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) constitutes nearly 85% [3].

Conventional treatment modalities such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy are often associated with severe side effects due to their nonspecific cytotoxic effects on healthy tissues [4]. Consequently, the demand for safer and more selective cytotoxic agents, especially those derived from natural sources has grown considerably in recent years. In this context, medicinal plants offer a promising reservoir of bioactive compounds with potential applications in oncology [5].

The genus Euphorbia (family: Euphorbiaceae), consisting of over 2,000 species distributed across temperate and tropical zones, has been traditionally used to treat a wide spectrum of diseases, including inflammatory disorders, infections, and skin conditions [6]. Members of this genus exhibit vast morphological diversity, ranging from herbaceous plants and shrubs to succulent and cactus-like forms [7]. Pharmacological studies have confirmed the presence of diterpenoids, triterpenoids, flavonoids, and polyphenols in various Euphorbia species, many of which exhibit cytotoxic, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial effects [8].

Numerous Euphorbia species have demonstrated notable anticancer activities. For instance, euphol, isolated from Euphorbia tirucalli, showed dose-dependent cytotoxicity in 73 human cancer cell lines and significantly suppressed lung metastasis in mouse melanoma models [9]. Euphorbia helioscopia was associated with increased apoptotic cell populations and cell cycle arrest in multiple cancer cell types, while compounds from Euphorbia kansui and E. fischeriana were reported to enhance reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, modulate mitochondrial membrane potential, and downregulate anti-apoptotic proteins such as Bcl-2 [10–12].

Other promising species include E. hirta, which demonstrated caspase-mediated apoptosis in breast cancer cells (MCF-7) [13]. and E. humifusa, which inhibited TNF-α and MMP-9 expression in metastatic breast cancer models through NF-κB signaling suppression [14]. Moreover, extracts from E. petiolata and E. hebecarpa were shown to increase IFN-γ production and promote lymphocyte proliferation, suggesting immunomodulatory effects with potential relevance in cancer immunotherapy [15].

Despite these promising findings, E. petrophila remains poorly studied. The present study therefore evaluated the preliminary in vitro anticancer-related activity of E. petrophila ethanol extract against human lung and breast cancer cell models, including analyses of cell viability, apoptosis, cell-cycle distribution, migration, invasion, and selected gene-expression changes.

Overall, this work was designed to generate preliminary in vitro evidence on the biological activity of E. petrophila ethanol extract and to identify key experimental gaps that should be addressed before any therapeutic interpretation can be made.

Material and methods

Plant material and extraction procedure

Specimens of E. petrophila were collected from the Yama Mountain region in Hekimhan, Malatya (Eastern Anatolia, Turkey). The collected plant material consisted of the aerial parts of the plant, which were carefully cleaned and air-dried in a cool, dry, and shaded environment, protected from direct sunlight. The dried plant samples were stored in a cool, dry place until further use. The herbarium specimen details of E. petrophila are provided in Table 1. For extraction, a 20 g sample of the powdered aerial parts of E. petrophila was subjected to solvent-assisted ultrasonic extraction. The aerial material was soaked in 150 mL of 96% ethanol for 2 h in an ultrasonic bath. The same process was repeated twice with 100 mL of ethanol for 30 min each. The resulting extract was filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper, and the pooled filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator to obtain the ethanolic extract. This process yielded 1.559 g of dry extract, representing an extraction yield of approximately 7.8% (w/w). The dried extract was stored at 4 °C in amber glass vials in a refrigerator until use. All experimental studies were conducted using this ethanol extract.

LC–MS/MS analysis

The phytochemical constituents of the E. petrophila ethanol extract were characterized using a Thermo Scientific UHPLC system coupled with a TSQ Quantum Access MAX triple quadrupole mass spectrometer, equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Thermo Accucore C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 2.6 µm) maintained at 30 °C. The mobile phase consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid (solvent A) and ethanol with 0.1% formic acid (solvent B), applied under a linear gradient program. The elution protocol was as follows: 0–2 min, 5% B; 2–10 min, linear ramp to 95% B; 10–12 min, held at 95% B; 12–13 min, return to 5% B; 13–15 min, re-equilibration. The flow rate was set at 0.3 mL/min with an injection volume of 10 µL. MS detection was conducted in negative ion mode with the following parameters: spray voltage 3500 V, capillary temperature 320 °C, sheath gas 35 arb, auxiliary gas 10 arb, and collision gas pressure 1.5 mTorr.Compound identification and quantification were performed in Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) mode by comparing retention times, precursor/product ion transitions, and fragmentation patterns with authentic standards and literature-reported values. Prior to injection, extract samples were diluted to 1 mg/mL in LC–MS-grade ethanol and filtered through a 0.22 µm PTFE syringe filter [16].

Cell lines and culture conditions

MDA-MB-231 (human breast adenocarcinoma, MCF10A (non-tumorigenic human mammary epithelial cells), A549 (human lung adenocarcinoma), and BEAS-2B (non-tumorigenic human bronchial epithelial cells) were procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). All four cell lines were cultured in DMEM medium, supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin solution, and maintained under standard conditions in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.In parallel, the non-tumorigenic human mammary epithelial cell line MCF10A was cultured in DMEM/F12 (1:1) basal medium, supplemented with 5% (v/v) horse serum, 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF), 0.5 μg/mL hydrocortisone, 100 ng/mL cholera toxin, and 10 μg/mL human insulin. All media and supplements were obtained from certified commercial suppliers and were prepared under sterile conditions. Cells were routinely passaged at ~ 80% confluence using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA and were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free prior to experimentation. These standardized conditions ensured reproducibility and physiological relevance across all downstream in vitro assays [17].

IC₅₀ value of analysis using the MTT assay

The anticancer potential of the plant samples was evaluated in accordance with the protocol adapted from Sharma et al. (2019) using four human cell lines: BEAS-2B (non-tumorigenic lung epithelial cells), A549 (lung adenocarcinoma), MCF10A (non-tumorigenic mammary epithelial cells), and MDA-MB-231 (breast adenocarcinoma). Cisplatin was used as a positive control. All cell lines were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. Once cells reached 70–80% confluence, the medium was removed, and cells were washed with sterile PBS (pH 7.4). Trypsin–EDTA was added to detach cells, followed by neutralization with fresh complete medium. The suspension was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 7 min, and the pellet was resuspended in fresh medium. Cells were then counted and diluted to a final density of 1 × 105 cells/mL, and 1 × 104 cells per well were seeded into 96-well plates. Plates were incubated for 24 h to allow cell adherence. Plant extracts were prepared in complete medium at final concentrations ranging from 0.8 to 800 µg/mL, and 100 µL of the test solution was added to each well after aspirating the old medium. Cells were incubated for another 24 h at 37 °C, 5% CO₂. Following treatment, the medium was aspirated, and 10 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) and 90 µL of fresh medium were added to each well to achieve a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL MTT.

Plates were incubated for 4 h, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm and 630 nm using an ELISA reader. IC₅₀ value of was calculated using the formula:

[(570 nm − 630 nm)_treated/(570 nm − 630 nm)_control] × 100. The IC₅₀ values were derived based on a logarithmic dose–response curve generated from the IC₅₀ value of data [18, 19].

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR analysis

After 24 h of treatment with E. petrophila extract and cisplatin, cells grown in 6-well plates were harvested by trypsinization and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 7 min. Total RNA was extracted from the resulting pellets using the PureLink™ RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen™) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quantity and purity were assessed with a Denovix DS-11 FX + spectrophotometer, and samples with an A260/A280 ratio of 1.8–2.0 were used for cDNA synthesis. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the ProtoScript® First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit. RT-qPCR was carried out on a StepOne™ Plus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using GoTaq® qPCR Master Mix (Promega). Each 20 μL reaction contained 100 ng cDNA, 200 nM forward and reverse primers, and nuclease-free water [20].

Cell cycle analysis by flow cytometry

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates and treated with E. petrophila extract and cisplatin for 24 h. Following treatment, the culture media were aspirated, and each well was washed three times with PBS. Cells were detached by adding 500 μL of 0.25% trypsin and incubating at 37 °C for 5 min. After detachment, 1 mL of fresh medium was added to each well, and cell suspensions were transferred into sterile microcentrifuge tubes. Cells were gently resuspended and fixed by adding 70% ethanol (Sigma) and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h. After fixation, cells were centrifuged for 7 min, washed twice with PBS, and incubated with 0.1 mg/mL RNase A in 500 μL PBS at 37 °C for 30 min to degrade RNA. Subsequently, cells were stained with 0.05 mg/mL propidium iodide (PI) and incubated in the dark at 4 °C for 30 min. PI-stained cells were analyzed using a BD Accuri™ C6 Plus flow cytometer (BD Life Sciences). A minimum of 10,000 events per sample were acquired, and cell debris was excluded based on forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) parameters. PI was excited with a 488 nm blue laser, and emission was detected using the FL2 channel (585/40 nm bandpass filter). Data were processed using BD Accuri™ C6 Plus software, and the distribution of cells across G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases was quantified. The impact of E. petrophila extract on the cell cycle was evaluated by comparing the phase distributions between treated and untreated groups of the same cell line [21].

Apoptosis analysis by Annexin V-FITC/PI staining

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates and treated with E. petrophila extract and cisplatin for 24 h. After treatment, media were removed, and wells were washed three times with PBS. Adherent cells were detached using 500 μL of 0.25% trypsin per well and incubated for 5 min at 37 °C. The detached cells were collected by adding 1 mL of fresh medium and transferred into sterile microcentrifuge tubes. Following centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 7 min, each cell pellet was resuspended in 100 μL of Annexin V binding buffer (provided in the kit). To each sample, 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC conjugate (90 µg/mL) and 10 μL of propidium iodide (PI; 0.5 mg/mL) were added. Samples were gently vortexed and incubated for 15 min in the dark at room temperature. After incubation, 400 μL of additional binding buffer was added to each tube prior to flow cytometric analysis. Apoptotic profiles were analyzed using a BD Accuri™ C6 Plus Flow Cytometer (BD Life Sciences). A minimum of 10,000 events per sample were acquired. Debris was excluded based on forward (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) signals. Migration and invasion assays using a transwell system

Cell migration and invasion abilities were assessed using the Boyden chamber (Transwell® system) with 8 μm pore-size polycarbonate membrane inserts (Transwell® Permeable Supports, Polycarbonate Membrane, Cat No: 3428, Corning Inc.). For migration assays, cells were seeded directly into the upper chamber without any matrix coating. For invasion assays, the upper surface of the membranes was coated with 1 mL of Matrigel® Basement Membrane Matrix (200–300 μg/mL, Corning®, Cat No: 354234) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to allow gel polymerization prior to seeding. In both assays, the Transwell inserts were placed into wells containing complete medium (chemoattractant) in the lower chamber, while cells suspended in serum-free medium were seeded in the upper chamber. After the incubation period (typically 24 h), non-migrated/invaded cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed with a cotton swab. Cells that had traversed the membrane were fixed, stained, and analyzed. Quantitative assessment was performed by flow cytometry using the BD Accuri™ C6 Plus system, and the data were processed using BD Accuri™ C6 Plus software. Migrated and invaded cell populations were compared across experimental groups to evaluate the effects of E. petrophila extract [23].

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted to assess the significance of differences between groups. For in vitro cytotoxicity data obtained from multiple treatment groups across different cell lines, two way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used to determine significant differences between treatments. Prior to ANOVA, data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was confirmed via Levene’s test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Experiments were performed as three independent biological replicates, each including technical triplicates.

IC₅₀ value of assay using MTT

The antiproliferative effects of E. petrophila extract on cancerous cell lines (A549 and MDA-MB-231) and non-tumorigenic epithelial cell lines (BEAS-2B and MCF10A) were assessed using the MTT assay. Cells were treated with varying concentrations of the extract and cisplatin ranging from 0.8 to 800 μg/mL for 24 h. IC₅₀ value of percentages were calculated spectrophotometrically using absorbance values obtained at 570 and 630 nm. IC₅₀ values were then determined from logarithmic concentration–response curves based on the calculated IC₅₀ value of percentages.

Discussion

The present study investigated the phytochemical profile and biological activities of E. petrophila, an endemic plant species collected from high altitudes of the Yama Mountain region in Malatya, Turkey. All experimental assays were conducted using ethanol extracts prepared from authenticated herbarium specimens (Herbarium No: 16549–2023, INUH). Taxonomic validation of plant material is critical for scientific traceability and reproducibility, particularly in phytopharmacological studies where misidentification can lead to erroneous interpretations of biological effects [24].

The LC–MS/MS results indicate that E. petrophila ethanol extract contains several phenolic acids and flavonoids that may contribute to its observed biological activity. However, because the study tested a crude extract rather than isolated compounds, the contribution of each constituent cannot be determined from the present data.

The presence of hydroxybenzoic acid and salicylic acid in the extract is noteworthy. Both compounds are phenolic acids that have been shown to exhibit strong antioxidant activities [25]. They may contribute to protecting cells from oxidative stress, which is a key factor in the progression of cancer. Oxidative damage can promote tumor growth and metastasis, and the antioxidant activity of these compounds could play a role in mitigating these effects [26]. Hydroxybenzoic acid, in particular, has been associated with the inhibition of cancer cell proliferation, making it an important bioactive component in the extract [27].

The most abundant compound in the extract is gallic acid, which was detected at 13,735.17 ng/mL. Gallic acid is a well-known antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agent with significant anticancer properties [28]. Studies have shown that gallic acid can induce apoptosis in cancer cells and inhibit the growth and migration of various cancer types [29]. This could explain the observed inhibitory effects of E. petrophila extract on cancer cell migration and invasion, as demonstrated in previous experiments with A549 and MDA-MB-231 cell lines.

Several flavonoids were also detected in the extract, including apigenin, rutin hydrate, quercetin, isoquercitrin, and kaempferol. Quercetin, in particular, has been extensively studied for its anticancer properties, including its ability to inhibit cell proliferation and migration in various cancer types [30]. Rutin hydrate, which was found at 1068.55 ng/mL, has been shown to reduce the invasion of cancer cells by inhibiting matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activity, a key enzyme involved in the breakdown of the extracellular matrix (ECM) during metastasis [31]. Isoquercitrin and apigenin also possess potent anticancer and anti-inflammatory effects, modulating pathways involved in cancer progression such as cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [32].

Additionally, ellagic acid and chlorogenic acid were found at substantial levels in the extract, with ellagic acid at 9490.81 ng/mL and chlorogenic acid at 608.96 ng/mL. Both compounds are known for their antioxidant and anticancer effects [33]. Ellagic acid, in particular, has been shown to modulate key signaling pathways such as NF-kB and AP-1, which are involved in inflammation and cancer progression [34].

The coexistence of these compounds may contribute to the observed bioactivity, but synergy was not directly tested. Future studies should use fractionation, compound-depletion, and combination-index approaches to determine whether the extract acts through additive, synergistic, or antagonistic interactions.

The high concentrations of gallic acid, quercetin-related derivatives, rutin, and ellagic acid support the rationale for further mechanistic testing. Nevertheless, the present evidence is insufficient to describe the extract as a therapeutic anticancer agent; it should be described as a candidate extract with preliminary in vitro bioactivity.

The IC₅₀ value of values presented in Table 5 show that E. petrophila extract and cisplatin exhibit distinct cytotoxic effects across different cell lines. In BEAS-2B cells (normal lung epithelial cells), cisplatin was more cytotoxic, with an IC₅₀ value of 31.09 ± 0.98 μg/mL, compared to E. petrophila extract, which had an IC₅₀ value of 55.52 ± 2.61 μg/mL. This indicates that cisplatin is more effective in reducing the viability of normal lung cells.

In A549 lung cancer cells, E. petrophila extract showed stronger cytotoxicity than cisplatin under the tested conditions. This finding is promising but should be interpreted cautiously because it depends on dose, exposure time, assay type, and the lack of broader toxicity data.

For MCF10A cells (normal mammary epithelial cells), cisplatin was slightly more effective, with an IC₅₀ value of 36.66 ± 1.23 μg/mL, compared to E. petrophila extract at 43.99 ± 1.92 μg/mL. These findings suggest possible differences in cellular sensitivity to the extract; however, the measurable effects observed in non-tumorigenic cell lines indicate that tumor selectivity cannot be concluded without selectivity index calculations and broader toxicity profiling.

In the MDA-MB-231 (triple-negative breast cancer) cell line, cisplatin was again more cytotoxic, with an IC₅₀ value of 27.31 ± 0.74 μg/mL, compared to E. petrophila extract at 62.19 ± 3.10 μg/mL. Although the extract showed differential effects across malignant and non-tumorigenic cell lines, the measurable reduction in viability and increased apoptotic populations observed in BEAS-2B and MCF10A cells indicate that tumor selectivity cannot be inferred from the present data alone.

Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated significant apoptotic induction in A549 (23.5%), followed by moderate effects in MCF10A (14.3%) and BEAS-2B (11.2%) cells. Interestingly, MDA-MB-231 cells showed lower apoptosis (10.7%), indicating possible resistance mechanisms. These results were further substantiated by the upregulation of Tp53 and CDKN1A expression levels, particularly in MCF10A and MDA-MB-231 cells (22.6% and 34.3%, respectively). Although E. petrophila extract increased TP53 and CDKN1A (p21) expression and promoted G0/G1 accumulation, the transcriptional response of CDK4 and CCND1 was not uniform across cell lines. In A549 and MCF10A cells, CDK4 and/or CCND1 transcripts were not reduced despite clear evidence of cell-cycle redistribution, suggesting that mRNA expression alone may not reflect the functional inhibition of CyclinD–CDK4/6 complexes. These findings support the possibility that p21-dependent checkpoint control may occur through post-transcriptional or post-translational regulatory mechanisms rather than simple transcriptional repression of CDK4 and Cyclin [35, 36].

On the other hand, E. petrophila extract may be associated with apoptotic responses through mechanisms that could potentially involve extrinsic death receptor related and or intrinsic mitochondrial pathways; however, direct activation of these pathways was not demonstrated in the present study and requires confirmation through protein level and functional assays [37]. Flavonoids and phenolic acids found in E. petrophila extract, such as quercetin, gallic acid, and ellagic acid, have been shown to upregulate p53 and CDKN1A expression, both of which are involved in cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. It is possible that ROS-related cellular stress may contribute to the apoptotic response induced by E. petrophila extract; however, ROS production, mitochondrial dysfunction, cytochrome c release, and caspase-9/caspase-3 activation were not directly demonstrated in this study. Therefore, involvement of the intrinsic mitochondrial apoptotic pathway should be interpreted as a hypothesis requiring further experimental validation [38]. The marked TP53 induction in non-tumorigenic cells highlights the need for genotoxicity evaluation.

The differential apoptotic responses observed among the tested cell lines suggest that E. petrophila extract may exert cell line–dependent effects. In A549 cells, the extract was associated with a higher apoptotic rate than cisplatin under the current experimental conditions; however, the mechanistic basis of this response remains uncertain. The potential involvement of multiple apoptotic pathways cannot be concluded without direct evidence of pathway activation, such as caspase activity, mitochondrial dysfunction, death receptor signaling, or DNA damage markers. Conversely, cisplatin induced a stronger apoptotic response in MDA-MB-231 cells, indicating that treatment sensitivity may vary according to cell-specific molecular features. Overall, these findings should be regarded as preliminary evidence of differential treatment responsiveness rather than definitive proof of distinct apoptotic mechanisms.

Exposure to E. petrophila extract induced a stress-responsive transcriptional pattern characterized by increased TP53 and CDKN1A (p21) expression in both malignant and non-malignant epithelial cell models. Together with the observed increase in apoptotic cell populations and the accumulation of cells in the G0/G1 phase, these findings are consistent with activation of cell-cycle checkpoint–related responses. Nevertheless, because the present study assessed transcript levels rather than protein activation or pathway dependency, the data should be interpreted as supportive of an association rather than definitive evidence of a p53/p21-mediated mechanism. In addition, the absence of uniform suppression of CDK4 and CCND1 across all cell lines suggests that transcriptional responses may not directly reflect functional CDK4/6 inhibition and that post-transcriptional regulation may also be involved [39]. Notably, CDK4 and CCND1 transcripts were not consistently downregulated across all cell lines, suggesting that transcriptional changes alone may not reliably indicate functional CDK4/6 inhibition. Although CDKN1A may potentially contribute to G0/G1 accumulation through post-transcriptional or post-translational regulation of Cyclin D–CDK complexes, this possibility requires confirmation by protein-level analyses and functional CDK activity assays. Furthermore, the pronounced induction of TP53 and CDKN1A in BEAS-2B and MCF10A cells highlights the need to define the extract’s therapeutic window more clearly and to directly assess potential DNA damage using approaches such as γH2AX detection or comet assay. These additional analyses would help determine whether the observed stress response reflects tumor-selective activity or a broader genotoxic effect in both malignant and non-tumorigenic epithelial cells [40]. In the TNBC model MDA-MB-231, interpretation of TP53 upregulation warrants caution given the frequent presence of mutant TP53 in this lineage, raising the possibility of TP53-independent CDKN1A regulation [41].

The obtained data show that both E. petrophila extract and cisplatin treatments significantly inhibited migration in MDA-MB-231 and A549 cell lines. In A549 cells, migration rates after treatment were 25.21% ± 2.23 for E. petrophila extract and 21.15% ± 2.76 for cisplatin. In MDA-MB-231 cells, migration rates were 20.45% ± 4.36 and 20.24% ± 1.25, respectively, for the same treatments. Compared to the control group, E. petrophila extract inhibited migration by 74.79% in A549 cells and 79.55% in MDA-MB-231 cells (p < 0.05). These findings indicate that E. petrophila extract has a strong potential to inhibit cancer cell migration and, consequently, limit tumor invasion.

The observed inhibition of cell migration may be associated with broader stress-response and cell-cycle regulatory changes induced by E. petrophila extract. Although ATM and ATR are key checkpoint kinases involved in the cellular response to DNA damage, the present study did not directly demonstrate DNA damage or activation of ATM/ATR signaling. Therefore, the relationship between migration inhibition and DNA damage response pathways should be considered hypothetical and requires further validation using protein-level and functional assays [42]. Upon activation by DNA damage, ATM/ATR signaling may regulate TP53 and CDKN1A, two important mediators of cell-cycle control. In the present study, E. petrophila extract and cisplatin treatments were associated with increased TP53 and CDKN1A mRNA expression, accumulation of cells in the G0/G1 phase, and reduced migratory activity [43].

The stronger reduction in migration observed after E. petrophila extract treatment may reflect cell line–specific differences in stress-response, cell-cycle redistribution, or viability-related effects. However, because ATM/p53/p21 pathway activation and functional dependency were not directly demonstrated, this interpretation should remain hypothetical, which is known to regulate cell cycle progression and cell motility [44]. The stronger reduction in migration observed after E. petrophila extract treatment may reflect cell line–specific differences in stress-response, cell-cycle redistribution, or viability-related effects. However, because ATM/ATR activation, p53/p21 protein regulation, and pathway dependency were not directly demonstrated, the mechanistic basis of migration inhibition remains uncertain [45, 46].

Although cisplatin also inhibits migration through CDKN1A transcriptional activation and cell cycle arrest, its effect on migration is less potent compared to E. petrophila extract. This discrepancy may reflect the different mechanisms by which cisplatin and E. petrophila extract exert their effects. Cisplatin primarily induces DNA damage and halts cell cycle progression at the G2/M phase, Although cisplatin and E. petrophila extract showed different patterns of migration inhibition, the present data are insufficient to define distinct mechanistic routes for these effects. Future studies should assess DNA damage markers, ATM/ATR activation, p53/p21 protein levels, and pathway inhibition to clarify whether these pathways contribute directly to migration suppression [47].

The results demonstrate that both E. petrophila extract and cisplatin treatments significantly inhibited invasion in MDA-MB-231 and A549 cell lines. In the A549 cell line, E. petrophila extract treatment reduced invasion to 12.50% ± 2.45, while cisplatin reduced invasion to 81.54% ± 6.76, compared to the control group (100% ± 6.58). Similarly, in the MDA-MB-231 cell line, E. petrophila extract reduced invasion to 15.40% ± 5.56, and cisplatin reduced invasion to 75.54% ± 7.25, compared to the control group (100% ± 5.69%). The differences in invasion are substantial, with E. petrophila extract reducing invasion by 87.50% (A549) and 84.60% (MDA-MB-231) relative to the control (p < 0.05). These findings suggest that E. petrophila extract possesses potent anti-invasive properties, surpassing cisplatin in inhibiting cancer cell invasion in both cell lines.

The significant reduction in cell invasion by E. petrophila extract correlates with its potential to target key molecular pathways involved in cancer metastasis. One likely mechanism of action involves the inhibition of Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT), a key process in cancer cell invasion and metastasis [48]. During EMT, epithelial cells acquire mesenchymal properties that enhance their migratory and invasive capacities [49]. E. petrophila extract may interfere with the activation of EMT markers such as N-cadherin, vimentin, and Snail, thus reducing cancer cell invasion [50].

Cisplatin, a well-known chemotherapeutic agent, likely inhibits invasion through its ability to induce DNA damage and activate the TP53 pathway [51]. Activation of TP53 can lead to cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, limiting the ability of cancer cells to invade surrounding tissues [52]. However, in this study, cisplatin showed less potent inhibition of invasion compared to E. petrophila extract, which could indicate that cisplatin’s effects are more focused on inducing cell death rather than specifically targeting the molecular pathways that regulate invasion.

Furthermore, Matrix Metalloproteinases (MMPs), particularly MMP-2 and MMP-9, play a critical role in the breakdown of the extracellular matrix (ECM) and enable cancer cells to invade surrounding tissues [53]. The marked reduction in invasion observed after E. petrophila extract treatment may be related to changes in invasion-associated cellular processes, potentially including extracellular matrix remodeling. Nevertheless, MMP expression, MMP enzymatic activity, and ECM degradation were not directly evaluated in this study. Therefore, the involvement of MMP-dependent mechanisms remains speculative and should be confirmed in future studies using assays such as gelatin zymography, MMP-2/MMP-9 expression analysis, or ECM degradation assays [54].

The inhibition of cell motility is another crucial aspect of invasion suppression [55]. The marked reduction in invasion suggests that E. petrophila extract may influence invasion-associated cellular behavior. However, the present study did not assess EMT markers, MMP activity, cytoskeletal remodeling, or PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK signaling. Therefore, the molecular basis of the anti-invasive response remains unclear and should be investigated in future mechanistic studie [56]. By modulating these pathways, E. petrophila extract could reduce the ability of cancer cells to migrate and invade. In contrast, cisplatin primarily exerts its effects by inducing DNA damage and activating apoptotic pathways, with less direct impact on the signaling pathways regulating cell motility and invasion.

Conclusion

This study provides initial in vitro evidence that Euphorbia petrophila ethanol extract exhibits antiproliferative, pro-apoptotic, anti-migratory, and anti-invasive activities in selected breast and lung cancer cell models, with a particularly pronounced response in A549 cells. These effects were associated with increased TP53 and CDKN1A mRNA expression and G0/G1 cell-cycle accumulation, suggesting possible involvement of stress-response and cell-cycle regulatory processes. However, because the present findings are limited to in vitro functional assays and transcript-level analyses, they should be interpreted as preliminary and correlative rather than mechanistically definitive. The measurable effects observed in non-tumorigenic epithelial cells further indicate that he extract showed selective cytotoxicity toward A549 cells compared with BEAS-2B cells; however, selectivity was not observed for MDA-MB-231 compared with MCF10A. Future studies should include selectivity index calculations, extended toxicity profiling, LC–MS/MS method validation, compound-specific analyses, protein-level mechanistic validation, and in vivo models to clarify the pharmacological relevance and safety profile of E. petrophila extract.
