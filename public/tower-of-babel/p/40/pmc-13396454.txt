Sex differences in post-exercise hypotension after a recreational beach tennis session in adults with controlled hypertension: a randomized crossover trial
Authors: Leandro de Oliveira Carpes, Nathalia Jung, Rodrigo Ferrari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to compare the effects of a single bout of beach tennis on post-exercise hypotension in men and women with hypertension. In this crossover trial, adults with hypertension were randomized into two experimental sessions: beach tennis and control sessions. For the beach tennis session, they warmed up with basic techniques for 5-min and played three matches lasting 12-min each, interspersed with 2-min recovery intervals. During the control session, the participants remained seated at rest without any exercise. Each protocol lasted for 45-min. Office blood pressure was assessed before and after the experimental sessions for 1-h. A generalized estimating equation analysis was used to compare the main effects of sex on blood pressure. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Fifty-eight participants (29 men and 29 women aged 35–60) participated in this study. Comparing beach tennis with control, systolic/diastolic blood pressure was lower after exercise in men (–17 mmHg, P < 0.001 / –8 mmHg, P < 0.001) and women (–10 mmHg, P = 0.04 / –4 mmHg, P = 0.016). The reduction in systolic blood pressure was greater in men than in women (–7 mmHg, P = 0.040). In adults with hypertension, a single recreational beach tennis session acutely reduced blood pressure in both sexes, with a more pronounced effect observed in men. Tailored beach tennis protocols may be necessary to elicit optimal benefits for women.Trial registration: This trial was registered in the Registro Brasileiro de Ensaios Clínicos (ReBEC) under identifier number RBR-9nm4zs9, with the first registration on March 01, 2024 (https://ensaiosclinicos.gov.br/rg/RBR-9nm4zs9).

Introduction

Regular physical activity is an important non-pharmacological tool for blood pressure control1, with chronic reductions in blood pressure being directly associated with the acute reduction resulting from a single exercise session, a phenomenon known as postexercise hypotension (PEH)2,3. Although traditional aerobic and resistance training exercises are recommended for individuals with hypertension4, adherence to these modalities is often limited. Recreational sports have emerged as attractive exercise alternatives for blood pressure management5,6. For example, beach tennis has been shown to reduce both acute and chronic blood pressure, although these findings are based on pooled analyses of men and women without distinction between sexes6–9, making it an appealing choice owing to its ease of learning and the sport’s growing popularity worldwide. The game is played on a sand court and combines elements of tennis and beach volleyball: players use racquets and low-pressurized tennis balls, and the objective is to hit the ball over the net (set at 1.70 m) without letting it touch the ground. Games are typically played in doubles (court 16 m × 8 m, 2 vs. 2), and scoring follows the rules of traditional tennis10. Other advantages of recreational beach tennis include the possibility to be played by diverse populations6,7,11 and fitness levels12 using its original rules, requiring no special adaptations to remain inclusive and engaging. The lower rate of exertion perceived (RPE) during the games6,7 and reduced injury risk compared to traditional sports13,14 are also significant factors. Furthermore, the intermittent nature of the sport promotes positive cardiovascular adaptations and high enjoyment levels6 make beach tennis a safe and feasible exercise for hypertensive individuals, with potential impact on long-term adherence, a key challenge in hypertension management15,16.

The magnitude of PEH has been associated with several factors, such as the intensity, duration, and type of exercise17–19. Additionally, this response seems to differ between men and women20–23. Recent studies suggest that men may exhibit a greater PEH following a session of traditional aerobic22 and strength exercises21,23 than women who perform the same protocol. However, these results were obtained from secondary analyses with small samples and evaluation of normotensive individuals21,22.

Unlike traditional exercises, in which the session intensity is easily controlled, ensuring a similar relative effort between men and women, the characteristics of team sports can generate significant differences in cardiovascular demands throughout a game. A recent study demonstrated significant differences in heart rate responses between middle-aged men and women throughout a 60-min handball session24, which could also influence the participants’ PEH magnitude and should be further investigated. To date, no study has specifically examined whether men and women with hypertension respond differently to a session of beach tennis, a sport with growing adherence among both sexes. Addressing this gap is crucial to guide personalized exercise prescriptions for this population. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the blood pressure responses of men and women with hypertension after a single beach tennis session. We hypothesized that men would experience greater blood pressure reduction than women following a single beach tennis session.

Materials/subjects and methods

Study design and participants

This randomized crossover trial was conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and in compliance with the Brazilian legal and regulatory framework for research involving humans (NR 466/12). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Hospital de Clínicas de Porto Alegre, Brazil (CAAE: 96,487,118.0.0000.5327), and registered on Registro Brasileiro de Ensaios Clínicos (ReBEC) under identifier number RBR-9nm4zs9. The protocol followed the CONSORT guidelines for nonpharmacological treatment25. Data collection was conducted in 2024.

Fifty-eight participants (29 men and 29 women) were randomly allocated to undergo a beach tennis or a non-exercise control session. Random blocks of four participants were generated by an epidemiologist using a computer software. The epidemiologist remained uninvolved in the participant recruitment or group assignment. The randomization list was kept hidden until assignment time (allocation concealment). Participants were recruited through telephone calls and social media. The inclusion criteria were men and women with previous diagnoses of hypertension between 35 and 65 years, using up to three classes or types of antihypertensive drugs, and who had not engaged in structured exercise programs in the past 3 months. Exclusion criteria included previous diagnoses of cardiovascular disease, including ischemic heart disease, stroke, or heart failure in the last 24 months; current smoking; and musculoskeletal problems that prevented participants from exercising. All participants read and signed an informed consent form before participating the study. Participation was voluntary, and all ethical data confidentiality and protection principles were followed.

Preliminary sessions

Each participant underwent a comprehensive clinical screening, including a resting electrocardiogram and hemodynamic and anthropometric measurements conducted in a laboratory setting. Height and weight were determined using a stadiometer and an analogic scale (FILIZOLA, Brazil), respectively, and subsequently used to calculate the body mass index (weight (kg)/height (m2)). Waist circumference was measured using a standardized inelastic measuring tape (Cescorf, Brazil) positioned at the midpoint between the iliac crest of the horizontal plane and lower costal region. The arm circumference at the midpoint between the acromion and the cubital fossa was assessed to determine the appropriate cuff size for blood pressure monitoring.

Standardized office hemodynamics (blood pressure and heart rate) were obtained after 20 min of seated rest according to the Brazilian National Guidelines in Cardiology26 using an automated oscillometric device (Omron Hem 705 CP, Illinois, USA). Participants were instructed to remain silent and use no electronic devices (i.e., smartphones or notebooks) during the measurement. An appropriately sized cuff was positioned approximately 2 cm above the antecubital fossa on the arm. Hemodynamic measurements were performed in both arms with a 1-min interval between measurements. The arm with the higher systolic blood pressure value was used in the evaluations during the study. The rate-pressure product was calculated using the systolic blood pressure and heart rate values (systolic blood pressure × heart rate).

During a subsequent visit, within 24–48 h following the initial visit, all participants were allowed to familiarize themselves with the sand court, beach tennis rules and basic techniques, and the RPE using the Borg CR10 scale27. The familiarization session lasted approximately 40 min and was composed of: (1) a brief explanation of beach tennis rules and scoring system (≈10 min); (2) technical practice of fundamental skills, such as racket grip instruction, forehand and backhand strokes, serves, volleys, and basic positioning and displacement on the sand court (≈20 min); and (3) a supervised short match simulation (≈5 min) to allow participants to experience the dynamic characteristics of the game, including accelerations, decelerations, and changes of direction. Additional familiarization sessions were provided, as needed, for participants requiring further practice to attain proficiency in playing the game. For the RPE familiarization, participants underwent a memory anchoring procedure, during which they were instructed to associate the numerical descriptors with corresponding physical sensations of effort. It was emphasized that ratings should reflect the overall sensation of effort, integrating both peripheral (muscular) and central (cardiorespiratory) signals, without being influenced by external factors or performance expectations. In addition, participants received instructions on the purpose of the enjoyment scale and were guided on how to rate their overall satisfaction with the session based on their immediate affective experience, ensuring a clear and consistent understanding of the response criteria prior to data collection.

Subsequently, each participant was categorized into one of three skill levels: beginner (poorly coordinated racket movements and little movement on the court), intermediate (more agile and coordinated movements), or advanced (broad dominance in all aspects of the game) in order to organize the schedule of beach tennis sessions by matching players of the same performance level, as ensuring optimal motivation and intensity levels during sports practice is imperative. According to our classification, the technical levels of the participants were as follows: the male group comprised 10 advanced, 14 intermediate, and 5 beginner players, while the female group consisted of 6 advanced, 13 intermediate, and 10 beginner participants.

Experimental sessions

The participants randomly performed two experimental sessions: beach tennis and control sessions. Participants were instructed to avoid any physical exercise for 24 h before the experimental sessions, maintain their usual dietary intake, and avoid alcohol, coffee, and other stimulant substances on the same day of the experimental session. Participants maintained their current antihypertensive medication regimen without changes in dosage or timing throughout the study period. The sessions started between 8:00 AM and 10:00 AM (at the same time of day to account for diurnal variations in blood pressure) and lasted approximately 2 h. The experimental session started with 20 min of rest before the protocols, 45 min of exercise or non-exercise control, and 45 min of passive recovery after that. The average interval between the experimental sessions was seven days to prevent any residual effects from one session to the next.

The beach tennis session started with a standardized 5-min warm-up consisting of basic techniques (i.e., serve, volley, forehand, and backhand) followed by three 12-min beach tennis matches with 2-min intervals between them. Regular beach tennis rules were used during the match, played by four participants (2 vs. 2) on an official beach tennis court (16 m long × 8 m wide with a net placed at 1.70 m height). In a preliminary session, an experienced researcher proficient in beach tennis was responsible for assessing the competition level of the participants to ensure that each participant matched players at a similar level. The researcher did not participate in any further assessments or data analyses.

During the beach tennis session, heart rate was continuously recorded using a heart rate monitor (Polar FT7, Finland), and the heart rate reserve was calculated using the following formula: the maximum heart rate was estimated using Tanaka’s equation (= 208–0.7 × age)28 minus the resting heart rate. The RPE was assessed using the CR-10 Borg Scale27. All participants were previously familiarized with the scale. We assessed enjoyment levels during the beach tennis session using the Physical Activity Enjoyment Scale (PACES)29. Each participant received a questionnaire and was asked to rate the level of enjoyment based on the following question: “How do you feel at the moment about the physical activity you have been doing?” The questionnaire consisted of 18 items rated on a 7-point bipolar rating scale. Eleven items were reverse-scored. Summing the individual item scores generated the overall PACES score. This yielded a possible range of 18 to 126, with higher PACES scores reflecting greater enjoyment.

A non-exercising control session was performed in a seated position at rest without engaging in any physical activity or using any electronic devices such as smartphones or notebooks throughout the session.

Assessments

The primary outcome of this study was the difference in the net effect of systolic blood pressure between men and women. Office hemodynamic assessments were performed before exercise protocols, and recovery at 15-min intervals for 45 min, according to the Brazilian guidelines of hypertension26.

To assess the interindividual variability of PEH, the net effect of each exercise session was calculated by the difference between blood pressure responses observed in the exercise and the control sessions as follows: PEH net effect = [(postexercise blood pressure − baseline blood pressure in the exercise session) − (post control blood pressure − baseline blood pressure in the control session)]. To categorize the participants as responders and nonresponders, the typical error (a measure of the variability or precision of repeated measurements) was calculated as follows: typical error = SDdifference/√2, where SDdifference is the standard deviation of the paired differences in blood pressure measured before the interventions in the exercise and control sessions30. Participants who presented with a PEH greater than a typical error were then classified as responders.

Due to the acute nature of the experimental sessions, complete blinding of the outcome assessors was not feasible, as visible physiological signs (e.g., diaphoresis and elevated heart rate) could indicate the prior condition (exercise vs. control). To mitigate potential bias, independent assessors who were not involved in the intervention sessions conducted the measurements. These assessors remained blinded to the study’s specific hypotheses. Furthermore, all data were anonymized prior to analysis, which was performed by a researcher blinded to the experimental conditions.

Statistical analyses

The sample size was estimated based on the results of a previous study with a similar design, which compared men and women, with men showing higher reductions in systolic blood pressure (– 7 mmHg) than women23. The sample size of 58 adults with hypertension (29 in each group) was calculated to test whether there was a minimum difference from the aforementioned study, accounting for a dropout rate of up to 10%. The calculation considered a power of 80%, a significance level of 5%, and a standard deviation of the difference of 9 mmHg. This calculation was performed using the PSS Health online tool31.

Two independent researchers entered the data in duplicates to prevent typing errors, and the amplitude and consistency of the data were checked. For the analysis, the dataset was coded and anonymized so that the researcher responsible for the analyses did not have access to the identification of the intervention conditions. The normality assumption was assessed using the Shapiro‒Wilk test, visual inspection of histograms, and Q‒Q plots. The results were expressed as the means, standard errors, and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Sex differences were compared for clinical characteristics using a t-test for independent samples and a chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test to verify the associations between antihypertensive medication and ethnicity. In hemodynamic data, average total 45’ was calculated using the average of the 3 values measured after the sessions (post 15’ + post 30’ + post 45’/3). A generalized estimating equation (GEE) analysis was used to compare the main effects between the interventions (beach tennis and control sessions) and groups, assessing the conditions (two groups: men and women) by time (time points). Post hoc comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni correction. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, and trend-toward interactions were set at P < 0.10. All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows version 22.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, United States).

Discussion

Our research confirmed that both men and women experienced PEH after playing beach tennis. Notably, men experienced greater reductions than women, and a higher proportion of men were classified as responders after a beach tennis session. In accordance with our hypothesis and aligning with previous research on this topic that used traditional exercise and had small sample sizes21,23, these results highlight the importance of considering sex-specific responses when prescribing exercises for blood pressure control.

In a previous study of older adults with hypertension23, men demonstrated greater PEH than women after a power training session (men: Δ −14 mmHg; women: Δ −7 mmHg). Our findings support these sex differences in PEH and demonstrate significant reductions in blood pressure 30 min after the conclusion of beach tennis practice (men: Δ −20 mmHg; women: Δ −9 mmHg). Given that PEH at the 30-min mark has been linked to long-term reductions in blood pressure and that a 5 mmHg decrease in systolic blood pressure decreases the risk of cardiovascular events by 10%32, beach tennis emerges as a promising non-pharmacological strategy for blood pressure management in both men and women. However, women may require higher exercise intensities or volumes to achieve blood pressure reductions comparable to those observed in men.

Although our study was not designed to investigate underlying mechanisms, PEH is influenced by a complex interaction of physiological and pharmacological factors, with important sex-specific differences33–35. The greater blood pressure reduction in men may be partially explained by a more pronounced sympathetic withdrawal34, whereas the sustained elevation in heart rate in both sexes indicates that thermoregulatory and metabolic demands may override reflex bradycardia during recovery36,37. In women, our exploratory observations suggest that medication class may have influenced the PEH response. Specifically, β-blockers may have facilitated greater blood pressure reductions by limiting compensatory increases in cardiac output38, while the absence of calcium channel blockers may have allowed for a greater exercise-induced vasodilatory reserve, contributing to the more pronounced fall in diastolic blood pressure observed in responders39. However, as mechanistic pathways were not directly measured and medication subgroups were limited in size, these exploratory observations were not the primary focus of this study and must be interpreted with caution as speculative, rather than definitive, physiological interpretations.

Beach tennis is a sport that involves brief intermittent bursts of rapid accelerations and decelerations, frequent changes in direction, and jumps on an unstable sandy surface, which requires quick reactions from players to consistently strike the ball before it comes in contacts with the ground12. In the present study, no significant difference in heart rate was detected between men and women during the beach tennis session. Similarly, no significant differences in RPE or enjoyment level were observed between men and women during beach tennis. This behavior has also been observed in other recreational sports such as netball games and handball24,40, where males and females exhibit similar physiological demands and perceptual responses. However, we acknowledge that physiological responses to exercise are multifactorial41 and influenced by other differences that may not be fully captured by internal load markers such as heart rate and RPE42. In intermittent sports like beach tennis, men often achieve higher absolute work rates and mechanical power outputs compared to women, even when relative internal loads appear similar. Consequently, men may experience a more potent vascular stimulus and greater shear stress during the session, potentially leading to the more pronounced PEH observed in our study43. Future research incorporating objective measures of external load (i.e., accelerometry or GPS-based tracking) is needed to further elucidate how these potential differences influence sex-specific responses.

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. The underlying physiological mechanisms of PEH were not directly assessed, which limits our ability to fully explain the observed sex-specific differences. While the magnitude of PEH suggests a potential for cardiovascular risk reduction, blood pressure monitoring was limited to 45 min after sessions. The absence of 24-h ambulatory blood pressure monitoring precludes conclusions regarding the duration of PEH. Another potential limitation is the absence of control for menstrual cycle phase, menopausal status, and contraceptive use among women participants, factors that could potentially influence vascular function and blood pressure responses. However, a recent meta-analysis44 reported no significant differences in exercise-induced blood pressure responses across menstrual cycle phases, menopausal status, or contraceptive use. Additionally, although participants maintained their usual antihypertensive medication regimen throughout the study and all sessions were conducted at the same time of day to minimize circadian influences, the exact timing of medication intake relative to the experimental sessions may have influenced the acute hemodynamic responses. To minimize bias, assessors were not informed of the study hypotheses and data was analyzed using a coded, anonymized set. However, full blinding was not possible due to the acute design of the sessions, which may be a potential limitation. Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths that enhance the reliability and validity of its findings. First, sample size calculations were meticulously performed to ensure adequate statistical power, minimize the risk of Type II errors, and allow for robust detection of clinically significant differences. Additionally, the study design included randomization and allocation concealment to reduce bias and ensure an objective outcomes assessment. We also employed standardized protocols and validated measurement tools to enhance the consistency and accuracy of the data collected. Moreover, the proposed beach tennis protocol represents a pragmatic intervention involving matches with four participants per game, strictly adhering strictly to the official rules of the sport. The research team refrained from regulating the game intensity, allowing participants to move freely and naturally during matches. This flexible approach captures the essence of recreational sports, where players have the autonomy to adjust their pace and intensity according to their individual preferences and abilities. This method not only reflects real-world playing conditions but also ensures that the training remains enjoyable and accessible to all participants, thereby promoting sustained engagement and physical activity.

Conclusion

In adults with controlled hypertension, a single recreational beach tennis session acutely reduced blood pressure in both men and women, with a more pronounced effect observed in men. Tailored beach tennis protocols may be necessary to elicit optimal benefits for women.
