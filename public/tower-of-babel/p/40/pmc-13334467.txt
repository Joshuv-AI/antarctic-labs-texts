Biomedical potential of bovine-derived hydroxyapatite: synthesis strategies, characterization and applications
Authors: Nahid Sultana, Mofassel Hossen Akash, Bidowra Moshin Mrittika, Hrishita Das, Kanij Fatema, Meher Nigar Maisha, Md. Joynal Abedin Muntasir, Mohammad Alamgir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydroxyapatite (HAp), a calcium phosphate material that closely resembles the mineral phase of natural bone, is widely studied due to its biocompatibility, bioactivity, and osteoconductivity, making it an important biomaterial in biomedical applications. With increasing demand for HAp-based materials, considerable research has focused on developing synthesis methods to tailor its structural and functional properties. Variations in synthesis strategies can significantly influence crystallinity, particle size, porosity, and surface characteristics of HAp, which in turn affect biological performance. Among biogenic sources, bovine-derived HAp has received attention due to its compositional similarity to natural bone mineral. Several studies report that, depending on extraction and processing conditions, bovine HAp can exhibit favorable crystallinity, mechanical stability, and porous architecture; however, these properties remain highly dependent on preparation parameters and require careful control. This review summarizes the synthesis of bovine HAp using conventional methods (thermal decomposition and hydrothermal techniques), hybrid approaches (calcination combined with vibro-milling and alkaline heat treatment, as well as ultrasonic-assisted spray drying), and emerging techniques such as subcritical water processing, transferred arc plasma, and annealing, each offering varying degrees of control over physicochemical properties. Characterization techniques, including X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning and transmission electron microscopy (SEM, TEM), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), are discussed for evaluating crystallinity, morphology, and elemental composition respectively. Furthermore, the biomedical applications of bovine-derived HAp are reviewed, including bone tissue engineering, bone grafting, dental repair, drug delivery, bioactive coatings, and antibacterial activity. While bovine-derived HAp shows promise as a sustainable and multifunctional biomaterial, further studies are required to address challenges related to processing consistency, long-term performance, and clinical translation.

Introduction

HAp is a calcium phosphate-based material with chemical similarity to the mineral phase of natural bone and teeth, and is widely recognized for its biocompatibility and bioactivity.1 Despite significant progress in biomaterials research, challenges remain in repairing or replacing damaged bone and dental tissues, particularly due to limitations in the mechanical performance & physiological properties and long-term stability of available synthetic materials. In this context, HAp-based biomaterials have been extensively investigated for their potential in tissue repair and regeneration. The rising global demand for HAp in biomedical applications, coupled with the push for sustainable and cost-effective biomaterials, has intensified research into its production from alternative sources through diverse synthesis routes.

Hydroxyapatite (Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2) crystallizes in a hexagonal system within the P63/m space group, defined by lattice parameters a = b ≠ c, with α = β = 90° and γ = 120°. Its structure consists of a stable three-dimensional framework composed of Ca2+ ions, PO43− tetrahedral, and OH− ions, the latter positioned in channels aligned along the c-axis. This lattice is highly accommodating to ionic substitutions, where Ca2+ can be substituted by cations such as Na+, Mg2+, Sr2+, or Zn2+; PO43− groups may be partially replaced by anions like CO32−, SO42−, or SiO44− and OH− ions within the channels can be exchanged with F− or Cl−.2 These substitutions can influence crystallinity, lattice parameters, morphology, and structural stability, thereby affecting the physicochemical and biological properties of HAp. Human bones and teeth are made up of calcium (∼24 wt%), phosphorus (∼10 wt%), proteins (∼22 wt%), and trace elements such as Na+, Zn2+, Mg2+, K+, Si2+, Ba2+, F−, Cl−, SO42−, CO32−, etc.3 Researchers have been trying to find out various processes for incorporation of these necessary ions into HAp to gain maximum similarity with the natural bones mineral composition while enhancing its functional properties.4

HAp is generally considered stable in physiological environments and has been reported to integrate with bone tissue with minimal adverse response such as toxicity, inflammation, or foreign body reactions.5,6 HAp possesses several biologically relevant features, including bioresorbability, osteoconductivity, biocompatibility, and ion-exchange capacity, which have been widely reported in the literature.1,7 Owing to these properties, HAp is widely utilized in biomedical applications such as bone tissue engineering,8 bone grafting,9 and dental repair.10 In addition, growing attention has been devoted to synthesizing HAp for advanced applications in drug delivery,11 bioactive coatings,12 and antibacterial materials.13

Among the various natural sources of HAp, bovine bone has been extensively studied due to its availability and compositional similarity to human bone.6 Its Ca/P ratio is typically close to the stoichiometric value (∼1.67), although slight deviations may occur due to the presence of trace elements.14,15 In contrast, HAp derived from chicken bone may be unsuitable for biomedical applications due to its phase instability, particularly at elevated temperatures, which can formation of undesirable phases such as β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP). In addition, it often exhibits inferior mechanical properties, including reduced hardness and toughness, as well as lower mineral density, making it less durable compared to bovine bone-derived HAp.16 Additionally, fish bone often yields carbonate-rich apatite with lower crystallinity and weaker mechanical properties.17 Furthermore, seashell species (mollusk, snail, oyster), being composed mainly of calcium carbonate, demand additional chemical conversion & thermal treatment steps before HAp formation.18 Seashells species and fish bones are prone to bio-accumulating hazardous substances, particularly heavy metals, which are incorporated into their tissues and skeletal structures rather than remaining on the surface. These contaminants are difficult to eliminate through conventional purification methods, raising concerns about their persistence.19,20 Consequently, the synthesis of HAp from seashells and fish bones may present potential health risks due to possible contamination. On the other hand, compared to other mammalian sources such as camels, horses, and porcines, bovine bones are more frequently studied for HAp extraction due to their availability and favorable biological composition and morphology.21

Bovine bone is abundantly available as a by-product of the meat industry, providing a potentially sustainable and low-cost source for HAp production. It is estimated that approximately 300 million bovines are slaughtered annually for human consumption worldwide, which generates 18–23 million metric tons of animal bone waste as cattle contain 14–17% bone, through various sources such as slaughterhouses, domestic use, restaurants, and so on. This massive volume of bone waste is often neglected or thrown in landfills and waterbody, which may spread of infectious diseases, foul odor emissions, and potential environmental contamination.22,23 The bone waste from bovine sources such as (cattle, buffalo, bison, yaks bones etc.) from meat industries can be utilized as a sustainable and low-cost source of HAp. It facilitates bone waste reduction and recycling, which is environmentally friendly and economically profitable.24–26 Synthetic HAp has a stoichiometric Ca/P ratio of 1.67, whereas HAp isolated from bovine bone is slightly deviated ratio due to trace ions such as Mg2+ Na+, Sr2+, Zn2+.3,27–29 These trace elements play major role in bone metabolism, mineralization and healing which are difficult to replicate in synthetically produced HAp.30–32 Natural sources such as mammalian bones,33–35 fish bones,36 biogenic sources37,38 are utilized for the synthesis of biologically favored HAp. HAp from bovine bone are proven better for biomedical application, most importantly, bone regeneration because they contain essential cations & anions and similar mineral phase to human bone.39 Studies has been reported that bovine-derived HAp offers higher crystallinity, superior mechanical strength, and a porous microstructure that supports nutrient transport and vascularization under specific conditions. A calcination temperature of 1000 °C has proven to be the best temperature for the isolation of the best quality HAp from the bovine bone due to its highest degree of crystallinity (86–87%) and Ca/P ratio (1.66).40,41

At the nanoscale, HAp exhibits increased surface area and reactivity, which can influence biological interactions. Nano-sized HAp has been reported to enhance protein adsorption, cell adhesion, and osteoblast activity compared to bulk materials.42 These nanoscale attributes directly influence key biological responses, including protein adsorption, cell adhesion, osteoblast proliferation, osteoconductivity, and resorption kinetics.43 Therefore, the extraction route whether calcination, hydrothermal processing, or subcritical water treatment, plays a decisive role in controlling structural characteristics such as crystallite size, lattice strain, carbonate retention, specific surface area, surface chemistry, and trace ionic substitution profiles. For instance, higher calcination temperatures may improve crystallinity but reduce surface area and carbonate content, thereby affecting bioactivity.44 Therefore, understanding the relationship between synthesis method, nanoscale structure, and biological performance is essential for the rational design of HAp-based biomaterials. This review therefore aims to provide an overview how structural characteristics of HAp dependent on synthesis strategies.

This review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of bovine-derived HAp, focusing on synthesis strategies, including thermal decomposition,45 hydrothermal process,46 subcritical water process,47 transferred arc plasma,48 annealing,49 precipitation,50 and hybrid approaches.51 Despite numerous synthesis methods having been devised, the synthesis of HAp with specific features remains hard because of the likelihood of formation of harmful intermediate products due to the synthesis condition.6 Thus, particular emphasis is placed on how synthesis conditions influence key physicochemical properties such as Ca/P ratio, crystallinity, particle size, morphology, and phase composition, and how these factors relate to biomedical applications. HAp synthesis methods from bovine bone

Thermal decomposition

Thermal decomposition is one of the most widely applied methods for extracting HAp from bovine bone, particularly from femur bones. Prior to thermal decomposition, raw bones undergo cleaning to remove debris, fats, marrow, and soft tissues. According to some previous research, pre-treatment techniques include boiling for extended periods followed by jet-water washing and air-drying to remove macroscopic adhering meat,45,52 then dried bone soaking in ethanol or chloroform to eliminate residual soft tissues, and treating with acetone to remove fats and collagen. The cleaned bones are then dried, cut into small pieces, rinsed with deionized water, and subjected to thermal treatment at various temperatures for different periods of time.53 In some studies, the bones were crushed after calcination, whereas in others, crushing was performed before calcination to obtain fine HAp powder (Fig. 2).54–56

Flow chart of extraction of HAp from bovine animal bone via thermal decomposition.

The calcination conditions strongly influence the properties of the resulting HAp. For instance, bovine femur bone calcined for 2 h at varying temperatures showed a reduction in the Ca/P ratio from 2.425 to 1.6 as the temperature increased. As EDX confirmed the presence of elemental composition of materials, so that trace elements such as Mg, Na, Cl, K, Si, and O in the materials easily confirm by the EDX. The SEM images revealing the morphology and particle size of the HAp particles. The particles size ranged between 10 and 150 nm and morpholology of the HAp particles apperas at needle-like and polygonal morphologies.57 On the other hand, XRD analysis indicated the crystal size, peak sharpness and crystallinity. XRD analysis of the previous study showed that, peak sharpness and crystallinity of the HAp particles increased with temperature and the best-defined peaks observed at 1250 °C.58 However, excessive heating did not always enhance crystallinity, as prolonged heat treatment could lead to phase transformation into β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP). Similar findings were reported that, when bovine bone was thermal process at 200–1000 °C for 5 h, the crystallinity of the HAp increases. The crystallinity reached 89.8% at 1000 °C, while the largest crystallite size was observed at 900 °C, with crystal diameters around 800 nm. The Ca/P ratios determined by EDX were 1.810, 1.696, and 1.911 at 700, 850, and 900 °C, respectively. Another investigation using thermal decomposition at 700–1000 °C for 3 h revealed the highest crystallinity at 900 °C. Increasing the heating temperature produced denser crystals with reduced porosity, although the Ca/P ratios (1.79–2.31) were higher than the stoichiometric value due to the presence of CaO or CaCO3 as secondary phases.10 Importantly, HAp stability depends on both temperature and duration. Short calcination (≤3 h) at 1000 °C generally maintains the HAp phase, while extended calcination (≥6 h) at lower temperatures (850 °C) can induce partial conversion into β-TCP, as confirmed by XRD analyses.59

Comparisons with other animal sources have shown differences in particle morphology and crystallite size. For example, camel bone calcined at 1000 °C for 3 h yielded larger crystallites (79–900 nm) compared to bovine bone (∼30 nm). XRD confirmed that bovine-derived HAp had smaller crystallites with 80.4% crystallinity, which is advantageous for biomedical use. Morphological differences were also observed: camel bone produced irregularly shaped Hap (Fig. 3a), while bovine bone produced agglomerated flakes with a porous structure (Fig. 3b). Interestingly, the Ca/P ratio of camel-derived HAp (1.6557) was close to the stoichiometric value, while bovine-derived HAp retained desirable the Ca/P ratio.52,60 In another study, powdered bovine bone heated at 750 °C for 6 h produced nanorod-shaped HAp particles with an average length of 300 nm, a Ca/P ratio of 1.65, and an extraction yield of approximately 66%.47 These results demonstrate that thermal decomposition of bovine bone can yield highly crystalline & nanostructured HAp with tunable physical properties depending on the range of temperature and duration of calcination (Table 1).

Hydrothermal process

Alkaline hydrothermal hydrolysis process

The alkaline hydrothermal hydrolysis process is a promising approach for extracting natural HAp bio-ceramic from bovine bone. This method exploits the ability of hydrothermal conditions to hydrolyze collagen and collagen-like proteins, such as keratin, in either an autoclave or under microwave heating. Since collagen and keratin share fibrous structures, the process effectively removes organic fractions while preserving the mineral component.71,72 In this method, bovine bones are first washed with water and acetone to eliminate fats and impurities, followed by drying at 160 °C for 48 h. The dried bones are then ground into fine particles (<450 µm). For the hydrolysis step, sodium hydroxide (96.5% purity) is used, with the bone powder mixed into a NaOH solution at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1 : 50. The mixture is placed in a stainless-steel hydrothermal reactor, sealed, and heated in a silicon oil bath. Typical reaction conditions involve 25 wt% NaOH at 250 °C for 5 h (Fig. 4).15

Flowchart of the extraction of HAp powder by the alkaline hydrothermal hydrolysis process.

The product obtained under these conditions exhibited a hexagonal crystalline phase of HAp, as confirmed by XRD, with an average crystallite size of 12.13 nm.73 FTIR spectra showed the presence of hydroxyl (OH−), phosphate (PO43−), and carbonate (CO32−) groups, confirming the formation of carbonated HAp. TEM and SEM analyses revealed a nanorod-like morphology, while FESEM provided detailed nanoscale structural imaging. TGA confirmed excellent thermal stability up to 700 °C, and EDX analysis indicated a Ca/P molar ratio of 1.86, close to stoichiometric HAp.15 These results collectively confirmed that alkaline hydrothermal hydrolysis effectively removes organic components while preserving the carbonate content inherent in biological apatite. Unlike thermal decomposition, which generally eliminates carbonate groups, this method yields carbonated hydroxyapatite, a composition considered highly desirable for biomedical applications due to its closer resemblance to natural bone mineral.

Oxidative hydrothermal treatment

Oxidative hydrothermal treatment (OHT) is an innovative process developed to recycle HAp from bovine bone while minimizing residual organic content. Unlike conventional hydrothermal or calcination methods, OHT incorporates molecular oxygen (O2) into the hydrothermal system, which enhances the hydrolysis and dissolution of organic matter within the bone matrix. This results in cleaner HAp with fewer organic residues.74 In this method, bovine bones collected from slaughterhouses are first prepared by manually scraping out the marrow, boiling in water for 1 h to remove soft tissue, and drying at room temperature for 24 h. The bones are then dehydrated in an oven at 120 °C for 2 h, crushed using a bone grinder, and loaded into a stainless-steel hydrothermal reactor with Milli-Q water. Prior to heating, the reactor is flushed with O2 or N2 gas (99.5% purity) for 15 min and homogenized using a gas impeller at 750 rpm. Hydrothermal reactions are carried out at 220, 250, or 280 °C for 0–2 h under controlled pressure. After treatment, the suspension is filtered, washed with 500 mL of Milli-Q water (3 times), and dried to obtain purified HAp powder (Fig. 5).

Flowchart of the extraction of HAp by the oxidative hydrothermal treatment.

The particle size of the final HAp can be tailored by post-milling, where aqueous suspension prepared by 30 wt% solid loading. Two conditions are applied depending on the targeted particle size: micron-sized particles are obtained with 1.0 mm zirconia beads, whereas submicron particles are produced with 0.3 mm beads. Structural analyses confirmed that OHT yields nanostructured HAp (HAp-OH). XRD revealed crystallite sizes of ∼20 nm, while SEM showed that OHT-derived grains are finer than those produced by thermal decomposition, where thermal treatment typically promotes grain growth. Morphologically, OHT generates nanoflakes, in contrast to the nanorods commonly obtained from thermal decomposition. One of the most significant advantages of OHT is its ability to produce HAp with a remarkably high specific surface area. Measured values range from 70.3–75.4 m2 g−1 for millimeter-scale particles and up to 164 m2 g−1 for nanoparticles, surpassing both natural calcined HAp and some synthetic nanometric powders.74 Since surface area is inversely proportional to particle size, the nanostructures obtained by OHT provide enhanced reactivity and potential biological performance, making this method highly attractive for biomedical applications (Table 2).

Advanced processes

Subcritical water process

Subcritical water (SCW), also known as pressurized low polarity water (PLPW), refers to liquid water maintained under high pressure at temperatures between its boiling point (100 °C) and critical point (374 °C, 22.1 MPa). Under these conditions, the dielectric constant, viscosity, and surface tension of water decrease, while diffusivity increases, giving it solvent properties similar to organic solvents. This enables SCW to dissolve a wide range of moderately polar and low-polarity compounds.91 Such characteristics make it highly effective for extracting organic components like collagen from bovine bone, leaving behind natural HAp. Fig. 6 represents the extraction of HAp from bovine bone by subcritical water process.

Flow chart represents the extraction of HAp from bovine bone by subcritical water process.

In this method, bovine bones are first cleaned with water and acetone to remove lipids and impurities, dried, and ground to <450 µm particle size. The powdered bone is then loaded into a Teflon crucible inside a cylindrical stainless-steel autoclave (6 cm inner diameter, 7.4 cm outer diameter, 15 cm height) with deionized water at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1 : 40. Nitrogen gas is flushed into the system to remove oxygen, and the sealed autoclave is heated in a silicon oil bath at 275 °C for 1 h, followed by rapid cooling in cold water. The resulting mixture is filtered, rinsed with distilled water, and dried at 80 °C for 30 min.47

Characterization confirms the efficiency of this process. XRD patterns show that fibrous collagen in raw bone causes peak dispersion, but after SCW treatment, the recovered HAp exhibits distinct and sharp diffraction peaks, comparable to standard crystalline Hap (Fig. 11a). TEM analyses indicated the particle size of highly crystalline HAp nanoparticles. EDX confirms the elemental composition of Ca and P with Ca/P molar ratio of 1.56, consistent with biological hydroxyapatite.92 SEM and FESEM reveal rapid collagen dissolution within 5 min, altering the bone's microstructure and yielding nanoflakes with an average particle size in the nanoscale range. At 275 °C and 1 h holding time, the process produces ∼69% yield of HAp nanoparticles (Fig. 10a). Importantly, SCW extraction preserves carbonate substitution within the apatite lattice, yielding carbonated HAp, which closely resembles biological HAp and is highly desirable for biomedical applications.47 While SCW processing enables efficient collagen removal under relatively moderate temperatures and preserves carbonate substitution within the apatite lattice, it should not be interpreted as inherently superior to high-temperature calcination. SCW may better retain nanoscale morphology and bio-relevant carbonate content, but high-temperature calcination provides more definitive elimination of organic residues and potential pathogens. Therefore, the selection between SCW and calcination should be guided by the intended application, balancing nanoscale structural preservation with biosafety requirements and regulatory considerations.

Transferred arc plasma process

Plasma processing has gained wide attention for synthesizing advanced materials due to its ability to achieve extremely high temperatures and rapid phase transformations.93 In the transferred arc plasma (TAP) torch, an electric arc is generated between a cathode and a work-piece, with an auxiliary arc briefly established between the electrode and the nozzle to initiate the process.94 This technique involves melting raw bovine bone in a plasma environment, followed by crystallization to form HAp or HAp-based composites.

In this method, bovine bones are first deproteinized for 1 h using reagent-grade NaOH, then cut into pieces (2 × 1 cm) and introduced into the plasma arc zone. Processing is performed in argon plasma using a 5 kW power input, with treatment times varied from 45 to 90 s. A DC TAP torch with a maximum output of 10 kW is employed, with a 25 mm electrode gap. The plasma gas flow rate and arc current are adjusted to maintain stable operating conditions. Within the plasma zone, temperatures reach ∼2980 °C, ensuring complete removal of organic matter. Although plasma systems can generate extremely high localized temperatures within the plasma zone, the HAp particles do not typically remain at these temperatures long enough to undergo complete thermal decomposition. The residence time of particles in the high-temperature plasma core is extremely short, followed by rapid cooling or quenching in either water or air once the particles exit the plasma region, leading to solidification of HAp nanoparticles. The resulting bulk material is subsequently sintered and sieved to obtain powders of desired sizes95,96 (Fig. 7).

Flow chart of extraction of HAp from bovine bone by transferred arc plasma process.

XRD analysis (Fig. 11d) revealed that prolonged plasma exposure progressively increased HAp peak intensity and reduced peak width, indicating higher crystallinity. Samples treated for 45–90 s displayed well-defined HAp peaks, while extended processing at 120 s resulted in secondary phases, including β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP), tetra-calcium phosphate (TTCP), and CaO, due to thermal decomposition of HAp, which are shown in the following equation:1Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2 ⇌ 2β Ca3(PO4)2 + Ca4P2O9 + ↑H2O2Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2 ⇌ 3β Ca3(PO4)2 + CaO + ↑H2O

EDX analysis showed that the Ca/P ratio increased with processing time: 1.82 (60 s) and 1.93 (90 s), both slightly above the stoichiometric value (1.67). At 120 s, however, the Ca/P ratio increased drastically to 4.73, indicating phase instability, resulting in the formation of TCP, TTCP, and CaO phases. Thus, a plasma processing time of 90 s was identified as optimal for producing stable HAp with high crystallinity. SEM images confirmed that TAP-derived HAp appeared as bulky structures with uneven surfaces (Fig. 10b), which could be subsequently milled and sieved into fine powders.95 Overall, TAP processing provides a rapid, high-purity synthesis route for crystalline HAp, where crystallinity and stoichiometry strongly influenced by plasma power, treatment duration feed rate, and quenching conditions.

Annealing

The annealing process plays a crucial role in the extraction and preparation of HAp from bovine bone, as it enhances crystallinity, purity, and thermal stability.97 This process involves heating the bone material to a specific temperature, maintaining it for a defined duration, and then gradually cooling it down. Higher annealing temperatures are more effective in completely eliminating collagen and other organic components, while slow cooling prevents the formation of undesirable secondary phases and ensures the stability & purity of crystalline HAp. Rapid cooling, in contrast, may lead to the development of amorphous or metastable phases.98

For this method, bovine bones are obtained from slaughterhouses and cleaned to remove visible tissues and impurities. The cleaning process typically involves boiling the bones in water for several hours to eliminate organic matter, followed by defatting with solvents such as acetone. The bones are then cut into rectangular blocks (10 × 5 × 5 mm) and deproteinized using alkaline treatment.99,100 After thorough rinsing and drying, the bone samples are annealed in an electric furnace at temperatures ranging from 400 °C to 1200 °C, with a heating/cooling rate of 5 °C min−1 and a holding time of 2 h at the target temperature. After annealing, the furnace is gradually cooled to room temperature at the same rate to stabilize the HAp phase. Finally, the material is ground and sieved to obtain powders of the desired size (Fig. 8).

Flow chart of extraction of HAp from bovine bone by annealing process.

Raw bone appears at yellowish or brown due to presence of organic matter. After annealing, the color of the bone samples changes depending on temperature: black at 400 °C, dark grey at 500 °C, and light grey at 600 °C. Samples annealed below 700 °C retain deeper colors, reflecting incomplete removal of organics. But the annealing temperature at 1100–1200 °C, the bone appears at white to bright white due to complete removal of organic matters. XRD analysis (Fig. 11c) shows that bovine bone annealed between 600 °C and 1000 °C yields pure HAp with no detectable secondary phases, while crystallinity increases with temperature. However, at 1100 °C and 1200 °C, minor peaks corresponding to β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) appear due to partial decomposition of HAp (eqn (2)). EDX results reveal that the Ca/P ratio increases slightly from 2.23 to 2.31 at 400 °C, but decreases to ∼1.85 after annealing at 1200 °C, possibly with the partial breakdown of HAp into β-TCP. SEM analysis (Fig. 10c) of samples annealed above 800 °C demonstrates a porous architecture formed by the removal of organics, while preserving the spongy, interconnected structure of natural bone. Such porosity is beneficial for bone ingrowth and osseous integration, making annealed bovine-derived HAp a promising and cost-effective material for clinical bone graft applications.49

Precipitation

This process involves chemical reactions between inorganic oxides in solution to form HAp powders. Initially, bovine bones are cut into small pieces, thoroughly washed, and boiled for 1 h to remove soft tissues and marrow. The bones are then rinsed with distilled water, deproteinized by soaking in NaOH for 24 h, and dried. Defatting is carried out by acetone treatment, followed by drying. The cleaned bones are subsequently sintered at 1000 °C for 4 h to remove remaining organic matter. The sintered bones are crushed into fine powders, yielding calcium oxide (CaO) as a precursor.26 The precipitation process begins with the preparation of a calcium hydroxide suspension, which is vigorously stirred, degassed, and heated for 1 h. Orthophosphoric acid is then added dropwise (1–2 drops per s) at 40 °C to induce the formation of a gelatinous precipitate, following the reaction:310Ca(OH)2 + 6H3PO4 → Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2 + 18H2O

To achieve stoichiometric HAp, the pH of the suspension is maintained above 10 using ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH). The precipitate is left in the mother solution overnight, after which the supernatant is decanted. The solid phase is separated either by centrifugation or paper filtration, rinsed several times with distilled water, and dried overnight at 80 °C. The resulting powders are further heat-treated at 650 °C, 850 °C, and 1050 °C for 7 h to enhance crystallinity (Fig. 9).101

Flow chart of extraction of HAp from bovine bone by precipitation process.

SEM analysis (Fig. 10d) revealed incomplete agglomeration of HAp particles at 650 °C due to their high surface area, while sintering at 850 °C and 1050 °C reduced agglomeration, leading to more compact structures. EDX analysis confirmed Ca/P molar ratio close to the stoichiometric value (≈1.67) at all temperatures. XRD patterns (Fig. 11b) matched those of commercial HAp, confirming phase purity (Fig. 12).101 Overall, precipitation provides a straightforward route to obtain stoichiometric, phase-pure HAp from bovine bone waste, with tunable particle morphology depending on sintering conditions (Table 3).

Hybrid processes

Calcination and vibro-milling process

A combined technique of calcination and vibro-milling has been reported for the extraction of HAp from bovine bone. After pretreatment, deproteinized bovine bone was first calcined at 800 °C for 3 h to eliminate organic matter and improve crystallinity. The calcined powders were then subjected to milling treatments, including conventional ball milling (24 h) followed by vibro-milling, in which ethanol was used as the milling medium. The vibro-milling duration was varied between 0 h and 8 h to investigate its effect on HAp morphology. This combined approach yielded nano-crystalline, needle-like HAp particles with diameters below 100 nm (58–63 nm). The Ca/P ratio of the prepared powder was found to be 1.66, consistent with the theoretical value for stoichiometric HAp, confirming the purity of the product. Results indicated that extending vibro-milling time increases the distribution density of needle-shaped HAp. However, milling durations longer than 8 h caused excessive fragmentation of nanocrystals into finer particles, resulting in inhomogeneous crystal sizes and irregular morphologies (Fig. 14). XRD analysis of samples processed at different vibro-milling times confirmed the formation of pure HAp, as all diffraction peaks matched the standard JCPDS file no. 9-432 (Fig. 13). SEM micrographs further demonstrated that well-defined, uniformly distributed needle-like structures (<100 nm diameter) were obtained after more than 2 h of vibro-milling. With extended durations beyond 8 h, however, particle size and shape became increasingly heterogeneous. Thus, an optimal vibro-milling duration of 2–4 h was recommended, as it provided a good balance between particle uniformity and nanoneedle distribution while maintaining structural integrity.6,51

Calcination and alkaline heat treatment

A combined alkaline heat treatment and calcination process has also been employed for extracting HAp from bovine bone.104,105 In this method, bone samples are first cleaned and treated with 4 M NaOH at 100 °C for 24 h. The alkaline treatment is repeated with fresh NaOH solution, and the samples are thoroughly washed until the filtrate reaches neutral pH. After drying, the treated bones are calcined at temperatures ranging from 800 °C to 1200 °C.6,41 Studies reported that pure HAp is obtained at 800 °C, whereas at 1200 °C, secondary CaO phases appear along with HAp. Interestingly, traces of CaO have also been observed in some cases when the calcination temperature exceeds 700 °C. The process yields HAp nanocrystals with particle sizes in the range of 70–180 nm (Fig. 15).52 The morphology and Ca/P ratios of the resulting HAp varied among studies. Some reports describe irregularly shaped nanocrystals with a Ca/P ratio of 1.70, whereas others observed plate-like nanocrystals with a slightly higher Ca/P ratio of 1.72. Both values are above the stoichiometric Ca/P ratio (1.67), attributed to the presence of trace CaO in the calcined samples.105,106

Ultrasonic-assisted spray drying

This process, a combination of three techniques: mechanical processing, ultrasonication, and spray drying, was employed to produce homogeneous HAp. In this method, pretreated bovine bones were first dried, ground into cube-like forms, and allowed to undergo spontaneous drying at room temperature until their color turned yellowish-white. The dried bones were further pulverized using a mortar and pestle, followed by calcination at 800 °C for 3 h in an electric furnace (Fig. 16). After calcination, the bones were cooled and milled using a ball mill for 24 h to achieve the desired particle size. For ultrasonic processing, 8 g of the ball-milled sample was dispersed in a sonication medium (aquabides or ethanol) at a concentration of 12%. The samples were then subjected to ultra-sonication at a controlled amplitude. The sonicated suspensions were subsequently processed by spray drying to obtain fine HAp powders.107

Interpretation and limitations of characterization techniques for HAp

Beyond simply identifying the presence of HAp, it is important to consider the specific information and limitations associated with commonly used characterization techniques. XRD is widely employed to determine phase composition and crystallinity of HAp. The crystallite size estimated using the Scherrer equation represents the size of coherent diffraction domains rather than the actual particle size. Therefore, crystallite sizes obtained from XRD may differ significantly from particle sizes observed by microscopy techniques such as SEM or TEM, particularly when particles consist of multiple crystalline domains. In systems where secondary phases such as TCP or calcium oxide are present, more rigorous analysis methods such as Rietveld refinement are recommended to enable quantitative phase identification. FTIR is commonly used to evaluate functional groups and detect carbonate substitution within the HAp lattice. However, FTIR alone may provide only qualitative evidence of carbonate incorporation. Complementary analytical approaches such as TGA, elemental analysis, or Raman spectroscopy can provide more quantitative insights into carbonate content and lattice substitution.

Surface-related properties are also particularly important for nanoscale HAp. Specific surface area, commonly measured by BET analysis, strongly influences adsorption behavior, ion exchange capacity, and drug loading efficiency. Similarly, zeta potential measurements provide insight into surface charge and colloidal stability, which are critical factors governing protein adsorption, cellular interactions, and dispersion behavior in biological environments. Therefore, combining structural, compositional, and surface characterization techniques is essential to obtain a comprehensive understanding of the physicochemical properties of nanoscale bovine-derived HAp and their implications for biomedical applications.

Biomedical applications of HAp

Bone tissue engineering

HAp is widely investigated as a biomaterial for bone tissue engineering due to its chemical and structural similarity to the mineral phase of natural bone. Its biocompatibility, bioactivity, and osteoconductivity have been reported to support bonding with host tissues, while its porous structure can facilitate cell adhesion, proliferation, differentiation, and vascularization. HAp-based scaffolds can provide structural support and contribute to a microenvironment conducive to bone regeneration. When combined with natural or synthetic polymers, growth factors, or stem cells, these scaffolds may better mimic aspects of the extracellular matrix by offering both structural and biochemical cues that support new bone formation.108 The Fig. 17 showed the structural building block of natural bone tissue, which is composed of collagen and HAp.

Compared to other calcium phosphate-based biomaterials such as β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) or bioactive glasses, HAp exhibits greater stability under physiological conditions and slower degradation, which is advantageous for long-term scaffold applications.110 Several studies suggest that, under controlled processing conditions, bovine HAp can exhibit favorable crystallinity, mechanical characteristics, and porous microstructures, making it particularly reliable for load-bearing and biomedical applications.14 However, the intrinsic brittleness and limited mechanical strength of pure HAp restrict its use in high-stress environments, leading researchers to develop composite scaffolds by integrating it with polymers such as collagen, chitosan, or polylactic acid, as well as reinforcing agents like graphene, carbon nanotubes, or metallic nanoparticles, which significantly improve toughness and flexibility.111

In addition, ion-substituted HAp, where trace elements such as Sr, Mg, Zn, or Si are incorporated into the crystal lattice, has been investigated for its potential to enhance osteogenic activity, angiogenesis, and antibacterial behavior.109 Advances in nanostructured and 3D-printed HAp scaffolds have enabled improved control over architecture, porosity, and mechanical properties, which are important for tissue integration. Nanoscale HAp, in particular, provides increased surface area for protein adsorption and cell interaction, and has been associated with enhanced biological responses compared to bulk materials. Furthermore, hybrid scaffolds incorporating bioactive molecules such as BMP-2 or VEGF have shown potential to promote osteoinduction and vascularization.112 Despite these developments, several challenges remain for clinical translation, including the need to control degradation rates, ensure reproducibility in large-scale fabrication, and address cost and regulatory considerations. Continued research is therefore required to optimize material design and processing strategies for reliable and effective clinical applications.

Bone grafting

HAp has been widely investigated for bone grafting applications due to its chemical similarity to the mineral phase of natural bone and its reported biocompatibility and bioactivity, enabling direct bonding with host bone tissue and making it a preferred material in orthopedic and maxillofacial surgery.1 As an osteoconductive material, HAp can support the ingrowth of bone cells and vascular tissues, contributing to bone regeneration. Compared to biological grafts such as autografts and allografts, biogenic HAp-based materials may offer advantages including reduced reliance on donor tissue and lower risk of disease transmission, although clinical performance depends on material design and implantation conditions.114 In comparison with other calcium phosphate materials, such as β-TCP, HAp is generally characterized by slower degradation and higher structural stability under physiological conditions, which may be beneficial for applications requiring prolonged mechanical support.115 Several studies report that, depending on processing conditions, bovine HAp can exhibit favorable crystallinity, mechanical properties, and porous structures that may support osteointegration and vascularization.14,116 Several studies have demonstrated the potential of HAp-based graft materials in the repair of craniofacial, spinal, and long bone defects.117–120Fig. 19 represents the uses of natural polymer based HAp for injured bone repair. However, reported outcomes vary depending on material composition, processing conditions, and clinical context, and direct equivalence with autografts is not universally established. Despite ongoing challenges, including control over degradation behavior, mechanical performance, and large-scale reproducibility, HAp-based graft materials, particularly those derived from bovine sources continue to be explored as promising options for bone grafting applications.

Dental applications

HAp has been widely investigated in dental applications due to its chemical similarity to the mineral components of teeth, particularly enamel and dentin. This similarity underlies its use in areas such as enamel remineralization, management of dentin hypersensitivity, periodontal regeneration, and as a bioactive component in restorative materials.122 Nanoscale HAp has been explored for remineralization therapies, as its small particle size may facilitate penetration into enamel microporosities and support mineral deposition, which can contribute to improved surface hardness and reduced sensitivity. In periodontal applications, HAp-based scaffolds can act as osteoconductive matrices that support alveolar bone regeneration and may promote the attachment of periodontal ligament cells, although outcomes depend on material formulation and clinical conditions. Fig. 20 demonstrates that nanoscale HAp creates an optimal microenvironment for osteoblasts to regulate genes responsible for cell adhesion, growth, and differentiation, which successfully drives appositional bone growth during dental implant osseointegration.123 In restorative dentistry, HAp has been incorporated into composites and cements to enhance bioactivity and potentially improve mechanical performance and long-term stability and long-term stability.124 Several studies suggest that, under controlled conditions, bovine-derived HAp can exhibit favorable crystallinity and microstructural characteristics that may support integration with dental tissues. These characteristics can be make bovine HAp an attractive option for maxillofacial bone grafts, dental implants, and guided bone regeneration procedures.14 Recent developments include HAp-based composite systems incorporating materials such as collagen, fluoride, and bioactive glass, which have been investigated to improve both functional and aesthetic outcomes in dental restorations. Bioactive glasses are well known for their ability to release ions and promote bonding with biological tissues, while HAp contributes structural compatibility and stability. The combination of these materials may therefore provide complementary properties, in which the bioactive glass phase enhances biological bonding and ion release, while the HAp phase contributes structural compatibility and long-term stability.

Drug delivery

HAp has been widely investigated as a carrier in drug delivery systems due to its biocompatibility, bioactivity, and ability to interact with therapeutic molecules through adsorption and ion-exchange mechanisms. These properties enable HAp-based materials to support localized and sustained release of various bioactive agents, including antibiotics, anticancer drugs, growth factors, and nucleic acids. Fig. 21 showed that HAp enhances the loading and sustained release of amoxicillin within polyvinyl alcohol/alginate films, creating an effective controlled delivery system for treating periodontal defects.11,125 The porous structure and surface characteristics of HAp can facilitate drug loading, while its biodegradability may allow gradual release under physiological conditions. Nanoscale HAp, in particular, has been explored for the development of drug carriers with tunable release behavior, which may be useful in applications such as bone defect repair, infection control, and cancer treatment. Compared to some polymer-based carriers, HAp-based systems may provide the additional benefit of osteoconductivity, potentially supporting simultaneous drug delivery and bone regeneration; however, the overall performance depends on formulation and application context. Bovine-derived HAp has also been studied in drug delivery applications due to its compositional similarity to natural bone apatite. Some studies suggest that, under controlled processing conditions, it may exhibit favorable structural stability and drug-loading characteristics. Recent advances include the development of functionalized HAp systems through surface modification with polymers, biomolecules, or magnetic nanoparticles. These modifications have enabled the design of stimuli-responsive systems that can modulate drug release in response to environmental triggers such as pH, temperature, or magnetic fields. Such multifunctional systems have been investigated for combined therapeutic and diagnostic (theranostic) applications, although further research is required to evaluate their long-term performance and clinical applicability.1,11

Bioactive coatings

HAp has been extensively investigated as a bioactive coating material for metallic implants due to its chemical similarity to bone mineral and its reported ability to support osseointegration. HAp coatings can contribute to improved interfacial bonding between the implant and surrounding bone tissue and may also provide a degree of corrosion protection, and mechanical stability depending on coating quality and processing conditions, thereby may reducing the risk of implant loosening or failure. Implants coated with HAp form a direct chemical bond with surrounding bone tissue, facilitating rapid bone formation at the implant interface.126 Among coating techniques, plasma spraying has been extensively used to deposit HAp layers, producing relatively thick coatings with good adhesion under optimized conditions.127 A simplified diagram in Fig. 22 demonstrates osseoinduction, initiated when cell membrane proteins bind and integrate into the biomimetic HAp precipitating on the degrading calcium phosphate surface.127 In addition, thin films of nanostructured HAp prepared using methods such as sol–gel deposition,128 electrophoretic deposition,12 or pulsed laser deposition129 have been explored for improved control over surface properties. These approaches can influence surface roughness, wettability, and protein adsorption, which are important factors for cell adhesion and proliferation. Compared to uncoated implants, HAp-coated surfaces have been reported to enhance biological responses and support bone-implant integration in various orthopedic and dental applications.130 However, the extent of improvement depends on factors such as coating thickness, crystallinity, adhesion strength, and in vivo conditions.14,15 Furthermore, modification of HAp coatings through doping with ions such as Zn, Mg, Sr, or Si has been investigated to tailor both mechanical and biological properties.131 These advancements underscore the potentiality of bovine-derived HAp as a reliable and cost-effective material for next-generation bioactive coatings that can ensure long-term implant stability.

Antibacterial materials

The development of antibacterial functionality in HAp-based materials has been widely investigated to address implant-associated infections, which remain a significant concern in orthopedic and dental applications. While pure HAp exhibits limited intrinsic antimicrobial activity, its structure can be modified through doping or functionalization with antibacterial agents such as Ag+, Zn2+, Mg2+, Cu2+, or SeO32− to impart anticancer, osteogenesis and antimicrobial properties (Fig. 23).109 HAp coatings incorporating such agents have been reported to reduce bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation while maintaining osteoconductive characteristics that support bone integration.132 In addition, nanostructured HAp, owing to its higher surface area, may provide enhanced capacity for drug loading and sustained release of antibacterial agents compared to bulk materials.133 Some studies suggest that, depending on processing conditions, microstructural features of bovine-derived HAp, may facilitate the incorporation of antibacterial agents.14 Recent research has focused on multifunctional HAp-based composites that combine antibacterial activity with bioactivity and structural support. Biosafety considerations

While bovine-derived HAp offers important compositional and structural advantages, its successful clinical translation depends on rigorous biosafety validation and strict process control. In contrast to purely synthetic HAp, biogenic materials require additional consideration of residual organic content, sterilization stability, contaminant risk, and batch-to-batch variability. Complete removal of collagen, lipids, and marrow components is essential to minimize immunogenicity and ensure biosafety. Common deproteinization strategies include high-temperature calcination (typically ≥1000 °C), alkaline hydrothermal hydrolysis, oxidative treatments, and plasma processing, all aimed at eliminating organic fractions while preserving the mineral phase.42,140 Verification of organic removal should be supported by analytical techniques such as TGA, TOC analysis, EDX, and FTIR.141 Sterilization represents another critical step that may influence nanoscale structural features. Frequently employed methods include gamma irradiation, steam autoclaving, and ethylene oxide sterilization.142,143 Each approach can affect the material differently: gamma irradiation may introduce minor lattice defects or alter carbonate substitution, autoclaving can promote partial hydrolysis or surface recrystallization in highly nanostructured powders, and ethylene oxide generally preserves crystallinity but requires thorough degassing to prevent cytotoxic residues. Plasma sterilization, when applied, may modify surface functional groups and wettability. Therefore, post-sterilization characterization using techniques such as XRD, FTIR, and BET surface area analysis is recommended to confirm structural integrity and preservation of surface chemistry.144

As an animal-derived biomaterial, HAp must also be carefully evaluated for potential contaminants. Heavy metals (Pb, Cd, Hg) should be quantified using ICP-OES or ICP-MS, endotoxin levels assessed through Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL) testing, and microbial contamination verified by sterility assays.145 XRD analysis is additionally required to detect residual CaO or unintended secondary phases. Best-practice processing includes sourcing from certified disease-free cattle populations, applying high-temperature treatments where appropriate to inactivate pathogens, validating alkaline or oxidative protocols for prion risk mitigation, and ensuring compliance with ISO 10993 biological evaluation standards. Such measures are essential to minimize pathogen transmission risk and facilitate regulatory approval.

Finally, unlike synthetic HAp, biogenic materials may exhibit compositional variability arising from factors such as animal age, anatomical bone location, dietary mineral intake, and processing parameters. To demonstrate reproducibility, a minimum characterization framework should include determination of the Ca/P molar ratio via ICP, phase purity assessment by XRD, crystallite size estimation, BET surface area measurement, FTIR confirmation of carbonate substitution, heavy metal screening, and endotoxin testing. Establishing standardized reporting criteria will enhance data reliability and improve translational confidence.

Although bovine-derived HAp is widely investigated due to its compositional similarity to human bone and its availability, concerns regarding zoonotic disease transmission, particularly Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy (BSE) must be critically addressed. BSE is a fatal neurodegenerative disorder in cattle caused by misfolded prion proteins and has historically raised concerns regarding cross-species transmission. However, the risk associated with bovine-derived biomaterials is significantly mitigated through stringent sourcing and high-temperature processing. Commercially and experimentally produced BHA is typically subjected to calcination temperatures ranging from 600–1200 °C.146 Such thermal treatments are known to denature and inactivate prion proteins, whose structural integrity is compromised at elevated temperatures, particularly under oxidative conditions. Studies indicate that prion infectivity is substantially reduced or eliminated following prolonged exposure to temperatures above 600 °C, which exceeds the thermal stability threshold of pathogenic prion proteins.147148149150

In addition, regulatory authorities such as the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) mandate strict control over sourcing bovine tissues from certified BSE-free regions and enforce traceability protocols for medical-grade biomaterials. Modern biomedical-grade bovine hydroxyapatite is therefore derived from inspected and disease-free cattle populations, further minimizing transmission risks. It is also important to note that hydroxyapatite derived from bovine bone consists exclusively of inorganic mineral components after calcination, with complete removal of organic matter, including collagen and associated proteins that could potentially harbor prions. Consequently, when proper sourcing and high-temperature processing protocols are followed, bovine-derived hydroxyapatite is considered biologically safe and has been successfully used in numerous clinical bone graft applications.

Challenges and future perspective

Despite significant progress in the synthesis and biomedical application of HAp, several challenges remain that must be addressed to unlock its full potential. Current extraction techniques from bovine sources, ranging from conventional thermal decomposition and precipitation to advanced methods such as sub-critical water treatment, transferred-arc plasma, ultrasonic spray drying, annealing, and hybrid processes, have demonstrated high-quality HAp with tunable properties. However, future research must focus on enhancing these methods for industrial scalability, cost-effectiveness, energy efficiency, and environmental sustainability.

On the application front, future prospects are equally promising. The intrinsic brittleness of HAp continues to limit its use in high-load-bearing environments, necessitating composite strategies that integrate HAp with polymers, bioglasses, graphene, carbon nanotubes, or metallic nanoparticles to improve toughness, flexibility, and bioactivity. Advances in nanotechnology, such as controlled synthesis of nanorods, nanoneedles, or hierarchical porous structures, offer exciting opportunities to mimic natural bone more effectively while enhancing drug-loading efficiency and antibacterial performance. Ion substitution with biologically active elements (Sr, Mg, Zn, Si) and hybrid scaffolds incorporating growth factors or stem cells are expected to further expand the multifunctionality of HAp for personalized regenerative therapies, though controlled release and reproducibility remain challenges.

Emerging fabrication techniques, including additive manufacturing (3D printing), electrospinning, and freeze-casting, will continue to play a central role in developing next-generation HAp scaffolds with precise pore architecture, interconnected porosity, tunable degradation rates, and superior mechanical performance. Nano HAp in particular, with its large surface area and high bioactivity, is expected to accelerate osteogenesis, vascularization, and integration with host tissues compared to bulk HAp. Furthermore, 3D-printed bovine-derived HAp scaffolds with optimized pore sizes (300–800 µm) have already demonstrated strong mechanical stability and excellent tissue integration, highlighting their potential for clinical translation. Importantly, bovine-derived HAp will likely continue to hold a comparative advantage over other natural sources (chicken, fish, seashells) due to its higher crystallinity, mechanical stability, and naturally porous microstructure, making it highly reliable for both load-bearing and non-load-bearing biomedical applications. At the same time, regional diversification of raw materials could reduce dependency on a single source, lower costs, and promote circular bio-economy models.

Looking forward, the convergence of smart technologies such as machine learning and artificial intelligence with experimental synthesis is expected to revolutionize HAp research by predicting relationships between synthesis conditions, crystal structure, Ca/P ratios, and biomedical performance, thereby accelerating optimization. Clinical translation, however, will depend on overcoming key hurdles, including batch-to-batch consistency, biosafety assurance, and adherence to regulatory standards, while scaling up eco-friendly and reproducible extraction techniques. Ultimately, bovine-derived HAp when combined with advanced fabrication, functionalization, and computational tools, is poised to remain a cornerstone material in regenerative medicine, offering transformative solutions in bone tissue engineering, bone grafting, dental repair, drug delivery, bioactive coatings, and antibacterial applications.

Several bovine bone derived HAp products are already commercially available and clinically used as bone graft substitutes. For example, Bio-Oss (Geistlich Pharma) is a deproteinized bovine bone mineral widely used in dental and maxillofacial bone regeneration due to its high porosity and osteoconductive properties. Similarly, Cerabone (Botiss Biomaterials) is a 100% pure, natural bovine bone mineral which processed via high-temperature (1200 °C) treatment to ensure organic removal and structural stability. Endobon (Biomet) is another example used as granular form in orthopedic and dental indications. These products demonstrate the clinical feasibility and scalability of bovine bone-derived HAp. Therefore, largescale bovine bone-derived HAp synthesis remains essential for next-generation material.

Conclusion

Bovine-derived HAp has emerged as one of the most promising biogenic materials for biomedical applications due to its close structural and chemical resemblance to natural bone minerals, excellent biocompatibility, bioactivity, and osteoconductive properties. Various synthesis techniques, ranging from calcination and precipitation to sol–gel and hydrothermal approaches, have enabled the production of bovine HAp with tunable crystallinity, porosity, and surface characteristics, making it highly versatile for diverse clinical applications. In bone tissue engineering and grafting, bovine HAp provides superior mechanical strength, slow resorption, and a porous microstructure that facilitates osteointegration and vascularization. In dentistry, it offers effective repair of enamel and dentin defects, while in drug delivery, its porous architecture and stability enable controlled and sustained release of therapeutic agents. Furthermore, bovine HAp is widely explored as a bioactive coating for metallic implants to improve osseointegration and reduce implant failure, as well as in antibacterial applications through doping or composite formation to prevent post-surgical infections. Despite its advantages, challenges remain in standardizing synthesis routes, tailoring degradation rates for specific clinical requirements, and ensuring reproducibility in large-scale production. Future research should focus on integrating bovine HAp with polymers, bioactive molecules, nano-structuring or 3D printing techniques to further enhance its functionality and application potential. Overall, bovine-derived HAp represents a sustainable, cost-effective, and clinically relevant material that bridges the gap between natural biominerals and advanced biomedical solutions.
