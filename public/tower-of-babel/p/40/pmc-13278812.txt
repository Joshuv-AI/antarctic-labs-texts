Optimizing Venous Thromboembolism Prophylaxis in Burn Patients Using Enoxaparin Through PBPK Modeling
Authors: Sabiha Rahman Mim, Venkata K Yellepeddi, Francine J Azeredo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBurn injuries cause physiological changes that alter enoxaparin pharmacokinetics, often leading to inadequate exposure and suboptimal venous thromboembolism (VTE) prophylaxis. Current weight‐based dosing and anti‐Xa monitoring provide limited guidance for dose optimization across burn severities. This study develops a physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) model of enoxaparin across burn phases to evaluate target attainment under standard dosing while accounting for burn severity, augmented renal clearance (ARC), and body weight, and to propose individualized dosing strategies to improve VTE prophylaxis. Real‐world data of 1288 anti‐Xa concentrations were collected from 408 burn patients receiving subcutaneous enoxaparin. A PBPK model was developed in healthy adults and extrapolated to burn patients by incorporating burn‐specific changes, including reduced absorption and decreased antithrombin levels. This validated PBPK model for burn patients was used to simulate dosing regimens across burn severities and ARC, with model performance assessed using average fold error (AFE) and absolute average fold error (AAFE). Sub‐therapeutic drug exposure was most prominent from 0 to 96 h post burn across all severities. In obese patients, dose escalation from 40 to 60 mg reduced subtherapeutic exposure from 27.3% to 16.9%. Although trough anti‐Xa concentrations increased with higher doses, levels remained lower in patients with ARC compared to those with normal renal clearance (NRC), indicating the need for dose adjustment in ARC. Standard enoxaparin dosing is frequently inadequate in burn patients, particularly early after injury and in those with ARC. PBPK‐informed precision dosing can improve VTE prophylaxis beyond weight‐based dosing with anti‐Xa monitoring alone.Keywords: anti‐Xa activity, burn patients, enoxaparin, physiologically based pharmacokinetics, venous thromboembolism

Introduction

Enoxaparin, a low‐molecular‐weight heparin (LMWH), is widely used for the prevention and treatment of venous thromboembolism (VTE). Compared with unfractionated heparin (UFH), enoxaparin offers more predictable pharmacokinetics, greater bioavailability following subcutaneous administration, and a longer half‐life [1, 2]. Its anticoagulant effect is primarily mediated through the inhibition of activated factor Xa, with a higher anti‐Xa to anti‐IIa (thrombin) activity ratio than UFH, resulting in potent anticoagulation and a lower risk of bleeding [2]. Owing to its safety and efficacy profile, enoxaparin is the LMWH most extensively studied in both surgical and medically ill populations, and it remains the primary choice of VTE prophylaxis in high‐risk groups [3, 4, 5, 6].

Burn patients represent a particularly vulnerable population at elevated risk for VTE. Burn injury is a sudden trauma that induces profound physiological changes, including increased vascular permeability, altered renal clearance, and hypermetabolism that complicates medical management [7, 8]. Globally, burns cause an estimated 180,000 deaths annually, most often from flame, electrical, or chemical exposure [9]. Mortality is closely linked to burn size, depth, and inhalation trauma. Beyond local tissue damage, burns trigger systemic inflammation, vascular dysfunction, and hypermetabolism that alter drug disposition [10, 11]. The pathogenesis of VTE in burn patients is multifactorial: thermal injury induces a hypercoagulable state by enhancing procoagulant activity and suppressing natural anticoagulants, while immobility, surgical procedures, and central venous catheters further elevate risk [7, 10]. The American Burn Association reports an overall VTE incidence of 0.61% (0.48% deep vein thrombosis (DVT), 0.18% pulmonary embolism (PE)), with risk exceeding 1% in patients with > 10% TBSA burns or requiring intensive care [12]. Importantly, the true incidence is likely underestimated due to clinically silent events that often go undetected, such as 15% of patients with PE dying before diagnosis or shortly thereafter [13]. A recent study showed that DVT rates are up to 23% with current recommended prophylactic dosing, rising to 60% in the absence of prophylaxis [14].

Hospital protocols recommend weight‐based enoxaparin for VTE prophylaxis in burn patients, with 30 mg every 12 h for those under 90 kg and 40 mg every 12 h for those over 90 kg [14, 15, 16]. Therapeutic drug monitoring is performed by measuring peak anti‐Xa levels about 4 h post‐dose, with a target range of 0.2–0.4 IU/mL for prophylaxis and 0.5–1.0 IU/mL [17] for the treatment therapeutic range.

Patients with burn injury are at high risk of developing deep venous thromboembolism. Despite being the standard of care for VTE incidence, enoxaparin dosing in burn patients is complicated by profound pathophysiological alterations. During the emergent (ebb) phase, reduced perfusion, capillary leakage, and edema impair subcutaneous absorption, delaying anticoagulant exposure [12, 18]. In later phases, hypermetabolism and augmented renal clearance (ARC) further lower systemic levels, increasing the risk of underdosing [8]. The study from Lin et al. [15] reported that 79% of patients were subtherapeutic before dose adjustment. Godat et al. [19] found that 50% remained low after 30 mg twice daily, and Cronin et al. [14] confirmed frequent failures with standard dosing. These findings emphasize the inadequacy of current regimens during the early post‐burn period when patients are most vulnerable to thromboembolic events.

Several studies have sought to improve enoxaparin thromboprophylaxis in burn patients beyond standard dosing. Lin et al. [15] found dose requirements correlated with total body surface area (TBSA), while Faraklas et al. [20] proposed a TBSA‐ and weight‐based equation that improved early attainment but still left many patients underdosed. Cronin et al. [14] confirmed the inadequacy of the standard 30 mg twice‐daily regimen. More recent work by McKinzie et al. [21] and Hamblin et al. [22] highlighted the benefits of higher or weight‐based regimens, with the latter estimating a median requirement of 0.43 mg/kg every 12 h. Despite these advances, 27%–49% of patients remain subtherapeutic before therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM), underscoring the limitations of empirical and regression‐based strategies [23]. Collectively, these findings highlight the need for a more mechanistic approach, such as physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling, that can account for burn‐related pathophysiological changes and guide individualized dosing strategies.

This study aims to develop a physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) model of enoxaparin across different phases of burn injury. The model integrates key pathophysiological alterations, including impaired absorption during the emergent phase, changes in antithrombin levels, burn severity, augmented renal clearance (ARC), and body weight. Using this framework, we evaluate target attainment under standard dosing regimens and propose individualized dosing strategies to optimize VTE prophylaxis in this high‐risk population.

Methods

Model Building Workflow

The PBPK model was constructed following FDA's general PBPK guidance, as no disease‐specific recommendations exist for burn populations [24]. An adult enoxaparin model was first developed based on physicochemical properties and validated against clinical data from six independent pharmacokinetic studies [25, 26, 27, 28]. Data from the included studies' adult concentration–time profiles were extracted using Plot Digitizer (version 4.6). All study populations consisted of healthy adult volunteers receiving a single dose of enoxaparin.

The study by Agnelli et al. was an eight‐way randomized crossover trial involving six participants (five males and one female), aged 20–28 years, with body weights ranging from 53 to 72 kg. Falkon et al. conducted an open‐label randomized crossover study with a 1‐week washout period; all participants were male, with a mean age of 24.8 ± 2.4 years and a mean body weight of 76.3 ± 8.9 kg. The study by Frydman et al. included 20 healthy volunteers in a crossover design, although demographic details were not fully reported. Kuczka et al. performed a double‐blind, single‐dose crossover study in 20 healthy adults equally distributed by sex, aged 27–37 years, with body weights between 66 kg and 90 kg.

Model performance was evaluated using average fold error (AFE) and absolute average fold error (AAFE), with acceptance criteria defined a priori [29]. Once validated, the model was adapted to burn patients by incorporating key physiological changes, including reduced subcutaneous absorption and decreased antithrombin levels. Predictions for the burn population were then evaluated using retrospective clinical data from the University of Utah Burn Center. Model development and simulations were performed using PK‐Sim and MoBi, version 11 (Open Systems Pharmacology Suite, www.open‐systems‐pharmacology.com). Data manipulation and visualization were carried out in R version 4.2 (tidyverse, ggplot2, tidyr, ggpubr).

Clinical Data Collection/Real‐World Data Source

Plasma samples for PBPK model evaluation were obtained from burn patients admitted to the University of Utah Health Center, including the Burn Trauma ICU, between July 2014 and January 2023. Demographic information and clinical data, including anti‐Xa concentrations and serum creatinine, were extracted from de‐identified electronic health records. Eligible patients had a documented burn injury caused by thermal, chemical, or electrical sources, received at least one dose of enoxaparin, and had at least one anti‐Xa measurement available. Patients were excluded if they had renal failure or sustained burns from cold or non‐thermal causes. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the University of Utah (IRB#00163524) and the University of Florida (NH00044570).

Adult PBPK Model Development

A whole‐body PBPK model comprising 15 standard organs was developed using PK‐Sim and MoBi. Subcutaneous administration of enoxaparin was described using a first‐order absorption rate constant obtained from the literature. Physicochemical properties and ADME parameters were also collected from published sources and are summarized in Table 1. Based on FDA reports, approximately 40% of enoxaparin elimination was attributed to renal clearance, with enzymatic degradation via heparinase also incorporated into the model structure [17]. Enoxaparin activity was evaluated using anti‐factor Xa (anti‐Xa) levels, the accepted surrogate marker of drug exposure. A conversion factor of 100 IU/mL anti‐Xa activity corresponding to 1 mg of enoxaparin was applied [32]. Binding to the pharmacological target, antithrombin, was characterized by an equilibrium dissociation constant (KD), assuming antithrombin concentrations remained in excess of therapeutic enoxaparin levels. The dissociation rate was optimized using digitized anti‐Xa concentration–time data from adult intravenous enoxaparin studies. The model was parameterized using KD and koff, with the association rate constant calculated as kon=koff/KD. Complex formation was described using standard mass‐action kinetics. A virtual population of 1000 individuals, with a 50:50 female‐to‐male ratio, was generated to reflect a balanced adult demographic distribution. Individual model predictions were initially performed using a standard European male reference subject [36]. The initial model was used to predict anti‐Xa concentration–time profiles for comparison with observed data.

PBPK Model Evaluation

The adult PBPK model was evaluated by comparing simulated and observed enoxaparin concentrations using AFE and AAFE. Predictive performance at the population level was assessed by generating 90% prediction intervals (5th–95th percentiles) of simulated concentrations and determining the proportion of observed data falling within these bounds. Model performance was considered acceptable when at least 80% of observed concentrations were captured within the 90% prediction interval, with AFE values between 0.5 and 2, and an AAFE ≤ 3 [37].

Burn PBPK Model Development

The adult PBPK model was extrapolated to burn patients by incorporating burn‐specific physiological changes. Burn severity was defined according to total body surface area (TBSA), consistent with established clinical classification systems, and patients were stratified into mild, moderate, and severe burn groups based on literature‐supported TBSA thresholds commonly used in clinical practice. These severity categories informed the physiological adjustments applied within the model [38]. These included a reduction in antithrombin levels by up to 25% during hospitalization, extending into the early hypermetabolic phase for moderate to severe burns and limited to the emergent phase for mild burns [35, 39]. The model accounts for changes in antithrombin reduction duration but does not differentiate the magnitude of reduction across burn severities. In addition, subcutaneous absorption of enoxaparin was reduced during burn shock and the flow phase across all burn categories [40, 41]. Antithrombin expression in plasma was scaled within the burn PBPK model to reflect reduced antithrombin levels throughout the simulation. However, the absence of patient‐specific antithrombin measurements introduces residual uncertainty, which our population simulations aim to partially capture through variability assumptions.

To capture interindividual variability, a virtual population of 100 individuals (50% female, 50% male) aged 18–64 years was generated in PK‐Sim, with demographic and physiological parameters sampled from built‐in distributions for organ size, blood flow, and biochemical processes. After implementing these burn‐related changes, model predictions were evaluated against observed clinical data. Overall model performance was assessed using twofold and threefold error criteria, with residual error evaluated against population‐predicted concentrations. Predictive accuracy was further characterized by using AFE, AAFE, and root‐mean‐square error (RMSE), stratified by dose, burn severity, phase, and body weight.

Result

Adult PBPK Model

The adult PBPK model for enoxaparin adequately described digitized concentration–time data, with an average AFE of 0.98 (range: 0.9 to 1.05) and an AAFE of 1.12 (range: 1.09 to 1.17) across six subcutaneous single‐dose studies (Figure 2, Table S1). Model parameters were iteratively optimized against observed values to obtain the best fit (Table 1). The model slightly overpredicted anti‐Xa concentrations following higher subcutaneous doses. Physiological assumptions included normal antithrombin levels (90%–120%) as implemented in the PK‐Sim healthy volunteer default and an absorption rate constant of 1.2 h−1 for subcutaneous administration.

Burn PBPK Model

In the burn PBPK model, observed versus predicted anti‐Xa concentrations stratified by burn severity showed acceptable predictive performance of the PBPK model (Figure S3), with most observations falling within the twofold error range. Consistent trends were observed across mild, moderate, and severe burn groups, with no substantial deterioration in model performance. Weighted residuals and residuals plotted against population‐predicted concentrations were generally centered around zero, supporting an adequate model fit (Figures S4 and S5); however, a gradual downward trend at higher predicted concentrations was observed, likely reflecting increased variability in patients with higher body weight (> 100 kg) and severe burns, where pathophysiology is more complex.

Prediction error metrics further supported these findings (Tables S3 and S4), with AFE values close to 1 and AAFE generally below 2, indicating good predictive accuracy. Model performance declined in patients with higher body weight, particularly > 100 kg, and in severe burn groups, where RMSE, MAE, and AAFE were higher, and the proportion within twofold error was lower. In contrast, mild‐to‐moderate burn patients and lower‐weight groups showed better agreement between observed and predicted concentrations.

During the emergent phase (first 72 h), a larger proportion of patients remained in the subtherapeutic range, although target attainment improved over time. To best reproduce the 4 h post‐dose anti‐Xa concentrations, the absorption rate constant was optimized to 0.4 h−1. Comparison of Figures 3 and 4 illustrates that patients in the flow phase were less likely to remain subtherapeutic, whereas trough concentrations tended to be overpredicted in the emergent phase across all dosing groups.

Stratified analyses by body weight demonstrated that obese patients (> 100 kg) were at the greatest risk for subtherapeutic exposure. At 30 mg q12h, 81.9% of these patients remained subtherapeutic. Increasing the dose to 40, 50, and 60 mg reduced the proportion of subtherapeutic troughs to 27.3%, 22.2%, and 16.9%, respectively. Similarly, in patients weighing 75–100 kg, increasing the dose from 30 to 50 mg decreased the subtherapeutic proportion from 38.0% to 14.7% (Table 2). These findings highlight the need for higher dosing in obese burn patients to achieve adequate anti‐Xa exposure throughout both the emergent and flow phases.

Burn PBPK Model With Augmented Renal Clearance

ARC driven by the hypermetabolic and hyperdynamic state of critical illness, is frequently observed in burn patients. In the clinical cohort, ARC was more prevalent among patients receiving higher enoxaparin doses, with approximately 73% of ARC patients treated with 50 or 60 mg subcutaneously twice daily. To capture this physiology, a virtual ARC population was developed in the PBPK model using a normalized eGFR of 164.8 mL/min/1.73 m2, derived as the mean from ARC patients in the dataset. Normalized eGFR was calculated using the Cockcroft–Gault equation, with ARC defined as CrCl > 130 mL/min/1.73 m2.

Simulation results indicated that, although increasing the dose enhanced enoxaparin exposure, patients with ARC frequently remained below the prophylactic anti‐Xa target range (Figure 5). Dose escalation improved target attainment in both normal renal function and ARC subgroups; however, residual subtherapeutic exposure persisted in ARC patients, highlighting the need for tailored dosing and monitoring strategies in this population.

Dosing Simulation/Guidance

The PBPK model provided accurate predictions of plasma anti‐Xa concentrations in mild‐to‐moderate burn patients weighing < 75 kg. In contrast, observed exposures in moderate to severe obese burn patients frequently fell below the prophylactic threshold following standard subcutaneous enoxaparin dosing. To address this, a virtual burn population (N = 100; weight range 45–115 kg) was simulated across multiple dosing regimens to identify strategies that achieved target minimum concentrations. For mild burns, patients weighing 75–100 kg were best covered with 40 mg SC twice daily instead of 30 mg during the emergent phase, while patients > 100 kg required 50 mg SC. Subtherapeutic exposure was most pronounced within the first 96 h post‐burn in patients with moderate to severe injury. In both the emergent and flow phases, dose escalation was necessary to achieve target levels. For moderate burns, an increase from 40 mg to 50 mg was recommended for patients weighing 75–100 kg as well as those > 100 kg during the flow phase. For severe burns, higher doses were required: 50 mg SC for patients weighing 75–100 kg and 60 mg SC for those > 100 kg to consistently achieve prophylactic anti‐Xa concentrations.

Discussion

Burn injury remains a significant source of morbidity and mortality in the United States, with over 400,000 cases annually requiring medical and surgical intervention [42, 43]. The profound metabolic and physiological alterations following burn injury place patients at heightened risk for VTE [10]. Reported incidence rates vary across studies, reflecting differences in study design, burn severity, and prophylaxis strategies [44]. In clinical practice, combined mechanical and pharmacological prevention is often used; however, the absence of burn‐specific prophylaxis guidelines has resulted in inconsistent dosing practices across centers. Current trauma guidelines recommend higher enoxaparin doses with anti‐Xa monitoring in select patients [16], but these recommendations cannot be directly extrapolated to burn populations given their unique pathophysiology. Our findings support the hypothesis that higher standard doses of enoxaparin, coupled with anti‐Xa–guided adjustments, may be both safe and effective in reducing VTE risk among burn patients.

In this study, we leveraged real‐world data from adult burn patients receiving enoxaparin and identified obesity and augmented renal clearance as significant predictors of anti‐Xa concentrations across both the ebb and flow phases. During the flow phase, increased cardiac output and reduced vascular resistance contribute to elevated renal blood flow and glomerular filtration rate, resulting in enhanced clearance of enoxaparin [10, 23]. To better capture these dynamics, we developed a novel PBPK model for burn patients, beginning with an adult reference model and subsequently adapting it to burn‐specific physiology. This represents the first PBPK model to address both burn severity and injury stage, providing a framework for evaluating dosing strategies under normal and augmented renal clearance conditions.

Although several studies have documented altered enoxaparin exposure in burn patients, no mechanistic PBPK models are available for this population. Existing work consists mainly of observational anti‐Xa studies, and empirical compartmental pharmacokinetic (PK) model structures cannot capture dynamic burn‐related physiological changes. For example, Lin et al. [15] and Cronin et al. [14] demonstrated that standard fixed dosing frequently yields sub‐prophylactic anti‐Xa levels and requires empirical dose escalation, while Lerner et al. [45] and Shenkoya et al. [23] developed compartmental population PK models using real‐world anti‐Xa data but without organ‐specific or phase‐specific physiology. In contrast, PBPK models represent individual organs and tissues using physiological parameters such as blood flow, organ volume, glomerular filtration, and protein binding, allowing explicit incorporation of burn‐related changes in cardiac output, capillary leak, augmented renal clearance, and antithrombin depletion. PBPK modeling is therefore advantageous in populations with complex, time‐varying pathophysiology and sparse sampling, enabling mechanistic extrapolation across burn phases and patient subgroups where traditional compartmental models are less reliable. In our analysis, the PBPK model identified covariates similar to those reported in previous population PK/PD studies, including body weight and burn severity (TBSA) [23, 46]. However, earlier population PK models [23] mainly included body weight as a structural covariate, while other clinical factors were handled as occasion variability. In contrast, our model integrates burn severity directly as a physiological determinant of drug disposition.

Augmented renal clearance (ARC) was also explicitly incorporated as a covariate in our framework, which distinguishes it from prior population PK approaches. This is consistent with previous experimental findings suggesting that ARC contributes to subtherapeutic exposure for renally cleared drugs such as enoxaparin [8]. By incorporating these factors mechanistically, the model provides a clearer understanding of variability in enoxaparin exposure across burn severity and renal function.

Previous PK models of enoxaparin have been developed in a wide range of populations, including adults, obese patients, children, infants, pregnant women, and critically ill patients with conditions such as unstable angina, myocardial infarction, and deep vein thrombosis [23, 46, 47, 48, 49]. However, modeling enoxaparin disposition in burn patients poses unique challenges. Subcutaneous absorption is markedly reduced during burn shock, and antithrombin levels decline sharply after hospital admission [35, 41]. The literature reports that antithrombin activity may fall by nearly 50% compared with healthy volunteers, with only partial recovery (approximately 25%) during follow‐up. In burn injury, increased vascular permeability leads to edema and fluid shifts, which can reduce subcutaneous blood flow and increase interstitial pressure, ultimately slowing drug uptake from the subcutaneous site. In this model, these effects were captured by reducing the absorption rate constant to reflect the combined influence of edema, altered perfusion, and increased vascular permeability. Edema may also increase the volume of distribution while further limiting drug absorption. Although these changes were not modeled as separate physiological processes, their overall impact on drug disposition was represented in a simplified way [50, 51].

In our study, burn patients receiving enoxaparin showed markedly lower exposure compared with healthy adults and with patients treated for other conditions, such as heart failure or angina, except those with renal failure. Incorporating burn‐specific physiological changes into the PBPK model improved prediction of drug levels, but variability remained high during the emergent phase, likely due to differences in burn extent, body weight, and individual responses. In the flow phase, the model tended to overpredict exposure in obese and severely burned patients with ARC. In patients with ARC, the dose–exposure relationship appeared attenuated at higher doses. The burn PBPK model adequately captured the observed data, and the residual variability can be explained by key covariates such as body weight and burn severity. Overall model performance was acceptable, with prediction variability largely reflecting the heterogeneity of burn patients rather than clear structural limitations. The reduced accuracy observed in severe burns and at higher body weights suggests that these groups may require more individualized dosing approaches beyond current assumptions. Enoxaparin is a highly water‐soluble compound [52] predominantly eliminated through the kidneys; therefore, enhanced glomerular filtration in ARC accelerates systemic clearance. While dose escalation proportionally increases peak (C4h) anti‐Xa concentrations, trough levels are more strongly governed by clearance during the dosing interval. As a result, trough concentrations may cluster within a relatively narrow range despite increasing doses, creating the appearance of a plateau or “saturation.” Importantly, this phenomenon reflects clearance‐driven attenuation of exposure rather than true nonlinear pharmacokinetics.

Standard prophylactic enoxaparin dosing for burn patients typically consists of 30 or 40 mg SC twice daily [14, 47, 53]. However, the PBPK simulations indicated that these regimens remain subtherapeutic in moderate‐to‐severe burn patients weighing > 75 kg during both the emergent and flow phases. Increasing the dose improved trough concentrations and reduced the likelihood of underdosing; for example, in moderately obese patients, escalation from 30 mg to 50 mg SC nearly doubled the proportion achieving therapeutic efficacy (Table 2).

Model predictions were particularly sensitive to burn severity and the extent of antithrombin loss, highlighting the importance of incorporating this parameter into dosing considerations. While the model simplified certain aspects of burn pathophysiology, it was designed to capture interrelated clinical scenarios relevant to enoxaparin pharmacokinetics. Hepatic alterations, such as changes in ALT, AST, and albumin, were not considered despite partial hepatic metabolism of enoxaparin through desulfation and depolymerization. In contrast, renal elimination was explicitly included, with approximately 10% of the administered dose excreted as active fragments and a total of 40% as active and inactive fragments, consistent with reported pharmacokinetic data [17].

Our model incorporated a 25% reduction in antithrombin levels during the early phase, which contributed to overprediction within the first 72 h. Consistent with the observed data, anti‐Xa concentrations increased after this period, suggesting recovery of antithrombin activity over time. These findings support a strategy of dose escalation during the early phase of admission, regardless of burn severity, to offset reduced anticoagulant activity. Although enoxaparin was administered using equation‐based dosing, further dose adjustments were still necessary to achieve prophylactic targets.

This study has several limitations. First, it has a retrospective design and relies on single‐center data. Second, potential handling errors in electronic medical records may also have introduced inaccuracies; however, quality control measures were implemented to minimize this risk. In addition, the modeling platform was unable to handle large‐scale population simulations across multiple dosing regimens in burn patients, potentially limiting the representation of extreme variability. This software‐related constraint has been acknowledged as a technical limitation. While these limitations are acknowledged, they are unlikely to alter the overall conclusions. A limitation of the current model is that it does not fully capture the complex, evolving physiological changes in obese burn patients, which may partly explain the remaining prediction error.

Despite these limitations, the relatively large sample size, heterogeneity of patient demographics and burn characteristics, multiple observations per subject, and biologically plausible parameter estimates strengthen confidence in the findings. The model is specifically tailored to burn patients, and future work should include benefit–risk evaluations to assess bleeding potential in clinical practice.

Conclusion

To date, this study represents the first PBPK model developed specifically for burn patients. Using extensive real‐world data, the analysis identified obesity and augmented renal clearance (ARC) as significant predictors of 4‐h anti‐Xa concentrations. The model adequately captured observed anti‐Xa exposures during both the emergent and flow phases. Simulation results suggest that dose escalation in the early emergent phase and continued adjustment throughout hospitalization improve the likelihood of achieving prophylactic targets. Importantly, patients who develop ARC require individualized dose modifications rather than reliance on equation‐based dosing to ensure adequate anticoagulant exposure.

Overall, this burn‐specific PBPK model provides a mechanistic framework to guide precision dosing of enoxaparin, offering a pathway toward more effective and safer prophylaxis against venous thromboembolism in this high‐risk population.
