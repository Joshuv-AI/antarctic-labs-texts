Differential oxido-reductive activities of aged garlic extract and S-allyl-cysteine in genetically modified Drosophila model of Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Olasunmbo Afolayan, Victor Nwaogu, Olumayowa Idowu, Olufunke Dosumu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder frequently associated with ageing. It is characterised by the loss of neurons in the hippocampus and cortex, leading to declines in cognitive function and memory. Recent research has spotlighted Aged Garlic Extract (AGE) and its bioactive component, S-allyl-cysteine (SAC), as promising candidates for the treatment of AD due to their potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties. These features help mitigate oxidative stress and neuronal damage associated with the disease. The present study aimed to evaluate the neuroprotective effects of AGE and SAC using a Drosophila melanogaster (Drosophila) model of Alzheimer’s disease. The Drosophila (UAS-Aβ > Elav-GAL4) were reared on a diet supplemented with varying concentrations of AGE (10 µL, 20 µL, and 40 µL) and SAC (at doses of 25, 50, and 75 mg/kg). Key parameters measured included larval motility, negative geotaxis, and oxidative stress markers. The AD models exhibited reduced motor abilities, decreased antioxidant levels, increased oxidative stress, and elevated acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity. However, treatment with different concentrations of AGE and SAC significantly improved locomotor function and modulated redox activities, along with a reduction in AChE activity. This study highlights the neuroprotective potential of AGE and its active component, SAC, in the Drosophila AD model, indicating that the beneficial effects of AGE are primarily attributed to SAC.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, Dementia, Drosophila melanogaster, Aged garlic extract, Neurodegeneration, Neuroprotection, S-allyl-cysteine

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is an age-related neurodegenerative disorder that leads to cognitive decline, memory loss, and personality changes. It accounts for up to 70% of dementia cases and is characterised by the abnormal processing and accumulation of the amyloid-β (Aβ) peptide, resulting in extracellular amyloid plaques [1]. The two key neuropathological features of AD are the formation of neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs), composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein [2]. As the disease advances, cognitive function steadily worsens. Its pathophysiology is closely linked to Aβ deposits in the basal forebrain, hippocampus, and cortex, as well as oxidative stress [2]. AD can arise from acquired factors or genetic predispositions. The neurotoxicity of Aβ42 primarily stems from its tendency to form soluble oligomers and fibrillar aggregates, which disrupt synaptic function, induce oxidative stress, and lead to neuronal apoptosis, all of which contribute to cognitive decline [3].

Drosophila melanogaster (Drosophila) shares more than 70% of its genes with humans and is a valuable model organism for studying human disorders. This facilitates the identification of numerous important molecular markers and key treatment targets. Due to its extremely short lifespan, Drosophila can be precisely manipulated using its extensive array of genetic tools. As a result, complex brain function-related problems can be addressed more quickly than in larger model species [4, 5]. Additionally, Drosophila serves as an effective model for AD research because of its genetic manipulability, conserved neurobiological pathways, and ability to express human Aβ42 in neural tissues, which reliably recapitulates key features of AD such as amyloid aggregation, neurodegeneration, and memory deficits [6].

The Canton-S Drosophila strain is commonly used as a wild-type reference in neurodegenerative disease research, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD). It has a stable genetic background, well-characterised behaviour, and a consistent lifespan, making it a dependable baseline for comparing transgenic models [7]. Additionally, prior studies on AD pathogenesis and treatments have used Canton-S flies as controls for human transgene models, such as those expressing amyloid-β or tau proteins [7, 8]. The GAL4/UAS system is a popular method for targeted gene expression in Drosophila, where the yeast transcription factor GAL4 regulates the timing and location of target gene expression in specific cell types. GAL4 binds to the upstream activating sequence (UAS), a 17-base-pair site, to activate downstream genes placed under UAS control in genetically modified models. Spatial control of Aβ42 transgene expression was also achieved via UAS-mediated methods, utilising pan-neuronal GAL4 expression driven by Elav [4].

Garlic (Allium sativum) has been a popular nutritional supplement for thousands of years, valued for its health benefits. Aged garlic extract (AGE), one of the most studied and commercially available forms, is made by soaking fresh garlic in distilled water or 15–20% aqueous ethanol over time at room temperature [9]. Additionally, AGE contains S-allyl-cysteine (SAC), a cysteine-derived compound with numerous health advantages [10]. Research indicates that SAC exhibits potent antioxidant, anti-cancer, anti-apoptotic, anti-inflammatory, anti-obesity, cardioprotective, neuroprotective, and hepatoprotective effects [11]. Moreover, the rate-limiting element in the production of glutathione (GSH) is cysteine, which is a component of SAC. Therefore, the amount of GSH (an antioxidant that maintains cellular homeostasis) produced in living cells depends on the availability of cysteine [5]. The potential neuroprotective effects of AGE and SAC are mechanistically supported by their ability to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS), inhibit lipid peroxidation, and downregulate the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby preserving neuronal integrity and function under pathological conditions such as AD [12, 13].

Therefore, this study aims to compare the effects of aqueous AGE and SAC on AD-like features in transgenic Drosophila by assessing larval motility, negative geotaxis, oxidative stress markers (MDA, GSH, NO, and SOD), and AchE enzymatic activity.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Analytical-grade reagents and substances were used throughout this investigation. The supplier of SAC (99% purity) was AK-Scientific Inc. in the USA. Additional reagents and chemicals were purchased locally.

Preparation of garlic extract

Fresh garlic bulbs were allowed to age for 4 months at a temperature range of 26–28 °C. The aged cloves were then crushed to extract the garlic juice, first sieved using a muslin cloth and then filtered through Whatman No.1 filter paper. The pure extract was collected in glass vessels and stored at −20 °C until needed for use [14].

Fly stock and crosses

The strains of Drosophila employed in this investigation, namely UAS-Aβ42arc/TM3, Elav-GAL4/FM, and Canton-S, were received from the Pharmacology, Therapeutics, and Toxicology Department of the University of Lagos, Nigeria, and were cultured at 18 °C to 23 °C in the laboratory.

In our experiment, transgenic UAS-Aβ Drosophila strains, which harbour the human amyloid-beta (Aβ) transgene under the control of the upstream activating sequence (UAS), were crossed with virgin female flies of the pan-neuronal Elav-Gal4 driver line. The Elav-Gal4 line expresses the yeast transcription factor Gal4 specifically in neuronal tissues. Upon crossing, the Gal4 protein expressed in the progeny binds to the UAS promoter, thereby inducing expression of the Aβ transgene specifically in neurons of the offspring. To ensure specificity and consistency, only female Elav-Gal4 flies were used as virgins to prevent uncontrolled mating, and they were crossed with male UAS-Aβ flies. The F1 progeny expressing both the Gal4 driver and the UAS-Aβ transgene were identified and selected for subsequent experiments, as they exhibit neuron-specific overexpression of Aβ. This method follows the well-established binary Gal4/UAS expression system in Drosophila, which is used for targeted gene expression [15].

The progeny was kept on cornmeal feed supplemented with 1% w/v brewer’s yeast, 1% w/v agar, and 0.8% v/w nipagin. Standard conditions included a 12-hour cycle of darkness and light, a temperature range of 22 °C to 24 °C, and a relative humidity of 60–70% [16].

Experimental design

Eight groups (control, Aβ/Elav, Aβ/Elav + AGE 10, Aβ/Elav + AGE 20, Aβ/Elav + AGE 40, Aβ/Elav + SAC 25, Aβ/Elav + SAC 50, and Aβ/Elav + SAC 75) were created from the experimental stock. A portion of the Aβ/Elav groups and the control (Canton-S) group were fed meals devoid of AGE. Six groups of the remaining Aβ/Elav strains were fed a diet containing 10, 20, or 40 (µL of AGE) or 25, 50, or 75 (mg of SAC) per kg diet, for 28 days. All experimental procedures were performed following the Institutional Guidelines for Proper Conduct of Animal Experiments and adhered to the principles of Animal Research. Ethical approval for the study was obtained and approved by the University of Lagos College of Medicine Animal Care and Use Research Ethics Committee (ACUREC), with ethical number CMUL/ACUREC/10/21/987.

Behavioral assays

Larval motility test

The larval motility assay was included to evaluate early-onset neuromotor dysfunction, which can be indicative of neurodevelopmental or early neurodegenerative changes triggered by AD-related genetic modifications. The third-instar larvae, which were on the loose, were used for the larval mobility test. A Petri plate containing 3% agarose was covered with a grid of 1 cm2 boxes, and the number of lines the larvae’s head crossed in 30 s was noted. After allowing the larvae to go through three trials, the study used the results from the average of the three experimental procedures [17, 18].

Negative geotaxis test

Twenty flies were put in 95 mm vertical tubes for the geotaxis test, and each group was given fifteen minutes to adapt. The flies were then softly tapped to the base of the tube after it had been flipped upside down. The number of flies that ascended the tube to a height of 8 cm was counted in 8 s. The percentage of all flies that climbed to the top of the tube was used to calculate the performance index (PI). This procedure was repeated three times, at one-minute intervals, and data were recorded for analysis [16, 18].

Biochemical assays

After using a fly nap to render the fly unconscious, they were weighed using a sensitive analytical balance and then homogenised using a round-bottom glass rod homogeniser and an Eppendorf tube in cold 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline with a pH of 7.2 at a ratio of 1 mg:10 µL. The homogenates were then cryo-centrifuged at 4 °C for 15 min at 2500 rpm [19]. Micropipettes were used to precisely measure the reagents. Following meticulous aspiration with a micropipette, the supernatant was transferred into Eppendorf tubes and utilised for various biochemical measurements.

Determination of GSH levels

The method outlined by Sedlak and Lindsay (1968) was used to estimate the amount of GSH in its reduced form. Ellman’s reagent (19.8 mg of 5,5-dithiobisnitrobenzoic acid (DTNB) in 100 ml of 0.1% sodium nitrate) and 3.0 ml of phosphate buffer (0.2 M, pH 8.0) were added to one millilitre (1.0 ml) of supernatant. The absorbance was measured at 412 nm ∑ =millilitre.34 × 104 M-1 cm-1 using a spectrophotometer [11].

Determination of SOD activity

SOD activity was determined using the method of Sun and Zigman (1978), which measures the inhibition of epinephrine auto-oxidation at pH 10.2. The reaction mixture contained 2.95 mL of carbonate buffer (pH 10.2), 0.03 mL of epinephrine (the substrate), and 0.02 mL of the sample. A blank containing buffer, epinephrine, and distilled water (0.02 mL) in place of the sample was used to account for spontaneous epinephrine oxidation. Absorbance was recorded at 480 nm for 5 min, and SOD activity was calculated based on the change in absorbance (ε = 4020 M⁻¹cm⁻¹) [20].

Determination of CAT activity

CAT activity was measured following the method of Aebi (1984). The reaction mixture consisted of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 10 mM H₂O₂. Using a SpectraMax plate reader, the decrease in absorbance of H₂O₂ at 240 nm (ε = 39.4 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹) was monitored every 10 s for 2 min. Catalase activity was expressed as µmol of H₂O₂ decomposed per minute per mg of protein [21].

Determination of MDA levels

MDA levels were measured using the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) assay, following a modified method by Karatas et al. (2002). Briefly, the fly homogenate supernatant was mixed with thiobarbituric acid (TBA) and heated in acidic conditions to form an MDA-TBA adduct. The absorbance of the resulting pink chromogen was measured spectrophotometrically at 532 nm. Results were expressed as µmol/mL [22].

Measurement of NO levels

NO levels were measured using the Griess reagent after enzymatic conversion of nitrate to nitrite. Nitrate reductase and NADPH were used as the reducing agents to convert nitrate to nitrite. Samples were then incubated with an equal volume of Griess reagent for 20 min at 25 °C, and absorbance was read at 595 nm. Nitrite concentrations, corresponding to total NO levels, were determined using a NaNO₃ standard curve [23].

Determination of ache enzymatic activity

AChE activity was measured according to the protocol of Ellman et al. (1961). A buffer containing 0.1 M potassium phosphate, pH 7.4, 1 mM DTNB, and 0.8 mM acetylthiocholine was used as the initiator. For two minutes, the reaction was seen at 412 nm at 30-second intervals [24].

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software version 8. Data were expressed as Mean ± SEM, and all experimental assays were conducted in triplicate (n = 3) per group. One-way ANOVA and Tukey’s post hoc multiple comparison test analysis were used to obtain P values for experimental assays.

Results

Effect of AGE and SAC on larval and adult locomotor activities in the Drosophila model of AD

The AD model (Aβ/Elav) groups exhibited a significant (p < 0.05) decrease in larval motility and adult negative geotaxis performance when compared to the control group. On the other hand, in AGE- or SAC-treated groups, there was a significant (p < 0.05) improvement in adult negative geotaxis and larval motility in the AGE- or SAC-treated fly population in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1A and B).

Effect of AGE and SAC on antioxidant parameters in the Drosophila model of AD

In comparison to the control, all AD model groups exhibited a statistically significant (p < 0.05) decrease in GSH level and a reduction in SOD and CAT activity. As opposed to the untreated AD model group, the flies in all of the AGE- or SAC-treated groups displayed a significant (p < 0.05) dose-graded rise in GSH level, along with enhanced SOD and CAT activities (Fig. 2A – C).

Effect of AGE and SAC on oxidative stress markers in the Drosophila model of AD

The findings demonstrated that, compared to the control, the MDA and NO levels increased significantly (p < 0.05) in all AD model groups. On the other hand, flies in all AGE- or SAC-treated groups displayed considerably (p < 0.05) lower levels of MDA and NO compared to the untreated AD model group (Fig. 3A and B).

We observed a significant (p < 0.05) increase in AChE activity relative to the control in all AD model groups. Conversely, when compared to the AD model group fed on an AGE- or SAC-free diet, flies in all AGE- or SAC-treated groups displayed significantly (p < 0.05) lower AChE activity (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The pathogenic characteristics of AD include the formation of extracellular amyloid plaque deposits in the brain and a notable loss of neurons. Amyloid plaques mainly consist of the 42-amino acid Aβ peptide [25, 26]. It is necessary to clarify the mechanisms by which Aβ42 induces abnormal protein phosphorylation and neurotoxicity to comprehend the complex pathophysiology of AD. Research has demonstrated that the pathogenic features of AD in mammalian neurons can be replicated in a transgenic fly model that expresses human Aβ42 [27].

The hallmarks of AD include the accumulation of Aβ in the central nervous system and progressive degeneration of cholinergic neurons, which can impair locomotor function [28]. The study’s findings indicate that during the locomotive function test, the AD flies exhibited markedly reduced larval movement and adult climbing abilities, resulting from the development of Aβ plaques, which prevent sensitive neurons from releasing neurotransmitters such as acetylcholine (Ach). Aβ causes the degeneration of cholinergic neurons, leading to decreased acetylcholine (ACh) release and resulting in motor dysfunction [29]. However, all AGE- or SAC-treated flies displayed noticeably better locomotor function. AGE has been found to have a high SAC content [30]. Prior studies have reported that SAC releases cysteine (an essential amino acid that protects against oxidative stress and is critical for behavioural modulation) to the cell [30, 31]. Cysteine supplements mitochondrial glutathione pool by providing the rate-limiting amino acid in the synthesis of glutathione [5].

The most prevalent and essential antioxidant in living organisms is GSH, which protects cellular components from peroxides, free radicals, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) [32, 33]. In living organisms, SOD primarily serves as the first line of defence against ROS-mediated cell damage [34]. The pathogenesis of AD is intimately linked to deficiencies in CAT, a critical antioxidant enzyme that breaks down hydrogen peroxide into oxygen and water [32, 35].

The present investigation suggests that the observed depletion of GSH and inhibition of SOD and CAT activities can be attributed to the Aβ-mediated rise in MDA and NO levels, which significantly disrupts the redox balance, as seen in AD flies. The observed locomotor deficits demonstrated in the AD flies may be attributed, in part, to oxidative stress-induced neuronal dysfunction. Elevated levels of MDA and NO indicate increased lipid peroxidation and nitrosative stress, both of which compromise neuronal membrane integrity and signalling. Concurrently, the reduction in GSH, SOD, and CAT suggests impaired antioxidant defence, rendering neurons more vulnerable to oxidative damage. Increased AChE activity further exacerbates cholinergic dysfunction by accelerating the breakdown of acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter essential for motor control. Together, this redox imbalance disrupts neural communication and contributes to the progressive decline in motor performance observed in the AD model.

All of the AGE- or SAC-treated groups saw an improvement in GSH level and SOD and CAT activity, supporting AGE’s antioxidant ability, which could be a result of its SAC content [10]. Similarly, previous research has found that garlic extract, which is rich in SAC content, reduces oxidative stress in fruit flies [36]. Furthermore, it could be suggested that the inherent antioxidants included in AGE may contribute significantly to mitochondrial bioenergetics [37]. While the precise mode of action of AGE is still largely unknown, its SAC content, which possesses antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neurotransmitter-modulatory qualities, enables it to elicit its protective mechanism [38].

Since MDA is a well-known secondary lipid peroxidation product, it has been widely used as a marker for cell membrane damage [39, 40]. The build-up of NO has been linked to proinflammatory mediation [41]. The development of AD has also been linked to increased AChE activity and cholinergic breakdown [42]. The current study demonstrated that enhanced AChE activity in the AD flies was accompanied by higher MDA and NO levels due to Aβ plaque build-up. In this study, the higher MDA and NO levels are associated with damage to tissue lipid components and DNA integrity [43]. Moreover, the increased AChE activity is linked to cholinergic neuronal loss in AD flies, which appears to impair locomotor function [44]. The AGE- or SAC-containing diets mitigated the elevated levels of MDA, NO, and AChE at all concentrations, of which this action may be explained by AGE’s antioxidant, free radical-scavenging, and neuromodulating properties and its active component, SAC [11, 45].

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that dietary supplementation with AGE and its constituent SAC significantly attenuates Aβ-induced AD-like symptoms in the transgenic Drosophila model. The intervention improved redox balance and restored locomotor function, suggesting a neuroprotective effect. While SAC likely mediates the observed benefits of AGE, the relationship between them remains to be fully elucidated. These findings support the therapeutic potential of AGE and SAC, providing a foundation for future translational research aimed at developing novel interventions for AD.
