Relationship between work environment and job satisfaction among associated degree nurses: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Ali Tayseer Abdalal, Raya Yousef Alhusban, Islam Oweidat, Majdi M. Alzoubi, Amany Anwar Saeed Alabdullah, Khalid Al-Mugheed, Sally Mohammed Farghaly Abdelaliem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Associate degree nurses constitute a substantial segment of the nursing workforce and play a crucial role in delivering frontline patient care across healthcare settings. Despite their importance, limited evidence exists regarding factors influencing their job satisfaction, particularly the quality of nursing work life (QNWL), within the Jordanian context. This study aimed to examine the relationship between QNWL and job satisfaction among associate degree nurses in Jordan. A cross-sectional study was conducted between March and April 2024 among 114 associate degree nurses working in 3 government hospitals in Jordan. Participants were recruited using purposive sampling. Eligible nurses held an associate degree in nursing, had at least one year of clinical experience, and were actively employed at the time of data collection. Nurses with less than one year of experience or those on extended leave were excluded. Data were collected using the Muller and McClosky Satisfaction Scale and Brooks’ QNWL Scale. Descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation coefficient were applied for data analysis. The overall QNWL was low (mean (M) = 108.11, SD = 17.03). The lowest scores were observed in the work life/home life domain (M = 16.84, SD = 4.67) and the work design domain (M = 28.24, SD = 5.06), reflecting challenges related to workload and work–life balance. Job satisfaction levels were generally low, with mean scores ranging from 1.08 to 1.73 on a five-point scale, indicating dissatisfaction across multiple job dimensions. In contrast, relatively higher satisfaction was reported in interpersonal aspects, including satisfaction with coworkers (M = 2.67, SD = 0.42) and interpersonal interaction (M = 2.31, SD = 0.93). A strong and statistically significant positive correlation was identified between the work environment and job satisfaction (R = 0.746, P < .001). The findings reveal significant concerns regarding workload, work–life balance, and limited extrinsic rewards among associate degree nurses in Jordan. Implementing targeted organizational strategies and policy interventions aimed at improving the QNWL may enhance job satisfaction and ultimately contribute to improved quality of patient care.

1. Introduction

The health situation in Jordan witnessed a remarkable development that reflects the positive health status of the population. The Jordanian health system is considered one of the best health service providers in the region.[1] In the mid-20th century, Jordan began to establish nursing schools and training programs to address the shortage of healthcare professionals.[1]

Associated degree nurses are defined as healthcare professional nurses who have completed a two-year associate degree program in nursing,[2] They are also referred to as associated degree nurses or Diploma Nurses. Associated degree nurses receive specialized training and education to provide basic nursing care and support to patients under the supervision of RNs and other healthcare professionals.[3] Associated degree nurses, who provide essential support to RNs, are an integral part of the nursing workforce. They typically work under the direct supervision of medical, RNs, or other health professionals and provide direct personal care and assistance with daily living (activities), to patients in a variety of healthcare settings, such as hospitals, clinics, and residential nursing care facilities.[2] The role of associated degree nurses in Jordan is to support RNs in providing care to patients, particularly in areas where there are shortages of RNs.[1] Associated degree nurses are an essential part of the healthcare system in Jordan, providing direct patient care and performing a range of clinical and administrative tasks.[4] Associated degree nurses play a pivotal role as the primary point of contact for patients and their families, working in close collaboration with registered nurses (RNs) and other healthcare professionals to ensure the delivery of optimal care.[5]

Several factors can influence job satisfaction, which refers to the extent to which employees feel positively about their jobs and various aspects of their work, such as their duties, relationships with coworkers, compensation, and opportunities for growth and advancement.[6] It is a subjective feeling that can be influenced by a variety of factors, including the individual’s personality, work environment, and the nature of the work itself.[6,7] Job satisfaction among nurses is a critical factor that impacts their motivation, productivity, and turnover rates[7] Research showed that nurses who are satisfied with their jobs are more likely to provide high-quality patient care and have lower rates of burnout and turnover.[8] Low job satisfaction negatively affects the quality-of-service delivery and the organizational commitment of healthcare employees. This, in turn, can result in increased turnover rates among nurses, affecting the quality of nursing services and organizational productivity.[9]

The work environment is a key factor that impacts job satisfaction among nurses.[10] Work environment refers to the physical and social conditions in which people work. It includes factors such as the layout and design of the workplace, the tools and equipment used to perform tasks, the company culture, the relationships between coworkers and supervisors, and the policies and procedures that govern behavior in the workplace.[10,11] However, several studies were conducted in Jordan have shown that the level of job satisfaction among nurse’s ranges from borderline to neutral.[5,11] A conducive work environment is essential for stimulating employees’ job satisfaction, commitment, and productivity, especially in the healthcare industry, where poor work environments can lead to strike actions.[5,12] A positive quality of nursing work life (QNWL) can lead to higher levels of employee engagement, motivation, and job satisfaction, while a negative QNWL can lead to stress, burnout, and turnover.[13]

QNWL refers to the overall well-being of nurses in relation to their work environment, including physical, emotional, and professional aspects.[14] It encompasses factors such as workload, job security, work-life balance, support from management, and opportunities for professional development.[15] A positive QNWL not only enhances job satisfaction and motivation among nurses but also improves patient care outcomes and organizational efficiency.[16] Literature consistently highlights that when nurses experience a supportive and healthy work environment, they are more likely to stay in their positions, show higher levels of engagement, and deliver better quality care. Conversely, poor QNWL is associated with burnout, job dissatisfaction, and increased turnover rates, which can negatively affect healthcare delivery.[16,17] The authors emphasize that these results not only corroborate earlier studies but also underscore the significance of supervisory actions, organizational structure, and the overall work environment in shaping job satisfaction.[17] This underscores the need for healthcare organizations to focus on improving leadership practices, workload management, and support systems to enhance employee well-being and job satisfaction. While existing studies have recognized the importance of a positive work environment in promoting job satisfaction,[8,10] there is a lack of comprehensive research that investigates the specific components of a positive work environment and their impact on job satisfaction among associate nurses in Jordan.[15] Overall, this study seeks to address the gaps by conducting a comprehensive examination of the QNWL and its impact on job satisfaction among associated degree nurses in Jordan. The findings of this study can be used by nurse educators to do awareness campaign for under and post graduate associated degree nurses about the characteristic of optimal QNWL and about their rights to have optimal QNWL.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design

A descriptive cross-sectional correlational design was employed to investigate the relationship between the QNWL and job satisfaction among associate degree nurses working in Jordanian governmental hospitals. This design enabled the assessment of study variables at a single point in time and facilitated the examination of associations between them.

2.2. Study setting and sample size

The study was conducted in 3 governmental hospitals located in the Amman, Zarqa, and Al-Balqa governorates. The first hospital, situated in Amman, is the largest governmental facility in the capital with a 1101-bed capacity and a large nursing workforce across multiple departments. The second hospital, located in Zarqa the second-largest city in Jordan – was established in 2015, has 493 beds, and is equipped with modern facilities but employs fewer nurses than the Amman hospital. The third hospital is located in Al-Salt city in the Al-Balqa governorate, established in 1962, and has a 152-bed capacity, providing a range of medical, surgical, and pediatric services.

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power software for correlation analysis based on a medium effect size of 0.30, a power of 0.95, and an alpha level of 0.05.[18] The minimum required sample size was 134 participants; however, to compensate for potential nonresponse, 150 questionnaires were distributed using convenience sampling across the 3 selected hospitals. A total of 114 completed questionnaires were returned and included in the final analysis, representing a shortfall of approximately 20 participants (14.9% below the target). This reduction in sample size may have decreased the statistical power slightly from the intended 0.95 to approximately 0.91 and may have increased the likelihood of Type II error. Additionally, the combined effect of convenience sampling and the smaller-than-planned sample size limits the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of associate degree nurses in Jordan. Although oversampling was implemented as a mitigation strategy, this limitation should be taken into account when interpreting findings, particularly those close to the threshold of statistical significance.

2.3. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were associate degree nurses currently employed in Jordanian governmental hospitals and had at least one year of experience in their current position. Participation required voluntary informed consent. Nurses were excluded if they did not hold an associate degree in nursing, had less than one year of experience, were on extended leave, or were not actively employed during data collection. Data were collected using a structured online survey distributed through Google Forms. Prior to data collection, the researcher visited the participating hospital units to explain the study purpose and invite voluntary participation. The survey began with a digital informed consent form that described the study purpose, procedures, estimated completion time, potential risks and benefits, and the voluntary nature of participation. Consent was obtained by requiring participants to select a checkbox before accessing the questionnaire. No identifying information, such as names, email addresses, or IP addresses, was collected to ensure anonymity. All responses were stored securely within the Google Forms platform and were accessible only to the primary researcher, with confidentiality strictly maintained.

2.4. Study instruments

The survey consisted of 3 parts. The first part included a 14-item sociodemographic questionnaire that collected information such as age, gender, marital status, and years of nursing experience. The second part assessed the QNWL using the English version of the QNWL scale,[19] which comprises 42 items across 4 domains: work life/home life, work design, work context, and work world. Items were rated on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree[1] to strongly agree,[6] with total scores spanning from 42 to 252. Cronbach alpha coefficients for this study ranged from 0.56 to 0.88 across subscales. While most subscales demonstrated acceptable to high internal consistency, one subscale had a coefficient of 0.56, which is below the commonly accepted threshold, possibly reflecting contextual or cultural influences on item interpretation. Nevertheless, the overall reliability of the instrument was considered acceptable. The third part of the survey measured job satisfaction using the McCloskey/Mueller Satisfaction Scale,[20] which contains 31 items across 8 subscales. Responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale from very dissatisfied[1] to very satisfied,[5] and subscale scores were interpreted using commonly applied guidelines: scores below 3 indicated dissatisfaction, a score of 3 was considered neutral, and scores above 3 reflected satisfaction. The Mueller Satisfaction Scale demonstrated high internal consistency in this study, with an overall Cronbach alpha of 0.89.

2.5. Data collection

Following approval from the institutional ethical review committee, data were collected using a cross-sectional online survey administered via Google Forms. The researcher visited the participating hospital units and met with associate degree nurses to explain the study purpose and invite voluntary participation. Participants were informed that participation was entirely voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without penalty.

A digital informed consent form was presented at the beginning of the online survey. This section detailed the study objectives, procedures, estimated completion time (15–20 minutes), potential risks and benefits, and the voluntary nature of participation. Participants were required to indicate consent by selecting a checkbox before proceeding to the questionnaire. Access to the survey was restricted to those who provided consent. Anonymity was maintained by not collecting any identifying information, such as names, email addresses, or IP addresses. All data were stored securely within the Google Forms platform and were accessible only to the primary researcher. Data were treated with strict confidentiality and used solely for research purposes.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Review Board Committee of Zarqa University (21/2022), the Ministry of Health Ethical Committee, and the participating hospitals prior to data collection. All methods adhered to relevant ethical guidelines, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were fully informed of the study’s purpose, procedures, voluntary nature, potential risks, and benefits through a detailed cover letter included in the electronic survey. Informed consent was obtained electronically from all participants before they accessed the questionnaire, and confidentiality and anonymity were emphasized throughout the study.

2.6. Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 25, with the significance level set at α = 0.05. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, frequencies, percentages, medians, and ranges, were used to summarize demographic characteristics and study variables. The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of the data. Pearson correlation coefficient was used for normally distributed variables to examine relationships between QNWL, job satisfaction, and selected sociodemographic characteristics. Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to determine the extent to which QNWL dimensions predicted job satisfaction. The assumptions of regression analysis were assessed prior to interpretation by examining the normality of residuals, evaluating multicollinearity through Variance Inflation Factor values, and assessing homoscedasticity using residual scatterplots. Only variables that met the required statistical assumptions were included in the final regression model.

4. Discussion

The main objective to investigate the relationship between QNWL and job satisfaction among associated degree nurses in Jordan. A statistically positive relationship between work environment and job satisfaction among associated degree nurses (R = 0.746), with a significance level of (P = .0001). Associated degree nurses are an important part in the health care system, and they should have a satisfactory quality of work life to improve employees’ morale and organizational effectiveness, and quality of care.[16] QNWL is a comprehensive measure that provides insights into aspects of the workplace that nurses and organizations can modify to enhance the overall QNWL.[14] Traditional measures like job satisfaction do not fully capture the intricacies of work-related factors, and employees’ feelings about their work or work environment.[21]

Overall, the associated degree nurses in our study had a low QNWL score. The findings we obtained align with the outcomes of a previous study reported that nurses in the Jazan area in Saudi Arabia found dissatisfaction with work life among nurses and found factors such as insufficient staffing and workload intensity may contribute to low job satisfaction and quality of work life for nurses.[22] Another study reported that nurses in the emergency room had moderate scores on the QNWL.[23] A recent study found that the overall QNWL among Jordanian nurses was moderate. It highlighted that enhancing key factors such as managerial support, equitable workload distribution, and stress reduction could significantly improve work life quality, boost job satisfaction, and enhance retention, particularly among critical care nurses.[14] Another study reported that Jordanian nurses working in challenging settings, such as refugee camps, experienced a low to moderate quality of working life, largely due to high stress levels, limited resources, and demanding work conditions.[24] These results suggest that associated degree nurses in our study felt that there was an insufficient number of staff nurses in their workplace. This also highlights concerns about workload intensity and nurse staffing levels, which are crucial aspects to consider for effective healthcare management and employee well-being. These findings were consistent with previous research on the topic, reinforcing the idea that multiple work environment factors play a critical role in determining job satisfaction. In particular, supervisor behavior and organizational climate were highlighted as crucial influences, with effective leadership and a positive workplace culture significantly enhancing job satisfaction.[25,26]

Our results showed that the overall job satisfaction mean score was low, indicating dissatisfaction their work. A previously reported moderate level of job satisfaction among RNs in Jordan.[27] In another study, the results showed that 75.3% of the respondents were dissatisfied with their working conditions.[28] In Jordan, A study identified job satisfaction as a key indicator of a nurse’s commitment and dedication to their profession.[29] Their study, which involved approximately 500 nurses from public hospitals, found that participants reported high levels of satisfaction with their workload, the support they received from management and colleagues, their overall job satisfaction, and their intention to continue working in the nursing field. Another study findings indicate that workload, job burnout, and turnover intention are negatively and significantly related to healthcare quality.[12] The low job satisfaction it may attributed to working relationship between associated degree nurses and others healthcare providers. Positive interactions, mutual respect, and a collaborative approach create a more cohesive and efficient team. Also, long working hours can result in job dissatisfaction.[30] Study revealed that RNs who worked shifts lasting 12 hours or longer reported lower levels of job satisfaction compared to those with shorter shifts.[31,32] Extended work hours for nurses can increase the likelihood of errors and reduce the amount of time available to dedicate to patients, potentially leading to unfavorable patient outcomes.[33] The policy maker should be focusing on improving the work environment within hospitals, ensuring manageable workloads, adequate staffing, and a positive workplace culture by recruiting more staff and associated degree nurses which can both decrease the workload and increase the job satisfaction.

Our results showed a statistically significant relationship between the quality of nursing work-life and job satisfaction. Our results revealed a statistically significant relationship between the QNWL and job satisfaction among nurses holding associate degrees. This finding aligns with national studies, which have similarly shown that nurses with lower academic qualifications tend to report varying levels of job satisfaction and work-life quality compared to those with higher degrees.[14] For example, national research in Jordan has demonstrated that associated degree nurses often face greater challenges in terms of workload, career advancement, and autonomy, which can negatively influence their job satisfaction and overall work life quality.[11,12] Such studies emphasize the need to account for educational background when evaluating and improving QNWL at a national level, suggesting that tailored interventions may be necessary for nurses with different educational qualifications.[24,28]

Given the presence of correlation, it is likely that a nurse’s level of job satisfaction is influenced by a range of work-related characteristics, including task diversity, autonomy, and skill usage. This suggests that enhancing the structure and design of nursing duties and responsibilities could help licensed practical nursing feel more satisfied with their jobs. In a similar manner, enhancing job satisfaction among nurses and associated degree nurses requires supportive coworkers and managers as well as a positive quality of work life.[34] When trying to increase nurses’ job satisfaction, healthcare organizations and legislators should take these factors into account. This can result in higher-quality patient care and a happier workplace.

5. Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design of the study limits our ability to assess the direction, strength, and significance of the relationships between the variables. As a result, causal inferences cannot be made, and the relationships observed may not reflect long-term trends or changes. Second, the sampling method and relatively small sample size (114 participants) may reduce the generalizability of the results. While efforts were made to include a diverse range of participants, the sample may not fully represent the broader population of associated degree nurses in Jordan. Additionally, this study lacks a comparative framework with previous research focused on associated degree nurses, which restricts the ability to contextualize the findings within existing literature. Future studies should explore this gap to better understand the specific factors influencing job satisfaction and work environment for this group of nurses.

6. Implications

The findings of this study reveal that nurses experience low levels of job satisfaction and QNWL, with workload, inadequate staffing, and poor work-life balance emerging as significant concerns. These outcomes underscore the urgent need for systemic interventions at the policy, organizational, and unit levels.

From a policy perspective, the results highlight the importance of strategic workforce planning to ensure safe nurse-to-patient ratios and sufficient staffing levels. National and institutional health policies must prioritize investment in human resources, particularly in high-stress and high-turnover units, to prevent burnout and turnover. Policymakers should also consider embedding flexible work models, such as part-time shifts and job sharing, within staffing frameworks to accommodate nurses’ personal and family needs.

In terms of leadership, the study points to the strong influence of the work context dimension, emphasizing the role of supportive and transformational leadership styles in promoting nurse satisfaction and performance. Nurse managers and healthcare leaders should be trained in relational leadership, emotional intelligence, and team empowerment to foster psychological safety, recognition, and shared governance. Effective leadership development programs can empower frontline leaders to respond proactively to workplace challenges and enhance team morale.

On the practice level, organizations must adopt a multi-pronged approach to address workload issues. This includes optimizing task distribution, leveraging support staff efficiently, and integrating digital solutions to reduce administrative burdens. In parallel, healthcare institutions should offer comprehensive mental health and well-being support, including access to counseling services, mindfulness programs, and family-friendly workplace policies that promote work-life harmony. Addressing the intertwined issues of staffing adequacy, workload management, and leadership capacity is essential not only for improving nurses’ job satisfaction and retention but also for ensuring higher quality of patient care and organizational performance. Integrating these findings into actionable strategies can strengthen health systems and support a more sustainable nursing workforce.

7. Conclusion

The findings of this study underscore the urgent need for healthcare organizations in Jordan to address the low levels of QNWL and job satisfaction among associate degree nurses. Targeted interventions aimed at reducing excessive workloads – such as improving nurse-to-patient ratios, implementing flexible scheduling, and optimizing shift rotations are essential to minimize burnout and enhance job satisfaction.

Strengthening communication between nursing staff and management is equally critical. Establishing open feedback mechanisms, promoting transparent leadership practices, and encouraging team-based collaboration may foster a more supportive and productive work environment. In addition, improving extrinsic reward systems, including competitive compensation, recognition initiatives, and access to professional development opportunities, is vital for enhancing job satisfaction and nurse retention.
