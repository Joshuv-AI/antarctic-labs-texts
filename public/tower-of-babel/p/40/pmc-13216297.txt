Geostationary satellites reveal long‑range transport of internal gravity waves from the November 2025 Hayli Gubbi eruption
Authors: Taichi Murakami, Kenji Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The 23 November 2025 eruption of the Hayli Gubbi volcano in Ethiopia generated internal gravity waves that were analyzed using combined observations from the geostationary satellites Himawari‑8 and Meteosat‑9. Several hours of sustained plume emission produced a wave packet embedded within SO2‑rich volcanic air masses, which propagated eastward under strong mid‑ to upper‑tropospheric westerlies. The propagation direction and phase speed matched the 300‑hPa wind field, indicating advection along the subtropical jet. Exceptionally dry conditions within the jet core (relative humidity < 30%) likely helped maintain the volcanic plume and prevented attenuation of the wave signal during long‑range transport. No clear signatures were detected in channels sensitive to the lower troposphere or stratosphere; this likely reflects the absence of any effective wave-trapping structure along the propagation path, which allowed vertical leakage of wave energy and limited downward penetration. This vertical leakage provides a consistent explanation for the lack of detectable signals over Japan in lower‑tropospheric channels. The combined satellite observations show that the wave packet propagated primarily within a narrow mid‑ to upper‑tropospheric layer constrained by background wind and humidity structure. These results demonstrate the value of multi‑satellite observations for detecting eruption‑generated internal gravity waves and highlight the environmental conditions that enable their long‑distance transport.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46300-8.

Introduction

A major explosive eruption occurred at the Hayli Gubbi volcano in Ethiopia (13°30′36″N, 40°42′59″E; 493 m a.s.l.) at approximately 08:30 UTC on 23 November 20251. Satellite observations indicate that the eruption column rose to 14 km and released an estimated 220,000 tons of sulfur dioxide (SO2)2. The plume was transported east-northeastward by the upper-tropospheric circulation and was projected to reach Japan after passing over India and China3. Geostationary satellite measurements from Japan, the United States, and Europe subsequently detected SO2-rich upper-tropospheric air masses moving along this trajectory4. Previous large-magnitude eruptive activity at Hayli Gubbi is inferred to have occurred more than 12,000 years ago5. The nearby Erta Ale volcano, located ~ 12 km to the northeast, exhibited southward-directed dike intrusion during its July 2025 eruption6. These characteristics raise the question of whether the 2025 Hayli Gubbi eruption generated detectable atmospheric-wave signatures during its long-range plume transport.

Explosive volcanic eruptions are known to excite a variety of atmospheric waves that propagate across the Earth’s surface. The 15 January 2022 eruption of Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai7,8 demonstrated this phenomenon clearly, producing Lamb waves9, Pekeris waves10–13 and mesoscale gravity waves14 that were detected by ground-based meteorological networks15 and satellite observations14,16–19. As these waves travel over the ocean as subtle perturbations in sea-level pressure, their interaction with Proudman resonance20, Green’s law21, and resonant responses within coastal basins can amplify wave heights by more than an order of magnitude. This mechanism is analogous to that responsible for meteotsunamis generated by atmospheric gravity waves associated with various meteorological disturbances22–24. The Tonga eruption produced volcanic meteotsunamis with amplitudes of several tens of centimetres to nearly 2 m worldwide25,26. While the Tonga event demonstrated the global detectability of atmospheric waves, subsequent eruptions have not exhibited comparable signatures.

Since 2022, several eruptions with plumes reaching the tropopause have been reported27–29. For example, the 16 April 2024 eruption of Ruang volcano in Indonesia produced aerosol dispersal into the equatorial stratosphere, as captured by satellite observations27. In addition, recent studies have shown that volcanic eruptions can generate upward-propagating mesoscale gravity waves that reach the mesosphere and even the ionosphere, providing an example of strong vertical coupling in the atmosphere. During the 2021 La Soufrière eruption, Terra/MISR observations revealed that the volcanic plume penetrated the tropopause to altitudes approaching 20 km, while subsequent VIIRS nightglow imagery detected concentric gravity waves in the mesopause region together with ionospheric disturbances identified in GNSS-TEC measurements29. However, despite these upward-propagating signatures, no prominent cases have been documented in which Lamb or Pekeris waves were detected within the troposphere by either satellite or ground-based observations. The 2025 Hayli Gubbi eruption similarly did not generate globally propagating Lamb or Pekeris waves, nor were volcanic meteotsunamis associated with pressure-wave passage reported. Nevertheless, the buoyancy generated at the interface between the eruptive plume and the surrounding atmosphere can excite internal gravity waves30–32, suggesting that wave packets may accompany long-range plume transport under upper-level winds. These considerations motivate a targeted investigation using high-frequency satellite observations.

Here, we apply the atmospheric-wave analysis framework developed for the Tonga eruption33 to the 2025 Hayli Gubbi event and demonstrate that internal gravity waves were detectable along the plume trajectory. Using observations from Europe’s Meteosat‑9 (Indian Ocean sector) and Japan’s Himawari‑8, we identify a wave packet that propagated from Ethiopia across India and China and ultimately reached Japan, nearly 10,000 km from the vent. We present the observational characteristics of these disturbances and discuss the factors enabling their long-range detectability, as well as the contrasts with atmospheric-wave signatures observed during the Tonga eruption.

Results

About 24 h after the eruption, the wave packet reached the Arabian Sea (Fig. 1b), and by 26 h it had arrived near the coast of Pakistan, subsequently crossing northwestern India, Nepal, the Himalayas, and into the southern Tibetan Plateau.

As the wave packet crossed 90°E and entered the Himawari‑8 domain, both the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T}_{bb}{\prime}{\prime}$$\end{document} signal and the red component of the Air Mass RGB became stronger than those observed west of 90°E in the Meteosat‑9 data (Fig. 1c). Differences in the central wavelengths of the corresponding channels between the two satellites may also contribute to the sensitivity differences. The wave packet continued eastward near 30°N from the Tibetan Plateau to the central‑eastern coast of China. After reaching the East China Sea, it shifted slightly northward, passed over Kyushu and Shikoku (Fig. 1d), and then traveled along the Pacific coast of Honshu before merging into a frontal system extending from a low‑pressure area over the Pacific.

The leading edge of the wave packet crossed 130°E and made landfall over Kyushu at approximately 12:40 UTC. The snapshot in Fig. 3 was taken about 2 hours and 20 minutes after landfall. Among the various wave-like patterns visible in the figure, Band‑10 reveals a distinct east–west elongated wave structure between 32°N and 34°N and between 128°E and 136°E, with longer wavelengths than the surrounding features. This structure corresponds to the wave packet that originated near Ethiopia.

In the Air Mass RGB image, this region coincides with a pronounced red area, indicating a dry air mass containing volcanic emissions. This spatial agreement between the wave packet and the volcanic plume, as discussed in the previous section, is clearly evident here as well.

Snapshots from other channels show narrow, northeastward‑elongated wave patterns in Band‑8 and Band‑9, but no distinct structures corresponding to those in Band‑10 are observed in the other channels. Although Band‑11 also responds to SO2 absorption, it is more sensitive to lower atmospheric layers than Band‑10, suggesting that volcanic particles did not significantly settle into the lower troposphere. The difference between Band‑12 and Band‑13 also fails to reveal clear wave structures.

These results indicate that the wave packet propagated together with the volcanic plume, forming wave structures primarily within the mid‑troposphere. The eastward propagation speed of the wave packet is estimated to be approximately 2.5° per hour in longitude, which corresponds to about 240 km/h (67 m/s) at 30°N latitude.

Discussion

In the previous section, we described the propagation pathway and vertical structure of the internal gravity-wave packet and the accompanying dry volcanic plume as captured by satellite observations. Here, we further discuss why these features were able to travel approximately 10,000 km from the eruption site to Japan, and how their characteristics differ from the atmospheric waves generated by the 2022 Tonga eruption.

Figure 5 shows snapshots of the horizontal wind speed and relative humidity at 300 hPa from the NCEP-FNL analysis38, together with the trajectory of the atmospheric wave packet (shaded). The position of the wave packet at each time is indicated by a green circle. Most of the propagation pathway lies within a dry air corridor with relative humidity below 30%, meaning that the plume had little opportunity to interact with moist air. The region around Hayli Gubbi is located near the leading edge of a trough formed by the southward meander of the subtropical jet, with a strong westerly flow extending beneath the jet core. This configuration indicates that the volcanic plume and the accompanying internal gravity waves were transported eastward along the prevailing westerlies. The strong horizontal flow along the westerly jet likely stretched the dry air mass into a narrow band, inhibiting lateral mixing with the more humid air masses situated to the north and south. Such a background environment appears to have been one of the key factors enabling the long-range transport of the dry plume and the wave packet. Focusing on the wind speed along the westerly jet, values are around 30 m/s over the Arabian Peninsula, increase to approximately 50 m/s over Pakistan and India, and exceed 70 m/s from the Tibetan Plateau to Japan. The propagation speed of the air parcel carrying the wave packet, estimated from satellite imagery, is broadly consistent with this wind-speed distribution, supporting the interpretation that both the plume and the internal gravity waves were advected long distances by the westerly jet.

For the vertical propagation, gravity-wave ducting39 can be important because it traps wave energy and reduces vertical leakage. However, ducting is not a requirement for vertical propagation; rather, it occurs when background wind and stability profiles combine to form a wave-guiding layer. Multiple atmospheric configurations may create ducts, including layers with strong vertical wind shear, regions near jet-stream maxima, or layers bounded by sharp stability transitions such as those near the tropopause. Previous observational studies have also shown that Doppler-type ducting associated with wind maxima is common in the upper atmosphere40.

Gravity waves that exist in the mid‑troposphere can propagate downward and induce small pressure fluctuations at the surface. When such pressure disturbances travel over the ocean for several hundred kilometers above water depths of several hundred meters, the resonance mechanism described in the Introduction may act to generate meteotsunamis23. Such stability layering represents only one possible ducting configuration relevant to certain events; it is not a universal requirement, because Doppler ducting due to the vertical wind profile can also produce wave-guiding conditions40.

In this study, we evaluated the presence any such trapping structure by calculating the Richardson number (Ri)41 using 300–500 hPa isobaric data and identifying the minimum Ri within the layer. Figure 6 shows the distribution of the minimum Ri, corresponding to the humidity distribution shown in Fig. 5. According to Fig. 6, the wave packet remained within a generally stable environment (Ri > 0.25) throughout its passage, except when it crossed over the Tibetan Plateau. A snapshot at that time (Fig. 6b) shows localized regions where 0 < Ri < 0.25. Although this temporary reduction in Ri suggests that vertical wind shear may have contributed to limited amplification or partial downward propagation of the waves, the spatial extent of the unstable layer was small, and its overall impact is considered minor. Consequently, no significant surface pressure disturbances attributable to downward‑propagating internal gravity waves were observed near the wave packet, nor were any clear sea‑level variations detected. A broader inspection of the reanalysis wind and humidity fields revealed no evidence that a coherent ducting layer—whether stability‑based or jet‑induced—developed along the plume trajectory. Although the subtropical jet provided strong horizontal advection, its vertical shear did not organize into an effective wave‑guiding structure. As a result, the wave packet remained confined to a relatively narrow mid‑ to upper‑tropospheric layer while experiencing continuous vertical energy leakage, which likely inhibited downward penetration into the lower troposphere. This interpretation is consistent with the absence of lower‑tropospheric signatures in the satellite observations over Japan.

The most critical factor distinguishing the atmospheric wave characteristics between the present Ethiopian eruption and the 2022 Tonga eruption is the difference in eruption scale. In the case of the Tonga eruption, the plume reached an altitude of 58 km, and the resulting umbrella cloud extended to a diameter of 500 km, representing an exceptionally large-scale event7,43. The rapid and massive release of high-pressure volcanic gases generated Lamb waves, Pekeris waves, and gravity waves that propagated from the surface to the thermosphere, traversing the global atmosphere16–19. These signals were observed across multiple infrared bands of geostationary meteorological satellites and were also detected as ionospheric disturbances.

In contrast, the plume from the Ethiopian eruption is estimated to have reached only up to the tropopause2. The emission of volcanic material continued for approximately 3–4 h, during which gravity waves were generated by buoyancy interactions between the plume and the surrounding atmospheric environment. Consequently, the wave packet in this case was embedded within the volcanic air mass, and its propagation range was largely confined to the transport path of the material itself.

The clear distinction between these two eruption-induced wave phenomena has important implications for future monitoring of volcanic atmospheric waves and associated meteotsunamis. The boundary between eruptions capable of generating globally propagating atmospheric waves, such as the Tonga event, and those that produce more localized but still long-range wave packets coupled with material transport, as in the Ethiopian case, may become increasingly well-defined through continued analysis of future eruption events.

Concluding remarks

In this study, we combined observations from multiple geostationary meteorological satellites—Japan’s Himawari and Europe’s Meteosat—to continuously track the propagation of internal gravity waves generated by the volcanic eruption that occurred around 08:30 UTC on 23 November 2025. As a result, internal gravity waves were clearly detected in the 6.2-µm and 7.3-µm water–vapor bands, which are sensitive to the mid- to upper troposphere, reaching the Japan region located approximately 10,000 km east-northeast of the vent. In contrast, no corresponding signals were detected in wavelength bands sensitive to the lower troposphere or stratosphere. This absence likely reflects the lack of any effective wave-trapping structure—whether stability-based or jet-induced—along the propagation path, which allowed vertical leakage of wave energy and limited downward penetration. The ability to capture the continuous evolution of the wave packet across the observational boundaries of satellites with different scan intervals and coverage demonstrates the significant potential of multi-satellite approaches for future atmospheric wave studies.

The internal gravity waves detected in this event were generated by several hours of sustained plume emission following the eruption and were advected eastward together with SO2-rich volcanic air masses by the upper-level westerlies. The eruption site was located beneath a southward-meandering branch of the subtropical jet, and the wave-bearing air parcels accelerated as they traveled along this strong westerly flow. The relative humidity within the jet core was below 30%, providing an exceptionally dry environment that likely played a key role in maintaining both the volcanic plume and the associated wave packet over long distances without significant attenuation.

The atmospheric waves observed in this case differ fundamentally from those generated by the 2022 Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha ‘apai eruption, in which the plume penetrated into the stratosphere and mesosphere and excited Lamb waves and Pekeris modes that propagated globally. Demonstrating that eruptions of different magnitudes can excite distinct types of atmospheric waves—and that these waves can be continuously monitored using geostationary satellite observations—provides an important step toward a more comprehensive understanding of volcanic atmospheric disturbances. Accumulating and comparing additional eruption cases in the future will help establish the conditions under which volcanic meteotsunamis and related atmospheric–oceanic responses occur, contributing to improved volcanic hazard assessment and early-warning capabilities.

Data and methods

Satellite data

This study used full-disk observations from the geostationary meteorological satellites Meteosat-9 and Himawari-8, together with brightness-temperature data from the high-frequency Japan-area observations of Himawari-8. The scan intervals are 15 min for Meteosat-9 full-disk observations, 10 min for Himawari-8 full-disk observations, and 2.5 min for the Himawari-8 Japan-area rapid-scan observations. The spatial resolution at the sub-satellite point is approximately 3 km for the infrared channels of Meteosat-9 and 2 km for those of Himawari-834,35.

Over Japan, two geostationary satellites—Himawari-8 and Himawari-9—are positioned in geostationary orbit. Himawari-9 began operational service on 13 December 2022, with Himawari-8 placed in standby mode as a backup. A communication failure occurred on Himawari-9 at around 15:30 UTC on 11 October 2025, and Himawari-8 was activated as a replacement from 12:00 UTC on 12 October until 04:50 UTC on 26 November 2025. For this reason, Himawari-8 data were used instead of Himawari-9 during the analysis period44. Himawari-8 paused full-disk observations at 02:50 UTC and 14:50 UTC on 23-25 November 2025 due to housekeeping.

As summarized in Table 1, the central wavelengths of corresponding channels differ slightly between the two satellites. In this study, we selected for analysis the Meteosat-9 channels whose central wavelengths most closely match those of the corresponding Himawari-8 channels.

Analysis period and brightness-temperature conversion

The analysis period for the full-disk observations spans from 08:00 UTC on 23 November 2025 to 00:00 UTC on 26 November 2025. For the Himawari-8 Japan-area observations, the analysis covers the interval from 00:00 UTC on 25 November to 00:00 UTC on 26 November 2025, corresponding to the period immediately before and after the passage of the atmospheric wave.

The Himawari-8 full-disk data contain digital counts of radiance, which were first converted to radiance and subsequently to brightness temperature. The Meteosat-9 full-disk data provide radiance values directly, and therefore only the radiance-to-brightness-temperature conversion43 was applied.

Physical basis for extracting wave signals

These perturbations appear as small fluctuations in satellite-measured brightness temperature. Non-adiabatic heating processes are assumed negligible because their characteristic timescales are much longer than the wave periods considered here.

Air Mass RGB construction

To visualize volcanic air masses, we generated the NOAA Air Mass RGB37 composite, which is widely used for detecting volcanic gas in the mid- to upper troposphere. The red component uses the difference between the WV Upper and WV-SO2 bands, the green component uses the difference between the Ozone Band and IR Cloud, and the blue component uses the inverted WV Upper field. Enhanced red coloration indicates increased SO2 content, whereas enhanced green coloration reflects stronger ozone absorption.

The initial reference grid was placed at the Hayli Gubbi vent. Reanalysis data

To examine the environmental conditions along the air-parcel trajectory, we used the NCEP-FNL reanalysis38. The dataset spans 00:00 UTC on 23 November to 00:00 UTC on 26 November 2025, with 6-hour temporal resolution. The global fields are provided on a 0.25° grid and include geopotential height, temperature, wind, humidity, and surface variables on pressure levels from 1000 hPa to 0.01 hPa. Wind and humidity fields on the mid- to upper-tropospheric levels relevant to the observational bands were extracted to visualize the moisture environment along the wave-propagation path. To examine one aspect of the environment relevant to wave trapping, we computed the Richardson number (Ri)41 for the 500–300 hPa layer. This analysis addresses only instability-related trapping and does not capture Doppler-type ducting mechanisms that depend on the vertical wind structure.

Surface pressure observations

To assess the extent to which internal gravity waves associated with the volcanic air mass produced measurable surface-pressure fluctuations, we analyzed barometric observations from the IRIS observation network42. Three stations located near the wave-propagation path—Ethiopia (8.89°N, 38.68°E), Djibouti (11.53°N, 42.85°E), and Dubai (24.95°N, 56.20°E)—were selected. The pressure data have a sampling interval of 1 s and a resolution of 0.01 hPa. A band-pass filter with a passband of 16–200 min, corresponding to the wave-period range detected in the satellite observations, was applied to extract the relevant pressure fluctuations.
