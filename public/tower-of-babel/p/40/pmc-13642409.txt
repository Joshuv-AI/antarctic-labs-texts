The clinical relevance of RAS pathway gene mutations in childhood B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia
Authors: Xiaoyu Chen, Li Gao, Yongping Zhang, Yixin Hu, Jian Pan, Zhiheng Li, Jun Lu, Yizhen Li, Shaoyan Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective: Notably, FLT3 and RAS pathway gene mutations represent a significant proportion of childhood B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) cases. Our study explores whether these patients have distinct clinical features and prognostic outcomes. Methods: We retrospectively analyzed 488 pediatric B-ALL cases from the CCCG-ALL-2015 cohort using whole transcriptome sequencing to assess the clinical features of FLT3 mutations, RAS pathway alterations, and their cooperative effects. Results: 192 patients (39.3%) carried at least one RAS pathway alterations, while 76 patients (15.6%) had FLT3 mutations. Strikingly, 6 of 11 NF1-mutated patients harbored concurrent FLT3 mutations. A significant correlation was observed between FLT3 and NF1 mutations (Phi coefficient = 0.16, χ² = 13.0, p < 0.001), NRAS and KRAS mutations (Phi coefficient = 0.19, χ² = 17.12, p < 0.0001), NRAS and PTPN11 (Phi coefficient = 0.138, χ² = 9.54, p = 0.0042). FLT3/NF1 co-mutated patients exhibited a higher frequency of abnormal karyotypes and had significantly poorer overall survival (OS) and event-free survival (EFS) (p < 0.05), particularly when compared to those without KMT2A rearrangements (p = 0.004 and p = 0.003, respectively). Collectively, RAS pathway alterations were not significantly associated with inferior OS or EFS. Multivariable Cox regression analysis confirmed that FLT3/NF1 co‑mutations were independently associated with inferior OS (HR = 12.39, 95% CI: 1.40-109.46, p = 0.024), and multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that they were also independently associated with Day 46 MRD ≥ 0.01% (OR = 9.50, 95% CI: 1.65–54.71, p = 0.01). Conclusion: Our findings suggest that FLT3/NF1 co-mutations-rather than other RAS pathway mutations, may be associated with adverse outcomes in pediatric B‑ALL, warranting further validation. Trial registration: The study was conducted with approval from the Institutional Review Board of Children’s Hospital of Soochow University (Approval number: 2019KS006).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00277-026-07164-0.

Introduction

B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL), the most common childhood malignancy, is characterized by the clonal expansion of immature B cells, leading to impaired normal hematopoiesis [1]. Over the past two decades, risk-stratified treatment strategies, including intensified chemotherapy regimens and minimal/measurable residual disease (MRD)-guided therapy, have significantly improved clinical outcomes in B-ALL [2, 3]. Although the overall survival rate for pediatric B-ALL now exceeds 90%, high-risk patients and those experiencing relapse continue to face treatment failure and dismal prognoses [4–6]. Consequently, elucidating novel molecular mechanisms and identifying therapeutic targets remain critical for improving outcomes in refractory or relapsed B-ALL.

The advent of whole transcriptome sequencing (WTS) has revolutionized the molecular classification of B-ALL, enabling the discovery of novel subtypes and refining risk stratification beyond conventional criteria. The FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) gene, located on chromosome 13q12, encodes a transmembrane receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) that plays a crucial role in hematopoiesis by serving as a key cytokine receptor [7]. FLT3 mutations occur in approximately 5–10% of pediatric B-ALL cases [8, 9]. While the prognostic significance of FLT3 mutations, particularly FLT3-ITD (Internal Tandem Duplication), is a well-established oncogenic driver in acute myeloid leukemia (AML), their impact on B-ALL outcomes remains unclear [7, 10]. FLT3 functions upstream of the RAS-ERK pathway, mediating extracellular signal transduction and activating downstream cascades, including the RAS pathway, to regulate cellular processes. Notably, RAS pathway mutations (e.g., NRAS, KRAS, PTPN11, NF1) are present in about 20–36% of pediatric B-ALL cases [8, 11–13]. Among these, NRAS and KRAS mutations are more frequent in pediatric than adult B-ALL, yet—like FLT3 mutations—they lack significant prognostic relevance [14, 15].

Earlier research on B-ALL has largely focused on single-gene alterations or fusion genes, leaving the clinical implications of combinatorial mutational patterns relatively unexplored. While growing evidence indicates that certain co-mutational signatures—particularly IKZF1 alterations coexisting with CDKN2A/B, PAX5, or PAR1 deletions—demonstrate robust correlations with unfavorable clinical outcomes [16, 17]. Previous studies have primarily focused on NRAS/KRAS interactions or common mutational subtypes involving RAS or FLT3. Notably, FLT3 and RAS (NRAS or KRAS) mutations exhibit significant mutual exclusivity in hyperdiploid B-ALL [8, 18, 19]. Subtype-specific analyses have further revealed distinct mutational patterns: FLT3, KRAS, and NRAS mutations are preferentially associated with hyperdiploid B-ALL, while NF1 mutations predominantly occur in hypodiploid subtypes [20, 21]. Given the high prevalence and heterogeneity of FLT3 and RAS pathway alterations, we sought to systematically investigate their co-mutational patterns and clinical significance in pediatric B-ALL [22, 23].

In this study, we performed WTS on 562 pediatric patients with newly diagnosed B-ALL to investigate the co-occurrence of FLT3 mutations and RAS pathway alterations (NRAS, KRAS, NF1, and PTPN11), as well as their associated clinical characteristics and prognostic implications.

Methods

Patients and data acquisition

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Children’s Hospital of Soochow University. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to treatment initiation, including consent for biological specimen collection and subsequent research use.

A total of 488 newly diagnosed pediatric patients with B-ALL treated according to the CCCG-ALL-2015 protocol was included in this study. All patients were aged 1–18 years. The median follow-up time was 2.43 years (IQR: 1.30–3.39 years). Clinical data, including date of diagnosis, age at diagnosis, sex, and other relevant variables, were retrospectively extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical records system.

Diagnostic genetic rearrangements were determined using fluorescence in situ hybridization, combined with WTS and reverse-transcriptase polymerase chain reaction. Gene mutations were identified using WTS, which was performed by a collaborating laboratory. The analysis was based on report data provided by the collaborating laboratory, including genetic rearrangements at diagnosis, mutation details, and other relevant findings. Only Grade 1 and Grade 2 (pathogenic and likely pathogenic) variants were included in our analysis. Grade 3 variants (variants of uncertain significance) were detected but excluded from our analysis.

Whole transcriptome sequencing

Ribosomal RNA was removed from total RNA using the Ribo-Zero method. The resulting RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA, which was used as a template to construct sequencing libraries. Whole transcriptome sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to detect gene fusions and SNVs at the RNA level. The mean read count of our sample was 136,952,383 reads (range: 99,631,850 to 140,923,276). For bioinformatics analysis, sequencing reads were aligned to the UCSC hg19 reference genome using the STAR software. Variant calling was performed using MuTect2, followed by annotation with VEP (Variant Effect Predictor). Gene fusions were predicted using FusionCatcher. Downstream data analysis of the alignment results was conducted using VEP (Variant Effect Predictor). Annotation was performed using multiple databases, including ClinVar, dbSNP, 1000 Genomes Project, gnomAD, ExAC, and COSMIC. Variant filtering criteria were as follows: only rare variants with a population frequency < 1% were retained, while common SNPs were excluded. Functional impact prediction was performed using the SIFT software, with ≤ 0.05 considered deleterious (D) and > 0.05 considered tolerant (T). Variants were classified into three grades based on clinical significance: Grade 1 (definitively associated with the disease), Grade 2 (possibly associated with the disease), and Grade 3 (variants of uncertain clinical significance). Sanger validation was performed only for Grade 1 and Grade 2 variants with a variant allele frequency ≥ 10%; variants with variant allele frequency below 10% were not validated due to technical limitations of Sanger sequencing.

Copy number alterations, including hyperdiploidy and hypodiploidy, were inferred from RNA-seq read depth using CNVkit (v0.9.8). Briefly, read depth per exon was normalized against a panel of normal control samples. Log2 ratio thresholds of > 0.3 and < -0.3 were used to define gains and losses, respectively. Hyperdiploidy was defined as the gain of at least five chromosomes, typically including chromosomes 4, 6, 10, 14, 17, and/or 18. Hypodiploidy was defined as a modal chromosome number < 44 or the loss of at least four chromosomes.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0.1.0) and R (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) with the following essential packages: survival for survival analyses, vcd for categorical data visualization, dplyr for data manipulation, and ggplot2 for graphical representations. Overall survival (OS) was calculated from the date of B-ALL diagnosis until death from any cause, with living patients censored at their last follow-up date.

Event-free survival (EFS) was measured from diagnosis to the first occurrence of: disease relapse, death from any cause or treatment failure with event-free patients censored at last follow-up. Categorical variables were analyzed using χ² tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate based on expected cell frequencies. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with between-group differences assessed by log-rank tests. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed to evaluate independent prognostic factors, utilizing both backward and forward stepwise selection based on likelihood ratio criteria (α = 0.05 for entry/removal). All statistical tests were two-sided, with p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 488 pediatric B-ALL patients who underwent WTS at diagnosis under the CCCG-ALL-2015 protocol were enrolled in this study. The study population had a median age of 5.08 years (range: 3.2–8.2 years) with a male predominance (60.9% vs. 39.1%; Supplemental Table S1). We identified RAS pathway mutations in 192 patients (39.3%), including NRAS (n = 98), KRAS (n = 96), PTPN11 (n = 51), and NF1 (n = 11) variants. Additionally, FLT3 mutations were detected in 76 patients (15.6%).

Co-occurrence analysis between FLT3 and RAS pathway genes

As illustrated in Fig. 1, nearly half of the patients with FLT3 mutations also harbored concurrent alterations in RAS pathway genes. To assess potential associations between FLT3 mutations and RAS pathway alterations, we performed Pearson residual analysis and chi-square tests. Notably, as shown in Fig. 2, co-mutation of FLT3 and NF1 occurred more frequently than expected by chance, as evidenced by a high Pearson residual (3.3). Statistical analysis confirmed significant positive associations between FLT3 and NF1 mutations (Phi coefficient = 0.16, χ² = 13.0, p < 0.001), NRAS and KRAS mutations (Phi coefficient = 0.19, χ² = 17.12, p < 0.0001), NRAS and PTPN11 (Phi coefficient = 0.138, χ² = 9.54, p = 0.0042), indicating non-independence and a strong tendency for co-occurrence. In contrast, no significant correlation was observed between FLT3 mutations and alterations in NRAS, KRAS, or PTPN11 (Supplemental Table S2).

Clinical characteristics of the enrolled patients

We compared the clinical and cytogenetic characteristics of patients with RAS pathway and FLT3 mutations (Supplemental Table S3) with those of wild-type patients. Patients harboring RAS pathway alterations exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of hyperdiploidy and DUX4 rearrangement (all p < 0.05), whereas the frequencies of ETV6::RUNX1, BCR::ABL1, PAX5-Alt and TCF3::PBX1 subtypes were markedly reduced compared to those without RAS pathway mutations (all p < 0.05). Among FLT3-mutated cases, hyperdiploidy was more common (p < 0.001), while BCR::ABL1, TCF3::PBX1 and ETV6::RUNX1 subtypes were underrepresented (p < 0.05). Beyond a markedly higher prevalence of hyperdiploidy (p < 0.001, p = 0.002, respectively) observed in both co-mutation groups, and a significantly lower frequency of the ETV6::RUNX1 fusion specifically in the NRAS/KRAS group (p = 0.03), patients with NRAS/KRAS or NRAS/PTPN11 co-mutations did not significantly differ from wild-type patients in other clinical characteristics. Patients with FLT3/NF1 co-mutations had a significantly higher prevalence of the PAX5 subtype (p = 0.012), compared to wild-type patients. Furthermore, the remaining four cases, accounting for 66.7% of the co-mutation group, could not be classified into any known subtype and were designated as B-other; this proportion was also significantly higher than that observed in wild-type patients (p = 0.023).

MRD was assessed on days 19 and 46 post-treatment. As shown in Supplemental table S3, PTPN11-mutated patients showed a significantly higher rate of MRD ≥ 0.1% than those without PTPN11 mutations (p < 0.001) at day 19. By day 46, two of six cases (33.33%) with concurrent FLT3 and NF1 mutations had MRD ≥ 0.1%, a proportion significantly elevated compared to patients lacking these co-mutations (1.3%, p = 0.004).

While not reaching statistical significance, we observed that 4 of 6 (66.7%) patients with FLT3/NF1 co-mutations exhibited abnormal karyotypes. In contrast, all 5 patients with NF1 mutations alone showed normal karyotypes (Table 1). Among the FLT3-mutated cases, molecular characterization revealed that 5 patients harbored FLT3-tyrosine kinase domain mutations (TKD), while 1 case presented with a juxtamembrane region insertion mutation-both classified as non-ITD domain alterations. Additionally, all patients with FLT3/NF1 co-mutations were classified as B-other subtype and could not be assigned to any current molecular subtype of B-ALL.

Overall survival

The overall 3-year OS rate for all enrolled patients was 97.9% ± 0.7%. As shown in Supplemental Table S4, both age at diagnosis > 10 years and initial WBC count ≥ 100 × 109/L were significantly associated with inferior OS (p = 0.02 and p = 0.004, respectively). Kaplan-Meier analysis of RAS pathway mutations and FLT3 mutations individually showed no significant prognostic impact. Strikingly, patients with concurrent FLT3 and NF1 mutations exhibited markedly worse outcomes, with a 3-year OS of 75.0% ± 21.7% compared to 98.0% ± 0.7% in wild-type patients (Fig. 1). This survival difference reached statistical significance (p = 0.005), while no other co-occurring mutations showed significant prognostic value (Supplemental Table S5).

Comparative survival analysis among molecular subtypes revealed distinct prognostic patterns (Fig. 1). Patients with FLT3/NF1 co-mutations demonstrated comparable OS to KMT2Ar (rearrangement) subtype (p = 0.286), but exhibited significantly worse outcomes compared to non-KMT2Ar subtypes (p = 0.004). Univariate analysis identified three clinical variables significantly associated with reduced OS (Supplemental Table S6). Subsequent multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis established two independent predictors of shorter OS: FLT3/NF1 co-mutations (HR = 12.39, 95% CI = 1.4-109.46, p = 0.024), WBC ≥ 100 × 109/L at diagnosis (HR = 5.75, 95% CI = 1.33–24.93, p = 0.019) (Fig. 2).

Event-free survival

The 3-year EFS for all enrolled patients was 89.1% ± 1.8% (Supplemental Table S1). Survival analysis identified several significant prognostic factors: age at diagnosis > 10 years (p = 0.02), male gender (p = 0.002), WBC ≥ 100 × 109/L (p < 0.0001), and PLT < 100 × 109/L (p = 0.03) and MRD ≥ 0.01% at day 19 (p = 0.04) were all associated with inferior EFS (Supplemental Table S4). Molecular characterization revealed that FLT3/KRAS co-mutations and ETV6::RUNX1 fusion correlated with favorable outcomes, while KMT2Ar predicted poor prognosis (all p < 0.05, Supplemental Table S4).

As shown in Fig. 1, comparative analysis demonstrated significantly worse 2 year-EFS in patients with FLT3/NF1 co-mutations versus those without (75.0% vs. 92.8% at 2 years; p = 0.0093). When compared to other B-ALL subtypes, FLT3/NF1 co-mutated cases showed inferior outcomes. Notably, the prognosis of FLT3/NF1 co-mutated patients was comparable to KMT2Ar cases (p = 0.716) but significantly worse than non-KMT2Ar patients (p = 0.003).

We subsequently conducted univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses to evaluate prognostic factors (Supplemental Table S6, Fig. 2). Based on previous subtype classifications [24], we further divided our cohort into three prognostic groups: favorable, intermediate, and unfavorable subtype groups which were validated in our Supplemental Table S4. Significant differences in clinical outcomes were observed among the three groups in our cohort (Supplemental Table S6). Among seven variables initially considered for multivariate modeling, four demonstrated independent prognostic significance. The final multivariate model identified MRD ≥ 0.01% at day 19 (HR = 2.34, 95% CI = 1.19–4.61, p = 0.014), molecular risk group (Compared to favorable subtypes, Intermediate subtypes: HR = 3.06, 95% CI = 1.36–6.84, p = 0.007; unfavorable subtypes: HR = 3.98, 95% CI = 1.41–11.26, p = 0.009) as significant predictors of reduced EFS, along with WBC ≥ 100 × 109/L and male gender (Fig. 2). Although FLT3/NF1 co-mutations were significantly associated with inferior EFS in univariable cox regression analysis (HR = 5.41, 95% CI: 1.3-22.58, p = 0.021), they did not emerge as an independent prognostic factor in the multivariable model (HR = 2.82, 95% CI: 0.62–12.80, p = 0.179).

However, apart from NF1 and KRAS, other genes in the RAS pathway, when co-occurring as mutated genes with FLT3, were not significantly associated with either EFS or OS probabilities using Kaplan-Meier methods based on log rank test (Supplemental Table S5).

Univariate and multivariable analysis of risk factors on MRD

Supplemental Table S1 displays the distribution of MRD positivity rates at different threshold levels on Day 19 and Day 46. As shown in Fig. 2 and Supplemental Table S7, multivariable analyses revealed that DUX4 (OR = 4.28, 95% CI = 2.05–8.94, p < 0.0001), ZNF384r (OR = 5.07, 95% CI = 1.41–18.27, p = 0.013), and Ph-like (OR = 7.1, 95% CI 1.81–27.79, p = 0.005) subtypes were independent risk factors for day 19 MRD ≥ 1%. Notably, ETV6::RUNX1 subtype showed consistent protection across all day 19 MRD thresholds (≥ 1%, ≥ 0.1%, and ≥ 0.01%), in contrast to DUX4 which remained a risk factor at all levels (all p < 0.05). Additionally, hemoglobin < 100 g/L at diagnosis (OR = 1.95, 95% CI = 1.13–3.35, p = 0.016), subtype of ETV6::RUNX1 (OR = 0.54, 95% CI = 0.3–0.96, p = 0.035) and Ph-like (OR = 10.04, 95% CI = 1.98–50.95, p = 0.005) were independently associated with Day 19 MRD ≥ 0.1%, while hemoglobin < 100 g/L at diagnosis, subtype of ETV6::RUNX1 were also independent predictors for day 19 MRD ≥ 0.01%, as well as ZNF384r subtype (OR = 4.82, 95% CI = 1.02–22.91, p = 0.05).

For day 46 MRD analysis, limited case numbers restricted evaluation to the ≥ 0.01% threshold, where FLT3/NF1 co-mutations (OR = 9.5, 95% CI = 1.65–54.71), and Ph-like subtype (OR = 6.33, 95% CI = 1.21–33.2, p = 0.03) showed significant independent associations with MRD persistence in multivariable analysis (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Genomic mutations in pediatric B-ALL play an increasingly critical role in defining molecular subtypes, refining risk stratification, and guiding therapeutic strategies [19]. However, certain rare mutation subtypes or combinatorial mutational patterns in B-ALL—along with their clinical implications—remain incompletely characterized. In this cohort of 488 pediatric B-ALL patients, FLT3 mutations and alterations in the RAS pathway were detected in 76 (15.6%) and 192 (39.3%) cases, respectively. Notably, approximately 50% of the FLT3-mutated patients harbored concurrent RAS pathway alterations. Our result shows that RAS pathway mutations occurred in 39.86% cases in China, the frequency in our cohort was consistent with previous studies [25–27]. Intriguingly, the co-occurrence between FLT3 and NF1 mutations was statistically significant and specific, unlike other RAS and FLT3 mutation pairs. Multivariate analysis indicated a potential independent predictive role of FLT3/NF1 co-mutations for adverse OS and Day 46 MRD ≥ 0.01%, although these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size. However, aside from FLT3/NF1 co-mutations, neither FLT3, NF1, nor other RAS pathway genes—either alone or in combination—showed a significant prognostic impact in our analysis.

Given the prognostic significance of the FLT3/NF1 co-mutation, we sought to contextualize it within the broader landscape of FLT3 biology. As mentioned above, FLT3 encodes a class III receptor tyrosine kinase that belongs to the RTK signaling pathway family and plays essential roles in normal and malignant hematopoiesis [28]. FLT3 kinase is typically activated by TKD point mutations or ITDs, with both types showing comparable prevalence in pediatric B-ALL [21, 29, 30]. In our co-mutated subgroup, TKD point mutations were predominant (5 of 6 cases). Although FLT3 inhibitors have shown success in AML [31], their efficacy varies considerably depending on the specific FLT3 mutation type. Supporting this notion, Gutierrez-Camino et al. [21] identified a new activating mutation of TKD that exhibited oncogenic properties and resistance to sorafenib. Therefore, targeted therapies for FLT3-mutated B-ALL are a promising yet underdeveloped area, warranting further investigation [29].

In contrast to FLT3, the role of NF1 mutations in pediatric B-ALL remains poorly characterized, and their clinical implications are largely unexplored [8, 20]. The NF1 gene encodes neurofibromin, a critical negative regulator of the RAS/MAPK signaling pathway. Loss of NF1 function leads to constitutive RAS-GTP activation and hyperstimulation of the RAS-ERK pathway [15]. While NF1 alterations are established drivers in other malignancies, including juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia and AML [32, 33], they are rare in pediatric B-ALL (1.9% in our cohort) and have been rarely studied in this context. Although NF1 mutation alone was not identified as an independent prognostic factor, FLT3/NF1 co‑mutations were significantly associated with worse OS and day 46 MRD positivity in multivariable analysis. This observation raises the possibility of a cooperative interaction, but given the limited sample size, this finding requires validation in larger cohorts. This synergies between NF1 and other genes or signaling pathway has also been established in other malignancies [34, 35]. We propose that this collaboration drives pathogenesis and adverse outcomes, particularly relapse and drug resistance. Furthermore, we observed a high frequency of karyotype abnormalities (5 of 6 cases, 83.3%) in the FLT3/NF1 co-mutated subgroup, albeit without a consistent pattern. This aligns with reports linking NF1 alterations to complex karyotypes in myeloid malignancies [36–38]. The high frequency of karyotype abnormalities in these cases led us to propose that FLT3 and NF1 mutations collectively impair DNA repair and disrupt cell cycle regulation, potentially explaining the poor outcomes in these co-mutated cases.

Beyond genetic subtypes, MRD remains a critical independent prognostic factor in pediatric B-ALL, with MRD-directed strategies significantly improving clinical outcomes [39]. The PTPN11 gene encodes a key protein tyrosine phosphatase that regulates multiple cellular processes including proliferation, differentiation, and oncogenic transformation through its phosphatase activity. Gain-of-function PTPN11 mutations, which are known to drive leukemic cell proliferation and pre-leukemic clone transition [40], were associated with a significantly higher rate of day 19 MRD positivity compared to wild-type cases. This finding may indicate a role for PTPN11 mutations in promoting treatment resistance through sustained proliferative signaling.

B-ALL is characterized by significant heterogeneity, particularly in its genetic subtypes (driven by distinct initiating events), genomic profiles, and clonal architecture. The heterogeneity of the clonal structure is evident in our study. these FLT3 mutations exhibited a broad spectrum of variant allele frequencies ranging from approximately 2% to 30%, indicating substantial clonal heterogeneity within this genetic subgroup, where leukemia cells frequently harbor more than one mutation, making co-mutations a common phenomenon. The limited cohort size precludes a definitive determination of whether FLT3/NF1 co-mutations reside within the same or distinct subclones in pediatric B-ALL. Nevertheless, the significant association between these co-mutations and adverse OS, as well as day 46 MRD positivity, in our cohort suggests a potential clinical relevance. Further research into this heterogeneity is crucial for achieving precision medicine, which aims to tailor individualized treatment strategies based on each patient’s unique genetic profile and clonal structure. Current risk-stratified therapy draws primarily upon genetic subtypes and other clinical features identified at initial diagnosis. Future strategies should consider integrate the complexity of clonal architecture and the presence of specific subclones. This will enable preemptive intervention against subclones with poor prognostic potential and facilitate their dynamic monitoring. If confirmed in larger cohorts and supported by functional studies, the observed association between FLT3/NF1 co-mutations and adverse outcomes may provide a rationale for future exploration of targeted agents, including FLT3 or MEK inhibitors, in this subset of patients.

However, as noted above, neither FLT3, NF1, nor other RAS pathway genes—either alone or in combination—showed a significant prognostic impact in our analysis, a finding consistent with previous studies, although the literature remains inconclusive. Some studies have further investigated the prognostic influence of clonal versus subclonal mutations but reported inconsistent results; for instance, Antic et al. [41] have suggested that neither clonal nor subclonal mutations significantly affect prognosis, whereas others have demonstrated that clonal mutations influence outcomes in standard- and high-risk patients [25]. Nevertheless, across these studies, there is a consensus that subclonal RAS pathway mutations are not significantly associated with prognosis. Due to the limited number of NF1-mutated cases, research on the specific role of NF1 in B-ALL remains lacking. A limitation of our study is the inability to reliably distinguish clonal from subclonal variants using RNA-seq data alone. Therefore, we could not determine whether the observed associations were driven by clonal or subclonal events. Furthermore, given our focus on NF1, the potential impact of nonsense-mediated mRNA decay, which may lead to underestimation of NF1 variant allele frequencies in RNA-seq data, must be considered—particularly considering the low variant allele frequencies observed in our analysis. In addition, the small number of cases resulted in wide confidence intervals, which represents an unavoidable limitation of our study. Future studies employing DNA-based sequencing or single-cell approaches are warranted to further characterize NF1 alterations and FLT3/NF1 co-mutations. Given the high frequency of RAS pathway alterations and the considerable heterogeneity of B-ALL, we hope that our findings may serve as a foundation for future investigations.

Our findings suggest that FLT3 frequently co-occurs with RAS pathway gene mutations, and that FLT3/NF1 co-mutations may be associated with inferior clinical outcomes. However, given the limitations of our study, these findings should be considered preliminary and warrant further validation in larger cohorts and functional studies.
