Utilization of Chalcone for Targeted Removal of Azo Dye From Wastewater
Authors: Cigdem Yorur Goreci, Birol Isik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn this study, a chalcone derivative (BrBP‐Ch), designed as a small molecule adsorbent, was evaluated for the removal of Congo red (CR) dye from aqueous solutions. The adsorbent was characterized using FTIR‐ATR, 1H NMR, 13C NMR, HRMS (Q‐TOF), SEM, XRD, and zeta potential. Optimal conditions were determined as a dosage of 0.04 g/10 mL, a pH of 4, and a contact time of 90 min. The raw data obtained from the adsorption were applied to two‐ and three‐parameter isotherms, and it was observed that the most suitable model was the Sips and Langmuir. The qm values from the Sips and Langmuir were calculated as 10.13 mg/g and 9.819 mg/g, respectively. Kinetic data were applied to pseudo‐first order, pseudo‐second order, intraparticle diffusion, and Boyd models. It was seen that the adsorbent could be reused repeatedly for up to five cycles. The findings reveal that small molecule adsorbents are a promising alternative for wastewater treatment.Keywords: adsorption, Chalcone, Congo red, isotherm, kinetic

Introduction

World‐wide water resources are experiencing significant levels of pollution because of the ongoing intensification of urbanization, industrialization, and even agriculture. For this reason, wastewater recycling and remediation is regarded as a viable option in comparison to identifying and/or finding new sources of clean drinking water. The scarcity of safe drinking water is a global concern since it has an impact on the health of the population as well as the economics of the entire world (Kloster et al. 2025; Paul et al. 2024; Roy et al. 2024). Wastewater from the paper, paint, plastic, and textile industries contains synthetic dyes that can severely disrupt the chemical and biological balance of aquatic systems (Iqbal et al. 2023).

Among these, azo dyes are major contributors to environmental pollution due to their exceptional structural stability. These compounds, which consist of aromatic rings and azo (–N=N–) bonds, can persist in aqueous environments for extended periods, potentially causing long‐lasting ecological impacts. Congo Red (CR) dye is a potent synthetic anionic diazo dye that is particularly beneficial in multiple sectors, especially the textile industry. Its vibrant hue and superior dyeing characteristics render it popular; yet its utilization entails considerable environmental repercussions. As a persistent and hazardous dye, CR presents significant ecological risks when discharged into wastewater. In contrast to CR, most persistent organic pollutants exhibit resistance to biodegradation and tend to accumulate in aquatic ecosystems, presenting potential hazards to aquatic organisms and human health. Exposure to CR has been linked to numerous toxicological effects, including carcinogenicity; thus, the elimination of such dyes from wastewater is a paramount concern (Al‐Omari et al. 2025; Duojie et al. 2024; Long et al. 2023). In this context, CR is widely studied as a model azo dye because of its anionic character and strong resistance to biological degradation (Bayram et al. 2023; Erim 2025; Turna 2025).

Various methods have been developed to remove CR from wastewater; however, most approaches present significant limitations, such as high energy consumption, excessive chemical usage, the formation of toxic by‐products, and challenges in process control (Elhady et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024). These drawbacks underscore the urgent need for alternative, efficient strategies for CR remediation. Adsorption has emerged as a practical and versatile technique for the removal of dyes from aqueous solutions. The efficiency of adsorbing anionic dyes, such as CR, is highly dependent on the chemical and structural properties of the adsorbent. In addition to electrostatic attractions, π–π interactions play a critical role in the adsorption mechanism due to the extensive aromatic framework of the dye molecules (Isik 2025; Sudarsan et al. 2025). Consequently, designing adsorbent materials at the molecular level to maximize these interactions is essential.

Chalcone compounds are structurally versatile molecules characterized by an α, β‐unsaturated carbonyl group positioned between two aromatic rings. This configuration provides conjugated systems capable of interacting with aromatic dyes and facilitates the formation of hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions through the carbonyl group and its substituents (Oter et al. 2024; Shafqat et al. 2020). Although chalcone derivatives are predominantly recognized for their biological activities, their potential in environmental remediation and, specifically, in the adsorption of CR has been explored in only a limited number of studies using natural chalcones (Menezes et al. 2025; Oueslati et al. 2024).

Despite the extensive documentation of various adsorbents—such as activated carbons (Sudarsan et al. 2025), metal–organic frameworks (Kumari et al. 2026), biopolymers (Arslanturk et al. 2026), clays (Kahsay et al. 2025), and functionalized nanoparticles—for the elimination of CR (Deivayanai et al. 2025), many of these systems continue to exhibit restricted selectivity for anionic azo dyes, inadequate π–π interaction potential, expensive synthesis methods, or instability under fluctuating pH conditions. Furthermore, most documented organic adsorbents exhibit an absence of systematic molecular design, especially with the optimization of conjugation length, electron distribution, and substituent effects to improve dye–adsorbent affinity. Conversely, chalcone‐based materials present a distinct advantage owing to their adjustable electronic structure, extensive π‐conjugation, and the potential to incorporate various functional groups that can concurrently enhance π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions with azo dyes. Nonetheless, synthetic chalcone compounds particularly designed for CR adsorption are mostly unexamined, and no comprehensive study has assessed the impact of tailored functionalization on adsorption efficacy. This study introduces a purpose‐designed chalcone derivative that fills a notable gap in the literature, offering a molecularly tailored adsorbent with improved interaction sites and potentially greater CR removal efficacy (Blall et al. 2026; Li et al. 2024; Oueslati et al. 2024; Thottathil et al. 2025).

Although several studies have investigated natural or simply substituted chalcones for dye removal, the adsorption performances reported to date remain limited due to their modest π‐electron density, insufficient structural tunability, and lack of strategically positioned functional groups capable of maximizing dye–adsorbent interactions. Most existing chalcone derivatives used for adsorption are either unmodified natural compounds or minimally substituted analogues, offering restricted control over electronic distribution and interaction sites. In contrast, the chalcone derivative synthesized in this work incorporates a heteroaromatic thiophene unit together with a brominated phenyl moiety, providing an extended π‐framework, enhanced electron delocalization, and multiple interaction domains tailored specifically to strengthen π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, and dipolar interactions with anionic azo dyes such as CR. This rational molecular design distinguishes the present study from previously reported chalcone‐based adsorbents and introduces a structurally engineered chalcone system with improved affinity, selectivity, and mechanistic predictability toward CR adsorption (Blall et al. 2026; Morsy et al. 2025; Oter et al. 2024).

The novelty of this study and the research gap lie in synthesizing a chalcone derivative with various functional groups and evaluating its capacity to remove CR from wastewater via adsorption. The structure of the synthesized chalcone derivative was elucidated by FTIR‐ATR, 1H NMR, 13C NMR, HRMS (Q‐TOF), XRD, and zeta potential. Furthermore, the adsorbent was characterized by pre‐ and post‐adsorption FT‐IR and SEM analyses. The adsorption process was systematically investigated under various experimental conditions, and the interactions between the dye and the chalcone derivative were discussed in detail. The findings are expected to improve the design of next‐generation adsorbents for azo dye removal and expand the applicability of chalcone chemistry in environmental applications.

Experimental Section

Materials and Methods

All chemicals and solvents used in this work were obtained from commercial sources and employed as received, without any additional purification. Reaction progress was routinely monitored by thin‐layer chromatography (TLC) on silica gel 60 F254 plates, with visualization under ultraviolet light. 1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance spectrometer operating at 500 and 125 MHz, respectively, using CDCl3 as the solvent and tetramethylsilane (TMS) as the internal reference. Infrared spectra were acquired using an attenuated total reflectance Fourier‐transform infrared (FT‐IR) spectrometer, and the absorption bands are reported in cm−1. HRMS (Q‐TOF) analysis using an Agilent 6530 LC‐QTOF instrument showed a molecular ion peak at m/z [found]. The morphology of synthesized chalcone derivative before and after CR dye adsorption was investigated by scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis. The crystallinity of adsorbent was determined using X‐ray diffraction (XRD, PANalytical Empyrean) spectroscopy. The zeta potential of adsorbent was determined by Malvern Instruments zetasizer.

Synthesis of Chalcone Derivative (BrBP‐Ch)

5‐Methyl‐2‐thiophenecarboxaldehyde (1.0 mmol) and the corresponding 4′‐(4‐bromophenyl)acetophenone derivative (1.0 mmol) were dissolved in ethanol (20 mL). 10% aqueous NaOH solution (5 mL) was added gradually at room temperature, and the mixture was stirred for 6–8 h (Akin Kazancioglu 2021). Reaction progress was monitored by TLC. The mixture was poured into ice‐cold water, neutralized with dilute HCl, and the precipitate was collected by filtration, washed, and dried. The crude product was purified by recrystallization from ethanol to yield the chalcone derivative (BrBP‐Ch) as a yellow solid. The synthetic pathway was illustrated in Scheme 1. Batch Adsorption Procedure

The removal effectiveness of CR dye was investigated by employing the chalcone derivative BrBP‐Ch as an adsorbent. This was done by employing UV–vis spectrophotometry (Peak Analysis C‐7200) in the range of 200 nm to 800 nm (498 nm for CR dye). The batch adsorption experiment was carried out by distributing the BrBP‐Ch adsorbent while maintaining a constant stirring speed of 120 rpm for a predetermined amount of time. Each experiment utilized 10 mL of freshly made CR dye solution at concentrations ranging from 10 mg/L to 50 mg/L. For a predetermined amount of time, the appropriate amounts of synthesized BrBP‐Ch, ranging from 0.01 g to 0.06 g, were added to each CR dye solution. For determining the removal efficiency, the adsorption experiment was carried out with contact periods ranging from 0 to 180 min. Furthermore, the influence of pH was scrutinized within a range of 4 to 10. To investigate the adsorption thermodynamics, the batch experiments were conducted at 298, 308, and 318 K. Following the conclusion of the experiment, the dye solutions were subjected to centrifugation at a speed of 9000 rpm, and the remaining dye concentration was evaluated with a UV–vis spectrophotometer. Results and Discussion

Characterization of C. officinalis L.

The FT‐IR spectrum of BrBP‐Ch adsorbent before and after CR dye adsorption was illustrated in Figure 1. The molecular structure of BrBP‐Ch was established with certainty based on complementary spectroscopic evidence. The IR spectrum reveals an intense absorption near 1643 cm−1, characteristic of the C=O stretching vibration of an α, β‐unsaturated ketone, while the absence of any aldehyde carbonyl signal confirms that the condensation proceeded to completion. Analysis of the pre‐ and post‐adsorption FT‐IR spectra reveals that CR is adsorbed onto the BrBP‐Ch surface. The post‐adsorption spectrum distinctly displays the characteristic bands of the –SO₃−Na+ groups (1200–1030 cm−1) and the azo group (N=N, ~1600 cm−1) associated with CR; additionally, shifts and alterations in intensity are noted in certain bands of BrBP‐Ch, particularly within the C=O and aromatic regions. The alterations result from the introduction of new vibrations to the spectrum caused by the attachment of dye molecules to the surface, signifying the efficacy of physical adsorption mechanisms such as π–π interactions and hydrogen bonding (Lafi et al. 2019). These data unequivocally affirm the adsorption of CR onto BrBP‐Ch.

Analysis of the 1H NMR data (Figure 2) shows two olefinic protons coupled with a large trans coupling constant (J = 15 Hz), indicating an E‐configured chalcone backbone. Moreover, the 13C NMR spectrum (Figure 3) exhibits a distinct resonance at δ 189.5 ppm corresponding to the carbonyl carbon, together with aromatic and thiophene carbon signals, all supporting the assigned structure.

Figures 4a,b illustrate the SEM images of the BrBP‐Ch adsorbent before and after adsorption, respectively. The SEM image before adsorption (Figure 4a) indicates that the surface exhibits increased roughness, layering, and a pronounced lamellar structure. The holes, fissures, and uneven porous architecture on the surface indicate the original open and accessible morphology of the adsorbent. Upon examination of SEM image after adsorption (Figure 4b), it is observed that the surface has grown markedly denser, more compact, and partially filled. The acuity of the lamellar structures has diminished, and a deposit‐like layer has developed on the surface. This alteration signifies that CR dye molecules have attached to the surface, occupying the gaps and modifying the surface shape in a coating fashion. Consequently, the SEM images indicate that the adsorbent surface was coated with after dye adsorption, confirming the efficacy of the adsorption process.

The SEM images of BrBP‐Ch adsorbent before (a) and after (b) CR dye adsorption.

Zeta potential (ζ) represents a direct measure of interfacial electrostatic behavior, providing insight into the surface charge characteristics and dispersion stability of adsorbent systems in aqueous environments, where the magnitude of ζ is commonly correlated with colloidal stability and interaction propensity (Delgado et al. 2007). In this study, the chalcone derivative (BrBP‐Ch) synthesized from 5‐methylthiophene‐2‐carbaldehyde and 4′‐(4‐bromophenyl)acetophenone exhibited a ζ value of approximately −27 mV (Figure S2), indicating a moderately high negative surface potential and sufficient dispersion stability. Chalcones (1,3‐diaryl‐2‐propen‐1‐ones) are π‐conjugated organic frameworks widely recognized for their structural versatility and strong intermolecular interaction capabilities, where heteroaromatic incorporation and halogen substitution play a significant role in modulating electron density distribution, dipole moment, and surface charge behavior (Batovska and Todorova 2010; Morsy and Hassan 2022; Nowakowska 2007; Sinha et al. 2024; Zhuang et al. 2017). Reported chalcone‐based systems exhibit a wide ζ potential range, from weakly negative values (−7 to −15 mV) in nanoparticle conjugates to strongly negative values (−35 to −40 mV) in nanoemulsion systems, emphasizing the sensitivity of electrokinetic properties to molecular architecture and surrounding environment (de Mattos et al. 2015; Sinha et al. 2024). The measured −27 mV thus reflects a structurally induced intermediate‐to‐high negative surface charge arising from the combined electronic contributions of the thiophene and brominated biphenyl moieties. Notably, in the adsorption of CR, an anionic azo dye bearing sulfonate groups, electrostatic repulsion is expected; however, adsorption proceeds effectively via dominant π–π stacking interactions between the conjugated chalcone backbone and the aromatic domains of the dye, supplemented by hydrophobic and dipole–dipole interactions (Dutta et al. 2021). These observations collectively demonstrate that adsorption efficiency in π‐conjugated chalcone systems is governed not solely by surface charge, but by the synergy between molecular electronic structure and non‐covalent interaction mechanisms, thereby highlighting the effectiveness of the designed chalcone derivative as a functional adsorbent for azo dye removal.

The XRD pattern of BrBP‐Ch chalcone derivative was illustrated in Figure 5. The XRD pattern of the BrBP‐Ch chalcone derivative displays numerous crisp, strong, and well‐defined reflections within the 10–30° (2θ) range, signifying a highly crystalline structure of the compound. Notably, pronounced diffraction peaks at around 12–15°, 18–22°, and 24–28° indicate the systematic organization of conjugated aromatic rings and the α, β‐unsaturated carbonyl system, a distinctive structural attribute of chalcone derivatives. This crystalline form is characteristic of chalcones, as planar π‐conjugated frameworks promote dense molecule packing and π‐π stacking interactions. These findings robustly endorse the hypothesis that BrBP‐Ch exhibits a well‐structured crystal lattice typical of chalcone derivatives. Moreover, large yet discernible low‐density patterns at elevated angles (35–50°) indicate the existence of secondary lattice planes linked to substituent‐induced torsional configurations. The heteroaromatic thiophene ring and brominated phenyl groups in BrBP‐Ch facilitate the emergence of supplementary weak reflections, indicating minor aberrations in the crystal packing while preserving overall crystallinity (Jadhav et al. 2017; Patil et al. 2007; Ravindrachary et al. 2005).

Influence of Operational Parameters

The effect of BrBP‐Ch adsorbent on the removal of CR dye was assessed by varying the BrBP‐Ch dosage: BrBP‐Ch—0.01‐0.06 g/10 mL. The findings were illustrated in Figure 6a. Increasing the BrBP‐Ch dosage progressively enhances adsorption until a specific threshold is reached. The maximum clearance rate of 92.3% was recorded for BrBP‐Ch at a dosage of 0.04 g/10 mL. Exceeding the optimum dose, the removal efficiency stabilized, signifying the saturation of accessible adsorption sites for CR dye attachment. On the other hand, a gradual decrease in adsorption capacity was observed, which may be due to agglomeration or overlap of the active moieties at high dosages. Increasing the BrBP‐Ch dosage from 0.01 g/10 mL to 0.06 g/10 mL reduced the adsorption capacity from 45.5 mg/g to 15.53 mg/g. Considering all these results, the optimum BrBP‐Ch dosage was determined to be 0.04 g/10 mL. Comparable results were noted when reviewing the literature (Akgul et al. 2024; Kamalesh et al. 2025; Sismanoglu et al. 2023). The research by Farasati Far et al. examined the adsorption of CR dye at varying concentrations of nanoclay/chitosan‐graft‐gelatin nanocomposite hydrogel adsorbent (0.5–4 g/L). In this study, it was noted that the optimal dose corresponds to the level at which the adsorbent attained saturation, corroborating our findings (Farasati Far et al. 2024).

The effect of various physicochemical parameters on CR dye adsorption onto BrBP‐Ch adsorbent: (a) BrBP‐Ch dosage, (b) initial pH, (c) initial concentration, and (d) contact time.

The pH is a crucial factor influencing surface adsorption and adsorption capacity in dye wastewater (Dizgah et al. 2025; Sen et al. 2024). The impact of employing the synthesized BrBP‐Ch adsorbent for the removal of CR dye solution across a pH range of 4 to 10 was examined. Figure 6b displays the results. The optimal removal rate of CR dye at pH 4 was determined to be 92.3% using the synthesized BrBP‐Ch adsorbent. The removal percentage diminished progressively as pH rose to 10, yielding removal percentages of 84.2%, 64.3%, 43.2%, and 22.3% for pH levels of 6, 7, 8, and 10, respectively. The functional groups on the BrBP‐Ch adsorbent ionize at the optimal pH of 4, resulting in a robust electrostatic interaction between the negatively charged anionic CR dye molecules and the positively charged adsorbent surface. As pH levels rise, the quantity of negatively charged sites on the adsorbent escalates. Most dyes demonstrate reduced adsorption levels at acidic conditions due to the prevalence of hydroxide ions at alkaline pH, which destabilize anionic dyes and compete with dye anions for adsorption sites on the adsorbent. The zeta potential measurement of BrBP‐Ch (ζ = −27 mV at neutral pH) indicates that the adsorbent possesses a substantially negative surface charge under standard conditions. The negative ζ value indicates that the surface is protonated solely in adequately acidic environments, where partial neutralization or a positive charge may arise from the protonation of heteroatoms in the chalcone structure. This elucidates the markedly enhanced adsorption at pH 4, where augmented protonation of carbonyl oxygen, thiophene sulfur, and aromatic substituents diminishes electrostatic resistance, facilitating adsorption predominantly via π–π stacking and hydrophobic interactions. As the pH rises, the surface increasingly acquires a negative charge (aligning with the measured ζ value), resulting in enhanced electrostatic repulsion between CR and anionic sulfonate groups, hence diminishing adsorption efficacy. Consequently, the incorporation of zeta potential data substantiates the notion that electrostatic effects influence adsorption without prevailing; rather, the process is regulated by π–π stacking and non‐electrostatic interactions characteristic of conjugated chalcone systems (Al‐Amrani et al. 2022; Khan et al. 2024).

This study examined the impact of the initial concentration of CR dye on its removal from the BrBP‐Ch adsorbent. The experimental study was performed within a concentration range of 10 to 50 mg/L. Figure 6c illustrates that the removal effectiveness of the BrBP‐Ch adsorbent declined from 95.3% to 70.7%. The results demonstrate that elevating the initial dye concentration results in a reduction of dye removal effectiveness. At diminished concentrations, CR dye efficiently adheres to the adsorbent surface owing to the plentiful availability of active sites. As the dye concentration rises, a finite number of active sites become saturated, so restricting additional adsorption. Increased dye concentration results in diminished accessibility of the dye to active sites and a reduction in dye release from the solution (Alamri et al. 2025; Duhan et al. 2025). On the other hand, it has been demonstrated that the ability of the BrBP‐Ch adsorbent to adsorb CR grows relatively quickly as the initial concentration increases. This capacity then stabilizes to a degree that corresponds to the saturation of the active sites of the adsorbents (El‐Habacha et al. 2024).

The contact time between the CR dye and the BrBP‐Ch adsorbent significantly affects the adsorption efficiency. Figure 6d shows the removal of CR dye at different temperatures and contact times. The BrBP‐Ch adsorbent exhibited adsorption efficiencies of 92.3%, 95.32%, and 97.76% at 298, 308, and 318 K temperatures, respectively, with an initial CR dye concentration of 20 mg/L for a contact time of 180 min. The adsorption process for the adsorbent exhibits a three‐phase behavior, starting with a high initial rate in the first few minutes, followed by a slowdown in the growth rate, and reaching equilibrium at the end of the experiment. The BrBP‐Ch adsorbent demonstrated superior adsorption efficiency with the absorption of a significant amount of CR dye in the first few minutes. After the initial rapid phase, adsorption slowed down considerably and eventually reached equilibrium. The reason for the high initial performance is the presence of a significant amount of active site on the surface of the BrBP‐Ch adsorbent. However, over time, vacant spots remain unoccupied or unabsorbed due to repulsive interactions of the CR dye with the solid‐phase volumes. Equilibrium was reached in approximately 90 min at all temperature values. The adsorption capacity increased from 9.819 mg/g to 11.85 mg/g with increasing temperature from 298 K to 318 K, indicating that the process occurs endothermically at increasing temperatures for the BrBP‐Ch adsorbent (Beigi et al. 2023; Rose et al. 2023). Similar behaviors were likewise noted for the adsorption of CR dye on native pumice stone, pumice stone altered with cetyl trimethyl ammonium bromide, and modified banana leaves (Rose et al. 2023; Shayesteh et al. 2016).

Adsorption Isotherms

Adsorption isotherm graphs were employed to elucidate adsorption effectiveness and the interaction between adsorbent and adsorbate. Figure 7a depicts the isotherm curves that demonstrate the correlation between equilibrium concentration and adsorption capacity. The isotherm curve indicates that the adsorption process adheres to L2 type adsorption at all temperature values as per Giles' isotherm classification (Giles et al. 1974). The adsorption capacity escalates with increasing concentration and attains saturation after a specific threshold. This effectively elucidates the Langmuir‐type adsorption process (Bekhoukh et al. 2025; Isik et al. 2023).

The isotherm curves of CR dye adsorption onto BrBP‐Ch adsorbent (a) and non‐linear isotherm model curves: (b) Langmuir, (c) Freundlich, (d) D‐R, (e) Temkin, (f) Sips, (g) Hill, and (h) Koble‐Corrigan.

This study elucidates the relationship between adsorbate and adsorbent while investigating the adsorption mechanism in greater detail by employing two‐parameter nonlinear isotherm models, including Langmuir (Langmuir 1918) (Figure 7b), Freundlich (Freundlich 1906) (Figure 7c), Dubinin‐Radushkevich (D‐R) (Chu et al. 2024) (Figure 7d), and Temkin (Temkin and Pyzhev 1940) (Figure 7e), as well as three‐parameter nonlinear isotherm models such as Sips (Sips 1948) (Figure 7f), Hill (Hill 1910) (Figure 7g), and Koble‐Corrigan (Koble and Corrigan 1952) (Figure 7h) on the raw data. The equations of all isotherm models were given in the supporting information file (Table S1). These models typically correlate equilibrium concentration with adsorption capacity at a specific temperature.

The Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms are two prevalent mathematical models that elucidate the correlation between the equilibrium concentration of the adsorbate and the equivalent quantity adsorbed by the adsorbent. Experimental findings demonstrated strong concordance (r2=0.9967) with the Langmuir isotherm, presuming a monolayer coverage of equally distributed active sites devoid of interactions among adsorbed molecules (Al‐Omari et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2023). The computed maximum adsorption capacity (qm) attained a value of 9.819 mg/g, as indicated in Table 1, underscoring the prospective efficacy of the synthesized adsorbent material for effective CR removal. Furthermore, it was observed that as the temperature increased from 298 K to 318 K, the qm value increased from 9.819 mg/g to 11.85 mg/g, which indicates the endothermic nature of the adsorption process. On the other hand, the fact that the Langmuir isotherm constant KL is in the range of 0–1 and the Freundlich isotherm constant “n” is in the range of 1–10 indicates that the adsorption process of the CR dye onto the BrBP‐Ch adsorbent occurred favorably. Additionally, the error functions in Table S1 were computed based on experimental and theoretical qe values for the isotherm models, with the results presented in Table 2. Table 2 demonstrates that the error functions of the Langmuir isotherm model are significantly lower than those of the Freundlich isotherm model, indicating that the Langmuir isotherm model is a more appropriate model for the adsorption process than the Freundlich model.

Numerous adsorbents were documented for the elimination of CR dye. Table 3 presents a comparison between the described adsorbent and BrBP‐Ch, which was examined as an adsorbent for the elimination of CR dye. The analysis of these data indicates that the newly synthesized adsorbent had the potential for CR dye elimination. While the qm value of BrBP‐Ch at 298 K (9.82 mg/g) is inferior to many inorganic or carbon‐based adsorbents documented in the literature (Table 3), this constraint must be understood in the context of the material's molecular structure and intended design purpose. Temperature‐dependent tests indicated that the adsorption capacity rose to 11.85 mg/g at 318 K, signifying that the adsorption process is endothermic and that the efficacy of BrBP‐Ch enhances at elevated temperatures. This increase indicates that the intrinsic π‐conjugated structure and functional groups of the chalcone derivative are more effective in interacting with CR at elevated thermal energy. Moreover, in contrast to numerous traditional adsorbents like activated carbons, metal oxides, or composite materials, BrBP‐Ch can be produced using a straightforward, low‐energy, and metal‐free process, thereby substantially diminishing manufacturing expenses and environmental repercussions. It's entirely organic, adjustable structure, extensive π‐systems, and replaceable functional groups facilitating π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions provide considerable benefits in selectivity vs. anionic azo dyes (Oueslati et al. 2024). Consequently, although its adsorption capacity at ambient temperature is moderate, its enhanced efficacy at elevated temperatures, economical synthesis, structural adaptability, high selectivity, and sustainability indicate that BrBP‐Ch presents a competitive and eco‐friendly alternative to numerous documented adsorbents.

The D‐R isotherm was originally formulated to empirically represent the adsorption of subcritical vapors onto microporous solids through a pore‐filling mechanism, positing that the adsorption process pertains to the occupancy of micropore volume rather than layer‐by‐layer adsorption on porous surfaces, thereby elucidating the porosity of the adsorbents. This method can delineate the adsorption mechanism characterized by Gaussian energy distribution on heterogeneous surfaces and is often applicable to the intermediate range of adsorbate concentrations, owing to its exhibited asymptotic nature (Gupta et al. 2023; Kloster et al. 2025; Vinayagam et al. 2023). The r2 parameter (0.9975) indicates that this model adequately fits the experimental data for CR dye adsorption on BrBP‐Ch adsorbent; however, upon examining the error function values, it is less applicable than the Langmuir and Freundlich models, although the D‐R model should not be disregarded in the results. The ED−R value, as indicated in Table 1, was determined to be 1.511–2.636 kJ/mol at different temperatures. The D‐R isotherm model suggests that the adsorption process may occur physically if the ED−R value is below 8 kJ/mol. Based on this value, it can be concluded that the adsorption of CR dye onto the BrBP‐Ch adsorbent occurs by a physical mechanism.

According to the Temkin isotherm, the free energy of adsorption is assumed to be a function of the surface coat in its most fundamental essence (Mahmud and Benamor 2023; Sayed et al. 2025). To consider the interactions that take place between CR molecules and the BrBP‐Ch adsorbent, the model incorporates many aspects. With a correlation coefficient of 0.9999, it is evident that the Temkin isotherm is likewise compatible with the experimental data that was acquired. This is evident from both Figure 7e and Table 1. It was determined that when BrBP‐Ch adsorbent is used for CR adsorption, it has a binding energy in the range of 1.111–1.272 kJ/mol in the temperature range of 298–318 K. As a result of the fact that the binding energy is lower than 8 kJ/mol, it is possible to propose that the adsorption process is carried out in a physisorption manner. The statistics from the D‐R isotherm are also supported by this. It is therefore possible to draw the conclusion, based on the findings, that the CR dye is adsorbed onto the BrBP‐Ch adsorbent by the process of physical adsorption. For interpreting the experimental data that was observed, all three isotherms can be utilized.

Compared to the Langmuir and Freundlich models, the Sips isotherm model is a more popular choice because it is an improved empirical formula of the two models. According to the non‐homogeneity of the adsorbent, the value of “m” is utilized to reflect this. In general, the homogeneity of the adsorbent surface increases in proportion to the degree to which the “m” value approaches 1. To conduct an accurate analysis of the adsorption process, the correlation coefficients of the Sips (Figure 7g) and Langmuir (Figure 7b) models are quite high. With a “m” value that is somewhat close to 1 (0.8651–0.9278), the Sips isotherm model likewise possesses a high degree of fit. The findings made it clear that the surface of the adsorbent is homogeneous, and that the Sips model can be reduced to the Langmuir model for increased simplicity. Accordingly, the Sips model and the Langmuir model are both suitable for use in this experiment due to their respective applicability (Djaber et al. 2025; Zhao et al. 2023).

The Hill isotherm is commonly employed to characterize the binding of adsorbates to the surface of a uniform substrate (Kueté et al. 2026; Lincold et al. 2024). This isotherm indicates that the adsorption of a ligand at one adsorbent site can influence other sites on the adsorbent; if nH > 1, there is cooperative adsorption between the ligand and other sites; if nH = 1, the adsorption is non‐cooperative; if nH < 1, the cooperation is antagonistic. For the BrBP‐Ch adsorbent, this value is around 1 (nH = 0.8651–1.005), signifying that the adsorption of each CR molecule is autonomous and does not influence other locations on the adsorbent. The correlation coefficient of this model is around one (0.9973), and the error functions are minimal; thus, it accurately characterizes the adsorption process onto the surface of the BrBP‐Ch adsorbent.

The Koble‐Corrigan model, which integrates the Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms, demonstrates versatility across different concentrations (Ismail et al. 2025; Liang et al. 2025). The Koble‐Corrigan model has optimal fit for dyes, evidenced by an r2 value of 0.9986 and minimal error function values, indicating a high capacity and affinity for dye molecules during the adsorption process. Examining the error functions in Table 2, the order of applicability of the isotherm models for the adsorption of CR dye onto BrBP‐Ch adsorbent was found to be as follows: Sips = Koble‐Corrigan>Hill > Langmuir>Freundlich > Temkin > D‐R.

If the graph of qt against t0.5 is linear, the adsorption mechanism is exclusively governed by intraparticle diffusion. Conversely, if the data exhibit numerous straight graphs, alternative factors govern the sorption process. The intraparticle diffusion graphs for the uptake of CR by the BrBP‐Ch adsorbent are linear and do not intersect the origin, indicating that intraparticle diffusion is not the rate‐limiting phase in the overall sorption process. The adsorption process may take alternative routes (Peter et al. 2025; Zauška et al. 2025). As can be seen in Figure 8c, the adsorption process that is generated by modifying the diffusion equation may be broken down into three distinct stages. There is a correlation between the initial orthogonal linear stage and the diffusion adsorption stage. During this stage, dye molecules from the solution largely diffuse to the surface of the adsorbents, which is where the membrane diffusion process takes place. A large reduction in the number of surface adsorption sites, which in turn leads to a fall in the adsorption rate, is one of the characteristics of the second stage, which is a relatively mild linear stage. The ultimate equilibrium stage is represented by the third stage, which is the stage in which the rate of particle diffusion steadily declines and eventually reaches equilibrium. This drop in speed is brought about by the fact that most of the dye molecules from the solution are adsorbed, which leads to a reduction in the concentration of the substances that are adsorbed (Subbaiah Munagapati et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2024).

A further investigation into the question of whether intraparticle diffusion or film diffusion is the rate‐limiting stage in the overall adsorption process was carried out with the use of the Boyd model. When attempting to determine the adsorption mechanism, the graph of Bt vs. t can be the tool of choice. It is possible that intraparticle diffusion will be the rate‐limiting phase if the graph of Bt vs. t is linear and passes through the origin. If this is not the case, then film diffusion will be the step that controls the overall rate. As can be observed in Figure 8d, the graphs of Bt against t for CR adsorption by BrBP‐Ch are linear, but they do not pass through the origin. This demonstrates that film diffusion is the step that is responsible for the rate‐limiting effect (Mahmood et al. 2025).

Possible Adsorption Mechanism

The potential adsorption mechanism of CR dye onto the BrBP‐Ch adsorbent is depicted in Figure 9. The adsorption properties of CR onto BrBP‐Ch suggest a mechanism primarily driven by various physical interactions, supported by FT‐IR and SEM studies. The presence of CR's distinctive –SO₃ − and N=N stretching bands in the post‐adsorption FT‐IR spectra, along with significant shifts in the C=O and aromatic vibration regions of BrBP‐Ch, substantiates that the dye exhibits substantial interaction with the surface and engenders novel vibrational modes. The spectral alterations indicate predominant π–π stacking between the azo‐linked aromatic structure of CR and the extended conjugated rings of BrBP‐Ch, augmented by hydrogen bonding between CR's amino/sulfonate groups and the carbonyl and N–H functionalities of the adsorbent, along with potential n–π* interactions.

Possible adsorption mechanism of CR dye adsorption onto BrBP‐Ch adsorbent.

SEM images corroborate this approach, revealing enhanced surface density and partial occlusion of lamellar gaps, suggesting that CR molecules establish a deposited layer on and within the adsorbent surface. The integration of the zeta potential values (ζ ≈ −27 mV at neutral pH) provides further insight into the adsorption mechanism. Despite the negatively charged surface of BrBP‐Ch theoretically encouraging electrostatic repulsion with the anionic sulfonate groups of CR, the significant adsorption observed at acidic pH suggests that surface protonation at low pH decreases the net negative charge density, thereby lessening electrostatic repulsion and facilitating π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding predominance. As the pH rises, the surface acquires a greater negative charge, enhancing electrostatic repulsion and resulting in a progressively diminished adsorption effectiveness. The cumulative evidence suggests that the adsorption of CR onto BrBP‐Ch is predominantly influenced by π–π interactions, hydrogen bonding, and n–π* interactions, whereas the adsorbent's moderately negative zeta potential and pH‐dependent surface protonation regulate the degree of electrostatic effects. The open and porous structure of BrBP‐Ch enhances molecule interactions, leading to a multilayer adsorption process guided by physical principles.

Reusability

Figure 11 illustrates the reusability of the BrBP‐Ch adsorbent for the adsorption of CR. The reusability analysis of BrBP‐Ch indicates that the adsorbent retains a significant fraction of its adsorption efficacy during five successive cycles, with removal efficiency declining from 92.3% in the initial cycle to 65.4% in the fifth cycle. This slow reduction might be ascribed to many variables typically encountered in organic adsorbent systems. Initially, poor desorption of CR during regeneration may result in the gradual occupation or obstruction of active π–π interaction sites and hydrogen‐bonding areas, thus diminishing the availability of accessible adsorption sites in following cycles. Secondly, repeated adsorption–desorption cycles may cause minor structural or morphological alterations on the adsorbent surface, including decreased surface roughness or partial pore compaction, which might impair dye accessibility. Third, competing interactions between CR residues and solvent molecules during the ethanol‐water regeneration phase may hinder the complete recovery of surface functional groups. Notwithstanding this mild decline, BrBP‐Ch's capacity to maintain over 65% of its performance after five cycles demonstrates significant regeneration efficiency and structural integrity. This sustainability corresponds with the reversible, physical characteristics of the adsorption mechanism, as evidenced by FT‐IR and XRD measurements, which confirm that BrBP‐Ch stays chemically unaltered and reusable, exhibiting only negligible performance degradation across successive operating cycles.

The reusability of BrBP‐Ch adsorbent.

The XRD pattern of BrBP‐Ch following CR adsorption and subsequent reuse cycles (Figure 12a) demonstrates that the distinctive sharp reflections present in the original material are predominantly maintained, especially in the 20–28° range; this suggests that the fundamental crystal structure of the chalcone derivative remains unaltered. The minor decreases in peak intensities and modest broadening result from the deposition of CR molecules on the crystal surface and the partial occupancy of lamellar voids, which alters the local order without compromising the primary crystal structure. The lack of additional diffraction peaks or notable peak changes during several adsorption–desorption cycles verifies that BrBP‐Ch remains free from phase transformation or structural degradation during reuse.

Limitations and Future Research

It is important to realize that the BrBP‐Ch chalcone derivative that was synthesized exhibited promising adsorption performance and good reusability. However, there are certain limitations that should be taken into consideration. First, the maximum adsorption capacity is still rather low when compared to that of other high‐surface‐area inorganic and carbon‐based adsorbents. This suggests that more structural optimization, such as the incorporation of electron‐rich or polar substituents, may be able to further improve adsorption performance. The adsorption mechanism was validated by FT‐IR, SEM, XRD, and zeta potential investigations. However, more advanced surface‐specific techniques such as XPS or solid‐state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) could provide a more in‐depth understanding of the interactions between the dye and the adsorbent, as well as confirm the type of surface binding sites. Third, despite the fact that the adsorbent is able to maintain an efficiency of more than 65% after five cycles of regeneration, there is a need for further exploration into its long‐term stability beyond five cycles and regeneration under alternative desorption environmental circumstances. In conclusion, this work was carried out in a laboratory setting that was under strict control. As a result, the evaluation of the performance of BrBP‐Ch in continuous‐flow systems, in the presence of competing contaminants, or by making use of industrial wastewater streams is an essential direction for future research. In order to facilitate the rational design of next‐generation chalcone‐based adsorbents with better capacity, selectivity, and operational resilience for wastewater treatment applications, it is necessary to address these limitations.

Conclusions

In this study, the chalcone derivative BrBP‐Ch demonstrated high efficiency in removing Congo red from aqueous solutions through a combination of π–π, hydrogen bonding, and n–π interactions. The results highlight several key advantages of chalcone‐based adsorbents: only a small amount is required for effective dye removal, they are naturally occurring bioactive molecules, and their structures are highly amenable to modification, allowing for the design of molecules specifically engineered for pollutants. Overall, this study demonstrates that small molecule chalcone derivatives represent a promising, sustainable, and versatile alternative to conventional polymeric or composite adsorbents for wastewater treatment applications.
