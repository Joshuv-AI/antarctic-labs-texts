Genome-wide association and polygenic risk score analyses of MASLD-related hepatic steatosis severity in a Taiwanese Han population
Authors: Ting-Yuan Liu, Jai-Sing Yang, Yu-Chia Chen, Shih-Chang Tsai, Yu-Jen Chiu, Chi-Chou Liao, Yen‐Ting Chang, Woei-Cheang Shyu, Long-Bin Jeng, Fuu-Jen Tsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatic steatosis is a highly prevalent metabolic condition and a major contributor to chronic liver disease, particularly in Asian populations. This study was conducted to identify genetic variants associated with the severity of ultrasound-defined hepatic steatosis in a Taiwanese Han population and evaluate the variants’ biological relevance through cross-ancestry meta-analysis and functional validation.MethodsWe conducted a genome-wide association study (GWAS) of 133,895 individuals with data in the Genetic Biobank of China Medical University Hospital. Hepatic steatosis severity was graded on an ordinal scale (0–3) using standardised ultrasonographic criteria. The polygenic risk score (PRS) under continuous shrinkage (CS) approach was used to construct a PRS for genetic risk stratification across severity grades.Findings:In the GWAS, we identified 1,229 single-nucleotide polymorphisms associated with hepatic steatosis severity (P < 1 × 10⁻5), with the strongest signals discovered at the PNPLA3 locus (rs738408 and rs738409). Background

Fatty liver disease (FLD), now classified under the broader category of steatotic liver diseases, can manifest as metabolic-dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), alcohol-related liver disease, or metabolic dysfunction and alcohol-associated liver disease [1]. Hepatic steatosis, characterised by excessive lipid accumulation within hepatocytes, is the core pathological feature of steatotic liver diseases and remains the most common chronic liver condition worldwide [1, 2]. Since 2023, international consensus statements have replaced the term nonalcoholic FLD (NAFLD) with MASLD to better reflect the central role of metabolic dysfunction and to avoid the stigma associated with the term ‘nonalcoholic’ [2]. Compared with NAFLD, MASLD is defined as hepatic steatosis in a person with at least one cardiometabolic risk factor.

The estimated global prevalence of hepatic steatosis is approximately 25–30% in the adult population, with rates exceeding 30% in many Asian countries, including Taiwan [3–6]. In Asia, the reported prevalence of NAFLD or MASLD is approximately 30%, and population-based studies in Taiwan have estimated the prevalence at approximately 36% [3–6]. Data derived from health examination cohorts in Taiwan further suggest that nearly 40% of adults may fulfil the criteria for MASLD [7]. MASLD frequently develops alongside metabolic comorbidities, including obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidaemia, and hypertension, contributing to increased risks of cardiovascular disease and liver-related morbidity [8]. Notably, a growing proportion of individuals with hepatic steatosis in Asia exhibit a ‘lean MASLD’ phenotype, characterised by normal body mass index despite clear evidence of metabolic dysregulation [9].

Under the updated nomenclature, individuals with hepatic steatosis and metabolic dysfunction are classified as having MASLD, whereas those with additional hepatocellular injury and inflammation are further categorised as having metabolic-dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis [10]. In clinical practice, providers assess a patient’s clinical history, metabolic risk factors [8, 9, 11], serum biomarkers of liver injury [12, 13], and results from imaging modalities (e.g., ultrasonography, computed tomography, or magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]) [14–16] to diagnose hepatic steatosis. Abdominal ultrasonography is widely adopted as a noninvasive and cost-effective tool in routine health examinations that enables large-scale assessment of hepatic fat accumulation in asymptomatic populations [17].

Hepatic steatosis is a multifactorial trait influenced by both environmental exposure and genetic susceptibility. Heritability estimates indicate that genetic factors account for a substantial proportion of interindividual variability in liver fat content and disease risk [18, 19].

Through genome-wide association studies (GWASs), multiple loci have been identified as contributors to hepatic steatosis and MASLD, including variants in PNPLA3, TM6SF2, GCKR, SAMM50, and PARVB [20–23]. Among these, the PNPLA3 I148M variant (rs738409) has consistently demonstrated the strongest genetic effect on hepatic fat accumulation across diverse populations [24]. Despite the rapidly increasing prevalence of MASLD throughout Asia, East Asian populations remain underrepresented in current GWAS datasets [25]. Furthermore, East Asians frequently develop hepatic steatosis and MASLD at lower body mass index levels than Western populations, suggesting potential differences in disease susceptibility and underlying genetic architecture [26]. Previous studies have also demonstrated ethnic differences in allele frequencies and effect sizes of established risk variants, including PNPLA3, highlighting the importance of conducting ancestry-specific genetic investigations [27]. Large-scale studies evaluating the genetic determinants of hepatic steatosis severity in Taiwanese Han individuals remain scarce [7, 19]. The Taiwanese Han population represents a relatively homogeneous East Asian population with limited genetic substructure compared with multi-ancestry cohorts, thereby reducing potential confounding due to population stratification and facilitating the identification of both shared and population-specific genetic factors associated with disease progression [28]. Therefore, investigating hepatic steatosis in a large Taiwanese Han cohort may provide important insights into the genetic basis of MASLD and improve the development of ancestry-informed risk prediction models.

Therefore, in this study, we performed a GWAS of ultrasound-defined hepatic steatosis severity in 133,895 Taiwanese Han participants from the China Medical University Hospital (CMUH) Genetic Biobank. To enhance the generalizability of our findings, we further conducted cross-ancestry meta-analyses using data from the Million Veteran Program (MVP) and the UK Biobank. In addition, we developed a polygenic risk score (PRS) to evaluate the utility of genetic risk stratification across steatosis severity grades and performed functional validation of the PNPLA3 I148M variant in hepatocyte models.

Materials and methods

Study design, data sources, and quality control

Electronic medical records from CMUH were used to identify individuals who underwent abdominal ultrasonography between 2003 and 2020. Hepatobiliary and gastroenterological specialists performed standardised ultrasound examinations to assess hepatic steatosis, which was graded on the basis of routine clinical ultrasound reports through a semiquantitative system comprising grades 0 (no evidence of hepatic steatosis), 0.5 (suspected hepatic steatosis), 1 (mild hepatic steatosis), 2 (moderate hepatic steatosis), and 3 (severe hepatic steatosis). Individuals with grade 0 on abdominal ultrasound were defined as the control group; those with grades 0.5–3 were classified as having hepatic steatosis. For individuals who underwent multiple abdominal ultrasound examinations during the study period, the maximum steatosis grade recorded was used to define disease status and severity. To improve diagnostic validity, only individuals with at least three ultrasound-based hepatic steatosis diagnoses recorded at CMUH were included. Individuals with an autoimmune liver disease (including autoimmune hepatitis, primary biliary cholangitis, and primary sclerosing cholangitis), hereditary hemochromatosis, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, Wilson disease, hepatitis B or C virus infection, type 2 diabetes mellitus, or dyslipidaemia were excluded from the ultrasound-defined control group (grade 0) to minimise misclassification of metabolically or pathologically driven hepatic steatosis in individuals without imaging evidence of fatty liver. This study was conducted under the CMUH Precision Medicine Project and approved by the institutional review board (approval numbers CMUH110-REC3-005 and CMUH111-REC1-176).

For each individual, clinical and genetic data were collected from the Genetic Biobank of CMUH, including age, sex, diagnostic codes, genotypes generated with the TPMv1 custom single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) array, abdominal ultrasound examination results, and levels of total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), gamma-glutamyl transferase, alanine transaminase, aspartate transferase, and haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c). After quality control and imputation, we performed a GWAS. The genetic data were subjected to a series of standard quality-control procedures. Variants with a call rate below 90% and individuals with more than 10% missing genotypes were excluded. Variants deviating from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (P < 1 × 10−6) or with a minor allele frequency (MAF) below 1E-04 were removed. Samples with outlier heterozygosis (more than three times the interquartile range) were excluded. Population structure was assessed through principal component analysis, and individuals who did not cluster with an East Asian population in the 1000 Genomes Project were removed. After imputation, variants with an imputation INFO score < 0.3 or genotype probability < 0.9 were filtered out to ensure reliable association testing [29, 30]. Analyses of association between SNPs and hepatic steatosis severity were conducted using PLINK version 1.90. Manhattan and quantile–quantile plots were created with the qqman package in R version 4.1.0, run within the RStudio integrated development environment [31].

Biological network analysis

Summary statistics from the GWAS were used to perform biological network analysis, with a significance threshold of P < 1 × 10 − 5 being used. A total of 1,229 SNPs associated with hepatic steatosis were included in the network analyses. Molecular interaction networks were generated with the Core Analysis module of Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA; Qiagen Sciences, Hilden, Germany; version 107,193,442). The statistical significance of each network was defined as a negative log of P > 1, as measured through Fisher’s exact test [30].

GTEx eQTL analysis

Expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) information was obtained from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) Project (v8). Summary-level eQTL statistics for PNPLA3 were extracted for adipose visceral (omentum) tissue. Normalised effect size, representing the estimated change in gene expression per effect allele, was used as the primary measure of an eQTL effect. Statistical significance was assessed on the basis of GTEx-reported P values.

Meta-analysis of SNP loci associated with hepatic steatosis

GWAS summary statistics for hepatic steatosis were obtained from the MVP and the UK Biobank. In the MVP, FLD cases were identified using the phenotype code Phe_571, and ancestry-specific GWAS summary statistics were available for individuals of European (MVP-EUR), African (MVP-AFR), Asian (MVP-ASN), and Hispanic (MVP-HIS) ancestry. The total MVP sample included 611,056 individuals across all ancestry groups. GWAS summary statistics on FLD in the UK Biobank were obtained from a publicly available dataset (GCST90081500), comprising 852 European ancestry cases and 376,493 European ancestry controls. We performed a generic inverse-variance weighted meta-analysis of SNP-level association statistics across cohorts and ancestry groups by using the tool METAL [32] (https://genome.sph.umich.edu/wiki/METAL_Documentation; version: generic-metal-2011-03-25), combining effect size estimates and standard errors. Only variants that passed study-specific quality control were included in the meta-analysis.

PRS-CS analysis

We used polygenic risk scores (PRSs) under continuous shrinkage (CS) [33], a Bayesian regression framework in which CS priors are employed to infer posterior SNP effect sizes from GWAS summary statistics, to calculate PRSs for hepatic steatosis. PRS-CS was implemented using the precomputed East Asian linkage disequilibrium (LD) reference panel derived from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 dataset (ldblk_1kg_eas), which was selected because the study population consisted exclusively of Taiwanese Han individuals [34]. Posterior SNP effect sizes were estimated separately for each autosomal chromosome and subsequently combined to generate genome-wide SNP weights. The complete genome-wide posterior SNP weights generated by PRS-CS and used for PRS construction are provided in Supplementary Table S5 to facilitate transparency and reproducibility. Individual-level PRSs were calculated as the weighted sum of risk alleles. Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to evaluate the association between the hepatic steatosis PRS and disease status. A series of nested models were fitted: (1) a model with PRS only; (2) a model with PRS adjusted for age and sex; and (3) extended models further adjusting for body mass index (BMI) and lifestyle-related factors, including cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, and betel nut chewing [35]. To assess model performance, we compared the models’ discriminative ability to evaluate the incremental contribution of genetic and nongenetic risk factors.

PheWAS analysis

Genetic correlation between SNPs derived from the hepatic steatosis GWAS and disease phenotypes was evaluated through logistic regression analysis in the phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) tool R package (version 0.12; available at https://github.com/PheWAS/PheWAS). Analyses were conducted with R version 4.1.0 in RStudio. PheWAS phenotypes were mapped to the standardised phecode schema, encompassing 10,750 and 3,113 unique codes from the tenth and ninth revisions of the International Classification of Diseases, respectively. The threshold for statistical significance was P < 5E − 05 [30].

In silico PNPLA3 (I148M) structural modelling and mutation stability analysis

To predict the three-dimensional structure of 1-acylglycerol-3-phosphate O-acyltransferase PNPLA3 (UniProtKB accession: Q9NST1), we used Openfold2 with UniRef50 multiple sequence alignment in GPU H200 acceleration of NVIDIA NIM. The highest-confidence model was selected for subsequent computational analysis. To assess the effects of single-point mutations on protein stability, we employed the Calculate Mutation Energy (Stability) protocol in BIOVIA Discovery Studio 2025. The isoleucine residue at position 148 was replaced with methionine (I148M) [HF1.1], and mutation energies were calculated under pH-dependent conditions using integration over proton-binding isotherms. The fractional protonation states of acidic and basic residues were determined on the basis of the CHARMM force field. The mutation energy was computed in kilocalories per mole [36].

In vitro functional assay

The human PNPLA3 I148M mutant was generated through site-directed mutagenesis, in which isoleucine was substituted with methionine at amino acid position 148. The mutated cDNA was subcloned into the mammalian expression vector pcDNA3.1( +) under the control of the cytomegalovirus promoter. The resulting construct, pcDNA3.1( +)-PNPLA3-I148M, and the empty vector control were transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells for large-scale plasmid amplification. Plasmid DNA was purified using a commercial plasmid purification kit in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, and DNA concentration and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically.

Human hepatocytes (HHs) were seeded at 3 × 105 cells per well in six-well plates and cultured overnight under standard conditions. Before transfection, the culture medium was replaced with antibiotic-free Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM). We transfected cells with either pcDNA3.1( +) empty vector or pcDNA3.1( +)-PNPLA3-I148M by using Lipofectamine 3000 in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 2 μg of plasmid DNA was mixed with 4 μL of P3000 reagent in 125 μL of Opti-MEM (Solution A), and 5 μL of Lipofectamine 3000 was diluted in 125 μL of Opti-MEM (Solution B). Solutions A and B were combined, incubated for 15 min at room temperature, and added dropwise to the cells. The transfected cells were incubated for 48 h before further experimentation. To generate stable cell lines, transfected HHs were trypsinised at 48 h after transfection and reseeded into 24-well plates. After overnight recovery, the cells were selected with DMEM containing 300 μg/mL G418 (Geneticin). The selection medium was replaced every 3 days until distinct resistant colonies were observed. Individual clones were isolated, expanded in six-well plates, and verified for PNPLA3 expression before use in subsequent experiments.

To prepare 100 mM palmitate stock solution, we dissolved palmitic acid in 0.1 mM NaOH heated at 70 °C. Then, 10% (w/v) fatty acid–free bovine serum albumin (BSA) solution was prepared in double-distilled water and maintained at 55 °C. To generate a 10 mM palmitate/1% BSA working solution, the appropriate volume of palmitate stock was mixed with 10% BSA and incubated at 55 °C for 30 min. The solution was then cooled to room temperature, sterile filtered, aliquoted, and stored at − 20 °C until use.

For free fatty acid (FFA) treatment and lipid staining, wild-type HHs and stable PNPLA3-I148M-expressing HHs were cultured in DMEM supplemented with penicillin (400 U/mL), streptomycin (50 μg/mL), L-glutamine (300 μg/mL), 0.2% foetal calf serum, and palmitate at the indicated concentrations. Cells treated with 0.4% (w/v) fatty-acid-free BSA alone served as controls. After their treatment, the cells were washed with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 3.7% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. The cells were subsequently permeabilised with chilled ethanol/acetic acid (19:1, v/v) at − 20 °C for 15 min. For intracellular lipid visualisation, cells were stained with either Oil Red O or AdipoRed assay reagent for 5 min at room temperature and then washed three times with PBS. Lipid droplet accumulation and distribution were examined using a fluorescence microscope [37].

Intracellular triglyceride levels were measured in the wild-type and stable PNPLA3-I148M-expressing HHs. The cells were seeded into T-75 flasks and treated with FFA for 24 h. Following this treatment, the cells were washed with PBS, trypsinised, and pelleted through centrifugation. The cell pellets were washed twice with 10% fatty-acid-free BSA to remove residual extracellular lipids. To extract total lipids, the cell pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of chloroform–methanol (2:1, v/v) and incubated for 1 h at room temperature with intermittent agitation. To induce phase separation, 200 μL of deionised water was added to the cell mixture, which was vortexed and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 5 min. The lower organic phase was collected and evaporated to dryness at room temperature. Lipid residues were reconstituted in a mixture of tert-butanol and Triton X-114/methanol (2:1, v/v). A glycerol-3-phosphate oxidase–based triglyceride assay kit was used in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions to determine triglyceride concentrations. Triglyceride levels were normalised to total cellular protein content, which was quantified through a bicinchoninic acid protein assay [37].

Statistical analysis

To compare baseline continuous and categorical variables, we performed Student’s t-test, the chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P < 0.05. All analyses were performed with SPSS version 22 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.1.0 [30].

Results

GWAS and biological network analysis of ultrasound-defined hepatic steatosis severity in a Taiwanese Han cohort

A total of 133,895 individuals of Taiwanese Han ancestry with available genotyping and clinical data from the CMUH Genetic Biobank were included in the analysis. Hepatic steatosis severity was assessed through ultrasonography and graded on an ordinal scale ranging from 0 (no hepatic steatosis) to 3 (severe hepatic steatosis). Individuals with suspected hepatic steatosis (grade 0.5) were excluded from the GWAS to reduce phenotype misclassification. Through GWAS analyses, we modelled hepatic steatosis severity as a quantitative trait through linear regression under an additive genetic model adjusted for age, sex, and principal components.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics stratified in accordance with hepatic steatosis severity are summarised in Table 1. Median age differed significantly between the severity groups (P < 0.001); in the groups of individuals assigned grades 0 (no hepatic steatosis) to 3 (severe hepatic steatosis), the median ages were 52.0 years (Q1–Q3: 37.0–66.0 years), 51.0 years (40.0–62.0 years), 53.0 years (42.0–63.0 years), and 45.0 years (36.0–56.0 years). The proportion of male individuals increased progressively as the hepatic steatosis severity increased, from 41.9% in the grade 0 group to 66.8% in the grade 3 group (P < 0.001).

Clear dose–response relationships were discovered between hepatic steatosis severity and metabolic traits. Median BMI increased from 23.0 (20.7–25.6) kg/m2 in the grade 0 group to 24.0 (21.8–26.3), 26.8 (24.6–29.5), and 29.9 (26.9–33.8) kg/m2 in the grade 1, 2, and 3 groups (P < 0.001). Similarly, the median triglyceride level rose from 115 (78–175) mg/dL in the grade 0 group to 130 (89–192), 180 (128–255), and 187 (138–260) mg/dL in the grades 1–3 groups, respectively (P < 0.001). By contrast, the median HDL-C level decreased steadily with increasing hepatic steatosis severity, declining from 45 (36–56) mg/dL in the grade 0 group to 37 (31–43) mg/dL in the grade 3 group (P < 0.001). Glycaemic and cholesterol-related markers also exhibited significant graded associations with hepatic steatosis severity. The median HbA1c level increased from 5.8% (5.5–6.8%) in the grade 0 group to 6.2% (5.7–%7.9) in the grade 3 group (P < 0.001). The total cholesterol and LDL-C levels progressively increased across severity categories (both P < 0.001). Collectively, these findings indicate that greater ultrasound-defined hepatic steatosis severity is accompanied by a worse metabolic profile, supporting the biological validity of the severity-based phenotype for downstream GWAS analyses (Supplementary Figure S1).

We constructed Manhattan (Fig. 1A) and quantile–quantile plots (Fig. 1B) to illustrate the genomic landscape of associations. Through the GWAS, we identified 1,229 SNPs that reached genome-wide significance at a level of P < 1E − 05 for hepatic steatosis severity (Supplementary Table S1). The most significant SNPs were rs738408 (BETA = 0.069934, P = 2.40E − 79, MAF = 0.387800) and rs738409 (BETA = 0.0698684, P = 2.77E − 79, MAF = 0.388600). IPA was performed on the 1,229 hepatic steatosis–associated SNPs (P < 1E − 05). The 20 most highly enriched pathways (Fig. 2A) included plasma lipoprotein assembly, remodelling, and clearance; NR1H2- and NR1H3-mediated signalling; NAFLD signalling; LXR/RXR activation; atherosclerosis signalling; DHCR24 signalling; maturity onset diabetes of young signalling; tight junction signalling; macrophage production of nitric oxide and reactive oxygen species; endothelin-1 signalling; FXR/RXR activation; glutaminergic receptor signalling (enhanced); phosphatidylglycerol biosynthesis II (nonplastidic); hedgehog ‘off’ state; cardiolipin biosynthesis II; interleukin-12 signalling and production in macrophages; retinoid metabolism and transport; carboxyterminal posttranslational modifications of tubulin; SNARE signalling; and cell cycle checkpoints (Supplementary Table S2). The GWAS and network analyses highlighted the key genes associated with hepatic steatosis susceptibility: APOB, APOE, BPTF, FTO, GATAD2A, GCKR, IFT172, MAU2, MTTP, PNPLA3, SAMM50, SUGP1, TM6SF2, and ZNF512 (Fig. 2B). The genes APOB, APOE, BPTF, FTO, GATAD2A, GCKR, IFT172, MAU2, MTTP, NR1H3, PNPLA3, SAMM50, SUGP1, TM6SF2, and ZNF512 were also significantly associated with hepatic steatosis (Fig. 2C). Regional association plots demonstrated strong linkage disequilibrium (linkage disequilibrium [LD] score r2 > 0.4) between PNPLA3, SAMM50 (Fig. 3A), FTO variants (Fig. 3B), and hepatic steatosis risk.

GTEx eQTL evidence for PNPLA3

Studies have indicated that rs738408 and rs738409 exhibit ‘consistent directional effects across all ancestries’, confirming that the PNPLA3 pathway is a fundamental biological driver of hepatic steatosis rather than a variable factor dependent on ethnic background [38–41]. To further evaluate the regulatory effects of PNPLA3-associated variants, we examined eQTL data from the GTEx project in adipose visceral (omentum) tissue. Both rs738408 (Fig. 4A) and rs738409 (Fig. 4B) exhibited significant eQTL signals for PNPLA3 expression. The normalised effect sizes indicated a positive direction of effect, suggesting increased PNPLA3 expression per effect allele for both variants. These findings are consistent with genotype-dependent expression patterns observed in GTEx visualisations and support the regulatory role of PNPLA3 variants in adipose tissue. Our GTEx eQTL results provide independent functional evidence linking genetic variation in PNPLA3 to altered gene expression in metabolically relevant tissue.

Meta-analysis with large-scale cohorts

We conducted a meta-analysis of data from the CMUH Genetic Biobank, the MVP (the MVP-EUR, MVP-AFR, MVP-ASN, and MVP-HIS cohorts), and the UK Biobank to investigate genetic associations with hepatic steatosis in individuals with Taiwanese Han ancestry. The unprocessed meta-analysis results for SNPs are presented in Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table S3. PRS-CS model prediction of hepatic steatosis and associated comorbidities

We employed PRS-CS to calculate PRSs on the basis of the GWAS summary statistics for each individual. Model performance was evaluated in an independent, preserved adult validation cohort. For receiver operating characteristic curve analyses, hepatic steatosis status was defined dichotomously: individuals without fatty liver (grade 0) served as controls, and those with suspected or definite fatty liver (grades 0.5–3) were classified as cases. The PRSs derived from the GWAS of hepatic steatosis exhibited strong discriminative performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.875, markedly outperforming BMI-based PRSs (AUC = 0.525). Addition of age and sex to the PRS-CS model did not substantially improve discrimination (AUC = 0.875), and further adjustment for BMI only minimally increased the AUC (0.876), indicating that the genetic risk captured by the PRS-CS model provides predictive information largely independent of conventional demographic and anthropometric factors (Fig. 6A). In parallel, the distribution of PRS values exhibited a clear monotonic increase across ultrasound-defined hepatic steatosis severity categories. The median PRS was lowest among the patients without fatty liver (grade 0), elevated in those with suspected fatty liver (grade 0.5), and further increased progressively across mild (grade 1), moderate (grade 2), and severe fatty liver (grade 3). The significant differences in PRS distributions between severity groups supported a dose–response relationship between polygenic risk burden and hepatic steatosis severity (Fig. 6B). Collectively, these results demonstrate that PRSs derived through PRS-CS analysis can robustly distinguish individuals with and without hepatic steatosis in a binary classification framework and simultaneously capture graded genetic risk across the full spectrum of disease severity.

PheWAS of hepatic steatosis

A PheWAS of the GWAS data on hepatic steatosis revealed substantial shared genetic susceptibility between hepatic steatosis and a broad spectrum of cardiometabolic and hepatorenal disorders. The most prominent associations were those with type 2 diabetes, diabetes mellitus, hypertension (including essential hypertension, hypertensive heart disease, and hypertensive heart and/or renal disease), lipid metabolism disorders, and hyperlipidaemia. Additional phenotypes associated with the hepatic steatosis PRS included overweight- and obesity-related conditions, morbid obesity, diabetic retinopathy, other chronic nonalcoholic liver diseases, chronic hepatitis, chronic liver disease and cirrhosis, gout and other crystal arthropathies, and renal failure (Fig. 7, Supplementary Table S4).

In silico and in vitro functional assay

To understand the structural effects of the isoleucine-to-methionine substitution, we performed in silico modelling and mutation energy analysis. Structural visualisation of the PNPLA3 protein revealed the location of the I148M mutation within a critical domain (Fig. 8A). Computational analysis indicated that the substitution yields mutation energy of 0.8 kcal/mol, greatly exceeding the stability threshold of > 0.5 kcal/mol. The energy profile across various pH levels indicated that the mutation exerts a consistent destabilising effect on the protein structure, particularly under physiological pH, potentially causing loss of biological function (Fig. 8B).

I148M-expressing cells exhibited increased triglyceride accumulation following lipid loading. ***P < 0.001

To assess the functional effects of the PNPLA3 I148M variant on hepatic lipid metabolism, we evaluated lipid droplet formation in and the intracellular triglyceride content of HHs. Oil Red O and AdipoRed staining following FFA treatment revealed pronounced elevation in intracellular lipid accumulation in cells expressing the I148M variant compared with that in wild-type (I/I) cells. Quantitative analysis revealed that the fold change in lipid staining intensity (FFA treatment versus control) was significantly greater in the PNPLA3-I148M-expressing cells, reaching approximately 4.0-fold for Oil Red O staining and 4.6-fold for AdipoRed staining, whereas the wild-type cells exhibited increases of approximately 2.0-fold and 2.1-fold, respectively (Fig. 8C; P < 0.001). Consistent with the staining results, biochemical quantification of intracellular triglyceride levels revealed no significant difference between wild-type and PNPLA3-I148M-expressing cells under basal conditions. Upon FFA exposure, triglyceride content was markedly elevated in both groups; however, the cells expressing the I148M variant exhibited significantly greater accumulation of intracellular triglyceride (0.46 mmol/g protein) compared with the wild-type cells (0.23 mmol/g protein; Fig. 8D; P < 0.001). These results indicate that the PNPLA3 I148M mutation is associated with increased lipid droplet formation and triglyceride accumulation in hepatocytes under lipid-loading conditions, supporting its role in promoting hepatic steatosis.

Discussion

Lau et al. [7] reported that in a Taiwanese cohort from the Taiwan Biobank, the fatty liver index (FLI) used as a surrogate marker for hepatic steatosis showed strong associations with variants in lipid metabolic pathways, particularly FTO and ZPR1, signals that are largely shared with European populations. Consistent with these findings, our GWAS analysis revealed robust associations at established hepatic steatosis loci—including PNPLA3, TM6SF2, GCKR, FTO, APOE, SAMM50, and PRPF6—reinforcing the notion that a core set of lipid- and lipoprotein-related genes underlies hepatic fat accumulation in East Asian populations. Recent GWAS analyses of MASLD in the Taiwan Biobank have similarly identified multiple variants within the PNPLA3 and SAMM50 loci as major genetic determinants of MASLD susceptibility in Taiwanese populations, further supporting the central role of these lipid metabolism–related genes in hepatic steatosis pathogenesis [42]. Unlike studies that define hepatic steatosis using indirect proxies such as the FLI or diagnostic criteria derived from clinical records, our study defined hepatic steatosis using abdominal ultrasound imaging and further stratified disease severity. We further graded hepatic steatosis in combination with detailed metabolic phenotypes—including BMI, triglyceride level, and HDL-C level—to capture both disease severity and its systemic metabolic context (Table 1). This design enabled us to delineate dose–response relationships between steatosis grades and adverse lipid profiles and to link steatosis-associated variants more directly to clinically relevant end points. Our study confirms the involvement of key loci and expands the spectrum of implicated genes and trait associations—including variants previously linked to obesity, dyslipidaemia, and metabolic syndrome—situating the hepatic steatosis phenotype within a wider polygenic and systemic metabolic network. Together, these complementary approaches support a model in which cohort-specific phenotyping strategies and population structure modify a conserved set of lipid-related loci, yielding partially overlapping but distinct genetic signatures that can be leveraged for genetic risk stratification and provide mechanistic insight into hepatic steatosis. These findings highlight the value of imaging-based phenotyping for improving genetic discovery and clinical risk stratification in hepatic steatosis.

Our analysis, grounded in abdominal ultrasound diagnosis, revealed a broad spectrum of 1,229 genome-wide SNPs with relevance to hepatic steatosis, including both established loci (e.g., PNPLA3, TM6SF2, GCKR, FTO, and APOE) and novel signals (e.g. NOP9, CIDEB, and LTB4R2; Figs. 1 and 2 and Supplementary Figure S1). The identified GWAS loci exhibit strong biological plausibility given their established roles in hepatic lipid metabolism, lipoprotein dynamics, mitochondrial function, and immuno-inflammatory pathways central to hepatic steatosis pathogenesis (Fig. 2). The most prominent signals were localised to PNPLA3 and TM6SF2, both of which regulate triglyceride mobilisation from lipid droplets and very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) secretion [43, 44]. The PNPLA3 rs738409 (I148M) variant impairs triglyceride lipase activity and promotes dysfunctional accumulation on lipid droplet surfaces, trapping polyunsaturated fatty acids and exacerbating steatosis [45, 46]. Similarly, TM6SF2 variants disrupt the late-stage lipidation of VLDL particles, redirecting triglycerides from systemic circulation to intrahepatic storage and mimicking a ‘hepatic sink’ phenotype [47]. Metabolic regulator genes GCKR and FTO further reinforce the polygenic architecture linking glycaemic control, adiposity, and hepatic lipogenesis [7, 48, 49]. GCKR risk alleles promote glucokinase activity, driving de novo lipogenesis and suppressing fatty acid oxidation, a canonical mechanism of NAFLD [50]. FTO, a validated shared locus of susceptibility with European cohorts, contributes to systemic obesity and dyslipidaemia, consistent with its effects on energy homeostasis and fat mass as an RNA demethylase [7]. APOE variants modulate lipoprotein clearance and cholesterol trafficking; specific isoforms have been associated with dyslipidaemia and accelerated NAFLD progression [51, 52].

Novel signals in NOP9, CIDEB, LTB4R2, and SAMM50 expand the mechanistic spectrum beyond lipid handling. CIDEB governs lipid droplet expansion and VLDL assembly; emerging evidence suggests loss of function protects against steatosis by promoting β-oxidation and PPARα activation, suggesting potential protective versus pathogenic allele effects [53, 54]. SAMM50 encodes a mitochondrial outer membrane translocase to link impaired β-oxidation, accumulation of reactive oxygen species, and mitophagy defects with lipid peroxidation and hepatotoxicity [55]. LTB4R2 initiates leukotriene-mediated inflammation, connecting lipid peroxidation products to TNF-driven hepatocyte injury and fibrosis [56]. Although the role of NOP9 in ribosome biogenesis requires functional validation, this association aligns with emerging evidence of translational dysregulation in metabolic stress [57]. Collectively, these loci delineate a convergent model in which genetic perturbations in lipid droplet dynamics (PNPLA3, TM6SF2, and CIDEB), lipogenesis (GCKR), mitochondrial bioenergetics (SAMM50), systemic adiposity (FTO), lipoprotein metabolism (APOE), and inflammation (LTB4R2) culminate in hepatic fat accumulation and systemic cardiometabolic derangements, providing a mechanistic basis for our GWAS, PRS, and PheWAS findings.

Other biological network analyses have highlighted lipid homeostasis, lipoprotein assembly and remodelling, and NAFLD-related signalling cascades, including nuclear receptor pathways (e.g., LXR/RXR and FXR/RXR), as central mechanisms connecting genetic variation to hepatic fat accumulation [7, 58, 59]. Our network and enrichment analyses, performed through IPA, extend these observations by revealing broader biological programs involving inflammatory signalling; oxidative stress; cell-cycle regulation; and cytokine, immune, and cardiovascular signalling. These findings suggest that genetic variants increase hepatic steatosis risk through hepatic lipid handling and also through pathways that link steatosis to cardiometabolic and inflammatory complications (Fig. 2).

To identify potential causal mechanisms underlying the GWAS signals, we investigated eQTL data from adipose tissue, revealing tissue-specific regulatory effects of PNPLA3 variants. Specifically, rs738408 and rs738409 exhibited significant eQTL associations with PNPLA3 expression in visceral (omentum) adipose tissue, and risk alleles were correlated with affected transcript levels (Fig. 4). This downregulation aligns with the established loss-of-function phenotype of PNPLA3, I148M (under strong LD with rs738408), which impairs triglyceride hydrolysis and lipid droplet remodelling in both hepatocytes and adipose depots. Our findings extend the hepatic-centric model of PNPLA3 dysfunction to a systemic adipose–hepatic axis, in which adipose-derived PNPLA3 variants may contribute to dysregulated FFA flux and visceral adiposity, a known driver of portal lipotoxicity and steatosis progression [60]. The observed eQTL effects were consistent across visceral and omentum compartments, both metabolically active depots linked to cardiometabolic risk. This finding provides orthogonal functional validation of our GWAS signals, underscoring the polygenic interplay between adipose lipid handling (PNPLA3 and FTO) and hepatic fat accumulation. Future colocalisation analyses with GTEx or adipose single-cell eQTL datasets will be essential to pinpoint target cell types and refine causal prioritisation. The rs738408 polymorphism is located in very close proximity to and is in high LD with rs738409. In large-scale GWASs, rs738408 often appears concurrently as the ‘sentinel’ or lead signal for the locus [41]. The rs738409 polymorphism is a missense mutation in which the amino acid isoleucine is replaced by methionine at position 148 of the protein (I148M) [61]. The genetic variant rs738409 in PNPLA3 encodes the I148M substitution and is the most robust genetic determinant of hepatic steatosis identified to date. Nevertheless, the precise biophysical mechanism through which this single amino acid change drives metabolic impairment remains incompletely understood.

To assess the generalisability of our findings beyond East Asian populations, we conducted a meta-analysis incorporating summary statistics from the UK Biobank and the MVP (MVP-EUR, MVP-AFR, MVP-ASN, and MVP-HIS geographic cohorts). This comprehensive analysis successfully replicated 22 SNPs from our GWAS; these results are summarised in Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table S3. Our meta-analysis provides comprehensive cross-ancestry evidence by (1) encompassing four major ancestral groups in the MVP database, (2) confirming signals obtained through imaging-defined hepatic steatosis phenotypes rather than algorithmic surrogates, and (3) demonstrating that 22 distinct loci—beyond the lipid-centric genes emphasised in other East Asian studies—are consistent across ancestral populations. This broader replication spectrum supports the influence of a polygenic architecture of hepatic lipid dysregulation across diverse human genetics, with varying effect sizes that are modulated by population-specific linkage disequilibrium and allele frequencies. The integration of our meta-analytic findings with our PRS and PheWAS results establishes a robust framework for polygenic risk prediction that transcends ethnic boundaries, facilitating precision medicine applications in diverse clinical settings.

PRS construction and validation enabled quantitative assessment of genetic predisposition to hepatic steatosis, and the PRS was found to have superior discriminatory performance compared with individual loci alone (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Figure S1). Notably, integration of PheWAS results revealed extensive pleiotropic associations with a broad spectrum of comorbid conditions, including type 2 diabetes, hypertension, obesity, chronic liver disease and cirrhosis, diabetic retinopathy, gout, and renal failure (Fig. 7). These findings position hepatic steatosis as a central nexus of systemic metabolic derangements, extending prior pathway-based evidence centred on lipid homeostasis by delineating a polygenic comorbidity network. Our results also underscore the translational potential of PRS for risk stratification and early intervention, particularly in high-burden East Asian populations. The strongest genetic associations were discovered across multiple disease categories, including endocrine and metabolic disorders, circulatory system diseases, digestive system diseases, infectious diseases, and genitourinary disorders. Collectively, these results establish a genetic basis linking hepatic steatosis susceptibility with a wide range of comorbid conditions, providing clinically relevant insights for integrated disease risk assessment and management [62].

Our in silico modelling revealed that the I148M substitution induces measurable structural destabilisation of PNPLA3, having mutation energy of 0.80 kcal/mol under physiological conditions (Fig. 8A and B). This value exceeds the commonly accepted 0.5 kcal/mol threshold for structural relevance, suggesting that replacement of isoleucine with the larger methionine residue perturbs the local hydrophobic environment and compromises the protein’s conformational integrity [63]. The observed reduction in structural stability is likely to cause either loss of function in canonical lipase activity or a gain-of-toxic-function phenotype at the lipid droplet surface. PNPLA3 exhibits triglyceride lipase activity and participates in lipid droplet remodelling, and the I148M variant has been linked to impaired triglyceride hydrolysis and reduced mobilisation of lipids from hepatic lipid droplets [63]. Consistent with prior evidence that PNPLA3-I148M accumulates on lipid droplets and interferes with normal lipolytic processes, including adipose triglyceride lipase–mediated lipolysis, the structural destabilisation identified in our model (Fig. 8B) may disrupt catalytic efficiency or alter dynamic interactions with lipid droplet-associated cofactors and proteolytic machinery [64]. The I148M substitution may render PNPLA3 relatively resistant to proteasomal degradation, leading to persistence and accumulation of the mutant protein on the lipid droplet surface, where it may attenuate lipolysis by sterically or functionally sequestering lipid droplet and coregulators away from other lipases (e.g., adipose triglyceride lipase) [65].

Our cellular data support this structure–function link. Under basal conditions, the intracellular triglyceride content did not differ substantially between genotypes; however, cells expressing PNPLA3-I148M exhibited markedly elevated sensitivity to lipid loading, accumulating nearly twice as much triglyceride as wild-type cells following FFA treatment (Fig. 8D), consistent with the results of previous overexpression and knock-in models [66]. This ‘double-hit’ scenario—in which structural instability compromises PNPLA3 function and the subsequent lipid stress amplifies metabolic defects—can plausibly explain why the I148M variant is particularly deleterious under high-fat or lipogenic conditions. Furthermore, the strong correlation between the 0.8-kcal/mol destabilisation energy and the pronounced increase in Oil Red O and AdipoRed staining intensities (Fig. 8C) indicates that preservation of PNPLA3 conformational integrity is essential for maintaining hepatic lipid droplet homeostasis and preventing excessive steatosis.

Of the imaging modalities used to clinically evaluate hepatic steatosis within the broader context of MASLD, abdominal ultrasonography is commonly applied as a first-line screening tool and facilitates the detection of steatosis in its early stages. However, its sensitivity is limited for mild steatosis, particularly when the hepatic fat content is below approximately 20–30%, and its diagnostic performance may be further reduced in individuals with severe obesity. For more advanced disease or for quantitative assessment of hepatic fat content, additional imaging modalities—such as computed tomography or MRI, particularly MRI–proton density fat fraction—are often required, and liver biopsy remains the reference standard for steatohepatitis and fibrosis assessment [67]. Furthermore, hepatic steatosis and MASLD are widely recognized as multifactorial disorders resulting from complex interactions between genetic susceptibility and environmental or metabolic factors [68]. Although our study identified significant genetic determinants of steatosis severity and demonstrated strong predictive performance of the PRS, factors such as obesity, dietary habits, insulin resistance, physical activity, and other lifestyle-related exposures also contribute substantially to disease development. Previous studies have suggested that the effect of the PNPLA3 I148M variant on hepatic fat accumulation and disease progression may be influenced by obesity, insulin resistance, and dietary factors, highlighting the importance of gene–environment interactions in MASLD [69, 70]. Therefore, the genetic associations identified in the present study should be interpreted within the broader framework of gene–environment interactions. Future studies integrating comprehensive dietary, metabolic, and lifestyle data will be important for elucidating these relationships and further improving individualized risk prediction strategies for MASLD.

Conclusion

Hepatic steatosis is a prevalent and potentially reversible metabolic condition, particularly when identified at an early stage and addressed through lifestyle interventions, such as regular physical activity and dietary modification. In Taiwan and across Asia, public health strategies increasingly emphasise early screening, healthy dietary behaviours, and awareness of cardiometabolic risk factors. Our genetic findings provide insights into the biological mechanisms underlying interindividual variability in hepatic steatosis severity and support the integration of polygenic risk information into precision prevention and risk stratification frameworks for metabolic liver disease.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, hepatic steatosis was assessed through abdominal ultrasonography, which provides a semiquantitative evaluation and is subject to interoperator variability. Although all ultrasound examinations were interpreted by experienced hepatobiliary and gastroenterological specialists during routine clinical care, interphysician variability in image acquisition and interpretation could not be fully excluded. Second, formal interobserver agreement metrics were unavailable in the electronic medical records used for this retrospective study. To mitigate potential misclassification, we adopted conservative phenotype definitions by requiring at least three independent hepatic steatosis diagnoses and defining disease severity as the maximum steatosis grade recorded across multiple examinations. Finally, although ultrasound-based grading is widely accepted and practical for large-scale epidemiological studies, it is less sensitive than histological assessment or advanced imaging modalities for detection of mild steatosis. Consequently, any residual misclassification was likely to be nondifferential and would bias associations toward the null.
