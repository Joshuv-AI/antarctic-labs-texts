Introduction

Osteoporosis is a highly prevalent disease associated with a substantial lifetime risk of fragility fractures. Despite its widespread occurrence, it remains markedly underdiagnosed and undertreated, underscoring the urgent need for increased clinical awareness and the implementation of proactive management strategies [1]. Among osteoporotic fractures, vertebral fractures (VFs) are the most common [2]. These fractures are not only associated with significant reductions in health-related quality of life but also correlate with increased morbidity and mortality [3, 4]. The risk of subsequent fractures is particularly elevated following an initial VF, with evidence indicating that 40–60% of recurrent fractures occur within the first two years of the first fracture—a period recognized as a critical window for therapeutic intervention [5, 6]. Once identified, several pharmacological therapies are available, including osteoanabolic and antiresorptive agents, which have demonstrated efficacy in reducing the risk of subsequent vertebral and non-vertebral osteoporotic fractures [7]. Nevertheless, despite the clinical significance of VFs and the availability of effective treatments, these fractures are frequently overlooked in routine clinical practice, with fewer than one-third of cases receiving clinical attention [8, 9]. Bridging this diagnostic gap is important, and one promising approach is the opportunistic detection of VFs on computed tomography (CT) scans performed for unrelated clinical indications. Given the substantial societal and economic burden associated with VFs, improved diagnostic strategies and broader implementation of cost-effective interventions may yield significant public health and economic benefits [10].

Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have introduced novel tools for enhancing the detection of osteoporotic VFs. A growing body of literature supports the utility of AI-based systems in facilitating systematic and accurate identification of VFs, particularly among older adults at elevated risk [11–15]. Although these technologies have demonstrated robust performance in controlled research settings, their integration into routine clinical workflows remains challenging. Key barriers include heterogeneity in imaging protocols and clinical practices [13], as well as difficulties in incorporating and communicating AI-generated findings within existing healthcare infrastructures—particularly in coordinating between radiology departments and osteoporosis management services.

Fracture Liaison Services (FLS) have been established as a cost-effective model for secondary fracture prevention, enabling identification of patients with osteoporotic fractures and initiation of appropriate treatment pathways [16]. Typically, FLS activation is contingent upon the presence of diagnostic coding (e.g., ICD codes) corresponding to common osteoporotic fracture types. However, due to the high rate of undetected VFs, these cases often fail to generate the necessary coding to trigger FLS involvement. Consequently, affected individuals may not receive further evaluation or appropriate osteoporosis therapy.

In a prior retrospective cohort study, we demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy of the AI algorithm IB Lab FLAMINGO (IB Lab GmbH, Vienna, Austria) for VF detection in CT thorax and CT abdomen, achieving a sensitivity of 86% and specificity of 99% [13]. Comparable performance metrics have been reported by other research groups [14, 15]. Nonetheless, successful integration into routine clinical practice requires the establishment of procedural workflows and validation of algorithm performance in real-time clinical environments. Furthermore, data regarding the prevalence of VFs and associated patient characteristics within the context of opportunistic screening in standard radiologic workflows remain limited.

In the present project, we have implemented AI-based VF screening using FLAMINGO (IB Lab) within the radiology workflow and established a pathway to our local FLS. The specific objectives of this study are to; (1) evaluate the feasibility and diagnostic performance of the Flamingo AI algorithm when integrated into a real-world clinical workflow, encompassing both elective and acute CT examinations performed for non-skeletal indications, (2) determine the frequency of VFs identified by the AI system, subsequently confirmed by radiologists, and triaged by the FLS, and (3) describe the prevalence of VF and characteristics of patients with VFs identified through opportunistic screening within a routine CT workflow.

Methods

Study design and overall workflow

This observational study analyzed outcomes from the clinical implementation of the FLAMINGO AI algorithm (IB Lab, Vienna, Austria). The study cohort comprised all patients ≥ 50 years who underwent CT examinations, including the thoracic and/or abdominal region, at Linköping University Hospital, Sweden (catchment area about 250,000 inhabitants), between October 20, 2024, and February 19, 2025, i.e., a four-month period. Exclusion criteria included individuals with CT examinations specifically performed for dedicated spine evaluation.

The AI algorithm was integrated into the clinical workflow alongside standard care, as illustrated in Fig. 1. All CT scans were screened in real time by the AI. Cases flagged as positive—indicating the presence of VF—were automatically added to a worklist within the PACS system. A dedicated radiologist reviewed all AI-positive cases on a weekly basis to confirm the presence of VF. All cases confirmed by the radiologist were subsequently referred to the FLS.

Workflow illustrating the standard clinical pathway for CT examinations (upper row) and an AI-assisted pathway, in which AI-generated results are processed via a parallel route (bottom row). Examinations flagged as positive by the AI algorithm (presence of vertebral fracture) are placed on a separate worklist, where a dedicated radiologist reviews and either confirms or refutes the diagnosis. Cases confirmed as positive are then directly referred to a Fracture Liaison Service (FLS) nurse for further management

Information about the project was included in the radiology appointment letter to inform patients that, in the event of unexpected findings, they might be contacted by a healthcare provider other than the referring institution.

AI integration

Scan selection

The AI algorithm was deployed on a dedicated server integrated into the hospital’s IT infrastructure. For each patient, an automated script—developed collaboratively by Sectra Imtec AB, IB Lab, the hospital IT team, and clinical stakeholders—selected a single CT stack for analysis. The script, executed immediately post-examination, identified scans covering the thoracic and/or lumbar spine. When multiple stacks were available, selection prioritized thinner slices (e.g., 1 mm over 3 mm) and softer reconstruction kernels, based on prior validation to optimize input quality for the FLAMINGO AI algorithm [13].

AI processing time

The AI analysis required approximately 10 min per CT scan. To accommodate this processing time, a predefined rule was implemented via a customized script (Sectra Imtec AB, Sweden), limiting the transmission rate to a maximum of one CT scan every 10 min from the CT scanners to the AI server.

AI reporting and PACS integration

Upon completion of the AI analysis, a report in the form of a secondary capture image, was transmitted to the PACS system. This report included information on the presence or absence of VF, the affected vertebral levels, and a sagittal image of the spine with annotated VF/VFs. To facilitate the generation of a worklist within the PACS system, data from DICOM tag 0018;1010 (Secondary Capture Device ID) was redirected into a free-field tag. This enabled the creation of worklists of cases analyzed by the AI, categorized as either “vertebral fracture detected” or “vertebral fracture not detected”.

Evaluation

An evaluation form was developed and integrated into the PACS system to facilitate secondary assessment and confirmation by the radiologist. The form included the following items: whether the AI assessment was accurate; if not, whether an obvious reason for the discrepancy could be identified; whether the VF was new; and whether the VF had been documented in the standard radiology report. The form was developed in collaboration with Sectra Imtec AB, Sweden, and enabled the export of a comprehensive list of all AI- and radiologist reviewed cases from the PACS system. This functionality also supported the creation of a dashboard for routine clinical monitoring and quality assurance.

Technical monitoring

A monitoring system was developed in collaboration with Sectra Imtec AB to continuously track the data transmission between the CT scanners and the FLAMINGO AI algorithm. This system enabled real-time oversight of the workflow, allowing for prompt identification and resolution of any disruptions or errors in the data flow.

Radiologist evaluation

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the standard radiological evaluation (usual care) remained unchanged throughout the study. In parallel, a dedicated radiologist independently reviewed and confirmed all VF flagged by the AI algorithm (Genant grade 2 or 3 [17]). The radiologists involved in this confirmation process were highly experienced: MW (17 years), ML (19 years), and TB (20 years). Each CT examination was evaluated by a single radiologist. In a previous internal validation study employing the same AI algorithm, conducted within the same clinical environment and involving the same radiologists analysing retrospective data, the inter‑rater reliability (Cohen’s kappa) ranged from 0.75 (95% CI 0.64–0.87) for thoracic scans (n = 128) to 0.92 (95% CI 0.84–0.99) for abdominal scans (n = 118), data not published before.

To assess the performance of the FLAMINGO AI algorithm in this real-world setting, all CT scans during the last 25 days of the project were re-reviewed by one radiologist (MW), i.e. also including those cases that the AI had flagged as negative for VF.

Clinical assessment

The FLS team, comprising specialized nurses and senior osteoporosis physicians, evaluated all radiologist-confirmed VFs using patient records. Based on local guidelines, patients were triaged into four categories: (1) requiring further evaluation/treatment, (2) no action needed (adequately treated), (3) no action due to terminal illness/death, or (4) no action due to high-energy trauma or living outside the catchment area. Referrals for further care were made to primary or specialist units as needed. Primary care and key departments (department of oncology, rheumatology, and emergency) were informed prior to the project to ensure coordination.

For each confirmed VF case, data collected included prior osteoporosis diagnosis, history of VF, current anti-osteoporotic treatment (with type, if applicable), free-text notes (e.g., terminal illness or death), and whether a referral was issued. Clinical triaging was conducted no earlier than three weeks after the CT to allow time for the clinician’s response to the radiology report.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 30. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies or percentages. Group comparisons were performed using Student’s T-test for continuous variables and the Chi2 test for categorical variables. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Language refinement

Language refinement was supported by Microsoft Copilot 365.

Results

Study population and CT examination characteristics

During the four-month study period, a total of 3971 unique individuals underwent CT examinations, with a mean age of 72 ± 11 years. Of these, 2018 (51%) were female. In total, 5147 CT examinations were analyzed, with each patient undergoing between 1 and 11 CT examinations during the study period (19% had ≥ 2 CT examinations).

Out of all screened patients, 566 individuals (14%) were identified by the AI algorithm as having one or more VF, which were subsequently verified by a radiologist. Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Most commonly patients with confirmed VF were referred to the radiology department from the emergency unit (26%), followed by the surgery, oncology, primary health care, and internal medicine departments (Table 1).

Feasibility

The FLAMINGO AI system was successfully integrated into the hospital’s IT infrastructure and was reported user-friendly by the radiologists. Re-evaluation of AI-flagged positive cases required approximately 2–4 h per week, corresponding to an average of 2–4 min per patient. Additionally, the radiologist provided consultation support to the FLS team for complex patient cases, spending approximately 30 min per week in this role.

A 4% failure rate was observed for CT exams not transmitted to the FLAMINGO AI server. Half were due to human error, primarily mislabelling (e.g., “CT abdomen” for non-abdominal scans). The remaining 2% had no identifiable cause.

During the four-month study, one AI processing failure occurred, promptly detected by radiologists and system monitoring. The issue—image pixelation due to memory overload—was diagnosed within minutes and resolved the same day through collaboration between hospital IT, the AI vendor, and the PACS provider.

Performance of the AI algorithm

Performance metrics of the AI algorithm are presented in Table 2 (confusion matrix in Supplement information 1). During the final 25 days of the study period, a total of 1159 CT scans were performed in routine clinical practice. Of these, 79% (n = 911) were classified as negative and 21% (n = 248) as positive by the AI algorithm. Among the 911 AI-negative cases, 17 (1.9%) were identified as false negatives, i.e. were confirmed having VF by the radiologist, and among 248 AI-positive cases, 73 were false positive (29%). Based on these findings, the FLAMINGO AI algorithm demonstrated a sensitivity of 91%, specificity of 92%, a positive predictive value (PPV) of 71%, and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 98%.

When analysing all CT scans (including data from 4 months), two primary causes of false positive VF detections were identified during the evaluation process (Supplement information 2). First, the AI algorithm occasionally misclassified large sclerotic vertebral metastases as fractures. Second, partial visualization of the uppermost or lowermost vertebrae—where only a portion of the vertebra was included in the scan—sometimes led to misinterpretation by the AI as VF. These findings were communicated to the AI provider for consideration in future model refinement and training dataset augmentation.

Vertebral fracture detection and clinical triage

As illustrated in Fig. 2, a total of 3971 unique patients were screened for VF during the four-month study period. Of these, 21% were flagged as positive by the AI algorithm, and 68% of those flagged cases were subsequently confirmed as true positives by a radiologist. Following clinical triage by the FLS-team, 49% of patients with radiologist confirmed VF were deemed to require further action—indicating that the fractures were previously undiagnosed, untreated, or inadequately managed. The remaining patients were either already appropriately treated prior to the study (37%) or were terminally ill or had died within weeks of the CT examination (15%).

Among all unique patients who underwent CT scanning during the study period, 14% were identified as having AI-flagged and radiologist-confirmed VF. Of these, half required further clinical intervention, representing 7% of the total screened population. In absolute terms, this equates to an average of 2–3 patients per day requiring follow-up care in our healthcare system.

Patient characteristics

Of the 566 patients with radiologist-confirmed VF, 36 were excluded due to high-energy trauma or residence outside the catchment area, leaving 530 eligible for FLS triage (Table 3). Mean age was 78 ± 10 years. Most patients (70%) were aged 70–89 years; 17% were < 70, and 12% ≥ 90 (Table 1 and Fig. 3). Prior osteoporosis diagnosis was present in 41%, and 38% were on treatment—mainly antiresorptives: alendronate (10%), zoledronic acid (16%), and denosumab (12%). Osteoanabolic use was rare (teriparatide 0.2%, no romosozumab).

Age distribution and clinical triage of patients with radiologist verified vertebral fracture

Seventy-seven patients were terminally ill or died shortly after CT, precluding further triage (mean age 81 ± 10 years; 64% women).

Only 42% (n = 223) of all VFs were documented in original radiology reports (averaging 1.8 patients reported per day). In 74% of cases, prior CTs showed at least one VF; 10% of the patients had no prior CTs.

Patients triaged as “no further action” had significantly higher rates of prior VF diagnosis (60% vs. 27%) and osteoporosis diagnosis (75% vs. 27%) and were more frequently treated for osteoporosis (78% vs. 8%) than those requiring follow-up. Among the latter, alendronate was most used; none received denosumab or osteoanabolics.

Stable (radiologically unchanged) VFs were more common in the “no further action” group (62% vs. 56%), who also had fewer CT-naïve patients (6% vs. 13%). VF documentation in radiology report was more frequent in both current (52% vs. 36%) and prior reports (57% vs. 43%) in the “no further action” group, suggesting greater prior clinical recognition. Among the patients requiring further clinical action (“Action needed”-group), 0.8 patients per day were identified in radiology reports, representing approximately one-third of all patients who required follow‑up (2.2 patients per day).

Discussion

In the present study, we report a high prevalence of VFs, identified in 14% of all patients aged 50 years and older undergoing acute or elective clinical CT examinations. Among these, half (7%) were triaged by the FLS as requiring further clinical intervention due to previously undiagnosed, untreated, or insufficiently managed osteoporosis. The integration of the FLAMINGO AI algorithm into the clinical radiology workflow aligned with the FLS pathway, was demonstrated to be both feasible and operationally robust. Monitoring systems for both the AI tool and the newly established workflow were successfully implemented, enabling real-time oversight and rapid resolution of technical issues. The algorithm exhibited strong overall performance with accuracy of 92%, although its PPV was slightly lower (71%) in this real-world clinical data compared to prior retrospective analyses [13].

Feasibility and performance of the AI algorithm

Integrating AI into clinical workflows poses challenges related to technical implementation, monitoring, and alignment with clinical workflow to ensure appropriate handling of AI outputs. In this project, integration occurred in a setting not fully optimized for AI, necessitating the development of customized solutions.

An AI algorithm can be connected to PACS either through case-specific integrations or standardized AI platforms. Platforms offer unified interfaces for multiple algorithms and vendors, improving usability and interoperability. Our study employed a custom integration due to the absence of a contractual link between the AI vendor and our present PACS platform (Amplifier) at the time when the project started. Although this approach enabled timely deployment, it necessitated extensive coordination among vendors, hospital IT, and radiology teams—an effort unlikely to be sustainable for large-scale AI implementations, where hospitals will likely need to orchestrate multiple AI solutions. Thus, integration via standardized AI platforms seems preferable in the future to streamline deployment, reduce costs, and enhance interoperability including generic solutions for several AI algorithms.

To facilitate ongoing evaluation of the AI algorithm and for clinical monitoring, a structured assessment form was developed in collaboration with the PACS provider. This form enabled the definition of examination-specific questions and response options, supporting continuous performance monitoring and clinical validation of the algorithm.

Monitoring of the AI workflow was conducted collaboratively by the PACS vendor (Sectra), the AI provider (IB Lab), and the hospital’s IT and radiology teams. Initial monitoring involved comparing the number of examinations transmitted to the AI server with the number of results returned to PACS. A failure rate of approximately 4% was observed, with 2% attributable to human error—primarily mislabeling of anatomical regions—and the remaining 2% having an unclear cause. The clinical evaluation form also supported real-time performance tracking via a PowerBI dashboard, enabling early detection of deviations. In parallel, the AI provider maintained an alert system on the server, which automatically flagged performance anomalies. Importantly, radiologists reviewing AI-generated results provided an additional layer of quality assurance, identifying occasional errors such as distorted image outputs that were not detectable by automated monitoring systems.

The FLAMINGO AI algorithm demonstrated robust diagnostic performance for VF detection, with a sensitivity of 91% and specificity of 92%, both of which exceed the average values reported in a recent meta-analysis of similar algorithms [18]. Furthermore, its performance surpassed that of the AI model evaluated in a recently published real-world study from Denmark [14] However, the PPV of 71% observed in this study was notably lower than the PPV reported in our prior evaluation study (98%) [13]. This discrepancy could be partially attributed to challenges in differentiating sclerotic vertebral metastases from VFs. A substantial proportion of the study population had underlying oncologic diagnoses, and it is likely that the algorithm was not trained on datasets representative of this patient group. In routine clinical practice, cancer patients undergo frequent CT examinations and therefore constitute a substantial proportion of the patient population in the radiology department. This limitation is clinically significant, as patients with malignancies—particularly those receiving corticosteroids or antihormonal therapies for breast or prostate cancer—are at increased risk of VFs [19, 20]. The differentiation between metastatic lesions and osteoporotic fractures remains a diagnostic challenge, underscoring the critical role of radiologist validation in AI-assisted workflows. These findings highlight the need for algorithm refinement and training on more diverse datasets to improve performance in complex patient populations [21]. Prospective real-world studies are crucial, as they provide more representative data and reveal practical challenges. Active engagement of clinical sites enables iterative optimization, ensuring AI tools achieve high clinical utility and seamless integration into routine workflows.

Vertebral fracture detection and clinical triage

In the present study, we demonstrate the practical utility of an AI-assisted VF detection system, integrated into a structured radiology-to-FLS workflow (Supplement information 3, Graphical abstract). Among patients flagged by the AI algorithm, a substantial proportion (68%) were confirmed as true positives by radiologists (4.3 patients per day). Notably, 49% of these confirmed cases required further clinical intervention after FLS triage, indicating that a significant number of VFs were previously undiagnosed, untreated, or inadequately managed. Of the 4.3 patients AI/radiologist fond with VF every day, only 1.8 patients per day (42%) were reported in routine radiology reports. According to aggregated statistics from the Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare, approximately 1.2 patients per day received a clinical diagnosis of VF (ICD codes S22/S32) in the catchment area 2023 (including not only CT scanned patients).

From a systems perspective, the integration of AI into routine CT workflows revealed a previously unrecognized need for clinical follow-up in approximately 7% of all scanned patients. This equates to 2.2 new patients per day in our center (of whom only 0.8 patients per day were reported in routine radiology reports), which serves a population of approximately 250,000 inhabitants. This volume is both clinically meaningful and operationally manageable, highlighting the value of embedding such technologies into radiology departments, where opportunistic fracture detection is often overlooked.

The potential of AI-based detection becomes especially impactful when it is embedded within a structured clinical pathway involving an FLS. In this model, VFs can be promptly identified and referred to an osteoporosis unit for comprehensive evaluation and treatment [22]. Failure to initiate osteoporosis treatment after a fragility fracture—commonly referred to as the osteoporosis treatment gap—remains a persistent global issue, and is reportedly increasing [23]. According to the International Osteoporosis Foundation (IOF), between 60 and 80% of women in European countries who sustain a fracture do not receive appropriate post-fracture osteoporosis treatment [23].

FLS has been shown to significantly reduce this treatment gap and lower the incidence of subsequent fractures [24]. However, it remains uncommon for FLS programs to follow up with patients whose VFs are incidentally detected on imaging performed for unrelated indications, such as chest or abdominal CT scans. The integration of AI-based opportunistic detection of VFs with systematic FLS follow-up, as demonstrated in our study, represents a novel and scalable strategy for secondary fracture prevention.

Among all patients identified with VF (n = 530, corresponding to 4.3 patients per day), 42% were reported in the clinical radiology reports (equivalent to 1.8 patients per day). According to aggregated statistics from the Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare, approximately 1.2 patients per day received a clinical diagnosis of VF (ICD codes S22/S32) in the catchment area 2023.

Focusing specifically on patients categorised as “Action needed” (2.2 patients per day), i.e., those requiring further clinical management, only 0.8 patients per day were reported in the radiology reports—corresponding to approximately one in three patients. These figures suggest that integrating AI with the FLS has substantial potential to increase radiology reporting rates and strengthen subsequent triage within the FLS workflow.

Further analyses of effectiveness regarding VF diagnosis and the initiation of appropriate osteoporosis treatment directly comparing “usual care” with an “AI‑supported opportunistic VF screening approach integrated with FLS” will be highly valuable.

Beyond improving patient outcomes, FLS programs have also been demonstrated to be cost-effective, reducing healthcare expenditures associated with refractures, hospital admissions, and surgical interventions [25, 26]. The ability to identify patients at risk and intervene earlier not only improves clinical outcomes but also contributes to more sustainable healthcare systems. In a planned follow-up study, we will also investigate if this is true in our study cohort.

Characteristics of patients identified with VF

In our study, the majority of VFs occurred in patients aged 70–90 years (70%), who also accounted for most CT referrals (57%). VF prevalence increased with age, from 4% in those aged 50–60 to 45% in patients aged ≥ 90, aligning with previous studies [27]. While CT exams were equally distributed by sex (49% men, 51% women), women had a significantly higher VF prevalence (62%), reflecting known osteoporosis risk differences [28]. Despite confirmed VFs on historical CTs, only a minority had prior osteoporosis (47%) and/or VF diagnoses (41%), or ongoing treatment (38%), highlighting a major diagnostic gap [23, 29]. Although the AI tool Flamingo was available, routine care radiologists reported only 42% of VFs, likely due to a lack of awareness about the AI integration as well as limited knowledge about clinical significance of VF. In our study we chose not to inform the radiologists about the new AI report in PACS, nor did we have any proactive information about the study and new flow. Thus, just adding an AI result without further information did not affect the reporting frequencies considerably.

Patients triaged as not requiring further action more often had prior VF and/or osteoporosis diagnoses and were more frequently treated for osteoporosis, suggesting better prior clinical attention. Conversely, those needing follow-up were markedly undertreated; only 8% received anti-osteoporotic therapy, mostly alendronate, with no use of denosumab or osteoanabolics (teriparatide or romosozumab)—despite guideline recommendations for high-risk patients [7, 30]. These findings underscore a global trend of VF underdiagnosis and undertreatment. A key differentiator between groups was imaging history; patients not needing further action had more prior CTs performed and better VF documentation, likely enabling earlier care. In contrast, missed or vague reporting in the undertreated group reflects missed intervention opportunities.

Limitations/strengths

Our study has several limitations. First, its single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, as the study was conducted at a tertiary university hospital, the patient population may be skewed toward individuals with more severe or complex health conditions. In particular, the presence of specialized units, such as oncology, may have contributed to a higher observed prevalence of VF.

Strengths of the study include a large sample size and a low risk of selection bias, as all women and men aged ≥ 50 years referred for CT imaging over a four-month period were included. Additionally, the availability of detailed data on prior osteoporosis and VF diagnoses, treatments, medical history, and radiological records enhance the robustness of the findings.

Conclusion

Although VFs are common and significantly increase the risk of imminent fractures, FLSs often fail to identify these cases. Integrating an AI algorithm to flag potential VFs showed to be feasible and robust improving the identification of at-risk patients and potentially reducing subsequent fractures. The new system identified 2–3 patients each day in need of further osteoporosis evaluation. These findings support the widespread adoption of AI in FLSs as a clinically effective approach. The cost-effectiveness of such a method requires further investigation, but its potential is assessed as promising with the present data in mind.
