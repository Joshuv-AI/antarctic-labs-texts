Validity, Reliability and Application of the Paediatric Posterior Drooling Scale
Authors: Denise C R M Koeken, Karen van Hulst, Marloes L J Lagarde, Robert F Pangalila, Corrie E Erasmus, Lenie van den Engel‐Hoek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThis study aims to assess the construct validity, inter‐rater and intra‐rater reliability and application of the newly developed Paediatric Posterior Drooling Scale (PPDS), a novel clinical screening tool that uses cervical auscultation (CA) to evaluate posterior drooling in children with neurological disorders.MethodsIn this cross‐sectional observational study, 69 children with central neurological disorders aged 1–18 years (mean 8.4 years, 50 males, 19 females) were included for analysis. CA was used by expert and trained speech language therapists (SLT). Due to the correlation between posterior drooling and oropharyngeal dysphagia, two classification systems for eating and drinking ability were used for convergent construct validity calculations: the Functional Oral Intake Scale (FOIS) for children complemented with the Eating and Drinking Ability Classification System (EDACS) in the case of cerebral palsy.ResultsPPDS had a moderate, negative correlation with the FOIS (Spearman's rho = −0.54, p < 0.001) and a moderate, positive correlation with the EDACS (Spearman's rho = 0.70, p < 0.001). PPDS had an excellent intra‐rater (intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.91, p < 0.001) and a good inter‐rater reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.85, p < 0.001). The perfect agreement percentage of the test–retest reliability was high (84%) for the score 0.ConclusionsThe PPDS is a valid and reliable clinically applicable screening tool for assessing posterior drooling by the SLT. The PPDS enables SLTs to identify posterior drooling more efficiently, non‐invasively and reliably in routine clinical settings. Timely recognition of posterior drooling can significantly impact clinical management, particularly by reducing respiratory complications. Performing subsequent PPDS assessments at different times when posterior drooling is suspected is important for the treating paediatrician to determine the underlying cause of posterior drooling. To establish the diagnosis of posterior drooling, the PPDS should not be used as a stand‐alone tool but as a bridge between clinical observations and the need for an instrumental assessment.Keywords: central neurological disorders, cervical auscultation, drooling, oropharyngeal dysphagia, paediatric

Introduction

Drooling, or lack of saliva control, is a normal phenomenon in development during childhood until the age of four (Johnson et al. 2001; Chaleat‐Valayer et al. 2016; van Hulst, van den Engel‐Hoek, et al. 2018). In children with neurological disorders, drooling can be the result of impaired oral motor functioning, reduced swallowing frequency, and difficulty maintaining an upright position of head and trunk (Erasmus et al. 2009). Therefore, drooling has been associated with oropharyngeal dysphagia, which is present in 50.4% of the children with cerebral palsy (CP), a neurological disorder. The prevalence of drooling in individuals with CP is 44% (Speyer et al. 2019).

Two types of drooling can be differentiated, which can appear independently or may co‐exist: anterior and posterior. Anterior drooling is defined as unintentional loss of saliva from the mouth, while posterior drooling is described as saliva spilling into the pharynx, leading to pooling of saliva or saliva aspiration (Jongerius et al. 2005; Lee et al. 2017; Delsing et al. 2021). This excess saliva can potentially enter the airway, causing issues such as coughing, choking or respiratory problems, especially when lying down or with the head in extension. The cause of posterior drooling is an impaired or missing trigger to swallow due to lack of sensorimotor control affecting swallowing coordination. These coordination problems characterize oropharyngeal dysphagia in children with central neurological disorders. As a result, posterior drooling is associated with an increased risk of aspiration, which can lead to substantial respiratory morbidity, requiring antibiotic treatment (Jongerius et al. 2005; Lee et al. 2017; Delsing et al. 2021; Pena et al. 2009; Hughes and Lambert 2022). Consequently, the reduced quality of life of these patients is seen in terms of illness, hospitalization and inability to join social activities. The prevalence of posterior drooling is unknown, but the estimation is 10%–15% in the population of children with complex neurologic disabilities (Delsing et al. 2021).

Posterior drooling might be missed or underdiagnosed in clinical practice due to lack of awareness or misidentification. Timely diagnosis is crucial to prevent respiratory morbidity, prolonged use of antibiotics and hospitalization, which hinders daily activities. Saliva aspiration is not visible through a videofluoroscopic swallow study. Therefore, valid and reliable diagnostic tools are needed. Speech language therapists (SLTs), physiotherapists, occupational therapists and paediatricians play a role in multidisciplinary management by recognizing and identifying both anterior and posterior drooling (AACPDM n.d.). Although validated instruments have been developed to measure anterior drooling, no validated instruments are available to detect or measure posterior drooling (van Hulst et al. 2012; Thomas‐Stonell and Greenberg 1988).

SLTs typically depend on clinical information to assess drooling severity, such as signs and symptoms of recurrent respiratory tract infections, number of recurrent episodes of respiratory infections and frequency of antibiotic use, history of lung infections and the need for frequent suctioning. Additional information can be obtained with an invasive assessment of fibreoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES), though complicated in children with neurological disorders. Afterwards, a Murray secretion scale score can be determined based on visual information about excessive secretions in the hypopharynx (saliva stasis) to predict aspiration risk (Murray et al. 1996; Leslie et al. 2004; Kuo et al. 2017). However, the use of this scale has not been studied in children. Another option is a non‐validated posterior drooling scale described by Shoval and colleagues (Hughes and Lambert 2022). This scale ranges from a score of 0 to 4 (0 = no choking or coughing, 1 = choking/coughing while lying down, 2 = choking/coughing while sitting up, 3 = one aspiration pneumonia in the last 12 months, 4 = two or more aspiration pneumonias in the last 12 months). This scale provides valuable information regarding choking or coughing; however, it lacks detailed information on saliva secretion and the stasis of breathing sounds.

All instruments described above are unsuitable for clinical use in children. To determine the severity of posterior drooling or to measure the effect of interventions, repeatedly testing is required. Posterior drooling can vary through time due to its multifactorial nature (e.g., the influence of head‐trunk position, tiredness, food‐induced saliva production before or after mealtime, neurological status and reflux or medication). There was a compelling need to develop a clinically useful scale capable of distinguishing the presence or absence of saliva in the airway based on the quality of breath sounds before and after swallowing saliva. Cervical auscultation (CA), which involves using a stethoscope to assess the quality of breathing and saliva swallowing sounds, appears to be a highly suitable adjunct for detecting posterior drooling (Leslie et al. 2004; Lagarde et al. 2016). Previous research showed that CA is a non‐invasive tool to detect swallowing (the act of passing food or saliva through the hypopharynx), in order to evaluate the quality of pre‐ and post‐swallow respiration sounds and to gain information about swallowing frequency (Lagarde et al. 2016). In 2017, SLTs of the saliva control outpatient clinic (SCOC) of the Radboud University Centre constructed the Paediatric Posterior Drooling Scale (PPDS; see Figure 1) to provide clear and uniform definitions for SLTs to score the presence and severity of posterior drooling by using CA (van Hulst et al. 2017). It consists of a nominal five‐point classification scale in which the lowest rating (0) indicates no posterior drooling, and ratings 1–4 indicate the possible presence and severity of posterior drooling. The development of this scale was based on the available research (i.e., CA can be used reliable to detect swallowing and to evaluate the quality of pre‐ and post swallow respiration) and the Murray secretion rate severity scale with the addition of the swallowing component (Murray et al. 1996; Leslie et al. 2004; Kuo et al. 2017; Lagarde et al. 2016). A small pilot study with the PPDS showed excellent inter‐rater reliability, but more extensive research and evaluation in a larger sample was needed (van Hulst et al. 2017). Therefore, this study aimed to determine the PPDS's (1) construct validity, (2) intra‐rater and inter‐rater reliability, and (3) assess its practical application in clinical settings.

Materials and Methods

This cross‐sectional observational study was conducted between January and July 2019 in two centres in the Netherlands, the Radboud University Medical Centre (RUMC) and Rijndam Rehabilitation (RR) a child rehabilitation centre.

Participants

After obtaining written informed consent from the parents/caregivers, children aged 1–18 years with central neurological disorders visiting the RUMC or visiting the SLT for various treatment goals at RR were included. No exclusion criteria were defined. No specific instructions were given to the children because only a spontaneous saliva swallow had to be assessed by CA within 2 min. Data on diagnosis, age and sex were collected.

Procedures

The PPDS score was based on CA during a speech language therapy session to create a safe environment for participants. The PPDS assessment was performed at least 1 h post‐meal to prevent any influence of food‐induced saliva production on the quality of breathing (Pedersen et al. 2018). The stethoscope was used to detect and listen to the quality of breathing before and after a spontaneous saliva swallow of the participant. The stethoscope was placed on the lateral side of the larynx (see Figure 2), and its soft earpieces were placed on the microphones of a voice recorder for direct sound recording (see Figure 3). A 2‐min audio recording was made, considering that a child is expected to swallow its saliva spontaneously at least once in this timespan (Senner et al. 2004). In some cases, the participant was asked to swallow. If no swallow occurred within 2 min, the audio recording was stopped to prevent discomfort, and a level 4 score was assigned in case of wet breath.

In this study, eight SLTs were engaged with different roles. Audio recordings were generated and scored by an expert SLT at RUMC or the SLT researcher, trained in the use of CA, receiving assistance from the participant's regular SLT at RR. For construct validity, the participant's regular SLT or the involved SLT of the saliva control team rated the Functional Oral Intake Score (FOIS) and, in the case of CP, the Eating and Drinking Ability Classification System (EDACS) also (Sellers et al. 2014; Benfer et al. 2017; van Hulst, Snik, et al. 2018; Crary et al. 2005; Dodrill 2015). Differences in scoring between pairs of SLTs for inter‐rater reliability were evaluated based on coded and anonymous audio recordings by a second researcher from the opposite centre to score a PPDS level. The USB stick was personally delivered by the first author.

Construct Validity

This study focused on convergent construct validity. It was assessed by comparing scores of the PPDS with the FOIS and the EDACS. To make comparison possible, a consensus score was needed in case of unequal PPDS scores between two researchers. This consensus was reached through discussion by two SLT researchers (see Section 2.5). The FOIS was used for construct validity because it has been proven to be a valid and reliable scale for stroke patients, with a high consensual validity, high criterion validity and a good inter‐rater reliability (Crary et al. 2005). The scale was developed for clinical documentation of changes in functional oral intake of liquids and food and is adapted for paediatrics (Dodrill 2015). Seven levels are described, in which the lowest score (1) indicates no oral intake, whereas the highest score (7) indicates a normal intake with no restrictions compared with peers (Crary et al. 2005; Dodrill 2015). The EDACS was used complementary in children with CP to classify the safety and efficiency of functional eating and drinking abilities. It has been proven to be a valid and reliable classification system for children with this diagnosis, with a strong correlation supporting validity, a good inter‐rater reliability and an excellent inter‐rater reliability and is widely used in clinical practice (Benfer et al. 2017; van Hulst, Snik, et al. 2018). Using the key features of safety and efficiency, the EDACS describes five distinct levels of ability of eating and drinking. The lowest score (I) indicates the ability to eat and drink safely and efficiently, whereas the highest score (V) indicates no ability to eat and drink safely and efficiently (Sellers et al. 2014; Benfer et al. 2017; van Hulst, Snik, et al. 2018). Both scales classify oral intake of children as a general indication for oral feeding function (Sellers et al. 2014; Benfer et al. 2017; van Hulst, Snik, et al. 2018; Crary et al. 2005). Unlike both FOIS and EDACS, which classify the swallowing of food or liquids, the PPDS focuses on swallowing saliva, expecting a low correlation between the scales.

Reliability Study

For inter‐rater reliability, differences in PPDS levels were assessed between the expert SLT researchers from RUMC and the trained SLT researcher from RR, scored based on the audio recordings. Intra‐rater reliability was evaluated by having the same researcher, who conducted the initial evaluation and generated the audio recording, score the recordings again after 2 weeks. Because four SLT researchers that performed the PPDS were not participating researchers in this study, intra‐rater reliability calculations were done in a subgroup of 50 participants (72%) by the other four researcher SLTs.

Application of the PPDS

Because of the multifactorial nature of posterior drooling, the outcome was assessed at two different times to measure the consistency of the scores. Therefore, 4 weeks after the initial PPDS examination, the assessment was repeated (test–retest) in a subset of 19 children from RR. Not all children could be retested due to the limited timespan of data collection planning.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized participant characteristics (age, sex and diagnosis). Construct validity was determined by calculating the correlation between the PPDS consensus ratings with the FOIS scores, using Spearman's rho. In the case of CP diagnosis, the correlation between the PPDS consensus ratings and the EDACS was scored as well. Inter‐rater and intra‐rater reliability of the PPDS scores were assessed using percentage perfect agreement and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). For inter‐rater reliability, a two‐way random‐effects model with single measurement and absolute agreement was used. For intra‐rater reliability, a two‐way mixed‐effects model with single measurement and absolute agreement was used (Dodrill 2015). Test–retest was assessed with percentage perfect agreement. Missing data were considered as missing completely at random and omitted from analysis. Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics 24.

Ethics

This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki principles. The Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act was not applicable in accordance with the Medical Ethical Committee (File number CMO: 2018‐4690).

Three participants were excluded from analysis because it was not possible to make a (reliable) recording due to technical reasons or a participant's refusal to undergo assessment. PPDS score could not be assigned (clear breathing for 2 min without swallowing) to four participants. As a result, analysis was done in 69 children. Reliability

Inter‐rater reliability, calculated over all 69 participants, showed a perfect agreement of 87%. An ICC of 0.85 (95% CI 0.77–0.90) showed a significant (p < 0.001) good inter‐rater reliability between expert and trained researchers (see Table 3). Intra‐rater reliability, calculated over 50 participants, showed a perfect agreement of 92%. Calculations were significant (p < 0.001) with an ICC of 0.91 (95% CI 0.84–0.95), indicating an excellent intra‐rater reliability (Cicchetti and Sparrow 1981).

Discussion

Posterior drooling can lead to pneumonia, serious respiratory morbidity and hospitalization and reduces quality of life, which emphasizes the importance of timely diagnosis. This study assessed the validity, reliability and application of the PPDS in children with central neurologic disorders. It is the first instrument developed to assess posterior drooling. In clinical care, questions regarding frequent gurgling, coughing, choking and history of respiratory health determine the presence of posterior drooling (Jongerius et al. 2005). The PPDS demonstrated significant, moderate correlations with FOIS and EDACS for convergent construct validity, along with significant, excellent intra‐rater and significant, good inter‐rater reliability. While we found poor and not significant test–retest reliability, the PPDS appears valid, reliable and applicable as an element in examining posterior drooling in clinical care and future research.

The excellent inter‐rater reliability of the PPDS was consistent with the previously found inter‐rater reliability in a small pilot study (van Hulst et al. 2017). The excellent inter‐rater reliability for the PPDS using CA is presumably due to both the provided clear definitions of the sounds and the participation of CA trained SLTs, which both can be described as strengths of this study. The previously found poor to fair inter‐reliability of CA is probably caused by unclear definitions used and a focus on the presence/absence of aspiration rather than the presence of saliva‐pooling (Lagarde et al. 2016). Another strength of this study is the generalisability to clinical practice because a wide population of children with various diagnoses were included in the participant group.

Despite the clear instructions and definitions of the PDDS, not all complex clinical scenarios could be handled based on this protocol. In general, not swallowing saliva within a 2‐min timeframe is atypical, even for children with CP who have lower swallowing frequencies (Crary et al. 2022). Typically developing children between the ages of 7 and 18 are swallowing at a mean interval of 48 s and children with CP between 57 and 80 s (Senner et al. 2004). The finding of clear breathing without swallowing within 2 min seems to be quite atypical and therefore important to document as reduced swallowing frequency is associated with (posterior) drooling (Erasmus et al. 2009). An addition to the PDDS, including scoring for ‘clear breath, no swallow for two minutes’ is recommended, as although this finding does not necessarily indicate the presence or severity of posterior drooling. Therefore, in this situation, it is advised to repeat the PPDS at a different time to clarify this. Another addition to the PPDS is to document the highest PPDS score when a participant swallowed multiple times, scoring different PPDS levels in the 2‐min observation. This approach highlights the potential problems a child might experience. Additionally, repeating the assessment at different times is recommended to capture possible variations throughout the day due to the multifactorial nature of posterior drooling.

The study's limitation arises from the skewed distribution of PPDS scores due to inclusion criteria. Because we defined no exclusion criteria, the included participant group was randomly selected within a child rehabilitation centre and neurological outpatient clinic and did not consist only of children with saliva control problems. The skew in the participant group is particularly evident when looking at the PPDS scores: 68% of the participants scored a 0 on the PPDS, indicating a clear breath before and after a saliva swallow. However, when we look at the estimated prevalence of posterior drooling in children with complex, severe or profound intellectual disabilities, which is around 10%–15% (Delsing et al. 2021), we observe that in the studied group of participants with central neurological disorders, a non‐zero score is found in 31% of the children, which would indicate that we have studied a representative group. Also, the FOIS scores, as shown in Table 2, are more normally distributed. Specifically, only 29% of the participants scored a FOIS level 6, that is, a total oral diet without restrictions, while the majority (71%) experienced some restrictions to their oral diet, indicating the presence of swallowing or feeding difficulties. However, not all the children with feeding or swallowing difficulties also have issues with swallowing saliva. It depends on the phase of swallowing in which the issues occur (Jongerius et al. 2005; Lee et al. 2017). Unfortunately, it was not possible to select posterior drooling as an inclusion criterion because prior to this study there were no valid or reliable diagnostic tools available.

The prevalence of 0‐scores in our study may have impacted the observed intra‐rater, inter‐rater and test–retest reliability. Previous studies described that CA assesses clear breathing more reliably than pharyngeal residue (Leslie et al. 2004; Lagarde et al. 2016; Zenner et al. 1995), potentially explaining the found higher level of agreement in the PPDS 0‐scores. Regarding test–retest reliability, the multifactorial nature of posterior drooling influencing its severity throughout the day (by factors as head‐trunk position, tiredness, food‐induced saliva production before or after mealtime, neurological status, reflux or medication use) presents an obstacle in establishing a favourable test–retest reliability in any PPDS score other than 0 (Hughes and Lambert 2022). This emphasizes the importance of performing the PPDS assessment at different times, days and situations, for example, in various postures (sitting/lying) or moments of the day in these vulnerable children.

In conclusion, the PPDS is a valid and reliable tool to assess the presence and severity of posterior drooling in clinical practice and could be used in future research. The PPDS is a useful, applicable and non‐invasive element in the posterior drooling examination, but should not be used as a standalone tool. By applying the recommendations including taking the highest score and repeating the assessment at different times, the PPDS can function as a bridge between clinical observations and the need for an instrumental assessment (FEES) to definitely diagnose posterior drooling. The consistency of posterior drooling in a child provided by multiple testing with the PPDS is essential information (knowledge) for the multidisciplinary team involved. Considering that posterior drooling can lead to substantial respiratory problems and pneumonia, timely diagnosis and a carefully considered multidisciplinary treatment plan can significantly impact children's prognosis.

This study contributes to the current body of knowledge by introducing the first structured and validated clinical screening tool specifically for posterior drooling, a gap previously unaddressed in paediatric care. By providing a reliable, non‐invasive and standardized method that can be easily applied by SLTs, the PPDS has the potential to serve as an alternative to the informal or non‐specific evaluation methods currently used in clinical practice.

Future research is necessary to investigate (1) if the use of the PPDS leads to a more patient‐centred treatment approach, (2) if the PPDS can be used to evaluate treatment and (3) the reliability and validity in adult patients.
