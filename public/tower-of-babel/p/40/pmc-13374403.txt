Serum IgA autoantibody profiling in systemic lupus erythematosus reveals associations with disease phenotypes
Authors: Laura E Eades, Artemis Papadaki, Katie Lennard, Alberta Y Hoi, Rangi Kandane-Rathnayake, Eric F Morand, William A Figgett, Fabien B Vincent
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKnowledge of the role of IgA isotype autoantibodies in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is lacking. We aimed to explore associations between serum IgA autoantibody profiles and SLE clinical phenotypes using an unbiased approach.Methods1613 serum IgA autoantibodies were quantified in 60 patients with SLE using the i-Ome protein array, customised to also detect antibodies against three interferons (IFNα1, IFNω and IFNγ). Using clustering analysis, patients were grouped according to IgA autoantibody profiles; associations between clusters and clinical features were assessed using multinomial logistic regression. Associations between individual autoantibodies and clinical features were assessed via differential abundance analysis and supervised learning.ResultsFive patient clusters were identified; patients in cluster 4, characterised by antitransforming acidic coiled-coil-containing protein 1 (anti-TACC1) and anti-cAMP-dependent protein kinase type I-alpha regulatory subunit (anti-PRKAR1A) IgA profiles, were significantly more likely to be of Asian ethnicity and have lupus nephritis than those in clusters 1 and 3. Patients in cluster 4 and those in cluster 3, characterised by antiangiomotin-like protein 2 (anti-AMOTL2) IgA profiles, were significantly more likely to use immunosuppression than those in cluster 2. Analysis of individual autoantibodies revealed a significant association between antisignal transducing adaptor molecule 2 (anti-STAM2) and haematological disease activity. Targeted analysis demonstrated associations between several autoantibodies to IFN-associated proteins and SLE phenotypes, including ethnicity, disease activity, immunosuppression and organ damage.ConclusionsClustering according to IgA autoantibody profiles suggests the presence of a subgroup of patients with SLE associated with Asian ethnicity, lupus nephritis and immunosuppression use. Additionally, a novel association between anti-STAM2 IgA and haematological disease was discovered. These findings demonstrate the pathophysiological relevance of IgA autoantibodies in SLE and suggest their potential as SLE biomarkers.Keywords: Lupus Erythematosus, Systemic; Biomarkers; Autoantibodies; Lupus Nephritis; interferon; immunoglobulin; omics

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a systemic, inflammatory, autoimmune condition, which is highly heterogenous and can affect virtually any organ system.1 SLE disproportionately affects women, as well as people of non-European ethnicity, including people of Asian, African and First Nations ethnicities worldwide.2 Clinical trials are frequently limited by the clinical and biological heterogeneity of patients, resulting in few treatments receiving regulatory approval in recent decades.1 Therefore, there is a need for improved stratification of patients with SLE into distinct biological subgroups, in order to better predict which patients are likely to respond to particular treatments. One such stratification method is the use of novel biomarkers, such as circulating proteins or autoantibodies.3

One of the defining characteristics of SLE is the presence of circulating autoantibodies.4 Currently, most knowledge regarding autoantibodies in SLE relates to IgG isotypes.5 IgG antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and IgG autoantibodies to double-stranded (ds) DNA and extractable nuclear antigens (ENA) are currently used as part of SLE classification criteria.6 Furthermore, certain ENA autoantibodies are associated with disease phenotypes, and anti-dsDNA titre forms part of the most widely used measure of SLE disease activity.7 8

Less is known about the role of IgA isotype autoantibodies in SLE. IgA antibodies are predominantly found at mucosal surfaces and in secretions, and play an important role in protection against pathogens at these sites.9 IgA isotypes corresponding to typical IgG autoantibodies associated with SLE have been described.9 10 For example, elevated levels of faecal IgA ANA and IgA autoantibodies to dsDNA and nucleohistone proteins have been described in patients with SLE,10 and serum anti-dsDNA IgA has been shown to correlate with SLE disease activity, similar to its IgG counterpart.9 IgA autoantibodies have also been postulated to play a pathogenic role in SLE; for example, circulating IgA autoantibodies to type VII collagen have been described in vesiculobullous SLE.11

High-throughput, high-plex serum autoantibody profiling provides a unique opportunity to simultaneously measure thousands of autoantibodies, thus facilitating the discovery of novel biomarkers.12 A recent study using such profiling technology in SLE by Lindblom et al demonstrated that IgG autoantibody profiles differed in patients with SLE compared with healthy controls.3 In addition, authors identified a number of novel IgG autoantibodies associated with disease activity as measured by the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K), including anti-PABPC1 and anti-APEX1.3 In another recent study of high-plex serum IgG autoantibody profiling performed by our group in the same SLE patient cohort as described in this study, several novel biomarkers associated with clinical phenotypes were identified (Eades et al, manuscript under review).

To our knowledge, only one study to date has examined high-plex serum IgA autoantibody profiles in SLE.3 In parallel with their findings regarding IgG isotypes, Lindblom et al found that IgA autoantibody profiles in patients with SLE differed compared with healthy controls.3 They also found several IgA autoantibodies that were associated with increased disease activity, including anti-interferon (IFN) regulatory factor 5 (IRF5) and anti-FEN1, as well as an IgA autoantibody associated with lupus low disease activity state (LLDAS), anti-MAPK12.3 However, no previous studies have explored associations between IgA autoantibody profiles and validated longitudinal clinical outcome measures in SLE, such as time spent in LLDAS or Definition of Remission in SLE (DORIS) remission, or used supervised machine learning to develop predictive IgA autoantibody models for clinical phenotypes and outcomes.

In this study, we therefore specifically aimed to generate high-plex serum IgA autoantibody profiles in patients with SLE using an unbiased approach, focusing on this comparatively understudied isotype and investigating their relationships with disease phenotypes and outcomes.

Materials and methods

The methodology and patient cohort for this study were the same as for our recent study of high-plex serum IgG isotype autoantibody profiling in SLE (Eades et al, manuscript under review).

Study participants

Sixty patients fulfilling classification criteria for SLE (the 1997 revised American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria or the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics (SLICC) criteria),6 13 enrolled in the Australian Lupus Registry and Biobank (ALRB)14 and recruited through the Monash Health Lupus Clinic, were included in this exploratory, hypothesis-generating single-centre retrospective study. Within this predefined sample size, a purposive sampling strategy was used to ensure balanced and structured representation across key clinical and demographic variables, particularly self-reported ethnicity, age, sex and disease activity as defined by SLEDAI-2K.7

Biological sample collection, processing and storage

A single serum sample from each study participant, collected between July 2019 and November 2020, without freeze/thaw cycle, was used for autoantibody profiling analysis. Venous blood collection, processing and serum storage was performed as described previously.15

Data collection and clinical assessment

Socio-demographic data (date of birth, sex, self-reported ethnicity) were extracted from the ALRB. Cross-sectional clinical data extracted from the clinic visit closest to study sample collection (within 30 days) included disease activity (SLEDAI-2K),7 flare (SELENA-SLEDAI Flare Index (SFI)),16 medication use and routine laboratory tests. Data relating to organ damage, assessed using the SLICC/ACR Damage Index (SDI),17 were extracted from the most recent annual visit. For a complete description of clinical data extracted, please see online supplemental table 1.

Longitudinal clinical data were extracted for each patient visit, from the time of ALRB enrolment to the end of the study period (October 2023) (median (IQR) length of study duration 9.5 (6.5, 13.1) years). Adjusted mean SLEDAI-2K (AMS) was derived as previously described.18 Active disease over the study period was defined as AMS >4, as previously described.19 Percentage time spent in LLDAS or DORIS remission across the study period were calculated, and categorised to <50% vs ≥50% of cumulative time in LLDAS or DORIS remission.20 Organ-specific disease activity was defined as the presence of a positive SLEDAI-2K score at least once across the study period for each clinical SLEDAI-2K organ domain, as previously described.19

High-plex serum autoantibody profiling

High-plex dual detection and relative quantification of serum IgA and IgG autoantibodies was performed by Standard BioTools (Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia), using the i-Ome protein array, based on Sengenics’ patented KREX protein folding technology (n=1613 full length and correctly folded antigens). This array was customised to include autoantibodies to IFNα1 (Uniprot ID: P01562), IFNγ (Uniprot ID: P01579) and IFNω (Uniprot ID: P05000). Only the IgA autoantibody profiles were explored in this study.

Statistical and bioinformatics analyses

Data analyses and visualisation were performed using R (V.4.4.0) and RStudio (V.2025.05.1). Online supplemental table 2 shows a full list of R packages and functions used for individual analyses. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Data transformation, normalisation and filtering

No imputation was required, as there were no missing values for autoantibody abundance. Log2 transformation of raw mean net intensity values was performed as part of the locally estimated scatter plot smoothing (LOESS) normalisation approach. Normalisation to the ‘average’ array was then performed using LOESS normalisation,21 followed by ComBat normalisation to facilitate batch correction, using the R packages and functions listed in online supplemental table 2. Negative control filtering (NCF) was then performed to account for background noise and ensure specificity in autoantibody detection. This resulted in the removal of approximately two-thirds of the autoantibodies; those removed were the most strongly correlated with non-specific binding control spots on the arrays and therefore least likely to be clinically significant.

Heatmaps and outlier assessment

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to allow identification of patient outliers via visual inspection of PCA plots. Heatmaps with hierarchical clustering were produced to visualise scaled and centred IgA autoantibody abundance across the patient cohort. Visual inspection of heatmaps was then performed to assess whether outlier status may be a result of technical error, and clinical data were reviewed to determine whether outliers shared similar clinical phenotypes.

Descriptive statistics

Patients were excluded from analysis of a particular clinical feature if they had missing data for that clinical feature. Categorical variables are reported as frequency (percentage). Normally and non-normally distributed continuous variables are summarised as mean (SD) or median (IQR), respectively.

Unsupervised clustering analysis

Using normalised, row-centred data from the subset of NCF-selected autoantibodies present in at least 5% of participants, unsupervised clustering was based on a Partitioning Around Medoids (PAM) approach, with the optimal number of clusters (k) informed by maximum silhouette width (online supplemental figure 1, see further detail in online supplemental methods). Associations between clusters (outcome) and clinical features (exposure) were explored using univariable and multivariable multinomial logistic regression. In multivariable analyses, covariates adjusted for included age and ethnicity; it was not possible to adjust for sex, as several clusters contained no male patients. In the multinomial logistic regression models, all clusters were directly compared with each other by using each cluster in turn as the reference cluster.

Differential abundance analysis

Differential abundance analysis was performed using multivariable linear regression following scaling and centering of the autoantibody matrix to investigate associations between clinical features (exposure) and autoantibodies (outcome). One set of multivariable models adjusted for the poly-specific autoantibody (PSA) signal only (autoantibody abundance~clinical feature+PSA), while the other adjusted for PSA signal, age, sex and ethnicity (autoantibody abundance~clinical feature+PSA+age+sex+ethnicity). Positive PSA signal reflects the poly-specific presence of many autoantibodies in an individual and has the potential to confound results if not adjusted for. PSA signal is not known to be associated with any autoimmune rheumatic diseases, including SLE, and therefore was thought unlikely to be of clinical significance in our patient cohort. Adjustment for multiple testing was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Inclusion of clinical features for multivariable modelling required a minimum of n=12 patients per subgroup, except in the case of SLEDAI-2K organ-specific involvement, in which subgroups with a minimum of n=4 patients were included for multivariable analyses adjusting for PSA signal only, to enable the exploration of less common organ involvement. However, no multivariable model adjusting for socio-demographic covariates is reported for these domains, as patient numbers were considered too small to allow adjustment for multiple covariates.

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator

Supervised machine learning using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) was performed, with a separate model trained to select predictive features for each categorical clinical feature of interest (outcome) (see further detail in online supplemental methods). Exposures in the LASSO models included all autoantibodies, as well as patient age, sex, ethnicity and PSA signal. Scaling and centering of both autoantibody abundance and covariates was performed as part of the LASSO models, in order to facilitate comparison between all potential predictors in the model. Results were not reported in cases where the LASSO model was considered unsatisfactory (overall model accuracy <0.7)

Targeted analysis of IFN pathway-associated IgA autoantibodies

Targeted analysis of IFN pathway-associated IgA autoantibodies was performed in addition to the unbiased analyses described previously, given the known role of IFN pathways in SLE.22 IgA autoantibodies to IFNα1, IFNγ and IFNω, as well as to interferon-induced protein with tetratricopeptide repeats 1 (IFIT1), interferon-induced 35 kDa protein (IFI35) and IRF5 were included in the targeted analysis. Associations between clinical features (exposure) and IFN-associated autoantibodies (outcome) were assessed using multivariable linear regression. Identical covariates were used for the multivariable differential abundance analysis models. Given this was a hypothesis-driven targeted analysis, no adjustment for multiple testing was performed.

Results

Cohort characteristics

The cohort comprised 60 patients with SLE with a median (IQR) age of 42 (34–52) years (table 1). As per the study design, the cohort was balanced across key characteristics, particularly with equal representation of European (50%) and Asian (50%) ethnicities, and patients with active (50%; SLEDAI-2K >4) vs inactive disease (50%; SLEDAI-2K ≤4) at the time of study blood sample collection. Overall, 90% of patients were female. A total of 62% had persistently active disease, defined as an adjusted mean SLEDAI-2K (AMS) >4 across the study period.

Serum IgA autoantibody profiles in SLE

A total of 534 serum IgA autoantibodies remained in the final dataset following data filtering via NCF. IgA autoantibody profiles were highly heterogenous across the patient cohort (figure 1A). PCA identified four potential outliers (figure 1A,B); although review of their clinical data revealed no defining characteristics to differentiate these patients from the rest of the cohort and heatmap assessment showed no indication of a technical issue affecting these samples. Therefore, these potential outliers were retained in the analysis (figure 1A).

Unsupervised clustering analysis

We first explored whether subgroups of patients with SLE existed according to serum IgA autoantibody profiles using an unbiased data-driven approach. Unsupervised PAM clustering (optimal number of clusters: 5) was used to determine patient clusters, using only autoantibodies with a positive signal in >5% of samples (n=27) (online supplemental figure 1). Cluster 1 consisted of patients with autoantibody profiles predominantly characterised by antitripartite motif containing 21 (anti-TRIM21), compared with profiles characterised by anti-IFNα1 and anti-IFNγ in cluster 2, anti-angiomotin-like protein 2 (anti-AMOTL2) in cluster 3, anti-cAMP-dependent protein kinase type I-alpha regulatory subunit (anti-PRKAR1A) and antitransforming acidic coiled-coil-containing protein 1 (anti-TACC1) in cluster 4 and antihaematopoietic lineage cell-specific protein (anti-HCLS1) in cluster 5 (figure 2). Patients in cluster 4 were significantly more likely to have biopsy-proven lupus nephritis (LN) than those in cluster 3 (adjusted OR 12.7; 95% CI 1.54 to 105; p=0.018), and those in cluster 1 on univariable regression only (adjusted OR 8.89; 95% CI 1.40 to 56.6; p=0.021), were more likely to use immunosuppressive medications than those in cluster 2 (adjusted OR 17.8; 95% CI 1.55 to 206; p=0.021) and were more likely to be of Asian ethnicity than those in cluster 1 on univariable regression only (OR 7.20; 95% CI 1.08 to 48.0; p=0.041). Patients in cluster 3 were more likely to use immunosuppressive medications other than prednisolone (OR 11.6; 95% CI 1.19 to 112, p=0.035) than those in cluster 2. There were no other significant associations between clusters and phenotypes or outcomes. Descriptive statistics for the unsupervised clustering analysis are shown in online supplemental table 3.

Associations between SLE clinical features and autoantibodies

Next, we used differential abundance analysis to investigate relationships between each autoantibody (n=534) and each cross-sectional and longitudinal clinical parameter of interest. In parallel, we sought to develop parsimonious autoantibody models for each of these clinical features, using supervised machine learning (LASSO), which could potentially be used to predict the presence of clinical features of interest in other SLE patient cohorts.

Antisignal transducing adaptor molecule 2 (anti-STAM2) autoantibody was positively associated with the presence of SLEDAI-2K haematological disease activity across the study period using multivariable differential abundance analysis adjusting for PSA signal only (β 2.09; 95% CI 1.20 to 2.98; p=0.009), and was identified in a LASSO model predictive of this clinical feature (figure 3). There were no other significant associations identified between other IgA autoantibodies and socio-demographics or other clinical features using differential abundance analysis.

In LASSO analysis of the cross-sectional clinical data, two predictive models were identified; one for active renal disease, comprising five autoantibodies (anti-ZBTB16, anti-TPM1, anti-ZNF207, anti-EEF1D, anti-IRF5) and one for steroid-free remission, comprising three autoantibodies (anti-TRAF3IP1, anti-PPP2R1A, anti-EEF1D) (table 2). In LASSO analysis of the longitudinal clinical data, two LASSO models were obtained, one for CNS involvement, comprising one autoantibody (anti-INA) and one for serositis, comprising five autoantibodies (anti-HMG20B, anti-MED22 anti-NUBP2, anti-CROT, anti-STAM2). No LASSO models were obtained for AMS, time spent in remission or time spent in LLDAS.

IFN pathway-associated IgA autoantibodies in SLE

In light of the known pathogenic role of IFN pathways in SLE,22 we performed targeted analyses to investigate relationships between SLE clinical phenotypes and IgA autoantibodies directed at components of IFN pathways. In cross-sectional analysis, using multivariable regression modelling (table 3), immunosuppressive medication use was negatively associated with anti-IFNα1 (adjusted β −0.34; 95% CI −0.65 to −0.02; p=0.035), anti-IFNω (adjusted β −0.32; 95% CI −0.59 to −0.05; p=0.022) and anti-IFNγ (adjusted β −0.35; 95% CI −0.67 to −0.02; p=0.037) (figure 4A–C). Organ damage was negatively associated with anti-IFI35 (adjusted β −0.16; 95% CI −0.31 to −0.02; p=0.025) (figure 4D). Asian ethnicity was positively associated with anti-IRF5 (adjusted β 0.21; 95% CI 0.09 to 0.33; p=0.001) (figure 4E), as was active renal disease (adjusted β 0.21; 95% CI 0.08 to 0.34; p=0.003) (figure 4F). Steroid-free remission was negatively associated with anti-IRF5 (adjusted β −0.16; 95% CI −0.311 to −0.004; p=0.044) and anti-IFIT1 (adjusted β −0.09; 95% CI −0.1803 to −0.0004; p=0.049) on multivariable regression adjusting for PSA phenotype only (data not shown).

Discussion

To date, research on autoantibodies in SLE has primarily focused on IgG isotypes, while knowledge of IgA autoantibodies in SLE is lacking, with only one previously published study examining high-plex serum IgA autoantibody profiles in patients with SLE.3 In the current study, we generated unbiased, high-plex serum IgA autoantibody profiles across 60 patients with SLE, and explored associations with detailed disease phenotypes and outcomes, including validated longitudinal clinical outcome measures. Unsupervised clustering analysis demonstrated a subgroup of patients with anti-PRKAR1A and anti-TACC1 IgA autoantibody profiles associated with Asian ethnicity, biopsy-proven LN and immunosuppressive medication use. Using supervised analyses, including differential abundance analysis and machine learning, we identified a novel IgA autoantibody, anti-STAM2, associated with haematological SLE. We also found a number of novel associations between IFN pathway-associated IgA autoantibodies and clinical features in SLE.

To our knowledge, this is the first time that patient clusters based on IgA autoantibody profiles have been demonstrated in SLE. The association of anti-PRKAR1A and anti-TACC1 autoantibody profiles with Asian ethnicity, biopsy-proven LN and immunosuppressive medication use is in keeping with known associations between Asian ethnicity, LN and immunosuppressive medication use,23 24 although anti-PRKAR1A and anti-TACC1 autoantibodies have not previously been described in patients with SLE. Interestingly, anti-PRKAR1A autoantibodies are directed against the cAMP-dependent protein kinase type I-alpha regulatory subunit of protein kinase A (PKA), which plays a role in signal transduction in T cells, and has been shown to be deficient in patients with SLE.25 Further research is warranted to explore whether the presence of anti-PRKAR1A IgA autoantibodies contribute to the PKA protein deficiency observed in SLE.

In addition, patients in a cluster driven by anti-AMOTL2 IgA were more likely to use immunosuppressive medications other than glucocorticoids than those in a cluster driven by anti-IFNα1 and anti-IFNγ. Although AMOTL2 autoantibodies have not previously been described in relation to SLE, this finding is in keeping with the negative association between both anti-IFNα1 and anti-IFNγ and immunosuppressive medication use that we found on multivariable regression, as discussed in detail below.

Compared with our recent study of IgG autoantibody profiles in patients with SLE (Eades et al, manuscript under review), we found fewer associations between individual IgA autoantibodies and clinical parameters of interest using differential abundance analysis and LASSO. This is in keeping with another recent study of high-plex serum IgG and IgA autoantibody profiling in SLE, which also found fewer clinical associations with IgA autoantibodies compared with IgG isotypes.3 This is not an unexpected finding, based on the circulating nature of IgG autoantibodies, in comparison with IgA isotypes, which are predominantly found in secretions and mucosal tissues.9

In differential abundance analysis using multivariable regression adjusted for PSA signal only, we found an association between anti-STAM2 and haematological disease activity. While it is important to note the small subgroup size of patients with haematological disease activity (n=4), anti-STAM2 was confirmed as being predictive of haematological disease activity using LASSO. STAM2 protein modulates the intensity and duration of janus kinase (JAK)26 and signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) signalling, particularly via the JAK2/STAT3 pathway.27 28 There have been no previous descriptions of anti-STAM2 autoantibodies in SLE. Given the known role of JAK signalling in SLE pathogenesis29 and the role of STAM2 protein, further research is warranted to investigate whether IgA autoantibodies to STAM2 are pro-inflammatory and contribute to haematological manifestations in SLE.

In targeted analysis of IFN pathway-associated IgA autoantibodies, we found associations between several autoantibodies to type I IFN-associated proteins and SLE clinical phenotypes. This is reflective of the known role of the type I IFN pathway in SLE pathogenesis.22 We found that IgA autoantibodies to IFNα1 and IFNω were negatively associated with immunosuppressive medication use. While IgG autoantibodies to IFNα1 and IFNω have previously been described in SLE (Eades et al, manuscript under review), and several other studies have reported the presence of autoantibodies to IFNα1 and IFNω without specifying their isotype,30 31 this is the first report of IgA autoantibodies to IFNα1 and IFNω in SLE. In our recent study, we reported an association between anti-IFNω IgG autoantibodies and reduced SLE disease activity (Eades et al, manuscript under review). Similarly, Wang et al reported an association between IgG autoantibodies targeting IFNα17 and IFNα8 and reduced disease activity and increased rates of remission in patients with SLE.12 Our finding that anti-IFNα1 and anti-IFNω IgA were negatively associated with immunosuppressive medication use is in keeping with this and is mechanistically plausible if these autoantibodies are neutralising, given the pro-inflammatory role of type I IFN proteins in SLE.22

We found that anti-IRF5 IgA was positively associated with Asian ethnicity and active renal disease and, conversely, negatively associated with steroid-free remission on cross-sectional clinical data analysis; anti-IRF5 IgA was positively associated with AMS on longitudinal clinical data analysis, suggesting an overall positive association between anti-IRF5 and SLE activity and severity. This is in keeping with a recent study by Lindblom et al, which showed that anti-IRF5 IgA is positively associated with SLEDAI-2K and negatively associated with LLDAS.3 Furthermore, in our recent study of high-plex serum IgG autoantibody profiling in SLE, anti-IRF5 IgG was associated with increased renal disease activity (Eades et al, manuscript under review). However, to our knowledge, this is the first time that anti-IRF5 IgA has been described in association with ethnicity in SLE, and our findings suggest that it may contribute towards the increased disease severity, in particular with regard to LN, seen in this population group.

Two other novel clinical associations of type I IFN pathway-associated IgA autoantibody specificities were observed. We found a negative association between anti-IFI35 IgA and organ damage. This is in keeping with our recent study showing a negative association between anti-IFI35 IgG and organ damage (Eades et al, manuscript under review). Finally, we found a negative association between anti-IFIT1 IgA and steroid-free remission. IFIT1 protein plays a regulatory role in the type I IFN pathway, and its expression has been shown to be increased in SLE skin lesions.32 Moreover, IFIT1 gene expression has been shown to be increased in patients with active compared with inactive SLE.33

We also found a novel association between type II IFN pathway-associated IgA autoantibodies and SLE, namely anti-IFNγ, which was negatively associated with immunosuppressive medication use. The role of the type II IFN pathway in SLE is less well-characterised than that of the type I IFN pathway, although IFNγ has previously been shown to be elevated in patients with SLE even years prior to diagnosis and is associated with overall and renal disease activity.34 35 Our finding of a negative association between anti-IFNγ and immunosuppression use is mechanistically plausible if neutralising, given the pro-inflammatory role of IFNγ in SLE35 but is not in keeping with a previous study showing a positive association between anti-IFNγ IgG and SLE disease activity.36

This study had several limitations. Our patient cohort size was modest, potentially rendering our study underpowered to reveal additional statistically significant associations between IgA autoantibodies and clinical features. This is particularly pertinent with regards to the clustering analysis, in which patient numbers in each cluster were small, and in relation to organ-specific disease activity. All patients in this study were recruited from a tertiary public hospital rheumatology clinic, potentially leading to a selection bias for patients with more severe disease. Under the assumption that serum abundance for most autoantibodies is relatively static, as supported by recent literature,37 we assessed autoantibody profiles at a single timepoint only. However, in future research, it would be prudent to reassess the autoantibodies associated with clinical features in our study at multiple timepoints, in order to confirm whether or not their levels fluctuate over time. Our results currently lack external validation because no independent cohort with comparable IgA autoantibody profiling and harmonised clinical phenotyping was available to reproduce our findings. While SLEDAI-2K provides a standardised and validated measure across patients, reliance on SLEDAI-2K-defined manifestations may limit phenotypic granularity and result in under-representation of features not captured by this instrument. Finally, the purposive, non-random sampling strategy used to achieve a balanced cohort may have introduced selection bias and limits generalisability to the broader SLE population. Restriction of the cohort to patients of Asian and European ethnicities also limits the generalisability of these findings to more diverse SLE populations. Limited ability to adjust for key demographic variables (age, sex and ethnicity) in some multivariable analyses due to small subgroup sizes may result in residual confounding and affect the robustness of certain associations, although this was partially mitigated by the use of purposive sampling to balance the cohort at the study design level. Therefore, the present associations should be interpreted as exploratory and require confirmation in larger, external multicentre datasets.

In conclusion, this is the first study to group patients with SLE into clusters based on IgA autoantibody profiles and to examine associations between validated longitudinal clinical outcomes and IgA autoantibody profiles in SLE. The discovery of a patient cluster, driven by anti-PRKAR1A autoantibodies that is associated with Asian ethnicity, active renal disease and immunosuppression use is in keeping with previous findings of PKA protein deficiency in SLE. Our novel finding of an association between anti-STAM2 IgA and haematological disease is of interest given the known role of STAM2 protein in JAK/STAT signalling. Taken together, these findings support the presence of distinct IgA autoantibody-defined patient subgroups in SLE, with IgA autoantibody profiles capturing clinically relevant heterogeneity, with potential relevance for disease stratification. The observed clinical associations suggest that IgA autoantibodies represent a complementary and previously underexplored dimension of SLE immunopathology. Comprehensive isotype-specific autoantibody profiling may therefore offer additional value in identifying patient subgroups and informing biomarker development. Further studies are needed to validate these associations in larger cohorts and to assess the functional role of these autoantibodies in SLE.
