Palliative care in systemic sclerosis: incorporating advance care planning into longitudinal care
Authors: Marissa B Savoie, Jessica L Logeman, Flavia V Castelino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Despite advances in rheumatological treatments, patients with systemic sclerosis (SSc) suffer with myriad symptoms and experience associated quality of life decrements. Although SSc-related mortality is reported around 53%, there is a paucity of existing literature describing advance care planning and palliative care in SSc. Here we review symptom management burden and strategies by organ manifestation: interstitial lung disease, pulmonary hypertension, gastrointestinal involvement, cutaneous and musculoskeletal manifestations and digital ischaemia. Then we review domains of palliative care in the context of SSc including psychosocial support, sharing prognostic information, discussing goals of care, advance care planning and comfort-focused care. Finally we suggest unmet research needs in palliative care and SSc.Keywords: Palliative Care, Quality of Life

Introduction

Patients with systemic sclerosis (SSc) experience symptoms related to skin, vascular, gastrointestinal, pulmonary and cardiac manifestations.1 2 In a large cohort of patients with SSc (23% with diffuse cutaneous SSc (dcSSc)), prominent symptoms included severe fatigue (54%), breathlessness (24%) and severe constipation (21%), leading authors to estimate that 72% of patients in their cohort met a threshold for specialist palliative care referral.3 Prognosis is variable and patients with severe disease may benefit from proactive goals of care conversations and symptom management. Many rheumatologists have limited experience discussing life-limiting diagnoses and advance care planning.4 5 We propose an advance care planning strategy that is tailored to a patient’s extent of disease and clinical manifestations. Determining if a patient is both eligible for and potentially interested in novel therapies is essential, given that hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT),6 chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy7 and lung transplantation8 are increasingly being evaluated in severe SSc. Incorporating palliative care principles allows rheumatologists to understand a patient’s values and assess whether such treatments are concordant with the patient’s goals.

Palliative care is an interdisciplinary approach to medical care focused on attending to emotional, spiritual and physical suffering related to serious illness; a tenet of palliative care practice is centring medical decision-making around the patient’s values and preferences. While the term palliative care often connotes involvement of medical doctors who are board-certified in hospice and palliative medicine, palliative care includes a broad interdisciplinary team of social workers, chaplains, nurses and providers. Primary palliative care refers to palliative care knowledge and skills required of all healthcare providers; in SSc this would involve palliative care provided by the rheumatologist and/or primary care clinician. Secondary palliative care refers to care provided by specialised palliative care providers on a consultative basis. A multidisciplinary team of experts including pulmonology, rheumatology and palliative care recently proposed an early integrated palliative care model in SSc,9 in which palliative care physicians would be nested within a specialised high volume tertiary centre clinic and would provide palliative review early in disease course. Authors proposed triggers for prompt referral to integrated palliative care including: high symptom burden, frequent hospital admission, disability, patient request for support with advance care planning, consideration of lung transplantation and consideration of HSCT.9

While secondary palliative care carries the advantage of expertise and interdisciplinary team structures (eg, palliative care social workers, nurses and chaplains), we highlight the importance of primary palliative care in caring for patients with SSc. First, SSc has the highest morbidity and mortality of all rheumatic diseases, and prognosis is highly variable. While palliative care doctors are very familiar with discussing prognosis in cancer, they may have less experience with prognostication and advance care planning in SSc, particularly if patients are suffering from organ involvement outside of interstitial lung disease (ILD). In these cases, rheumatologists provide invaluable prognostic insight and present best and worst-case scenarios regarding possible response to immunomodulatory therapy. Second, there are significant workforce constraints in US-based palliative care specialised physicians; a workforce gap between 6000 and 18 000 palliative care physicians has been reported, which is now likely to be an underestimate given worsening of trends over time.10 11 In many institutions palliative care outpatient clinics are associated with a cancer centre and access to palliative care for non-oncological indications can be challenging. Experts have proposed a foundation of primary palliative care provided by primary care and disease-specific experts (eg, oncology, cardiology, rheumatology) with consultation from palliative care specialists for challenging symptom management and complex dynamics or conflict around advance care planning.12 Because of the rare frequency of SSc, limited exposure of non-rheumatology clinicians to SSc and significant workforce constraints for specialty palliative care providers, the rheumatologist plays a critical role in providing primary palliative care in SSc.

McDonald et al recently piloted an early, integrated palliative care clinic colocated in a SSc clinic at an academic referral centre.13 Triggers for referral from a rheumatologist included high symptom burden, frequent admissions, decreased functional status, consideration of lung transplantation or HSCT, request for advance care planning or perceived poor prognosis. Symptom management was the most common trigger for referral. Palliative care evaluations focused on symptom management and advance care planning were carried out by a physician with training in palliative care and pulmonary medicine with experience in SSc. They found 96% of patients accepted the referral to palliative care clinic. Over 52 clinic visits with 24 patients the following topics were addressed frequently: non-pharmacological physical symptom management (discussed with 96% of patients), pharmacological management (medications changed for 88%), serious illness conversations (88% of patients participated) and advance care planning (83% of patients participated). Authors concluded that high uptake of referrals and participation in symptom management and advance care planning indicates an unmet need for palliative care in SSc.

Here, we review existing evidence around advance care planning and palliative interventions in SSc. We review key symptom management issues in the domains of the cardiopulmonary, gastrointestinal, cutaneous, musculoskeletal and vascular systems. To support clinicians in identifying patients who may benefit most from palliative care, we review prognosis and predictors for severe disease. We describe our approach to advance care planning and supportive care in SSc, including incorporation of primary and secondary palliative care. We describe advance care planning and psychosocial support interventions that can be incorporated into clinical care.

Supportive care issues by organ manifestation

Interstitial lung disease and pulmonary hypertension

In patients with ILD (with or without secondary pulmonary hypertension (PH)) or pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), we counsel that dyspnoea may develop or progress over time. There have been significant improvements in strategies to manage the underlying pathophysiology of ILD and PAH (which will not be covered here);14 15 however, despite treatments, dyspnoea remains a significant problem for many patients with SSc ILD and/or PAH. Many patients with ILD and PAH develop dyspnoea with minor activities or even at rest. In a study of 132 Australian patients with SSc-PAH, it was reported that health-related quality of life scores were lower compared with patients with SSc without PAH,16 particularly in the domains of physical functioning, general health and vitality.16 Similarly, in a study of 48 Italian patients with SSc-ILD, there were low health-related quality of life scores, with Baseline Dyspnoea Index correlating with quality of life scales, suggesting that palliating dyspnoea should be a key focus of care.17 Patients with SSc-PAH and SSc-ILD experience significantly lower health-related quality of life.

Experts in SSc-ILD and systemic autoimmune rheumatic disease ILD have described a role for longitudinal palliative care as part of holistic care for patients with SSc-ILD.18,20 The European Respiratory Society developed a clinical practice guideline on the role of palliative care for people with ILD or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and suggest adding a palliative care approach when patients or caregivers have unmet physical, psychological, social or spiritual/existential needs.21 They emphasise the complementary functions of active disease management (eg, immunosuppression, pulmonary vasodilators) and palliative care and cite a recent meta-analysis demonstrating lower emergency department utilisation and symptom burden among adults who received palliative care for a primary non-cancer illness.22 They acknowledge low quality of evidence underpinning recommendations and suggest multiple lines of inquiry regarding when to start palliative care, evaluating palliative care and advance care planning interventions, and implementation studies.

In a study of ILD-focused provider comfort with palliative care, investigators sent an electronic survey to providers at 68 Pulmonary Fibrosis Foundation Care Centers.23 91% of providers reported feeling comfortable discussing prognosis and 67% reported using palliative care services for patients with ILD. In contrast, in a study surveying 200 rheumatologists about palliative care, 72% of rheumatologists reported never or rarely discussing advance care planning and 68% had not referred a patient to palliative care in the last year.5 However, 67% of rheumatologists felt that more of their patients could benefit from palliative care. Among rheumatologists and palliative care providers, 2% reported receiving education specifically about palliative care in rheumatology. Rheumatologists raised barriers including uncertainty about when to refer patients, concern that patients will think the clinician is ‘giving up’ on them and lack of access to palliative care for non-oncology indications. Despite significant knowledge and experience gaps in palliative care, in this study rheumatologists indicated that palliative care would be beneficial for their patients.

Non-pharmacological approaches can be helpful in managing dyspnoea in ILD or PAH (table 1). Use of a fan blowing air onto a patient’s face has been demonstrated to improve dyspnoea in randomised controlled clinical trials and should be recommended to patients with persistent dyspnoea given little risk involved.24 25 Pulmonary rehabilitation is recommended by the American Thoracic Society for adults with ILD (including those with progressive fibrotic ILD) based on low to moderate-quality evidence, suggesting improvements in exercise capacity, health-related quality of life and dyspnoea.26 Another non-pharmacological intervention with emerging evidence is a dyspnoea support service that includes a fan, dyspnoea crisis plan with physical therapy and/or occupational therapy, guidance on pacing and exercises, and a palliative care provider visit.27 In a randomised trial administering this dyspnoea support service to patients with refractory breathlessness (18% with ILD), improved breathlessness mastery was observed in the intervention group compared with usual care at 6 weeks of trial therapy.27 While most evidence supporting dyspnoea treatments is derived from COPD and ILD cohorts, patients with SSc and refractory dyspnoea likely similarly benefit from non-pharmacological interventions including pulmonary rehabilitation, a fan and a dyspnoea crisis plan.

Pharmacological management of dyspnoea and cough in SSc (table 1) is largely extrapolated from ILD or COPD literature. Home supplemental oxygen should be initiated to treat exertional desaturations and allow for physical activity. Guidelines on dyspnoea related to advanced lung disease from the American College of Chest Physicians reiterate the importance of addressing dyspnoea and comorbid anxiety.28 While opioids are often used to palliate dyspnoea, effectiveness may vary widely by clinical scenario; in a small randomised clinical trial of standing morphine four times daily, dyspnoea did not change significantly in the morphine group versus placebo.29 30 Opioid medications or gabapentin may be helpful in suppressing the cough reflex.31 Benzodiazepines may be used as a palliative treatment for anxiety or as an adjunctive agent for dyspnoea. There are safety concerns regarding respiratory depression with opioids and benzodiazepines in patients with chronic respiratory failure; in a cohort study of 13 patients with ILD starting long-term oxygen (7% with an underlying rheumatologic diagnosis), there was an association between high-dose benzodiazepines (daily dose ≥15 mg oxazepam equivalent) and increased mortality compared with low-dose benzodiazepines although these data may reflect confounding by indication.32 While opioids and benzodiazepines may be used for palliation of dyspnoea and cough, there is limited evidence of efficacy in the ILD population.

Gastrointestinal involvement

Fibrosis, smooth muscle atrophy and vasculopathy in SSc leads to symptomatic gastrointestinal pathology including oropharyngeal dysphagia, oesophageal dysmotility, gastro-oesophageal reflux (GERD), gastroparesis, gastric antral vascular ectasia, small intestinal hypomobility with intestinal pseudo-obstruction and colonic dysmotility. Gastrointestinal symptoms (most commonly GERD) affect about 94% patients with SSc, and many patients have symptoms refractory to medical treatment.33 Medical treatment for GERD typically includes trials of proton pump inhibitors and histamine 2 receptor antagonists. Promotility agents such as metoclopramide have been used to treat oesophageal dysmotility, gastroparesis and small bowel dysmotility (table 1).34 Gastrointestinal involvement is the reported cause of death in 3–4% of patients with SSc.1 35 36 In a prospective study in Japan, 74 patients diagnosed with chronic intestinal pseudo-obstruction (24% with SSc, mean age 49 years) were followed and 31% received parenteral nutrition.37 Over an average of 4 years of observation, there were nine deaths (12% of cohort), 56% attributed to infection. Symptoms of depression were common; 38% had a positive depression screen using the Patient Health Questionnaire 2. 69% of patients indicated interest in palliative care interventions and cited pain, anxiety and social isolation as important concerns. The authors concluded that patients with chronic intestinal pseudo-obstruction suffer despite medical treatment and would benefit from palliative care assessment.

Malnutrition is another important complication in SSc with a prevalence of 5–15%.38,41 Malnutrition in SSc is often multifactorial and related to depression, deconditioning, increased metabolic demands from pulmonary manifestations, in addition to gastrointestinal dysmotility, prandial symptoms and malabsorption.42 Interventions include dietary counselling targeted at gastroparesis, oral supplemental nutrition and prokinetic medications. If initial interventions are insufficient and malnutrition is primarily related to oesophageal rather than intestinal pathology, providers may recommend enteral feeding via percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy with jejunal extension to ensure postpyloric feeding and decrease reflux.

Parenteral nutrition may be recommended when oral and/or enteral nutrition has failed; however, parenteral nutrition carries significant risks and implications for quality of life, and we advocate for discussing goals of care prior to initiation. Risks of parenteral nutrition include serious infection, volume overload and catheter-related thrombosis. Parenteral nutrition can be offered at home, with close supervision from caregivers and visiting nurses. In small case series the risks of parenteral nutrition complications appear similar in SSc compared with non-SSc indications; two of the eight patients in the study continued parenteral nutrition for more than 100 months.43 In another case series of 12 patients with SSc receiving parenteral nutrition, the 2-year survival rate was 58% and all patients died from SSc-related complications not related to parenteral nutrition.44 The high death rate (42%) at 2 years suggests that the need for parenteral nutrition in SSc is a marker of severe global decline and prognosis may be limited by non-gastrointestinal organ manifestations.

When counselling patients about parenteral nutrition, it is important to consider whether malnutrition is the most proximal probable cause of mortality. For example, if a patient meets clinical criteria for malnutrition but is more symptomatic from severe ILD and PAH, a clinician may assess that the risk of infectious complications from parenteral nutrition outweighs the low probability that parenteral nutrition may prolong survival. As with oncological patients, many patients with SSc have multiorgan dysfunction that will be life limiting irrespective of nutritional status; a palliative approach would incorporate this prognostic assessment when counselling about parenteral nutrition.

Disabling musculoskeletal manifestations, cutaneous manifestations and digital ischaemia

Musculoskeletal manifestations of SSc include arthralgias, arthritis, tendon friction rubs, calcinosis cutis and joint contractures. The hands are most frequently affected by calcinosis cutis, sclerodactyly and joint contractures, particularly decreased flexion of the metacarpophalangeal joint and impaired extension of the proximal and distal interphalangeal joints.45 For calcinosis cutis, possible interventions include protective silicone sleeves and surgical resection (table 1). In an observational study of patients with SSc in which musculoskeletal manifestations and disease outcomes were followed for 3 years, contractures (defined as range of motion <75%) were present in 73–82% of participants and 9–11% had knee contractures.45 Pain associated with contractures can be treated with acetaminophen, topical non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medications, or pregabalin or gabapentin if there is a neuropathic component to pain (table 1). Knee contractures in some cases may impair ambulation and lead to need for assistive devices. Hand and wrist contractures may impair a patient’s ability to complete domestic and occupational tasks. Occupational therapy for upper extremity function in patients with SSc may involve exercises, splinting and thermal modalities and is associated with improved patient-reported physical function outcome measures.46

In addition to discomfort and disability secondary to skin tightening, many patients experience pruritus that is associated with decreased mental and physical functioning in SSc.47 48 Pruritus has been reported to significantly affect 42–43% of patients with SSc.47 48 We recommend starting with topical treatments such as emollient creams, topical capsaicin and topical menthol (table 1). If pruritus is causing significant symptoms and first-line therapies are not effective, other options include short term topical corticosteroids, topical calcineurin inhibitors or oral antihistamines, or gabapentin. Pruritus is associated with the degree of skin involvement and may improve as skin thickening improves later in the disease course.

Digital ischaemic ulcers develop in 36–44% of patients with SSc during their disease course,49 50 and digital ischaemia may lead to severe complications of gangrene or autoamputation. Evidence from the Digital Ulcer Outcome Registry, a European prospective cohort of patients with SSc-related digital ulcers, suggests that patients with digital ulcers experience work impairment, with mean percentage work missing/impaired 42–48%.51 Patients with digital ulcers reported 50% impairment in ability to perform regular daily activities. Many patients required assistance at home to compensate for disability from digital ulcers. Digital ischaemic ulcers are associated with depression, pain and lower physical and social functioning.52 53 Behavioural modifications to decrease Raynaud’s severity include mittens, hand coverings, dressing warmly, portable hand warmers and avoiding triggers (eg, stress, caffeine, stimulants, tobacco) (table 1). If critical ischaemia develops from arterial vasospasm, regional nerve block or botox injection can be performed to decrease sympathetic intervention to the affected digit and surgical sympathectomy can be considered.54 During the acute period of digital ischaemia where autoamputation can occur, some patients may require a continuous intravenous vasodilator (epoprostenol) with close haemodynamic monitoring inpatient.55 Despite use of medications such as calcium channel blockers, prostacyclin analogues, endothelin antagonists and phosphodiesterase 5 inhibitors to treat digital ischaemia, some patients still develop ulcers, pitting scars and autoamputation and associated disability.

Domains of palliative care

Psychosocial support

At time of diagnosis, patients may be more apprehensive about the diagnosis of SSc itself than burdened by physical symptoms. In one study using the SSc Needs Questionnaire, the highest domain of unmet need was the psychological/spiritual/existential domain rather than the physical or health services domains.56 Specifically, participants reported needs related to anxiety and stress, fear that the disease will worsen and uncertainty about the future; this was reported more frequently in unmarried participants.

Depression is a common comorbidity in SSc, and patients with SSc are at a significantly higher risk of depression compared with patients with other rheumatic disease.57 The rate of depression in SSc has been reported at 1658–22%,57 and some advocate for screening for depression in SSc.59 Several groups have identified associations between dyspnoea, gastrointestinal symptoms and skin involvement with depression.60 61 Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors are generally recommended as first-line treatment for depression in clinical guidelines (table 1).62 For patients with comorbid pain, serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors may be chosen as first-line treatment for depression. Non-pharmacological strategies for depression or anxiety include cognitive behavioural therapy and increased physical activity.63 64 Depression should be addressed in SSc, as it can often be effectively treated with a combination of counselling and medications.

Additional research supports a connection between spiritual well-being and improved SSc-specific quality of life, although causation cannot be presumed as spiritual satisfaction and life satisfaction/quality of life are similar concepts.65 We recommend maximising psychosocial support including peer support groups and patient education forums and include resources in box 1. Collaboration with social workers in the outpatient rheumatology or primary care setting is another intervention to address disease-related anxiety and coping with illness.

Psychoeducational interventions are a promising tool to improve psychosocial support and disease understanding; the benefits of psychoeducational interventions have been demonstrated in rheumatoid arthritis.66 Kwakkenbos et al conducted a psychoeducational intervention in 41 patients with SSc, consisting of 13 90-minute sessions over 3 weekends.67 Led by a multidisciplinary team, the sessions addressed medical topics, exercise techniques and stress management. The study found significant improvements in helplessness and acceptance of limitations, which lasted for 6 months postintervention.

Sharing prognostic information

Some patients may seek specific prognostic information. We assess prognosis based on disease subtype (limited cutaneous SSc (lcSSc) vs dcSSc) and presence of pulmonary manifestations (ILD or PAH). Diffuse cutaneous involvement is a well-established risk factor for mortality; studies have shown that 10-year survival rates are significantly lower in dcSSc (53–62%) compared with 75–79% in lcSSc.68 69 Patients with lcSSc and lack of end-organ involvement several years after initial diagnosis constitute a low-risk phenotype; Ioannidis et al found that patients with lcSSc, lack of renal, cardiac or pulmonary involvement 3 years into disease course, and negative antitopoisomerase I antibodies did not have increased mortality risk.70 In large cohorts including patients with lcSSc and dcSSc, 58–64% of patients died from SSc-related causes.1 36 71 A mortality risk stratification model in SSc published by Elhai et al identified multiple demographic and clinical predictors of low survival; the most significant three were age over 65, elevated C reactive protein and diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (DLCO) less than 60% predicted.71 Among patients with in the upper quartile of mortality risk scores, median survival at 3 years was 53%. Applying this prognostic model may aid in discussions on advance care planning and palliative care.

Most mortality from SSc is secondary to respiratory failure from ILD. In a recent clinical trial for SSc-ILD, 87% of SSc-related deaths were attributable to respiratory failure.72 If ILD is diagnosed, patients are at higher risk for SSc-related mortality. At time of ILD diagnosis, extent of disease by high-resolution CT and severity of restriction or impaired gas exchange by pulmonary function test (PFT) are associated with increased mortality.73 74 Other disease-related factors associated with mortality in SSc-ILD include the presence of antitopoisomerase I antibodies, diffuse subtype, comorbid PH and elevated CRP.75 Male sex and older age are also associated with mortality in patients with SSc-ILD. Rheumatologists and pulmonologists may prescribe immunomodulatory or antifibrotic therapies to slow the progression of ILD, the details of which will not be addressed here.76

PH in SSc may be secondary to PAH (group 1), ILD (group 3) or left heart disease (group 2).77 While there have been advances in the treatment of PAH and ILD, PH remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality in SSc. In a meta-analysis of 2244 patients with SSc-PH, 3-year survival was 52% (95% CI 47% to 58%).78 Survival was worse among patients with ILD-associated PH (35% survival at 3 years) compared with patients with PAH (56% survival at 3 years). Poor prognostic factors include reduced DLCO and pericardial effusion in patients with ILD-associated PH. In patients with SSc-PAH, poor prognostic factors included lower cardiac index, increased pulmonary vascular resistance, right atrial pressure, mean pulmonary arterial pressure, lower mixed venous oxygen saturation, age at PAH diagnosis, male sex, lower 6-minute walk distance, lower DLCO and pericardial effusion.78 79

If ILD or PAH progresses despite standard therapies, we recommend having a serious illness conversation to signpost disease progression and to solicit the patient’s goals and values, which helps determine preferences and candidacy for interventions such as clinical trial participation, HSCT or lung transplant evaluation.80 Clinical trials for ILD often exclude patients with very advanced ILD, thus referral should be considered at time of diagnosis as well as at time of disease progression.

Discussing goals of care and advance care planning

End-stage progressive SSc is challenging to define. While there is no consensus on end-stage progressive SSc, we use this term to describe patients who have severe restrictive lung disease based on PFTs, severe gastrointestinal manifestations leading to severe malnutrition and/or parenteral nutrition use, or when manifestations of SSc limit patients from completing activities of daily living independently. When SSc is thought to be the most likely life-limiting condition, we strongly recommend discussion of the patient’s goals, values and preferences.

Advance care planning is a core function of palliative care, defined in 2017 by experts as a process that enables individuals to define goals and preferences for future medical treatment and care, to discuss these goals and preferences with family and healthcare providers, and to document as needed.81 During this process, it is important to explore an individual’s health-related experiences, illness understanding, future care goals and the option of identifying a personal representative for medical and legal decisions on the individual’s behalf if they are not able to participate. Advance care planning often involves reflection, conversation and completion of an advance healthcare directive, a legal document detailing preferences for medical interventions and decision-making authority. There are online resources which provide evidence-based, patient-centred information on creating an advance care directive and related topics (box 1).82

Palliative care interventions, including advanced care planning, have demonstrated improvement in patient-centred outcomes in small studies of patients with ILD. A randomised trial of a phone-based palliative care nurse intervention for patients with end-stage ILD found improvements in palliative outcome and mental health scores, and a higher proportion of patients passed at a preferred place of death (usually home).83 Another trial involving a nurse-led early palliative care intervention for patient–caregiver dyads with ILD demonstrated increased completion of advance care planning and improved caregiver knowledge and confidence.84 Advance care planning is beneficial at any stage of a patient’s chronic illness, however it becomes increasingly crucial as SSc progresses.

Serious illness communication has been defined as a longitudinal process focused on patient goals of care, communication preferences, illness understanding and prognostic understanding.85 Conversation guides have been designed to help providers deliver challenging news clearly and sensitively. A well-established framework to embark on this conversation is the Serious Illness Conversation Guide.86 This guide includes suggested patient-tested language to set up the conversation, assess for illness understanding, share challenging information compassionately, explore the patient’s goals and values, and summarise those preferences. Open-ended questions in the serious illness conversation may allow patients to ask questions about prognosis and end of life care.

Ideally, patient values and tolerance for invasive care would be discussed prior to referral for lung transplant or HSCT. Although risks of HSCT are discussed extensively with patients prior to grafting, Spierings et al conducted qualitative semistructured interviews around the decision-making process in HSCT and found that many patients underappreciated risk associated with HSCT for SSc.58 Of 21 participants undergoing HSCT, 12 patients (all males) believed that they had lower treatment risk compared with other patients and two reported they were told ‘HSCT is always successful, and without risks or complications’. In interviews more than half of patients voiced a preference to be guided by the rheumatologist in their decision around HSCT. Many patients met with a peer who had undergone HSCT which they reported as helpful. These data underscore the value of longitudinal discussions around HSCT and lung transplantation with a trusted rheumatologist and pulmonologist, even if detailed information needs to be deferred to transplant specialists. Advance care planning conversations conducted by rheumatologists, as a form of primary palliative care, may identify diverse patient preferences that impact treatment course.

While there may be considerable uncertainty around prognosis in SSc, a serious illness conversation may reveal patient preferences that may inform discussions around code status and/or end-of-life care planning. For patients with pulmonary involvement, discussions around code status may benefit from input from the patient’s pulmonologist. Patients with severe PH have particularly poor outcomes with intubation due to the risk of positive pressure ventilation precipitating acute right ventricular failure.87 Similarly, in severe ILD a patient’s pulmonologist may be able to comment on risk of prolonged ventilator wean and outcome of tracheostomy or death. Well-documented code status conversations and the presence of an advance care directive at a patient’s residence can rapidly communicate a patient’s wishes to emergency providers, preventing potentially unwanted interventions such as cardiopulmonary resuscitation, intubation or vasopressors. While serious illness conversations about clinical worsening may reveal a patient’s preferences to avoid invasive care, many patients may still prefer to continue all possible aggressive treatments.

Comfort-focused care in systemic sclerosis

In some scenarios, patients with end-stage SSc at end of life may choose comfort-focused medical care. Several case reports describe home-based palliative care for patients with end-stage SSc and use of hand-held fans and oral morphine.88 89 Similar to other severe medical illnesses, in SSc referral to hospice may be appropriate if prognosis is expected to be less than 6 months. On referral to hospice, care would usually be transferred to a hospice physician and nursing team who can provide medications such as opioids and benzodiazepines to treat pain, dyspnoea and anxiety. For patients suffering with pain and dyspnoea, patients and caregivers will discuss trade-offs between wakefulness and symptom control. Rheumatologists may be less familiar referring to hospice, and it is important that they are aware of what caregiver services are offered through local hospice agencies. For example, many home hospice agencies require 24/7 caregiver support (often provided by patient’s family members) for all activities of daily living including toileting, transfers and bathing. Patients with severe SSc may have joint contractures restricting mobility that could require support beyond what caregivers at home can provide. In these scenarios, a rheumatologist may recommend referral for a hospice house or inpatient admission for acute symptom management and case management support in determining a discharge setting with adequate support for comfort-focused care.

Future research

Considering the substantial morbidity and mortality of SSc, there are numerous opportunities to use both primary and secondary palliative care to improve clinical care. While studies of palliative care interventions for dyspnoea in end-stage ILD may be extrapolated usefully to patients with SSc-ILD, the clinical course of severe SSc often also involves painful and debilitating vascular, cutaneous, gastrointestinal and musculoskeletal manifestations which require individualised symptom management. We propose the following research priorities: (1) Seek patient perspectives on utilisation of individualised SSc prognostic models in advance care planning, (2) assess impact and feasibility of focused palliative care intervention such as symptom management consultation with a palliative care specialist or palliative care consultation for advance care planning/serious illness conversation at critical timepoints in illness journey (ie, ILD progression, prior to starting advanced therapies such as TPN, HSCT, CAR T or lung transplantation), (3) evaluate where patients with end-stage SSc receive care at end-of-life (ie, inpatient hospital, inpatient hospice or home hospice) and (4) evaluate risks and possible benefits of discontinuing immunomodulatory treatment in the case of severe progressive disease.

Conclusion

Many patients with SSc suffer with pain, dyspnoea, psychological distress and a shortened lifespan comparable to that of patients with metastatic cancer. Prior studies indicate a high prevalence of fatigue, dyspnoea and gastrointestinal concerns among patients with SSc. Understanding a patient’s values and goals is crucial to providing goal-concordant recommendations around intensive and/or experimental treatments such as HSCT, CAR T-cell therapy and lung transplantation. Surveys of patients with SSc demonstrate interest in palliative care consultation and case reports indicate effective collaboration between rheumatology and palliative care providers for those with end-stage SSc. Patients with SSc would greatly benefit from key palliative care services, including proactive goals of care conversations and comprehensive symptom management.
