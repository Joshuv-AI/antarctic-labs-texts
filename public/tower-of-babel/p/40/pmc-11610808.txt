Patterns of identity: Lip prints, tongue prints and mandibular canine width as markers for gender determination—A cross-sectional study
Authors: Priyanka Rana, Dirasantchu Suresh, M. Chandra Sekhar, Y Mercy Sravanthi, Karan Hiral Mehta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Forensic odontology plays a pivotal role in identifying individuals in legal contexts. This study analyses the efficacy of lip prints, tongue prints and mandibular canine width in determining gender, aiming to contribute to the evolving field of forensic dentistry.Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted involving 100 participants, analysing lip prints based on Tsuchihashi’s classification, recording tongue shapes and features through the use of alginate impressions and measuring mandibular canine width using a vernier calliper. The findings were reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology checklist.Results:In lip prints, Tsuchihashi’s Type I lip print was predominantly found in females, whereas Tsuchihashi’s Type III prevailed in males. Tongue shapes revealed a predominance of pentagonal shapes in males and ovoid shapes in females. The lingual apex was blunt in males and sharp in females. Mandibular canine width was higher in males than females on both the left and right sides.Conclusion:The findings of this study support the use of lip prints, tongue prints and canine width in determining gender as ancillary and supporting methods.

Introduction

Dental tissues are often utilized in determining the age, gender and ethnic background of the person who may either be a victim or a suspect. The Fédération Dentaire Internationale (FDI) has defined forensic odontology as ‘that branch of dentistry which, in the interest of justice, deals with the proper handling and examination of dental evidence, and with the proper evaluation and presentation of dental findings’.[1] There have been different approaches implemented to ascertain the age, gender and ethnicity of an individual through dental tissues.[2] Teeth serve as an indispensable tool in identification by comparing the antemortem and postmortem records. However, even in cases where antemortem records are accessible, challenges may arise due to tooth loss or damage to restorations. To address these issues, there is a need for an immutable parameter that can be utilized.[3]

Similar to the prints found on our fingers, palms and soles of the feet, lip prints are also individualistic and stay consistent over the course of an individual’s lifetime. Lips have distinct furrows and ridges. Tsuchihashi coined the term ‘sulci labiorum rubrorum’ to describe the wrinkles and grooves observed on the lips. These unique characteristics make lip prints valuable in forensic investigations. The resulting impression created by these grooves is commonly referred to as a lip print, and its analysis is known as cheiloscopy.[1] Cheiloscopy, a forensic investigative method used to identify individuals based on their lip patterns, involves the examination of unique grooves present on the lips. These lip traces are not only hereditary but also believed to be distinct for each individual.

Lip prints, similar to fingerprints, can serve as physical evidence found at a crime scene. Lip prints can be collected directly from the lips or indirectly from items like garments, vessels, cigarettes, doors and windows. However, it is essential that the prints be obtained within a day of death as beyond 24 h, the postmortem changes in lips may render the data inconclusive. Lip print pattern is different based on whether the prints are acquired while the mouth is in open or closed position. When the mouth is closed, the lips display well-defined grooves, whereas when the mouth is open, these grooves become less distinct and difficult to analyse.[4] Pathological conditions of the lips including mucocele, postsurgical changes, lack of support due to missing anterior teeth, presence of debris or chaffing of the lip surface, heavy application of lipstick or excessive stretching of cellophane tape can affect the recording of lip prints.[3]

The tongue can be easily protruded from the mouth facilitating its examination. Also, each individual’s tongue is unique in its shape and surface characteristics. The utilization of tongue prints for forensic identification is still in its early stages. For this technique to be effective, it is essential to have the antemortem photographs or impressions capturing the morphology of the tongue. Alginate impressions serve as an effective means to preserve lingual morphological characteristics by replicating even the most intricate details that are exclusive to each person’s tongue. Combining a lingual impression with a corresponding photographic image can establish reliable methods for identification purposes in forensic dentistry.[5]

Unlike lip prints and tongue prints, the mandibular canine width offers a unique dimension for gender determination. As teeth exhibit reliable differences between genders, the mandibular canine width, in particular, has shown promise as a distinctive feature for identification.[67]

This study aimed to contribute to the existing body of knowledge by assessing the effectiveness of lip prints, tongue prints and mandibular canine width in determining gender. We have explored the patterns of lip prints and tongue shapes alongside the measurable dimension of mandibular canine width for a comprehensive analysis. By combining traditional and emerging forensic methods, this study seeks to enhance the accuracy and reliability of identification processes in forensic dentistry.

Methods

Study design and setting: A cross-sectional study was conducted in the Department of Oral Medicine and Radiology, after obtaining ethical clearance from the Institutional Ethics Committee. The primary objective was to assess the efficacy of lip prints, tongue prints and mandibular canine width in determining gender. No pre-specified hypothesis was defined for this study. The findings were reported according to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

Study participants: According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 100 (50 males and 50 females) students were chosen. All the participants of the study were undergraduate students of the dental college based on the convenience sampling method.

Inclusion criteria: Healthy individuals and participants who were willing to participate in the study without any pathology of the tongue or lip, healthy periodontium and caries-free canines.

Exclusion criteria: Students with trauma, scars, gross or developmental deformity of lips or tongue, participants allergic to lipstick or alginate, participants with orthodontic devices or caries or periodontal disease, missing canine, HIV and contagious diseases, gag reflex, gigantism and dwarfism were excluded from the study.

Methodology

Lip prints: Each participant was instructed to clean his/her own lips by rinsing with water, followed by allowing the lip to air-dry. To achieve a consistent and uniform application of the dark-coloured lipstick, it was carefully glided on in one fluid stroke and evenly distributed using cotton swabs. The adhesive or sticky side of the tape was placed over the lips in a relaxed position and was pressed evenly. The tape was then removed with great care to avoid distorting the lip impression. Afterwards, the tape was pasted onto the sheet [Figure 1]. The lip prints of each individual were analysed, classified and labelled according to Tsuchihashi’s classification[6] [Table 1 and Figure 2].

Tongue prints

For the tongue prints, the tongue was cleaned with a sterile compress. An impression was taken using alginate of the dorsal surface of the tongue and its lateral edges to capture the characteristics. The resulting moulds were then filled with dental stones to have relevant positive models for identification [Figure 3]. The cast was then analysed. Standardized procedures were followed to ensure consistency in capturing intricate details of the tongue morphology.

Canine width

The width of the mandibular canine was measured directly in the oral cavity using a vernier calliper by measuring the distance between the mesial and distal contact points [Figure 4].

Measurement of mesiodistal width of mandibular canine using a vernier calliper

To avoid intra-observer bias, the findings were assessed by repeating 10% of the measurements 10 days apart.

Statistical analysis: The obtained data was then subjected to statistical analysis, maintaining a significance level of ≤ 0.05. Descriptive statistics were calculated for quantitative variables. To explore variations across lip print types and tongue shapes, mandibular canine width was categorized into groups. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to assess significant differences in means among these groups, and Bonferroni multiple comparisons tests were conducted for post-hoc pairwise comparisons.

Results

This study included 100 participants comprising both males and females with ages ranging from 18 to 24 years of age. Lip prints were assessed based on Tsuchihashi’s classification[6] categorizing them into five types (Type I to Type V). The distribution of lip print types served as one of the exposures in the study.

Males most commonly exhibited Type III lip pattern, followed by Type II whereas females showed Type I as the most common pattern, followed by Type III and Type IV [Graph 1]. Tongue shapes and features were recorded using alginate impressions to capture the unique morphology of each participant’s tongue. The distribution of shape of the tongue was predominantly pentagonal followed by rectangular and ovoid in males, whereas it was mostly ovoid, followed by pentagonal and rectangular in females.

Distribution of lip prints

The lingual apex was blunt in most of the male participants, and it was sharp in most of the female participants. Hence, from this study, the lingual apex for males is predominantly blunt and it is sharp for females. None of the participants had a geographic tongue or scrotal tongue.

The mandibular canine width was measured using a vernier calliper, providing a quantitative measure for gender determination. On both the right and left sides, higher mean mandibular canine width was recorded in males (7.46 and 7.47, respectively) compared with females (6.76 and 6.76, respectively).

On the right side, higher mean mandibular canine width was recorded in Type II lip print samples followed by Type III, Type IV and Type I lip print samples, respectively. The difference among them was found to be statistically significant (P < 0.001). Furthermore, it was observed that statistically significant difference existed between Type I and Type II (P < 0.001), Type I and Type III (P < 0.001), Type II and Type IV (P < 0.01) as well as between Type III and Type IV (P < 0.01).

On the left side, higher mean mandibular canine width was recorded in Type II lip print samples followed by Type III, Type IV and Type I lip print samples, respectively. The difference among them was found to be statistically significant (P < 0.001). Furthermore, it was observed that statistically significant difference existed between Type I and Type II (P < 0.001), Type I and Type III (P < 0.001), Type II and Type IV (P < 0.01) as well as between Type III and Type IV (P < 0.01) [Table 2].

Discussion

Forensic odontologists work with other experts to determine the gender of human remains by examining dental and cranial characteristics. Identification of individuals is important in cases involving unknown deceased persons, such as in homicides, suicides, accidents and mass disasters. It is also crucial for identifying living individuals who have gone missing due to amnesia or in cases where culprits conceal their identities. Dentists play an integral role in criminal investigations by gathering information through the analysis of bite marks, lip prints and teeth discovered at crime scenes.[3]

Although the primary focus of the study was on gender determination and in the context of forensic dentistry, the findings can also aid primary care physicians in diagnostic support. For instance, certain tongue shapes or variations in lip patterns may indicate underlying health issues or genetic tendencies. Certain lip print patterns have been linked to the development of non-syndromic cleft lip with/without cleft palate. The study can, therefore, be a valuable tool in primary disease care offering an affordable and non-invasive screening approach.[8]

This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of lip prints, tongue prints and canine width in gender determination. The classification system by Tsuchihashi[6] was used to record lip prints. In this study, Type I lip print patterns were commonly observed among females, whereas Type III patterns were more prevalent among males. Mani et al.,[9] Randhawa et al.[10] and Sandhu et al.[11] also reported similar findings of predominance of Type I lip print patterns for both genders. Likewise, Kaul et al.[12] also noted that Type I was the most frequently observed lip print pattern and Type IV was the least. On the contrary, Prasad et al.[13] reported Type III as the most common pattern in both genders. Krishnan et al.[14] reported a predominant presence of Type IV among males, unlike our findings. Thermadam et al.[15] reported males having a predominance of Type I and Type I lip groove patterns, whereas females showed Type IV and Type V lip groove patterns that differ significantly from the findings of this study. These differences can also be attributed to the different geographical locations and population base of these studies highlighting the need to broaden the anthropological database by conducting further studies in various geographical regions worldwide.[15]

A preliminary study conducted by Stafenescu[5] examined the lingual morphological aspects and their significance in identifying individuals. The study revealed that males had a blunt tip of the tongue, whereas females had a sharp one, consistent with the findings of this study. Similar results were also observed in Mani et al.’s[9] study, where a pentagonal shape was predominantly seen in males, while an ovoid shape was mostly found in females. Bhargava et al.[16] classified tongue shapes based on traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) and found that a square shape was more common in males and a circle in females. In addition, he also classified tongue shapes according to Stafenescu and reported that the U-shaped tongue predominated in both genders. However, obtaining accurate tongue impressions can be challenging as it requires maintaining protrusion for an extended period.

Like lip prints and tongue prints, teeth also exhibit unique patterns such as morphology, crown size, and root lengths that can be used to differentiate between males and females. Among all, the canine teeth show the most reliable differences between genders. In this study, on the both right and left sides, higher mean mandibular canine width was recorded in males compared to females. Similar findings were reported in studies by Rajarathnam et al.[7] and Kumawat et al.,[17] where mandibular canine width measurements were higher in males than in females.

Limitations: Although our study contributes valuable insights, we acknowledge certain limitations. The sample size, though representative, may warrant expansion for broader generalizability. Furthermore, this study was a cross-sectional study; longitudinal studies can provide information about the persistence of these features.

In addition, inherent variations in lip print patterns and difficulties in obtaining accurate tongue impressions may introduce some degree of subjectivity. Further research with more diverse populations and a larger sample size is warranted to address these limitations and strengthen the validity of our findings.

Conclusion

Owing to evolution, there has been a decrease in sexual dimorphism in tooth dimensions making it more challenging to distinguish between the modern male and female individuals based solely on dental characteristics. However, the need for gender determination and person identification has reached an all-time high. In light of this, this study delves into alternative methods that can aid in determining gender such as lip prints and tongue prints. Our findings support the use of lip prints, tongue prints, and canine width in determining gender as ancillary and supporting methods. However, it is important to take into account the limitations of each method such as variations in lip print patterns and difficulties obtaining accurate tongue impressions as well as age-related changes in canines.
