Association of Excessive Daytime Sleepiness With Long‐Term Incident Dementia: The Role of β‐Amyloid and White Matter Injury
Authors: Xiaowen Zhou, Guangshi Zhang, Qili Hu, Hanyu Shao, Tao Yang, Jie Wang, Xiaoniu Liang, Qianhua Zhao, Ding Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONExcessive daytime sleepiness (EDS) is common in community‐dwelling older adults, but its association with long‐term incident dementia remains unclear. The roles of Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathology and vascular‐related white matter injury in this association were merely studied.METHODSWe included 617 dementia‐free participants from the Shanghai Aging Study with up to 10 years of follow‐up. At baseline, EDS was assessed with the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) and plasma phosphorylated tau (p‐tau)217 was measured using a single‐molecule immune‐array assay. Cognitive decline was evaluated using the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), and incident dementia was determined by expert consensus during follow‐up. A magnetic resonance imaging marker of white matter injury (peak width of skeletonized mean diffusivity [PSMD]) was derived from diffusion tensor imaging, and gray matter volume was assessed from T1‐weighted imaging in a subcohort.RESULTSAt baseline, 66 participants (10.7%) had EDS and showed lower MMSE scores. EDS was associated with a higher risk of incident dementia (hazard ratio [HR] 2.06, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.09–3.92), especially among individuals with low p‐tau217 (HR 4.94, 95% CI 1.75–13.93) independent of sleep disturbance after adjusting for age, sex, and education. Higher ESS was related to AD pathology–dependent patterns of brain atrophy and to higher PSMD. PSMD modified the associations between EDS and cognitive decline, and incident dementia. Participants with both high PSMD and EDS showed the highest dementia risk.DISCUSSIONEDS may help identify older adults with low AD biomarker burden who are at increased risk of dementia, independent of nocturnal sleep disturbance. White matter injury may contribute to this risk, supporting a vascular vulnerability pathway.

INTRODUCTION

Excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS) is a common condition among older adults, with prevalence ranging from 10% to 25%. 1 , 2 It is characterized by an increased propensity to fall asleep during the day and is associated with multiple adverse health outcomes, including cardiovascular disease, stroke, and elevated mortality risk. 3 , 4 Recent epidemiological evidence has suggested that sleepiness‐related behavioral profiles are associated with later cognitive impairment and dementia, supporting the idea that “sleepiness” can be a prodromal marker rather than a benign complaint. 5 , 6

Proposed mechanisms of EDS include dysregulation of sleep–wake control, impaired glymphatic clearance of neurotoxic proteins, and alterations in amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau metabolism. 7 Mechanisms of EDS–dementia association still remain uncertain. Some studies interpret EDS as an early clinical symptom of Alzheimer's disease (AD)‐related neurodegeneration. 8 , 9 Moreover, emerging neuropathologic evidence suggests that wake‐promoting nuclei involved in sleep–wake regulation, such as the locus coeruleus and lateral hypothalamic area, may be affected by early tau pathology, providing a plausible substrate for EDS before overt dementia. 10 , 11 Other evidence suggests that EDS is not associated with Aβ 12 , 13 and may instead reflect vascular brain vulnerability, 14 , 15 such as white matter injury. 16 , 17 In particular, it remains unclear whether EDS is mainly an Aβ‐related symptom or a marker of white matter injury. It may also represent a heterogeneous condition: the impact of EDS on future dementia risk depends on the underlying pathology burden during the preclinical stage. Moreover, the brain regions associated with EDS, and whether these patterns differ across pathology profiles, remain poorly understood.

To address these gaps, we analyzed data from a 10‐year, prospective, community‐based cohort of older adults. We aimed to (1) examine the association between baseline EDS and long‐term cognitive decline as well as brain atrophy; (2) determine whether this association differs by the Aβ pathology burden, and the role of white matter injury in such association.

METHODS

Study participants

Study participants were recruited from the Shanghai Aging Study, a community‐based, longitudinal cohort study established in 2010 in Shanghai, China. 18 The detailed descriptions of the study design and recruitment procedures have been reported elsewhere. 18 , 19 In the current study, we included participants who (1) were aged ≥ 60 years, (2) were dementia free, (3) had completed an Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) questionnaire at baseline, (4) donated a blood sample at baseline, and (5) had participated in at least one follow‐up interview. Participants were excluded if they (1) had cerebral large vessel stenosis (> 50%), (2) had hydrocephalus or brain tumors, (3) had contraindications to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or (4) were unable to cooperate the examination at baseline and follow‐ups.

This study was approved by the medical ethics committee of Huashan Hospital, Fudan University. All participants and/or their legally authorized representatives provided their written informed consent.

EDS assessment

Demographic information, including age, sex, and years of formal education, was collected via an interviewer‐administered questionnaire.

Daytime sleepiness was assessed at baseline using the ESS, an 8‐item standardized instrument evaluating the likelihood of dozing (scored 0–3) during routine activities. Total scores range from 0 to 24, with higher values indicating greater sleepiness. 20 In the current study, EDS was defined as an ESS score ≥ 9. This cut‐off value has been used and verified in prior studies among a Chinese population. 21 , 22

Sleep disturbance was evaluated at baseline using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). As a potential confounding variable, sleep disturbance was defined as a PSQI global score ≥ 6, a threshold commonly adopted in Chinese populations to indicate clinically relevant poor sleep. 23

Neuropsychological assessments and cognitive diagnosis

Each participant underwent a comprehensive battery of neuropsychological tests. The global cognition was assessed by the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE). A neuropsychological battery was used to evaluate the domain‐specific cognition including memory, attention, language, executive function, and visuospatial function. The detailed description and normative data of neuropsychological tests have been published previously. 18 , 24 An expert panel, consisting of two neurologists, a neuropsychologist, and a neuroepidemiologist, conducted a consensus diagnosis of cognitive status based on all available medical, neurological, and neuropsychological examinations. 18 , 24 Dementia was diagnosed according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 4th edition (DSM‐IV) criteria. 25 Dementia‐free was defined as the absence of a dementia diagnosis at baseline.

Blood biomarker quantification

Overnight fasting blood samples were collected, centrifuged, and stored at −80°C at baseline, and were used for biomarker assays. Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid plasma phosphorylated tau 217 (p‑tau217) was quantified using ultra‐sensitive single‐molecule array (Simoa) technology (Quanterix) on the automated Simoa HD‐X platform (GBIO). The ALZpath Simoa p‐tau217 v2 (Cat No: 104371) assay kit was used according to the technical notes. 26 , 27

Follow‐up procedure

From April 1, 2014 to December 31, 2023, dementia‐free participants at baseline were evaluated at follow‐up (Figure S1 in supporting information). Cognitive function was evaluated using the same neuropsychological battery at baseline. Consensus diagnosis of incident dementia was conducted by the same panel of experts using the same diagnostic criteria as at baseline. For the primary analysis, follow‐up time was calculated from baseline to dementia diagnosis or the last interview. In sensitivity analyses, mortality through December 31, 2023 was incorporated as a competing event, with follow‐up censored at dementia, death, or last interview.

MRI acquisition and processing

Diffusion‐weighted imaging and T1‐weighted structural images of MRI were acquired using a standardized protocol at baseline and during follow‐up visits (2016–2024). Full acquisition parameters are provided in Table S1 in supporting information.

T1‐weighted images were processed with FreeSurfer (v7.2.0) using the standard automated reconstruction/segmentation pipeline, with manual quality control and edits when needed following FreeSurfer recommendations. 28 Peak width of skeletonized mean diffusivity (PSMD) at baseline was derived from diffusion MRI using an automated workflow based on tract‐based spatial statistics in FSL. 29 , 30 PSMD was then calculated as the difference between the 95th and 5th percentiles of skeletonized mean diffusivity values, providing a summary measure of diffuse white‐matter microstructural alterations. Details are provided in the Methods section in supporting information.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean (standard deviation [SD]), median (interquartile range [IQR]), or n (%), and compared using t tests or chi‐squared tests (χ 2), as appropriate. Dementia incidence rates (per 1000 person‐years) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using Poisson methods. All tests were two sided, with P < 0.05 considered significant.

Participants were stratified into high and low plasma p‑tau217 subgroups based on baseline median value (0.295 pg/ml). In the neuroimaging subcohort, participants were stratified into high and low PSMD subgroups based on baseline median value (4.32 × 10−4 mm2/s).

Linear regression adjusted for age and sex was used to examine the associations of EDS with baseline pathology markers, baseline MMSE, and annual MMSE decline rate, and interaction terms were added to test effect modification by PSMD and p‐tau217. Dementia incidence was estimated using Kaplan–Meier curves and compared by log‐rank test, and Cox models were used to assess the association between EDS and dementia risk after adjustment for age, sex, education, and sleep disturbance, with effect modification by PSMD tested using an interaction term.

Competing‐risk analyses treating death as a competing event were performed as sensitivity analyses using the Gray test and Fine–Gray models adjusted for the same covariates as the primary Cox models.

MRI analyses were conducted in the neuroimaging subcohort. Region of interest (ROI)‐wise linear regression was used to examine the associations of baseline ESS with baseline ROI volumes and longitudinal ROI volume change rates, adjusting for age, sex, and intracranial volume. All MRI analyses were performed within low/high p‐tau217 strata to characterize pathology‐dependent neuroanatomical patterns. Multiple testing across ROIs was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure within each analysis family. Test statistics were used for visualization. Baseline MRI was available in a subcohort of participants (N = 361), and longitudinal MRI analyses were restricted to those who additionally underwent repeat MR scans during follow‐up (N = 194); baseline characteristics of participants with and without baseline MRI, and of those with and without longitudinal MRI among the baseline MRI subcohort, are provided in Tables S2 and S3 in supporting information.

Statistical analyses and visualization were conducted in R (version 4.4.1) using packages tableone, gtsummary, dplyr, lme4, ggplot2, ggeffects, gtsummary, patchwork, survival, survminer, ggpubr, forcats, grid, and fsbrain.

Association between EDS and incident dementia

Over a median follow‐up of 10 years, 55 participants (8.9%) developed dementia. Incidence rate of dementia in EDS was significantly higher than that in the non‐EDS group (18.1 vs. 8.1 per 1000 person‐years, P = 0.018). Among all participants, baseline EDS was associated with a higher risk of incident dementia in the univariate model (HR [95% CI]: 2.06 [1.09, 3.92], P = 0.027); however, such association was not statistically significant after adjusting for age, sex, and education years (Table 2).

Interaction analyses supported that the association between EDS and incident dementia differed significantly across p‐tau217 groups, with a more pronounced association observed in the low p‐tau217 group (P for interaction = 0.025; Table 2). Spline‐based analyses using continuous plasma p‐tau217 levels further indicated a significant non‐linear interaction (likelihood ratio test P = 0.012; Figure S2 in supporting information).

In sensitivity analyses accounting for death as a competing event, EDS remained associated with a higher dementia risk in the overall sample and the low p‐tau217 group, but not in the high p‐tau217 group (Tables S5–S6 in supporting information).

Correlations of ESS with global cognitive decline

In all participants, baseline ESS was not significantly associated with annual decline rate of MMSE scores (β [95% CI], 0.004 [−0.009, 0.016]; P = 0.552). The estimates remained non‐significant in the low p‐tau217 group (β [95% CI], −0.003 [−0.020, 0.013]; P = 0.677) and in the high p‐tau217 group but with a trend toward slower decline (β [95% CI], 0.011 [−0.008, 0.031]; P = 0.245). In linear mixed‐effects models using longitudinal MMSE scores, EDS was not associated with faster MMSE decline in all participants (P for follow‐up year x EDS interaction term = 0.735), nor in the low p‐tau217 subgroup (P for interaction term = 0.978) or the high p‐tau217 subgroup (P for interaction term = 0.655; Table S7 in supporting information).

EDS did not significantly modify the associations of p‐tau217 with baseline MMSE or annual MMSE decline rate (P for interaction = 0.164 for baseline MMSE; P for interaction = 0.585 for annual MMSE decline rate; Figure 2A–B).

ESS and distinct brain structure patterns

Among participants with MRI at baseline, higher ESS was related to smaller volumes in the left choroid plexus and anterior corpus callosum and to larger volume in the right subcallosal gyrus (Figure 3). The pattern of associations differed by p‐tau217 status, with higher ESS linked to smaller regional volumes in the low p‐tau217 group and larger regional volumes in the high p‐tau217 group. Among participants with longitudinal MRI, higher ESS was correlated with faster atrophy in fronto‐temporal areas, the right insular complex, the right lateral superior temporal region, and peri‐central cortices (Figure 3; P uncorrected < 0.05; P > 0.05 after FDR correction). The correlation was observed in the right long insular gyrus and central sulcus of the insula in the low p‐tau217 group (P < 0.05 after FDR correction; Figure 3).

PSMD as a moderator to the association of EDS with long‐term incident dementia

In 244 participants with diffusion MRI, we found that baseline ESS was positively associated with PSMD (β [95% CI], 0.180 [0.054, 0.307]; P = 0.005), and this association persisted after adjustment for age and sex (β [95% CI], 0.161 [0.027, 0.295]; P = 0.019). Interaction analyses further indicated that associations of PSMD with baseline MMSE and MMSE decline rate were stronger among participants with EDS than among those without EDS (P for interaction < 0.001 and P for interaction = 0.009, respectively; Figure 2C–D). PSMD also modified the association between EDS and incident dementia (P for interaction = 0.017). The four‐group Kaplan–Meier curves stratified by PSMD and EDS showed significant difference in cumulative dementia incidence rates (log‐rank P = 0.0064), with the highest observed in the high PSMD × EDS group (Figure 4). Notably, within the low p‐tau217 group, adding PSMD attenuated 53% of the dementia risk.

DISCUSSION

In this 10‐year community‐based prospective cohort, we found that EDS was associated with high risk of cognitive decline especially in individuals with low p‐tau217 independent of sleep disturbance. In the low p‐tau217 group, higher ESS was associated with smaller brain volumes at baseline and accelerated atrophy in salience/arousal and peri‐central sensorimotor systems, and occipito‐temporal territories. PSMD modified the association between EDS and dementia. Participants with high PSMD and EDS showed the highest dementia risk. In the low p‐tau217 group, adding PSMD attenuated 53% of dementia risk, suggesting that white matter injury may contribute to the sleepiness–dementia link when Aβ burden is low.

Our findings are consistent with prior epidemiological studies showing that EDS is associated with cognitive decline and dementia risk in older adults. 5 , 6 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 A meta‐analysis of longitudinal cohorts reported that EDS was significantly related to increased risk of all‐cause dementia (risk ratio [95% CI]: 1.68 [1.07–2.66]), and was also associated with cognitive decline. 39 However, most previous studies were limited by shorter follow‐up durations and primarily focus on Western populations, leaving limited evidence from Asian populations. Nakakubo et al. reported that EDS and long sleep duration were independent risk factors for cognitive decline (adjusted odds ratio [OR; 95% CI]: 1.43 [1.01–2.03]) in a Japanese cohort with a 4‐year follow‐up. 35 Nocturnal sleep disturbance can contribute to daytime sleepiness. However, few studies considered such confounders in the association of EDS and cognition. 5 , 35 Our results showed that EDS was associated with higher incident dementia exclusively in individuals with low AD pathology, independent of sleep disturbance. The lack of association between EDS and MMSE‐based outcomes should be interpreted cautiously. As a brief global screening tool, the MMSE may be insensitive to subtle longitudinal changes and does not adequately capture domains potentially affected by EDS, such as attention and executive function. 40 , 41 By contrast, incident dementia was determined by expert consensus based on comprehensive neuropsychological and clinical assessment, which may explain its stronger association with EDS.

We found that the association of EDS and dementia was largely confined to older adults with low Aβ pathology, not in those with high Aβ pathology. Our MRI findings suggested that in participants with low Aβ pathology, higher ESS was significantly correlated to faster atrophy of the right insular complex. This region integrates interoceptive signals and helps shift attention and control in response to salient internal states, such as fatigue and sleep pressure. 42 It also forms part of networks that maintain functional connectivity with subcortical wake‐promoting nuclei, such as the locus coeruleus and lateral hypothalamic area, which are critical for arousal regulation. 11 Importantly, prior neuroimaging evidence indicated that EDS was associated with greater longitudinal Aβ accumulation, suggesting that sleepiness may emerge early around amyloid changes rather than only in later tau‐dominant stages. 8

We found white matter injury as an important substrate underlying the prognostic significance of EDS, which was consistent with previous evidence. A community‐based Chinese study showed that EDS was associated with larger white matter hyperintensity volumes. 16 Elwood et al. 5 found that EDS was specifically associated with vascular dementia (OR = 4.44) in a 10‐year study. In our neuroimaging subcohort, ESS was positively associated with PSMD, and associations between PSMD and cognition were significantly stronger among participants with EDS than those without EDS. White matter injury may disrupt long‐range communication across fronto‐insular, peri‐central, and temporo‐frontal circuits. 43 This may increase attentional lapses and perceived sleepiness and also strengthen the link between microstructural damage and cognitive vulnerability. This interpretation is consistent with the role of PSMD as a diffusion‐derived, reproducible marker of global white matter integrity associated with cognitive decline and incident dementia. 29 , 30 However, these should be interpreted as exploratory covariate attenuation rather than evidence of mediation or causality. Bidirectional relationships and shared upstream vascular mechanisms may also contribute to the associations among EDS, white matter injury, and dementia. Moreover, because most ROI‐wise associations did not survive multiple‐comparison correction and the MRI analyses were conducted in a relatively small, selected subcohort, these imaging findings should be regarded as hypothesis generating and not as definitive evidence.

Taken together, our results suggest a “pathology‐dependent” interpretation of EDS–cognition. In individuals with low Aβ pathology, EDS behaves more like a prodromal risk phenotype that indicates brain vulnerability, particularly white matter injury. In those with high Aβ pathology, EDS may be more of an accompanying symptom whose incremental prognostic value is reduced because Aβ pathology already dominates dementia risk.

A growing neuropathologic literature has indicated that wake‐promoting nuclei such as the locus coeruleus, lateral hypothalamic area, and tuberomammillary nucleus are vulnerable to early tau pathology and neuronal loss in AD, and clinicopathologic research further links degeneration in these subcortical arousal systems to human sleep symptoms. 10 , 11 , 44 , 45 In addition, plasma p‐tau217 primarily reflects overall circulating AD biomarker burden and is more closely related to global or cortical amyloid/tau pathology than to focal subcortical tau burden. 46 , 47 Thus, low plasma p‐tau217 in our cohort should not be taken to exclude regionally selective tau pathology in wake‐promoting nuclei. Rather, EDS in the low p‐tau217 group may still reflect early tau‐related sleep–wake circuitry dysfunction, while also capturing non‐AD vulnerabilities such as white matter injury.

Key strengths of our study include the long‐term follow‐up in community‐dwelling older adults and biomarker‐based pathological stratification. We integrated plasma p‐tau217, a validated blood biomarker linked to Aβ pathology, 46 , 48 with PSMD, a diffusion MRI marker of small vessel–related white matter injury. 29 , 30 This design allowed us to examine the roles of multimodal markers across co‐existing pathologies in association between EDS and cognition.

Several limitations should be noted: First, EDS was measured using the self‐reported ESS questionnaire so recall bias and misclassification are possible. Future studies should include objective measures, such as polysomnography or objective daytime sleep tests. Second, EDS is a common symptom of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), which is related to cognitive impairment. 49 However, we did not have clinical data on OSA and therefore could not adjust for it directly. The sleep disturbance assessed by PSQI may partially capture related sleep problems, but it is not a specific proxy for sleep‐disordered breathing. In addition, circadian disorders and sedating medication use were not systematically assessed. Thus, we could not fully distinguish whether EDS reflected unmeasured sleep‐disordered breathing or more specific vascular‐ or AD‐related processes. Third, although we adjusted for core demographic and sleep‐related confounders, residual confounding by other dementia‐related factors such as those highlighted in the Lancet Commission framework cannot be excluded. 50 In addition, the sample size was modest, and subgroup analyses after stratification by plasma p‐tau217, EDS status, and MRI availability were substantially smaller. This reduced statistical power, increased estimate uncertainty, and limited our ability to adjust for additional covariates without risking overfitting. Therefore, findings from these subgroup analyses should be interpreted cautiously and not as definitive evidence of no association. The same issue also limited subtype‐specific analyses of incident dementia, so all‐cause dementia remained the primary outcome. Fourth, the imaging findings should be interpreted as hypothesis generating rather than definitive. Participants who were younger and had better baseline cognition were more likely to undergo MRI, and not all those with baseline MRI had longitudinal scans, which may have introduced attrition‐related bias in the longitudinal imaging analyses. In addition, the relatively small MRI sample size limited statistical power, particularly in stratified analyses, and most ROI‐wise associations did not survive FDR correction. Moreover, the longitudinal MRI data were collected over an extended period, during which scanner hardware upgrades and acquisition protocol changes occurred. Although harmonized processing procedures were applied, residual measurement heterogeneity may still have influenced the estimates of longitudinal atrophy. Furthermore, we used a single plasma p‐tau217 measurement as our main AD biomarker and did not have cerebrospinal fluid or positron emission tomography confirmation. This limited our ability to characterize AD pathology status more fully and, importantly, provided no information on regional tau burden. In particular, we could not determine whether individuals with lower plasma p‐tau217 nevertheless harbored focal tau pathology in subcortical wake‐promoting nuclei. Therefore, the mechanistic interpretation of EDS in relation to AD‐related pathology should be considered cautiously. And last, because lifestyle patterns, sleep habits, vascular risk profiles, and health‐care access may differ across populations, the generalizability of our findings from this urban Chinese community cohort to non‐Chinese populations should be interpreted with caution and validated in other settings.

Our study found that EDS was associated with long‐term cognitive decline primarily among participants with low p‐tau217. White matter injury appeared to be an important contributor. These findings support EDS as a risk symptom in older adults who do not yet show high Aβ pathology burden, and suggest that vascular‐related brain injury may be an important pathway linking sleepiness to dementia. Future studies should verify these results by incorporating objective sleep assessment and multimodal blood and imaging biomarkers in larger sampled populations, and further investigate the related mechanisms.
