Assessing the Impact of Reduced L1 Bone Density on Surgical Stabilisation of Rib Fractures in Elderly Patients: A Study on Fracture Nonunion Rates, Analgesia Usage and Hospital Length of Stay
Authors: Joseph K Hwang, Philip A Jeremic, Ian Hughes, John Grieve, Elizabeth Wake, Don Campbell, Bhavik Patel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionRib fractures following falls are common among patients over 70 years of age. While nonoperative management is standard, surgical stabilisation of rib fractures (SSRF) is increasingly performed in selected elderly patients despite concerns about frailty and comorbidities. Although osteoporosis significantly increases fracture risk, rib fractures have not traditionally been categorised as fragility fractures.MethodsThis study retrospectively evaluated bone mineral density (BMD), represented by L1 vertebral Hounsfield units (HU) and correlated clinical outcomes in patients with rib fractures from trauma. A control group of 100 age‐ and sex‐matched nontrauma patients was included for comparison.ResultsOverall, trauma patients exhibited higher mean L1 HU values, 125.0, compared to controls, 105.5 (p = 3 × 10−5). Within the patients sustaining rib fractures, the low‐energy trauma group had lower mean L1 HU values (112.6) than those from high‐energy trauma (133, p = 0.002), suggesting a link between lower BMD and susceptibility to rib fractures. Low BMD (L1 HU ≤ 110) was not associated with worse clinical outcomes such as hospital length of stay, analgesia requirements or fracture healing, whether managed operatively or nonoperatively. Low BMD did not affect rib union post‐SSRF, with no hardware failures. These results suggest that low BMD alone should not contraindicate SSRF.ConclusionThe authors recommend routine osteoporosis screening using opportunistic L1 HU measurement on trauma CT scans, particularly in elderly patients sustaining low‐energy trauma, to facilitate early osteoporosis management and reduce future fracture risk.Keywords: bone mineral density (BMD), computed tomography (CT), geriatric trauma, Hounsfield units (HU), rib fracture

Introduction

Rib fractures following falls in patients over 70 years of age are a common presentation to emergency departments worldwide [1]. Management in this population is usually nonoperative, though recent literature indicates an increasing trend toward surgical stabilisation of rib fractures (SSRF) in select elderly patients [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. The Chest Wall Injury Society (CWIS) guidelines for SSRF list ‘significant comorbidities and frailty’ as relative contraindications, specifically citing active malignancy, severe cardiopulmonary disease and terminal illness. Such comorbidities increase the risks of rib fixation, making SSRF technically challenging [1]; however, low bone mineral density (BMD) or osteoporosis has not been defined as a significant comorbidity. Thus, individual patient assessment if required for risk evaluation of SSRF. Nonetheless, the CWIS guidelines suggest that carefully selected older patients may still derive substantial benefit from SSRF. Our institution routinely performs SSRF (with RibLoc bi cortical screw plating system, Acumed) in patients over 70 without standard preoperative osteoporosis screening (Supporting Information S1). The difference in technique for the geriatric population at our institution is to use longer plates across the fracture line in order to spread the tension and accept a near but not total, approximation for reduction.

Gold standard way of measuring BMD is using dual X‐ray absorptiometry (DXA) as g/cm2 of scanned area or, less frequently, by quantitative computed tomography radiography (QCT) as g/cm3 of scanned volume. Low BMD is a well‐established risk factor for fractures, including rib fractures. Mai et al. showed that each standard deviation (0.15 g/cm2) decrease in femoral neck BMD doubles the risk of sustaining a rib fracture (HR 1.9; 95% CI, 1.4–2.6 in men; HR 2.1; 95% CI, 1.6–2.8 in women) [7]. Fractures resulting from low‐energy mechanisms (e.g., wrist, hip, vertebra or humerus fractures from standing‐height falls) are classified as fragility fractures and are common in patients with low BMD [8, 9, 10]. Rib fractures, however, are not typically categorised as fragility fractures. Given the strong association between fragility fractures and low BMD, international guidelines recommend osteoporosis screening in patients with fragility fractures [8, 10, 11]. Osteoporosis is defined by a T‐score (standard deviations from the mean BMD of a health young adult) ≤ −2.5. This systemic skeletal disorder is especially prevalent in older populations, particularly among women over 70 [8, 9, 10, 11].

In CT imaging, Hounsfield units (HU) quantify tissue density [12]. By convention, water is defined as 0 HU, air as‐1000 HU, and dense cortical bone typically measures in the hundreds of HU. QCT is based on CT calibrated to various BMDs using solid phantoms made of calcium hydroxyapatite. However, CT has been extensively investigated as a tool for estimating BMD in opportunistic screening, using HU measurements directly to infer bone density and identify osteoporosis [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. The first lumbar vertebra (L1) is commonly used for such assessments because it is included on routine chest and abdominal CT scans. Studies have demonstrated high inter and intraobserver reliability in measuring L1 HU [14, 20, 21]. L1 HU correlates strongly with DXA‐derived BMD and fracture risk, although variations in patient demographics and CT scanner settings can influence absolute HU values [21, 22, 23, 24].

Establishing a link between low L1 HU value in patients presenting with trauma and rib fractures could change practice by prompting earlier osteoporosis evaluation and management in these patients [8, 9, 10]. Our institution routinely performs SSRF in patients over 70 without standard preoperative osteoporosis screening. This study aims to retrospectively review our experience and address this gap by:

Identifying the distribution of L1 vertebral body HU values (as a surrogate for BMD) in trauma patients ≥ 70 years old with ≥ 3 acute rib fractures from blunt chest trauma necessitates early screening for osteoporosis.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study at a single tertiary trauma centre over a 5‐year period ending January 2023. Inclusion criteria were patients aged 70 years and older who sustained blunt chest trauma resulting in three or more acute rib fractures confirmed on CT scan within 48 h of admission. We excluded patients managed by nontrauma specialties or not assessed by the trauma service, as well as those with incomplete records or missing CT images in our Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). Institutional ethics approval was obtained prior to data collection. A query of the hospital trauma database initially identified 361 patients meeting the injury criteria, of whom 131 were excluded (Figure 1) due to the above criteria. The final study sample consisted of 230 patients. An additional control group of 121 age‐ and sex‐matched patients without trauma (undergoing CT for other indications on the same dates as the trauma patients) was identified via PACS searches.

Number of patients who met inclusion criteria (blue arrows) and number of patients who were excluded (right column).

Data Collection

We collected patient demographics and medical comorbidities from electronic medical records, which were summarised using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [25]. Frailty scores were not consistently documented for the time period of this study; thus, they have been excluded. Admission CT scans were reviewed to assess vertebral bone density. Specifically, multiple radiology trainees measured HU values in an oval region of interest on the anterior third of the L1 vertebral body on axial, coronal and sagittal reconstructions (Figure 2). The mean of these L1 HU measurements was recorded for analysis as an estimate of BMD. If the L1 vertebra was unsuitable for measurement due to local pathology (lesion, fracture) or artefact, an adjacent vertebra (T12 or L2) was used instead. For each patient in the control group, L1 HU was measured using the same protocol.

Axial, coronal and sagittal slices of a study group patient demonstrating the mean HU of the selected region of interest (ROI) in the anterior third of the L1 vertebral body.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was the L1 vertebral body HU value on the admission CT scan. We also stratified patients by L1 HU to define a low BMD group as HU ≤ 110 based on prior literature. Secondary outcomes included: hospital length of stay; involvement of the APS for specialised rib fracture analgesia management; use of patient‐controlled analgesia (PCA) and its duration; use of regional analgesia techniques (nerve blocks or local anaesthetic infusions) and their duration; and the incidence of rib fracture nonunion or hardware failure on follow‐up chest CT at 12 months. We also documented the management approach for each patient (operative vs. nonoperative). Mechanism of injury was categorised as high‐energy trauma (HET, e.g., motor vehicle or motorcycle crashes, heavy machinery accidents), medium‐energy trauma (MET, fall from height > 1 m) or low‐energy trauma (LET, e.g., falls from standing height or from a chair).

Statistical Analysis

Histograms and summary statistics, mean, standard deviation, median and interquartile range were used to describe the distribution of HU in the rib fracture and control groups. Following visual inspection of histograms for normality, age was summarised as mean and standard deviation and CCI, total number of ribs fractured and hospital LOS were summarised as median and interquartile range (IQR). Sex, energy‐mechanism of injury, use of the acute pain service and SSRF were described by their category proportions. Linear regression and t‐test were used to assess the association of energy‐mechanism of injury, CCI and use of SSRF and the acute pain service to L1 HU. Linear regression was used to estimate the effect of L1 HU on hospital LOS (log transformed). Potential covariates considered were age, sex, CCI, total number of ribs fractured, energy of trauma, pneumothorax, haemothorax, flail chest and pulmonary contusions. Each variable was tested singly and considered along with HU in a multivariable model if p < 0.1 and retained if p < 0.05. Comparison of L1 HU between the rib fracture and control groups was undertaken with a t‐test. Similar comparisons, using chi‐square tests, were performed based on the dichotomous, HU ≤ 110, low BMD variable. The effect of the low BMD dichotomy on days PCA was tested using the nonparametric rank‐sum test.

Results

A total of 230 trauma patients met the inclusion criteria, of whom 89 (39%) were female. Low‐BMD (an L1 HU ≤ 110) was recorded in 98 (43%). In this subgroup, 46 (47%) were female. The median (IQR) age of all patients was 78 (73, 85) years, and 79 (74, 87) years in the low‐BMD subgroup, and 77 (73, 84) years in the higher‐BMD group (p = 0.08). Comorbidity burden was high: the median Charlson Comorbidity Index was 5 (4, 6) in the overall cohort, 5 (4, 6) in the low‐BMD subgroup and 4.5 (3, 6) in the higher‐BMD group (p = 0.31).

Patients who sustained high‐energy trauma (HET, n = 92) had a mean (SD) L1 HU of 133.0 (4.6), which was similar to the mean of 131.6 (6.3) in those who fell from a height > 1 m (MET, n = 50, p = 0.86). In contrast, patients with low‐energy trauma (LET, ground‐level falls, n = 87) had a markedly lower mean L1 HU of 112.6 (4.8) than the HET group (p = 0.002) or the MET group (p = 0.016) (Figure 3). The mean (SD) L1 vertebral HU in the trauma cohort was 124.8 (45.2), compared to 106.7 (29.9) in the age‐ and sex‐matched control group (n = 121, who had no acute trauma, p = 2.8 × 10−5).

Mean L1 vertebral body density by mechanism of trauma. HET: high energy trauma (motor vehicle accident, pedestrian vs. motor vehicle); MET: fall from height (fall from ladder, fall from roof, fall from steps/stairs), LET: low energy trauma (fall from standing height or less).

Out of 230 patients, 27 (11.7%) underwent SSRF during their hospitalisation, whereas 203 (88.3%) were managed nonoperatively (with analgesia and supportive care). Notably, the mean L1 HU was virtually the same in surgically managed patients, 124.5 (43.1), as in those managed conservatively, 125.0 (45.7; p = 0.95).

There was no evidence of a relationship between mean L1 HU and hospital LOS (log transformed; p = 0.90) after adjusting for pulmonary contusions (p = 0.04), which was the only covariate identified. This was also the case when LI HU was dichotomised into low or normal BMD subgroups (p = 0.78).

The median (interquartile range; IQR) for days of patient‐controlled intravenous analgesia (PCA) was 3 (2, 5) in the normal BMD group and 3 (2, 4) in the low BMD group (p = 0.57).

Discussion

Analysis of L1 HU in trauma patients with > 3 rib fractures identified a significant proportion of patients with bone density concerning for osteoporosis (41% with L1 HU < 110). Further subgroup analysis into trauma mechanism shows statistically significant differences in average L1 HU based on trauma mechanism. High‐energy trauma patients' rib fractures had higher L1 HU than those from low‐energy trauma, reflecting poorer bone mineral density in the low‐energy trauma group. This suggests that patients presenting with multiple rib fractures, especially in those from low‐energy trauma, should routinely and aggressively be referred for further testing (such as DXA and blood test) to guide treatment options.

Overall, there were no significant differences in hospital LOS, PCA usage or duration of regional analgesia between patients with L1 HU < 110 and those with HU ≥ 110, regardless of whether they underwent SSRF or conservative management. This suggests that, in our cohort, low bone density alone did not translate into worse short‐term clinical outcomes after rib fractures. Most patients (204 of 230, ~89%) were managed nonoperatively.

Among the surgical group, those with lower bone density (HU ≤ 110) had broadly similar hospital stays (20.5 days vs. 22.7 days) and analgesia requirements compared to those with higher HU. One notable difference was in the SSRF subgroup: patients with low BMD were more likely to receive regional analgesia infusions (80% vs. 31%), suggesting a potentially greater need for multimodal pain control in the osteoporotic group. The low operative numbers in the study can be due to multiple factors, which include but are not limited to poor patient physiology, reluctance from the system to support the operation (No availability of ICU/HDU support postoperatively) and patient or next of kin declining the procedure. There were no cases of rib nonunion or hardware failure on follow‐up in either the low L1 HU or high L1 HU surgical patients, indicating that SSRF provides effective fixation even in patients with low bone density; however, we are aware that the small number of patients is not adequate to show that lower BMD is not a risk factor for hardware issues.

The trauma patients in our study had a higher average L1 HU (125.0) than the matched control group (105.5). The control patients, who were imaged for nontrauma reasons (often chronic disease or cancer staging), appeared to represent a less robust population with somewhat lower BMD. This finding suggests that trauma patients over 70 in our study may have had better overall bone quality compared to an age‐matched nontrauma population, highlighting the need for individualised assessment of bone health.

There are a few limitations to this study: it is difficult to adjust for the severity of underlying injuries (number of rib fractures [not just < 3 or 3+], uni‐ vs. bilateral, flail chest, haemo‐ or pneumothorax, pulmonary contusions, other injuries, etc.), not just medical co‐morbidities using the CCI to come to a conclusion with regard to osteoporosis and outcomes of rib fractures.

There could be a possibility of interobserver variability as multiple radiology trainees performed L1 HU assessment; however, this was addressed by protocolised methodology, which averaged multiple different views for the averaging of the L1 HU value.

Conclusion

In elderly trauma patients (> 70 years) with multiple rib fractures, low bone density (L1 HU < 110) was a common finding that was missed during our routine management. This study suggests osteoporosis screening using L1 HU on trauma CT scans could be considered in low‐energy trauma mechanisms. This may lead to early further investigations toward osteoporosis screening in the geriatric population.

Even though only a small volume of patients with low bone density underwent SSRF in this study, low BMD should not be considered as an absolute contraindication to SSRF and may need alteration in techniques to achieve good postoperative outcomes.
