Coverage-Dependent Adsorption Behavior of N‑Containing Gases on Nb2CT x MXenes: A DFT Study
Authors: Huamin Gao, Jun Ji, Yiwen Li, Chang Hao, Zitong Li, Mingling Li, Dechun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The regulation mechanism of surface functional group coverage on Nb-based MXenes for nitrogen-containing gas adsorption is systematically explored via density functional theory calculations. The adsorption behaviors of typical nitrogen-containing gases on Nb2CT x MXenes with gradient -F and -O surface coverage are comprehensively characterized. Optimal selective adsorption is revealed to be achieved by partial functional group coverage via balancing active site exposure and electron transfer efficiency, while full coverage is found to significantly weaken gas-surface interfacial interactions. The intrinsic correlation among adsorption energy, adsorption distance, and charge transfer quantity is confirmed, providing critical theoretical guidance for the rational design of high-performance MXene-based gas-sensing and nitrogenous pollutant capture materials.

Introduction

Climate change and atmospheric pollution represent two interconnected sustainability challenges on a global scale, forming a vicious feedback loop in which the deterioration of one exacerbates the other. Increasing emissions of hazardous gasessuch as CO, SO2, NO x , NH3, and CH4not only alter the fundamental composition of the atmosphere but also contribute to serious environmental threats, including ozone layer depletion and acid rain, alongside posing substantial risks to public health. , Among these pollutants, nitrogen-containing gases are of particular concern due to their diverse sources and associated hazards. For example, NH3, a highly toxic constituent widely used in fertilizers, is identified as a priority hazardous pollutant that must be removed from industrial emissions prior to atmospheric release. However, the desorption of NH3 typically requires multistep reaction processes or elevated temperatures exceeding 450 K, which considerably restricts its application in gas sensors and high-efficiency capture systems. Consequently, there is a growing urgency to develop novel sensing materials that can enhance detection sensitivity and overcome existing limitations in gas sensor performance.

Two-dimensional (2D) materials, including graphene, nanobelts, phosphorene, MXenes, and transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), characterized by their exceptional physicochemical properties and tunable surface functionalities, have emerged as promising candidates for next-generation gas-sensing platforms. − MXenes are 2D transition-metal carbides/nitrides synthesized by selectively etching A-group from the layered MAX phase. , The general expression for MXene is M n+1X n T x , where T x are the surface terminations (such as –O, −OH, –F, and –Cl) formed from the different etching processes. − Their tunable surface chemical groups, high electrical conductivity, and specific surface area make MXenes promising candidates for gas sensors. ,,,

With the development of computational materials science, DFT calculations are widely used for their unparalleled atomic-level insights into sensing mechanisms and material properties. , In the field of gas sensing, DFT calculations reveal the interaction between gas molecules and basis materials. − Shamim et al. studied the gas-sensing potential of Nb2C and Mo2C MXene nanosheets and their heterostructures for detecting CO and NO, finding that gas molecules were adsorbed on the Mo and Nb sites of the nanosheets, with the Nb site being more favorable. Rafique et al. investigated the CO2 adsorption behavior on Ti2C and Nb2C MXene through density of states (DOS) analysis, which revealed that the CO2 molecule dissociates into CO and O atoms on the MXene surface. Kharb et al. investigated the sensing mechanisms of TiVCO2 for eight environmentally relevant gases and found that NO exhibits the strongest yet reversible physisorption, accompanied by a significant charge transfer and Fermi-level state modulation. In contrast, NH3 and NO2 show strong chemisorption but suffer from irreversible structural distortions, while H2, CO, SO2, H2S, and CH4 exhibit weak physisorption with negligible electronic perturbations.

Notably, most previous DFT studies on MXene-based gas sensing have focused on Ti-based MXenes. Systematic investigations of Nb2C MXeneespecially the effect of different surface terminations on its interaction with nitrogen-containing gasesremain lacking. In this work, we investigated the adsorption behavior of Nb2C MXene with different terminal groups (pristine, –F, and –O) toward N-containing gases (N2, NH3, NO, and NO2). Special attention is paid to the distinct adsorption behaviors of NO2, whose N–O bonds tend to cleave under non-100% coverage, owing to its high reactivity and strong interaction with Nb active sites and electronegative functional groups. The systematic exploration of coverage-gradient models also enables the identification of optimal surface configurations that balance active site accessibility and electron transfer efficiency. Collectively, this study establishes a quantitative correlation between surface functionalization and gas adsorption performance, laying a theoretical foundation for the targeted modification of Nb2CT x MXenes in environmental remediation applications.

Method

First-principles calculations were performed using the CASTEP code within the density functional theory (DFT) framework. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional, supplemented by the Grimme DFT-D3 dispersion correction, was employed to describe electron exchange-correlation interactions, while ultrasoft pseudopotentials modeled the electron–ion interactions. Periodic 3 × 3 × 1supercell surficial configurations were constructed by cleaving the bulk structure along the (001) crystallographic plane, and a vacuum layer of 15 Å was introduced perpendicular to the surface to mitigate periodic boundary artifacts. For the systems with 33% and 66% coverage, 8 random configurations with distinct functional group distribution were preconstructed. Geometric optimization and total energy calculation were performed for all candidate configurations, and the unique configuration with the lowest total energy was selected as the sole model. The plane-wave cutoff energies and k-points were used as 500 eV and 3 × 3 × 1, respectively. To effectively address the convergence difficulties caused by the discontinuity of the density of states at the Fermi level, the smearing width was set to 0.1 eV. All models were relaxed until the forces on each atom were <0.03 eV/Å, and the residual stress of the system was converged to 0.05 GPa. The maximum atomic displacement was set at 0.001 Å, and the total energy change during the optimization finally converged to 10–5 eV/atom.

The adsorption energy of N-containing gas can be calculated as follows 1 Eads=ENb2CTx*−E*−ENb2CTx where E ads is the adsorption energy, E Nb2CT x is the energy of Nb2CT X surficial configuration, E Nb2CT x * is the energy of Nb2CT X surficial configuration with gas molecular adsorption, and E* is the energy of the gas molecule. The adsorption distance was computed as follows: for 0%, 33%, and 66% systems, the average basal plane fitted by all outermost Nb atoms of the Nb2CT x MXene was set as the reference plane. For the 100% system, the reference plane was selected as the T x layer. The vertical distances from all non-hydrogen heavy atoms of the adsorbed gas molecule to this reference plane were calculated, and the arithmetic mean of all these vertical distances was taken as the average adsorption distance of the adsorption system.

The charge density difference (CDD) was calculated using the universally accepted standard formula for surface adsorption DFT studies 2 Δρ=ρNb2CTx*−ρ*−ρNb2CTx where ρNb2CT x * is the total charge density of the fully optimized gas-adsorbed Nb2CT X MXene complex system, and ρ* and ρNb2CT x are the charge densities of the isolated gas and Nb2CT X MXene substrate, respectively.

Results and Discussion

Structures of Nb2CT x MXenes

Nb2CT X MXenes are composed of 3 and 5 atomic layers, respectively. The C atomic layer is located at the central position, which connects two layers of Nb atoms, while the outermost layer consists of modified functional groups T x (–F and –O). After geometry optimization, the most stable configurations of Nb2CT X MXenes are shown in Figure , where all the T x groups are located above the hcp-site of three neighboring Nb atoms, consistent with the findings in earlier studies. , The Nb-T x bond length is calculated to be 2.283 and 2.094 Å for Nb2CF2 and Nb2CO2, respectively. Although F has the smallest atomic radius, its highest electronegativity, relatively low charge transfer quantity (0.47 e), and a bond population of 0.40 determine that the Nb–F bond is dominated by ionic bond character (Table ). In contrast, when T x is O, both the charge transfer between Nb and O (Nb loses 1.05 e and O gains 0.67 e) and the bond population (0.94) are significantly higher than those of the Nb–F bond. These characteristics are hallmarks of strong covalent bond interactions, correlating with the shorter Nb–O bond length (2.094 Å), as covalent bonds tend to be shorter due to stronger atomic orbital overlap.

The charge density difference (CDD) patterns provide an intuitive visualization of the electron distribution across different Nb2CT x MXenes. In this figure, regions of electron accumulation and depletion clearly delineate the extent of the electron transfer between Nb and T x . It is evident that the electron transfer between Nb and O in Nb2CO2 is the most pronounced, shown by a more distinct separation of electron-rich and electron-poor regions around the Nb–O bond, which is in excellent agreement with the quantitative data presented in Table . The bond energy of covalent bonds is generally higher than that of ionic bonds because of the stronger orbital overlap in covalent interactions. This fundamental chemical principle explains why MXenes terminated with -F can be easily modified into O-terminated MXenes: the Nb–F bonds are thermodynamically favorable to break and allow the replacement of -F by O atoms. , In contrast, the resulting Nb–O bond stabilizes the O-terminated structure, ensuring the feasibility of such surface-modification processes.

Band structure calculations were performed along the standard high-symmetry path Γ-M-K-Γ for the 2D hexagonal Brillouin zone of the (001)-oriented Nb2CT x MXene slab, with high-symmetry points explicitly defined as Γ (0, 0, 0), M (1/2, 0, 0), and K (1/3, 1/3, 0). Figure presents the band structures and total density of states (TDOS) for three distinct Nb2CT x MXene configurations, where a pivotal common feature across all four Nb2CT x systems is that the Fermi level cuts through both the valence bands (VB) and conduction bands (CB) of each MXene. This phenomenon is a defining hallmark of metallic electronic behavior, as it indicates the absence of an intrinsic energy band gap between the VB and CB, thereby confirming that all four Nb2CT x MXenes inherently exhibit metallicity and possess baseline electrical conductivity.

Band structures and total density of states of (a) Nb2C, (b) Nb2CF2, and (c) Nb2CO2.

With respect to the TDOS, a prominent variation is observed following surface functionalization relative to the pristine Nb2C. Quantitatively, the TDOS value of pristine Nb2C at the Fermi level is 3.05 states/eV. After surface modification, this Fermi-level TDOS undergoes a substantial reduction: it decreases to 1.84 states/eV for Nb2CF2 and to 1.41 states/eV for Nb2CO2. Notably, the DOS at the Fermi level is a critical metric reflecting the number of available charge carriers for electrical transporthigher Fermi-level DOS values correspond to a larger pool of free charge carriers, which directly favors enhanced electrical conductivity. Thus, the observed reduction in Fermi-level DOS following surface functionalization explicitly demonstrates that the surface electrical conductivity of Nb2CT x MXenes is attenuated after the introduction of -F and -O terminal groups.

The partial density of states (PDOS) provides the electronic states and orbital hybridization of different structures (Figure ). In the pristine Nb2C MXene structure (Figure a), significant hybridization between Nb d-orbitals and C p-orbitals is evident around −5 eV. This hybridization signifies the strong covalent bonding between Nb and C atoms within Nb2C MXene, defining its intrinsic electronic properties. Upon adsorption of -F onto the Nb2C surface (Figure b), a strong hybridization between Nb d and F p-orbitals is observed at approximately −7.5 eV, accompanied by the splitting of the Nb d states into three distinct peaks. This phenomenon is attributable to the exceptionally high electronegativity of F, which causes a substantial withdrawal of electron density from the Nb d-orbitals, leading to a reorganization of their energy levels. When -O is adsorbed (Figure c), a broad hybridization occurs between Nb d-orbitals, O p orbitals, and the intrinsic C p orbitals. This widespread hybridization extends over an energy range from −2.5 eV to −7.5 eV, presenting as a diffuse, single broad peak in the Nb d-orbital PDOS. This suggests a stronger and more complex covalent bonding interaction between Nb and oxygen, leading to a greater degree of electron delocalization. While oxygen is more electronegative than carbon, its electronegativity is lower than that of fluorine. The interaction between oxygen and Nb results in a more uniform electron cloud distribution, causing the Nb d-state energy peaks to merge into a broad envelope.

Partial density of states (PDOS) of (a) Nb2C, (b) Nb2CF2, and (c) Nb2CO2.

The observed changes in the PDOS of Nb2CT x MXenes are a result of the interplay between the electronegativity of the adsorbate and the energy alignment of the atomic orbitals. The high electronegativity of F leads to electron localization and the splitting of Nb d state peaks, while the strong covalent interaction of Nb–O promotes electron delocalization, forming a broad Nb d state peak.

The work function values of the Nb2CT x models (Figure ) exhibit distinct differences that are predominantly regulated by surface functional groups, offering critical insights into their electronic properties and their application potential. Specifically, Nb2CO2 features the largest work function (5.72 eV), indicating prominent electron-accepting characteristics. In contrast, Nb2C (4.25 eV) and Nb2CF2 (4.28 eV) show nearly identical work functions, reflecting moderate and balanced electron-donating/accepting capabilities. It can be deduced that the O groups substantially increase the work function, and the F groups exert a negligible influence on the work function of pristine Nb2C. The high work function of Nb2CO2 is favorable for the electron collection in electronic devices.

The Adsorption Energy of Nb2CT x MXenes for N-Containing Gases

The coverage of surface functional groups on MXene is a core parameter for regulating its performance and altering properties like adsorption and catalysis. − Thus, we calculated the E ads value of Nb2CT x with different functional group coverage toward four N-containing gases (Figure ), where panels (a), (b), (c), and (d) correspond to the N2, NO, NO2, and NH3 systems, respectively. In this work, E ads is defined as E ads = E Nb2CT x * – E* – E Nb2CT x , where a more negative and a larger absolute value of E ads both indicate stronger adsorption interaction between the gas molecule and Nb2CT x substrate. In Figure (a), the E ads value of Nb2CF2 toward N2 first decreases to −3.39 eV at 33% coverage and then increases gradually, while that of Nb2CO2 increases continuously toward less negative values with increasing coverage, corresponding to a gradually weakened adsorption interaction. In Figure (b), the adsorption energy of both materials toward NO reaches a peak at 33% coverage (−4.70 eV for Nb2CF2, −4.21 eV for Nb2CO2), followed by a gradual increase with increasing coverage. Notably, in Figure (c), the pristine Nb2C shows the most negative E ads value (−13.52 eV) toward NO2 compared to other target gases and the E ads of Nb2CO2 decreases sharply to −25.27 eV at 33% coverage. In Figure (d), the adsorption energy of both materials toward NH3 exhibits small overall fluctuations from 0% to 66% coverage and shows a slight increase at 100% coverage.

The Correlations between Adsorption Energy, Adsorption Distance, and Charge Transfer Quantity

The adsorption configuration of gas molecules is determined by the intrinsic properties of the gas molecules and the characteristics of the adsorbent surface. Modifications to either of these two factors can alter the adsorption configuration of gas molecules, and in extreme cases, even induce dissociation of the adsorbed species. Figures S1–S8 present the adsorption configurations of four N-containing gas molecules on Nb2CT x MXene surfaces terminated with -F or -O at varying coverage degrees. Among these molecules, NO2 exhibits the most pronounced changes in adsorption configuration, which maintains a stable molecular configuration only when adsorbed on Nb2CT x surfaces with 100% -F or -O coverage, whereas cleavage of N–O bonds is observed in all other coverage scenarios. In this section, we conduct a systematic analysis of the adsorption behaviors of N-containing gas molecules on Nb2CT x MXene surfaces with varying -F and -O coverage degrees, incorporating key characterization including adsorption configurations, adsorption energies, adsorption distances, and charge transfer properties.

Adsorption energy (E ads) and adsorption distance (D ads) between adsorbate and adsorbent active sites are key parameters that reflect interaction strength. Shorter adsorption distances contribute to stronger interactions and higher absolute adsorption energy values. In this work, certain gas molecules undergo significant deformation or decomposition following adsorption, and variations in surface functional group coverage give rise to a nonplanar MXene surface. Consequently, when determining the adsorption distance, we define it as the average distance between the gas molecules and the surface.

Figure and Table depict the quantitative correlation characteristics among adsorption energy (E ads), adsorption distance (D ads), and charge transfer quantity (Q t) in -F/-O-terminated Nb2CT x MXene adsorption systems. All four subfigures are divided into three interaction regions based on the value of E ads, which is the strong interaction region (E ads < −12 eV), middle interaction region (−3 eV < E ads < −8 eV), and weak interaction region (E ads > −3 eV). Overall, E ads exhibits a negative correlation with D ads and a positive correlation with Q t, which aligns with the fundamental principles of interfacial interactions: short-range interactions intensify with decreasing distance, and charge transfer serves as the core electronic effect driving adsorption. For 100% functional group coverage systems, all gas-substrate combinations display weak adsorption except for NO2 (middle-strength adsorption on Nb2CF2), which arises from excessive functional groups densely covering the substrate surface, occupying Nb active sites and impeding direct contact between adsorbates and the substrate, thereby weakening interfacial interactions.

From the perspective of gas molecules, NO2 exhibits the strongest interaction with Nb2CT x , showing E ads more negative than −12 eV on F-terminated Nb2CT x surfaces (at 0%, 33%, and 66% functional group coverage) and an even more negative E ads (<−25 eV) on the 33% O-terminated counterpart. This ultrahigh adsorption energy arises from the heightened reactivity of active Nb sites on the 33% O-terminated substrate, a process that is potentially coupled with the cleavage of N–O covalent bonds in the adsorbed NO2 molecule. NO exhibits middle-strength adsorption, with E ads stably ranging from −5 to −3 eV under 0%, 33%, and 66% F/O coverage, indicating that the regulatory effect of the functional group type and coverage within this range on NO adsorption is relatively limited. However, NO shifts to weak adsorption at 100% coverage, which is directly associated with the competitive occupation of active sites by functional groups at a high coverage. Adsorption interactions of N2 and NH3 are dominated by weak physical interactions. N2 shows a middle-strength interaction only in 33% and 66% F-terminated systems, while both molecules exhibit weak adsorption under all other conditions. This distinct adsorption behavior is closely correlated to the polarity of gas molecules and the electronegativity of surface functional groups.

The Charge Transfer between Gas and Nb2CT x MXenes

As evidenced by previous studies, transition-metal elements inherently possess d-orbital electrons due to their intrinsic electron configuration, which gives rise to their tunable chemical reactivity and strong orbital hybridization capacity, thereby endowing MXene materials with excellent adsorption and catalytic performance. ,, When the transition-metal sites are fully terminated by functional groups, the interactions between the d-orbital electrons and gas molecules are shielded or attenuated, which is manifested as decreased adsorption energy and diminished catalytic activity. The variation trend of adsorption energy in the systems with 100% -F or -O coverage, as presented in Figure , is consistent with this mechanism. However, we further observed that partial functional group coverage leads to substrate-dependent adsorption behaviors toward different gas molecules. To elucidate the underlying mechanism, we investigated the adsorption performance of MXenes with 33% functional group coverage by combining charge density difference analysis, since this coverage model exhibits the most distinct modulation effects on gas adsorption.

The interfacial charge rearrangement characteristics of the four gases show significant differences, which are dominated by molecular polarity and chemical reactivity. The CDD plots of the four isolated gas molecules are provided in Figure S9. For nonpolar N2 shown in Figure , the charge rearrangement is extremely weak on pristine Nb2C and 33% O-terminated Nb2CO2 surfaces, and there is no obvious change in the molecular adsorption configuration. In the 33% F-terminated Nb2CF2 system, the weak ionic Nb–F bond undergoes slight elongation under the repulsion of N2 molecules, which drives the tilting of N2 molecules and the formation of a continuous charge accumulation region at the gas–solid interface, thus improving the charge transfer efficiency and enhancing the adsorption energy to 3.39 eV. In sharp contrast, the strongly polar and highly reactive NO2 induces drastic interfacial charge rearrangement on all three surfaces (Figure ). On pristine Nb2C and 33% F-terminated Nb2CF2, the intense charge transfer between NO2 and Nb sites drives the cleavage of one N–O bond, forming dissociated NO and O species, with Qt exceeding 0.6 e and adsorption energy over 12 eV. In the 33% O-terminated Nb2CO2 system, the high electronegativity of the O functional groups induces more drastic interfacial electron rearrangement, driving complete cleavage of both N–O bonds in NO2 molecules. This deep dissociation behavior is accompanied by the maximized enhancement of interfacial interactions, leading to an absolute adsorption energy value surpassing 25 eV. This result fully illustrates the synergistic enhancement mechanism of adsorption interactions between strongly polar molecules (NO2) and high-electronegativity functional groups (-O), where the electronic effects of the two are coupled to significantly promote chemical bond activation and dissociation, thereby substantially improving the adsorption strength.

Top and side views of CDD distributions for N2 molecules adsorbed on Nb2C, 33% F-terminated Nb2CF2, and 33% O-terminated Nb2CO2 surfaces.

For NO (Figure ) and NH3 (Figure ), their charge transfer behaviors fall between the two extremes of N2 and NO2. NO, as a polar reactive molecule, shows a moderate expansion of the charge accumulation region on 33% terminated surfaces compared to pristine Nb2C. The slight tilting of the NO molecule promotes the orbital overlap between N atoms and Nb active sites, enhancing the charge transfer efficiency without obvious bond cleavage or molecular deformation. In comparison, NH3 exhibits highly consistent weak charge transfer characteristics across all three surfaces. The molecule maintains a stable vertical adsorption configuration without tilting or bond cleavage and only forms localized weak charge accumulation between the N atom and Nb sites. The negligible charge transfer in all NH3 systems confirms that adsorption is dominated by weak van der Waals interactions (Figure ).

The effect of surface functional groups on charge transfer also shows an obvious gas selectivity. For inert N2, only the weakly ionic -F group with low bond energy can effectively regulate its charge transfer behavior via bond deformation and active site exposure. For highly reactive NO2, both -F and -O groups can enhance the interfacial charge transfer, and the high electronegativity -O group shows a more significant synergistic enhancement effect with polar NO2 molecules. For NO and NH3, the type of functional groups has a limited effect on their charge transfer intensity: the charge transfer of NO is moderately enhanced at 33% coverage, while NH3 maintains weak charge transfer regardless of the functional group type, which is determined by the intrinsic molecular properties of the two gases (Figure ).

Synergistic Effect between Nb and Surface Functional Groups

Given the excellent adsorption capacities of 33% Nb2CF2 and 33% Nb2CO2 toward N2 and NO2, respectively, we further analyzed these two adsorption processes and the corresponding local partial density of states (LPDOS), as illustrated in Figure .

Adsorption process and LPDOS images of N2 (a, c) on 33% Nb2CF2, and NO2 (b, d) on 33% Nb2CO2, respectively, with the surface atoms marked in large size.

For the adsorption process of N2 on 33% Nb2CF2 shown in Figure (a), N2 molecules undergo a configuration transition from vertical orientation to significant tilting from pre-adsorption to post-adsorption. This transition is essentially a structural rearrangement dominated by the ionic nature of the Nb–F bond. Because of the relatively low bond energy of the Nb–F bond (compared to the Nb–O bond), the repulsive force exerted by N2 molecules on adjacent F atoms during adsorption drives F atoms to migrate to new adsorption sites, thereby exposing more Nb active sites.

Regarding the adsorption process of NO2 on 33% Nb2CO2 in Figure (b), the ultrahigh adsorption energy (25.27 eV) and complete N–O bond cleavage originate from a dual synergistic effect. The high polarity of the NO2 molecules enables their O atoms to form strong coordination bonds with Nb active sites. Meanwhile, the average bond energy of N–O bonds in NO2 (466 kJ/mol) is lower than that in NO (631 kJ/mol), providing a thermodynamic basis for bond cleavage. Second, the high electronegativity of surface -O functional groups induces a strong electrostatic attraction to the partially positively charged N atoms in NO2. This synergistic effect causes a drastic rearrangement of the electron cloud of NO2 molecules, leading to the complete cleavage of both N–O bonds.

Figure (c,d) displays the local projected density of states (LPDOS) characteristics of N atoms from N2 adsorbed on 33% Nb2CF2 and N atoms from NO2 adsorbed on 33% Nb2CO2, respectively. The core difference lies in the degree of electronic orbital overlap between N atoms and Nb atoms, which directly determines the significant difference in the adsorption energy between the two types of systems.

For the adsorption system of N2 on 33% Nb2CF2 in Figure (c), the LPDOS spectrum shows that the p-orbitals of N atoms and d-orbitals of Nb atoms only exhibit low-intensity overlap peaks in the energy around −5 eV. This feature indicates that the degree of electronic orbital overlap between them is limited, and the number of overlapping electrons is small. Although F-termination enhances the adsorption energy of Nb2C toward N2, the charge transfer quantity between them is relatively small due to the nonpolarity and inertness of N2. Ultimately, the electronic interaction between N and Nb is weak, corresponding to an adsorption energy of only −3.39 eV. In contrast, for the adsorption system of NO2 on 33% Nb2CO2 in Figure (d), the LPDOS spectrum presents a high-intensity overlap peak. The p-orbitals of N atoms, p-orbitals of O atoms, and d-orbitals of Nb atoms form a continuous and strong hybridization peak in the energy range of −7.5 ∼ −2.5 eV. This phenomenon indicates that the electronic orbital overlap region between N atoms in NO2 and Nb atoms is significantly expanded, and the number of overlapping electrons is greatly increased. The high polarity of NO2 and the high electronegativity of O functional groups produce a synergistic effect, driving the complete cleavage of N–O bonds, enabling N atoms to contact Nb active sites more fully, and further enhancing electronic orbital overlap.

The electronic orbital overlap characteristics of the LPDOS spectrum are directly positively correlated with the adsorption energy. The wider the electronic overlap region and the greater the number of overlapping electrons between N atoms and Nb atoms, the stronger the interatomic interaction and the higher the adsorption energy. This rule clearly explains why the adsorption energy of NO2 on 33% Nb2CO2 is much higher than that of N2 on 33% Nb2CF2, providing direct electronic structure evidence for the regulation of adsorption selectivity of MXene-based materials. ,

Conclusion

This study systematically investigates the adsorption behavior of N-containing gases on Nb2CT x MXenes with different functional groups (-F and -O) and coverage degrees (0%, 33%, 66%, 100%), revealing that functional group coverage is a core regulatory parameter for adsorption performance. Key findings are as follows:(1)33% coverage achieves the optimal adsorption effect for most target gases as it balances active site exposure and electron transfer efficiency. Nb2CO2 at 33% coverage exhibits the most negative adsorption energy (−25.27 eV) for NO2, accompanied by complete N–O bond cleavage, while Nb2CF2 at 33% coverage enhances N2 adsorption via Nb–F bond deformation.(2)At 100% coverage, excessive functional groups occupy Nb active sites, leading to weakened interfacial interactions and weak adsorption of most gases. Pristine Nb2C shows strong affinity for NO/NO2, while N2 and NH3 mainly undergo weak physical adsorption due to limited charge transfer.(3)The adsorption energy is negatively correlated with the adsorption distance and positively correlated with charge transfer. This work demonstrates that precise control of functional group coverage enables targeted regulation of MXene gas adsorption selectivity and strength, providing critical theoretical guidance for the design of high-performance N-containing gas-sensing and -capture materials.
