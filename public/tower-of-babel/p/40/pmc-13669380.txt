Triploidy is prominent in the duckweed Lemna minor complex
Authors: Todd P Michael, Phuong T N Hoang, Bradley W Abramson, Allen Mamerto, Buntora Pasaribu, Jörg Fuchs, Evan Ernst, Nicholas Allsing, Luca Braglia, Semar Petrus, Veit Schubert, Nolan Hartwick, Megan Wang, Yi-Feng Chen, Mariele Lensink, Tiffany Duong, Kelly Colt, Manuela Bog, K Sowjanya Sree, Laura Morello, Klaus-J Appenroth, Ingo Schubert, Robert A Martienssen, Eric Lam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuckweeds (Lemnaceae Martinov) are aquatic monocotyledonous plants known to be the smallest and fastest-growing flowering plants on Earth. Many species are morphologically indistinguishable due to their reduced structures, yet molecular evidence suggests that visually similar clones could be distinct species or hybrids. Using a comprehensive suite of genomic, molecular and cytogenetic methods, we analyzed 58 presumed Lemna minor clones to resolve their relationships. Our study revealed extensive genome plasticity within the “Lemna minor complex,” identifying diploid and triploid L. minor clones, as well as allodiploid and allotriploid interspecific hybrids, in addition to a novel African-clade L. minor lineage. Triploidy in the L. minor complex is prevalent in nature, occurring in 33% of the clones previously identified as L. minor in our collection, and is associated with enhanced growth metrics under optimal conditions. For asexually propagative species such as duckweeds, where the triploid block is not a deterrent, triploid progenies may become the majority as has been observed in numerous wild Lemna populations. Our study shows that polyploidy and hybridization in these primarily asexually propagating plants could play important roles in their population dynamics and evolution under changing environments.Subject terms: Evolutionary genetics, Genetic variation

Introduction

Duckweeds (family Lemnaceae) are rapidly gaining recognition as versatile organisms with broad applications, owing to their simple body plan, rapid growth, and adaptability to various aquatic environments1. They are emerging model plants whose small size and fast life cycles facilitate genetic and physiological research, providing insights into fundamental biological processes2,3. Their high protein content has also spurred interest in duckweed as a sustainable feed supplement and a potential food source, including for bioregenerative life-support systems in space4–6. Together with other attributes amenable for sustainable food and fuel production7–9, duckweed is a promising resource to address critical challenges in food security, environmental sustainability, and innovative plant research on Earth and beyond.

The Duckweed family comprises 35 species and three nothospecies across the five genera Spirodela, Landoltia, Lemna, Wolffiella, and Wolffia, which vary in size from about 15 mm down to less than 1 mm2. Spirodela, the earliest-diverged duckweed genus, has the largest fronds and produces 10–15 roots. In contrast, Landoltia and the single-rooted Lemna species have smaller fronds and fewer roots. Wolffia and Wolffiella represent the smallest and most recently diverging genera which lack roots altogether, while some Wolffia species can double their biomass in less than 24 h10. Although duckweeds primarily reproduce asexually by budding new fronds from progenitor fronds, most species can also reproduce sexually via flowering and seed formation under suitable conditions11,12. More recently, three nothospecies involving hybrids between species in the Lemna genus have been recognized, which demonstrated that interspecific hybrids could be prevalent in evolution of this family13–15.

Since duckweed predominantly grows by clonal propagation, and flowering as well as fertile seeds are rare for most species, they must be maintained as a living collection to facilitate researchers working on the same genetic backgrounds. Therefore, Prof. Elias Landolt established and maintained the Landolt Duckweed Collection, which was started more than 70 years ago. Many of the accessions from this collection are now housed at the Rutgers Duckweed Stock Cooperative (RDSC; http://www.ruduckweed.org/), and the National Research Council, CNR, Milan, Italy (https://biomemory.cnr.it/collections/CNR-IBBA-MIDW), together representing more than 1000 clones across all five genera and their species collected worldwide by researchers from 1953 to the present11. In our attempts to characterize this collection from the genome perspective, genome sizes were estimated from selected clones revealing an order of magnitude difference across the family from 160 megabases (Mb) in Spirodela polyrhiza clones to 2203 Mb per haploid genome (1 C) in some Wolffia arrhiza clones2,16–19. Considerable intraspecific genome size variation was also noted for some species like L. minor, which ranged from 356-604 Mb, and yet the differences did not correlate with increased ribosomal DNA or other features tested16. The problem with L. minor is that it is hard to distinguish morphologically between different accessions and closely related Lemna species20,21 (Fig. S1). Recent molecular and genomic evidence suggests that clones labeled as L. minor in the RDSC may represent different species or previously cryptic hybrids such as L. minor × L. turionifera (L. ×japonica) and L. minor × L. gibba (L. ×mediterranea)13,14,22–25. Therefore, the exact species identity for most of the L. minor clones in the collection remains ambiguous.

In this work, we systematically evaluated all 58 clones originally designated as L. minor housed at the RDSC to determine their species identity and genomic traits. Our approach integrated whole-genome sequencing (WGS), flow-cytometric genome size measurements, molecular markers, chromosome counting, and genomic in situ hybridization (GISH). We also leveraged our previous chromosome-scale genomes of L. minor, L. gibba, L. turionifera, and L. ×japonica along with short read (SR) Illumina assemblies to construct a super-pangenome and examine genome dynamics within the “Lemna minor complex”. This encompasses a clade of morphologically similar and genetically interconnected duckweed species, whose close evolutionary relationships, frequent hybrid formation, and subtle morphological differences make species delimitation challenging10,23,25–27. We clarified genome composition, ploidy levels, relatedness, and evolutionary origins of the analyzed clones, thereby explaining the observed variability in genome size and chromosome number. This new clarity in genome composition and identity provides the foundation to begin a systematic comparison between ploidy and growth-related traits using the newly defined groupings among these and future Lemna isolates.

Results

Resequencing L. minor from the RDSC duckweed collection

We analyzed all 58 clones designated as L. minor from the Rutgers Duckweed Stock Cooperative (RDSC) (http://www.ruduckweed.org/) by Illumina SR resequencing to investigate the genomic variation and potential misidentifications. As noted above, recent studies have reported that some presumed L. minor clones in the RDSC are actually hybrids, specifically L. minor × L. turionifera (L. ×japonica) and L. minor × L. gibba (L. ×mediterranea)13,14,22,25. Assuming a 1 C genome size of ~400 Mb, we targeted a median sequencing depth of 60x coverage per haploid genome (Supplementary Data S1, S2). We mapped the reads to a concatenated reference genome of L. minor (Lmin9252) and L. turionifera (Ltur9434) to detect hybrid presence as well as their genome composition25. The overall mapping rate was 98% for most of the clones except four (9425a [67%], 7264 [29%], 5635 [30%], and 8292 [11%]) (Table 1; Supplementary Data S1). Recent barcoding studies using TBP (Tubulin intron-Based Polymorphism) identified clone 9425a as L. ×mediterranea, a triploid hybrid between L. minor and L. gibba, containing one L. minor genome and two L. gibba genomes (MGG)14,28. We remapped clone 9425a to a concatenated version of the L. minor (Lmin9252) and L. gibba (Lgib7742a) genomes, which increased mapping from 67% to 98% confirming that this clone was L. ×mediterranea.

The percent of the genome covered revealed three distinct groups: 1) 53.4% (31/58) were L. minor clones, mapping with high coverage (~92%) to the L. minor genome and low mapping to L. turionifera (6 to 42%); 2) 41.4% (24/58) were L. ×japonica (L. minor × L. turionifera) hybrids, mapping with high coverage (95%) to both L. minor and L. turionifera; and 3) two clones (7264, 5635) deviated from this pattern, mapping with lower coverage to both genomes consistent with their low overall mapping rates (Table 1; Supplementary Data S1, S2). Clone 8292 with the overall mapping rate of 11%, covered the L. minor and L. turionifera genome at 91% and 6% respectively, suggesting that it was in fact L. minor; however, the low overall mapping rate was due to bacterial contamination (~88% Stenotrophomonas). Since clones 7264 and 5635 had lower overall mapping rates and low (~7%) coverage of the L. minor and L. turionifera genomes, we mapped it against other published duckweed genomes and found that both clones mapped at a high overall rate and coverage to Landoltia punctata23 (Table 1, Supplementary Data S1, S2). Our results confirm that three presumed L. minor clones in the RDSC have been misidentified, revealing a new hybrid of L. ×mediterranea (9425a) and two from the genus Landoltia (7264, 5635).

Next, we looked at coverage depth across the clones to estimate ploidy since we have shown that L. ×japonica clones consist of allodiploid (a specific type of hybrid that has one haploid genome from two distinct species, which has also been referred to as homoploids) and triploid hybrids25,29, which could explain the difference in genome size estimates. All the predicted L. minor clones mapped with a ~ 53x and ~2x coverage depth to the L. minor and L. turionifera genomes respectively, while in contrast, the L. ×japonica clones mapped at 33x and 22x coverage depth, respectively. Therefore, all clones were sequenced to depths approaching the intended 60x coverage depth, except clone 8292 that has a significant number of bacterial reads. The L. ×japonica clones further separated based on the ratio of L. minor (M) to L. turionifera (T) genome coverage depth into ratio of ~1, ~2, and ~0.5, which suggested that we have 7 MT allodiploids, 16 MMT triploids and one MTT triploid, respectively25. Therefore, the presumed L. minor clones were 53.4% L. minor, 41.4% L. ×japonica, 3.4% Landoltia punctata, and 1.7% L. ×mediterranea.

K-mer genome size and heterozygosity estimate

While we successfully clarified the species identity of the 58 presumed L. minor clones, we also sought to estimate their genome sizes. Therefore, we performed k-mer frequency-based genome size estimation using Illumina SR sequencing data. The estimated genome sizes ranged from 318 to 741 Mb (Table 1), spanning a wider range than previous flow cytometry estimates (356–604 Mb)16. The median heterozygosity rate was 0.68%, with values ranging from 0.07% to 3.05% (Table 1). The k-mer frequency profiles were grouped into three distinct patterns (Figs. 1A-C; S2): 1) single peak; 2) double peak with the first peak taller; and 3) double peak with the second peak taller. K-mer profiles with two peaks typically indicate heterozygosity or polyploidy, whereas a single peak suggests a homozygous diploid genome30. As expected, clones with heterozygosity below 0.2% consistently exhibited a single k-mer peak, while those with higher heterozygosity displayed a secondary peak or hump (Figs. 1A-C; S2). The genomes clustered into three k-mer-predicted average genomes sizes of 330, 514 and 723 Mb that matched with the L. minor diploid, L. ×japonica triploid, and L. ×japonica allodiploid respectively (Fig. 1D). Heterozygosity was almost exclusively associated with the L. minor diploid clones, whereas the L. ×japonica allodiploid exhibited a single peak and low heterozygosity, despite its k-mer-predicted genome size being double that of the L. minor and L. turionifera base genome (330 Mb) (Fig. 1D). Since sequencing coverage confirms that the allodiploid clones contain both L. minor and L. turionifera genomes, explaining the 2x genome size prediction, their extremely low heterozygosity suggests that the k-mer frequency distribution appears haploid-like. This likely results from the high genetic divergence between the parental genomes, which reduces detectable heterozygosity despite the hybrid nature of the genome. These results in conjunction with flow-cytometric genome size determination, chromosome counting and genomic in situ hybridization (see below) indicate that relying solely on k-mer analysis may lead to misinterpretation of the data.

Validating ploidy with smudgeplot

While we established the ploidy levels for all the clones based on coverage depth, we wanted to see if we could recapitulate these results using a k-mer based approach. Therefore, we leveraged the k-mer based Smudgeplot, a computational tool for ploidy inference, to estimate ploidy levels across the presumed L. minor clones31. The k-mer-based Smudgeplot estimates of ploidy generally agreed with our classifications (Supplementary Data S1; Fig. S3), but there were several important exceptions. For three of the diploid L. minor genomes Smudgeplot predicted one as tetraploid (clone 7194) and two as triploid (clones 9533 and 8866). In addition, three of the L. ×japonica triploids were predicted to be diploids by Smudgeplot (clones 9436, 9541, and 7436). However, Smudgeplot accurately predicted all of the L. ×japonica allodiploid clones as diploids (Supplementary Data S1). Smudgeplot is sensitive to sequencing coverage, and performs best above 15x sequencing depths31. At least for the three L. ×japonica triploids, the effective haplotype coverage was borderline, so it is possible that the discrepancies observed reflect issues stemming from lower than optimal sequencing depths.

Flow cytometry derived genome size estimates

Flow cytometry is a powerful tool for estimating genome size, providing rapid and quantitative measurements of cellular DNA content32. We performed flow cytometry on all of the duckweed clones studied here and compare the results to our sequencing-based genome size estimates, with additional validation by two independent laboratories that maintain these clones (Table 1; Supplementary Data S1). Despite some quantitative differences across labs (Supplementary Data S1), most estimates followed the relative genome size trends observed in the original study16. Specifically: 1) Confirmed L. minor diploids had genome sizes of ~400 Mb, consistent with the 1 C haploid base genome size; 2) L. ×japonica triploids had genome sizes 1.5× larger than diploids (510–650 Mb); 3) L. ×japonica allodiploids had genome sizes of >400 Mb, somewhat higher than L. minor diploids due to the larger genome of L. turionifera19 (Table 1). We also examined flow cytometry results for clones that were potentially misclassified by Smudgeplot. Clone 9533, initially predicted as a diploid L. minor based on k-mer and depth analysis, was identified as triploid by Smudgeplot; a finding that was confirmed by flow cytometry (641 Mb). Clone 7194, classified as a diploid L. minor by most methods, was called tetraploid by Smudgeplot but confirmed as diploid by flow cytometry (398 Mb). Two of the three L. ×japonica triploids that were incorrectly called diploid by Smudgeplot had flow cytometry estimates supporting triploidy (7436: 557 Mb; 9436: 641 Mb). Flow cytometry results differed between laboratories for clones 7018, EL022, 9437, 9440, and 8625, with discrepancies of roughly one ploidy level. Clone 7405 was consistently identified as triploid L. × japonica by all methods except flow cytometry in one laboratory, which indicated diploidy. These differences likely reflect the use of non-identical biological samples under the same clone identifiers and/or the used reference species with assumptions made about their genome sizes. Smaller discrepancies are attributable to variation in reference standards and assumed genome sizes. When observed under identical laboratory conditions, intraspecific genome size differences likely result from long-term clonal propagation. Overall, flow cytometry is a reliable method for genome size estimation when performed under equivalent conditions.

Cytogenetics validate genomics-based results

A robust approach to confirm ploidy status is direct chromosome counting33. We selected a subset of clones for chromosomal visualization to validate our genome size and sequencing-based predictions (Fig. 2). As expected, clones 7210 and 7753, representing both homozygous and heterozygous L. minor diploids, had 42 chromosomes (2n = 2x = 42). However, clone 9533, which appeared diploid based on read depth and k-mer analysis but was classified as triploid by flow cytometry and Smudgeplot, was confirmed to have 63 chromosomes (2n = 3x = 63) and provided an example of a triploid L. minor clone. Furthermore, clones 8625, 7123, and 8434, all identified as L. ×japonica allodiploids, consistently exhibited 42 chromosomes (2n = 2x = 42), confirming their allodiploid status.

Genome assembly to clarify species assignment

De novo genome assembly using Illumina SR typically results in modest-sized contigs, yet it effectively captures gene space and provides valuable insights into genome relationships30. Therefore, we de novo assembled the Illumina SRs and found the genomes ranged in quality with N50 lengths spanning from 3 to 21 Kb, which is consistent with assemblies from short reads (Table 1; Supplementary Data S1). The genomes grouped into two size classes at 300–400 Mb and 600 Mb that matched with L. minor diploid and L. ×japonica allodiploid/triploid respectively (Fig. 1E). Among the predicted diploid L. minor clones, five individuals (9533, 8211, 7650, 8866, and 7753) had relatively larger assembled genome sizes (400 Mb) and were also predicted to have the highest heterozygosity (1.5–3.1%). While clone 9533 was subsequently proven to be an autotriploid L. minor as described above, the larger assembled genome sizes in the other heterozygous diploids can be attributed to the incomplete collapse of haplotypes, a common issue in SR assemblies. When heterozygosity is high, haplotypes contain sufficient variation to be assembled as separate contigs, leading to inflated genome size estimates. This pattern is further supported by a strong negative correlation (r = −0.8) between N50 contig length and heterozygosity (Fig. S4), indicating that higher heterozygosity results in shorter contigs and larger total assembly sizes due to partial haplotype duplication. The L. ×japonica allodiploid/triploid assemblies were approximately twice the size of the diploid L. minor genomes, consistent with separate assembly of both subgenomes. This interpretation is reinforced by mapping these assemblies back to the L. ×japonica 8627 reference genome25, which revealed a clear separation of subgenomic sequences (Fig. S5). The genome assemblies estimated that the base L. minor genome size was ~350 Mb in contrast to the value estimated by flow cytometry (~400 Mb), which could reflect the fact that SR assembly does not generally assemble repeat sequences like centromere and transposable elements well. The larger variation observed previously by flow cytometry16 was most likely due to L. ×japonica triploids.

Reference-free super-pangenome

A super-pangenome extends the traditional pangenome concept by incorporating multiple species within a genus or across related taxa, rather than being confined to a single species34. We leveraged the highest-quality 38 SR de novo genome assemblies in our dataset to construct a reference-free super-pangenome (Fig. 3); we also leveraged published chromosome-scale genomes to place the super-pangenome in the context of L. ×japonica, L. turionifera as well as published genomes of other duckweed genera10,23,25–27 (Fig. S6). Using PanKmer35, we built a shared k-mer matrix to assess genome-wide relationships across the diploid L. minor and L. ×japonica allodiploid and triploid clones (Fig. 3). The reference-free super-pangenome matrix revealed three primary clusters, with a clear outlier grouping of Landoltia punctata (clones 5635 and 7264). Additionally, L. ×mediterranea (clone 9425a) branched separately with the known diploid L. minor clone Lmin550017, supporting its distinct hybrid status. A particularly useful validation came from clones DWC123 and 7283, which were descendants of the same clone independently maintained by two stock centers for over at least two decades. Their close clustering in the super-pangenome matrix confirms the reliability of our approach in capturing genomic relationships.

The three main clusters in the super-pangenome included two on either side of the matrix that contained the diploid L. minor clones, while the third cluster in the middle contained the L. ×japonica allodiploids and triploids (Fig. 3). The top left L. minor diploid cluster included the published Lmin5500 clone17, which suggested that the bottom right L. minor diploid cluster might represent a distinct sub-class. Moreover, the top left L. minor diploid cluster shared more k-mers with the L. ×japonica allodiploid/triploid cluster than the other diploid cluster; this indicates that the diploid clusters may represent different clades as well as suggesting that the L. minor diploids from the top left cluster were the sub-class that contributed to the L. ×japonica hybrids. When we included all published duckweed chromosome-scale genomes in the super-pangenome, the L. ×japonica allodiploid/triploid cluster further supported the hybrid nature with L. turionifera and suggested that the L. ×mediterranea hybrid 9245a may have shared k-mers with clone 7210 from the L. minor African-clade (Figure S6). One potential explanation is that the L. minor parental genome contributing to the L. ×mediterranea hybrid has common ancestry with the 7210 African lineage. However, currently, there is insufficient information to resolve the relationship between L. × mediterranea and clone 7210. In any case, our approach enabled a reference-free comparison of genome structure and hybridization patterns, which enhanced our ability to differentiate subclasses within L. minor, track hybrid origins, and explore genome evolution at a broader taxonomic scale. These findings demonstrate that super-pangenome matrices provide a powerful genome-wide approach for rapidly assessing relationships between clonal populations within a species and even across genera.

Tubulin intron-based polymorphism (TBP) validation

TBP calling is another approach that has been utilized to clarify the relationship between Lemna species as well as to identify L. ×japonica as a hybrid derived from L. minor and L. turionifera13,20,22. All the presumed L. minor clones (except the Landoltia and L. ×mediterranea clones) were validated using the TBP marker; both cluster as well as PCA analyses were consistent with three groupings like the super-pangenome matrix (Fig. 4; Supplementary Data S1). The top left cluster from the super-pangenome matrix overlapped with the true L. minor diploid clones that are similar to Lmin5500 (L. minor 5500-like; Fig. 4). The center super-pangenome cluster overlapped with the L. ×japonica cluster as predicted. TBP analysis suggested clone 7868 was L. ×japonica triploid, with likely MMT composition, consistent with the flow cytometry-based genome size of 644 Mb (Table 1). However, mapping of the SR sequence reads indicated a mix up of clones was likely to have taken place in the sequencing stage. The third TBP cluster that was distant compared to the other two clusters (L. minor African-clade), overlapped with the lower right cluster of L. minor diploids in the super-pangenome analysis, which we also found to be more distant in the k-mer matrix. All these clones come from Africa or several Middle Eastern countries, such as Iran and Turkey. Therefore, we have termed this subclass the “African-clade”. Considering the apparent diversity of this cluster in the super-pangenome matrix, the “African-clade” could represent a different species or subspecies, or even further hybrids, but distinct from the recently identified L. ×mediterranea clones14,28.

Confirmation of the hybrids by genomic in situ hybridization (GISH)

To confirm the ploidy status and genome components of L. ×japonica triploids and allodiploids, we performed GISH studies for select clones. Using species-derived probes generated from L. minor (M) and L. turionifera (T) genomes, we visualized the sub-genomes at the chromosome level (Fig. 5). For the L. ×japonica allodiploid clone 8434 (Ljap8434), GISH confirmed a diploid chromosome number (Figs. 2D; 5A), reinforcing its classification. Clone 9533, originally identified as diploid based on k-mer analysis and read depth, was later predicted as triploid by Smudgeplot and flow cytometry. Our GISH analysis validated the triploid classification (2n = 3x = 63; 641 Mb/1 C) by demonstrating that all chromosomes were predominantly labeled by L. minor DNA (Fig. 5B). This confirms that 9533 is a L. minor autotriploid (see also Fig. 4), not a hybrid, explaining why it initially clustered with diploid clones. Notably, 9533 and 8292 are the only confirmed L. minor triploids in our dataset. Additionally, GISH confirmed that clone 8627 is a L. ×japonica triploid (2n = 3x = 63)25, further supporting our ploidy classifications. These results reinforce the importance of combining sequencing-based and cytogenetic methods to accurately classify ploidy and genome composition, particularly in hybrid species.

Summary of presumed L. minor clone identity

Integrating results from multiple orthogonal approaches and by independent laboratories, we reclassified the 58 presumed L. minor clones based on their species identity and ploidy level (Table 1; Supplementary Data S1). We found that 53% were L. minor clones that separated into two sub-classes (5500-like and 7210-like), and that clones 9533 and 8292 were the only two L. minor triploids in this collection studied. However, 47% of the collection was mis-called. Most of them were reassigned to L. ×japonica allodiploid (12%), and allotriploid (29%) hybrids that have derived from hybridization with L. turionifera (Table 1; Supplementary Data S1). We only found one clone (7425a) in this collection (1.7%) that was a hybrid between L. minor and L. gibba (L. ×mediterranea). Finally, we identified two clones (3.4%) that were from the monospecific genus Landoltia, suggesting that confusion in assigning clone identity across the Lemna genus extends to other genera. These findings underscore the importance of integrating multiple genomic and cytogenetic tools for accurate species and ploidy classification, as misidentifications remain common in collections of morphologically similar duckweed species.

Polyploids may provide growth advantage in some environments but not in others

Polyploidy has been hypothesized to enhance vigor and environmental adaptability, whereas diploids are often thought to be specialized for more narrow ecological niches36. In Lemna, both interspecific and intraspecific hybrids may benefit from increased growth rate and environmental flexibility due to heterosis and genomic buffering37. We measured growth parameters for 20 selected clones that have been genomically resolved in this study, which included diploids and polyploids from the L. minor complex: relative growth rate (RGR), doubling time (DT), relative yield (RY), and mean frond area (MA)38. Both L. ×japonica allodiploid and triploid hybrid clones exhibited superior growth performance across most of these traits when compared with the diploids. Allodiploids outperformed diploids with reasonable significance (p-values of <0.5) in DT, RY, and RGR, but showed the smallest effect on MA (Table 2; Supplementary Data S3, S5). Although the sample number is the smallest for the L. minor triploids (N = 2), since these are the only two of this genotype in our collection, the triploid L. minor clones (9533 and 8292) displayed the largest mean area (MA, P < 0.05) among all classes. The L. ×japonica triploids (N = 3) comes in a close second for largest average MA and together with the L. minor triploids provided a stronger case (N = 5) that a size advantage in MA is associated with triploidy in comparison to diploids (N = 15). In contrast, diploid L. minor clones were the most variable and exhibited lower overall vigor, reinforcing the hypothesis that polyploidy and hybridization can contribute to greater robustness and growth potential.

Polyploids have also been hypothesized to exhibit greater vigor and adaptability across a wider range of environmental conditions39. In a second independent exploratory experiment we examined this in the L. minor complex by measuring relative growth rate (RGR), relative yield (RY), and doubling time (DT) for one representative clone from each class (L. minor diploid, L. ×japonica allodiploid, and L. ×japonica triploid) under five different light and temperature conditions at 7 and 14 days (Figure S7; Supplementary Data S4, S5). Growth was assessed at two time points since earlier growth rates tend to be faster and more linear with less plant crowding and higher nutrient availability. Significant differences (P < 0.01) were observed for both conditions and clone effects on RGR and RY, while DT showed no significant associations (Figure S7; Supplementary Data S4, S5). Under control conditions, L. ×japonica allodiploid and triploid grew 24% and 35% faster than the L. minor diploid. A larger growth advantage emerged under low-light conditions, where the hybrids exhibited 81% (allodiploid) and 51% (triploid) higher growth rates compared to the diploid. Importantly, under higher temperature or high light stresses, the allodiploid and triploid hybrids both showed less growth compared to the diploid L. minor plants. These findings suggest that while polyploidy or hybridization may confer a growth advantage under specific environmental conditions, particularly low-light stress, they could be less tolerant to more extreme abiotic stressors such as high temperature or excess light. However, due to the small number of sample size in this exploratory study, future studies with larger numbers of these genotypes from our collection of genome-resolved clones from the L. minor complex would be necessary to confirm and extend these initial observations.

Discussion

Lemna minor, also known as Common Duckweed, is widely regarded as the most abundant and broadly distributed duckweed species on Earth11. In this work, we leveraged a multi-pronged approach with several orthogonal methods to resolve the species and ploidies of 58 previously presumed L. minor clones from the RDSC collection (see chart in Figure S8 for a summary of our work-flow). Our results re-classified these L. minor clones into: 1) two classes of diploid L. minor clones that includes a grouping we propose to designate as the African-clade (L. minor A); 2) allodiploid and triploid L. ×japonica (L. minor × L. turionifera) hybrids; 3) one triploid L. minor × L. gibba (L. ×mediterranea); and 4) two clones from the Landoltia (7264, 5635) genus. In addition, WGS revealed two L. minor autotriploid clones (8292, 9533), one of which was validated by GISH and TBP (9533) (Figs. 4 and 5) and the other (8292) by TBP. Moreover, now that we could clearly group the clones by species and ploidy, an initial study showed that the L. minor related triploids and hybrids could display growth advantages over the L. minor diploids under ambient conditions but not under some abiotic stressors such as excess light or high temperature. These results set the precedent for proper characterization and assignment of future clones added to the collection as well as providing a well-curated collection to enable more in-depth research to determine how polyploidy may impact duckweed growth and population dynamics.

Our analysis revealed some discrepancies between clones from two different laboratories at the U.S. (New Jersey) and Italy (Milan), a phenomenon also observed in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana. Notably, even the widely used reference line Columbia-0 (Col-0) has been shown to accumulate distinct genomic variations when maintained in separate labs40. For instance, we found that flow-cytometry results differed by lab for five clones (7018, EL022, 9437, 9440, and 8635), most likely reflecting either experimental mislabeling or the same accession number representing different biological samples (Supplementary Data S1). However, the two clones (DWC123 [UTCC270], and 7283) that were separated by at least two decades in two different collections, but originated from the Landolt Duckweed Collection, were exactly the same (Table 1; Fig. 3). These results highlight that in order to get reliable results, multiple approaches including direct ones (e.g. GS and GISH) in addition to probabilistic ones (e.g. k-mer based methods), are essential, in particular when looking at species complexes like L. minor where polyploidy and interspecific hybrids appear to be rather common.

For duckweeds, a puzzling phenomenon remains: triploids and interspecific hybrids, both of which arise only through sexual reproduction, appear to occur relatively frequently in populations that predominantly propagate asexually14,15,22. Polyploidy predominantly arises either by fusion of unreduced gametes or after spontaneous chromosome doubling, resulting in allopolyploids if allodiploid hybrids were involved41. Triploids appear after fusion of a reduced with an unreduced gamete and can be maintained only by asexual propagation, because irregular meiotic chromosome segregation does not yield viable gametes, as it was recently confirmed in Lemna hybrids29. The apparent loss of genes involved in the small RNA-mediated triploid block pathway as well as of genes that suppress the formation of unreduced gametes seem to enable the frequent occurrence of stable triploid populations in Lemna25. Moreover, the predominantly asexual propagation habit of duckweeds would be consistent with the avid viability of triploids arising in duckweeds. We found that 41% (24/58) of the previously curated L. minor strains (based predominantly on morphological basis) in the RDSC collection were a mixture of L. ×japonica with 71% (17/24) of those being triploid and 29% (7/24) allodiploids (Table 1; Supplementary Data S1). A similar situation was found for presumed wild L. minor clones collected across natural populations in Switzerland where over 50% were later identified as L. ×japonica triploids by WGS24. Their low genetic variability indicated that triploids may emerge, clonally multiply and exploit specific environments. Similarly, among wild presumed L. minor clones from Eastern Europe, molecular markers were used to show that L. ×japonica was prominent20,21. Thus, sexual hybrids including triploids are more common than previously thought in nature, suggesting that triploid L. ×japonica may confer some level of growth advantage over its diploid parental clones in particular habitats. While our data from our growth experiments in this work appears to confirm this hypothesis, more in-depth analysis with larger number of isolates would be necessary to strengthen these preliminary results and conjectures (Table 2; Fig. S7).

Duckweeds exhibit a wide range of genetic diversity and reproductive strategies that influence their adaptability to different environments. Some species, such as Spirodela polyrhiza, maintain large population sizes but low sequence variations42, which suggests that even minor genetic advantages may enhance clonal propagation and facilitate the colonization of new micro-environments. In contrast, some wild populations of presumed L. minor have been found to harbor 4 to 20 distinct genotypes per site, reflecting extensive genetic diversity that could aid in local adaptation43. Supporting this, L. minor exhibits twice as much genetic variation in fitness per site compared to other species, consistent with strong purifying selection acting to counteract environmental fluctuations44. While hybrids (allodiploids and triploids) were not explicitly resolved in these previous studies, their presence may contribute to local adaptation and population dynamics. Our initial findings align with this framework, as hybrid and polyploid genotypes outperformed diploids under ambient conditions but could have reduced performance under certain abiotic stressors such as higher temperatures or excess light (Fig. S7; Table 2; Supplementary Data S3-5). Although the growth advantage of triploids appears modest in our study (yet significant), a 5% increase in relative growth rate (RGR) may allow a particular clone to rapidly outcompete and dominate a population over time. In this model, even a small number of diploid or triploid clones that persist under different conditions could act as a genetic reservoir to revert the population to its more advantageous genotype upon significant change in the environment to outcompete other macrophytes.

In conclusion, our comprehensive characterization of presumed L. minor clones from the RDSC collection underscores that these clones form a “L. minor complex” that includes L. minor diploids and autotriploids, as well as allodiploids and reciprocal triploids from interspecific hybrids with either L. turionifera or L. gibba species14,25. Rather than a uniform species, the L. minor complex is thus far more genetically diverse and dynamic than previously recognized and its naming would be consistent with previously suggested treatment for populations that combine unisexual reproduction, interspecific hybrids and ploidy variants to generate diversity45. Similarly, a “Lemna aequinoctialis complex” has recently been described15, suggesting that the Lemna genus may exploit its environment through various interspecific hybrids. Thus, sexuality plays a more pivotal role in duckweed evolution than once assumed. These findings provide clues to how polyploidy and hybridization may promote ecological success and genetic diversity in duckweeds that predominantly grow by asexual propagation. They also created a knowledge base and genetic resource for future work by the community to more systematically characterize their evolutionary dynamics, taxonomy, and facilitate biotechnological application of duckweeds.

Methods

Duckweed clones

Lemna minor clones were received from the Rutgers Duckweed Stock Collective (RDSC; http://www.ruduckweed.org/). Clones were checked for sterile conditions and if they were not sterile they were re-sterilized per the RDSC protocol. 58 clones were grown under 12 h light and 12 h dark at 22 °C. The collection location and all associated information are reported in Supplementary Data S1.

DNA extraction for genome sequencing

Clones were collected, patted dry to remove excess water and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. Frozen tissue was ground in a mortar and pestle under liquid nitrogen. HMW DNA was isolated using a modified BOMB protocol (#6.4; https://bomb.bio/protocols/). DNA quality and quantity were assessed on a bioanalyzer and HMW status was confirmed on an agarose gel.

Genome sequencing

Illumina 2 × 150 bp paired end reads were generated for genome size estimates, short-read genome assembly and polishing genome long read assemblies. Libraries were prepared from HMW DNA using NEBnext (NEB, Beverly, MA) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq (San Diego, CA). Resulting raw sequence was only trimmed for adaptors, resulting >60X coverage of the diploid L. minor genome (350 Mb).

Variation estimation by read mapping

Illumina paired end reads were mapped to the L. minor diploid reference genome (Lmin5500)17, as well as the updated L. minor 9252 and L. turionifera 9434 genomes25 using minimap246. Clone 9425a was also mapped to a concatenated version of L. minor 9252 and L. gibba 7742a. Mapping rates were summarized in Supplementary Data S1, S2. Resulting sorted bam files were analyzed for mapping statistics using samtools47 and variants were called using samtools mpileup48 and vcftools49.

Genome size estimation by k-mer frequency

K-mer (k = 31) frequency was estimated with Illumina paired end reads (2 × 150 bp) using Jellyfish (v2.3.0)50 and analyzed using in house scripts and GenomeScope and GenomeScope231,51. Genome size, heterozygosity and repeat content were first estimated with GenomeScope51, which reports haploid genome size (Table 1; Supplementary Data S1). Ploidy was estimated using GenomeScope2 and smudgeplot31 (Table 1; Supplementary Data S1).

Short read genome assembly

Illumina paired end reads were assembled with Spades (v3.14.0) with standard settings52. Resulting contigs were mapped to the L. minor reference genome (Lmin5500) to estimate completeness and statistics were determined (Table 1; Supplementary Data S1).

Reference-free pangenome (PanKmer)

The short read-based genome assemblies were used to construct a pangenome using PanKmer35. Briefly, a 31-mer index was constructed using PanKmer35 with default parameters using the short read Illumina assemblies. Jaccard and ANI similarities for all assemblies in both indexes were calculated and visualized using PanKmer by setting the clustermap metric parameter to “--metric jaccard” and “--metric ani”, respectively.

Flow cytometry (FC) genome size estimates

At Rutgers

The methods used to prepare the nuclei suspensions were based on the protocol for estimation of nuclear DNA content in plants using flow cytometry53. Sixteen clones of L. minor and four additional plant species of known ploidy, Spirodela polyrhiza (158 Mb), Arabidopsis thaliana (134 Mb), Brachypodium distachyon (272 Mb), and Fragaria x ananassa (800 Mb), were analyzed. These species, along with two of the L. minor strains known to be diploid, were used as standards to aid in ploidy estimation of the remaining fourteen L. minor varieties. For each strain and standard, 120 mg of fresh tissue was harvested and placed in a petri dish with 4 mL of Galbraith’s buffer, a solution consisting of 45 mM MgCl2, 20 mM MOPS, 30 mM sodium citrate, and 0.1% (vol/vol) Triton X-100. The tissue was gently chopped into small pieces with a disposable razor blade to release nuclei. After the homogenate was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer, the filtrate was evenly distributed into four microcentrifuge tubes. Propidium iodide was used to stain the DNA; 50 μL each of 1 mg/mL PI and 1 mg/mL RNase A were added to each of three tubes, the fourth left unstained to act as the control. The samples were incubated on ice for about 15 min and gently shaken periodically before analysis through the flow cytometer (BD/Accuri C6+ Flow Cytometer). The flow cytometer was set to collect data on 10,000 events, with the minimum core size and flow rate due to the small size of the nuclei. The selected threshold was 80,000.

At the IPK

The genome size of Lemna clones was determined following the procedure according to Dolezel et al.53 using the CyStain PI Absolute P reagent kit (Sysmex-Partec) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fresh frond tissue was co-chopped with fresh leaf tissue of one of the internal reference standards, either Raphanus sativus ‘Voran’ (Gatersleben Gene bank accession: RA 34; 1.11 pg/2 C) or Lycopersicon esculentum ‘Stupicke Rane’ (Gatersleben Gene bank accession: LYC 418; 1.96 pg/2 C). The nuclei suspensions were measured either on a CyFlow Space flow cytometer (Sysmex-Partec) or on a BD Influx cell sorter (BD Biosciences). An example for the gating strategy used is shown on Fig. S9. Each clone was measured at least four times on two different days.

Tubulin intron-based polymorphism (TBP) analysis

The TBP amplification protocol and data analysis were performed, from 30 ng of total genomic DNA, according to the procedure described54. The polymorphisms in length of both intron regions (I and II) of the ß- tubulin genes were scrutinized, and each DNA sample was independently analyzed twice.

Growth rate and frond area estimation

For the experiments presented in Table 2 and Supplementary Data S3, clones for relative growth rate (RGR; day−1), doubling time (DT; day), relative weekly yield (RY; week−1), and mean area (MA; cm2) estimation were acclimatized by pre-cultivation for four weeks under axenic conditions in a climate cabinet (PK-520, poly klima GmbH, Freising, Germany) under continuous white light (100 µmol m-2 s-1 PAR) and constant temperature of 25 °C ± 1 °C. The cultivation medium (N medium) was changed weekly to avoid nutrient deficiency55. For the main experiment, a colony consisting of three to four fronds was placed in a plastic cup containing 40 ml of N medium. After seven days, the number of fronds was recorded again and the relative growth rate was calculated as RGR = (ln xt7 – ln xt0) / (t7 – t0)38. The frond area was measured as the mean value of three single fronds per clone in six replicates using a digital microscope (VFX 7000, Keyence, Neu-Isenburg, Germany).

Duckweed growth rate estimation under differential conditions

To compare potential impact of ploidy and hybridization on stress response (Fig. S7 and Tables S4, 5), L. minor clones were obtained from RDSC (Rutgers Duckweed Stock Collection) at Rutgers the State University of New Jersey, New Brunswick, NJ, USA. The clones were maintained on 0.5X Schenk-Hildebrand medium with 0.8% w/v agar and 0.3% sucrose with the addition of 100 mg/L cefotaxime. The clones were kept at 25 °C under illumination of 150 μmol m−2s−1 light (16 h light/8 h dark) using a Caron PM-7304-50 growth chamber. Five frond colonies of L. minor clones from stock cultures were grown for 2 weeks in a solution containing 0.5X Schenk-Hildebrand medium and 0.3% sucrose. For growth measurement, ~100 mg fresh fronds of each clone were then transferred to a 177 ml glass jar containing 50 ml of fresh 0.5X Schenk-Hildebrand medium and 0.3% sucrose solution. The experiment was carried out in 3 replicates with samples collected at days 7 and 14. Tissues of L. minor clones were harvested and dried overnight at 55 °C in 1.5 ml Eppendorf tubes and the growth rates measured (Relative Growth Rate [RGR]; day−1), Doubling time (DT; day), Relative yields (RY; week−1) according to procedures as described38. For experiments under “control” conditions, the L. minor clones were maintained at 25 °C under illumination of 150 μmol m−2s−1 light (16 h light/8 h dark). Otherwise, temperature and light intensity were changed as described for the stressor experiments to examine effects by shifts in these environmental parameters.

Trait mean value analysis

A one-way analysis of variance using Welch’s ANOVA test was performed to test whether ploidy had a significant effect on each measured trait. Following significant ANOVA results, Games-Howell post-hoc tests were conducted to identify pairwise differences between ploidy groups. Statistical analyses were performed in Python using the pingouin (0.5.5) library.

Treatment analysis

A two-way analysis of variance using ANOVA test was performed to test whether ploidy and experimental treatment had a significant effect on each measured trait. Post-hoc tests using Tukey’s HSD were conducted to identify specific differences between ploidy groups. Statistical analyses were performed in Python using the pingouin (0.5.5) and statsmodels (0.14.2) libraries.

Chromosome counting

The fronds were grown in liquid nutrient medium under 16 h white light of 100 µmol m−2 s−1 at 24 °C55. The mitotic chromosome spreading was carried out as described56. Fronds were treated in 2 mM 8-hydroxylquinoline, fixed in fresh 3:1 absolute ethanol: acetic acid, softened in PC enzyme mixture [1% pectinase and 1% cellulase in Na-citrate buffer, pH 4.6], macerated and squashed in 45% acetic acid. After freezing in liquid nitrogen, chromosome spreads were treated with pepsin [50 µg/ml in 0.01 N HCl], post-fixed in 4% formaldehyde in 2x SSC [300 mM Na-citrate, 30 mM NaCl, pH 7.0], rinsed twice in 2x SSC, 5 min each, dehydrated in an ethanol series (70, 90 and 96%, 2 min each) and air-dried. Chromosome counting was performed within different slices of 3D-SIM image stacks19.

DNA extraction for genomic in situ hybridization

For each sample, 0.3 g of fresh and healthy fronds were harvested and cleaned in distilled water, put into a 2 ml Eppendorf tube with two metal balls, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and ground by a ball mill mixer (Retsch MM400). The genomic DNA of the studied species was isolated using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (cat. nos. 69104-Qiagen). DNA was eluted by 200 µl buffer AE and quality checked by electrophoresis. Genomic DNA was sonicated before labelling.

Probe preparation

Sonicated genomic DNA (1 µg) was labeled with Cy3-dUTP (GE Healthcare Life Science) or Alexa Fluor 488-5-dUTP (Life Technologies) by nick-translation, then precipitated in ethanol57 with sonicated unlabeled DNA of the other presumed parental species as carrier DNA in excess. Probe pellets from 10 µL nick translation product for GISH probes were dissolved in 100 µL hybridization buffer [50% (v/v) formamide, 20% (w/v) dextran sulfate in 2× SSC, pH 7] at 37 °C for at least 1 h. The ready-to-use probes were stored at −20 °C.

GISH (Genomic in situ hybridization)

Probes were pre-denatured at 95 °C for 5 min and chilled on ice for 10 min before adding 20 µL probe per slide. Two-rounds of GISH with alternatively labeled genomic probes of the presumed parental species were performed to investigate the distribution of the corresponding probe signals on the chromosome complement of the tested clones as described19.

Super-resolution microscopy

To analyze the ultrastructure and spatial arrangement of signals and chromatin at a lateral resolution of ~120 nm (super-resolution, achieved with a 488 nm laser), spatial structured illumination microscopy (3D-SIM) was applied using a Plan-Apochromat 63x/1.4 oil objective of an Elyra PS.1 microscope system and the software ZENblack (Carl Zeiss GmbH)58.
