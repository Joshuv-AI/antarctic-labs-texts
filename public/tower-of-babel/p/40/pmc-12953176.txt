Aggressive Prostatic Stromal Sarcoma Mimicking Benign Prostatic Enlargement in a Middle‐Aged Man: A Case Report
Authors: Farid Saleh, Esther Lekei, Zerra Israel Cheyo, Ramadhan Faki, Godwin Nnko, Glory Makupa, Aisha Mohamed, Frank Bright, Alex Mremi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTProstatic stromal sarcoma is an exceptionally rare mesenchymal malignancy with aggressive behavior and poor prognosis. It often presents with nonspecific lower urinary tract symptoms, frequently mimicking benign prostatic hyperplasia. We report a 51‐year‐old man who presented with hematuria and long‐standing obstructive urinary symptoms. Imaging revealed a massively enlarged prostate with a low prostate‐specific antigen level. The findings strongly favored benign prostatic enlargement. Given the severity of bladder outlet obstruction and absence of overt radiologic malignancy, open retropubic prostatectomy was performed for presumed benign disease. Histopathological examination unexpectedly revealed prostatic stromal sarcoma, with immunohistochemistry negative for epithelial, lymphoid, and common stromal markers. Postoperative staging performed following histologic diagnosis demonstrated extensive locally advanced pelvic disease and distant pulmonary metastases within weeks of surgery. Despite surgical intervention, the patient rapidly developed locally advanced pelvic disease with pulmonary metastases and succumbed to the illness before initiation of palliative chemotherapy. This case underscores the diagnostic challenges, aggressive natural history, and limited therapeutic options for prostatic stromal sarcoma, particularly in low‐resource settings, and emphasizes the need for heightened clinical suspicion and early multidisciplinary involvement when intraoperative findings are encountered.

Introduction

Prostatic stromal sarcoma (PSS) is an extremely rare malignant mesenchymal tumor of the prostate, accounting for less than 0.1% of all prostatic malignancies [1]. Originating from specialized hormone‐dependent prostatic stroma, PSS is characterized by aggressive local invasion, early metastasis, and poor clinical outcomes [1, 2]. Clinically, it often presents with nonspecific lower urinary tract symptoms indistinguishable from benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), while prostate‐specific antigen levels are typically normal [3]. As a result, diagnosis is frequently delayed until histopathological evaluation following surgical intervention. Due to its rarity, standardized treatment guidelines are lacking, and outcomes remain unfavorable, particularly in resource‐limited settings [1, 4]. High‐quality case documentation with clear diagnostic reasoning, operative findings, and histopathological correlation is essential to improve recognition and management of this rare entity. We report a fatal case of PSS in a middle‐aged man initially managed as benign prostatic enlargement, highlighting diagnostic pitfalls and the aggressive disease course.

Case History

A 51‐year‐old man presented with painless gross hematuria of 2 weeks' duration, associated with passage of clots. He also reported a two‐year history of progressive lower urinary tract symptoms, including urinary frequency, nocturia, urgency, weak stream, and sensation of incomplete bladder emptying. He noted a painless suprapubic swelling that reduced in size after voiding. There was no history of weight loss, fever, back pain, neurological deficits, tobacco use, or occupational toxin exposure. He had a history of recurrent urinary tract infections.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

Initial Clinical Assessment (Month 0)

On examination, the patient was slightly wasted but clinically stable. Abdominal examination revealed suprapubic distension, which was soft with a full, non‐tender percussion note. Digital rectal examination demonstrated a markedly enlarged, firm, symmetrical prostate with preserved median sulcus, findings considered more consistent with benign prostatic enlargement than carcinoma. Laboratory investigations showed hemoglobin of 12.2 g/dL, normal renal function, and a prostate‐specific antigen level of 1.33 ng/mL.

Radiologic Evaluation and Diagnostic Rationale (Month 0)

Ultrasound revealed a massively enlarged prostate measuring 261 cc with intravesical protrusion, a distended bladder with significant post‐void residual urine, and left‐sided hydronephrosis. The combination of severe bladder outlet obstruction, a markedly enlarged but smooth prostate on digital rectal examination, and a low PSA level favored a working diagnosis of benign prostatic enlargement rather than primary prostatic malignancy. Cross‐sectional imaging was not initially performed because clinical and sonographic findings did not suggest locally invasive or metastatic disease at presentation, and the patient required urgent relief of obstruction.

Operative Intervention (Week 2)

A preoperative diagnosis of bladder outlet obstruction secondary to benign prostatic enlargement was made. The patient underwent urethrocystoscopy and retropubic prostatectomy. Intraoperatively, the prostate was noted to be markedly enlarged, irregular, and unusually friable, with areas of hemorrhage and necrosis—features atypical for benign prostatic hyperplasia but not definitively diagnostic of malignancy. Highly irregular and fragile prostatic mass weighing approximately 450 g was enucleated. The specimen was submitted for histopathological examination.

Gross Pathology Findings

Gross examination of the specimen obtained at retropubic prostatectomy showed a markedly enlarged, irregular, and friable prostatic mass, removed in a piecemeal fashion, measuring approximately 450 g, with loss of normal lobulated architecture. Cut sections revealed fleshy tan‐white areas with focal hemorrhage and necrosis.

Conclusions and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Histopathologic Diagnosis (Week 3 Postoperatively)

Postoperatively, the patient initially recovered well; however, he later developed recurrent lower urinary tract symptoms, suprapubic distension (Figure 1A,B), and intermittent hematuria. Microscopy demonstrated a highly cellular spindle‐cell neoplasm with marked nuclear atypia, brisk mitotic activity, and areas of tumor necrosis infiltrating prostatic stromal tissue. The morphological features were consistent with high‐grade PSS (Figure 2A,B). Immunohistochemistry showed positivity for vimentin and CD34 and negativity for S100, CD117, cytokeratin, SMA, and CD45, supporting a diagnosis of high‐grade PSS (Figure 3A–D).

Staging and Disease Progression (Week 4–6 Postoperatively)

Subsequent contrast‐enhanced CT imaging (Figures 4 and 5) demonstrated a large infiltrative pelvic mass of 10.8 × 9.3 × 8.2 cm in dimension involving the bladder wall, bilateral hydroureteronephrosis, extensive pelvic and para‐aortic lymphadenopathy, pulmonary metastases, and mild ascites. These findings indicated either rapid postoperative progression or previously occult metastatic disease not detected on initial evaluation.

Multidisciplinary Management and Outcome (Week 6–10)

The case was discussed at a multidisciplinary tumor board, where palliative chemotherapy was recommended, with renal‐friendly options including single‐agent doxorubicin or a combination of gemcitabine and docetaxel. Radiotherapy was considered for local symptom control but deferred due to rapid clinical decline and extensive metastatic burden. Unfortunately, the patient deteriorated rapidly and died before systemic therapy could be initiated, about 4 weeks after surgery.

Discussion

Prostatic stromal sarcoma (PSS) is a highly aggressive neoplasm arising from specialized prostatic stroma and represents the malignant end of the spectrum of stromal tumors, distinct from stromal tumors of uncertain malignant potential (STUMP) [5]. Patients typically present at a younger age compared with adenocarcinoma and often exhibit severe obstructive urinary symptoms with normal PSA levels, as observed in this case [6, 7].

Preoperative diagnosis is challenging due to nonspecific clinical and radiologic features, frequently leading to misdiagnosis as BPH [8]. In the present case, the diagnostic pathway was driven by severe bladder outlet obstruction requiring urgent surgical relief, low PSA level, and absence of overt radiologic features of malignancy on ultrasound. These factors collectively reduced clinical suspicion for sarcoma and justified initial management as benign disease. Histopathology remains the cornerstone of diagnosis, with immunohistochemistry aiding exclusion of epithelial, lymphoid, and other mesenchymal tumors. The tumor in this case demonstrated vimentin and CD34 positivity with the absence of epithelial and smooth muscle markers, supporting a high‐grade stromal sarcoma phenotype [8].

PSS is characterized by rapid local progression and early distant metastasis, most commonly to the lungs, lymph nodes, and bones [9, 10, 11]. Complete surgical resection offers the best chance for disease control; however, even with surgery, prognosis remains poor, as evidenced in our patient [3]. The rapid emergence of extensive pelvic recurrence and pulmonary metastases within weeks of surgery underscores the aggressive biology of high‐grade PSS and the likelihood that micrometastatic disease was already present at initial presentation. PSS can be managed by robotic procedure; however, surgery alone is inadequate [12, 13]. The role of chemotherapy and radiotherapy is not well defined, and responses are variable [9, 10]. In low‐resource settings, delayed diagnosis, limited access to advanced imaging, and restricted oncology services further worsen outcomes [14, 15]. This case highlights the importance of early multidisciplinary discussion and consideration of preoperative cross‐sectional imaging or biopsy when intraoperative findings are atypical, even when initial clinical assessment favors benign disease.

Conclusion

Prostatic stromal sarcoma is a rare but highly aggressive malignancy that can masquerade as benign prostatic hyperplasia, particularly in patients with normal PSA levels. A clearly documented diagnostic timeline, careful correlation of clinical, imaging, operative, and histopathological findings, and heightened awareness of atypical intraoperative features are essential to improving early recognition of this rare entity. Early histopathological evaluation, prompt staging, and multidisciplinary management are essential, although prognosis remains poor, especially in resource‐limited settings.
