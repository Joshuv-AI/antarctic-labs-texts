Comparative transcriptome profiling reveals molecular mechanisms of freezing stress responses in cultivated and wild Cicer species
Authors: Melike Bakır, Saffet Teber, Tolga Can, Abdullah Kahriman, Cengiz Toker, Eric Bishop von Wettberg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chickpea is a globally important food legume, but its productivity is significantly constrained by low-temperature stress, particularly during autumn and winter sowing, as well as by sudden temperature fluctuations in late spring. Although wild relatives of chickpea exhibit enhanced cold tolerance, their molecular responses to freezing stress remain poorly understood. In this study, we compared cold-tolerant and cold-sensitive cultivated chickpea genotypes ILC8262 and ILC533 along with three wild relatives, Cicer bijugum, Cicer reticulatum, and Cicer echinospermum, using de novo transcriptome analysis through RNA sequencing. A total of 1.38 billion raw reads were generated, resulting in the assembly of 181,756 transcripts and 56,556 unigenes. Between 9,921 and 11,149 DEGs were identified per genotype under freezing stress. Functional annotation identified 453 unigenes related to cold response, while 519 DEGs were associated with cold or freezing tolerance based on gene ontology enrichment. Wild Cicer species showed a broader and more dynamic transcriptional response to freezing stress, including the activation of key calcium signaling components such as glutamate receptor-like channels, calmodulin-like proteins, CBL-CIPK modules, and calcium-dependent protein kinases, as well as MAPK cascades and genes in the ICE-CBF-COR pathway. Among the wild species, C. bijugum exhibited the highest number of species-specific DEGs and TFs, suggesting a distinct and potentially powerful cold adaptation strategy. In addition, we identified 8659 SSRs and 232,271 SNPs, with a subset validated as polymorphic markers. This study provides novel and comprehensive insights into the molecular mechanisms of freezing tolerance in chickpea and offers valuable genomic resources for breeding freezing-tolerant varieties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19801-1.

Introduction

Chickpea (Cicer arietinum L.) is a key food legume grown globally. The global cultivation area for chickpeas was reported to be 14.1 million hectares, with a production volume of 16.5 million tons in 2023. Among cool-season food legumes, chickpeas rank first globally in the total harvested area1. However, cold stress, classified into chilling (between 0 and − 15 °C) and freezing stress (< 0 °C), causes significant challenges to chickpea growth and development2. Chickpea is exposed to cold stress not only during the vegetative stage when sown in autumn in the Mediterranean region, but also during the flowering and podding stages in the Indian subcontinent and Australia. Sudden temperature drops in autumn, freezing conditions during winter, and unexpected cold spells in late spring collectively result in yield losses of up to 40% in chickpea production3. Despite these challenges, autumn sowing offers significant yield advantages over spring sowing, with reported increases of 60% to over 100% attributed to extended growing periods and favorable rainfall during winter and early spring4–10. Nevertheless, the limited availability of cold-hardy chickpeas suitable for autumn planting continues to restrict yield potential in these environments11,12.

The genus Cicer L. comprises 46 species, including 10 annual and 36 perennial species13–15. Among the annuals, C. arietinum is the only cultivated species, while C. echinospermum P.H. Davis, C. reticulatum Ladiz.,and C. turcicum Toker, Berger & Gokturk are its closest wild relatives, represent the primary and secondary gene pools, respectively15–17. Wild Cicer species exhibit greater cold tolerance compared to cultivated chickpea, making them valuable genetic resources for breeding programs. Initial evidence of cold tolerance was reported in C. microphyllum18, followed by comprehensive assessments that quantified freezing tolerance in C. bijugum Rech. f., C. reticulatum, and C. echinospermum based on 1 to 9 standardized evaluation scales19,20. Additionally, some wild species displayed chilling tolerance during reproductive stages, further underscoring their potential for crop improvement21.

Although field-based physiological studies have demonstrated that wild Cicer species exhibit significantly greater cold tolerance than cultivated chickpea, genomic resources for these wild species remain limited. Recently, studies have focused on identifying QTLs related to cold stress tolerance using an RIL population derived from C. arietinum and C. reticulatum22,23. Furthermore, functional markers were developed in C. arietinum and C. reticulatum using Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) technology to support chickpea breeding programs24. More recently, Kalve et al.25 explored transcriptomes and QTLs associated with freezing tolerance in C. reticulatum wild accessions under controlled conditions, providing valuable insights into the genetic basis of cold tolerance in this species. However, no comprehensive study has yet been conducted to generate functional genomic resources across multiple wild Cicer species to identify freezing tolerance-associated genes. This gap highlights the need for comparative transcriptomic approaches to better understand the molecular mechanisms underlying freezing stress responses in wild Cicer species with diverse cold tolerance capabilities.

In this study, we conducted a de novo RNA-seq transcriptome analysis to compare cultivated chickpea C. arietinum with wild species including C. reticulatum, C. echinospermum, and C. bijugum under freezing stress. Using Illumina sequencing, we identified species-specific cold-responsive candidate genes and performed gene ontology (GO) enrichment and pathway analyses to uncover key biological processes and metabolic pathways modulated by freezing conditions. This comparative transcriptomic approach provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms of cold tolerance and delivers valuable genomic resources to support the development of chickpea varieties better adapted to cold-prone environments.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

Cold-tolerant wild Cicer species, including 3 accessions of C. echinospermum (S2Drd_065, Gunas_062, and Karab_092), 7 accessions of C. reticulatum (Cudi1_022, CudiA_127, CudiA_101A, Oyali_082, Sirna_060, Umurl_501, and Umurl_502) from Harran University, Turkiye, and 3 accessions of C. bijugum (AWC2, AWC4, and AWC6) from Akdeniz University, Turkiye, were tested for freezing tolerance. C. echinospermum_S2Drd_065, C. reticulatum_Cudi1_022, and C. bijugum_AWC6 were selected to compare freezing stress responses at the transcriptomic level with two C. arietinum lines, cold-sensitive ILC533 and cold-tolerant ILC8262. Seeds were nicked and surface-sterilized by immersion in 10% sodium hypochlorite for 5 min, washed three times with sterile distilled water, and soaked in distilled water for 24 h. The seeds were germinated in petri dishes in a growth chamber under a 16/8 h light/dark cycle at 22/20 °C (day/night). After 3–4 days, germinated seeds were transferred to plastic vials and maintained under the same photoperiod at 24/20 °C (day/night).

Experimental design and freezing stress treatment

Twenty-eight seeds for both freezing stress and control groups were sown into vials for each species. Acclimatization was applied to seven-day-old seedlings in the following steps: 16 °C/16 °C (day/night) for 1 day, 16 °C/4 °C (day/night) for 1 day, and 4 °C/4 °C (day/night) for 4 days. At the end of the acclimatization stage, the lights in the climate test cabinet were turned off, and the temperature was gradually reduced by − 6 °C within 8 h (1–1.5 °C/h) and held at − 6 °C for 2 h. Control plants remained in the growth conditions mentioned above throughout the experiment. We confirmed the cold tolerance phenotype of the wild Cicer species in preliminary experiments, where they exhibited survival after exposure to frost conditions, unlike the cold-tolerant and sensitive cultivated chickpea lines. Cold-stressed plants and control plants were collected at the same time, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 86 °C until RNA extraction. For each sample, five plants were pooled, and three biological replicates were performed, resulting in 30 samples.

RNA isolation, library construction, and sequencing

RNA-Seq data processing, de novo assembly, and differential expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from both cold-treated and control plants using the SIGMA Spectrum™ Plant Total RNA Kit (Sigma Life Science, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quality and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, Agilent Bioanalyzer, and agarose gel electrophoresis (0.8%). RNA with a RIN ≥ 7 was used for library construction with the TrueSeq RNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina Technologies). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform, generating 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads for each sample.

Sequence quality was assessed using FastQC, which included checks for Q20, Q30, and GC content. Raw Illumina paired-end reads were processed to remove adapter sequences, low-quality bases, and undetermined bases using Cutadapt26 and custom Perl scripts. The high-quality clean reads from all samples were de novo assembled using Trinity 2.4.027 on the Seven Bridges Genomics Cloud Platform28. Assembly quality, transcript quantification (https://github.com/COMBINE-lab/salmon), and differential expression analysis were conducted using edgeR29. Differentially expressed genes were selected based on a log2 fold change greater than 1 or less than − 1 and an FDR-adjusted p-value < 0.05.

For transcript annotation, DIAMOND30 was used. BLAST and HMMER tools were employed to identify homologous sequences in the UniProt database, while Gene Ontology (GO) (http://www.geneontology.org), SwissProt (http://www.expasy.ch/sprot/), KEGG (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/), and eggNOG (http://eggnogdb.embl.de/) annotations were used for functional categorization. GO molecular function terms were used to identify differentially expressed transcription factors. To visualize common and species-specific differentially expressed genes, the InteractiVenn tool was used31.

Validation of RNA-Seq data by qRT-PCR

To validate the RNA-seq results, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed on nine randomly selected differentially expressed genes. Primers (Supplementary Table 4) were designed using Primer3 (http://bioinfo.ut.ee/primer3-0.4.0/). Two micrograms of total RNA per sample were reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Transcriptor First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). qRT-PCR was conducted with SYBR Green 1 Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a LightCycler® 480 system (Roche). Each gene was measured in triplicate with three biological replicates, and the expression levels were normalized to the internal control gene elongation factor 1 alpha (EF1α)32. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method33.

SSR mining and marker development

Simple Sequence Repeats (SSRs) were identified from the assembled unigenes using MISA (http://pgrc.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/), focusing on motifs from 1 to 6 nucleotides with a minimum of five tandem repeats. Primers for the identified SSRs were designed using Primer3 (v.0.4.0) (http://bioinfo.ut.ee/primer3-0.4.0/). The polymorphism of SSR markers was evaluated in the same Cicer genotypes used in the cold stress experiments, using the PCR reaction method as previously described by Bakır and Kahraman34. PCR amplification was performed using standard protocols, and polymorphisms were assessed by electrophoresis on 3.5% MetaPhor agarose gels24.

SNP analysis

SNPs in the coding regions were identified using the SAMtools and bcftools packages35, based on alignments to the reference genome generated with HISAT2. Variants were called and filtered to retain high-confidence SNPs with a quality score (QUAL) ≥ 50 and read depth (DP) ≤ 100. The filtered variants were annotated using ANNOVAR36 to identify functionally relevant genetic variants.

Results

Illumina sequencing and denovo transcriptome assembly

Illumina sequencing generated 1,381,704,268 raw reads, with each library contributing between 37 and 53 million reads from thirty libraries derived from five Cicer lines. After quality control, which involved trimming adaptor sequences and removing low-quality reads, 1,338,406,438 clean reads were retained, representing 96.86% of the raw data. The sequencing quality was robust, with Q30 scores exceeding 95% for all libraries, and the GC content was consistently ~ 42% across all treatments (Table 1). De novo transcriptome assembly produced 181,756 transcripts and 56,556 unigenes. The N50 values for transcripts and unigenes were 1,597 bp and 1,676 bp, respectively, reflecting a high-quality assembly (Table 2). A detailed length distribution of the assembled transcripts and unigenes was given in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Functional annotation of unigenes

To assign putative functions to Cicer unigenes, the sequences were aligned to the GO, KEGG, NR, SwissProt, and eggNOG databases with default parameters (e-value ≤ 1e−5). Of the 56,556 unigenes, 37,312 (65.97%), 34,845 (61.61%), 29,571 (52.29%), 23,869 (42.20%), and 15,628 (27.63%) unigenes exhibited homologous sequence matches in the NR, eggNOG, GO, SwissProt, and KEGG databases, respectively (Supplementary Table 1).

Based on GO annotations, 29,571 unigenes were classified into 50 functional groups spanning biological processes (58,141 terms), cellular components (56,048 terms), and molecular functions (48,679 terms) (Fig. 1a). In the biological process category, the most abundant terms were biological process (GO:0008150, 3677 unigenes), regulation of transcription, DNA-templated (GO:0006355, 1894 unigenes), and transcription, DNA-templated (GO:0006351, 1422 unigenes). For cellular components, the dominant terms included nucleus (GO:0005634, 8239 unigenes), cytoplasm (GO:0005737, 4673 unigenes), and plasma membrane (GO:0005886, 4044 unigenes). Within the molecular function category, molecular function (GO:0003674, 3390 unigenes), protein binding (GO:0005515, 2496 unigenes), and ATP binding (GO:0005524, 2185 unigenes) were the most prominent terms (Fig. 1a). Additionally, 453 unigenes were identified as cold stress-responsive based on GO annotations. Of these, 384 unigenes were linked to response to cold (GO:0009409), 46 unigenes to cellular cold acclimation (GO:0009631), and 23 unigenes to response to cold acclimation (GO:0070417).

KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, http://www.kegg.jp/) annotations provided further insights into molecular interactions, functions, and gene networks37.A total of 15,628 unigenes were categorized into five major functional groups connected to 137 KEGG reference pathways (Fig. 1b). The “metabolism” category, encompassing 10,479 unigenes, was the most highly represented and was dominated by pathways involved in carbohydrate metabolism (2468 unigenes), lipid metabolism (1161 unigenes), and amino acid metabolism (1128 unigenes). Within the “genetic information processing” group (5698 unigenes), key pathways included translation (2321 unigenes), folding, sorting, and degradation (1872 unigenes), and transcription (977 unigenes).

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) under freezing stress

To investigate the molecular basis of the freezing stress response among Cicer species, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) based on TPM (transcripts per million) with log2 thresholds > 1 or < − 1 and a statistical significance cutoff of FDR-adjusted p-value < 0.05. Homologous proteins for these DEGs were identified using BLAST against the UniProt database. A Venn diagram was generated to visualize shared and species-specific DEGs across the Cicer species (Fig. 2). Additionally, PCC and PCA were used to show the pattern of DEGs in Cicer species under freezing stress (Fig. 3).

The total number of DEGs varied among species under freezing stress conditions. In C. arietinum_ILC533, 9,921 DEGs were identified, including 4564 upregulated and 5357 downregulated genes. Similarly, C. arietinum_ILC8262 exhibited 9481 DEGs, comprising 4251 upregulated and 5230 downregulated genes. C. bijugum displayed 10,324 DEGs, with 4843 upregulated and 5481 downregulated genes, whereas C. reticulatum showed 11,149 DEGs, including 6084 upregulated and 5065 downregulated genes. In C. echinospermum, 9930 DEGs were identified, consisting of 4887 upregulated and 5043 downregulated genes. A total of 3185 DEGs were found to be common across all five species. Species-specific DEGs included 661 in C. arietinum_ILC533, 480 in C. arietinum_ILC8262, 2492 in C. bijugum, 1766 in C. reticulatum, and 894 in C. echinospermum. Interestingly, although C. reticulatum had the highest total number of DEGs (11,149), C. bijugum exhibited a greater proportion of species-specific DEGs (3262, 34.04%) compared to C. reticulatum (1837, 17.96%) (Supplementary Dataset File 1). This finding may explain why C. bijugum demonstrates superior cold stress tolerance compared to C. echinospermum, C. reticulatum, and C. arietinum.

In addition to the upregulated and downregulated DEGs, genes that were either “turned on” or “turned off” exclusively under freezing stress conditions were identified. The total number of turn-on DEGs was 45, 48, 57, 69, and 39 for C. arietinum_ILC533, C. arietinum_ILC8262, C. bijugum, C. reticulatum, and C. echinospermum, respectively. On the other hand, the total number of turn-off DEGs was 219, 314, 180, 31, and 189 in C. arietinum_ILC533, C. arietinum_ILC8262, C. bijugum, C. reticulatum, and C. echinospermum, respectively (Supplementary Dataset File 3). Notably, 13 turn-off genes were shared across all Cicer species, while no turn-on genes were shared among Cicer species. These results highlight the distinct gene expression dynamics contributing to the freezing stress tolerance of Cicer species.

Transcription factors in response to freezing stress

Transcription factors (TFs) serve as crucial regulators in plant responses to abiotic stresses, including cold stress. In this study, a total of 9127 TFs were identified among the DEGs, with homologous proteins validated via BLAST analysis against the UniProt database. Of these, 1814 TFs were found to be common across all Cicer lines. Species-specific TFs included 303 in cold-sensitive C. arietinum ILC533, and 189, 1202, 357, and 749 in the cold-tolerant lines C. arietinum ILC8262, C. bijugum, C. echinospermum, and C. reticulatum, respectively (Fig. 4).

The identified TFs were classified into 58 TF families, with the bHLH, NAC, ERF, MYB-related, and C2H2 families dominating across all groups (Fig. 5). These families are known for their roles in modulating plant stress responses, development, and metabolic pathways. Similar to the observed patterns of turn-on and turn-off DEGs, certain TFs were exclusively activated under freezing stress, while others were repressed despite being expressed under normal conditions. The number of turn-on TFs was 30, 29, 32, 31, and 25 for C. arietinum_ILC533, C. arietinum_ILC8262, C. bijugum, C. reticulatum, and C. echinospermum, respectively. Conversely, the number of turn-off TFs was 149, 88, 24, 11, and 132 for C. arietinum_ILC533, C. arietinum_ILC8262, C. bijugum, C. reticulatum, and C. echinospermum, respectively. Notably, no common turn-on TFs were identified across all Cicer species, whereas 6 turn-off TFs were shared among all species. Species-specific activated and shutdown TFs were given in Supplementary Dataset File 4.

Functional analysis of DEGs in response to freezing stress

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was used to classify the functional roles of DEGs across Cicer species under freezing stress conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2). Specific GO terms were enriched in each species, highlighting key differences in their stress responses. In C. arietinum_ILC533, the most enriched terms included “chloroplast” (GO:0009507), “molecular function” (GO:0003674), and “regulation of transcription, DNA-templated” (GO:0006355). In C. arietinum_ILC8262, “molecular function” (GO:0003674), “transcription factor activity, sequence-specific DNA binding” (GO:0003700), and “extracellular region” (GO:0009414) were predominant. For C. bijugum, the most represented terms were “regulation of transcription, DNA-templated” (GO:0006355), “transcription, DNA-templated” (GO:0006351), and “extracellular region” (GO:0009414). In C. echinospermum, the enriched terms included “molecular function” (GO:0003674), “regulation of transcription, DNA-templated” (GO:0006355), and “DNA binding” (GO:0003677). Finally, for C. reticulatum (C5), the most enriched terms were “chloroplast” (GO:0009507), “molecular function” (GO:0003674), and “transcription factor activity, sequence-specific DNA binding” (GO:0003700).

The GO knowledgebase further facilitated the classification of 519 DEGs associated with cold and freezing responses. Among these, 385 were related to “response to cold” (GO:0009409), 47 to “cold acclimation” (GO:0009631), 24 to “cellular response to cold” (GO:0070417), 15 to “response to freezing” (GO:0050826), and 7 to “cellular response to freezing” (GO:0009266) (Supplementary Dataset File 2). These genes were evaluated as key genes in response to freezing stress in Cicer species. Among them, the genes involved in signal perception and transduction, including CMLs, CBLs, CIPKs, CDPKs (CPKs), MAPKs, MEKKs, MKKs, MPKs, genes associated with transcriptional regulation, such as CBF/DREB1 pathway genes, as well as cold-responsive genes, such as COR genes, KINs, LTIs, were particularly noteworthy (Fig. 6). More importantly, the expression patterns of these genes revealed distinct regulatory dynamics among Cicer species under freezing stress, highlighting both species-specific adaptations and conserved molecular mechanisms underlying cold stress responses.

KEGG pathway analysis of DEGs revealed that significantly upregulated genes across all Cicer species were primarily associated with carbon metabolism and photosynthesis. In contrast, significantly downregulated genes were mostly involved in the spliceosome and protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum pathways. KEGG enrichment of DEGs also showed that the most enriched pathways in C. arietinum ILC533, C. arietinum ILC8262, C. echinospermum, and C. reticulatum included carbon metabolism, starch and sucrose metabolism, plant hormone signal transduction, and plant–pathogen interaction. However, in C. bijugum, biosynthesis of amino acids was one of the most represented pathways instead of plant hormone signal transduction (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Analysis of SSR and SNP

SSR-containing unigenes were identified following the assembly. A total of 8659 SSRs were detected from 56,556 unigenes. Trinucleotide repeats (39.9%) and dinucleotide repeats (30.9%) were the most prevalent SSR types, followed by mononucleotide repeats (19.2%). Other SSR types, such as tetranucleotide (2.6%), pentanucleotide (3.1%), and hexanucleotide (4.03%) motifs, were observed at lower frequencies compared to dinucleotide and trinucleotide motifs (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Among dinucleotide and trinucleotide repeat motifs, TC/CT (8.3%) and TTC/AAT (2%) were the most frequent, respectively.

To validate and optimize SSR markers, 55 SSR-containing sequences were randomly selected from each SSR type and tested across the four Cicer species studied (Supplementary Table 2). Of these 55 potential SSR markers, 30 (54.5%) successfully amplified in all Cicer species with the expected products. The PCR products were resolved on Metaphor agarose gels (Supplementary Fig. 5). Of the remaining markers, some SSRs showed species-specific amplification, and 10 markers failed to amplify in any species (Supplementary Table 3). Among the tested markers, 28 were identified as polymorphic SSR markers across the Cicer species.

Along with the SSR analysis, potential SNPs were found by comparing raw data to the assembled reference database. A total of 425,479 high-confidence variant positions were detected, including 232,271 SNPs across 55,080 genes (Supplementary Fig. 4b). The identified SNPs comprised 290,808 transitions and 187,125 transversions. The transition between cytosine (C) and thymine (T) was the most frequently observed variation across all Cicer species. While further validation of these SNPs is required, their annotation suggests they represent potential polymorphic markers within cold-regulated transcripts.

Validation of DEGs by qRT-PCR

To confirm the accuracy and reliability of the RNA-seq results, nine putative cold-responsive DEGs were randomly selected from all Cicer species for validation using qRT-PCR analysis. Among these, five genes were upregulated and four genes were downregulated under cold stress conditions. Even though the fold-change values from qRT-PCR were not exactly the same as those from RNA-seq, the overall patterns of gene expression were similar for all the tested DEGs. These results have validated the reliability of the RNA-seq data and confirmed expression trends of DEGs identified under freezing stress in this study.

Discussion

Cold stress is a major environmental factor limiting chickpea yield and production, particularly in regions where low temperatures during early vegetative and reproductive stages can cause significant damage. To minimize these effects, chickpeas are traditionally sown in spring; however, research has shown that autumn or early winter sowing can increase seed yield by at least 60% and, in some cases, more than double it8–10. Despite these potential yield advantages, the genetic bottleneck in domesticated chickpeas, partly due to natural and farmer-driven selection for spring sowing, has limited their adaptability to cold stress. Crop wild relatives (CWR) represent a valuable genetic reservoir for broadening the genetic base of chickpeas and enhancing cold tolerance. Despite significant progress in understanding cold stress mechanisms in plants, the molecular basis of low-temperature tolerance in wild chickpeas remains unclear. Therefore, this study provides valuable insights into cold stress response mechanisms of cold-tolerant wild (C. echinospermum, C. reticulatum, and C. bijugum) and both cold-sensitive and cold-tolerant cultivated (C. arietinum) Cicer genotypes through comparative analysis.

The untapped genetic diversity of wild Cicer species and landraces holds immense potential for enhancing stress resilience in chickpea38. These wild relatives not only exhibit greater resistance to various biotic and abiotic stresses but also serve as vital reservoirs of adaptive traits and cold tolerance genes19–21,25,38. Based on crossability, wild Cicer species have been categorized into three gene pools. C. reticulatum and C. echinospermum belong to the primary gene pool (GP1)39,40 and readily hybridize with cultivated chickpea, often producing fertile offspring depending on the parental lines41. C. bijugum, classified within the secondary gene pool (GP2), has also been successfully crossed with domesticated chickpea, yielding a substantial number of seeds42. Therefore, we have worked on cultivated and wild Cicer species to understand their response to freezing conditions. The results showed that the wild Cicer accessions S2Drd_065, Cudi1_022, and AWC6 are more tolerant than the other tested wild accessions and cultivated cultivars.

Low-temperature tolerance in plants is a complex quantitative trait regulated by numerous genes. The differential gene expression analysis revealed the complexity of gene regulation in the cold/freezing stress tolerance mechanism in chickpea species. A significant number of genes were expressed differently in all Cicer species (between 9921 and 11149). The GO enrichment analysis of DEGs highlighted numerous biological processes affected by freezing stress. Overall, genes associated with transcription factor activity, DNA-binding, oxidation-reduction processes, protein kinase activity, and metal ion binding were significantly represented across all species. However, C. bijugum, C. reticulatum, and C. echinospermum exhibited a higher number of genes involved in key cold response processes such as chloroplast and thylakoid membrane function, ATP binding, and defense responses compared to the cultivated genotypes (C.arietinum_ILC533 and C.arietinum_ILC8262). These patterns suggest that wild Cicer species may engage more robust or diverse molecular pathways to cope with freezing conditions, supporting their enhanced cold tolerance. These findings reinforce recent reports by Kalve et al.25 and Akbari et al.43, which also emphasized the importance of transcriptional coordination and signal transduction in cold tolerance.

Plants sense the temperature changes through plasma membrane receptors and initiate calcium influx into the cytosol by Ca2+-permeable channels44. Among these channels, glutamate receptor-like channels (GLRs) and MID1-complementing activity (MCA) facilitate Ca2+ influx into the cytoplasm45 and contribute to cold tolerance by activating downstream signaling cascades involving protein kinases46,47. In this study, we observed differential expression of these channels in response to freezing stress across Cicer species. While MCA1, GLR3.3, and GLR3.4 were responsive to freezing stress, GLR3.3 was the only isoform significantly upregulated across all Cicer species, suggesting a conserved role in cold stress adaptation. In tomato, cold acclimation has been shown to significantly induce GLR3.3, contributing to enhanced chilling tolerance48.

Following Ca2+ influx, various calcium-binding proteins act as intracellular sensors, including calmodulins (CaMs), calmodulin-like proteins (CMLs), calcineurin B-like proteins (CBLs), and calcium-dependent protein kinases (CDPKs or CPKs). Our data show that CaM2 was significantly upregulated in response to freezing in all Cicer species except C5, whereas CaM3 was induced across all species. Notably, several CML genes were differentially regulated under freezing stress. Among these, CML1 was consistently upregulated, while CML8, CML10, CML25, CML31, and CML50 were downregulated across all species. Interestingly, CML18, CML19, and CML30 were significantly upregulated only in C. reticulatum, suggesting a species-specific response that may contribute to enhanced freezing tolerance in this genotype. Similar cold-induced CML expression profiles have been reported in C. arietinum and C. reticulatum25, as well as in other species such as Chrysanthemum seticuspe49 and Bergenia purpurascens50.

The CBL-CIPK signaling network, a central component of calcium-mediated stress adaptation51, also plays a critical role in the cold stress response52. In our study, distinct expression patterns of CBL and CIPK genes were observed across Cicer species under freezing conditions, highlighting both conserved and species-specific regulatory mechanisms. Among CBL genes, CBL1 was upregulated exclusively in C. bijugum, while CBL10 was consistently downregulated in C. arietinum_ILC533, C. arietinum_ILC8262, C. echinospermum, and C. reticulatum. CBL8 was induced in C. arietinum_ILC8262, C. echinospermum, and C. reticulatum, whereas CBL4 showed strong upregulation in C. arietinum_ILC533, C. arietinum_ILC8262. Notably, CBL9 was downregulated in all species except C.bijugum, suggesting its involvement in species-specific calcium homeostasis regulation under freezing stress. CIPK genes also exhibited diverse expression responses. CIPK1 was downregulated in all species except C. reticulatum, and CIPK10 and CIPK25 were consistently downregulated across all species. In contrast, CIPK11 was upregulated only in C.bijugum and C. reticulatum, while CIPK12 showed increased expression in C. arietinum_ILC533, C.bijugum, and C. reticulatum. Strikingly, CIPK4 was upregulated in all species, indicating a potentially conserved role in cold stress adaptation. Conversely, CIPK9 was downregulated in all species, though the reduction was less downregulated in C. reticulatum, suggesting variation in regulatory mechanisms. These results reveal the complexity of the CBL-CIPK regulatory module during freezing stress and suggest both evolutionary conservation and divergence in Cicer species. Supporting these findings, 13 CIPK genes, including CIPK1, CIPK9, CIPK10, and CIPK25, were reported to be cold-inducible in Bergenia purpurascens50. Additionally, CBL-CIPK genes were upregulated during cold acclimation and recovery phases in wild chickpea (C. reticulatum, Kesen_075)25.

Another major class of Ca2+ sensors, the CDPKs (or CPKs), also exhibited notable transcriptional changes under freezing conditions. CPK1, CPK14, and CPK20 were consistently downregulated in all Cicer species; however, their expression levels remained relatively higher in C. reticulatum, potentially indicating a more robust or sustained calcium signaling capacity in this genotype. Taken together, our findings offer new insights into the molecular responses to cold stress in Cicer species, particularly with respect to calcium signaling pathways. The differential expression of GLRs, CMLs, components of the CBL-CIPK network, and CDPKs suggests that certain species—especially C. reticulatum —may have evolved more effective calcium-mediated strategies to cope with freezing stress. Future studies involving the functional characterization of key candidate genes and their downstream targets will be essential for elucidating the molecular basis of cold tolerance in Cicer.

MAPK cascades, particularly MEKK1–MKK1/2–MPK4 and MKK5–MPK3/6, are well-characterized regulators of cold signaling53,54. MEKK1–MKK2–MPK4 pathway promotes freezing tolerance by antagonizing the MKK5–MPK3/6 cascade, which destabilizes ICE1 and suppresses CBF expression in Arabidopsis54,55. In our study, key MAPK components were predominantly downregulated across Cicer species. Both MEKK1 and MKK1/2 were consistently suppressed, while MPK4 was not detected in any genotype. MKK5 was significantly downregulated only in C. reticulatum. MPK3 was slightly downregulated in most species but upregulated in C. bijugum, whereas MPK6 was not detected. These results resemble findings from Arabidopsis mpk3/mpk6 mutants, which exhibit enhanced freezing tolerance due to reduced ICE1 degradation55. Although ICE1 was not differentially expressed in our dataset, the expression patterns of MPK3/6 suggest that either a different transcriptional induction system or post-transcriptional regulations may contribute to the modulation of ICE1 activity in Cicer. These results support the idea of a complex and potentially species-specific role of MAPK pathways in the freezing stress response of Cicer species. Together, these differences underscore the complex and possibly species-specific roles of MAPK pathways in the cold stress response among Cicer species. On the other hand, Kalve et al.25 reported stronger MAPK downregulation in cultivated chickpea compared to wild accession under freezing stress. Similarly, Akbari et al.43 observed MKK2 was upregulated in cold-tolerant chickpea genotype, but induction was not significant in cold-sensitive chickpea genotype under cold stress. These differences between the studies may be due to the genetic background of the genotypes, experimental design, or stress regimes, particularly the duration and intensity of cold versus freezing treatments.

The ICE–CBF–COR pathway plays a key role in regulating plant responses to low-temperature stress56. The CBF/DREB1 pathway, particularly CBF3/DREB1A, is regulated by the MYC-type transcription factor ICE157. However, the pathway has not been explained well in chickpeas. In our study, CBF3/DREB1A was significantly downregulated in C. arietinum_ILC533, C. echinospermum, and C. reticulatum, while it was not detected among the differentially expressed genes in C. arietinum_ILC8262 and C.bijugum. Although ICE1 itself was not differentially expressed in any genotype, its regulatory importance remains well established. Previous studies have shown that ICE1 controls the expression of CBF3/DREB1A and regulates approximately 40% of COR genes and nearly half of all cold-responsive transcription factors58. The observed downregulation of CBF3/DREB1A in specific Cicer genotypes may indicate the involvement of alternative ICE1-like regulators in mediating CBF induction under freezing stress, as proposed by Miura et al.59 or alternative posttranscriptional regulation and posttranslational modification of this pathway. DREB1E, which is also known for enhancing freezing tolerance, is highly upregulated in all Cicer genotypes, whereas it was significantly upregulated in only the tolerant genotype in Akbari et al.43 under cold stress.

COR genes play an important role, either through CBF-dependent or independent pathways, in mediating both chilling and freezing tolerance in plants60. In our study, we found that COR2 and COR413PM1 were upregulated in all Cicer species; however, the induction of the COR2 gene was less significant in the cold-sensitive genotype C. arietinum_ILC533. COR410 was significantly downregulated in all species, but it was less significant in C. reticulatum. COR413IM1 showed upregulation in all genotypes, but its expression was most significant in C. bijugum. While most KIN genes were downregulated under freezing stress, LTI65 was upregulated in all genotypes, with stronger induction observed in wild Cicer lines. Kalve et al.25 reported that COR413 showed similar expression patterns in both genotypes in their study, aligning with our observation that COR413PM1 and COR413IM1 were induced across all Cicer species.

Transcription factor (TF) families such as bHLH, MYB-related, NAC, ERF, C2H2, WRKY, and C3H exhibited notable differential expression across all Cicer species under freezing stress, though the degree of regulation varied by genotype. These TF families are well known to participate in cold and freezing tolerance mechanisms43,61. Similarly, Kalve et al.25 also reported the induction of these TFs in the cold-tolerant wild chickpea genotype Kesen_075. Among the families, bHLH was the most abundantly represented across all species. Interestingly, C2H2 TFs were more prevalent in the cold-sensitive genotype C. arietinum_ILC533, whereas WRKY was more enriched in C. echinospermum, and C3H was predominantly observed in C. reticulatum. These patterns suggest both conserved and genotype-specific roles of transcription factors in cold stress responses among cultivated and wild Cicer species.

Beyond differential expression levels, we also identified genes that were exclusively activated (“turn-on”) or repressed (“turn-off”) in response to freezing stress, providing insights into binary regulatory shifts under extreme conditions. Cold-tolerant species, particularly C. bijugum and C. reticulatum, exhibited a greater number of turn-on genes, suggesting active recruitment of cold-responsive pathways as a potential adaptive mechanism. In contrast, C. arietinum_ ILC533, the most cold-sensitive genotype, showed the highest number of turn-off genes and transcription factors, reflecting a widespread repression of transcriptional activity under freezing stress. The identification of common turn-off TFs across all species, but no common turn-on TFs, suggests that Cicer species may share a conserved strategy of repressing key regulatory elements in response to cold, whereas transcriptional activation appears to be more species-specific. These results underscore that cold tolerance in Cicer species is not only dependent on the induction or repression of stress-responsive genes but also on the selective silencing of some genes to preserve cellular functions under freezing conditions.

In addition to transcriptomic insights, the identification of SSR and SNP markers provides valuable genomic resources for chickpea improvement. Trinucleotide and dinucleotide repeats were the most prevalent SSR types, consistent with patterns in other legumes where coding-region SSRs tend to preserve reading frames. A representative set of SSRs was validated across multiple. Cicer species, with over half amplifying successfully and many exhibiting cross-species polymorphism, underscoring their utility for comparative genomics and marker-assisted breeding. Species-specific amplification further suggests potential for identifying loci associated with adaptive divergence. SNP discovery also revealed extensive variation, with transitions—especially C↔T—being the most common, reflecting known patterns in cold-stressed plant genomes. Many SNPs were located in genes differentially expressed under freezing stress, suggesting they may serve as functional markers linked to cold response pathways. Together, these SSRs and SNPs complement the expression profiles and enrich the genomic toolkit available for dissecting cold tolerance in Cicer species.
