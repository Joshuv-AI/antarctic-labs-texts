Functional regulation and cross-talk of type III and type VI secretion systems in Salmonella
Authors: Xingyi Li, Ying Ding, Yu Sun, Yiting Gu, Wanli Feng, Yanyan Jia, Huimin Chen, Songbiao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn Salmonella, the type III and type VI secretion systems (T3SS and T6SS) play critical roles in pathogenicity. The bacterium harbors two distinct T3SSs: T3SS-1, encoded by SPI-1, promotes host cell invasion, whereas T3SS-2 (located within SPI-2) facilitates systemic infection. Both systems translocate effector proteins that disrupt normal host cell functions. Meanwhile, the T6SS enhances competitive fitness in the gut by targeting rival bacteria and supports evasion of the host immune response. Recent evidence indicates that these secretion systems share common regulatory components, including the PhoP-PhoQ and PmrA-PmrB two-component systems, as well as signaling molecules such as cyclic di-GMP (c-di-GMP), allowing coordinated activation and functional adaptation during infection. However, the precise mechanisms governing their crosstalk remain poorly understood. This review summarizes the current knowledge of the composition, function, and regulation of T3SS and T6SS in Salmonella, examines their interplay, and provides perspectives for future research into pathogenesis.

Introduction

Bacterial survival, proliferation, and dissemination within host organisms rely on the secretion of proteinaceous virulence factors [1–3]. This process is mediated by specialized bacterial secretion systems, which play essential roles in bacterium-eukaryotic host interactions [4,5]. In Gram-negative bacteria, at least six distinct secretion systems have been identified, designated type I to VI (T1SS-T6SS), whereas Gram-positive bacteria employ a unique type VII system (T7SS) [6,7]. Among these, five secretion systems – T1SS, T3SS, T4SS, T5SS, and T6SS – have been reported in Salmonella[8,9].

The pathogenicity of Salmonella is closely linked to its specialized bacterial secretion systems [1,2,10], notably the T3SS and T6SS. These systems have been extensively studied and are known to execute complex and interconnected regulatory functions during infection [11]. The T3SS is a transmembrane export complex that can assemble structures such as extracellular appendages, flagellar filaments, and injectisomes. Salmonella possesses two independent, virulence-associated T3SSs, encoded by pathogenicity island 1 (SPI-1) and pathogenicity island 2 (SPI-2), respectively [12–14]. By delivering diverse effector proteins into host cells, the T3SS disrupts host cell signaling, proliferation, and apoptosis, thereby compromising normal cellular functions and enhancing bacterial pathogenicity [15]. The T6SS functions as a contact-dependent molecular machine that translocates effectors to inhibit competing bacteria [16,17], thereby augmenting Salmonella’s competitive fitness in the gut environment [18,19].

Salmonella must dynamically balance survival and pathogenicity, maintaining an equilibrium among survival, transmission, proliferation, and infection to maximize its fitness within the host [20]. Moreover, studies have revealed that the T6SS and T3SS can share regulatory components, including the PhoP-PhoQ and PmrA-PmrB two-component systems and signaling molecules such as c-di-GMP [21,22], suggesting potential crosstalk between the systems during Salmonella infection. This review provides a concise overview of the composition and function of the two secretion systems in Salmonella, the regulatory roles of T3SS and T6SS during infection, and the mechanisms underlying their crosstalk. It aims to lay a foundation for the future development of novel antimicrobial agents targeting T3SS and T6SS.

An overview of the composition and pivotal roles of the T3SS and T6SS in Salmonella

The composition and function of T3SS

Among the five secretion systems of Salmonella, the T3SS plays a decisive role in establishing effective infection within host cells [23,24]. The T6SS also contributes significantly to the infection process, enhancing the survival and pathogenic capacity in the host environment [18,19,25].

The SPI-1 encoded T3SS-1 is a complex molecular machine composing over 20 proteins, which are primarily organized into a base and a needle‑like structure. The base is anchored in the bacterial membrane, while the needlelike structure spans the bacterial envelope to sense the host environment [26]. As a major virulence system of Salmonella enterica, the type III secretion system encoded by SPI-2 is assembled after the bacteria are taken up by mammalian cells and form Salmonella-containing vacuoles (SCVs). The injectisome consists of a needlelike structure on the surface of bacterial cells and translocon proteins. After being secreted through the needlelike structure, the translocon proteins insert into the vacuolar membrane to form a pore, which provides a channel for the translocation of various effector proteins [27].

Among the various proteins that constitute the T3SS, SpaO, OrgA, OrgB, InvI, and InvC form the sorting platform, with SpaO playing a central role in its assembly. The export apparatus is composed of InvA, SpaP, SpaQ, SpaR, and SpaS. The base of the needle complex is formed by InvG, PrgH, and PrgK, while the needle itself is formed by PrgI and is anchored to the base by an inner rod consisting of PrgJ. The needle tip complex is assembled from SipB, SipC, and SipD (Figure 1A) [28–30]. T3SS-1 and T3SS-2 function at different stages of infection. T3SS-1, encoded by SPI-1, mediates bacterial invasion of host epithelial cells and early intestinal colonization by delivering effector proteins that modulate host cytoskeletal dynamics and inflammatory signaling. In contrast, T3SS-2, encoded by SPI-2, supports intracellular survival and systemic infection by translocating effectors that remodel the Salmonella-containing vacuole (SCV). Core T3SS-2 effectors, including SseF, SseG, and SifA, govern the intracellular positioning, membrane stability, and organellar interactions of the SCV, thereby establishing a permissive replicative niche [31]. Another group, known as accessory effectors, such as IpaH, SseM, and PipB2 [32]. They extensively interfere with host innate immune signaling, cell death pathways, and antigen presentation through enzymatic activities – including deubiquitination, glycosylation, and protease activity – thereby suppressing host defenses [33,34]. Effector profiles vary significantly across different Salmonella serotypes, and this diversity underlies the bacterium’s adaptation to distinct hosts and infection niches. The composition and function of T6SS

T6SS, a “phage-like” structure, is widely distributed among Gram-negative bacteria [35]. The T6SS utilizes specialized delivery domains to translocate effector proteins into target prey cells [36,37]. Recently, Bernal et al. identified a novel delivery domain named PIX, which facilitates the recognition of hundreds of previously unknown effectors [38]. During interbacterial competition, the T6SS secretes multiple effector proteins that specifically target cell wall components of competing strains, while cognate immunity proteins are co-produced to prevent self-harm [39,40]. This mechanism enables bacteria to efficiently eliminate competitors and secure ecological niches. In host-pathogen interactions, the T6SS not only modulates immune cell functions but also disrupts the commensal gut microbiota, thereby providing pathogenic bacteria with advantages for colonization and survival. At the population level, the T6SS can orchestrate collective behaviors through quorum-sensing or mutual induction mechanisms, enhancing the overall fitness and competitiveness of the bacterial community. This multi-layered regulatory strategy establishes the T6SS as a key system for bacterial adaptation and pathogenesis within complex environments [41].

In Salmonella, the T6SS functions as a crucial adaptive and virulence factor that significantly influences environmental fitness and pathogenic potential across different serotypes [2,42]. Structurally, the T6SS is a highly conserved and complex protein secretion apparatus, composed of at least 13 core structural components and a variable set of accessory proteins, which collectively deliver effector proteins into target cells through a contraction-driven mechanism [43]. The Salmonella T6SS exhibits a typical modular organization. Its core framework consists of a transmembrane complex and a cytoplasmic complex. The transmembrane complex includes three proteins – TssJ, TssM, and TssL – which form an anchoring structure spanning the inner and outer membrane. The cytoplasmic complex, centered on the hub protein TssK, mediates the connection between the transmembrane complex and the effector protein delivery apparatus. Notably, in some strains, TssK contains a host-targeting domain. The effector delivery apparatus is built around a contractile sheath formed by alternating TssB and TssC subunits, which encapsulated an inner tube composed of Hcp hexamers and a puncturing module tipped with VgrG and PAAR proteins (Figure 1B) [44–46]. The function of the T6SS relies on dynamic conformational changes of the sheath: extension for loading effector proteins and contraction to generate mechanical force that drive the puncturing module to inject effectors into target cells [47]. Functional specificity is often determined by effector proteins bound to the C-terminus of VgrG, while PAAR proteins enhance the stability of the apparatus [39]. Brunet et al. experimentally confirmed the role of VgrG as the tip protein of the T6SS “molecular syringe,” highlighting its essential function in facilitating effector entry into target cells during sheath contraction [48]. Salmonella harbors multiple T6SS gene clusters, such as SPI-6 (S. Typhimurium), SPI-19 (S. Gallinarum), SPI-20 (S. IIIa 62:z4,z23), SPI-21 (S. IIIa 62:z4,z23), and SPI-22 (S. Bongori) [49,50]. Variations in structural protein sequences lead to strain-specific differences in membrane anchoring efficiency and sheath contraction kinetics. Additionally, certain VgrG proteins contain integrated host-targeting domains. Structurally, the Hemolysin Coregulated Protein (Hcp) hexamer resembles the tail sheath of bacteriophages. Both Hcp and VgrG can serve dual roles as structural components and effector proteins, while some effectors are transported via adapter proteins such as PAAR (Proline-Alanine-Alanine-Arginine) [45,51,52]. Among the regulatory components, ClpV (Caseinolytic Protease V) acts as a key ATPase that plays a vital role in governing the cyclic assembly and disassembly of the T6SS [48,52,53]. Most T6SS components are unique to this system, and their precise interactions ensure its efficient operation. The T6SS also modulates pathogenicity induced by the T3SS, thereby promoting sustained bacterial persistence within the host [54]. However, the precise mechanisms underlying the cross-regulation and functional balance between these distinct secretion systems, as well as the identities of upstream and downstream signaling molecules involved in their perception and coordination, remain unclear.

Roles of T3SS and T6SS at different stages of Salmonella infection

The progression of Salmonella intestinal infection follows a distinct temporal sequence, in which T3SS and T6SS drive the infection process through stage-specific functional switching and synergistic interaction. The initial phase of Salmonella pathogenesis is mediated by the T3SS-1, which facilitates the invasion of intestinal epithelial cells and triggers inflammatory responses [55]. Subsequently, after entry into macrophages, the bacteria activate T3SS-2 expression to support intracellular survival and replication. In contrast, T6SS functions predominantly during the early stages of bacterial colonization and later stages of infection, where it helps counteract host immune defenses and eliminate competing microorganisms [21,56]. Using the T3SS and T6SS of S. Typhimurium as a model, we propose classifying the infection process into four distinct stages: pre-colonization, intestinal colonization, intracellular survival, and systemic dissemination.

(1) Pre-colonization phase: dominant role of SPI-6 T6SS

Studies in murine and avian infection models have shown that the SPI-6 T6SS of S. Typhimurium mediates interbacterial competition in the intestine, providing a competitive advantage by eliminating rival microbiota [57]. Within the first 2–5 days of infection, T6SS enables Salmonella to clear Klebsiella aerogenes and Klebsiella variicola [52]. This antagonistic activity depends on the binding of Hcp1 to Tae4, through which the Tae4 effector protein is delivered into target cells. Activation of SPI-6 T6SS at this stage is induced by intestinal bile acid signals, which stimulate SPI-6 T6SS expression and promote ClpV-mediated ATP hydrolysis to power system assembly. Additionally, bile acids indirectly suppress premature T3SS-1 activation, preventing early inflammatory responses that could hinder bacterial colonization [52]. Notably, this mechanism is not universal; E. coli, for example, can express the Tai4 protein to inhibit Tae4 activity and thereby evade elimination (Figure 2A) [48]. (2) Colonization Phase: Central Role of T3SS-1

During intestinal colonization, the T3SS−1 plays a central role [58]. Effector proteins such as SpiA, secreted via T3SS-1, induce actin-dependent membrane ruffling that facilitates S. Typhimurium invasion into non-phagocytic epithelial cells (Figure 2B) [59]. Concurrently, T3SS-1 recruits neutrophils during enterocolitis, leading to reduction and structural reorganization of the gut microbiota, which further promotes Salmonella colonization [60,61]. Once T3SS-1 is activated, the PhoP-PhoQ two-component system downregulates HilA, its key transcriptional activator, thereby shutting down T3SS-1 and priming the sequential activation of T3SS-2 and SPI-9 T6SS in subsequent stages.

(3) Intracellular survival phase: core functions of T3SS-2

After S. Typhimurium enters macrophages, T3SS-2 is activated to support intracellular survival. The SsrB regulator, which controls SPI-2 expression, concurrently represses T6SS activity to avoid functional interference between the two systems [62,63]. Investigations of several SPI-2 effector genes confirm their essential role in sustaining bacterial viability inside host cells [64]. Within both epithelial cells and macrophages, the SPI-2 system promotes the formation of Salmonella-induced filamentous structures (SIFs) connected to SCVs (Figure 2C). These structures help shield SCVs from host antimicrobial mechanisms, enhancing immune evasion [65,66]. SPI-2 effectors also suppress host immune signaling pathways, aid nutrient acquisition [67], and inhibit key pro-inflammatory cytokines in macrophages, including IL-1β, IL-8, TNF-α, IL-23α, GM-CSF, and IL-18 [68]. Moreover, effectors such as SopE2 reprogram macrophage metabolism [69]. During this phase, SsrB-mediated repression of Scis silences T6SS, establishing a “T3SS-2 activation – T6SS repression” regulatory axis.

(4) Systemic dissemination phase: contribution of SPI-6 T6SS

Following intracellular establishment, host environmental cues elevate c-di-GMP levels. This second messenger negatively regulates T3SS by binding to chaperone proteins (e.g. CesD, SycD) while relieving H-NS-mediated repression of T6SS [54,70]. In the systemic dissemination phase, the SPI-6 T6SS markedly enhances Salmonella replication within macrophages through coordinated expression of core genes (e.g. sciG, sciS) and accessory clusters (e.g. Cluster 1, Cluster 3) [46]. Late in macrophage infection, structural components such as Hcp and VrgS are strongly upregulated, creating conditions that favor bacterial dissemination via macrophage trafficking (Figure 2D) [62]. Furthermore, the T6SS effector ClpV triggers M1 macrophage activation and promotes a shift from M2 to M1 polarization. Macrophages can differentiate into classically activated M1 macrophages and alternatively activated M2 macrophages, which exhibit distinct immune functions. M1 macrophages promote Th1 responses and exert pro-inflammatory effects, whereas M2 macrophages drive Th2 responses and possess anti-inflammatory and immunoregulatory capabilities [71]. Therefore, the T6SS exacerbates the host inflammatory response, facilitating persistent infection and systemic dissemination of Salmonella [72].

Regulatory roles of Salmonella T3SS on host functions

The T3SS is a highly conserved molecular apparatus that is widely present in various Gram-negative pathogenic and commensal bacteria [73].

Effector molecules mediated by T3SS-2 promote bacterial survival in phagocytes [74]. This system facilitates bacterial replication within the membrane-bound SCVs in host macrophages by secreting a variety of effector proteins [75]. T3SS-2 effectors contribute to the basolateral trafficking of SCVs in apical intestinal epithelial cells during the early stage of infection [76,77].

During infection, multiple effector proteins (e.g. AvrA, SipA) delivered by the T3SS-1 are injected into the host cell (Figure 3A). By interacting with host molecules, these effectors manipulate cellular responses and facilitate bacterial internalization (Figure 3B) [78,79]. Specifically, during epithelial invasion, SipA binds directly to host actin, inducing cytoskeletal rearrangements that are essential for Salmonella’s successful entry into host cells (Figure 3A) [80]. The expression of T3SS-1 is strictly controlled by a sophisticated multi-layered regulatory network that integrates internal genetic information with external environmental signals. At its core lies a positive feedback loop centered on the transcription factor HilA [81]. HilC, HilD, and RtsA directly activate hilA transcription, and HilA can further activate its own expression as well as that of other regulators [82]. It also directly drives the expression of downstream genes such as invF and sicA. InvF, assisted by the chaperone SicA, activates genes encoding structural and effector proteins of the T3SS [83]. This core network is finely modulated by various global regulatory systems. The two-component system BarA/SirA positively regulates T3SS-1 expression by promoting the transcription of non-coding RNAs CsrB/C, which antagonize the inhibitory RNA-binding protein CsrA [84]. Meanwhile, the histone-like protein H-NS broadly suppresses transcription of high A+T-content genes, including those in SPI-1, and this repression can be partially counteracted by the transcriptional regulator LeuO [85,86]. Environmental signals are also accurately sensed and integrated into the regulatory network. Factors such as osmolarity, pH, oxygen, and Mg2 + concentration influence the stability of key regulatory factors through systems like PhoP/Q and PhoB/R [87]. Bile inhibits the expression and activity of T3SS-1 in S. Typhimurium via BarA/SirA pathway [88]. Intestinal metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids, can modulate T3SS-1 expression through BarA/SirA or directly affect HilD stability in S. Typhimurium [89]. Additionally, Lon protease downregulates T3SS-1 expression after bacterial invasion by degrading HilC and HilD, while LoiA activates T3SS-1 by inhibiting lon gene expression [90,91]. Roles and regulatory mechanisms of the T6SS in Salmonella infection and pathogenesis

Wang et al constructed knockout mutants of the Hcp protein family (Hcp1, Hcp2, and Hcp3) in S. Typhimurium [42]. Through in vitro and in vivo experiments, they demonstrated that these proteins promote bacterial invasion, intracellular survival, and replication, directly contributing to virulence [92,93]. The three Hcp subtypes exhibit functional divergence: Hcp1 plays a major role in bacterial competition, motility, and survival within eukaryotic cells, likely through the regulation of flagellar gene expression; Hcp2, despite being highly homologous to Hcp1, performs distinct functions during early murine infection and does not participate in Tae4 binding or antibacterial activity [94,95]; and Hcp3 acts as an auxiliary protein that synergistically enhances the function of Hcp1 [42]. Functional connections also exist among Hcp proteins and other T6SS components. As the tip protein of the T6SS “molecular syringe,” VgrG adopts a closed conformation upon binding to Hcp; this conformational change is thought to regulate effector loading and T6SS assembly through signal transduction [96]. ClpV, a critical ATPase, drives sheath disassembly and recycling via ATP hydrolysis, ensuring continuous T6SS operation. It also facilitates effector delivery by forming complexes with Hcp1 and Tae4 [48,52]. Moreover, ClpV enhances bacterial virulence by inducing gut dysbiosis, M1 macrophage polarization, and activation of the NLRP3 inflammatory pathway, while simultaneously improving bacterial tolerance to environmental stressors such as bile salts and acidic conditions in S. Typhimurium [18,46]. Besides, the T6SS effector Tlde1a possesses dual enzymatic activities, allowing it to cleave the terminal D-alanine of the tetrapeptide stem or replace it with non-canonical D-amino acids. This disrupts cell wall homeostasis in competing bacteria, leading to their elimination. In constrast, the formation of an effector-immunity pair with the immunity protein Tldi1a prevents self-damage [97].

The T6SS is subject to multi-level regulation. Intestinal bile acids can activate the SPI-6 T6SS by promoting the dissociation of H-NS and inducing ClpV expression, thereby energizing the system operation [52]. Under iron-replete conditions, Fe2+ forms a complex with Fur that cooperates with H-NS to repress ClpV transcription; conversely, under iron-limiting conditions, this repression is relieved, leading to T6SS activation (Figure 4) [46]. Additionally, H-NS can directly bind to the SPI-6 gene cluster and silence its expression by blocking RNA polymerase binding [48]. During the early stage of intestinal colonization, the T6SS effector protein Hcp1 is essential for killing Klebsiella in vitro, and this activity is mediated by its specific interaction with the antibacterial amidase Tae4 in S. Typhimurium [52]. ClpV further strengthens the competitive advantage against intestinal commensals, a phenotype that is especially evident under nutrient-limited conditions [18]. In the intracellular infection phase, the T6SS promotes bacterial dissemination by inducing M1 macrophage polarization and activating inflammatory responses [72]. It also impairs intestinal barrier integrity by down-regulating the expression of tight junction proteins, including ZO-1 and Claudin-1, thereby exacerbating epithelial cell damage [18,97,98]. Further studies have also confirmed that the SPI-6 T6SS of S. Typhimurium primarily functions during the early intestinal colonization stage, while its role shifts toward immune regulation during the intracellular phase [52], and the activities of these effector proteins exhibit host specificity, and their mechanisms have been validated in both murine and avian models [99].

Crosstalk mechanisms between T3SS and T6SS secretion systems

The T3SS and T6SS achieve functional synergy and spatiotemporal adaptation through complex genetic regulatory networks [100]. These systems operate within an integrated regulatory framework that involves environmental signal sensing, interactions between core components, and functional balancing mediated by signaling molecules. Collectively, these mechanisms enable sustained bacterial infection within the host. The crosstalk between T3SS and T6SS can be classified into the following categories.

Response regulator-mediated cross-talk between T3SS and T6SS

Salmonella T3SS (SPI-1, SPI-2) and T6SS (SPI-6, SPI-19, SPI-20, SPI-21, SPI-22) share environmental signal-sensing hubs and virulence-regulatory nodes, with their core regulatory components exhibiting interconnected cross-talk. For example, in S. Typhimurium, the two-component system PhoP-PhoQ – which regulates T3SS-1 and T3SS-2 [5], shows bidirectional interaction with the PmrA-PmrB system that controls T6SS: PhoP-PhoQ is essential for PmrA-PmrB expression, while PmrA also influences PhoP expression [22,101]. Inside macrophage phagosomes, acidic pH and low magnesium levels trigger autophosphorylation of PhoQ, which subsequently phosphorylates PhoP (Figure 5A) [102]. PhoQ further activates PhoP at the post-transcriptional level [103], thereby inducing expression of both the T3SS-2 and SPI-6 T6SS. Moreover, studies in S. Enterica have shown that PhoP transcriptionally activates iraP, whose product stabilizes RpoS. Subsequently, the MviA response regulator inhibits transcription of igaA in an RpoS-dependent manner. igaA encodes an inner membrane protein that inhibits excessive activation of the Rcs regulatory system in enteric bacteria [104], thereby indirectly modulating RcsB, a major regulator of the T6SS (Figure 5A). The SPI-19 T6SS is a virulence factor present in only a few serotypes of Salmonella, and S. Dublin, S. Gallinarum and S. Pullorum all harbor this virulence factor [44]. It is known that the SPI-19 T6SS encoded by SPI-19 is involved in the infection and colonization of S. Gallinarum in the host [106]. Previous transcriptome analyses have shown that the expression levels of genes associated with the T3SS are significantly downregulated in the ΔSPI-19 mutant, which suggests that the function of SPI-19 T6SS is closely related to the regulation of T3SS gene expression [107]. To further identify the SPI-19 genes that regulate T3SS gene expression, we constructed mutants of the genes encoding potential regulatory factors (RS09140 and RS09275) or Sel1-like proteins (RS09150 and RS09155), and the results revealed that the deletion of these two Sel1-like protein-encoding genes leads to the downregulation of T3SS gene expression and a reduction in the bacterial colonization ability and virulence in chickens. Therefore, the two Sel1-like protein-encoding genes (RS09150 and RS09155) are involved in the subsequent bacterial infection of avian cells and survival in macrophages by modulating the expression of T3SS regulatory factors, primarily SPI-1-associated genes [107]. Based on the above evidence, we conclude that the two Sel1-like proteins encoded by RS09150 and RS09155 in SPI-19 of S. Pullorum participate in the bacterial infection of chickens by mediating the expression of T3SS genes, which indicates a potential regulatory crosstalk between SPI-19 T6SS and T3SS in Salmonella.

Temporal crosstalk between T3SS and T6SS through cascade regulation

The functional switching between T3SS and T6SS depends on sequential activation through cascade regulation. The PhoP/PhoQ two-component system controls the expression of Spi/SSA genes inside macrophages by regulating the SsrB/SpiR system [108]. SsrB is a transcriptional activator of T3SS-2, while SpiR serves as its cognate sensor kinase (also called SsrA). Spi/SSA is a unique T3SS that operates exclusively during S. Enterica invasion of eukaryotic cells. Expression of the Spi/Ssa system and its effectors relies on SsrB/SpiR [109]. PhoP not only regulates transcription of the response regulator SsrB but also binds directly to the ssrB promoter [108]. Furthermore, SsrB activates multiple T3SS-2 promoters and counteracts H-NS–mediated silencing, thereby regulating the T3SS encoded within SPI-2 [110]. Simultaneously, SsrB cooperates with PhoP to modulate SPI-6 T6SS expression [62]. PhoP also negatively regulates the hilA gene, a key activator of T3SS-1), thus suppressing the SPI-1 function [111]. In addition, studies in S. Enterica have shown that SsrB downregulates sciS, which encodes an inner-membrane component of T6SS (Figure 5B) [112]. Using a Boolean model of the T3SS-T6SS regulatory network, Chandrani Das et al. revealed an antagonistic relationship between the two systems inside macrophages: the major T6SS inner-membrane component SciS is activated only when SsrAB is turned off. In other words, T6SS is repressed during active SPI-2 expression and is induced only when SPI-2 is shut down. SsrAB and SciS detect similar environmental cues – such as low osmolarity and low levels of magnesium and calcium – through distinct pathways (directly or indirectly), enabling the sequential activation of T3SS-2 and T6SS in the macrophage microenvironment [21]. This temporal control mechanism facilitates functional switching of secretion systems across different stages of infection.

Signaling molecule-mediated crosstalk between T3SS and T6SS

The activities of the T3SS and T6SS are coordinated through a functional balancing mechanism mediated by signaling molecules. Research has demonstrated that the diguanylate cyclase (DGC) Yeaj, in conjunction with the quorum-sensing signal autoinducer-2 (AI-2), promotes the synthesis of cyclic diguanylate monophosphate (c-di-GMP). Additionally, intestinal bile salt signals further enhance intracellular c-di-GMP accumulation, which subsequently represses the expression of T3SS-1 genes in S. Typhimurium. Moreover, c-di-GMP negatively regulates T3SS function by binding to T3SS chaperone proteins such as CesD, SycD, and LcrH [54]. In parallel, c-di-GMP interacts with the global regulator H-NS, alleviating its repression of T6SS gene expression (Figure 5C) [70]. Collectively, these findings establish that T3SS and T6SS engage in molecular crosstalk via the second messenger c-di-GMP.

Conclusion and prospect

Not only do T3SS and T6SS each play crucial roles in Salmonella invasion, colonization, infection, and immune evasion, but they also achieve functional synergy through an elaborate gene regulatory network. Two-component systems such as PhoP-PhoQ and PmrA-PmrB serve as core signaling hubs. They enable temporally controlled activation via key regulatory factors such as SsrB and MviA, while finely balancing functional switching in response to signaling molecules like c-di-GMP and H-NS – adapting to the environmental demands of different infection stages. Throughout the four phases of intestinal infection, T3SS and T6SS collaborate efficiently: during the pre-colonization phase, T6SS mediates the competitive elimination of intestinal commensal microbiota; in the colonization phase, T3SS-1 facilitates epithelial cell invasion; in the intracellular phase, T3SS-2 ensures intracellular survival and proliferation; and in the late stage of infection, T6SS promotes systemic dissemination. Together, these systems form an integrated pathogenic cascade that drives Salmonella through the complete infection process.

Although the molecular mechanisms of T3SS and T6SS have been partly elucidated, several key scientific questions remain to be addressed. The PhoP-PhoQ two-component system can regulate the expression of T3SS pathogenicity islands by modulating the SsrB/SpiR pathway, and PhoP-PhoQ is also essential for controlling the T6SS-associated PmrA-PmrB system. We therefore hypothesize that, after modulating PmrA-PmrB expression, the PhoP-PhoQ system may still require an unidentified activator to directly regulate the expression of T6SS pathogenicity islands.

Regarding synergistic regulation, further work is needed to resolve uncharacterized crosstalk nodes within the signaling network and to improve our understanding of how different environmental factors influence these systems and how these systems respond to them. At the functional level, research should focus on the cooperative mechanisms among Hcp protein family members (Hcp1, Hcp2, and Hcp3), as well as the interactions between T6SS effector proteins and the host immune system – both important avenues for elucidating bacterial intracellular survival strategies. In addition, regarding the interaction between SPI-19 T6SS and T3SS in Salmonella, the specific mechanisms by which the two aforementioned Sel1-like proteins promote the expression of T3SS-associated genes remain to be elucidated.

An indepth analysis of the synergistic mechanisms between T3SS and T6SS in Salmonella holds profound implications for developing novel antibacterial agents and for designing strategies to reduce and replace antibiotics. In the development of new anti-Salmonella therapeutics, current insights into T3SS and T6SS regulatory mechanisms provide a foundation for designing antibodies or small-molecule drugs that target key components of these systems. For instance, antibodies directed against T3SS and T6SS could neutralize bacterial effector proteins, prevent host cell invasion, and thereby inhibit Salmonella colonization and infection. Moreover, pharmacological inhibition of essential T3SS and T6SS genes could effectively disrupt Salmonella pathogenicity and attenuate virulence. Such targeted agents not only offer high specificity and low side-effect profiles in treating Salmonella infections – potentially improving therapeutic outcomes – but also act on virulence mechanisms rather than directly killing bacteria. This approach avoids exerting selective pressure on bacterial survival, thereby mitigating the broad-spectrum activity and drug-resistance issues associated with conventional antibiotics.
