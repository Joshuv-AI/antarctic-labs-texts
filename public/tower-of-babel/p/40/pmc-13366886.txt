Evaluation the effectiveness of the multimodal situational blended teaching model in higher vocational pediatric nursing education: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Xuejun Xiao, Ying Xie, Xiaohong Dai, Lijuan Li, Xiaoji Huang, Wanchao Wang, Yan Gao, Lingzhi She, Xiangfeng Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith continuous advances in healthcare technologies and the growing societal demand for high-quality pediatric nursing care, the limitations of traditional lecture-based teaching methods in pediatric nursing education for higher vocational students have become increasingly evident. Teacher-centered, lecture-based instruction may be insufficient in fostering students’ learning motivation, cultivating positive learning values, promoting active learning attitudes, and supporting the development of critical thinking skills. To address these challenges, this study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of a multimodal situational blended teaching model in higher vocational pediatric nursing course, with the goal of providing practical insights for improving the quality of nursing talent development.MethodsA quasi-experimental study was conducted using convenience sampling among two consecutive cohorts (2022 and 2023) of students enrolled in the three-year higher vocational nursing program at Guilin Medical University, China. The 2022 cohort served as the control group and received traditional lecture-based instruction, while the 2023 cohort constituted the intervention group and was taught using a multimodal situational blended teaching model. At the end of the semester, data on academic performance, learning values, learning engagement, critical thinking abilities, and satisfaction with course reform were collected via an online questionnaire administered through Wenjuanxing and analyzed quantitatively.ResultsThe intervention group demonstrated significantly higher scores than the control group in academic performance, learning values, learning engagement, and critical thinking abilities (all p < 0.05). They also reported significantly greater satisfaction with four dimensions of the course reform: overall satisfaction with the course delivery, satisfaction with the design and organization of practical components, satisfaction with the availability and quality of learning resources, and satisfaction with the effectiveness of teacher-student interactions and feedback mechanisms (all p < 0.05).ConclusionsThe multimodal situational blended teaching model was associated with better learning outcomes and higher satisfaction in higher vocational pediatric nursing education. However, the cross-sectional design and self-reported data preclude causal inference; longitudinal studies are needed to assess its long-term impact on competency development.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04653-5.

Introduction

During the “14th Five-Year Plan” period, one of the fundamental principles guiding the development of nursing services in China highlights the necessity of increasing the provision of maternal and child healthcare services. This initiative aims to accelerate the recruitment and training of pediatric nursing professionals to address the current shortage of qualified personnel in this critical area [1]. With the full implementation of China’ s three-child policy in 2021, there has been a significantly increase in public demand for professionalize child care services. In particular, society has placed higher expectations on the professional qualifications of child care personnel and the standardization of service quality [2]. Pediatric nursing, as a core discipline in nursing programs at higher vocational colleges, aims to cultivate high-quality technical and skill professional equipped with spcialized knowledge and practical competencies in caring for individuals during the early-life stage [3]. Due to constraints such as limited instructional hours, heavy content load, and the strong emphasis on clinical practice, pediatric nursing courses in Chinese higher vocational colleges currently still predominantly rely on the traditional lecture-centered classroom model [4]. Although student-centered teaching approaches such as problem-based learning (PBL) and the flipped classroom are becoming mainstreem in medical education reform, lecture-based learning (LBL) remains irreplaceable for the efficicent transmission of foundational medical knowledge. Its structured and systematic delivery is particularly suited to large-class settings and institutions with relatively limited technological resources [5]. Moreover, face-to-face lecturing not only facilitates immediate techer - student interaction and the timely clarification of misunderstandings, but also conveys professional values implicitly through instructors’ nonverbal communication and vivid clinical narratives, thereby effectively fostering the development of a strong professional identity among medical students [6, 7]. However, because traditional lecture-based teaching is primarily characterized by one-way knowledge transmission and insufficient instructor - student interaction [8], it is poorly suited to cultivated higher-order competencies such as critical thinking [9], clinical adaptability [10] and humanistic caring [11]. This limitation is particularly pronounced in nursing education, which emphasizes emotional engagement and contextual responsiveness. Paediatric nursing depends heavily on core competencies such as empathic understanding, emotional support, and family communication, key skills that cannot be adequately cultivated in a classroom environment dominated by didactic knowledge transmission [12, 13]. Given that higher vocational pediatric nursing programmes is to cultive prcatice-oriented, technically skilled personnals for grassroots healthcare [14], its core lies in achieving deep integration between practical competencies and humanistic care. There is an urgent need to transform the teaching paradigm to overcome the structural limitations of traditional instruction in fostering higher-order professional attributes.

Blended learning, by seamlessly integrating online self-directed study with face to face interactive practice, synergistically combines the immediacy and immersion of traditional in-person instruction with the flexibility and adaptability of online learning [15], effectively bridging the gap between knowledge acquisition and competency development. Rooted in constructivist learning theory [16]and Kolb’ s experiential learning cycle [17], this pedagogical model positions learners to construct meaning actively through experience, reflection, and collaboration within authentic or simulated contexts. In medical education, empirical studies have demonstrated that blended learning not only significantly enhances student engagement [18], but also effectively fosters the development of critical thinking [19], self-directed learning [20], and interpersonal skills [21], thereby improving overall learning outcomes [22]. Therefore, by reconfiguring the instructional process of “input-internalization-application, ” blended learning effectively advances the transition from knowledge transmission to competency generation.

Despite its demonstrated potential in medical education, blended learning remains insufficient for cultivating the core competencies required in pediatric nursing professionals if it lacks dynamic responsiveness to the complexities of clinical situations and deep integration of the affective dimensions inherent in care. Unlike adult patients, children exhibit unique physiological and psychological characteristics, often manifesting as limited verbal expression, heightened emotional sensitivity, and extensive family involvement [13]. These features place demands on nursing students that extend beyond technical proficiency, requiring competencies such as empathetic recognition, crisis de-escalation, and effective communication with multiple stakeholders, including the child and their family members [23]. To address the limitations of current blended learning models in terms of situational authenticity and affective engagement, systematically integrating multimodal situational elements, such as standardized patients, virtual simulations, scenario-based audiovisual materials, and role-playing [24] into the blended learning framework has emerged as a key strategy for enhancing both instructional immersion and clinical authenticity. Such an integrated instructional design creates a highly realistic and interactive learning environment that enables students to develop critical thinking skills [25], self-directed learning and innovation capacities [26] through simulated practice, while simultaneously strengthening instructor - student and peer-to-peer interactions [27]. In turn, overall teaching effectiveness is enhanced and faculty professional development is fostered [28, 29]. It is evident, therefore, that while blended learning optimizes instructional structure, multimodal situation enhances the authenticity of learning content. Their synergistic integration offers a systematic response to the demand in higher vocational pediatric nursing education for cultivating “practice-oriented plus humanistic” composite competencies, thereby furnishing a more targeted practical pedagogical model for curricular reform.

Materials and methods

Design and participants

A quasi-experimental design with non-concurrent intact cohorts was adopted, as random assignment was not feasible in the current higher vocational education context due to ethical, administrative, and practical constraints. The Institutional Ethics Committee explicitly opposed offering the intervention to only a subset of students, citing principles of educational equity and the vocational education mandate of “universal competency attainment. ” Furthermore, students were uniformly assigned by the Academic Affairs Office to fixed cohorts in accordance with the official talent development curriculum; re-randomization was administratively unfeasible and prone to intervention contamination through peer interaction. Compounding these challenges, large class sizes (approximately 80 students per cohort), fixed credit hours, and limited teaching resources rendered the implementation of parallel randomized arms particularly difficult. This design adheres to the SQUIRE 2.0 guidelines for reporting educational interventions in real-world health professions education [30].

This study employed a convenience sampling approach to recruit participants from two consecutive cohorts of three-year higher vocational nursing students at the College of Nursing, Guilin Medical University, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, China. The two cohorts were comparable in key aspects, including admission criteria, curriculum design, allocation of teaching resources, and course assessment methods, with no statistically significant differences observed (all p > 0.05).

Inclusion criteria were as follows: ① Enrollment as a full-time higher vocational nursing student; ② Voluntary participation in the teaching activities and data collection procedures associated with this study; ③ Possession of basic learning capacity and language comprehension skills sufficient to complete course-related tasks independently. Exclusion criteria included: ① Non-full-time student status; ② Prolonged absence from class due to illness, clinical placement, or other reasons, resulting in inability to fully engage in the teaching process; ③ Presence of cognitive impairments or severe psychological conditions that could adversely affect learning outcomes; ④ Concurrent involvement in other pedagogical reform initiatives that might confound the study results.

Group assignment was not based on individual selection or researcher-imposed intervention. Instead, two intact student cohorts were formed according to students’ natural year of enrollment and the official academic schedule established by the University Academic Affairs Office. All participants followed the institutionally mandated 2022 Talent Development Plan for Higher Vocational Nursing Students throughout their coursework. Specifically: The control group comprised 172 students from the 2022 cohort (enrolled in September 2022), who took the Pediatric Nursing course between March and June 2024 under the conventional teaching approach; The intervention group consisted of 171 students from the 2023 cohort (enrolled in September 2023), who took the same course during the identical academic period (March to June 2025), receiving the multimodal situational blended teaching model.

Throughout the study period (2024–2025), the nursing program at our institution maintained high stability in its curriculum structure, teaching faculty, and allocation of instructional resources. No major educational policy changes or structural reforms were implemented during this time, thereby minimizing the potential influence of time-related confounding factors on the study outcomes.

This study adhered strictly to ethical research principles. Although Pediatric Nursing is a compulsory course in the nursing curriculum, participation in all research-related data collection activities, including questionnaire surveys and psychometric scale assessments, was entirely voluntary. All students were explicitly informed prior to enrollment that declining to participate would have no adverse impact on their course grades, academic evaluations, or relationships with instructors. Written informed consent was obtained from every participant. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Guilin Medical University (Approval No.: GLM20241001).

Teaching implementation process

Control group

Students in the 2022 cohort were taught using a traditional lecture-based approach, in which instructors systematically delivered theoretical content and demonstrated nursing procedures. Clinical case scenarios were integrated into the lectures to stimulate classroom discussion and foster students’ critical thinking skills. Additionally, immediately following each focused instructional micro-unit (e.g., after a mini-lecture on “complications of ventricular septal defect”), a brief clinical scenario-based quiz (taking approximately 2–3 min to complete) was administered in real time via Rain Classroom [31], a widely adopted mobile learning platform in Chinese higher education. Students were required to apply newly acquired knowledge to analyze the scenario or make clinical decisions, thereby enabling real-time assessment of their conceptual understanding and ability to apply core knowledge in simulated clinical contexts.

Theoretical Foundation Traditional education often focuses solely on the impact of specific types of data on learners, neglecting signals from other data modalities. Relying on a single sensory channel for information acquisition typically results in a partial understanding of reality, potentially leading to the phenomenon of “blind men feeling an elephant” [32]. In contrast, multimodal theory emphasizes the effective integration of information from various modalities, such as visual, auditory, and tactile by cross-referencing learning materials across these modalities. This integrative approach enables learners to construct more comprehensive and multidimensional cognitive representations. Moreover, the application of multimodal semiotics, such as images, language, and digital resources, in teaching enhances knowledge visualization, increases information density, and stimulates multiple senses. By situating authentic educational issues within a data-driven paradigm, this approach facilitates a re-examination and reinterpretation of pedagogical practices. It also triggers internal associative learning, promoting deeper and more holistic knowledge acquisition. Consequently, it supports cognitive development and knowledge internalization, effectively enhancing learners’ perception of clinical contexts, strengthening role immersion, and fostering empathy as well as ethical professional dispositions [33].Teaching Process Based on multimodal theory, the research team implemented the multimodal situational blended teaching model in the Pediatric Nursing course for Grade 2023 nursing students at the higher vocational level. Evaluation metrics

At the end of the semester, the following outcomes were compared between the two groups: academic performance, scores on learning values, levels of student engagement, and critical thinking abilities. Additionally, course satisfaction was assessed exclusively among students in the intervention group.

Academic Performance An equal weight (50% each) was assigned to the final theoretical examination and the situational case-based practical examination. The final theoretical examination (maximum score: 100) consisted of 40 points of multiple-choice questions assessing students’ recall and comprehension of foundational knowledge, and 60 points of short-answer and case-analysis questions evaluating clinical reasoning and problem-solving abilities. The exam was aligned with national curriculum standards and nurse licensure examination requirements. All students completed the 120-minute closed-book test under standardized proctoring conditions. Scoring was independently conducted by two instructors using a pre-defined rubric under double-blind conditions, with cross-verification to enhance objectivity and inter-rater reliability. The scenario-based case examination aimed to evaluate students’ clinical competence and professional competence in a simulated real-world healthcare setting. Students were assigned to small groups (3–4 members per group, based on student ID numbers) to manage an identical standardized pediatric clinical scenario. The case, including patient presentation, clinical challenges, and expected nursing interventions, was consistent across all groups. Assessment conditions were strictly controlled, with uniform time limits, simulation environments, and examiner instructions. Trained assessors rated each group’s performance using a structured scoring rubric that addressed teamwork, procedural accuracy, clinical judgment, and execution quality. All scoring was performed under blinded conditions to minimize bias and ensure fairness. Academic Performance An equal weight (50% each) was assigned to the final theoretical examination and the situational case-based practical examination. The final theoretical examination (maximum score: 100) consisted of 40 points of multiple-choice questions assessing students’ recall and comprehension of foundational knowledge, and 60 points of short-answer and case-analysis questions evaluating clinical reasoning and problem-solving abilities. The exam was aligned with national curriculum standards and nurse licensure examination requirements. All students completed the 120-minute closed-book test under standardized proctoring conditions. Scoring was independently conducted by two instructors using a pre-defined rubric under double-blind conditions, with cross-verification to enhance objectivity and inter-rater reliability. The scenario-based case examination aimed to evaluate students’ clinical competence and professional competence in a simulated real-world healthcare setting. Statistical analysis

All data were collected using Wenjuanxing [45] (https://www.wjx.cn), a widely adopted online survey platform in China that supports anonymous responses and structured data export. Students in both the intervention and control groups completed the questionnaire within seven days after the conclusion of the Pediatric Nursing course, during a designated class session via WeChat login. This standardized procedure ensured consistency across groups in terms of timing, technical environment, and administration protocol. The approach aligns with our institution’ s digital education policy and effectively safeguards data anonymity, integrity, and inter-group comparability.

A total of 343 questionnaires were distributed by the researchers, and all 343 were returned, yielding a 100% response rate. After coding, the data were independently entered into WPS Spreadsheets by two researchers and cross-verified for accuracy. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All tests were two-tailed, with a significance level set at α = 0.05.

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Demographic characteristics following a normal distribution are reported as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD); categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Similarly, academic performance, as well as total and subscale scores for Learning Values, Learning Engagement and critical thinking abilities, are expressed as Mean ± SD. Between-group comparisons of demographic variables were conducted using independent-samples t-tests (for normally distributed continuous variables) or chi-square tests (for categorical variables). Group differences in total academic performance, Learning Values, Learning Engagement and Critical Thinking Abilities scores were analyzed using independent-samples t-tests. For non-normally distributed variables, such as course reform satisfaction, the Mann–Whitney U test was employed for between-group comparisons.

Discussion

Academic performance at course completion

At the end of the Pediatric Nursing course, students in the intervention group scored significantly higher than those in the control group on the final theoretical examination, situational case-based practical examination, and academic performance. These findings suggest that, compared to conventional instruction, the multimodal situational blended teaching model may be associated with more favorable academic outcomes. Notably, similar pedagogical innovations have shown promising trends in vocational nursing education. For example, Chen et al. [46] reported that implementing blended instruction supported by information technology can facilitate students‘ mastery of both theoretical and practical knowledge—a direction largely consistent with the findings of the present study.

It should be emphasized that this study employed a post-only design, with no baseline data collected on students‘ academic performance prior to the intervention. Therefore, although statistically significant between-group differences were observed at the endpoint, we cannot determine whether these reflect genuine “improvement” or “progress”, nor can we rule out the influence of other potential confounding factors, despite the groups being comparable in baseline demographic characteristics. Moreover, participation in any structured nursing curriculum would be expected to yield some degree of learning gain, representing general learning progression. The relatively higher scores in the intervention group may thus indicate potential added value of this teaching approach beyond standard instruction, though such an interpretation warrants caution.

A related concern is whether the observed performance advantage might stem primarily from transient memory effects facilitated by multimodal presentation (e.g., videos, images), rather than deeper learning. However, it should be noted that the academic performance in this study incorporated a high-stakes, delayed summative assessment—the final theoretical examination, which accounted for 50% of the academic performance. This exam was administered at the end of the semester and included 40 multiple-choice questions and 7 integrated clinical case scenarios requiring students to interpret complex symptoms, prioritize nursing diagnoses, and formulate evidence-based interventions. The design of these items explicitly targeted higher-order cognitive skills such as conceptual integration and clinical reasoning, rather than simple recall of recently presented information. The fact that the intervention group significantly outperformed the control group on this delayed, high-weight, and cognitively demanding component (p < 0.05) suggests that the observed advantage likely reflects more durable and meaningful learning, rather than short-term memory retention alone.

It is plausible that certain elements of the instructional design contributed, at least in part, to the observed performance advantage in the intervention group. Instructors delivered abstract theoretical content through multiple modalities, including text, video, images, audio, electronic devices, and physical models, enabling more intuitive and vivid presentation of complex concepts [28]. Compared to traditional single-modality instruction, which primarily relies on verbal or textual input, this multimodal approach may provide richer perceptual stimuli and thereby support students’ comprehension of challenging material. Furthermore, clinical cases were integrated into the teaching process, complemented by role-playing and virtual simulation technologies, to create learning contexts that approximate real-world clinical settings. Such strategies may help students connect theoretical knowledge with practical application, aligning with principles of experiential learning [25]. Additionally, the blended teaching model was implemented throughout the entire course. Instructors utilized online platforms to deliver learning resources and monitor student engagement, which may have partially addressed the limited interaction commonly observed in traditional face-to-face classrooms [47]. Collectively, these components fostered a learning environment conducive to deep processing and transfer across contexts, potentially accounting for the intervention group’ s superior performance on higher-order cognitive tasks.

Differences in learning values, learning engagement and critical thinking abilities at course completion

At the end of the course, students in the intervention group scored higher than those in the control group on measures of learning values, learning engagement, and critical thinking abilities. However, it should be noted that both groups demonstrated generally positive levels across all these indicators. This pattern aligns with findings from prior studies in vocational nursing education [39, 48]. Importantly, because baseline data were not collected, we cannot determine whether either group experienced within-group changes over time, nor can we attribute the observed between-group differences at posttest to genuine developmental gains. The findings should therefore be interpreted as cross-sectional associations rather than evidence of actual skill development. For example, although both groups scored above the scale midpoint on the critical thinking scale [43], indicating generally favorable self-perceived dispositions—the higher scores in the intervention group may more likely reflect greater perceived relevance or confidence in applying knowledge within clinical contexts, rather than actual improvement in objective reasoning ability. Given that assessment relied entirely on self-report questionnaires and lacked corroborating evidence from qualitative interviews or performance-based tasks, conclusions regarding substantive development of critical thinking must be made with considerable caution.

The observed between-group differences may be partially attributable to specific features of the intervention design. Prior to class, a variety of digital resources were provided via an online platform; their flexibility and personalization may have supported students‘ autonomous preparation [49]. During face-to-face sessions, instructors integrated multimedia elements, such as videos, models, and audio, with authentic clinical cases to create rich, context-based learning experiences [50]. After class, students engaged in media-supported social practice, applying learned knowledge to real-world scenarios, which may have reinforced knowledge retention and encouraged sustained engagement [51]. Collectively, these components offered multiple representational formats and opportunities for active knowledge application, which could enhance students’ perceived value of the learning content and deepen cognitive involvement, potentially explaining, at least in part, why the intervention group reported more favorable outcomes on these self-report measures.

Notably, despite the theoretical advantages of this multimodal situational blended teaching approach, its practical implementation encountered several real-world challenges. Some students reported initial difficulties adapting to frequent transitions among online asynchronous activities, face-to-face role-playing, and virtual simulations, particularly in the absence of clear transitional guidance, which sometimes led to cognitive overload or ambiguity about learning objectives. This phenomenon has also been documented in other studies on multimodal instruction [52]. Additionally, occasional technical issues with the virtual simulation platform (e.g., login failures, interaction delays, or video lag) briefly disrupted instructional flow and affected the continuity of some students‘ participation. From the instructor‘ s perspective, ensuring coherence in content and consistency in pedagogical logic across different modalities required substantial time investment in cross-platform resource integration, activity sequencing, and contingency planning. These practical complexities suggest that the effectiveness of multimodal instruction depends not only on the sophistication of its theoretical design but also on reliable technical infrastructure, well-structured instructional scaffolding, and mutual adaptation by both teachers and students to the blended format. Without such supporting conditions, even an innovative pedagogical model may struggle to translate its potential advantages into tangible learning outcomes.

Potential explanations for between-group differences in course satisfaction

At the end of the course, students in the intervention group reported significantly higher self-rated satisfaction than those in the control group across all four dimensions: overall satisfaction with the course delivery, satisfaction with the design and organization of practical components, satisfaction with the availability and quality of learning resources, and satisfaction with the effectiveness of teacher-student interactions and feedback mechanisms (p < 0.05). This between-group difference may be attributable to systematic variations in instructional design between the two approaches.

Specifically, the multimodal situational blended teaching model integrated online resources, virtual simulations, and offline practical activities, offering a more diverse range of learning experiences. This diversity may better accommodate individual learning preferences and paces, thereby enhancing students‘ subjective perception of overall course delivery [46]. In practical sessions, simulating real or virtual clinical scenarios allowed students to practice in environments closely resembling actual clinical settings; such design may have strengthened their perceived relevance and utility of the practical content, which could account for their higher satisfaction ratings in this domain [52]. Furthermore, intervention group students had flexible access to structured learning materials on the online platform, a feature that may have been interpreted as improved resource availability and quality. By sharing high-quality teaching materials through the platform, instructors also partially mitigated the limitations of relatively scarce resources typically associated with traditional lecture-based instruction. Regarding interaction and feedback, the model combined multiple channels, including online discussion forums, instant messaging, and offline assessments such as skill evaluations, to establish more frequent and diversified teacher–student communication. Students may thus have received more timely and personalized feedback, leading to higher ratings of interaction effectiveness. In contrast, traditional lecture-based teaching primarily relied on in-class questioning or post-class assignments, resulting in less frequent and less individualized feedback, which may have influenced satisfaction scores.

Nevertheless, it should be noted that satisfaction is a subjective measure susceptible to expectancy effects, novelty bias, or social desirability. The study did not assess students‘ prior attitudes toward the pedagogical reform nor control for potential short-term positive biases arising from the novelty of the new approach. Therefore, the observed between-group differences should be interpreted as reflections of students‘ perceptions within a specific instructional context, rather than as comprehensive evidence of the long-term value or generalizability of the teaching model.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, a post-test-only quasi-experimental design was employed, comparing two consecutive academic cohorts. Although baseline characteristics were comparable and key instructional elements, such as instructors, curriculum, and assessments were held constant, unmeasured time-varying confounders cannot be excluded. Second, the study was conducted at a single higher vocational nursing college in China, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Third, while the self-developed scales demonstrated good internal consistency and content validity, their broader psychometric properties warrant further validation. Critically, reflective assignments were not formally scored, and no delayed post-tests or standardized instruments (e.g., the California Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory) were used, making it difficult to differentiate short-term performance from sustained learning gains. Moreover, the proposed mechanisms, such as multimodal resources enhancing engagement or clinical simulations facilitating knowledge application, lack empirical support from process data (e.g., learning analytics or qualitative insights) and remain largely theoretical. As noted in the manuscript, deeper conceptual integration likely requires sustained metacognitive scaffolding beyond a single semester.

Future multi-site, longitudinal studies incorporating baseline assessments, follow-up evaluations, and process-oriented measures are needed to rigorously evaluate the long-term impact of this instructional model.

Conclusions

This study found that, at the end of the pediatric nursing course, students receiving multimodal situational blended instruction scored higher than those in the traditional lecture group on measures of academic performance, learning values, learning engagement, critical thinking abilities, and course satisfaction. These differences may reflect the model’ s potential to support diverse and contextually grounded learning experiences. However, given the absence of baseline data and reliance on self-reported measures, the findings should be interpreted as cross-sectional associations rather than evidence of causal impact or long-term effectiveness. Future research employing longitudinal designs and mixed methods is needed to examine its influence on deep learning and sustained competency development.
