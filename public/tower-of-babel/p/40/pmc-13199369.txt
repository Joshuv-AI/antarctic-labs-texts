Awareness motivations and barriers related to rhinoplasty among adults in Palestine, Syria, Lebanon, Jordan and Iraq
Authors: Salahaldeen Deeb, Alhareth M. Amro, Hamdah Hanifa, Husam Amarin, Hazar Dakkak, Yahya Kayed AbuJwaid, Mayar Z. Deeb, Anas K. Assi, Mohammed Ehmidat, Menan Alaa Alazawi, Sawsan Ahmad Al-Qeam, Deema Alkassar, Razan Moghnieh, Yousif Ammar Abbas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rhinoplasty is among the most commonly performed cosmetic surgical procedures worldwide, yet public awareness, motivations, and perceived barriers related to the procedure remain insufficiently characterized in Middle Eastern populations. This study aimed to evaluate awareness, motivations, perceived barriers, and prior rhinoplasty experience among adults across five Levantine countries. A cross-sectional, web-based survey was conducted between January and October 2025 among adults aged 18 years and older residing in Palestine, Syria, Iraq, Jordan, and Lebanon. Participants were recruited through snowball sampling via social media platforms. An Arabic questionnaire adapted for a multicountry Levantine sample was used to assess sociodemographic characteristics, prior rhinoplasty experience, knowledge, information sources, motivations, and perceived barriers. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, one-way analysis of variance, and multivariable logistic regression to examine factors associated with prior rhinoplasty. A total of 4,238 participants were included (mean age 21.8 ± 2.0 years; 2,882 females [68.0%] and 1,356 males [32.0%]). The overall prevalence of prior rhinoplasty was 13.4%, with significant cross-country variation (p < 0.001), highest in Iraq (25.7%) and lowest in Palestine (3.7%). The mean knowledge score was 8.15 ± 1.13, with statistically significant but numerically small differences across countries (p < 0.001). Participants rated therapeutic motives more highly than cosmetic motives (3.97 ± 1.07 vs. 3.29 ± 1.15; p < 0.001). Perceived barriers included financial barriers only (8.4%), social barriers only (11.4%), and both financial and social barriers (15.1%), with significant intercountry differences (p < 0.001). In the adjusted analysis, country of residence, age, and knowledge score were associated with prior rhinoplasty. This multi-country cross-sectional study found significant variation in awareness, motivations, perceived barriers, and prior rhinoplasty across respondents from five Levantine countries. Although awareness was generally high, specific misconceptions persisted, and therapeutic motives were more strongly endorsed than cosmetic motives. Because the study used a cross-sectional design and non-probability online sampling, the findings should be interpreted as associations within the study sample rather than as causal effects or nationally representative estimates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46839-6.

Introduction

Rhinoplasty is one of the most frequently performed cosmetic surgical procedures worldwide and is undertaken for both aesthetic refinement and correction of functional nasal deformities1. Beyond its reconstructive role, rhinoplasty has important psychological and social dimensions, as it may influence self-perception, self-esteem, and overall quality of life2. In recent years, demand for aesthetic surgery has increased across Middle Eastern populations, where facial appearance often carries substantial sociocultural significance and rhinoplasty has emerged as a particularly common form of cosmetic intervention3.

Despite this growing interest, public awareness, motivations, and perceived barriers related to rhinoplasty remain insufficiently characterized in Arab and Levantine populations. Existing literature, much of it derived from Western or single-country settings, suggests that interest in rhinoplasty is shaped by a combination of therapeutic motivations, such as improving nasal breathing or correcting deformity, and cosmetic motivations related to facial appearance, attractiveness, and self-confidence4. In Levantine settings, these considerations may further interact with sociocultural expectations, religious values, and socioeconomic circumstances, all of which may influence attitudes toward cosmetic procedures as well as perceptions of their indications, risks, and potential benefits5.

Social media and online health platforms have become increasingly important sources of information about cosmetic surgery, particularly among younger adults. Although these channels can improve access to medical and aesthetic information, they may also disseminate inaccurate or non-evidence-based content, thereby contributing to misconceptions and unrealistic expectations regarding surgical safety, outcomes, and candidacy6. Accordingly, understanding how informational sources, knowledge, and perceived barriers relate to attitudes toward rhinoplasty is important for supporting evidence-based education and ethically grounded, patient-centered counseling7.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate awareness, motivations, perceived barriers, and prior experience related to rhinoplasty among adults across five Levantine countries: Palestine, Iraq, Jordan, Lebanon, and Syria. By comparing cross-country patterns in knowledge, information sources, motivations, barriers, and self-reported prior rhinoplasty, this study seeks to characterize factors associated with rhinoplasty-related decision-making in the region. These findings may help inform culturally sensitive awareness efforts, clinical counseling, and future research on aesthetic surgery in Levantine populations.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This study employed a cross-sectional, web-based design to assess awareness, motivations, perceived barriers, and prior experience related to rhinoplasty across five Levantine countries. Data were collected between January and October 2025 using a structured, self-administered questionnaire created on Google Forms. Participants were recruited through snowball sampling via social media platforms, including WhatsApp, Facebook, and Instagram. Although this strategy facilitated rapid dissemination across multiple settings, it may have preferentially reached younger, highly educated, and digitally connected individuals. No financial or non-financial incentives were offered for participation.

Participants

Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years and older who were able to read the survey language, provide electronic informed consent, and complete the online questionnaire. No upper age cap was imposed; therefore, the observed age range in the final sample reflects the recruitment pattern of the online snowball sampling strategy rather than an eligibility restriction. The survey targeted residents of Palestine, Syria, Iraq, Jordan, and Lebanon. Country eligibility was determined using a self-reported country-of-residence item included in the questionnaire, and responses from outside the five target countries were excluded from the final dataset. Duplicate or clearly invalid submissions were removed during data screening. To minimize erroneous responses, the survey required participants to explicitly select their country of residence from a predefined list of the five target countries. Although this approach helped restrict responses geographically, country of residence remained self-reported and could not be independently verified. Records with three or more missing items were excluded. After excluding 24 incomplete responses, 4,238 participants were included in the final analysis: Syria (n = 960), Iraq (n = 934), Jordan (n = 848), Palestine (n = 812), and Lebanon (n = 684).

Questionnaire development and translation

The questionnaire was adapted from the instrument reported by Tawalbeh et al.8, which assessed awareness, sources of information, motivational factors, and barriers related to rhinoplasty. Compared with the original Jordan-focused questionnaire developed for young Jordanian females, the present version was adapted for an Arabic-speaking adult sample across five Levantine countries by expanding the sociodemographic section and revising country-specific wording, while retaining the original domains related to knowledge, information sources, motivations, and barriers. The questionnaire was adapted from the published instrument described by Tawalbeh et al. As the instrument was derived from a published source and used for academic research purposes, no separate formal permission was required for its use in this study.

The questionnaire was translated into Arabic by an expert translator with experience in medical and health research instruments. The translated version was subsequently reviewed and reconciled by the research team to ensure linguistic accuracy, conceptual equivalence, and cultural appropriateness across the participating countries. Face and content validity were assessed before field implementation, and minor wording refinements were made accordingly. The prefinal Arabic version was pilot-tested in 30 participants to assess clarity, comprehensibility, and acceptability. Based on pilot feedback, minor language adjustments were introduced before final distribution. The knowledge scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in the study sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.82); however, a full formal psychometric validation study of the adapted Arabic instrument was not performed. The final questionnaire is provided as Supplementary Appendix S1.

Scoring and reliability

Knowledge responses were summed to generate a composite score ranging from 0 to 9, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge of rhinoplasty. Motivation items were averaged within the predefined therapeutic and cosmetic domains on a 1–5 scale, with higher values indicating stronger endorsement. Perceived barriers were categorized into four mutually exclusive groups: none, financial only, social only, and both financial and social barriers. Free-text responses were used only for the description of prior cosmetic procedures and were cleaned and categorized using keyword-based rules into Face, Hair, Breasts, Body/Buttocks, and Other/Unspecified categories. Each participant contributed at most once to each category, and within-country percentages for prior-procedure types were calculated among respondents with a history of cosmetic surgery. Internal consistency of the multi-item scales was assessed in the final study sample.

Data management and statistical analysis

Data were exported from Google Forms and analyzed using SPSS version 25 and Python. Descriptive statistics are presented as means ± standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Between-country differences in continuous outcomes, including knowledge score and mean therapeutic and cosmetic motivation, were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Associations between categorical variables, including prior rhinoplasty prevalence, information sources, and perceived barrier categories, were assessed using chi-square tests. Pearson correlation was used to examine the relationship between age and knowledge score. Within-participant differences between mean therapeutic and cosmetic motivation scores were assessed using a paired-samples t-test.

To identify factors associated with prior rhinoplasty (yes/no), a multivariable logistic regression model was fitted including country of residence, age, and knowledge score. These variables were selected as the primary explanatory factors of interest because they were conceptually interpretable across all countries and less vulnerable to reverse-temporality concerns than attitudinal variables. Motivation and barrier measures were therefore summarized descriptively and were not included in the primary adjusted model. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported. Linearity in the logit for age was assessed using the Box-Tidwell approach, multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, and model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, Nagelkerke R², area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, and classification accuracy.

Ethical approval

The study protocol, survey instrument, and electronic consent procedure were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee of Al-Quds University, Palestine (approval no. 578/REC/2025); the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Damascus University, Syria (approval no. 2178/2025); the Ethics Committee of the College of Medicine, University of Misan, Iraq (approval no. IRAQ.COMUOM.RESEARCH.REC.1492.82); the Institutional Review Board of Jordan University of Science and Technology, Jordan (approval no. 32/97/2025); and the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the American University of Beirut, Lebanon (approval no. BS-2025-0139). Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants before they accessed the survey. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Participants and baseline characteristics

After excluding 24 incomplete responses, a total of 4,238 participants were included in the final analysis. The overall mean age was 21.8 ± 2.0 years (range 18–25). The sex distribution comprised 2,882 females (68.0%) and 1,356 males (32.0%). Most respondents were single (79.2%), and the sample was highly educated, with 78.2% holding a bachelor’s degree and an additional 7.5% holding a postgraduate degree. University students constituted the majority of the sample, reflecting the online recruitment strategy and the high proportion of young adults in the study population (Table 1).

The study sample was drawn from five Levantine countries: Syria (n = 960), Iraq (n = 934), Jordan (n = 848), Palestine (n = 812), and Lebanon (n = 684). Significant between-country differences were observed in age, marital status, and educational attainment (Table 1). Iraqi and Jordanian respondents had the highest mean age (22.4 ± 2.2 and 22.4 ± 1.9 years, respectively), whereas Palestinian respondents had the lowest mean age (21.1 ± 1.7 years; p < 0.001). The proportion of single respondents was highest in Palestine (91.9%) and Lebanon (88.6%), while marriage was more common in Iraq (22.5%) and Syria (12.1%). Educational attainment also differed significantly across countries, with the highest proportion of bachelor’s-level respondents in Palestine (92.1%) and the highest proportion of postgraduate respondents in Lebanon (22.8%) (Table 1).

Overall, 568 respondents (13.4%) reported a prior history of rhinoplasty. This prevalence differed significantly across countries (χ² = 474.9, p < 0.001), with the highest proportion observed in Iraq (25.7%) and the lowest in Palestine (3.7%). These differences indicate substantial variation in self-reported prior rhinoplasty across the participating countries. The proportion of respondents reporting no prior rhinoplasty was correspondingly highest in Palestine and lowest in Iraq (Fig. 1; Table 1).

Knowledge and awareness of rhinoplasty

Overall awareness of rhinoplasty was high across the study sample. The mean composite knowledge score was 8.15 ± 1.13 out of a maximum of 9 (Table 2), and 50.4% of participants answered all nine knowledge items correctly. Most respondents recognized that rhinoplasty can alter nasal shape, may improve breathing problems, and carries potential postoperative risks. At the same time, some misconceptions persisted, particularly with respect to whether rhinoplasty is exclusively cosmetic and whether outcomes are uniform across patients.

Knowledge score was not significantly correlated with age (Pearson r = 0.01, p = 0.49), suggesting that awareness was broadly high across the relatively young age distribution represented in this sample. A statistically significant difference in mean knowledge score was observed across countries (ANOVA F(4,4233) = 28.2, p < 0.001); however, the absolute differences were small. Jordanian respondents had the highest mean knowledge score (8.49 ± 0.94), followed by Lebanese respondents (8.17 ± 1.03), Palestinian respondents (8.10 ± 1.00), Syrian respondents (8.05 ± 1.05), and Iraqi respondents (7.97 ± 1.28) (Table 2). Similarly, the proportion of respondents achieving a perfect score varied across countries, ranging from 39.6% in Syria to 71.2% in Jordan (χ²(4) = 218.95, p < 0.001).

Respondents with prior rhinoplasty had slightly higher knowledge scores than those without prior rhinoplasty (8.37 ± 1.03 vs. 8.10 ± 1.13; independent-samples t-test, t(4236) = 5.36, p < 0.001). Although this difference was statistically significant, it was numerically small. Given the overall high mean score and the large proportion of perfect responses, the observed between-group and between-country differences should be interpreted cautiously.

Sources of information on rhinoplasty

The most commonly reported primary source of information about rhinoplasty was social media, cited by 39.5% of respondents overall, followed closely by consultation with doctors (37.0%) (Table 2). Smaller proportions reported medical literature or journals (13.8%) and friends’ or family advice (9.6%) as their main source of information. These distributions differed significantly across countries (χ²(12) = 285.41, p < 0.001).

Marked cross-country variation was observed in the dominant source of information. In Iraq, consultation with doctors was the most frequently reported source (50.1%), exceeding social media (25.9%). In contrast, respondents in Lebanon, Palestine, and Syria more often relied on social media as their primary source, with proportions of 50.0%, 47.3%, and 44.6%, respectively. Jordan showed a more balanced pattern, with 38.7% citing doctors and 33.0% citing social media. Reliance on medical literature or journals was most commonly reported in Jordan (16.7%), Syria (16.5%), and Palestine (15.0%), whereas advice from friends or family was more frequent in Iraq (15.6%) and Jordan (11.6%) than in Palestine (1.7%) (Table 2).

Motivational factors for rhinoplasty

Across the full sample, therapeutic motives were endorsed more strongly than cosmetic motives (3.97 ± 1.07 vs. 3.29 ± 1.15; paired-samples t-test, t(4237) = 31.43, p < 0.001) (Table 3). This suggests that respondents more strongly associated rhinoplasty with functional concerns, such as breathing difficulty, chronic nasal obstruction, or correction of deformity, than with appearance-related goals alone.

Between-country differences were also observed in both therapeutic and cosmetic motivation scores (Fig. 2; Table 3). Therapeutic motives differed significantly across countries (ANOVA, F(4, 4227) = 44.23, p < 0.001), with the highest mean in Syria (4.30 ± 0.74), followed by Palestine (4.08 ± 1.01), Lebanon (3.93 ± 1.10), Jordan (3.83 ± 1.12), and Iraq (3.70 ± 1.24). Cosmetic motives also differed significantly across countries (ANOVA, F(4, 4227) = 38.59, p < 0.001), with the highest mean in Jordan (3.53 ± 1.25), followed by Iraq (3.41 ± 1.15), Lebanon (3.39 ± 1.07), Syria (3.22 ± 0.98), and Palestine (2.90 ± 1.21).

Taken together, these findings indicate that both functional and aesthetic considerations were relevant to respondents’ attitudes toward rhinoplasty, although therapeutic motivations predominated overall. The cross-country differences suggest that the relative balance between therapeutic and cosmetic considerations may vary across settings.

Perceived barriers to rhinoplasty

When asked about barriers to rhinoplasty, 65.1% of respondents reported no obstacle that would completely prevent them from pursuing surgery (Table 3). Among the remainder, 11.4% reported social barriers only, 8.4% reported financial barriers only, and 15.1% reported both social and financial barriers. Thus, 34.9% of respondents reported at least one barrier overall, whereas 23.5% reported a financial component either alone or in combination with social barriers.

Barrier profiles differed significantly across countries (χ²(12) = 307.3, p < 0.001). Iraq had the highest proportion of respondents reporting no obstacle (75.6%), followed by Syria (69.6%), Lebanon (69.3%), and Palestine (68.5%), whereas Jordan had the lowest proportion reporting no obstacle (41.7%). Social barriers alone were most common in Jordan (18.2%) and Iraq (11.6%), while financial barriers alone were most common in Jordan (13.0%) and Lebanon (10.5%). Combined social and financial barriers were particularly prominent in Jordan (27.1%), compared with 14.9% in Lebanon, 13.8% in Palestine, 12.3% in Syria, and 8.4% in Iraq (Table 3; Fig. 3).

These results indicate that perceived barriers to rhinoplasty were not uniform across countries. However, because the survey assessed predefined barrier categories rather than formally coded qualitative narratives, the barrier findings should be interpreted as structured survey-item results rather than as in-depth qualitative themes.

Factors associated with prior rhinoplasty

A multivariable logistic regression model was used to examine factors associated with prior rhinoplasty. In the adjusted analysis, country of residence, age, and knowledge score were significantly associated with a self-reported history of rhinoplasty.

Compared with respondents from Iraq, respondents from Palestine had substantially lower adjusted odds of prior rhinoplasty (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 0.13, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.09–0.20, p < 0.001). Lower adjusted odds were also observed for respondents from Syria (aOR = 0.27, 95% CI 0.21–0.37, p < 0.001), Lebanon (aOR = 0.40, 95% CI 0.30–0.53, p < 0.001), and Jordan (aOR = 0.54, 95% CI 0.42–0.68, p < 0.001), again using Iraq as the reference category. Older age was associated with higher odds of prior rhinoplasty (aOR = 1.19 per year, 95% CI 1.13–1.25, p < 0.001), and a higher knowledge score was also associated with higher odds of prior rhinoplasty (aOR = 1.30 per 1-point increase, 95% CI 1.18–1.43, p < 0.001), as shown in Table 4. Model diagnostics showed no evidence against linearity in the logit for age (Box–Tidwell p = 0.663) and low multicollinearity (VIF range 1.03–1.63). Model discrimination was acceptable (AUC = 0.729; Nagelkerke R² = 0.133), although the Hosmer–Lemeshow test suggested imperfect calibration (χ²(8) = 92.83, p < 0.001).

Among respondents who reported a previous cosmetic procedure, facial procedures predominated across all participating countries. Discussion

This multicountry cross-sectional study examined awareness, informational sources, motivations, perceived barriers, and prior rhinoplasty among respondents from five Levantine countries. Four findings merit emphasis. First, prior rhinoplasty varied substantially across countries, with the highest prevalence observed in Iraq and the lowest in Palestine. Second, awareness of rhinoplasty was generally high, although several misconceptions persisted. Third, therapeutic motivations were endorsed more strongly than cosmetic motivations. Fourth, perceived barriers differed across countries, with most respondents reporting no absolute obstacle but a substantial minority reporting social, financial, or combined barriers. These findings suggest that rhinoplasty-related attitudes and experiences vary across Levantine settings; however, because of the cross-sectional design and non-probability online sampling, the results should be interpreted as associations within the study sample rather than as causal or nationally representative estimates.

The marked cross-country variation in prior rhinoplasty is one of the most notable findings of the study. Similar heterogeneity has been described in other Middle Eastern populations, where acceptance of cosmetic surgery and willingness to undergo aesthetic procedures appear to vary according to social norms, body-image attitudes, and healthcare context1,4,5. In the present study, country of residence should not be interpreted as a causal factor in itself, but rather as a contextual proxy that may reflect differences in access to cosmetic services, private-sector availability, media exposure, economic conditions, and social acceptability. Accordingly, the observed intercountry variation should be interpreted cautiously and not reduced to a single explanatory framework.

Awareness of rhinoplasty was high overall, as reflected by the mean knowledge score and the large proportion of perfect responses. This finding is broadly consistent with previous regional work reporting high public familiarity with rhinoplasty and cosmetic procedures in Middle Eastern populations8,9. Nevertheless, familiarity did not necessarily imply complete understanding. Some respondents still held misconceptions regarding whether rhinoplasty is exclusively cosmetic and whether outcomes are uniform across patients. This distinction is clinically important, because awareness of a procedure’s existence or popularity does not guarantee accurate expectations regarding indications, outcomes, or limitations9,10. In addition, the statistically significant differences in knowledge across countries were numerically small, and the high overall scores raise the possibility of limited score discrimination or a ceiling effect. The association between higher knowledge score and prior rhinoplasty should therefore also be interpreted cautiously, as temporal direction cannot be established in a cross-sectional study.

The pattern of informational sources adds further context to these findings. Social media was the most frequently reported primary source of information overall, followed closely by consultation with doctors. This is consistent with prior literature showing that digital platforms increasingly shape perceptions of aesthetic procedures and cosmetic-health behavior2,11. Social media may increase exposure to information and normalize discussion of rhinoplasty, but it may also amplify selective visual representations and non-evidence-based claims, thereby widening the gap between familiarity and informed understanding2,11. The co-dominance of physicians and digital media in the present study highlights the need for clear, evidence-based communication from clinicians to counterbalance misinformation and unrealistic expectations.

Therapeutic motivations were endorsed more strongly than cosmetic motivations across the study sample. Respondents more strongly agreed with reasons related to breathing difficulty, chronic congestion, and deformity correction than with purely appearance-related motives. This differs somewhat from studies in which aesthetic enhancement or body-image concerns predominated as drivers of cosmetic surgery interest1,4. However, the present finding should not be interpreted as indicating that cosmetic considerations were unimportant. Cosmetic motives remained relevant, particularly those related to self-image, confidence, and perceived attractiveness. Rather, the findings suggest that respondents in this sample more often framed rhinoplasty in functional or medically legitimate terms. In Middle Eastern settings, where cosmetic intervention may be interpreted through religious, familial, or moral frameworks, therapeutic justification may also carry greater social acceptability6,12. Because these meanings were not directly measured, however, this interpretation should remain cautious.

The observed variation in motivational profiles across countries further supports the view that rhinoplasty-related decision-making is context sensitive. Therapeutic motives were highest in Syria and Palestine, whereas cosmetic motives were highest in Jordan and Lebanon. These differences may reflect variation in the perceived acceptability of functional versus aesthetic indications, as well as broader differences in media exposure and local patterns of cosmetic practice. Prior regional literature suggests that appearance-related decision-making is shaped by interacting individual, social, and cultural influences, including ethnic identity, perceived beauty norms, and community expectations6,13. The present findings are compatible with that literature, but they do not permit direct inference regarding why one motivational profile predominated in one setting more than another.

Perceived barriers also showed meaningful cross-country variation. Although approximately two-thirds of respondents reported no absolute obstacle to rhinoplasty, the remainder reported social barriers, financial barriers, or both. These findings suggest that access to rhinoplasty is influenced not only by personal preference, but also by affordability and social acceptability. Financial barriers are plausible in elective aesthetic surgery, which is often self-financed14, whereas social barriers may reflect family expectations, moral discourse, or anticipated social judgment12,15. At the same time, the barrier variable was measured using predefined survey categories rather than formal qualitative coding, and the study did not directly assess household income, insurance coverage, family attitudes, stigma intensity, or geographic access. These interpretations should therefore be regarded as plausible but not conclusive.

The multivariable analysis showed that country of residence, age, and knowledge score were associated with prior rhinoplasty. These findings are informative, but they should not be interpreted causally. As noted above, country likely represents multiple contextual influences rather than a discrete determinant. The association with age should also be interpreted in light of the relatively narrow and predominantly young age distribution of the sample16. In addition, females accounted for 68.0% of respondents, which may have influenced the observed distribution of attitudes and prior rhinoplasty. Previous literature has shown that motivations for cosmetic surgery may differ by sex and social role1,17, and future studies should aim for more balanced recruitment.

This study has several strengths, including its large sample size, inclusion of five Levantine countries within a single analytical framework, and assessment of multiple domains relevant to rhinoplasty decision-making. The use of an instrument adapted from prior regional work also enhances comparability with earlier studies8. These strengths should nevertheless be considered alongside important limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the online snowball-sampling strategy likely overrepresented younger, highly educated, digitally connected, and female respondents, thereby limiting generalizability. Country eligibility was self-reported and may have been subject to some misclassification. All measures were self-reported and therefore vulnerable to recall bias, social desirability bias, and variation in item interpretation. In addition, the high knowledge scores raise the possibility of a ceiling effect, and although the Arabic questionnaire showed acceptable internal consistency, it did not undergo full psychometric validation in the present multicountry sample. Item-level difficulty and discrimination were also not formally assessed. Despite these limitations, the study provides useful insight into rhinoplasty-related attitudes and experiences in an understudied regional context. The findings support the need for culturally sensitive public education, evidence-based preoperative counseling, and future studies using more representative sampling and stronger measurement validation.

Conclusion

This multicountry cross-sectional study found that awareness of rhinoplasty was generally high among respondents from five Levantine countries, although some misconceptions persisted. Therapeutic motives were endorsed more strongly than cosmetic motives, and prior rhinoplasty varied substantially across countries. In the adjusted analysis, country of residence, age, and knowledge score were associated with prior rhinoplasty. Because the study used a cross-sectional design and non-probability online sampling, these findings should be interpreted as sample-based associations rather than as causal or nationally representative estimates.
