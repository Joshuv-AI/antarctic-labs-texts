Soma-derived 30-nt small RNAs are coupled with chromosome breakage and precisely target nontransposon DNA against elimination in Euplotes vannus
Authors: Liping Lyu, Bijia Ding, Jinyu Fu, Iwona Rzeszutek, Estienne C. Swart, Mariusz Nowacki, Feng Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Metazoans suppress transposons via small RNA–mediated silencing, but ciliates physically eliminate transposons from somatic genomes. Here, we report the high-quality germline genome assembly of a marine ciliate Euplotes vannus, where ~80% are transposons and other germline-specific DNA that has to be precisely eliminated during soma development. We demonstrate that a class of soma-derived 30-nucleotide small RNAs precisely targets nontransposon DNA against elimination during this process. Small RNA–mediated targeting remains functional across varying small RNA lengths and is compatible with heterozygous sites. These small RNAs are cleaved by Dicer-like ribonuclease from long noncoding RNAs, which are bidirectionally transcribed of somatic chromosomes, initiated at subtelomeric chromosome breakage sequences (5′-TTGAA-3′). On the basis of these findings and time-course transcriptomic profiling, we propose a model elucidating the molecular mechanism of DNA elimination in Euplotes. These findings provide insights into the role of small RNAs in transmitting genetic information across generations and maintaining genome stability.

INTRODUCTION

Transposons, also known as “jumping genes,” are mobile genetic elements that can change their positions within a genome (1–3). These elements contribute to genetic plasticity and evolution while posing potential threats to genome stability (1–4). Therefore, organisms have evolved diverse mechanisms for regulating transposons. Among the best-known mechanisms are small RNA (sRNA)–mediated transcriptional and posttranscriptional silencing achieved through DNA methylation and heterochromatin formation (5). However, ciliates, a large and diverse group of single-celled eukaryotic organisms, have evolved a more extreme strategy of transposon control through extensive, sRNA-mediated DNA elimination that removes transposons from their somatic genome. Ciliates have two types of nuclei within one cell: the diploid germline micronucleus (MIC), which carries the entire genetic information, including transposons, but is transcriptionally silent during vegetative growth, and the polyploid somatic macronucleus (MAC), which contains almost all protein-coding sequences and is transcriptionally active (6–10). During the sexual process (typically conjugation), the MIC produces gamete nuclei through meiotic division, while the parental MAC gradually degrades, and the new MAC develops from fertilized zygotes following the genome-wide elimination of MIC-limited sequences, including transposons and other repetitive DNA (6–8, 11–14). As a result, the transcriptionally active MAC is generally transposon free.

Following the discovery of the DNA elimination process in ciliates, a large diversity of genomic features and genome rearrangement mechanisms among different species have been reported, which also demonstrate the vital roles of P-element induced wimpy testis (Piwi)–interacted sRNAs (15–20). Previous studies initially proposed the “scan RNA (scnRNA) model” in oligohymenophorean ciliates such as Tetrahymena and Paramecium (12, 16). This model highlights the subtractive comparison of the germline-derived sRNAs (termed scnRNAs; ~25 nt in Paramecium and 25 to 29 nt in Tetrahymena) to the parental somatic genome. Nonmatching scnRNAs complementary to germline-limited DNA are then transported into the developing new MAC (21–24), where they target DNA sequences for elimination, potentially by introducing histone methylation marks and recruiting domesticated transposases that cleave the DNA (25–30). In the cases described above, it is generally understood that sRNAs originating from transposable elements (TEs) ultimately lead to the inhibition or degradation of the TEs themselves. However, subsequent studies in hypotrichous ciliates (Oxytricha and Stylonychia) revealed a different mechanism known as the “macronucleus RNA (macRNA) model.” In this model, ~27-nt macRNAs derived from the transposon-free somatic genome play a role in protecting soma-destined sequences from deletion, rather than marking germline-specific sequences (GSSs) for excision (18, 19, 31).

The diverse sRNA-mediated mechanisms among ciliate species might be related to their notably varying proportion of germline-limited sequences. For instance, over 90% of the germline DNA is eliminated during new MAC development in Oxytricha trifallax compared to the ~30% in Tetrahymena thermophila and Paramecium tetraurelia (6, 7, 32). The production of sRNAs from the parental somatic genome in Oxytricha is therefore economical, since they only need to compare the new developing MAC once (versus comparing the parental MAC and then the developing MAC in Tetrahymena and Paramecium) and predominantly target the minority class of the germline genome (~10% of the Oxytricha germline that is retained in the soma). Oxytricha diverged from Tetrahymena and Paramecium ~1.1 billion years ago (32, 33). It is still unknown whether these mechanisms involving soma-derived sRNAs are specific to hypotrichous ciliates, leaving a large gap in our understanding about the evolution of the opposite sRNA mechanisms. The somatic genome of most hypotrichs is extremely fragmented into gene-sized “nanochromosomes,” each encoding only one or a few genes (e.g., over 18,000 nanochromosomes in Oxytricha) (8, 34–37). How these sRNAs are generated from tens of thousands of nanochromosomes remains unclear.

Euplotid ciliates are highly distinctive ciliates, which are considered as the sister group of hypotrichs but have diverged from each other about 900 million years ago (33). They have some unique translation features, e.g., different stop codon reassignment (UGA is reassigned to cysteine) (38) and a high frequency of +1 programmed ribosomal frameshifting (39, 40). Euplotids also have gene-sized nanochromosomes in the MAC genome (39, 41–43). However, a partial MIC genome survey indicated that their MIC genome is less interrupted by GSSs, with less scrambling sequences compared to Oxytricha (13, 39, 42). To better understand the evolution and molecular mechanisms of sRNA–mediated DNA elimination and fill the gap in our understanding of the opposite sRNA mechanisms, here, we obtained the high-quality assembly of the germline genome in the marine ciliate Euplotes vannus. Comparative analysis between the MAC and MIC genomes revealed that marked DNA elimination (~80%) occurs during new MAC development in Euplotes. A class of development-specific 30-nt sRNAs was then investigated through high-throughput sequencing, and their roles in precisely directing DNA elimination were elucidated by subsequent experiments. We found that the presumed precursors of these sRNAs were bidirectionally transcribed from the parental MAC chromosomes, initiating at the conserved subtelomeric chromosome breakage sequences (5′-TTGAA-3′). We then investigated the role of Dicer-like ribonuclease (RNase) in sRNA processing and validated its function through gene knockout experiments. On the basis of the above findings and time-course transcriptomic profiling, we also proposed a model elucidating the molecular mechanism of DNA elimination in Euplotes. Our findings not only shed light on the molecular mechanisms of sRNA in governing genome dynamics but also broaden our understanding of genome stability and adaptation across diverse eukaryotic lineages.

RESULTS

Approximately 80% of the germline genome is eliminated during soma development

The enriched Euplotes MIC DNA was sequenced to a coverage of ~70× using PacBio long-read sequencing technology. De novo assembly of HiFi PacBio reads with a combination of HiCanu, HiFiasm, and QuickMerge yielded an ~431-Mb haploid MIC assembly (Table 1, table S1, and fig. S1). The MIC assembly contains ~94% of all the bases in the MAC assembly. Of the 25,057 contigs with both telomeres in the MAC assembly, nearly all of them (~99%) are at least 90% covered in the MIC assembly, and the majority (~97%) are covered by single MIC contigs. The mean coverage of all MAC contigs is over 98.7% (table S1 and fig. S1M), suggesting completely resolved germline-somatic rearrangement maps.

By sequence comparisons with the MAC genome, the MIC genome appears to be fragmented into more than 140,146 precursor DNA segments [macronuclear destined sequences (MDSs)], which are subsequently rearranged and fused at short direct repeats (pointers, located at the boundaries of MDS and GSS) to form the functional MAC genome (Fig. 1A). MDSs comprise ~20% of the MIC genome, among which 13% are coding sequences and 7% are noncoding (Fig. 1B). Nearly all of the MDSs are longer than 25 bp, and most are shorter than 1000 bp, with an average length of 603 bp (Fig. 1C).

The 80% of germline-specific DNA segments eliminated during rearrangement are broadly termed as GSSs. They are further classified into TE GSS and non-TE GSS based on the presence or absence of TEs (Fig. 1A). On average, each TE GSS contains approximately eight TEs. All of the TE GSSs contribute to ~65.8% of the germline genome, whereas non-TE GSSs account for about 13.9%. TE GSSs tend to be longer but less abundant compared to non-TE GSSs (Fig. 1D). The mean size of TE GSSs is about 8517 bp, nearly eight times larger than non-TE GSSs (average of 1062 bp), whereas the number (32,126) is less than half that of non-TE GSSs (77,121).

We identified at least 233,054 transposons in ~70.6% of the GSSs, collectively comprising 48.2% (207.9 Mb) of the germline genome (Fig. 1B and fig. S2A). Among the transposons in E. vannus, the majority belong to the Tc1/mariner superfamily, with Tec transposons (transposon of Euplotes crassus) being the most abundant (79,337, ~34.0% of all TEs; Fig. 1B). Each Tec consists of three open reading frames (ORFs) encoding a transposase (ORF1) (44–46), a tyrosine-type recombinase (ORF2) (47), and a protein of unknown function (ORF3) (46). We identified 49,837 complete Tec transposons containing three ORFs in appropriate orientation, comprising 33.6% (144.7 Mb) of the MIC genome. We also identified 8264 partial Tec, 80% (6595) of which contain the ORF1. On the basis of the mRNA sequencing (mRNA-seq) data, ~4% TEs were observed to be transcribed during conjugation, while 8040 transposons were exclusively expressed during MAC anlagen development (Fig. 1E).

The short direct repeats at both ends of GSS (pointers; as depicted in Fig. 1A) are mostly 2 bp and are 5′-TA-3′ (Fig. 1F). This feature resembles the ends of Tc1/mariner transposons (44, 45, 48, 49). The 5′-TA-3′ repeats are also pronounced in Paramecium (50) and Blepharisma (20), where the GSSs are excised by domesticated PiggyBac transposases (part of the Tc1/mariner family). Previous research in Euplotes has revealed that staggered cuts at both boundaries of GSS generate 5′-overhang ends consisting of 5′-TA-3′ and additional terminal residues (51). The two adjacent MDSs then associate via their terminal 5′-TA-3′, resulting in a MAC DNA molecule with one copy of the direct repeat. In E. vannus, most of the longer pointers also contain the 5′-TA-3′ dinucleotide motif (Fig. 1F). Considering that the pointers are identified on the basis of the overlaps between two adjacent MDSs in somatic genomes, both the entire or part of the overlaps could biologically serve as the pointer repeats, indicating the overestimation of direct repeat size and the possibility of using 5′-TA-3′ as functional repeats in E. vannus (fig. S2B). In addition, ~65.7% of the pointers are located within protein coding regions (Fig. 1G), and about 83.0% are located within mRNA transcription regions (fig. S2C), suggesting the necessity for precise elimination of most GSSs.

In addition, the distribution of MDSs on germline chromosomes tends to be clustered. Most non-TE GSSs are adjacent to other non-TE GSSs rather than TE GSSs (fig. S2D), supporting the trend that non-TE GSSs cluster rather than distribute evenly throughout the genome along with TE GSSs. Given that non-TE GSSs are much shorter than TE GSSs, their clustering may also contribute to the clustering of MDSs, a pattern also observed in Oxytricha MIC genome (6). For example, for a ~5-Mb germline chromosome with both telomeric repeats (1.1 kb at 5′ [C4A4] and 19.8 kb at 3′ [G4T4]; Fig. 1H), most MDSs are gathered into four clusters. The GSSs in each MDS cluster are mostly short non-TE GSSs, while TE GSSs are primarily located between clusters.

Massive accumulation of 30-nt sRNAs corresponding to the MAC genome during conjugation

A time-course analysis of sRNAs revealed an abundant class of ~30-nt sRNAs that were exclusively accumulated during E. vannus conjugation (Fig. 2A). The accumulation of these sRNAs was observed during early conjugation [8 hours postmixing (hpm), meiosis I stage], reached the highest level at 12 hpm (60.9% of all sRNAs detected at this time point), and persisted at a high level until 75 hpm (54.1% of all sRNAs detected at this time point), coinciding with the presence of well-developed new MACs observed under microscopy (table S2).

The conjugation-specific 30-nt sRNAs were mapped to the somatic and germline genomes, respectively. Approximately 85.7% of the 30-nt sRNAs could be mapped to either the MAC genome or de novo–assembled transcripts (a complement to the incomplete macronuclear genome), with an additional 9.7% aligning with the MIC genome (Fig. 2A). Moreover, over 99% of all the MAC chromosomes could be mapped by 30-nt sRNAs since 12 hpm (Fig. 2B).

To further investigate the origin of 30-nt sRNAs, we mapped them to different genomic regions: exons, introns, MDSs, and GSSs. The genome-wide analysis revealed a high and similar sequence coverage of both exons and introns by 30-nt sRNAs (average of 93.0% for exons and average of 91.3% for introns; Fig. 2E). However, the coverage of GSSs (6.3%, which might be misclassified MDSs) was notably lower than that of MDSs (86.4%) (Fig. 2E). We further calculated the proportion of 30-nt sRNA reads that are spliced at GSS/intron locus using the CIGAR representations of read alignments from HISAT2. Briefly, unspliced reads were identified on the basis of their matching CIGAR strings, indicating the unclipped alignment with the reference at the GSS locus (as depicted by the green reads in Fig. 2D), while spliced reads were identified on the basis of the “MNM” strings, indicating the “matched-skipped-matched” mapping of a read at the intron locus (as depicted by the light blue and dark blue reads in Fig. 2D). This calculation supports that nearly all of the 30-nt sRNAs originate from templates lacking GSSs but containing introns (Fig. 2F).

Furthermore, the abundance of 30-nt sRNAs per nanochromosome displayed a moderate correlation with somatic DNA copy number from 8 hpm onward, but a poor correlation with mRNA transcript levels (Fig. 2C and fig. S3A), suggesting that their precursors are likely not pre-mRNAs. This is also supported by the observation that some MAC nanochromosomes exhibited high coverage by 30-nt sRNAs but minimal or no coverage by RNA-seq reads. The above results indicated that these 30-nt sRNAs are derived from transcription of the MAC genome, which is distinct from the transcription that gives rise to mRNAs.

Pronounced 5′-uridine bias and posttranscriptional 3′ adenylation of endogenized 30-nt sRNAs

The conjugation-specific sRNAs, notably 29- to 33-nt sRNAs, each contribute more than 1% of total sRNAs at that length, exhibited a strong 5′–uridine (U) bias (Fig. 2G and fig. S3B). This bias is a characteristic feature of cleavage by ciliate Dicer-like proteins (Dcl; missing N-terminal domains of full-length Dicer proteins), which are responsible for processing long double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) into smaller, mature functional sRNAs. The 5′-U bias was first observed at 8 hpm and was most pronounced in 30- and 31-nt sRNAs, extending to 29- and 32-nt sRNAs from 12 hpm onward and 33-nt sRNAs starting at 30 hpm (fig. S3B).

At the 3′ ends, a bias of one to three terminal adenylates was observed in 31- to 33-nt sRNAs (Fig. 2G) and revealed possible posttranscriptional additions based on the genomic base matching rates (Fig. 2H). For the 29- and 30-nt sRNAs, as well as 31- to 33-nt sRNAs at early conjugation (0 to 4 hpm), each base aligned perfectly with the MAC genome. However, from 8 hpm onward, the time when 30-nt sRNAs began to accumulate, matching rates of the last one to three bases in 31- to 33-nt sRNAs were notably lower than those in the earlier time points (48.2% versus 99.5%). Presumably, a substantial proportion of the 48.2% perfect matches were unmodified 31- to 33-nt sRNAs (i.e., without posttranscriptional 3′ adenylation).

To determine whether sRNAs of other sizes (aside from 30-nt ones), such as the adenylated 31- to 33-nt sRNAs, were generated by specific chromosomes, we compared the coverage of each chromosome by 30-nt sRNAs with that by 29- and 31- to 33-nt sRNAs. We found that every MAC chromosome produced much more 30-nt sRNAs than any others (Fig. 2I and fig. S5, A and B). In addition, consistency between 30-nt sRNAs and the other sRNAs was only observed before 4 hpm (i.e., before conjugation). Since 8 hpm, nearly all the nanochromosomes were dedicated to synthesizing large amounts of 30-nt sRNAs (fig. S5A). A similar trend was observed in the mean sRNA base coverage depth of chromosomes by diverse sizes of sRNAs (fig. S5B), with the 31-nt sRNAs displaying a slightly better correlation with 30-nt ones, likely due to their higher abundance.

Microinjection of synthetic sRNAs, including 30 nt and varying sizes, disturbed normal DNA elimination

To elucidate the biological function of conjugation-specific 30-nt sRNAs, we microinjected synthetic sRNAs, both single- and double-stranded, targeting a normally deleted GSS, into the cytoplasm of conjugating cells. We selected one of the four paralogous genes (c13490.g1) encoding a lipase with relatively low expression at most sampling stages (except for under low temperature stress; fig. S6A). Therefore, this gene is unlikely to be a housekeeping gene essential for vegetative growth, and, thus, its disruption would not be lethal, enabling us to examine the retention of GSS in the offspring cells. The gene rearrangement structures and experimental design were outlined in Fig. 3A. The target gene is separated into five MDSs by four GSSs on the MIC DNA locus, with the target GSS (20 bp) being shorter than a 30-nt sRNA molecule and containing at least one thymine (T) within 10 nt upstream and downstream (i.e., allowing a single 30-nt/bp 5′-U sRNA to cover the entire GSS) (Fig. 3, A and D). Microinjections were performed between 8 and 12 hpm, when endogenous 30-nt sRNAs were being synthesized.

Subsequently, we examined whether this targeting GSS is retained in the new MAC of the offspring cells by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analyses (Fig. 3B). Two pairs of PCR primers (MDS p1 and MDS p2) were used to examine GSS retention in the new MAC. Both primer pairs could efficiently amplify the GSS-retained and GSS-excised DNA of the MAC DNA, while MIC DNA was not able to be amplified because of its low copy number. MDS p1 located on the adjacent MDSs of the target GSS, yielding products of different sizes that can be easily distinguished by gel electrophoresis. Specifically, the injection of double-stranded sRNA molecules or coinjection of both sense and antisense sRNAs resulted in almost complete retention of the corresponding GSS. To assess the specificity of synthetic sRNAs, we performed PCR using MDS p2 primers, which could amplify a region spanning adjacent GSSs that are not targeted by synthetic sRNAs. Sequencing of the cloned PCR products confirmed that only the target GSS was retained (Fig. 3D). Notably, no obvious GSS retention signal was observed in the PCR products using MDS p1 primers of single-stranded sRNA–injected cells (Fig. 3B). However, additional PCR experiments using GSS p1 primers, which could specifically amplify DNA with retained GSS, confirmed that only a minority of DNA copies retained the target GSS (Fig. 3B).

Furthermore, to examine the impact of sRNA sizes, we designed double-stranded sRNAs of varying sizes (25, 27, 33, and 35 bp), all containing a 5′-terminal U, to assess their effect on GSS retention (as depicted in Fig. 3D). Similar to the 30-bp sRNA, sRNAs longer than 30 bp also led to nearly complete retention of corresponding GSS in all DNA copies (Fig. 3C). Microinjection of 27-bp sRNAs also led to GSS retention, but the efficiency was lower compared to the longer ones. However, the GSS+ signals were hardly detected in progeny cells of those injected with 25-bp sRNAs, even when using GSS p1 primers designed for specific amplification of GSS retained DNA.

The GSS retention induced by sRNA injection is heritable through sexual and asexual reproduction, although it appears unstable during sexual reproduction. Two GSS+ strains were cultured for at least 50 divisions, and the GSS+ DNA copies were stably inherited (Fig. 3E). Among 47 cell lines of the sexually reproduced F2 progenies, 14 lines (29.8%) contained nearly exclusively GSS+ DNA copies, 3 lines (6.4%) contained nearly exclusively GSS- DNA copies, and 30 lines (63.8%) contained both GSS+ and GSS− copies in varying proportions.

Thirty-nucleotide/base pair sRNAs ensure the precise excision of GSSs and are compatible with heterozygous sites

Synthetic sRNAs can induce the retention of normally deleted GSSs. Most of GSSs in E. vannus exceed 30 bp in length (Fig. 1D) and are situated within gene-coding regions (Fig. 1G). This raises questions about whether GSSs are precisely excised during new MAC development and whether this process affects the gene function of the offspring.

To address these questions, we investigated one of the Dcl proteins in E. vannus that lacks N-terminal domains typically found in canonical Dicer proteins (Dcr). Its MIC DNA sequence contains a 2904-bp GSS that must be eliminated during rearrangement. Dcl are theoretically capable of cleaving long dsRNAs into sRNA precursors and have been shown to be essential for the production of conjugation-specific sRNAs in Tetrahymena and Paramecium (17, 52). Through BLAST searches using the Dcl peptides of Tetrahymena and Paramecium, we identified five Dcr/Dcl homologs in E. vannus (Fig. 4B and fig. S6B). Among these, Dcl1 (c20113.g1), which consists of only two RNase III domains, exhibits the closest evolutionary relationship to Dcl2/3/5 in Paramecium and Dcl1 in Tetrahymena (Fig. 4B), all of which are responsible for the production of conjugation-specific sRNAs. Dcl1 in E. vannus is specifically expressed during conjugation and is significantly up-regulated before sRNA production (Fig. 4E and fig. S6, B and D). Thus, Dcl1 in E. vannus is likely responsible for the production of conjugation-specific sRNAs.

Microinjection targeting the long GSS (2904 bp) of the Dcl1 gene demonstrated that 30-bp sRNAs are essential for the precise excision of GSS. We designed two 30-bp sRNAs covering the 3′ end of the 2904-bp TE GSS, which contains a Tec ORF1 (Fig. 4, A and C). These two sRNAs overlapped by 5 bp, covering 36 bp of the GSS and 19 bp of the neighboring pointer and the MDS. The upstream sRNA (sRNA 1) comprises all GSS bases and begins with 5′-UA, resembling the pointer repeats. The downstream sRNA (sRNA 2) also begins with 5′-UA but covers only 11-bp GSS bases. Coinjection of these two sRNAs was expected to introduce two types of disturbed MAC rearrangement products: one retaining the 36-bp GSS sequence covered by both sRNAs and the other only retaining the downstream 11-bp GSS sequence covered by sRNA 2 (Fig. 4A). As anticipated, we observed both cases of GSS retention with precisely 11 and 36 bp in F1 progenies (Fig. 4C). In total, we obtained eight GSS+ strains: Five strains contained the 11-bp retention, two strains contained the 36-bp retention, and one strain contained both (strain 33B; Fig. 4C).

During the interaction of sRNAs with the template, perfect base pairing may not be necessary. A heterozygous site at the 3′ end of the GSS (cytosine or adenosine; shown in Fig. 4C) was detected by clone sequencing. Although the synthetic sRNAs were primarily designed with an adenosine at the corresponding position and thus not expected to accommodate heterozygous copies, GSS copies with cytosine were also retained in the F1 progenies (Fig. 4C). Both heterozygous copies were even observed in the same cell (strain 39A; Fig. 4C).

The Dcl1 mutant represents a successful gene knockout in euplotids and confirms the essential role of Dcl in sRNA biogenesis

In general, abnormal retention of GSSs can severely affect the biological function of the target gene. We induced conjugation between two Dcl1-mutant strains with different mating types (strains 15B and 33B; as shown in Fig. 4C), both of which contained almost exclusively GSS+ DNA copies. At the early stage of conjugation, we detected the Dcl1-mutant mRNA (GSS+; Fig. 4C). The abnormally retained 11- and 36-bp GSS introduced an in-frame stop codon (TAA), thereby prematurely terminating the translation. Even if the TAA is read through by +1 frameshifting, which is less likely to occur, the following codons are out of frame. Therefore, there is no normally functional Dcl protein. As a result, Dcl1-mutant cells lose the ability to produce mature 30-nt sRNAs (Fig. 4D). Mating pairs of the mutant cells began to die since the early stage of new MAC development (48 hpm; Fig. 4F), coinciding with the genome-wide excision of GSSs. At least 75% of the progeny cells could not complete conjugation or survive, highlighting the essential role of Dcl1 in Euplotes genome rearrangements.

Coordinated bidirectional transcription of 30-nt sRNA precursors initiated from the subtelomeric Euplotes chromosome breakage site motifs across tens of thousands of nanochromosomes

The data so far indicated that soma-derived sRNAs in Euplotes target DNA sequences against excision in the developing soma, similar to the mechanisms in Oxytricha and Stylonychia (18, 19), as opposed to Tetrahymena and Paramecium, where scnRNAs are derived from the germline genome and ultimately target DNA for elimination (22, 52). scnRNAs in Tetrahymena and Paramecium are produced by Dcl cleavage of dsRNA precursors (Dcl2/3/5 in Paramecium and Dcl1 in Tetrahymena). However, how sRNAs are generated from tens of thousands of nanochromosomes remains unknown. Given the essential role of Dcl1 in Euplotes sRNA production and its closest evolutionary relationship to Dcl proteins in other ciliates (Dcl2/3/5 in Paramecium and Dcl1 in Tetrahymena, which cleave dsRNA), the existence of dsRNA precursors in Euplotes is likely also necessary.

Immunofluorescence staining with anti-dsRNA antibodies revealed that dsRNA specifically appeared in the MAC of the conjugating cells (Fig. 5). In the wild-type (WT) cells, these dsRNA signals persisted in the parental MAC during the very early conjugation stage (Fig. 5, left). As the MAC fragmented, the signals disappeared from anterior MAC fragments and became exclusively localized to posterior fragments since the meiotic divisions (~8 to 12 hpm). As the new MAC developed, the signals were specifically localized to MAC anlagen and maintained until the late developmental stage (Fig. 5). In comparison, the dsRNA signals persisted in all the MAC fragments until after the first postzygotic nuclear division in the Dcl1-mutant cells (Fig. 5, right). This indicated that there might be excessive dsRNA accumulation in parental MAC in the mutant cells. Moreover, dsRNA still accumulated in MAC anlagen in the surviving cells.

Strand-specific ribosomal RNA (rRNA)–depleted RNA Illumina sequencing revealed that the dsRNAs during prezygotic divisions (12 hpm) exhibited near-perfect complementarity to the MAC genome (Fig. 6A). The sequencing libraries included both mRNA and long noncoding RNA (lncRNA). Considering the compact architecture of the nanochromosomes in Euplotes, it would be challenging to distinguish mRNA and lncRNA on sense strands, as their transcribed regions might overlap notably. Therefore, we focused on the antisense transcripts. Antisense transcripts (i.e., lncRNA) of MAC nanochromosomes were detected at all conjugation stages. The sequence coverages of nanochromosomes by antisense transcripts were notably higher at 12 hpm (mean, 75.7%; median, 81.5%; Fig. 6A) compared to other stages [~40% during new MAC development (30 to 50 hpm) and ~20% during vegetable growth (0 and 75 hpm)].

To investigate whether these antisense transcripts were reverse replicated from mRNA, we further focused on the splicing of their introns (Fig. 6A). However, the considerably lower expression level of antisense transcripts compared to sense ones (fig. S7A) posed challenges for the complete assembly of antisense transcript isoforms. Therefore, we calculated the retention score of each intron based on sequencing reads. These scores quantify the proportion of unspliced RNA reads at specific loci: A score of 0 indicates exclusively spliced transcripts, a score of 1 indicates fully unspliced transcripts, and scores between 0 and 1 indicate alternative splicing. For the antisense transcripts, they mostly have a score of 0 during vegetative growth (0 and 75 hpm), indicating their main origins from mRNA. At 12 hpm, at least 94% of the introns were detected with a positive score (>0) and 45% with a score equal or close to 1, indicating the retention of introns in all the isoforms and that they were not related to mRNA. During MAC anlagen development (30 to 50 hpm), the unspliced transcripts still appear but not as many as those at 12 hpm. In addition, more introns were detected with a score of 0, which were rare at 12 hpm. For the sense transcripts, the appearance of more introns with a positive score (>0) at 12 hpm indicated the transcription of unspliced lncRNA on the sense strand.

Furthermore, to infer the transcription start sites (TSSs) and transcription end sites (TESs) of both sense and antisense transcripts, we performed Oxford Nanopore full-length transcriptome sequencing using rRNA-depleted RNA from WT and Dcl1-mutant cells. During library preparation, a 3′ RNA adapter was ligated to the rRNA-depleted RNA before first-strand cDNA synthesis. Subsequently, a 5′ DNA adapter was ligated to the resulting cDNA before second-strand synthesis. This protocol preserves transcript polarity and enables accurate identification of transcript boundaries.

For sense transcripts aligned to the 5′ subtelomeric regions of MAC chromosomes, analysis of transcript termini revealed three distinct peaks of TSSs located ~10 to 30 bp downstream of the 5′ telomere, with a ~10-bp interval between peaks (Fig. 6B). Among these, the peak at ~20 bp downstream was the most prominent, representing one-third of the observed sense TSSs. At the 3′ end, in contrast, ~8% of sense transcripts terminated ~20 bp upstream of the 3′ telomere, while the remaining termini formed a broader distribution spanning 0 to 20 bp from the telomere.

Similar patterns were also observed in antisense transcripts (Fig. 6B). The main difference from sense transcripts was that the 30-bp peak was considerably less pronounced. The TES distribution of antisense transcripts closely resembled that of sense transcripts, showing a sharp peak at exactly 20 bp upstream of the telomere and a broader distribution within the 0- to 20-bp range.

Although there were no notably differences in the distribution patterns of transcript termini between WT and Dcl1-mutant cells, the sequencing depth analysis revealed a notable increase in antisense transcript abundance in the Dcl1 mutant (Fig. 6C). For quantification, each MAC chromosome was divided into 100 bins, and the average base depth per bin was normalized as counts per million reads (CPM). In WT cells, the 5′ ends of antisense transcripts were more frequently captured than their 3′ ends. By contrast, in Dcl1-mutant cells, not only was the mean CPM approximately twofold higher, but the 3′ ends of antisense transcripts were also captured more frequently than the 5′ ends.

Considering the unspliced introns and high MAC chromosome coverage by the lncRNA transcripts, as well as the accumulation of antisense transcripts in Dcl1-mutant cells, they appear to successfully construct a copy of the MAC chromosomes, which is similar to our observation about the 30-nt sRNAs (Fig. 2B). Their genome-wide occurrence specifically at 12 hpm, coinciding with the accumulation of 30-nt sRNAs (Fig. 2A), also suggested a strong possibility that they serve as precursors for 30-nt sRNAs.

The boundaries of 30-nt sRNA precursors indicate transcription initiation at the Euplotes chromosome breakage sites (E-CBSs). Strictly, the conjugation-specific 30-nt sRNAs did not form a complete copy for most nanochromosomes because their telomeric repeats and both subtelomeric regions were poorly covered by 30-nt sRNAs (fig. S7B). Estimations of transcription boundaries of 30-nt sRNA precursors, based on alignment coordinates on MAC chromosomes, revealed that the TSS and TES of the precursors are 17 to 20 bp away from both telomeres (Fig. 6D). In Euplotes species, a conserved 5-bp motif of 5′-TTGAA-3′ was observed at the same subtelomeric regions (Fig. 6E and fig. S7, C and D) (39, 40, 43, 53), which has been termed as the E-CBSs based on its proposed role in MIC chromosome fragmentation and telomeric repeats addition in MAC chromosomes (54). In E. vannus, the E-CBS elements were detected within both subtelomeric regions of 71.6% of the MAC chromosomes and 24.2% at either 5′ or 3′ end (fig. S7C). The mean distance between E-CBS and transcription boundaries of 30-nt sRNA precursors is less than 3 bp, suggesting a strong correlation between them.

Transcriptome analysis reveals numerous candidate genes associated with DNA elimination in Euplotes

Previous studies have provided insights into the key functional genes underlying programmed genome rearrangements in Tetrahymena and Paramecium (15, 55, 56). We identified the homologs of these genes in E. vannus and the other five Euplotes species via one-way BLAST searches (Fig. 7A and fig. S8B). The time-course RNA-seq also revealed that over half of the genes in E. vannus (53.9% of 39,179; fig. S8A) were differentially expressed during conjugation, and 6750 are conjugation-specific genes.

We first searched the homologs of genes implicated in conjugation-specific sRNA production (Fig. 7A and fig. S8B). Homologs of RNA polymerase II subunit 2 (RPB2) and Suppressor of Ty 4 meiosis B (Spt4mB; a component of Spt4/5 transcription elongation complex) were significantly up-regulated at early conjugation (before 12 hpm), consistent with their reported roles in sRNA precursor transcription (57). Eight homologs of RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRP) are highly expressed during conjugation, among which three showed marked up-regulation at the early stage (Fig. 7B). In addition, as described above, Dcl1 and two Piwi homologs were strongly expressed during early conjugation, supporting a conserved role in sRNA biogenesis. Two homologs of Ema1, a gene known to mediate nascent transcript recognition in the developing MAC of Tetrahymena (58), were also significantly up-regulated during conjugation in E. vannus, suggesting their functional conservation.

We further investigated the genes involved in chromatin modification. In Tetrahymena and Paramecium, the Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2 complex, also called Ezl1 complex) has been reported to be responsible for trimethylation of H3K9 and H3K27, heterochromatin formation, and TE control (28, 59–61). Although Euplotes may have evolved distinct Polycomb-mediated regulatory mechanisms for genome rearrangement compared to Tetrahymena and Paramecium, we still identified one PRC2 core component (Ezl1, a histone methyltransferase), which was up-regulated during MAC development in E. vannus (Fig. 7B and fig. S8B). A homolog of Jumonji C demethylases 1 (Jmj1), an H3K27 demethylase, also showed up-regulation, underscoring the importance of histone methylation dynamics in DNA elimination.

DNA excision machineries in ciliates are likely to be directed by domesticated transposases. In the present study, we identified 28 MAC-encoded transposase homologs that are highly expressed during new MAC development, 10 of which contain Mutator-like element (MULE) transposase domains and are specifically expressed during this stage (Fig. 7C). In addition, we observed some of the MIC-encoded TEs (1.14% of the Tec ORF1) in E. vannus that are transcriptionally active during new MAC development. Both the MAC-encoded transposase homologs and the MIC-encoded Tec transposase may contribute to DNA elimination in Euplotes.

In addition to domesticated transposases, genes involved in DNA cleavage and repair were also identified. Three homologs of Suppressor of Ty 16 (Spt16), essential for DNA cleavage in Paramecium, were significantly up-regulated, particularly during late conjugation when the new MAC develops. Homologs of Ku70/80 (the Ku heterodimer), key players in the nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ) repair pathway, and SUMO Small ubiquitin-related modifier 3 (SUMO III)/Ubiquitin-like modifier-activating enzyme 2 (UBA2), components of the SUMOylation machinery, were also strongly expressed during conjugation, implying their roles in processing or stabilizing the developing MAC genome.

DISCUSSION

The majority of the germline genome is extensively eliminated during soma development in spirotrichs

The extensive DNA elimination involving 60 to 90% of the germline genome is probably common among spirotrichs, much higher than the 25 to 30% eliminated MIC genome observed in many oligohymenophorean ciliates such as T. thermophila and P. tetraurelia (7, 32). For the species in the class Spirotrichea with well-assembled somatic and germline genomes, reports are currently limited to Oxytricha, Tetmemena, and Euplotes (6, 42). Programmed genome rearrangements are so far the most extensive in Oxytricha, eliminating over 90% of its germline DNA during soma development (6, 37). The recently reported somatic and germline genomes of Tetmemena are around 61 and 237 Mb, respectively, indicating ~75% elimination of the germline genome (42). However, this proportion may be underestimated because of the incomplete and fragmented germline assemblies (N50 = 14 kb). In Euplotes species, somatic genomes typically range from 50 to 100 Mb, fragmented into tens of thousands of gene-sized nanochromosomes (39–43, 62). The germline genome of E. crassus was predicted to undergo ~80% elimination during anlagen development (63). However, recent findings on Euplotes woodruffi suggested an ~58% elimination of the germline genome (MAC of 72.2 Mb versus MIC of ~172 Mb), although this figure may also be underestimated because of fragmented and incomplete germline assemblies (N50 = 44 kb) (42). In the current investigation, we assembled the ~431-Mb germline genome (N50 = 1.57 Mb) of E. vannus and reported a nearly complete soma-germ map by comparing it with the previously reported ~85-Mb somatic genome assembly (39).

The extensively eliminated sequences in spirotrichs comprise numerous TEs. In Oxytricha, a variety of TEs are found in the MIC, with telomere-bearing elements (TBEs) of the Tc1/mariner family being the most abundant (6, 64). The Oxytricha MIC genome contains ~10,000 complete TBEs and ~24,000 partial TBEs, which occupy ~15.2% (75 Mb) of the genome. Tetmemena, in the same family as Oxytricha, contains over 3000 TBE ORFs, which account for 1.8% (4.3 Mb) of its MIC genome (42). In Euplotes, E. crassus has been reported to have an abundant transposon family called Tec elements, which includes three ORFs (ORF1, ORF2, and ORF3) (44–46, 65). In addition, nearly all GSSs in Euplotes found to date have 5′-TA-3′ dinucleotide repeats at each boundary, which resemble the terminal sequences of Tc1/mariner transposons and therefore are thought to be remnants of ancient TEs (39, 49, 50, 66). In E. woodruffi, 1559 Tec ORFs were found to occupy 2.1% (3.6 Mb) of its MIC genome (42). In the present study, we detected a much larger number of complete Tec elements in E. vannus based on the high-quality genome assembly. All the ~233,000 transposons occupy nearly 50% of the germline genome, including ~135-Mb (31.3% of the germline genome) Tec ORFs. The large number of Tec elements in E. vannus explains its notably larger MIC genome size versus its congener E. woodruffi. This is similar to or even more extensive than the O. trifallax germline genome, where TBEs occupy a large proportion (15.2%). It indicates that different types of transposons may be active and have invaded different ciliate species to varying degrees, shaping the evolution of genome structure. This is further supported by studies in a broader range of ciliate species, including an investigation of nine Paramecium species (67), which provided the first direct evidence that TEs drive the extensive proliferation of GSSs. In addition, research in Blepharisma revealed that miniature inverted-repeat TEs (MITEs) are a notable source of GSSs, contributing to the largest transposon content variation due to their highly prolific nature (20).

The precise elimination of germline-limited DNA in Euplotes is determined by conjugation-specific sRNAs

As mentioned above, TEs and transposon-derived DNA comprise a varying proportion of ciliate germline genomes. Ciliates have also evolved diverse sRNA-mediated mechanisms that ensure the complete elimination of transposons in the somatic genome. Investigations in Tetrahymena and Paramecium have revealed a similar pathway, which uses germline-derived scnRNAs to mark eliminated DNA (16, 22). The bulk of transposon sequences and repetitive DNA in Paramecium and Tetrahymena are found in intergenic or intronic regions and eliminated imprecisely (7, 68, 69). However, studies in Paramecium have shown that the small GSSs (shorter than 150 bp), mostly found in coding regions, require precise excision but are less sensitive to sRNA depletion (22). Although less is known about the recognition of these short GSSs, the elimination of some of them still depends on the development-specific PRC, which is also responsible for the imprecise elimination of TEs (28). It is inferred that these short GSSs in Paramecium are evolutionarily more ancient and the cells may have acquired the ability to recognize and excise them without the help of sRNAs (28, 67, 70).

In contrast, GSSs in spirotrichs are most often located within coding regions and therefore must be precisely excised (6, 13, 42, 71). Previous studies in Oxytricha and Stylonychia have demonstrated the crucial role of a class of ~27-nt Piwi-interacting RNA (piRNAs) complementary to the parental MAC genome in MDS retention (18, 19). Our research supports a protective role in MDS retention by a class of ~30-nt sRNAs in Euplotes (Figs. 3 and 4). In addition, we further explored the precise retention of target DNA regions mediated by synthetic sRNAs, while other GSSs of the same gene remained unaffected. More broadly, the mapping quality of the 30-nt sRNA reads, which reflects the alignment specificity in the genome-wide context, also supports a low off-target probability against the entire germline genome (fig. S4A).

Moreover, we also propose that the precise boundary identification of MDSs is also achieved by ~30-nt sRNAs based on our experiments that a synthetic microinjected 30-nt sRNA only specially retained the corresponding part of a long TE GSS (Fig. 4). Coinjecting two sRNAs in the present study further supported the essential role of pointer repeats during DNA elimination. Among the cell progeny with retained GSSs, we detected cases where both sRNAs were functional, as well as cases where only sRNA2, containing a pointer repeat at the 5′ end and the 3′ overlapping with downstream MDS, was functional (Fig. 4). However, sRNA1, only containing the 5′ pointer repeat but lacking the 3′ repeat, was not found to be functional individually.

The flexible targeting of sRNAs is compatible with heterozygous sites

Despite the precise targeting of sRNAs, complete base pairing may not be strictly required during the interaction of sRNAs with the template. As shown in the present study, the synthesized haplotype sRNAs could also have a protective effect on allelic copies with a single-base mismatch (Fig. 4). Similar results were obtained in Oxytricha, where the 27-nt sRNA with two mismatches can have a protective effect (18). The consequent results suggest a more flexible mechanism for the base pairing of sRNAs with targeting loci.

For most organisms, the precise targeting of sRNAs is not determined by all the bases. In Caenorhabditis elegans, 21-nt piRNAs target germline mRNAs through seed sequence–based interactions similar to those of microRNAs (miRNAs), requiring a perfect match in nucleotides 2 to 8 and additional base pairing in nucleotides 14 to 19 (72, 73). In Drosophila ovaries and early embryos, piRNA targeting rules for mRNA binding without cleavage involve a shorter seed sequence (nucleotides 2 to 6) and a longer 3′ region sequence (nucleotides 16 to 24) (74). Piwi proteins mediate a weak seed-mRNA pairing, requiring extended downstream pairing for target recognition. However, beyond the seed region, Piwi tolerates intermittent mismatches, allowing piRNA targeting of evolving TE transcripts or more divergent mRNAs (75, 76).

This mismatch tolerance may also exist in spirotrichs and contribute to the genetic stability and the evolutionary diversity of ciliates. Considering the heterozygosity and high copy number of the parental MAC and the unequal segregation during MAC amitosis, the pairing compatibility between the sRNAs and MDS sequences in the developing MAC can ensure the retention of targeting sequences from different alleles. On the other hand, if mutations occur in the germline genome, then this compatibility during sRNA targeting can transmit these mutations to the offspring and potentially further contribute to gene diversity and even speciation. Thus, restricted mismatch tolerance both ensures the accuracy of the targeting process and allows epigenetic diversity in ciliates. The accurate but somewhat tolerant sRNA targeting represents a powerful mechanism for maintaining genetic stability and renewing the somatic genome through sexual reproduction. However, to what extent mismatches can be tolerated remains unclear. For example, the roles for the sRNAs spanning MDS-MDS junctions remain to be elucidated.

The compatibility with diverse sizes of sRNAs aligns with the Dicer-Piwi pathway

In most eukaryotes, sRNAs vary in size, with piRNAs typically ranging from 24 to 33 nt, while small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) or miRNAs are typically 20 to 25 nt long (77–80). Different ciliates have evolved mechanisms using sRNAs of varying sizes (25 to 29 nt) to guide genome rearrangements (17–20, 52). For example, Paramecium uses two classes of sRNAs in the developmental genome rearrangement process, with the early scnRNAs being ~25 nt long and the late internal eliminated sequence sRNAs (iesRNAs) ranging from 23 to 31 nt (17, 22). In Tetrahymena, both early and late scnRNAs are 26 to 32 nt in length, mainly 28 to 30 nt, with 29-nt RNAs being the most abundant (52). In spirotrichs, both Oxytricha and Stylonychia use ~27-nt piRNA in genome rearrangements (18, 19). In the present study, we found that the conjugation-specific sRNAs responsible for genome rearrangements in Euplotes are ~30 nt, which is longer than those observed in other species. For canonical Dcr, the distance between the Piwi-Argonaute-Zwille (PAZ) domain and the RNase active site determines the length of the sRNA product. Previous studies in ciliates indicated that Dcl enzymes also cleave dsRNA with preferences for different sizes (81, 82). In Paramecium, Dcl3 cleaves sRNAs of varying sizes, whereas Dcl2 produces specific 25-nt sRNAs, and both proteins have to cooperate to form mature ~25-nt early scnRNAs. The varying sizes of late iesRNAs are cleaved by Dcl5. In Tetrahymena, both early and late scnRNAs are cleaved by Dcl1, indicating that Dcl1 also cleaves sRNAs of varying sizes (82). In the present study, we show that the conjugation-specific sRNAs in Euplotes are cleaved by Dcl1 with preferences for ~30 nt. Although all these Dcl enzymes contain a PAZ domain and a pair of RNase III domains, it is still structurally unclear how these proteins produce sRNAs of varying lengths.

These Dcl cleaved conjugation-specific sRNAs in ciliates are loaded onto specific Piwi proteins (16–18, 22, 52). In the present study, although conjugation-specific sRNAs in Euplotes are ~30 nt, injections of longer 33- and 35-bp RNAs or shorter 27-bp RNAs also resulted in GSS retention, which means that the Piwi protein in Euplotes is compatible with varying sizes of sRNAs. Similar results were also obtained in Tetrahymena and Paramecium. In Tetrahymena, the Piwi protein Twi1p is responsible for binding both early and late scnRNAs (26 to 32 nt in length) (83). In Paramecium, Ptiwi09 binds only scnRNAs, Ptiwi10 binds only iesRNAs, and both proteins do not have a length preference for the bound sRNAs (84).

Present experiments in Euplotes showed that both single- and double-stranded sRNAs can target GSS regions for retention, potentially indicating the involvement of the Slicer domain in Piwi. However, it was also observed that injections with single-stranded sRNAs were less efficient compared to injections with double-stranded sRNAs or mixtures of sense and antisense sRNAs, which may anneal to form double-stranded molecules (Fig. 3). This means that both single- and double-stranded sRNA molecules are recognized and bound by the Piwi proteins. It is different in Oxytricha, where injection of synthetic single-stranded 27-nt RNAs corresponding to normally deleted regions led to their retention (18). A previous study in Tetrahymena showed that the passenger strand of RNA molecules is cleaved by the Slicer domain of the Piwi protein (Twi1p) upon loading of Dcl cleaved double-stranded sRNA (83). One of the Piwi proteins containing the Slicer domain (Eviwi02) is found to coexpress with Dcl1 during conjugation in Euplotes (fig. S9, B and C) (33). Piwi proteins could bind both single- and double-stranded sRNA intermediates, with the former not requiring the activity of the Slicer domain. The observed difference in injection efficiency between single- and double-stranded sRNAs may be related to technical reasons in the experimental manipulation. dsRNA is more stable and resistant to degradation than single-stranded RNA, resulting in higher efficiency in microinjection experiments.

The 3′-end nontemplated mono- or oligoadenylation of endogenized 30-nt sRNAs is likely associated with their degradation

Emerging evidence indicates that sRNAs exhibit a diversity of terminal modifications, including phosphorylation, methylation, adenylation/uridylation, exonucleolytic degradation, and other terminal modifications. These modifications play important roles in regulating the stability, function, and interactions of sRNAs (85–87).

In ciliates, the 3′-end 2′ O-methylation of scnRNAs has been identified in Tetrahymena (88), which stabilizes sRNAs by shielding them from 3′ uridylation. Oxytricha’s piRNAs have been found to contain 5′ monophosphate but lack 3′-end modifications (18). In Euplotes, we observed the nontemplated addition of adenylate(s) to the 3′ ends of 30-nt sRNA molecules. sRNAs longer than 30 nt tend to exhibit a higher frequency of 3′ terminal adenylates′, which show poor alignment to the genomic sequence (Fig. 2H), in contrast to the preceding matched bases, indicating posttranscriptional adenylation. A similar but weaker adenylation signal was also observed in the 27-nt piRNAs in Oxytricha, but not in the 25- to 29-nt scnRNAs in Tetrahymena or Paramecium (fig. S4, C and D).

Adenylation of sRNAs in various species serves diverse functions, including either degradation or stabilization of sRNAs, as seen in Arabidopsis (89), zebra fish (90), and human cells/exosomes (86). We further searched for homologs of the noncanonical enzymes involved in 3′-end modification of sRNAs: the polyadenylate polymerase Germline development defective 2 (GLD-2) (91, 92), which mediates 3′ adenylation of miRNA to increase stability in human cells; and the polymerase Poly(A) polymerase-associated domain containing protein 5 (PAPD5) and the exonuclease Poly(A)-specific ribonuclease (PARN) (93), which also mediate 3′ adenylation of miRNA but facilitate 3′ to 5′ degradation. Among all the 37 homologs we found in Euplotes, three of PAPD5 and one of PARN were observed to be significantly up-regulated during the late conjugation (fig. S4B). The function of these adenylated sRNAs in Euplotes is uncertain. However, given the late accumulation of adenylated sRNAs during the conjugation, along with the late expression of PAPD5 and PARN homologs, it is highly likely that the adenylation for 30-nt sRNAs in Euplotes is associated with their degradation after fulfilling their protective role in genome rearrangement.

Developmental sRNAs are generated from long dsRNAs via bidirectional transcription initiated at subtelemeric E-CBS sites in the MAC chromosomes

In the present study, we provided evidence supporting the protective roles of soma-derived sRNAs in Euplotes. Similar findings have also been reported in other spirotrichs (e.g., Oxytricha and Stylonychia) with extensively fragmented MAC genomes (18, 31). However, the endogenous biogenesis of these sRNAs remains unclear. In these species, the sRNA biogenesis occurs in the early stages of conjugation, from the formation of conjugating pairs to the initiation of anlagen development, spanning only a short period of ~12 hours (11). During this time, in addition to the production of genome-wide sRNAs, a large number of functional genes also have to be transcribed from the nanochromosomes in support of development (fig. S8A). Therefore, simultaneously regulating the synthesis of a sufficient amount of sRNAs from tens of thousands of chromosomes represents a major regulatory challenge.

It has been hypothesized that the biogenesis of ~27-nt sRNA precursors in Oxytricha originate from telomere-primed transcription of both DNA strands (31). However, this hypothesis is challenged by structural evidence that telomeres occur in a stable G-quadruplex conformation (94, 95), which is likely incompatible with efficient telomere-priming activity due to restricted accessibility [as reviewed in (96, 97)]. An investigation of RNA-seq in Stylonychia advocated the idea that a pool of transcribed mRNAs could serve as the substrate for ~27-nt sRNA biogenesis (19). However, this model does not account for the retention of non-mRNA regions (e.g., introns and untranscribed regions) and inactive genes, nor does it address the potential conflict between mRNA translation and sRNA processing (whether they should be translated or cleaved).

In the present study, the poor coverage of telomeric and subtelomeric regions by ~30-nt sRNAs in Euplotes does not support the use of telomeres as TSSs for sRNA precursors (fig. S7B). The comparable coverage of introns and exons (Fig. 2, D to F), along with the low correlation between mRNA expression levels and 30-nt sRNA abundance (Fig. 2C and fig. S3A), does not support the hypothesis that mRNAs serve as precursors of sRNAs.

On the basis of the present data, we propose that the conserved E-CBS motifs at both subtelomeric regions of MAC chromosomes in Euplotes may function as the TSSs for initiating bidirectional transcription. Strand-specific RNA-seq also recovered both sense and antisense transcripts of the MAC chromosomes with the TSS at the E-CBS motifs. Taking the conserved E-CBS motif at subtelomeric regions as TSS can facilitate genome-wide transcription within a couple of hours. It can also overcome the shortcomings regarding the use of telomeres as TSS or the use of mRNA as substrate for generating double-stranded sRNA precursors. The moderate correlation between ~30-nt sRNA expression level and somatic DNA copy number, which is better than that between these sRNAs and RNA-seq, also supported that all MAC chromosomes are transcribed at levels roughly proportional to the underlying DNA copy number (Fig. 2C and fig. S3A). In addition, E-CBS or a similar motif was also found in diverse Euplotes species (fig. S7D), indicating an evolutionarily conserved mechanism for conjugation-specific sRNAs production.

The presence of dsRNA precursors was confirmed by anti-dsRNA immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 5). Loss of Dcl1 in Euplotes, homologs of Dcl2/3/5 in Paramecium and Dcl1 in Tetrahymena that cleaves dsRNA, impairs the production of mature 30-nt sRNAs (Fig. 4). It also indicates that the sRNA precursors are double stranded. However, the mechanism by which single-stranded RNAs are converted to double-stranded remains unclear. There are two hypotheses: The two separate single-stranded sense and antisense transcripts get annealed or become double-stranded through complementary strand synthesis mediated by RdRPs, similar to the RdRP-mediated synthesis of siRNAs in Paramecium (98) and other eukaryotes (77). Among the eight RdRP homologs we found in E. vannus, at least three had pronounced expression peaks during the early conjugation stage (Fig. 6C and fig. S9A), which indicated that they may play roles in dsRNA synthesis. Therefore, we prefer the second hypothesis, also presuming that spontaneous annealing of complementary RNA strands may be inefficient in a crowded transcriptome environment.

Integrating the outcomes in the present study, we proposed a model depicting the role of soma-derived 30-nt sRNAs in the genome rearrangement process of E. vannus (Fig. 8). During early conjugation, parental MAC chromosomes undergo global bidirectional transcription initiated at subtelomeric E-CBS motifs (5′-TTGAA-3′). These transcripts are likely converted into dsRNAs by RdRP and processed into sRNAs by the Dicer-like enzyme Dcl1. These sRNAs are then loaded onto Piwi proteins (probably Eviwi02 and/or Eviwi01) and transported into the developing new MAC. Sequence-specific sRNA-Piwi targeting precisely protects homologous DNA regions from elimination, with tolerance for heterozygous sites to preserve allelic integrity and ensure faithful inheritance. Conversely, GSSs lacking sRNA-Piwi protection are excised, while chromosome breakage at conserved CBS sites and subsequent telomere addition yields mature MAC nanochromosomes. Posttargeting, the 30-nt sRNAs undergo 3′ nontemplated adenylation, presumably marking them for degradation. This model highlights a unique strategy for sRNA-mediated genome regulation and indicates that sRNA production is evolutionarily co-opted with chromosomal breakage—a dual-function system enabling both transposon control and genome restructuring.

Proposed model depicting the role of soma-derived 30-nt sRNAs in the genome rearrangement process of E. vannus.

The left panel illustrates the nuclear events during conjugation. The right panel depicts the proposed molecular mechanism by which 30-nt sRNAs mediate precise DNA elimination. During early conjugation, parental MAC chromosomes are globally transcribed, using E-CBS (5′-TTGAA-3′) as TSSs. dsRNAs are likely synthesized by RdRP and subsequently cleaved into sRNAs by Dcl1. These sRNAs are loaded onto Piwi proteins (most likely Eviwi02 and/or Eviwi01). Guided by the sequence specificity of the sRNAs, the sRNA-Eviwi complexes are transported into the developing new MAC, where they precisely target DNA regions against elimination. Notably, sRNA targeting can tolerate heterozygosity, ensuring proper inheritance after fertilization. GSS regions lacking sRNA-Eviwi targeting are eliminated, while chromosomes break at CBS sites, followed by telomere addition to form mature MAC chromosomes. In addition, the 30-nt sRNAs undergo nontemplated adenylation at their 3′ ends, likely marking them for degradation after fulfilling their roles.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample preparation

E. vannus strains EvL and EvB2 with different mating types were used in this study. Cell culture and conjugation induction were performed as previously described (11, 39, 99). Conjugation was induced by mixing reactive cells of these two strains. Cells were collected 0, 4, 8, 12, 30, 50, and 75 hpm for total RNA extraction. Two replicates were prepared for each time point. The developmental stage was confirmed by nuclear morphology based on previous reports using Hoechst 33342 under the same conditions (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Jiangsu, China) (11).

Germline genomic DNA isolation, sequencing, assembly, and annotations

Total genomic DNA was extracted using phenol-chloroform and digested using 40 U of the methylation-sensitive Dpn I restriction endonuclease [catalog no. R0176, New England Biolabs (NEB)] for 3 hours to deplete bacterial DNA. Germline DNA molecules longer than 10 kb were isolated from the processed DNA using a BluePippin system. The HiFi SMRTbell sequencing library was constructed using the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit (2.0) with ~15-kb segments. The isolated DNA was sequenced with PacBio long-read system in circular consensus sequencing mode. The sequencing strategy yielded around 1 Tb of subreads and 300-Gb HiFi reads.

The HiFi reads were assembled using hifiasm (0.19.5-r587) (100) and Canu (2.2, -pacbio-hifi) (101), respectively. The haplotigs and overlaps in each assembly were purged using Purge_Dups (1.2.5) (102) based on the read mapping by minimap2 (2.24-r1122) (103) to produce a haplotype genome assembly. To further deplete contaminating sequences, BLAST (2.10.0+) (104) was used to search the purged contigs (parameter: -evalue 1e-10) against its somatic genome (39) and GenBank’s nucleotide database (up to November 2023), respectively. Nonsoma contigs that could align with bacterial sequences with a query coverage more than 50% or had relatively high GC content (>40%) but low sequencing depth (<10×) or extremely high GC content (>50%) but relatively low sequencing depth (<30×) were also removed as contaminations (fig. S1, B to P). Subsequently, the two sets of independently assembled contigs were merged and rejoined using quick_merge (105). The metrics of the final assembly were evaluated using Quast (106).

For genome rearrangement annotations, SDRAP (107) was used to annotate MDSs, pointers, and MIC-specific regions in E. vannus following a previous study (42) (minimum percent identity for preliminary match annotation = 95, minimum percent identity for additional match annotation = 90, and minimum length of pointer annotation = 2).

The repeat content in the MIC genomes was identified by RepeatModeler (1.0.10) (108) and RepeatMasker (4.0.7) (www.repeatmasker.org) with default parameters. TBE and Tec ORFs of TEs annotated by RepeatModeler were detected following previous studies (42, 64). The chromosomal structure of the ~5-Mb germline chromosome was visualized using Circos (109).

RNA extraction and high-throughput sequencing

Total RNA extraction was performed with the TRIzol reagent (catalog no. 15596026, Invitrogen). To detect sRNA, total RNA was separated on a 15% denaturing polyacrylamide gel in 1× TBE buffer and stained with gel stain (catalog no. GS101, Transgen, China). The loading amount for each sample was 15 μg of total RNA.

The sequencing library construction and Illumina sequencing for mRNA, sRNA, and rRNA-depleted RNA were performed by Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). Briefly, the mRNA-seq libraries were generated using NEBNext Ultra RNA library prep kit for Illumina (catalog no. E7530, NEB) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Before mRNA library preparation, polyadenylated RNA was isolated using magnetic beads coupling of oligo(dT) (catalog no. N401-01/02, VAHTS mRNA Capture Beads, Vazyme, China). The sRNA libraries were prepared using VAHTS sRNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina V2 (catalog no. NR811). Strand-specific rRNA-depleted RNA-seq libraries were generated using the NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (catalog no. E7420, NEB) following the manufacturer’s instructions. rRNA depletion was attempted using TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Gold Kit (catalog no. 20020599, Illumina). The qualified libraries were pooled and sequenced on Illumina platforms with paired-end reads of 150 bp (mRNA and total RNA libraries) or single-end reads of 50-bp (sRNA libraries) sequencing strategy. In total, we sequenced at least 6 Gb of raw data for each mRNA library, 10 Gb of raw data for each strand-specific rRNA-depleted RNA library, and 10 million reads for each sRNA library. The raw reads were evaluated and processed by FastP [v0.20.0, -c (only for PE reads), -q 30 (only for sRNA reads)] to discard low-quality reads (110).

Full-length, rRNA-depleted RNA library preparation and Oxford Nanopore sequencing were performed by Benagen Technology Co. Ltd. (Wuhan, China). rRNA was depleted from total RNA using the Ribo-off rRNA Depletion Kit (catalog no. N406-01, Vazyme, China). The resulting RNA was ligated at the 3′ end with an RNA adapter using the T4 RNA Ligase 1 (catalog no. M0204S, NEB). First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using Maxima H Minus Reverse Transcriptase (catalog no. EP0752, Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by second-strand synthesis and amplification using the LongAmp Taq DNA Polymerase (catalog no. M0287S, NEB). The resulting double-stranded cDNA was purified using AMPure XP beads and used for library preparation. The sequencing was carried on a PromethION platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK). Raw ONT data were basecalled by dorado (v0.9.0, https://github.com/nanoporetech/dorado), filtered with NanoFilt (2.8.0, -q 7 -l 10) (111), and trimmed using pychopper (2.7.10, -m edlib, https://github.com/epi2me-labs/pychopper).

Update of MAC genome annotations

Sequencing data were aligned with the E. vannus MAC genome reported previously (39). The MAC genome gene annotation was updated on the basis of more informative mRNA-seq data during conjugation that we obtained in the present study, allowing us to capture and supplement conjugation-specific genes. mRNA transcripts were assembled using StringTie (2.2.1) (112) based on the reads alignments with the MAC genome by Hisat2 (2.2.1, --dta) (113). The MAC genome assembly with all the introns (identified from the mapped transcripts) removed was aligned against the Swiss-Prot database (up to November 2023) using BLAST (blastx -query_gencode 10 -evalue 1e-5) (104) to identify ORFs. Conventional UGA stop codons were modified to encode cysteine as reported before (38). For the transcripts lacking ORFs identified, TransDecoder (5.5.0, -G Euplotid, https://github.com/TransDecoder/TransDecoder/) was used to extract the long ORFs and predict the likely coding regions of them. The ORFs identified by BLAST alignments were then used to build a training set for AUGUSTUS (3.3.3) (114), which was used to perform de novo gene prediction for the genes without assembled transcripts. Frameshifting events were detected following the protocol outlined in previous studies (39, 40). Briefly, transcripts exhibiting multiple reading frames that aligned with identical proteins from the SwissProt database, with less than 10 bp between frames, were identified. Predicted protein products were annotated by aligning them to Pfam-A database (34.0) using InterProScan (5.56-89.0) (115) and to SwissProt database using DIAMOND (2.0.15.153, --evalue 1e-5) (116).

Transcriptome analysis

The mapping of sRNA reads was performed using Bowtie2 (2.3.4.2) (117). The qualified reads were initially filtered by aligning them to the genome of the food source Escherichia coli (GenBank: NZ_CP125702.1). Contig coverage by sRNA reads was calculated by SAMtools (1.14) (118) and BEDTools (2.30.0) (119). Base composition trends across sRNAs for particular size classes were visualized using weblogo2 (120). Read abundance across sequence features of interest was estimated using featureCounts (2.0.1) (121). Strand-specific total RNA reads were aligned to the relevant genomes using Hisat2 (2.2.1, --dta --rna-strandness RF) (113) and assembled using StringTie (1.3.6, --rf) (112).

Alignment and assembly of mRNA-seq data were performed as described above. The RNA-seq data during vegetative growth (39) were used as background for detecting differentially expressed genes (DEGs) during conjugation. The expression fold changes were estimated by DESeq2 (122). Genes with at least fourfold changes in expression and adjusted P values (Padj; through Benjamini-Hochberg procedure) less than 0.1 were classified as DEGs. Gene coexpression networks were constructed using WGCNA (123).

Qualified ONT reads were aligned with the MAC genome using Minimap2 (2.24-r1122, -x splice -uf -k14) (103). Aligned bam was converted to GFF file using spliced_bam2gff (https://github.com/nanoporetech/spliced_bam2gff). Gffcompare (0.11.5, -k --strict-match) (124) was then used to identify antisense transcripts based on the classification code “x.” Mean per-bin base depth across all chromosomes was calculated using the coverage module of BEDTools (2.30.0) (119). Data visualizations were performed using R packages including ggplot2 (125), ggpointdensity (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/ggpointdensity/index.html), and pheatmap (https://github.com/raivokolde/pheatmap).

RNA oligonucleotide synthesis and microinjections

sRNAs were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and dissolved in nuclease-free water to a final concentration of 20 nM. The double-stranded sRNAs were used directly, while the single-stranded sRNAs were heated to 65°C for 1 min and chilled on ice before experimental use. The dissolved sRNAs were injected into the cytoplasm of one of the paired cells between 6 and 12 hours postmixing, using Eppendorf InjectMan 4 with the FemtoJet 4i. Cells were examined for donut shapes (prominent, clear developing new MACs) between 20 and 60 hpm and transferred into separate wells for vegetative growth. The observation was performed under an “Olympus SZX16” light microscope. After 7 to 10 postconjugation divisions, single cells of each isolation were directly used in each PCR reaction to detect GSS retention. The sequences of RNA oligonucleotides and PCR primers are listed in table S3.

Survival test and developmental characterization of Dcl1 knockdown

Two Dcl1-mutated strains, generated by microinjection with synthesized sRNAs (GSS+), were cultured for at least 30 days (30 to 60 generations of vegetative growth) before being starved to induce conjugation. Thirty-six mating pairs were placed separately into each well (filled with sterile marine water) of 12 three-well slides and placed in a humidified chamber. We then observed and counted cells using light microscopy (Olympus SZX16) at 12, 24, 36, 48, 72, 84, and 96 hpm.

Phylogenetic analysis

Euplotes Dcr/Dcl proteins (Dcr1 to Dcr3 and Dcl1 to Dcl2) were retrieved on the basis of one-way BLAST–by BLASTP (104) against the somatic genome assembly (39), using Tetrahymena Dcl1 (TGD: TTHERM_00284230), Paramecium Dcl1 to Dcl5 (ParameciumDB: PTET.51.1.P0360062, PTET.51.1.P0210241, PTET.51.1.P099007, PTET.51.1.P1280115, and PTET.51.1.P0070121), and Oxytricha Dcl1 to Dcl3 and Dcr [ciliates.org and (126): Contig15171.0.g96, Contig119.0.g64, Contig18208.0.g33, and Contig17740.0.g110] as the query sequences. Phylogenetic analysis of Dcr/Dcl proteins was performed using maximum likelihood via IQtree (v2.1.4, -b 1000) (127) after aligned using T-coffee (v11.00) (128). Bootstrap values were obtained from 1000 resampled datasets. Visualization was performed by figtree (v1.4.4, https://github.com/rambaut/figtree).

Immunofluorescence staining

Conjugating cells were collected and suspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 4% formaldehyde and 0.5% Triton X-100 for 30 min at room temperature for fixation and permeabilization. After washing three times with PBST [PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 1% Tween 20], cells were incubated at 4°C overnight with the primary antibody against dsRNA (1:1000; catalog no. 10010200, J2, Scicons, Szirák, Hungary). Next, cells were washed three times with PBST and then incubated with secondary antibody (1:4000; catalog no. A11017, Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse immunoglobulin G, Invitrogen) for 30 min at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, China; final concentration, 0.1 μg/ml) for 15 min at room temperature. Both the primary and secondary antibodies were diluted in TBST/BSA [10 mM tris and 0.15 M NaCl (pH 7.4), containing 0.1% Tween 20 and 3% BSA]. The observation was performed under a “ZEISS AXIO Imager D2” microscope, equipped with an Axiocam 506 camera for photographic documentation.
