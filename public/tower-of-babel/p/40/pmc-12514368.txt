Why patients say no: Patient barriers to epilepsy surgery among patients with drug‐resistant epilepsy in Singapore
Authors: Zhibin Tan, Meenatchii Shanmugham, Sangeetha Adiyapatham, Zhi En Chan, Zhi Yi Chan, Hua Chan Ling, Jia Yi Shen, Tushar Gosavi, Deidre Anne De Silva, Shih Hui Lim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEpilepsy surgery is globally underutilized due to disease‐, system‐, and patient‐related barriers. These barriers are suboptimally understood in Asia. Patient‐related barriers are of particular interest because they are culture‐specific and may present a significant obstacle regardless of resource availability. This study aims to identify patient‐related barriers in Singapore, an ethnically heterogeneous Asian country.MethodsWe conducted a cross‐sectional survey of focal drug‐resistant epilepsy (DRE) patients attending the neurology specialist outpatient clinic of a major tertiary hospital in Singapore. The survey contained 28 items assessing patient perceptions of epilepsy and its treatment, and 7 items on the patient's socioeconomic status. It was adapted with permission from a previous Canadian study.ResultsA total of 66 patients completed the survey. Most patients (74.2%) were aware of surgery as an option for the treatment of epilepsy, and patients with university degrees were more likely to be aware (p = 0.007). Most (69.7%) viewed surgery as a “last resort,” and 83.3% overestimated the risks of surgery. Even when hypothetically offered guarantees of surgical success, only 57.6% of patients responded favorably to surgery, with those acknowledging their seizures as disabling being more likely to respond favorably (p = 0.027). Financial considerations did not significantly influence reactions to hypothetically successful surgery (p = 1.000).SignificanceThere are major patient‐related barriers to epilepsy surgery among DRE patients in Singapore, contributed by an overestimation of surgical risks and an indifference to seizure‐related disability in some patients. Based on our findings, financial subsidies alone may not suffice in addressing patient‐related barriers; accompanying patient education efforts may be important.Plain Language SummaryA survey of patients with refractory epilepsy in Singapore revealed that patients are relatively averse to undergoing pre‐surgical evaluation and surgical treatment for epilepsy, despite scientific evidence of effectiveness. Some of the major barriers include patients overestimating the risks of epilepsy surgery and perceiving seizures to be non‐disabling. Our findings suggest that financial subsidies for surgery alone may not overcome patients' aversion to surgery, and that patient education is also required.Keywords: Asia, barriers, drug‐resistant, epilepsy, patient, surgery

INTRODUCTION

As per International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) recommendations, evaluation for epilepsy surgery is indicated in any patient with drug‐resistant epilepsy (DRE), defined by a failure of adequate trials of two or more appropriately chosen and dosed anti‐seizure medications (ASMs), whether used as monotherapies or in combination, to achieve sustained seizure freedom. 1 , 2 Sustained seizure freedom is defined as a minimum of three times the longest pre‐intervention inter‐seizure interval (determined from seizures occurring within the last 12 months), or 12 months, whichever is longer. 1 The indication is even stronger for patients with focal epilepsy. 3

DRE is common. International studies suggest a prevalence of DRE of over 30% among adults with epilepsy. 4 A study in 2014 suggested that the prevalence in Singapore was 21.5%. 5 In accordance with ILAE recommendations, it is preferred for patients who are drug‐resistant to undergo pre‐surgical evaluation. Even if patients with DRE do not subsequently undergo surgery, the pre‐surgical evaluation is of significant benefit to them. 2 For instance, medical care delivered by a specialized epilepsy team is associated with longer life expectancy. 6 In addition, as many as one third of patients with presumed DRE may be identified to have non‐epileptic attacks rather than epileptic seizures, and, therefore, can be appropriately weaned off anti‐seizure medications. 7 Pre‐surgical epilepsy evaluation can also help to identify the etiology and type of epilepsy that a patient has, allowing for more targeted treatment of the underlying cause, as well as appropriate selection of ASMs. 2

Despite the high prevalence of DRE, and despite the benefits of pre‐surgical evaluation and epilepsy surgery for patients with DRE, epilepsy surgery remains globally underutilized. 8 The reasons for this underutilization span disease‐, system‐, and patient‐related factors. For instance, disease‐related factors such as a non‐lesional MRI brain scan, and system‐related factors such as physician perceptions of the utility of epilepsy surgery and timing of referral have been described to impact the utilization of epilepsy surgery. 8 A Canadian study published in 2013 described significant patient‐related barriers to epilepsy surgery, such as a preference for alternative medicine over surgery (46.7%), a perception that surgery should be a “last resort” (61%), and an aversion to surgery even if they were guaranteed to become seizure‐free without damage to their brains (14%). 9

In Asia, the barriers to epilepsy surgery remain suboptimally understood. A previous ASEAN study, conducted mainly through the perspective of healthcare providers, identified a lack of expertise, facilities, and funding as key factors contributing to underutilization. 10 In some parts of Asia, such as Singapore, there are potentially sufficient resources to overcome barriers, but these barriers must first be identified. There is a need to study patient‐related barriers in particular, since culture‐specific differences may have a greater influence on patient‐related barriers than on other factors.

The aim of this study is to identify patient‐related barriers to epilepsy surgery in Singapore, where the population is predominantly Asian but ethnically heterogeneous.

METHODS

This is a cross‐sectional study of focal DRE patients attending the adult neurology specialist outpatient clinic of a major tertiary hospital in Singapore, from October 2024 to June 2025. The study protocol was approved by the SingHealth Central Institutional Review Board (CIRB), reference number 2024/2071.

The electronic medical records (EMR) of patients attending three epileptologists' clinics were screened by two investigators 1 week before their scheduled visits. The inclusion criteria sought subjects who (1) were at least 21 years of age, (2) had a current diagnosis of focal drug‐resistant epilepsy fulfilling the ILAE criteria of failure of adequate trials of two or more ASMs to achieve sustained seizure freedom, and (3) had the ability to provide informed consent themselves. The exclusion criteria were (1) resolved epilepsy and (2) a component of generalized epilepsy. All patients provided informed consent.

The attending epileptologist was consulted to confirm the eligibility of each patient identified, based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Whenever data from the EMR were inadequate to determine DRE status, the attending epileptologist was also consulted to make the determination. If the attending epileptologist required clinical information from the next planned consultation, the determination was only made after that consultation took place. Only patients whose eligibility were confirmed by their attending epileptologists were approached for recruitment.

Upon recruitment to the study, a 35‐item survey was administered (Appendix S1). No pre‐survey information or counseling on epilepsy surgery was provided. The survey was adapted from the questionnaire used in a previous Canadian study conducted by Hrazdil et al., 9 with permission from the copyright owner (license number 6011260456020). Questions in the survey were modified for contextualization to Singapore. Items that referred to driving were removed because Singapore law indiscriminately disallows driving for anyone with active epilepsy. An option regarding long‐distance travel for treatment was removed, as it was irrelevant in the context of a small city‐state like Singapore. The options for questions about race, education, and income were modified to reflect Singaporean ethnic groups, educational tiers, income sources, and income brackets. After these modifications, the survey had a Flesch–Kincaid reading ease score of 69.32. The survey was administered in English and conducted digitally, hosted on a secure data collection platform maintained by the Singapore government.

In addition to the survey, data on patients' epilepsy classification, most recent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan of the brain, and previous electroencephalography (EEG) findings were collected from the EMR.

Data were analyzed using SPSS (version 26.0, Chicago, IL, USA). The Pearson Chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests were used for categorical variables. The Mann–Whitney U‐test was used to analyze non‐normally distributed data. Multivariate analysis was performed using logistic regression.

Most respondents (90.9%) had not had epilepsy surgery before, but the majority (74.2%) were aware of surgery as an option for the treatment of epilepsy. Patients with undergraduate or post‐graduate degrees were more likely to be aware of surgery as a treatment option for epilepsy (p = 0.007). The most common sources of information on epilepsy surgery were the patient's neurologist (85.7%) and the Internet (24.5%). When asked about their level of trust in healthcare providers, nearly all (93.9%) respondents expressed trust, with the remaining 6.1% returning a neutral answer. No participants expressed mistrust in healthcare providers.

The majority of patients (71.2%) opined that epilepsy surgery was moderately or very dangerous, and most patients (83.3%) overestimated the risk of serious side effects from surgery to be more than 10%. Nearly half (47.0%) believed that epilepsy surgery was more dangerous than uncontrolled seizures; these patients had no correlation with patients who indicated that their seizures were not disabling (p = 0.383). A total of 69.7% of patients believed that epilepsy surgery should be considered a “last resort.”

Patients who had undergone epilepsy surgery before were less likely to view surgery as dangerous (p = 0.006); none of them overestimated the risk of surgery (p < 0.001). However, their history of epilepsy surgery did not appear to protect them against a perception that surgery is more dangerous than uncontrolled seizures (p = 0.202) or an impression of surgery as a “last resort” (p = 0.357).

The most common concerns surrounding epilepsy surgery were getting a new health problem (65.2%), perioperative complications (62.1%), and finance‐related concerns (50.0%). Compared to epilepsy surgery, 47.0% preferred a trial of alternative medicine, and 65.2% preferred to participate in research studies testing new non‐surgical treatment.

About a quarter of respondents (25.8%) indicated that they had previously been discouraged from undergoing epilepsy surgery. The most common sources of discouragement were family (82.4%) and friends (29.4%); the most frequently cited reason was a perceived high risk of surgery (52.9%).

When asked about their candidacy for epilepsy surgery, respondents who were uncertain formed the largest group (44.9%), followed by respondents who were candidates but were uninterested (24.2%). Out of those who were uninterested despite their candidacy, the most cited reason for their disinterest was a perceived high risk of surgery (68.8%).

The most common goal of epilepsy treatment was seizure‐freedom (74.2%). Despite this, when presented with the hypothetical situation of guaranteed lifelong post‐operative seizure‐freedom, with or without ASMs, and no resulting damage to the brain (henceforth known as “guaranteed successful surgery”), only 57.6% of all patients responded affirmatively (i.e., somewhat agree or strongly agree) to the notion of undergoing surgery. On univariate analysis, respondents who were less than 45 years‐old, came from households with monthly incomes exceeding $3000 Singapore dollars (SGD), have undergraduate or post‐graduate degrees, or perceive their seizures to be disabling were more likely to respond affirmatively. On logistic regression, a perception of seizures being disabling retained a significant association with affirmation of guaranteed successful surgery (odds ratio 6.993, p = 0.027). DISCUSSION

Our study is the first in Singapore to explore and quantify patient‐related barriers to epilepsy surgery.

We found significant patient‐related barriers to epilepsy surgery, relative to Canadian patients as reported by Hrazdil et al. Figure 1 compares the results of our survey, in selected items. We observed that 69.7% of Singaporean DRE patients considered surgery to be a “last resort,” compared to 61% among Canadian patients with focal epilepsy. 9 This suggests the presence of a strong aversion to epilepsy surgery among Singaporean DRE patients. This aversion may be partially attributable to a lack of knowledge, leading to an overestimation of surgical risks. While the risk of serious side effects from epilepsy surgery is known to be less than 10%, we found that 83.3% of Singaporean DRE patients overestimated this risk, compared to 60% of Canadian focal epilepsy patients. 9 , 11 Furthermore, among the respondents who were candidates for epilepsy surgery but were uninterested, the most commonly cited reason for their disinterest was a perception of high surgical risk. A lack of access to public information may be contributory to risk overestimation, as suggested by the fact that only two respondents had learned about epilepsy surgery from the newspapers, while none had heard about surgery from epilepsy agencies or support groups. The two major epilepsy support groups in Singapore—Singapore Epilepsy Foundation and Epilepsy Care Group—do not avail any information on epilepsy surgery on their websites.

We observed that respondents with university degrees were more likely to be aware of epilepsy surgery, perhaps suggesting that public awareness efforts should target patients with lower levels of education; though we hesitate to draw conclusions about whether access to information will necessarily influence a patient's knowledge. For instance, a greater openness to information among people of higher academic achievement may account for the difference in awareness by level of education, which public education campaigns cannot modify. 12

Nonetheless, the fact that an overwhelming 93.9% of respondents expressed trust in healthcare providers (such as doctors and nurses) attests to the influence that healthcare workers have among Singaporean DRE patients. This high level of trust in healthcare providers may reflect a broader trend among Asian‐predominant populations, since it is corroborated by other studies. 13 , 14 The choice of presenters for information about epilepsy surgery should be informed by this finding. Furthermore, previous studies have suggested troubling levels of unawareness regarding the current standards of practice for epilepsy surgery among clinicians themselves. 15 , 16 Taken in conjunction with our earlier concern that public education campaigns may have an uncertain impact, steps to improve physician awareness will be important, so that physician‐driven education in the clinics can be improved. 17 It is also worth noting that approximately one‐quarter of DRE patients had been discouraged from having surgery, most frequently by their family and friends. This finding may inform providers' choice of who to invite for epilepsy surgery counseling.

Even when the effect of misjudging surgical risk should have been mitigated by the hypothetical offer of guaranteed successful surgery, only 57.6% of Singaporean DRE patients responded favorably. We found that patients who perceived their epilepsy/seizure disorder to be disabling were more likely to respond favorably to guarantee successful surgery. This suggests that for some patients, even if their miscalculated risk of surgery were to be addressed by counseling or education, a benign impression of their condition may continue to limit their interest in surgery.

The adult DRE patients in our study had a mean age of 47.7 years old, which is older than studies of DRE patients performed at centers with pediatric or adolescent patients. 18 This older age profile allowed us to observe a trend of adult patients under 45 years of age being potentially more receptive to guaranteed successful surgery. This may be because patients who are further away from the age of life expectancy are more likely to assign a greater value to any long‐term benefit from surgery than older patients would. Another possible explanation is that older patients may have a general aversion to invasive medical procedures. While this has not been specifically investigated in epilepsy patients, other studies have observed greater wariness of surgical procedures in general among older adults. 19 Given that epilepsy surgery in older adults has been reported to have good outcomes matching those seen in younger patients, extra attention should be given to identifying and addressing age‐related concerns when counseling DRE patients on epilepsy surgery. 20 , 21

As Singapore's healthcare system has a mixed financial structure in which patients share at least some of the cost of their own treatment, we wondered if the challenge of financing surgical treatment hindered acceptance of guaranteed successful surgery. However, while 50.7% of respondents had indicated concerns over “how the surgery would be paid for,” this concern did not have any statistical correlation with disinclination toward guaranteed successful surgery. This finding is important, because it suggests that financial subsidies for epilepsy surgery need to be accompanied by efforts to address non‐financial patient barriers.

Our study found significant support for alternative medicine and investigational non‐surgical treatment of epilepsy, as preferred alternatives to surgery. This is a potentially useful finding for Asian countries such as Singapore, where there are ongoing efforts to integrate traditional and alternative medicine into national health programs. 22 , 23 For instance, Singapore might consider exploring acupuncture for patients who declined surgery, since acupuncture has been associated with modestly reduced seizure frequency and improved quality of life when combined with Western pharmacological treatment of epilepsy. 24 , 25

A limitation of our study is the lack of prospective follow‐up; hence, limiting our ability to correlate our survey responses with actual clinical outcomes, such as eventual consent and participation in epilepsy surgery. However, 9.1% of our respondents were patients who had undergone epilepsy surgery before, yet continued to have an active diagnosis of DRE (and hence met our inclusion criteria). These patients' survey responses thus offered valuable insight. Having undergone surgery before, these patients would have had a more accurate mental image of what epilepsy surgery entails. Accordingly, we found that they were less likely to perceive surgery as dangerous or risky. This suggests that patients' aversion toward epilepsy surgery could be contributed to by a knowledge gap of what is done during surgery, and hence can potentially be allayed with clear descriptions and depictions. Similarly, our observation that surgery‐experienced patients were less likely to have financial concerns about surgery suggests that such concerns can be allayed by the provision of detailed and accurate information on costs.

The tendency for patients who had undergone epilepsy surgery to view seizures as non‐disabling did not reach statistical significance, possibly because of the small number of patients. However, a benign impression of their seizures may explain our observations that previous surgery did not increase the chance of a favorable response to guaranteed successful surgery, reduce the perception that surgery is more dangerous than their already benign seizures, nor change their impression of surgery as a “last resort.”

Another limitation of our study is the over‐representation of female (63.6%) patients in our cohort, potentially introducing bias. Female epilepsy patients are more prone to polytherapy than male patients, and hence may more easily meet the definition of DRE. 26 , 27 However, the significant predominance of female DRE patients in our study is unlikely to be explained solely by this tendency. A potential bias toward female patients may have been contributed by our strategy of recruiting patients from our outpatient clinic, since male epilepsy patients are more likely to default on clinic appointments. 28

CONCLUSION

Our survey demonstrates significant patient‐related barriers to epilepsy surgery among DRE patients in Singapore, an ethnically heterogeneous country. These barriers are contributed by an overestimation of surgical risks by patients and kin and, for some patients, a perception that seizures are not disabling. These barriers may potentially be mitigated through public education efforts, with particular attention to patients with lower educational achievement. Efforts to counsel and educate patients should leverage Singaporean patients' high trust in healthcare providers. Subsidies for epilepsy surgery should be accompanied by efforts to address non‐financial barriers such as those described above. Our finding of substantial support for alternative medicine and investigational non‐surgical treatment may encourage efforts to integrate traditional and Western medicine, provided effectiveness and safety data are favorable.

While our study focused on identifying and assessing patient‐related barriers, future studies should evaluate physician‐related and system‐related barriers to epilepsy surgery, which can also vary by region and country.
