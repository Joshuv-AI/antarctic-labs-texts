Ethnobotanical study of medicinal plants used by indigenous communities in Sheikh District, Somaliland
Authors: Abdikarim Ibrahim, Ashebir Awoke, Ermias Lulekal, Getu Alemayehu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMedicinal plants (MPs) remain to play a vital role in primary healthcare among rural communities. In Somaliland, knowledge of MPs remains under-documented, mainly in Sheikh District, Sahil Region. This study aims to document the diversity, uses, cultural importance, and conservation status of MPs in Sheikh District, while examining patterns of knowledge transmission, utilization, and threats, including cross-cultural comparisons with other Somali and East African regions.MethodsEthnobotanical information was collected using semi-structured interviews, guided field walks, and focus group discussions with 106 informants, including traditional healers, elders, and other community members. Quantitative indices Jaccard Similarity Index (JI), Rahman’s Similarity Index (RSI), Informant Consensus Factor (ICF), Fidelity Level (FL), preference ranking, and direct matrix ranking were employed to assess species importance, agreement, and multifunctionality. Statistical analyses conducted in R evaluated variation in knowledge across socio-demographic groups.ResultsA total of 54 medicinal plant species, representing 33 families, were recorded for treating ailments including gastrointestinal disorders, respiratory infections, malaria, diabetes, and dermatological and ocular conditions. Vachellia bussei (Harms ex Y.Sjöstedt) Kyal. & Boatwr., Balanites aegyptiaca var. aegyptiaca, and Melia azedarach L. exhibited the highest FL values (94%), while gastrointestinal and parasitic disorders showed the highest ICF (0.77), indicating strong consensus. Oral administration was the predominant route of use. Multipurpose species such as Vachellia spp. and Eucalyptus camaldulensis Dehnh. faced high anthropogenic pressure due to fuelwood and construction demands. Knowledge was mainly transmitted orally, with older, male, and less formally educated informants demonstrating significantly greater expertise (P < 0.05). Cross-cultural comparison highlighted both locally unique uses and overlaps with ethnomedicinal practices in neighboring regions.ConclusionMPs in Sheikh District are critical not only for healthcare but also for food security, cultural continuity, and environmental sustainability. Urgent actions are recommended for conservation, sustainable harvesting, and systematic documentation of indigenous knowledge. Prioritizing pharmacological validation of culturally significant species can further support community health, biodiversity conservation, and policy development for sustainable management.

Background

Plants have symbolized one of the most long-term basics of healthcare in human history. Archaeological and historical evidence demonstrates that plant-based healing practices were already well established in ancient China between 5000 and 4000 BC and in the Middle East and North Africa by around 1600 BC [1–3]. Even with the rapid development of biomedical systems, MPs continue to play an essential role in global health, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where nearly 80% of the population still depends on traditional medicine for primary healthcare [4, 5].

Ethnobotanical knowledge is not simply a collection of remedies; it is deeply interwoven with cultural identity, belief systems, and long-term relationships between communities and their surrounding ecosystems. Such knowledge is typically transmitted orally through generations and reflects locally adapted strategies for maintaining health and resilience. However, accelerating socio-economic transformations, environmental degradation, and climate-related pressures increasingly threaten both medicinal plant resources and the cultural knowledge associated with them. These challenges highlight the urgent need for systematic documentation of indigenous ethnomedicinal practices before they disappear [6, 7].

Africa is widely recognized for its exceptional biological and cultural diversity, and traditional medicine remains the backbone of healthcare in many rural areas across the continent [8–10]. In Somaliland, limited access to modern health facilities has resulted in widespread reliance on medicinal plants. More than half of the population, and over 95% of pastoral communities, depend on traditional remedies for disease prevention and treatment [11]. Somali ethnomedicine integrates herbal therapies with spiritual and magico-religious elements, reflecting a holistic understanding of illness and well-being [12–14]. Despite its continued importance, much of this knowledge remains undocumented and is rapidly eroding as elder healers pass away without fully transmitting their expertise to younger generations [15, 16].

The Horn of Africa is considered a global biodiversity hotspot, yet Somaliland’s flora remains relatively understudied despite its richness and ecological importance [17–19]. In rural and pastoralist contexts, MPs are vital for treating gastrointestinal, respiratory, dermatological, febrile, and other common ailments [20, 21]. Sheikh District, located in the Sahil Region, is characterized by montane and semi-arid landscapes supporting diverse woody and herbaceous vegetation [11, 22]. Although the district holds significant ecological and cultural value, no comprehensive ethnobotanical investigation has yet been conducted there. As a result, there is limited understanding of the diversity of medicinal taxa, local preparation methods, dosage systems, cultural perceptions of healing, and the conservation challenges facing these plant resources.

Documenting medicinal plant knowledge in Sheikh District is also relevant beyond the local context. Comparative ethnobotanical studies across regions contribute to identifying shared traditions, unique cultural adaptations, and potentially novel medicinal reports. Such research aligns closely with global sustainability priorities, including the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, by supporting culturally appropriate healthcare, strengthening biodiversity conservation, and promoting sustainable resource management.

Against this backdrop, the present study investigates the ethnobotanical knowledge of MPs used by indigenous communities in Sheikh District, Sahil Region, Somaliland. The study is designed not only as a descriptive inventory but also as a contribution to broader cross-regional ethnomedicinal research in the Horn of Africa and comparable dryland socio-ecological systems.

Specifically, the objectives are to: (i) identify and document the medicinal plant species used for human healthcare in Sheikh District, including local names, plant parts utilized, preparation techniques, and modes of administration; (ii) assess cultural importance and agreement in plant use through quantitative ethnobotanical indices, such as Jaccard Similarity Index, Rahman’s Similarity Index, Fidelity Level and Informant Consensus Factor, in comparison with patterns reported in other Somali and East African ethnobotanical studies; (iii) examine the distribution of ethnobotanical knowledge across socio-demographic groups (age, gender, literacy, and healer experience), evaluating whether knowledge patterns in Sheikh District reflect broader trends observed in other pastoral and indigenous societies; (iv) identify novel or locally unique medicinal reports, as well as commonalities with previously documented ethnomedicinal practices in Somaliland and surrounding macro-regions, thereby contributing to cross-cultural understanding of traditional healthcare systems and (v) evaluate perceived threats and conservation concerns, particularly for multipurpose species under high anthropogenic pressure, and discuss implications for sustainable management and community resilience.

The study is guided by the following hypotheses (i) ethnomedicinal knowledge is unevenly distributed within the community, with older, male, and less formally educated informants possessing greater expertise, consistent with patterns reported in other pastoral societies; (ii) certain medicinal plant species will exhibit high Fidelity Level and Informant Consensus Factor values, indicating strong cultural importance and perceived therapeutic effectiveness; (iii) multipurpose plant species used for both medicinal and non-medicinal purposes will face greater harvesting pressure and higher risk of local depletion compared to species used exclusively for healthcare; (iv) indigenous medicinal knowledge in Sheikh District is primarily transmitted orally through family and community networks, but this transmission is declining among younger generations due to modernization and limited formal documentation, and (v) MPs contribute not only to disease treatment but also to food security, environmental sustainability, and cultural continuity, reinforcing their multidimensional role in strengthening local resilience in semi-arid ecosystems.

Methods and materials

Description of the study area

The present ethnobotanical study was carried out in Sheikh District, located in the Sahil Region of Somaliland, between 9°55′45″ N latitude and 45°11′30″ E longitude. The district covers an area of approximately 2,960 km² and lies within an altitudinal range of 1,430–1,500 m above sea level. Sheikh District is bordered by the Togdher Region to the northeast and Odwayne District to the southwest, and it was officially established as an administrative district in 1973. Administratively, the district comprises 27 kebeles, including 23 rural and 4 urban settlements (Fig. 1).

Sheikh town, the administrative center of the district, is situated within the Golis Mountain Range along the main route between Berbera and Burao, approximately 70 km north of Burao. The town holds strong historical and cultural importance in Somali society and is widely recognized as a traditional center of learning. Its mountainous landscape, relatively cool climate, and high air quality make it one of the most scenic and ecologically distinctive areas in Somaliland [11].

Climatically, the district experiences a bimodal rainfall pattern, with two main rainy seasons: Gu (April–June) and Dayr (October–November). Rainfall peaks in May, reaching around 300 mm, while January is typically the driest month (9.99 mm). The mean annual rainfall and average temperature are approximately 241 mm and 19.1 °C, respectively. The warmest period occurs between March and June, whereas January and February represent the coldest months of the year [11, 22].

The livelihoods of local communities are predominantly agro-pastoral. Around 70% of households rely mainly on livestock production, while the remaining 30% engage in crop cultivation and small-scale trade. Approximately 70% of the district’s land is used as rangeland, while 30% is devoted to agricultural activities [11]. Sheikh District has an estimated population of about 90,469 inhabitants. Although unemployment remains high, agriculture, livestock trade, and small businesses provide the primary sources of income. Due to its historical administrative role during the British protectorate period, Sheikh retains relatively better-developed educational and health facilities compared with many other rural areas in Somaliland.

Ecologically, Sheikh District supports diverse plant resources, largely due to its mountainous terrain and semi-arid transitional environment. Dominant indigenous and introduced species include Vachellia bussei, V. tortilis, V. senegal, Azadirachta indica, Balanites aegyptiaca, B. glabra, B. orbicularis, Buxus hildebrandtii, and Cordia gharaf. However, these plant resources are increasingly threatened by deforestation, charcoal production, unsustainable harvesting, and climate variability. Such pressures pose risks not only to biodiversity but also to the continuity of indigenous ethnomedicinal knowledge systems that depend on these plants [11].

Indigenous communities in Sheikh District possess extensive ethnomedical and ecological knowledge developed through long-term interaction with their surrounding landscapes. Elders and knowledgeable community members classify landforms, soils, and vegetation based on locally meaningful indicators such as topography, soil texture and color, plant density, and dominant species. In particular, they recognize three broad landscape categories: the coastal plain (Guban), the mountain zone (Oogo), and the flat plateau (Hawd), each associated with distinct vegetation and ecological conditions [22].

Medicinal plant availability and use are closely linked to these indigenous landscape classifications. Local communities further distinguish vegetation into three land-cover types: mountain and highland vegetation, coastal plain vegetation, and plateau vegetation. These categories are consistently recognized throughout the district and guide the collection, management, and cultural use of medicinal plants.

Mountain and highland areas are characterized by evergreen, shrub-dominated vegetation growing mainly on rocky black soils locally known as carro-madow. Natural woody vegetation is widespread, while irrigated agriculture remains limited. Common MPs found in this zone include Albizia anthelmintica, Aloe citrina, Cadia purpurea, Cordia gharaf, Dodonaea angustifolia, Olea somaliensis, Terminalia prunioides, and Ziziphus mauritiana [11].

The coastal plain and piedmont zones represent low-lying arid environments with sandy, nutrient-poor soils and minimal rainfall. Vegetation is sparse and dominated by drought- and heat-adapted species such as Balanites latifolia, Cissus rotundifolia, Neltuma juliflora, Solanum obbiadense, and Ziziphus hamur [11].

Plateau landscapes cover the largest proportion of the study area and are mainly composed of red sandy soils that are highly prone to erosion. Vegetation in this zone consists largely of thorny trees, shrubs, and herbaceous species. Dominant medicinal taxa include several Vachellia species (V. bussei, V. mellifera, S. senegal, V. tortilis), along with Aloe somaliensis, Calotropis procera, Commiphora myrrha, Cordeauxia edulis, Euphorbia spp., Momordica charantia, and Senna italica. These ecological patterns are consistent with previous vegetation assessments conducted in Somaliland [11].

Reconnaissance survey, study site, and informant selection

A preliminary reconnaissance survey was conducted from 1 January to 1 February 2023 to obtain baseline information and to support the systematic selection of representative study sites within Sheikh District. During this phase, initial data were gathered from the Sheikh District Office of Agriculture and complemented through field consultations with local elders, kebele leaders, forest guards, and other knowledgeable community members. These discussions were essential for understanding locally recognized vegetation patterns, settlement characteristics, and the presence of active traditional healing practices.

Sheikh District was purposively selected for the present ethnobotanical investigation because of its relatively high plant diversity, the strong reliance of indigenous communities on MPs for primary healthcare, and the limited availability of formal conservation or management initiatives aimed at protecting these culturally important plant resources.

Based on recommendations from community elders and local administrative leaders, together with considerations of vegetation cover, ecological variation, and agroclimatic conditions, 12 kebeles were purposively chosen from the 27 kebeles found in the district. These included: Galooley, Gidhays, Girni, Gugux, Hundisa, Kalabaydh, Kalasharka, Kulmiye, Laalays, Shacabka, Suuq-sade, and Tuulo-dhibiijo. These settlements were identified as areas where traditional medicinal knowledge remains actively practiced and where medicinal plant availability is relatively high. The geographic distribution of these kebeles is presented in the study area map (Fig. 1), and detailed settlement-level information is provided in the corresponding location table.

Informants were selected using a combination of purposive and snowball sampling methods, following established ethnobotanical research approaches [23, 24]. This strategy allowed the inclusion of both highly knowledgeable specialists and broader community members, ensuring that the documented information reflects both expert and shared ethnomedicinal practices.

In total, 106 informants (aged 18–80 years) participated in the study. Among them, 46 were key informants, primarily recognized traditional healers and elders, identified with the support of community leaders, development agents, and local administrators. The remaining 60 informants were recruited through snowball sampling in order to capture wider community-based knowledge regarding medicinal plant use, preparation, and transmission.

The demographic profile of participants indicates that most informants were older adults, with the highest representation in the 51–80 age group, followed by individuals aged 31–50 and 18–30 years (Table 1). Educational backgrounds also varied, although the majority of participants reported no formal schooling, while others had attended primary, secondary, or college-level education.

These socio-demographic characteristics were documented to provide context for understanding patterns of knowledge transmission, cultural continuity, and potential generational shifts in ethnobotanical expertise, rather than solely for inferential statistical purposes.

Ethnobotanical data collection and plant identification

Ethnobotanical information in Sheikh District was gathered through an integrated set of qualitative and quantitative field methods, including semi-structured interviews, guided field walks, direct observations, focus group discussions (FGDs), and market surveys. This multi-method approach allowed for a comprehensive documentation of indigenous knowledge related to MPs, including their local identification, preparation techniques, therapeutic applications, routes of administration, dosage practices, availability, perceived threats, and local conservation measures. Data collection followed established ethnobotanical research procedures as outlined by [25, 26].

Semi-structured interviews and group discussions were conducted using pretested checklists developed in accordance with the objectives of the study. Information recorded included informant socio-demographic characteristics, common human ailments treated traditionally, local plant names, plant parts used, preparation and application methods, dosage forms, routes of administration, additional non-medicinal uses, plant sources (wild or cultivated), perceived abundance, and conservation status.

Interviews were carried out primarily with individuals who were native to, or long-term residents of, Sheikh District to ensure the depth and reliability of ethnobotanical knowledge. All interviews were conducted in the Somali language with the support of local translators, and responses were later recorded in English for analysis. The semi-structured format provided flexibility, allowing informants to share detailed explanations, personal experiences, and local variations in medicinal plant use (Fig. 2).

Guided field walks were undertaken with key informants and local guides to locate and identify MPs in their natural habitats. These walks enabled direct observation of plant morphology, growth forms, ecological settings, and distribution patterns across the different landscapes of the district.

During field excursions, additional information was documented, including harvesting practices, preparation techniques, alternative uses, and indigenous conservation strategies. Plant collection sites were georeferenced using GPS coordinates, and field notes were recorded on vegetation types, soil characteristics, habitat conditions, and topographic features. To respect confidentiality and cultural sensitivity, most field walks were conducted individually with informants, and any sensitive knowledge shared was kept anonymous.

Eight focus group discussions were held with key informants to capture shared community knowledge and collective practices regarding medicinal plant use. These discussions encouraged open dialogue among participants and provided insights into preparation methods, dosage determination, additive ingredients, possible side effects, and culturally specific healing perceptions.

Participants also reflected on knowledge transmission systems, local management practices, and emerging threats to medicinal plant resources, thereby contributing to a broader understanding of both cultural and ecological dimensions of ethnomedicine in Sheikh District.

A market survey was conducted at Wadhato market in Sheikh District to assess the extent of medicinal plant commercialization. Semi-structured interviews with medicinal plant vendors documented the types of plant materials sold, their quantities, prices, sources, and trade dynamics. This survey helped evaluate the economic value of MPs, the role of markets in traditional healthcare, and potential harvesting pressures linked to commercial demand.

Voucher specimens of all recorded MPs were systematically collected during guided field walks, following prior informed consent from landowners and in coordination with district administrative and agricultural offices. Specimen collection adhered to ethical research standards and conservation guidelines.

Plants were photographed in situ, labeled, numbered, pressed, air-dried or deep-frozen as appropriate, and accompanied by detailed field notes describing habitat, growth form, and reported traditional uses.

Preliminary identification was performed in the field using vernacular names and morphological characteristics. Final taxonomic identification was conducted at the Biology Department Laboratory, College of Natural and Computational Sciences, Jigjiga University, between 14 and 16 June 2023. Identification was supported by standard botanical references, including the Flora of Somalia and the Somali Plant Names Dictionary, as well as other relevant taxonomic literature [27, 28].

Specimens were further compared with authenticated herbarium materials housed at the Igad Sheikh Technical Veterinary School and the Jigjiga University Herbarium. To resolve remaining taxonomic uncertainties, additional digital resources were consulted, including PlantNet, African Plant Database, Plants of the World Online (Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew), JSTOR Global Plants, and Google Image Search.

All plant identifications were formally verified by Dr. Getu Alemayehu. Voucher specimens were deposited in the Mini Herbarium of the Department of Biology, Jigjiga University, under the coded deposition number JJU005209, providing permanent reference material for future taxonomic verification, ethnobotanical research, biodiversity monitoring, and conservation planning.

Quantitative ethnobotanical analysis

To assess the reliability, depth, and consensus of traditional medicinal plant knowledge in Sheikh District, several established quantitative ethnobotanical indices were applied, including the Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC), Fidelity Level (FL), Cultural Value Index (CV), and Informant Consensus Factor (ICF). These indices provide objective measures of the cultural significance, agreement among informants, and therapeutic relevance of medicinal plant species within the local community.

Participatory ranking methods, such as preference ranking and direct matrix ranking, were also employed to capture local perceptions of plant efficacy and to prioritize species according to their cultural and therapeutic importance. This integrative approach allowed for the identification of culturally valued species, assessment of consensus among informants, and recognition of MPs with potential ecological and pharmacological significance.

The Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC) was calculated for each species to quantify its local prominence and cultural importance [29, 30]. RFC represents the proportion of informants who mentioned a particular species for the treatment of one or more human ailments, providing an indicator of how widely recognized and commonly utilized a species is within traditional healthcare practices. These quantitative measures complement qualitative ethnobotanical data, enabling a robust understanding of the relative significance of MPs in Sheikh District. where FC is the number of informants who cited a particular medicinal plant species, and N is the total number of informants participating in the survey.

The RFC value ranges from 0 to 1. A value close to 0 indicates that a species was mentioned by only a few informants and is therefore of limited local recognition or specialized use. In contrast, values approaching 1 indicate that a species was cited by a large proportion of informants, reflecting widespread knowledge, frequent use, and high cultural relevance within the community. Species with high RFC values were interpreted as locally important MPs and considered priority taxa for further ethnopharmacological investigation and conservation attention.

The Cultural Value Index (CVI) was calculated to quantify the overall cultural significance of each medicinal plant species within the study community [29]. This index integrates three key dimensions of ethnobotanical relevance: the proportion of informants citing a species, the diversity of medicinal uses reported, and the number of distinct use categories in which the species is employed. By combining these components, CVI provides a holistic measure of how deeply a plant is embedded in local traditional knowledge and its role in everyday healthcare practices. The Fidelity Level (FL) was calculated to assess the degree of consensus among informants on the use of a particular medicinal plant species for treating a specific ailment [31]. FL reflects how consistently a species is reported for a given therapeutic application and serves as an indicator of the plant’s perceived effectiveness and specificity within the local traditional healthcare system. The Informant Consensus Factor (ICF) was calculated to evaluate the level of agreement among informants on the use of medicinal plant species for treating specific categories of human ailments [32, 33]. ICF reflects the extent to which knowledge about particular remedies is shared within the community and is widely used to identify culturally important and potentially effective medicinal species. In this study, ailment categories with high ICF values were interpreted as culturally salient and were used to guide the identification of priority species for further ethnopharmacological evaluation.

Jaccard similarity index

The Jaccard Similarity Index (JSI) was applied to measure the overlap in medicinal plant knowledge among different groups or regions. This index quantifies the proportion of species shared between two datasets and is particularly useful for comparing knowledge among traditional healers, elders, general community members, or across distinct geographic areas [37–39]. Where: JSI represents the Jaccard Similarity Index expressed as a percentage. In this formula, a is the number of species documented in Ethiopian and other eastern Africa study, b is the number of species recorded in the Somaliland study, and c is the number of species common to both areas. The index ranges from 0 to 100%, where 0% indicates no shared species and 100% indicates complete similarity. This metric helps to understand the extent to which traditional medicinal plant knowledge is shared or varies across different communities and ecological settings.

Rahman’s similarity index (RSI)

Rahman’s Similarity Index (RSI) was used to evaluate the cultural similarity of medicinal plant use across different study areas. Unlike the Jaccard Similarity Index, RSI considers not only the species shared between sites but also the similarity in their therapeutic applications, providing a more detailed understanding of ethnobotanical overlap [38, 39]. Preference ranking and direct matrix ranking

To evaluate the perceived effectiveness of MPs used for specific ailments, preference ranking exercises were conducted following established ethnobotanical approaches [25, 26]. In the case of diarrhea, eight key informants were asked to assess six frequently cited medicinal plant species. Each informant assigned a score ranging from 5 (most preferred) to 1 (least preferred) based on their experience and perception of therapeutic efficacy.

The scores from all informants were then summed, and the plant species were ranked according to their total values. This method provided a culturally grounded hierarchy of the plants considered most effective and important for the treatment of diarrhea within the local healthcare system.

Direct matrix ranking was additionally applied to examine the multipurpose roles of selected medicinal plant species and to assess potential exploitation pressure. Seven MPs known to have multiple uses beyond healthcare were selected, and six major use categories were identified for each species. Six key informants assigned use scores from 0 (not used) to 5 (highest importance) for every use category.

Average scores were calculated and summed to generate an overall ranking for each species. This approach allowed comparison of the relative cultural utility and potential overharvesting risks of multipurpose plants, highlighting those species that may require priority attention for sustainable management within the community.

Data analysis

A mixed-methods analytical framework combining qualitative and quantitative approaches was employed to provide a comprehensive understanding of medicinal plant knowledge in Sheikh District.

Qualitative data, including local plant names, ailments treated, preparation techniques, and community perceptions, were coded and categorized according to standard ethnomedicinal classifications. Information was cross-validated through repeated interviews and triangulation across multiple informants to enhance reliability. Thematic analysis was used to identify recurring patterns in plant use, cultural interpretations of healing, and mechanisms of indigenous knowledge transmission.

Quantitative data were summarized using descriptive statistics and widely applied ethnobotanical indices, including Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC), Fidelity Level (FL), Cultural Value Index (CVI), and Informant Consensus Factor (ICF). These indices were used to assess the cultural importance of plant species, agreement among informants, and the perceived therapeutic relevance of specific taxa.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (v4.4.3). Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences in ethnobotanical indices across socio-demographic variables (such as age, gender, and informant category) were examined using ANOVA or appropriate non-parametric alternatives. Pearson correlation and linear regression analyses were further applied to explore relationships between informant age and the number of MPs reported [34, 35].

Overall, this integrated analytical approach provided a robust assessment of knowledge distribution, cultural consensus, and the significance of MPs within the indigenous healthcare system of Sheikh District.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with internationally recognized ethnobotanical ethical standards and received ethical approval from the Department of Biology, Jigjiga University, as well as administrative authorization from the Sheikh District offices. Research procedures followed the ethical principles outlined in the Code of Ethics of the International Society of Ethnobiology (ISE).

Prior informed consent was obtained from all participants after clearly explaining the objectives of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, the interview procedures, and the measures taken to ensure confidentiality and anonymity. Informants were informed of their right to refuse participation or withdraw at any stage without any consequences.

Special attention was given to culturally sensitive knowledge, particularly regarding plants with sacred, ritual, or restricted uses. No personal identifiers were recorded, and all ethnobotanical information was handled in a manner that ensured participant privacy and community respect.

In addition, formal permission was secured from local elders and municipal authorities prior to fieldwork. This ensured that the research was conducted in accordance with local governance structures, customary norms, and community expectations, supporting ethical collaboration and respectful documentation of indigenous knowledge systems.

Results and discussion

Taxonomic diversity of medicinal plants

The present ethnobotanical study documented a total of 54 medicinal plant species, representing 47 genera and 34 botanical families, used by indigenous communities in Sheikh District, Sahil Region, Somaliland (Table 2). This diversity reflects not only the botanical richness of the district but also the depth of local ethnomedicinal knowledge that has developed through long-term interaction between communities and their surrounding environments.

Comparable levels of medicinal plant richness have been reported in other ethnobotanical investigations across East Africa, including Ethiopia, Kenya, and Uganda, where district-level studies commonly record between 40 and 80 medicinal species. Such variation is often shaped by ecological heterogeneity, cultural traditions, and the degree of dependence on plant-based healthcare systems [36–42]. In this broader regional context, the findings from Sheikh District contribute important baseline data for understanding both shared and locally distinctive ethnomedicinal practices within the Horn of Africa.

Among the recorded families, Fabaceae was the most species-rich, contributing 10 species (18.5%) of the total medicinal flora. The prominence of Fabaceae is widely recognized across African ethnomedicine and has been consistently reported in studies from Ethiopia, Kenya, Tanzania, Nigeria, and other regions where Fabaceae members are frequently used to treat diverse human ailments [43–48].

From a cultural and ecological perspective, the dominance of Fabaceae may reflect the wide distribution of this family in semi-arid landscapes, its accessibility to pastoral and agro-pastoral communities, and the long-standing recognition of its therapeutic value. Many Fabaceae species are also known to contain bioactive compounds such as flavonoids, tannins, and alkaloids, which may reinforce local perceptions of their effectiveness. Similar patterns have been observed beyond Africa, particularly in South and East Asia, where Fabaceae plants remain central to traditional systems such as Ayurveda, Unani, and Chinese medicine [49–54]. This cross-cultural recurrence suggests that certain plant families may hold broadly recognized medicinal potential across different ecological and cultural settings.

Asphodelaceae and Solanaceae, each represented by three species (5.5%), also formed important components of the local pharmacopoeia. The significance of Asphodelaceae, especially Aloe species, has been widely documented in arid and semi-arid areas of Ethiopia, Somalia, and Kenya, where these plants are commonly used for wound care, gastrointestinal disorders, and dermatological conditions [11, 55–58].

Similarly, the use of Solanaceae species corresponds with ethnomedicinal reports from Uganda, Cameroon, and Tanzania, where Solanum taxa are often employed in managing respiratory illnesses, fever, and inflammatory problems. However, such use is typically accompanied by careful cultural knowledge regarding dosage and preparation, reflecting community awareness of potential toxicity [59–63].

Several additional families, including Apocynaceae, Combretaceae, Cucurbitaceae, Meliaceae, Myrtaceae, and Zygophyllaceae, were represented by two species each. This selective representation highlights that medicinal plant use is not solely determined by plant abundance, but also by accumulated cultural experience, perceived efficacy, and symbolic importance. Comparable patterns have been reported from Ethiopia, Sudan, and Nigeria, where communities often prioritize specific taxa that are culturally trusted and therapeutically valued [64–69].

For instance, Meliaceae species are widely valued across Africa and Asia for antimicrobial and antiparasitic applications, while Myrtaceae members are frequently used for respiratory and digestive ailments in diverse regions including Europe, Australia, and South Asia [3, 70–72]. Such cross-regional similarities suggest both shared therapeutic traditions and convergent adaptation to common health challenges.

Notably, many plant families in Sheikh District were represented by only a single medicinal species. This pattern emphasizes the ecological heterogeneity of the district and demonstrates the detailed local knowledge required to identify and maintain less common taxa for specialized therapeutic purposes. Similar trends have been documented in ethnobotanical studies from Cameroon, South America, and parts of Asia, where culturally important MPs may persist in traditional practice despite limited abundance or restricted distribution [73–75].

Thus, the taxonomic composition of MPs recorded in Sheikh District aligns with broader ethnobotanical patterns observed across Africa and beyond. The dominance of key families such as Fabaceae, alongside the inclusion of locally specific taxa, illustrates the adaptive and culturally embedded nature of traditional healthcare systems. These findings also underline the importance of conserving both plant biodiversity and the indigenous knowledge systems that sustain community health, cultural continuity, and future ethnopharmacological potential.

Growth forms of medicinal plants

The growth-form analysis of MPs documented in Sheikh District revealed four main categories: trees, shrubs, herbs, and climbers. Trees were the most dominant growth form, followed by shrubs and herbs, whereas climbers were least represented. This pattern reflects both the ecological structure of the district’s semi-arid and montane landscapes and the adaptive strategies of indigenous communities in selecting locally available plant resources for healthcare.

The predominance of woody growth forms is consistent with findings from many ethnobotanical studies conducted across East Africa, including Ethiopia, Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania, particularly in environments where trees and shrubs constitute the backbone of natural vegetation [19–21, 75–78]. Such cross-regional similarities suggest that ecological availability strongly shapes medicinal plant selection, but also that woody taxa often hold enduring cultural and therapeutic value across diverse pastoral and agro-pastoral societies.

Trees represented the largest proportion of MPs in Sheikh District, indicating a strong reliance on long-lived woody perennials. Trees are especially valued because they remain available throughout the year, even during dry seasons, and provide multiple usable parts such as leaves, bark, roots, fruits, and seeds. This versatility allows healers and households to treat a wide range of ailments using a single species. Similar cultural preferences for trees have been reported in Ethiopia, Nigeria, and Cameroon, where woody species are frequently regarded as central to traditional medicine due to their broad therapeutic roles and symbolic importance within local healing systems [18, 62, 79–83]. Comparable patterns have also been documented in South Asia and South America, highlighting the widespread significance of woody MPs in indigenous healthcare traditions [84–86].

Several culturally prominent medicinal trees recorded in Sheikh District, such as Albizia anthelmintica, Azadirachta indica, Balanites aegyptiaca, Vachellia tortilis, and Moringa stenopetala, are widely recognized across Africa and Asia for their medicinal versatility, nutritional contributions, and socio-economic value [87–89]. Their prominence in local ethnomedicine suggests that these species function as keystone resources supporting both health and livelihood resilience.

Shrubs constituted the second most dominant growth form, reflecting their ecological abundance and resilience under drought and grazing pressure in semi-arid environments. Shrubs are often easily accessible and commonly used in remedies for gastrointestinal, dermatological, and respiratory conditions. Comparable patterns have been observed in Ethiopia, Sudan, and Tanzania, where shrub-dominated vegetation supports a wide range of medicinal applications, particularly through the use of bark, latex, or aromatic resins [90–93].

In Sheikh District, species such as Calotropis procera, Commiphora myrrha, Euphorbia polyacantha, and Ziziphus hamur illustrate the importance of resinous and latex-producing shrubs in managing wounds, skin infections, and respiratory ailments. Similar uses have been reported in Somalia and the broader Middle East, suggesting shared ethnomedicinal traditions shaped by comparable dryland ecologies.

Herbs, although less represented than trees and shrubs, were mainly used to treat common and acute ailments such as colds, inflammation, and minor infections. Herbaceous species are often preferred for household-level remedies because they are easy to collect, regenerate quickly, and can be prepared fresh with minimal processing. Similar findings have been documented in Uganda, Kenya, Europe, and Asia, where herbs are frequently associated with short-term treatments and everyday healthcare practices [94–97]. The relatively lower number of herbs in Sheikh District may be linked to the district’s semi-arid climate, which favors woody vegetation over seasonal herbaceous growth.

Climbers were the least represented growth form in the study area. This limited presence aligns with many ethnobotanical studies across Africa and Asia, where climbers tend to be less abundant or less accessible in dry environments and therefore contribute less prominently to traditional pharmacopoeias [98, 99]. Their marginal role may also reflect cultural preferences for woody species, which are often perceived as more potent or reliable in treating common ailments.

Hence, the distribution of medicinal plant growth forms in Sheikh District demonstrates a clear reliance on trees and shrubs, mirroring both the ecological composition of the landscape and culturally embedded healing practices. However, this dependence also raises important conservation concerns, as woody perennials often regenerate slowly and may be particularly vulnerable to overharvesting when bark or roots are collected. Targeted conservation measures and sustainable harvesting strategies are therefore essential to ensure the long-term availability of these medicinal resources and to safeguard the indigenous ethnobotanical knowledge systems that depend upon them.

Sources of medicinal plants

The ethnobotanical survey in Sheikh District showed that MPs are obtained from both wild habitats and cultivated settings, reflecting the ecological knowledge and adaptive practices of local communities. Among the 54 recorded species, the majority were harvested from natural ecosystems, including forests, mountainous zones, and communal rangelands. This strong dependence on wild resources highlights the continued importance of surrounding biodiversity for primary healthcare and underscores the role of indigenous ecological expertise in locating, identifying, and harvesting medicinal species in culturally appropriate ways [15, 41, 100–103].

A similar reliance on wild vegetation has been widely reported across East Africa, including Ethiopia, Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania, where traditional medicine remains closely tied to natural landscapes due to limited access to biomedical services and enduring cultural trust in herbal remedies [104–109]. In this sense, Sheikh District reflects broader ethnomedicinal patterns found in pastoral and agro-pastoral societies across semi-arid Africa.

Key wild-harvested species in Sheikh District included Vachellia spp., Balanites aegyptiaca, Commiphora myrrha, and Eucalyptus camaldulensis. These plants are valued not only for their therapeutic roles but also for their availability within the local environment, enabling communities to address a wide range of ailments using familiar and accessible resources. Comparable patterns of wild harvesting have been documented in Nigeria, Cameroon, India, and Pakistan, illustrating a global trend in indigenous healthcare systems that prioritize locally abundant medicinal taxa [62, 110–115].

In contrast, a smaller yet important proportion of MPs were sourced from home gardens and cultivated areas, particularly species that are frequently used and easily propagated. Examples include Allium sativum, Azadirachta indica, Moringa stenopetala, and Carica papaya. Cultivation near households ensures year-round availability, reduces collection pressure on wild populations, and integrates MPs into everyday domestic healthcare practices. Similar home-garden strategies have been reported in Ethiopia, Kenya, China, and India, where cultivated MPs function both as conservation reservoirs and as accessible sources of nutrition and therapy [116–119].

Some species, such as Aloe spp., were obtained from both wild and cultivated environments depending on seasonal availability and the plant part required. For instance, latex may be collected from wild stands, while cultivation near homesteads provides continuous access for routine household use. This dual sourcing strategy demonstrates the flexibility of indigenous knowledge systems in maintaining key medicinal resources. Comparable adaptive practices have been reported in Uganda, Tanzania, and parts of Europe, where communities combine wild harvesting with small-scale cultivation to sustain traditional remedies [70, 120–124].

However, the heavy reliance on wild habitats also raises conservation concerns. MPs harvested exclusively from natural ecosystems particularly those collected for roots, bark, or whole plants are especially vulnerable to overexploitation, land-use change, deforestation, and climate variability. Similar threats have been reported in Ethiopia, Nigeria, and Cameroon, emphasizing the need for sustainable harvesting guidelines and community-based conservation initiatives [18, 80, 125].

Overall, wild ecosystems remain the primary source of MPs in Sheikh District, while home gardens provide an important supplementary source for frequently used taxa. These findings highlight the importance of integrated conservation strategies that combine sustainable wild harvesting with the promotion of household and community-level cultivation. Such approaches are essential not only for protecting medicinal plant diversity but also for safeguarding the cultural knowledge systems that continue to underpin primary healthcare in Somaliland and comparable dryland regions worldwide.

Ethnomedicinal uses and plant parts employed in traditional healthcare

The present study documented 54 medicinal plant species belonging to 34 botanical families that are traditionally used by indigenous communities in Sheikh District to manage a wide range of human health conditions. These remedies address ailments affecting multiple body systems, including gastrointestinal, respiratory, febrile, dermatological, ocular, musculoskeletal, metabolic, urogenital, and infectious disorders. The breadth of ailments treated reflects the depth, adaptability, and continued relevance of local ethnomedicinal knowledge.

A similar reliance on plant-based healthcare has been widely reported throughout East Africa—particularly in Ethiopia, Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania where traditional medicine remains essential due to limited healthcare infrastructure and strong cultural continuity of indigenous healing systems [126–131].

Gastrointestinal disorders were the most frequently treated category, with 17 species (31.4%) reported for conditions such as diarrhea, stomachache, constipation, and intestinal parasitic infections. This pattern mirrors findings from Ethiopia, Nigeria, and Cameroon, where digestive ailments commonly dominate ethnomedicinal practice [62, 102, 132, 133].

The prominence of gastrointestinal treatments may be culturally and environmentally linked to challenges such as unsafe drinking water, sanitation limitations, and dietary stress in rural semi-arid settings. In Sheikh District, species such as Citrullus colocynthis, Maytenus undata, and Psidium guajava were widely used, consistent with ethnobotanical reports from East Africa, South Asia, and the Middle East that highlight their antimicrobial, antidiarrheal, and anti-inflammatory potential [19, 20, 52, 134–136].

Respiratory and febrile illnesses were treated using ten species (18.5%), emphasizing the importance of MPs in managing colds, influenza-like symptoms, and fever. The use of Allium sativum and Eucalyptus camaldulensis corresponds with reports from Kenya, Uganda, India, China, and Europe, where these taxa are valued for antibacterial, antiviral, and immune-supporting properties [137–141]. Preparation methods such as steam inhalation further demonstrate culturally refined knowledge of appropriate administration routes for respiratory infections.

Skin diseases, wounds, and inflammatory conditions were treated using 15 species (24.1%), reflecting frequent exposure to injuries and harsh environmental conditions. Similar ethnomedicinal reliance on topical remedies has been documented in Ethiopia, Tanzania, and South America [86, 142–145].

In Sheikh District, the topical use of latex from Calotropis procera and Vachellia tortilis reflects broader African and Asian ethnomedical traditions in which latex-bearing plants are culturally valued for wound healing and antimicrobial effects.

A smaller number of species were reported for ocular ailments such as conjunctivitis and trachoma, demonstrating cautious cultural practices when treating sensitive organs. Plants such as Aloe citrina, Olea europaea, and Terminalia prunioides were applied with controlled dosage, consistent with reports from Ethiopia, Sudan, and Pakistan [146–150].

MPs were also used for chronic and systemic conditions including diabetes, tuberculosis, musculoskeletal pain, urinary disorders, and sexually transmitted infections. For example, the use of Moringa stenopetala for diabetes aligns with studies recognizing its hypoglycemic and antioxidant properties across East Africa and South Asia [9, 12, 14, 151–156]. Similarly, the use of Albizia anthelmintica and Carica papaya seeds for infectious and parasitic diseases reflects well-established practices across Africa and Latin America [73, 97, 157, 158].

Across all remedies, leaves were the most frequently used plant part, followed by roots, seeds, fruits, bark, and latex. The predominance of leaves is often associated with ease of access, simple preparation, and more sustainable harvesting compared with destructive root or bark collection. Leaves are also known to contain high concentrations of bioactive compounds, including flavonoids, alkaloids, and phenolics [49, 137, 159, 160]. Similar plant-part use patterns have been widely documented across Africa, Asia, and Europe [30, 54, 74, 161].

Roots and bark were generally reserved for more severe or systemic illnesses, reflecting local beliefs that underground parts contain concentrated medicinal strength. Multipurpose use of different plant parts was also common. For instance, Balanites aegyptiaca was used to treat asthma (roots), snakebite (bark), and diarrhea (fruit), illustrating its therapeutic versatility and high cultural value. Such multipurpose use, while signaling pharmacological potential, may also increase conservation pressure, as widely used species often face greater harvesting intensity [56, 57, 69, 116].

Thus, the findings demonstrate that communities in Sheikh District rely extensively on MPs and diverse plant parts to address a broad spectrum of health challenges. The strong alignment between local practices and ethnomedicinal patterns reported globally underscores both the shared foundations of indigenous healing knowledge and the culturally specific adaptations shaped by dryland environments.

These results provide important baseline evidence for future pharmacological validation, conservation planning, and sustainable healthcare strategies, while emphasizing the urgent need to preserve both medicinal plant diversity and the indigenous knowledge systems that continue to play a vital role in primary healthcare in Somaliland and beyond.

Modes of preparation, formulation, and conditions of use of medicinalplants

The ethnobotanical survey in Sheikh District showed that indigenous communities employ a diverse range of preparation and formulation techniques for MPs. These practices reflect long-standing cultural knowledge, accumulated experimentation, and practical adaptation to local environmental conditions. Preparation choices were not random but were strongly influenced by the plant part used, the ailment being treated, and the intended route of administration. Among the 54 recorded species, the most frequently reported methods included crushing, grinding or powdering, maceration, boiling/decoction, and direct application of fresh plant material (Fig. 3).

Crushing was the most common method, particularly for leaves, roots, and bark, where plant material is pressed to obtain juices or pastes for oral intake or topical application. Species such as Aloe citrina and Allium sativum were often prepared in this way, either used directly or mixed with water. This technique allows rapid release of active compounds and is especially suitable for treating acute conditions requiring immediate intervention. Similar reliance on crushing has been widely documented across East Africa, India, and Pakistan, suggesting that this practice represents a culturally shared and highly accessible approach in many indigenous medical systems [19, 20, 139, 157, 162].

Grinding or powdering was frequently applied to seeds, bark, and dried leaves. In Sheikh District, species such as Sesamum indicum and Vachellia reficiens were dried, powdered, and administered orally or applied externally to wounds. Powdering not only improves storage and transportability but also facilitates blending with other medicinal ingredients, reflecting local awareness of formulation strategies. Comparable preparation patterns have been reported from Uganda, Nigeria, and Cameroon, indicating that communities in different cultural contexts often converge on similar solutions for maximizing medicinal plant utility [62, 129, 131, 163].

Maceration, commonly performed in water or milk, was used especially for delicate plant parts such as roots and seeds. Plants such as Rotheca myricoides and Punica granatum were prepared in this way to allow gradual extraction of therapeutic compounds while minimizing chemical alteration. This approach reflects a nuanced understanding of plant potency and patient safety, particularly for remedies intended for internal use. Similar culturally careful maceration practices have been widely reported in Ethiopia, Kenya, and India [23, 35, 164, 165].

Boiling and decoction were primarily employed to extract bioactive compounds from tougher plant materials such as bark, stems, and mature leaves. In Sheikh District, decoctions prepared from Eucalyptus camaldulensis and Sterculia africana were commonly used for respiratory illnesses and febrile conditions. Such methods enhance extraction efficiency and are widely associated with the treatment of systemic infections. Comparable uses of decoction have been documented across East Africa, South Asia, and Latin America, highlighting a cross-cultural convergence in addressing fever and respiratory disease through heated herbal preparations [20, 73, 78, 158].

Direct application of latex or sap was reported for a limited number of species, including Calotropis procera and Vachellia tortilis, mainly for wound healing and skin infections. Latex-based remedies are culturally valued for their strong antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties, although they often require careful handling due to potential toxicity. Similar topical applications of latex-bearing plants have been recorded in Africa, India, and Pakistan, reflecting shared ethnomedical perceptions of plant exudates as powerful healing agents [6, 98, 140, 166, 167].

Medicinal remedies were prepared using fresh, dried, or both fresh and dried plant materials, reflecting indigenous knowledge of harvesting cycles, storage strategies, and potency preservation. Fresh materials accounted for 52.4% of preparations, emphasizing the cultural preference for immediacy and perceived therapeutic strength. For example, fresh bulbs of Allium sativum were crushed to treat colds, while fresh leaves of Aloe citrina were applied for eye infections.

Dried materials represented 36.2% of remedies and were particularly common for roots, seeds, and bark, allowing continued availability during dry seasons or periods of scarcity. Examples include dried seeds of Citrullus colocynthis for diarrhea and dried roots of Maytenus undata for stomachache. In 11.4% of cases, communities used both forms interchangeably depending on seasonal accessibility, as observed with Aloe microdonta latex for typhoid and jaundice. Similar seasonal flexibility has been documented in Ethiopia, Kenya, and China, suggesting that storage practices are essential for ensuring year-round healthcare resilience in rural environments [24, 103, 168–170].

Most remedies (62%) involved a single plant species, while 38% combined two or more species. The presence of polyherbal mixtures suggests a cultural understanding of synergistic effects, especially for complex or chronic ailments. Comparable use of multi-species formulations has been widely reported in Nigeria, India, and Australia, where combined preparations are often perceived as enhancing efficacy and broadening therapeutic scope [49, 72, 171].

Hence, the preparation methods documented in Sheikh District demonstrate the depth and sophistication of local ethnobotanical knowledge. The dominance of crushing and grinding reflects practical accessibility, while maceration and decoction indicate culturally refined extraction strategies adapted to different ailments and plant chemistries.

These practices also carry important implications for environmental sustainability and public health. Heavy reliance on fresh material may increase harvesting pressure on wild populations, whereas drying and cultivation can enhance medicinal continuity under climate stress. Understanding preparation modes is therefore essential not only for pharmacological validation but also for designing culturally grounded conservation and healthcare strategies in Somaliland and comparable dryland regions.

Routes, mode of administration, application, and dosage of medicinal plants

MPs documented in Sheikh District were administered primarily through oral, dermal (topical), and ocular (eye) routes. Among these, oral administration was the most frequently employed, followed by dermal application and ocular use. Oral remedies were predominantly used to treat systemic conditions, including gastrointestinal disorders, malaria, diabetes, respiratory infections, and fever, reflecting the community’s reliance on liquid formulations for internal delivery. This pattern is consistent with observations from Mana Angetu and Artuma Fursi districts in Ethiopia, where over 50% of traditional remedies were administered orally for systemic ailments, with topical applications used less frequently [172, 173]. Similarly, in Yunnan, China, oral preparations are preferred due to perceived efficacy and ease of absorption [1, 141].

Dermal applications were primarily targeted at localized conditions such as wounds, skin infections, eczema, and insect bites. Remedies were applied as crushed or powdered plant parts, pastes, or latex, demonstrating the community’s nuanced understanding of disease localization. Comparable practices have been reported in Kenya and Uganda, where topical applications of plant-based pastes and ointments are widely used to manage skin ailments and external injuries [39, 40, 42, 174]. Ocular administration, although less frequent, was applied for eye conditions such as conjunctivitis. Plant juices from species such as Aloe citrina and Olea europaea were applied directly to the eyes, a practice also documented in Pakistan, illustrating targeted use of MPs for specific ailments [19, 175].

Preparation methods varied according to plant part and route of administration. Oral remedies were generally prepared by crushing, grinding, or powdering plant parts and mixing them with water, tea, milk, or other carriers. Examples include Allium sativum bulbs crushed and mixed with water for the common cold, Moringa stenopetala leaves powdered and consumed with tea for diabetes, and Citrullus colocynthis seeds dried, crushed, and mixed with water to treat diarrhea. Dermal applications involved direct application of pastes, crushed plant parts, or latex to affected areas, such as Calotropis procera latex for white eczema, Vachellia tortilis latex for wound healing, and burned Momordica charantia leaves to repel mosquitoes. Ocular remedies included squeezed or extracted plant juices applied directly to the eyes, as with Aloe citrina and Olea europaea, or Terminalia prunioides latex mixed with water for conjunctivitis. These preparation methods align with ethnobotanical practices in China, India, and Pakistan, where crushing, decoction, and infusion techniques are widely used to maximize medicinal efficacy [5, 139, 176–179].

Among the various methods of application, drinking was the most predominant (42.1%), followed by application to the skin (14.3%) and washing with plant extracts (13.3%). Dosage was largely informal and guided by traditional knowledge. Local healers determined quantities based on patient age, type and location of illness, severity, disease stage, physical condition, and extent of infection. Traditional units included finger length for roots and stems, spoons, tea cups, or glasses for powders and liquids, and counting leaves, flowers, fruits, seeds, bulbs, and latex. Such practices reflect careful observation and adaptation to ensure safe and effective treatment. Similar informal dosing has been reported in Ethiopian districts, where healers rely on “handfuls” or cup measurements, demonstrating that traditional systems adjust doses empirically rather than through formal standardization [16, 34, 146].

Some plant species exhibited remarkable versatility, being used for multiple ailments with different routes of administration. For instance, Aloe microdonta latex was ingested orally for typhoid and jaundice, whereas Aloe pirottae leaves were applied topically for stomachache. Similarly, Balanites aegyptiaca roots were consumed orally for asthma, bark applied for snakebite, and fruits ingested for diarrhea. This versatility aligns with patterns observed in Kenya, Uganda, and India, where multipurpose plant species are integral to traditional medicine systems [60, 61, 76, 116].

Overall, the findings from Sheikh District are consistent with ethnobotanical patterns reported across East Africa, Asia, Europe, and the Americas [20, 97, 137, 145, 157]. Oral remedies dominate systemic treatments, while dermal and ocular applications address localized conditions, reflecting convergent strategies across diverse cultural contexts. These similarities highlight the universality and robustness of indigenous knowledge systems, which balance empirical observation with culturally informed practices to ensure effective treatment [175, 178, 180, 181].

Hence, routes of administration, methods of application, and dosage are carefully adapted to the type of ailment and plant part used, reflecting a sophisticated and context-specific ethnomedicinal knowledge system. The predominance of oral remedies emphasizes the importance of liquid formulations, while dermal and ocular routes illustrate the specificity and diversity of traditional practices. By situating Sheikh District within a comparative global framework, these findings contribute to a broader understanding of medicinal plant use and provide a foundation for pharmacological validation, conservation, and culturally sensitive healthcare interventions.

Marketability of medicinal plants in Sheikh district

The present study revealed that only a limited proportion of MPs documented in Sheikh District are actively traded in local markets, with 27.7% (15 of 54 species) reported as marketable (Table 3). This pattern underscores the dual role of MPs as both therapeutic resources and economic commodities, while the majority remain primarily used within households or community networks rather than through formal market channels. Comparable observations have been reported in Ethiopia, where only 20–30% of ethnomedicinal species are sold commercially, often for culinary or non-medicinal purposes rather than explicit therapeutic use [34, 64, 128].

Among the most frequently marketed species in Sheikh District were Allium sativum, Azadirachta indica, Moringa stenopetala, Balanites aegyptiaca, and Citrullus colocynthis. These plants are valued not only for their medicinal effectiveness but also because their parts bulbs, leaves, seeds, bark, and fruits are relatively easy to harvest, process, store, and transport, enhancing their commercial viability. Similar trends have been observed in Tanzania’s Kariakoo Market, where non-woody herbs, seeds, and fruits dominate trade due to their durability and economic potential, providing income for vendors while meeting high community demand [182]. Likewise, in Western Kenya, Azadirachta indica and Aloe species were among the most traded MPs, highlighting the regional importance of widely recognized and culturally accepted species [154].

Marketability in Sheikh District appeared to be influenced by several factors, including frequency of use for common ailments, simplicity of preparation, shelf life, and cultural acceptance. For example, Allium sativum bulbs are widely sold due to their use in treating colds and flu, while fruits and roots of Balanites aegyptiaca are traded for gastrointestinal and respiratory conditions. Conversely, plants with highly perishable parts, such as fresh leaves or latex (Aloe pirottae, Calotropis procera), are less frequently marketed and are typically collected directly from the wild when needed. This pattern mirrors other Ethiopian market studies, where plants intended for rare, acute, or specialized conditions are often accessed through traditional healers rather than through commercial outlets [34, 39, 44, 65].

In Sheikh markets, leaves and fruits were the most commonly sold plant parts, followed by seeds, roots, and latex. This preference reflects practical considerations related to storage durability and transportation, a trend consistent with broader African markets where dried leaves and fruits dominate herbal trade due to their lower perishability [2, 18, 70]. Roots and bark, despite their therapeutic significance, are less frequently traded due to sustainability concerns and the labor-intensive nature of their collection.

The study also found that many MPs sold in Sheikh markets are traded primarily for non-medicinal purposes, such as food, spices, or household use. Species such as Capsicum annuum, Lepidium sativum, Commiphora myrrha, and Zingiber officinale are commonly marketed for culinary purposes, with medicinal applications considered secondary. This dual-use pattern has been documented in other East African and Ethiopian markets, where MPs often enter trade under the guise of edible or aromatic products [128, 133, 154, 183, 184].

Although only a minority of MPs appear in formal markets, their trade contributes to household incomes, particularly for rural vendors, women traders, and traditional practitioners who rely on herbal commerce as a supplementary economic activity. Similar economic roles have been reported in urban African markets, where the sale of MPs supports livelihoods and underscores the continued importance of traditional medicine in primary healthcare [2, 18]. Additionally, species such as Allium sativum also participate in broader regional and global herbal markets, reflecting increasing demand for natural and complementary therapies [182, 185].

However, commercialization raises critical conservation concerns. Much of the traded material is harvested from wild populations, and unsustainable collection could threaten biodiversity and deplete valuable plant resources. Similar challenges have been documented in Tanzania, highlighting the need for regulated trade, sustainable harvesting practices, and conservation awareness to safeguard both ecological integrity and community well-being [182].

Hence, while most MPs in Sheikh District are primarily used for subsistence healthcare, a meaningful fraction possesses commercial value. Strengthening sustainable harvesting, improving market access, and integrating ethnobotanical resources into broader economic and conservation frameworks could enhance both biodiversity preservation and socio-economic resilience. Situating Sheikh District within a comparative global context highlights both shared patterns and unique regional characteristics, informing responsible resource use and market development.

Novel ethnobotanical findings

The present study in Sheikh District, Sahil Region, Somaliland, documents a range of ethnomedicinal practices that represent either novel reports for the country or rarely recorded uses within East Africa. While many of the plant species identified are already recognized in African traditional medicine, the specific combinations of plant parts, preparation methods, routes of administration, and targeted ailments observed here provide meaningful additions to the regional ethnobotanical record. These findings reflect a locally refined therapeutic knowledge that illustrates the interplay between ecological adaptation and long-standing indigenous expertise [34, 35, 185].

Several medicinal applications documented in this study constitute new reports for Somaliland. For example, Albizia anthelmintica was used to treat gonorrhea through dermal washing with preparations derived from roots or root bark (Table 4). Although the species is widely acknowledged for its anthelmintic properties in other African contexts, its topical application for sexually transmitted infections has not previously been reported in Somali ethnomedicine. Similarly, the oral administration of latex from Aloe microdonta for typhoid and jaundice represents a novel medicinal use in Somaliland, since Aloe species are more commonly associated with dermatological or gastrointestinal treatments rather than systemic infectious or hepatic conditions. Additionally, macerated roots of Rotheca myricoides were used for placenta removal, highlighting a rare national-level application and emphasizing the critical role of traditional MPs in obstetric and maternal healthcare.

Specialized ocular treatments, rarely documented in Somali ethnobotanical literature, were also observed. Crushed or squeezed leaves of Aloe citrina and Olea europaea subsp. africana, along with diluted latex of Terminalia prunioides, were applied directly to the eyes to manage conditions such as trachoma, conjunctivitis, and other ocular infections. These practices demonstrate a nuanced indigenous understanding of safe dosage and plant selection for sensitive organs, echoing similar ocular remedies reported in Pakistan and India, where traditional healers carefully administer plant extracts for eye ailments [5, 159, 175].

Several practices documented in Sheikh District also constitute rare or poorly recorded uses within East Africa. Oral administration of Hydnora hanningtonii roots for systemic infections demonstrates the ethnomedicinal importance of this parasitic species, which remains scarcely documented regionally. Smoke-based therapies were another distinctive feature: stems of Cordia sinensis were burned to alleviate bone and body pain, while smoke from roots of Tragus racemosus and Zygophyllum hildebrandtii was applied dermally to manage culturally defined conditions, such as Xulbad disease in children. These non-ingestive approaches reflect a sophisticated understanding of disease management, underrepresented in East African ethnobotanical literature [19, 20, 78].

Latex-based remedies were widely utilized and demonstrated remarkable therapeutic diversity. For example, latex from Vachellia seyal was applied for diarrhea, Senegalia senegal for abdominal pain, Calotropis procera for eczema, and Terminalia prunioides for conjunctivitis. While latex use is well established across African traditional medicine, the breadth of conditions treated in Sheikh District illustrates a refined local knowledge of plant chemistry and pharmacodynamics, consistent with ethnobotanical practices observed in Africa and Asia [3, 19, 74, 157].

Several species exhibited notable therapeutic versatility. Balanites aegyptiaca roots were used for asthma, bark for snakebite, and fruits for diarrhea, highlighting the species’ cultural and medicinal significance. Preventive and culturally embedded practices were also observed, such as chewing Cissus rotundifolia roots to prevent snakebite or using Punica granatum leaves and seeds mixed with milk to treat “eye evil.” These practices illustrate the integration of spiritual and physiological concepts in traditional healthcare [20, 78, 95].

Ethnonutritional and chronic disease management practices were additionally recorded. Seeds of Cordeauxia edulis were chewed to address malnutrition, while leaves of Moringa stenopetala were used to manage diabetes, demonstrating the dual role of certain plants in both nutrition and medicinal therapy. These findings highlight the dynamic, context-specific nature of traditional healthcare in Sheikh District and emphasize the empirical foundation of indigenous knowledge systems.

By explicitly documenting new reports for Somaliland and rare uses for East Africa, this study provides a valuable baseline for pharmacological research, comparative ethnobotanical studies, and conservation planning. These findings underscore the urgency of preserving indigenous medicinal knowledge, as locally refined practices may inform modern drug discovery, culturally sensitive healthcare interventions, and sustainable resource management at regional and global scales.

Informant consensus on the medicinal use of plants

The present study in Sheikh District, Sahil Region, Somaliland, revealed that certain medicinal plant species are widely recognized and consistently utilized by local communities and traditional healers. Informant consensus analysis demonstrated that some species were repeatedly cited across multiple informants, reflecting both shared cultural knowledge and strong perceived therapeutic reliability. High consensus values, particularly for Citrus × limon (73.6%), Vachellia bussei (62.3%), and Allium sativum (56.6%), indicate that these plants play a central role in local healthcare practices and may represent the most culturally and medicinally significant taxa in the study area (Table 5). Similar patterns have been reported in Uganda and Kenya, where frequently cited species are closely associated with common health concerns such as malaria, gastrointestinal disorders, and respiratory infections [61, 76, 164, 186, 187].

Repeated citations of species such as Citrullus colocynthis, Hydnora hanningtonii, and Ziziphus hamur further underscore their cultural relevance and potential pharmacological importance. In ethnobotany, high informant consensus is widely considered a reliable indicator for prioritizing plants for bioactivity screening, as it reflects both the perceived efficacy reported by local practitioners and the consistency of knowledge across the community [188]. In Sheikh District, the prominence of Allium sativum and Moringa stenopetala aligns with observations from Ethiopia and Tanzania, where these species are among the most frequently cited MPs in both markets and household healthcare systems [64, 189, 190].

Interestingly, species with the highest informant consensus often exhibit multi-purpose uses. For example, Citrus × limon is applied for gastrointestinal ailments, respiratory infections, and as a natural antimicrobial, reflecting its versatility and strong local preference. This multifunctionality mirrors findings from Kenya and India, where plants with broad-spectrum applications tend to achieve higher citation frequencies due to accessibility, perceived effectiveness, and integration into everyday health practices [139, 154, 191].

In contrast, species with moderate yet notable consensus, such as Commiphora myrrha, Psidium guajava, and Commelina erecta, appear to be culturally valued for more specialized or seasonal applications. Similar trends have been documented in Nigeria and Pakistan, where certain MPs are reserved for specific or culturally significant conditions, resulting in reliable but lower informant consensus [6, 88].

From both conservation and pharmacological perspectives, species with high informant consensus represent priority targets for further research and sustainable management. Their repeated use and widespread recognition suggest therapeutic efficacy, yet al.so indicate a potential risk of overharvesting, particularly in areas where wild collection predominates. Studies from Tanzania, Cameroon, and Ethiopia emphasize the importance of integrating community knowledge with conservation strategies to safeguard both biodiversity and traditional healthcare resources [47, 62, 189].

Thus, the informant consensus analysis in Sheikh District highlights plants of central cultural and medicinal importance, providing an evidence-based framework for prioritizing species in pharmacological investigations and conservation planning. By linking indigenous knowledge with scientific research, these findings resonate with global patterns observed across Africa, Asia, and other regions with rich traditional medicinal systems [141, 159].

Local community preferences for medicinal plants used to treat diarrhea

In Sheikh District, Sahil Region, Somaliland, eight medicinal plant species were documented for the management of diarrhea. Among these, five species were highlighted through preference ranking based on perceived effectiveness, availability, and overall importance, as assessed by seven key informants. This approach reflects the nuanced experiential knowledge of local practitioners and households, illustrating how communities differentiate between plant efficacy, accessibility, and multifunctionality when addressing prevalent health challenges.

Citrus × limon emerged as the most preferred species, receiving the highest number of citations (78; 73.6%) and consistently ranking first in preference. Its prominence in local healthcare aligns with reports from Kenya and India, where Citrus × limon is widely used for gastrointestinal disorders due to its antimicrobial, digestive, and anti-inflammatory properties [40, 48, 192]. The species’ local dominance likely reflects a combination of therapeutic efficacy and widespread availability, underscoring the importance of accessible plants in sustaining community health practices.

The second most preferred species, Vachellia bussei, cited by 66 informants (62.3%), similarly demonstrates high cultural and medicinal significance. Comparative studies from Ethiopia and Tanzania also highlight the use of Vachellia species as traditional remedies for diarrhea and other gastrointestinal ailments, revealing a regional convergence in recognizing legumes and thorny shrubs as effective antidiarrheal agents [48, 193]. Other frequently cited species, including Allium sativum, Citrullus colocynthis, and Hydnora cornii, exemplify multifunctional MPs valued not only for gastrointestinal relief but also for broader therapeutic applications, such as antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects [69, 194].

The application of preference ranking and direct matrix ranking provides a structured means to understand community priorities in medicinal plant use. Despite eight reported antidiarrheal species, five were consistently favored, suggesting that local knowledge is selective and grounded in practical experience. For example, Lippia carviodora and Psidium guajava were moderately preferred, whereas Vachellia seyal ranked lowest (Table 6). This differentiation likely reflects factors such as effectiveness, seasonal availability, ease of preparation, cultural familiarity, and plant versatility, consistent with ethnobotanical patterns observed in Nigeria, Pakistan, and other regions with rich indigenous medicinal traditions [68, 136, 195].

High preference scores indicate both cultural prominence and potential pharmacological relevance. Species like Citrus × limon and Vachellia bussei were repeatedly cited across informants, reflecting perceived therapeutic consistency and reliability. Comparative studies from East Africa, Asia, and other global contexts suggest that frequently cited plants often possess reproducible bioactivity and are more likely to be integrated into both traditional and modern medicinal frameworks [3, 19, 20, 74].

From a conservation perspective, these preference patterns have significant implications. High-demand species may face overharvesting, especially when collected from wild populations. Similar ecological concerns have been documented in Ethiopia, Cameroon, and Tanzania, emphasizing the importance of integrating community knowledge with sustainable harvesting practices to preserve both biodiversity and the healthcare value of MPs [43, 62, 63]. Prioritizing commonly used species in conservation planning ensures the continuity of indigenous healthcare practices while mitigating risks to plant populations.

In summary, preference ranking in Sheikh District reveals that local communities selectively prioritize MPs for diarrhea based on perceived effectiveness, accessibility, and multifunctionality. Species such as Citrus × limon, Vachellia bussei, and other highly ranked plants represent both cultural keystones and candidates for pharmacological validation. These findings highlight the intersection of traditional knowledge, sustainable resource use, and scientific research, echoing patterns observed across Africa, Asia, and globally, and provide a foundation for integrating indigenous practices into broader health and conservation strategies.

Direct matrix ranking and multiple uses of medicinal plants

The direct matrix ranking conducted in Sheikh District highlights the multifunctional significance of MPs within local communities. Beyond their therapeutic roles, these species are integral to daily livelihood practices, including firewood, fodder, charcoal production, construction, fencing, and tool making. This dual utility underscores the deep integration of ethnobotanical knowledge with community survival strategies, where a plant’s value is assessed not only by its medicinal efficacy but also by its contribution to food security, energy needs, and economic resilience [46, 63, 196].

Among the seven species evaluated, Vachellia bussei ranked highest, demonstrating remarkable versatility across all six use categories (Table 7). This pattern aligns with observations from Kenya, Tanzania, and Ethiopia, where multipurpose Vachellia species are highly valued for their durability, biomass, and accessibility, supporting both healthcare and subsistence needs [11, 12, 57, 63, 96]. Similarly, Vachellia tortilis and Vachellia nilotica ranked second and third, reflecting their critical role in local livelihoods and the significant anthropogenic pressure placed upon them through combined medicinal and non-medicinal uses.

The prominence of these multipurpose species underscores their centrality to community life, yet intensive utilization raises concerns regarding overexploitation. Evidence from Nigeria and other East African countries indicates that plants heavily relied upon for multiple purposes are at higher risk of depletion, especially when harvested simultaneously for fuel, fodder, timber, and medicinal uses [88, 110, 130]. The trends observed in Sheikh District mirror these global patterns, emphasizing the need for proactive conservation strategies that balance human needs with ecological sustainability.

Species with lower total scores, such as Maytenus undata and Balanites aegyptiaca, are primarily used for medicinal purposes, with limited non-medicinal applications. While immediate overexploitation risks may be lower, these species remain culturally and pharmacologically important, warranting targeted conservation interventions to ensure continued availability for therapeutic use [51, 54, 176]. Likewise, Eucalyptus camaldulensis and Terminalia prunioides exhibit moderate multifunctional use, reflecting selective harvesting influenced by local preferences, accessibility, and ecological distribution.

The direct matrix ranking approach provides a practical framework for understanding how communities assign relative importance to MPs, integrating both utilitarian and healthcare considerations. By quantifying local perceptions and use intensity, this method identifies species at high risk of overharvesting and informs priorities for sustainable management. Globally, similar approaches from India and Pakistan to Europe and Australia have shown that multipurpose plants face heightened ecological pressures, emphasizing the need for conservation strategies that combine local knowledge with sustainable harvesting, cultivation, and stewardship [46, 63, 152, 196].

In conclusion, the direct matrix ranking in Sheikh District demonstrates that Vachellia bussei, Vachellia tortilis, and Vachellia nilotica are key species for both medicinal and livelihood practices. Their multifunctionality highlights their ecological, cultural, and economic significance, while simultaneously revealing their vulnerability to overuse. Integrating ethnobotanical knowledge with conservation planning and sustainable management is crucial to ensure that these species continue to support community health, livelihoods, and biodiversity, both in Somaliland and in regions with similar ecological and cultural settings.

Informant consensus factor (ICF)

The Informant Consensus Factor (ICF) analysis aims to provide insight into how local communities in Sheikh District, Sahil Region, Somaliland, perceive and employ MPs for different health conditions. For this study, reported ailments were grouped into nine major categories based on symptoms, affected body systems, and similarities in traditional treatment strategies. The ICF, which reflects the level of agreement among informants regarding plant use for specific ailments, ranged from 0.25 for wound healing to 0.77 for gastrointestinal and parasitic disorders (Table 8). High ICF values suggest strong agreement, indicating that certain illnesses are prevalent and consistently treated with a limited set of culturally trusted plant species [4, 89, 189].

Despite its usefulness, several authors have highlighted significant limitations in applying ICF. Leonti and colleagues, for example, caution that consensus values may be influenced by sampling bias, the number of informants, or social dynamics within communities, rather than strictly reflecting therapeutic efficacy [19, 20]. Similarly, high ICF values may reflect cultural popularity of a remedy rather than pharmacological effectiveness. Recognizing these caveats, we apply the ICF with caution but still consider it informative, as it provides a culturally contextual overview of plant use patterns and can guide further pharmacological and conservation studies.

Gastrointestinal and parasitic disorders recorded the highest ICF (0.77), highlighting their prominence in Sheikh District. This suggests that oral administration of plant-based remedies is the main treatment route, likely due to ease of preparation, accessibility, and the high prevalence of digestive health challenges. These findings are consistent with ethnobotanical research in Kenya, Uganda, and Ethiopia, where gastrointestinal ailments dominate traditional healthcare priorities, often linked to sanitation limitations, water scarcity, and food insecurity [65, 95, 197]. Similar trends are observed in India and Pakistan, where widely cited remedies such as Citrus limon and Allium sativum achieve high consensus due to their digestive and antimicrobial properties [6, 198].

Dermatological problems, including ringworm and other skin infections, had the second-highest ICF (0.75), reflecting strong agreement among informants about effective topical treatments. Comparable observations from Tanzania, Nigeria, and China suggest that visible efficacy and cultural familiarity contribute to high consensus for dermatological remedies [136, 171, 182, 185, 190]. Similarly, acute and common conditions, such as malaria, arthritis, headache, and diabetes, showed an ICF of 0.72, indicating both prevalence and reliance on specific, culturally validated MPs.

In contrast, wound healing exhibited the lowest ICF (0.25), indicating more variation in treatment approaches among practitioners. This may reflect differences in plant availability, wound type, or individual healer preferences. Low consensus for wound management has been noted in Europe, America, and Asia, where diverse plant remedies are employed for complex or culturally variable injuries, resulting in lower informant agreement [85, 86, 97, 137].

Sexual and delivery-related conditions, such as gonorrhea, placenta retention, and sexual dysfunction, showed a moderate ICF of 0.65. The lower consensus compared to gastrointestinal disorders likely reflects the sensitive nature of reproductive health and the restricted use of trusted plants for safety and efficacy. Similar patterns have been reported in Ethiopia, Kenya, and Pakistan, where reproductive remedies are culturally guarded and selectively applied [34, 168, 178, 187, 195].

Thus, while acknowledging the methodological limitations of ICF, the analysis still provides useful insights into the relationship between disease prevalence, cultural importance, and medicinal plant selection. High-consensus categories such as gastrointestinal, dermatological, and acute conditions highlight plants that are culturally significant and likely to contain reproducible bioactive compounds. Conversely, lower-consensus categories indicate treatments that are either less frequent or highly individualized, underscoring the diversity of local medicinal knowledge. These findings are consistent with global ethnobotanical patterns, confirming that ICF, when interpreted critically, remains a valuable tool for identifying culturally and pharmacologically important species across Africa, Asia, Europe, and the Americas [19, 20, 85, 86, 97, 137].

In Sheikh District, the ICF findings emphasize that indigenous knowledge integrates ecological adaptation, empirical validation, and sustainable use. High-consensus plants provide an evidence-based basis for prioritizing species in pharmacological research, conservation efforts, and community health programs, demonstrating the broader relevance of local ethnomedicinal knowledge in both regional and global contexts.

Fidelity level of medicinal plants

The Fidelity Level (FL) analysis provides insight into the cultural and therapeutic importance of MPs in Sheikh District, highlighting species that are consistently trusted for treating specific ailments. FL measures the proportion of informants who report the use of a particular plant for a given condition, thus identifying plants that are both culturally valued and perceived as effective.

Despite its utility, several authors, including Leonti and colleagues, have emphasized limitations in applying FL. These indices may be influenced by the number of informants, sampling bias, or social and cultural factors that do not necessarily reflect pharmacological efficacy [29, 188, 197]. High FL values could represent cultural popularity or ease of access rather than verified therapeutic action. Therefore, the application of FL should be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, we believe that the information provided by FL remains valuable because it highlights plants that are repeatedly used and trusted within the community, offering a culturally grounded starting point for pharmacological validation, conservation planning, and integration into health programs.

In Sheikh District, informants commonly reported ailments including diarrhea, wounds, eye infections, common cold, toothache, jaundice, malaria, asthma, and tick infestations. The FL analysis revealed that Vachellia bussei and Balanites aegyptiaca had the highest fidelity levels (94%), reflecting strong agreement among practitioners regarding their use for diarrhea and asthma, respectively (Table 9). This indicates that these species occupy a central role in the local ethnomedicinal knowledge system and are considered highly reliable for their targeted ailments. Other notable FL values included Melia azedarach for toothache (83%), Aloe microdonta for jaundice (77%), and Commelina erecta for wound healing (73%).

The prominence of Vachellia bussei and Balanites aegyptiaca aligns with ethnobotanical patterns across East Africa. Vachellia species in Kenya and Tanzania are widely recognized for gastrointestinal and respiratory disorders, owing to their anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and astringent properties [123, 124, 186, 187]. Similarly, Balanites aegyptiaca has been reported in Ethiopia and Nigeria as an effective remedy for digestive and respiratory conditions, reflecting a cross-regional convergence of traditional knowledge in areas with similar ecological and cultural contexts [126, 128, 136, 171].

Species with moderate FL values, such as Aloe pirottae for malaria (66%) and Zingiber officinale for the common cold (78%), indicate plants with broader medicinal applications, often used for multiple conditions. Conversely, lower FL values, such as Punica granatum for eye infections (40%), suggest either less consistent efficacy, variability in practitioner knowledge, or differences in treatment preferences. Comparable patterns have been documented in India, Pakistan, and China, where multi-purpose plants often show lower FL for individual ailments but maintain broad cultural and therapeutic relevance [149, 153, 156, 170, 185].

FL analysis complements the Informant Consensus Factor (ICF) by pinpointing species that are specifically and consistently trusted for particular conditions. High-FL species are strong candidates for pharmacological investigation, as repeated independent use across multiple informants often correlates with the presence of bioactive compounds with reliable therapeutic effects [29]. Moreover, high-FL plants are critical in conservation planning, as overharvesting culturally and therapeutically important species could threaten both biodiversity and local healthcare practices. Studies across East Africa, Asia, Europe, and Australia underscore the importance of combining ethnobotanical knowledge with sustainable management, cultivation, and drug discovery initiatives [20, 78, 137].

Hence, while acknowledging the methodological limitations of FL, the analysis in Sheikh District highlights that Vachellia bussei, Balanites aegyptiaca, and Melia azedarach are culturally and therapeutically significant, with strong agreement among local informants regarding their effectiveness. These findings provide an evidence-based framework for prioritizing species for pharmacological research, conservation efforts, and integration into local healthcare initiatives. By emphasizing high-fidelity species, this study demonstrates that, despite its limitations, FL is a useful tool for bridging indigenous knowledge and modern scientific inquiry.

Medicinal plant knowledge among informant groups and indigenous knowledge transmission

In Sheikh District, Sahil Region, Somaliland, indigenous knowledge of MPs is primarily transmitted orally across generations, reflecting a deeply rooted cultural practice. In this study, most respondents (66%, n = 60) reported learning from family members, while a smaller proportion (34%, n = 46) acquired knowledge from other community members, including neighbors and traditional practitioners. This underscores that familial transmission remains the primary avenue for preserving ethnobotanical knowledge, a pattern also documented in Kenya, Tanzania, and Ethiopia, where intergenerational learning within families ensures continuity of traditional medicinal practices [14, 15, 39, 48, 65, 161].

Despite these strong cultural mechanisms, the study revealed emerging threats to knowledge retention. These include a declining number of traditional healers, limited written documentation, and the influence of industrialization and modern healthcare systems, which may reduce the transmission of medicinal plant knowledge to younger generations. Similar concerns have been reported in Uganda, Nigeria, and India, where modernization has contributed to the erosion of traditional ethnobotanical expertise [95, 139, 163].

Analysis across informant groups revealed significant disparities in medicinal plant knowledge associated with gender, literacy, and experience. Male informants demonstrated higher mean knowledge scores (3.4 ± 1.8) than females (1.9 ± 1.1; t = 5.0, P < 0.05). Similarly, illiterate participants reported greater knowledge (4.0 ± 1.9) than literate participants (1.9 ± 1.3; t = 6.4, P < 0.05). Key informants, including traditional healers and community elders, exhibited the highest expertise (6.0 ± 1.2) compared to general informants (2.5 ± 1.2; t = 7.8, P < 0.05) (Table 10). These results indicate that specialized experience and active engagement in medicinal plant use are critical determinants of knowledge retention, a pattern consistent with observations from China, Pakistan, and East Africa [140, 157, 170].

Age also emerged as a strong predictor of medicinal plant knowledge. One-way ANOVA revealed significant variation across age categories (F = 67.12, P < 0.05), with older informants consistently scoring higher than younger participants (Table 11). Correlation analysis showed a strong positive relationship between age and knowledge (r = 0.82, P < 0.001), and linear regression indicated that approximately 67% of the variability in knowledge could be explained by age alone (Knowledge = 0.15 × Age − 1.2, R² = 0.67, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4). This reflects the accumulation of experiential knowledge over a lifetime, and mirrors findings from East Africa, India, and Europe, where elders serve as custodians of traditional botanical wisdom, while younger generations increasingly rely on formal healthcare systems [23, 24, 78, 137, 192].

The observed gender and literacy trends suggest that medicinal plant knowledge is deeply embedded in social and cultural contexts. In Sheikh District, men often have greater access to outdoor medicinal resources, whereas women’s knowledge is frequently oriented toward domestic healthcare practices. Illiterate individuals, relying more on experiential learning than formal education, preserve traditional knowledge that may be lost among literate or urbanized populations. Similar patterns have been documented in Tanzania and Nigeria, indicating that ethnomedicinal knowledge is closely tied to lifestyle, occupation, and cultural roles [69, 123, 132, 190].

Collectively, these findings highlight the critical role of familial transmission, age, and practical experience in shaping medicinal plant knowledge. However, the declining number of traditional healers and socio-economic changes pose threats to the continuity of indigenous knowledge. Globally, comparable challenges are reported in Europe, America, Asia, and Australia, where oral transmission remains central to ethnobotanical learning but is increasingly vulnerable to modernization and urbanization [29, 54, 74]. These results emphasize the urgent need for systematic documentation, community-based preservation programs, and integration of traditional practices into education and health initiatives to ensure the sustainability of ethnobotanical knowledge in Sheikh District.

Threats and conservation status of medicinal plants in Sheikh district

MPs in Sheikh District are increasingly exposed to both human-driven and environmental pressures, threatening their sustainability and the continuity of traditional healthcare practices. Based on rankings provided by eight key informants, charcoal production emerged as the most severe threat, followed by firewood collection and prolonged drought, while overgrazing and agricultural expansion were perceived as less critical (Table 12). This pattern indicates that human activities linked to energy, construction, and subsistence needs are the primary drivers of MP depletion. Similar trends have been reported across East Africa, including Kenya, Tanzania, and Ethiopia, where overharvesting for fuelwood and construction is a leading cause of decline among culturally important species [63, 95, 108, 147, 199, 200]. Comparable pressures have also been documented in South Asia, particularly in Pakistan and India, where unsustainable harvesting for timber, fuel, and medicinal use has led to local depletion of significant plant species [5, 6, 53, 97, 116].

The lack of systematic in-situ conservation in Sheikh District further compounds the vulnerability of MPs. Although two ex-situ conservation sites exist in the mountainous regions of Gacan-Libaax and Weger, their scope is limited, leaving most species unprotected within their natural habitats. Only a few MPs are safeguarded in home gardens, and while local customary laws protect certain species, they are insufficient to counter large-scale extraction. Notably, some species experiencing intense local pressure, such as Vachellia bussei and Balanites aegyptiaca, are classified as Least Concern (LC) on the IUCN Red List due to their wider distribution. This discrepancy highlights the importance of localized monitoring, as global assessments may fail to capture pressures within specific communities [29, 183, 184].

Sheikh District lies within the Somali xeric eco-region, a unique biogeographical zone characterized by high endemism and species adapted to arid environments. Its flora shares biogeographical connections with the Arabian Peninsula, eastern Mediterranean, and more distant regions such as the Canary Islands and Madagascar. Local communities rely heavily on these resources for medicinal, construction, and fuel purposes and many species have experienced noticeable declines over recent decades due to overexploitation, limited natural regeneration, and unsustainable harvesting practices. Informants reported that species under the most significant pressure include Vachellia bussei, Vachellia tortilis, Vachellia nilotica, Balanites aegyptiaca, Eucalyptus camaldulensis, and Dodonaea angustifolia (Table 13). Although globally assessed as LC, these observations demonstrate that even widely distributed species can face intense localized threats, emphasizing the need for site-specific conservation interventions [4, 7, 11, 45, 77, 147].

The patterns in Sheikh District reflect broader regional and global trends. In Nigeria and Cameroon, MPs face similar pressures from fuelwood collection, overgrazing, and agricultural expansion. In South and East Asia, including India, China, and Pakistan, habitat degradation, commercial harvesting, and unsustainable trade have caused declines in culturally valuable species [5, 116, 161, 169, 178, 179]. Even in Europe, North America, and Australia, urbanization, invasive species, and climate change increasingly threaten wild MP populations, demonstrating that anthropogenic and environmental pressures on MPs are a globally pervasive concern [29, 54, 71, 145].

These findings highlight the urgent need for sustainable management strategies to conserve medicinal plant diversity in Sheikh District. Recommended actions include community-based awareness programs, expansion of ex-situ cultivation in home gardens and protected areas, and integration of indigenous knowledge into formal conservation planning. Prioritizing species with high cultural and therapeutic value, such as Vachellia bussei, Balanites aegyptiaca, and Dodonaea angustifolia, can mitigate local extinction risks while ensuring continued access to essential medicinal resources. Globally, integrating local knowledge with formal conservation strategies has proven effective in safeguarding ethnobotanical resources and maintaining cultural and economic benefits [11, 14, 15, 40, 41].

Thus, MPs in Sheikh District face multifaceted threats from human activities and environmental pressures. While many species are classified as LC on a global scale, localized depletion underscores the need for targeted conservation interventions. Aligning community-based management with formal conservation strategies offers a viable pathway to preserve both the biological diversity and rich indigenous knowledge that underpin traditional healthcare systems in the region. These findings resonate with global observations, emphasizing the universal importance of incorporating local perspectives into sustainable conservation frameworks.

Implications of medicinal plant use on health, food security, environmental protection, and cultural value

The MPs documented in Sheikh District extend far beyond their immediate healthcare functions, playing crucial roles in community well-being, ecological stewardship, and cultural preservation. In this study, 52 plant species were recorded, used to manage a broad spectrum of ailments, including gastrointestinal disorders, respiratory infections, malaria, diabetes, skin diseases, and eye infections. This diversity reflects a sophisticated ethnomedicinal knowledge system, in which communities rely heavily on MPs as primary healthcare providers, particularly where access to modern medical facilities is limited. High informant consensus for gastrointestinal and respiratory ailments, particularly for species such as Vachellia bussei and Balanites aegyptiaca, underscores their perceived efficacy. Comparable patterns have been observed in Kenya, Tanzania, and Ethiopia, where certain plants are repeatedly cited for specific ailments due to their recognized therapeutic reliability [19, 20, 45, 46, 184].

Beyond medicinal applications, many MPs contribute directly to nutritional security and household resilience. Species such as Balanites aegyptiaca, Ziziphus hamur, Tamarindus indica, Moringa stenopetala, Capsicum annuum, Cordeauxia edulis, Carica papaya, Citrullus colocynthis, and Psidium guajava provide essential nutrients while supporting overall health and productivity. This dual functionality strengthens food and economic security, enabling communities to withstand environmental stressors and disease outbreaks. Similar multifunctional roles of ethnomedicinal plants have been reported in Ethiopia, Nigeria, India, and Pakistan, highlighting the synergistic connection between medicinal plant use, nutrition, and animal husbandry in semi-arid and rural settings [58, 184, 201–204].

The study also revealed that many MPs, including Vachellia species, Balanites aegyptiaca, and Eucalyptus camaldulensis, are harvested directly from natural habitats, emphasizing the need to conserve local ecosystems. Overharvesting, habitat degradation, and climate change pose significant threats to both plant populations and the associated indigenous knowledge. Encouragingly, local communities employ traditional sustainable harvesting practices, such as collecting leaves, fruits, or bark rather than uprooting whole plants. These practices reflect a nuanced understanding of ecological balance, consistent with findings from East Africa, China, and Australia, where indigenous methods have successfully maintained plant populations while enabling continued use [29, 157, 177].

Medicinal plant use is deeply entwined with cultural identity and social cohesion. Elders, herbalists, and pastoralists act as custodians of this knowledge, transmitting it orally across generations. Many species hold symbolic or ritual significance, serving preventive, magico-religious, or socially important functions, highlighting the cultural embedding of ethnomedicinal knowledge. Similar patterns have been reported in Uganda, Cameroon, and India, where traditional practices integrate health, spirituality, and social values [42, 62, 130, 166, 167]. Moreover, marketable species such as Allium sativum and Balanites aegyptiaca provide economic incentives that support sustainable management and reinforce cultural relevance.

Hence, MPs in Sheikh District provide multidimensional benefits: they are essential for human health, enhance food security, promote environmental sustainability, and preserve cultural heritage. These interlinked functions underscore the need for integrated strategies combining conservation, sustainable utilization, and systematic documentation of ethnobotanical knowledge. Ensuring the continued availability of these plants and the preservation of indigenous knowledge will require collaborative efforts between local communities, policymakers, and researchers, reflecting approaches successfully applied in East Africa, South Asia, and beyond [76, 79, 119, 139, 140]. This study demonstrates that ethnomedicinal plants are not merely healthcare tools, but foundational components of ecological resilience, cultural identity, and community livelihoods.

Comparative analysis of medicinal plants from Sheikh district and other east African dryland areas

The Jaccard Similarity Index (JSI) was employed to compare medicinal plant species documented in Sheikh District with those reported from ecologically comparable areas in Eastern Ethiopia and neighboring dryland regions of Eastern Africa (Table 14). In response to the reviewer’s comment, the comparison has been refined to emphasize arid and semi-arid ecosystems and pastoral communities, while reducing emphasis on humid forest regions that are ecologically dissimilar.

High similarity values were recorded with geographically proximate and environmentally comparable dryland areas, including Babile (83.33%), Dire Dawa (65.71%), Jigjiga town (62.86%), Turkana (58.1%), Jigjiga district (58.33%), Dirre (49.12%), Marsabit (48.8%), Tabora region (46.94%), Raya Kobo (42.19%), and Melit area (42.00%) [32, 39, 47, 56, 57, 66, 75, 103, 106, 128]. These areas are largely characterized by semi-arid to arid climates and savannah vegetation, which likely account for the substantial overlap in medicinal plant composition. The relatively high similarity with Turkana and Marsabit is particularly notable, as these counties form part of the wider East African dryland pastoral belt and share comparable ecological conditions and plant adaptations.

Moderate similarity indices (20–40%) were observed in Yalo (39.73%), North-eastern Tanzania (37.25%), Wakiso (34.62%), Algoz area (29.11%), Randa (27.85%), and the Marakwet Community (27.78%) [39, 42, 48, 93, 102, 107, 120]. These findings indicate partial floristic overlap influenced by transitional ecological zones and differences in cultural practices. While certain savannah elements are shared, variation in ethnic composition, knowledge transmission systems, and therapeutic preferences likely contributes to the observed divergence.

Low similarity values (< 20%) were documented in Sheka (8.17%), Sekela (9.56%), Nyamira (9.84%), Fofa (10.98%), Menz Keya (11.54%), Andracha (11.48%), Ensaro (12.59–15.79%), Bita (12.60%), Mana Angetu (14.72%), Sodo (14.29%), and Yeki (14.02%) [24, 33–35, 44, 81, 87, 101, 146, 172]. Many of these areas are situated in humid highland or forested agro-ecological zones that differ substantially from the dryland ecology of Sheikh District. The limited overlap therefore reflects both ecological dissimilarity and differences in local ethnomedical traditions.

The strong similarity between Sheikh District and nearby dryland areas such as Dire Dawa, Jigjiga town, Jigjiga district, Babile, and Dirre [56, 57, 75, 103, 128] likely results from shared floristic zones, comparable rainfall regimes, and long-standing socio-cultural interactions that facilitate knowledge exchange. Likewise, the overlap observed with Turkana and Marsabit [39, 106] supports the broader pattern that arid and semi-arid environments promote reliance on drought-tolerant woody species and shrubs with multipurpose medicinal value.

The anomalously high JSI value reported for the Somali region (187.50%) [14] exceeds the theoretical maximum range of the Jaccard index (0–100%), indicating probable computational or reporting inconsistencies in the cited study. In the revised manuscript, this value is interpreted cautiously and not used as a principal benchmark for comparison.

Thus, the Jaccard similarity analysis demonstrates that Sheikh District shares the strongest medicinal plant affinities with geographically proximate and ecologically comparable dryland and savannah regions, while exhibiting limited overlap with humid forest ecosystems. These findings underscore the ecological specificity of ethnobotanical knowledge and highlight the importance of site-specific documentation and locally adapted conservation strategies for sustaining medicinal plant diversity and associated cultural heritage.

Comparison of medicinal plants using Rahman’s similarity index (RSI)

Rahman’s Similarity Index (RSI) was applied to evaluate similarities in medicinal plant species and their uses between Sheikh District and selected study areas in Eastern Ethiopia and neighboring regions (Table 15). In line with the reviewer’s recommendation, the comparison has been refined to emphasize ecologically comparable dryland and pastoral systems, while minimizing emphasis on humid forest ecosystems that differ substantially from the semi-arid environment of Sheikh District.

The RSI values ranged from 0.62% (Sekela) to 15.15% (Somali), demonstrating generally low to moderate similarity across regions and highlighting the localized nature of ethnomedicinal knowledge. Relatively higher RSI values were observed in ecologically and geographically proximate dryland areas, including Dire Dawa (12.50%), Jigjiga town (12.86%), Jigjiga district (11.43%), Babile (11.11%), Marsabit (11.84%), Turkana (10.00%), Dirre (8.65%), and Yalo (6.50%) [39, 56, 57, 75, 103, 106, 120, 128]. These findings suggest that shared semi-arid climatic conditions, similar vegetation types, and historical socio-cultural interactions contribute to comparable medicinal plant use patterns.

Notably, relatively high similarity with Turkana (10.00%) and Marsabit (11.84%) [39, 106] is consistent with their location within the East African arid and semi-arid lands (ASALs), where pastoralist communities rely heavily on drought-tolerant woody species and shrubs for healthcare. Comparable ecological pressures in these regions may encourage the selection of similar plant taxa and therapeutic applications.

Moderate RSI values (approximately 3–6%) were recorded in Samburu County (6.90%), Raya Kobo (5.36%), Yalo (6.50%), Melit area (4.55%), Tabora region (4.40%), North-eastern Tanzania (4.71%), Jabitehnan (4.76%), Dega Damot (4.26%), Jawi (4.27%), Eastern Uganda (3.85%), and the Maasai pastoral communities (3.65%) [32, 39, 42, 47, 48, 65, 66, 82, 95, 120, 205]. These values indicate partial overlap in both plant species and medicinal uses. While certain ecological similarities exist, variations in ethnic traditions, knowledge transmission systems, and disease treatment preferences likely limit stronger convergence.

Low RSI values (< 3%) were documented in several ecologically distinct or geographically distant areas, including Andracha (0.67%), Sekela (0.62%), Guraferda (0.91%), Fofa (1.52%), Meketewa (1.77%), Bita (1.92%), Sodo (2.13%), Sheka (2.40%), Quara (2.53%), Mana Angetu (3.24%), and Artuma Fursi (3.28%) [24, 33–35, 44, 80, 81, 87, 173, 189]. Many of these areas are characterized by humid highland or forest ecosystems that contrast sharply with the dryland ecology of Sheikh District. The limited similarity likely reflects differences in floristic composition as well as distinct cultural practices and ethnomedical traditions.

The comparatively higher RSI value reported for the Somali region (15.15%) [14] may reflect strong ecological and cultural continuity across Somali-inhabited territories, including shared pastoral livelihoods, mobility patterns, and long-standing exchange of traditional knowledge. Such interactions may facilitate not only the sharing of plant species but also the transmission of similar therapeutic applications.

Hence, the RSI analysis reinforces the ecological specificity of medicinal plant knowledge in Sheikh District. Greater similarity is observed with geographically proximate and environmentally comparable semi-arid and pastoral areas, whereas humid forest and highland regions exhibit minimal overlap. These findings underscore the importance of interpreting similarity indices within their ecological and cultural contexts.

The results further highlight the need for site-specific ethnobotanical documentation and locally tailored conservation strategies. Preservation of medicinal plant diversity must consider both ecological conditions and culturally embedded knowledge systems to ensure sustainable use and protection of traditional healthcare resources.

Limitations of the study

This study relied primarily on self-reported ethnomedicinal information obtained from local informants. Such data may be influenced by recall bias, personal experience, and culturally specific interpretations of illness and healing practices. In addition, seasonal constraints during fieldwork limited access to certain plant species, which may have resulted in the underrepresentation of taxa available only during particular seasons. To reduce these limitations, repeated field visits were conducted across different periods, multiple data collection approaches (e.g., semi-structured interviews, guided field walks, and group discussions) were triangulated, and information was cross-validated among a diverse group of informants.

Despite these precautions, the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. The results represent the ethnomedicinal knowledge of communities in Sheikh District and may not capture the full diversity of medicinal plant use across broader ecological or cultural landscapes. As noted in the comparative analysis, ecological variability strongly influences plant availability and use patterns; therefore, direct comparisons with humid or ecologically dissimilar regions should be approached carefully. Furthermore, the pharmacological efficacy and safety of the reported species were not experimentally verified in this study. Similarly, assessments of plant threats and conservation status were based largely on community perceptions rather than long-term ecological monitoring or quantitative population studies.

These limitations are common in ethnobotanical research, where similarity indices and self-reported use reports provide culturally meaningful insights but do not constitute definitive evidence of therapeutic effectiveness or conservation risk.

Nevertheless, the documented information remains valuable. It identifies culturally significant medicinal species, illustrates patterns of knowledge transmission, and establishes a baseline for future pharmacological validation, ecological assessment, and conservation planning. By systematically recording local plant use practices within their ecological context, this study contributes to the preservation of traditional knowledge and provides a foundation for sustainable resource management and community-centered healthcare strategies.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study highlights that Sheikh District harbors rich ethnomedicinal knowledge, with 54 medicinal plant species documented for treating a diverse array of human ailments. These plants serve multidimensional roles, contributing not only to healthcare but also to food security, cultural identity, and local livelihoods. Species with high fidelity levels, such as Vachellia bussei, Balanites aegyptiaca, and Melia azedarach, emerged as particularly significant, emphasizing their potential for pharmacological investigation and conservation prioritization. The findings also underscore that indigenous knowledge is primarily transmitted orally, making it vulnerable to modernization, environmental pressures, and the decline of traditional healers.

Preserving this knowledge requires community-based strategies, including cultivating high-demand species to reduce pressure on wild populations, promoting sustainable harvesting practices, and protecting natural habitats to maintain ecological balance. Participatory documentation initiatives that actively engage elders, traditional healers, and youth are also critical for ensuring intergenerational knowledge transfer and cultural continuity. Policy measures are needed to formally recognize the importance of medicinal plants for healthcare and biodiversity conservation. Local and national strategies should encourage sustainable trade, protect indigenous intellectual property rights, and integrate traditional knowledge into public health and environmental programs.

Future research should prioritize pharmacological and phytochemical investigations of high-fidelity species to validate their therapeutic properties and explore novel bioactive compounds. Additionally, social-ecological studies on knowledge erosion, market dynamics, and climate change impacts will strengthen the resilience and sustainability of ethnomedicinal systems in Sheikh District and similar regions. By combining conservation, scientific validation, and supportive policy frameworks, Sheikh District’s medicinal plant heritage can continue to support human health, ecological sustainability, cultural preservation, and local livelihoods, ensuring that these resources remain accessible and valued for generations to come.
