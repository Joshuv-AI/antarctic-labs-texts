Human papillomavirus-encoded microRNAs: key regulators in cervical cancer development
Authors: Sanae Bencheikh, Hajar Lemriss, Amal Souiri, Jihane Akachar, Khalid Laarej, Rabii Ameziane El Hassani, Sanaâ Lemriss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman papillomaviruses (HPVs) cause diverse cutaneous and mucosal diseases, with several genotypes strongly associated with cervical cancer. Beyond the well-established role of cellular microRNAs (miRNAs) in gene regulation, increasing evidence shows that HPV also encodes its own viral miRNAs (v-miRNAs). These v-miRNAs modulate both viral and host gene expression, influencing key pathways involved in oncogenesis, including cell cycle control, apoptosis, immune evasion, and epithelial – mesenchymal transition. By shaping these regulatory networks, HPV-derived miRNAs promote viral persistence and contribute to malignant transformation. Their stability and specificity also make them promising biomarkers for cervical cancer diagnosis and prognosis, although clinical translation remains challenging. This review provides an updated overview of HPV-encoded miRNAs, their validated molecular targets, and their roles in tumour development. It also highlights emerging therapeutic strategies and future perspectives for integrating miRNA-based approaches into precision oncology for HPV-related cervical cancer.KEYWORDS: HPV, HPV-encoded microRNAs, viral microRNAs, cervical cancer, oncogenesis, biomarkers

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains a major public health concern, particularly among women in developing countries, where healthcare infrastructure often lags behind. After lung, colorectal, and breast cancers, cervical cancer ranks fourth among cancers in women worldwide [1]. Approximately 600.000 new cases are estimated to be diagnosed annually, resulting in 340.000 deaths worldwide [1]. In countries with advanced healthcare systems, cervical cancer mortality has decreased significantly, mostly because to efficient screening and vaccination programmes. Nevertheless, its incidence remains distressingly high in many developing countries [2].

In Morocco, Cervical cancer is the second most common cancer among women. The age-standardized incidence rate (ASR) is estimated at 15 per 100,000 women in Casablanca and 13 per 100,000 in Rabat figures that is markedly high compared to other North African nations like Egypt, where the ASR is only 2.3 per 100,000 women [3].

The causal link between high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) infection and cervical cancer has been well-established through various epidemiological and functional studies [4].

HPV and virus-encoded miRnas

Genomic characterization of HPV

HPVs are non-enveloped, double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) viruses that belong to the Papillomaviridae family [5,6]. Human papillomavirus (HPV) is the most prevalent oncogenic virus associated with infection-related carcinogenesis [7], with its highest prevalence occurring in early adulthood [8]. Papillomaviruses have been extensively identified in both humans and animals [9]. To date, over 280 types have been documented across vertebrate species, with more than 200 strains known to infect humans [10]. The main factor used for classification of HPV types is their oncogenic potential.

Low-risk human papillomavirus (HPV-LR), such as HPV 6 and 11, are commonly associated with benign lesions like anogenital warts, which exhibit minimal risk of malignant transformation [11]. In contrast, HR-HPV, notably types 16 and 18, are closely associated to cervical intraepithelial neoplasia and cervical cancer [9]. Although nearly half of all HPV infections are spontaneously cleared within one-year, HR-HPV types can persist over time, and chronic infections significantly increase the risk of progressing to invasive cervical carcinoma.

HPV enters epithelial cells by binding to receptors on the surface of keratinocytes, followed by intracellular transport of the viral particle [12]. Viral RNA splicing can be detected approximately 12 hours post infection, with robust viral replication occurring within 24 hours after viral capsid binding [13].

The HPV virions have a diameter of about 60 nm and are composed of an icosahedral capsid that contains 72 capsomeres and double-stranded DNA genome of about 8000 base pairs is reformed by this capsid [5,9]. The viral genome contains eight open reading frames (ORFs), is organized polycistronically, and produces viral mRNAs via alternative splicing mechanisms [14]. This circular dsDNA genome is organized into three main regions: (1) the early (E) region, which encodes proteins critical for viral replication and host cell transformation, including E1, E2, E4, E5, E6, and E7; and (2) the late (L) region, which encodes the structural capsid proteins L1 and L2; and (3) the upstream regulatory region (long control region), a noncoding region that regulates viral gene transcription and DNA replication [15]. E6 and E7, along with other early genes, play pivotal roles in viral genome replication, virion synthesis, and, critically, cellular transformation [16].

MiRnas encoded by virus

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are a class of small, non-coding RNA molecules that serve as key regulators of gene expression, influencing numerous cellular processes across diverse species [17]. The discovery of miRNAs marked a major insight in molecular biology by illuminating the complex mechanisms underlying post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression [18].

Initially, termed small temporal RNAs (stRNAs) because of their involvement in orchestrating the timing of developmental events, miRNAs were first identified in the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans in 1993 by Ambros and colleagues [19,20]. In order to guarantee their survival, reproduction, and spread, viruses have developed complex strategies. Among these, the production of virally encoded miRNAs has emerged as a key mechanism [21].

These miRNAs, characterized by their non-immunogenic properties [22], are capable of regulating gene expression within both the viral and host genomes [23]. This alteration promotes the long-term viability of infected cells and makes immune evasion easier [24]. The first identification of viral miRNAs was made in 2004 by Pfeffer et al., who successfully cloned miRNAs from Epstein – Barr virus (EBV)-infected cells [19,25]. It appears that viral miRNAs (v-miRNAs) play a significant role in the survival and proliferation of viruses by implementing various mechanisms of immune evasion [24]. Both v- miRNAs and host-derived miRNAs (host miRNAs) can influence the expression of various host- and virus-encoded genes [26]. To ensure their survival, viruses hijack the host cell’s biosynthetic machinery while simultaneously inhibiting key innate immune responses, including interferon signalling and apoptosis [25]. While larger viruses often rely on proteins to inactivate these defences, the compact coding capacity of many viruses makes miRNAs an efficient alternative for silencing specific host genes [18].

Multiple studies have confirmed that viruses actively encode their own miRNAs to modulate both their replication cycle and interactions with host cells [18,26]. The functional roles of these viral miRNAs highlight their importance in orchestrating viral persistence, replication, and immune evasion by finely tuning virus-host dynamics [27]. To date, more than 250 v-miRNAs have been identified, offering valuable insights into their biogenesis and functional roles [20]. While the functions of miRNAs in DNA viruses are well characterized, the capacity of RNA viruses to encode miRNAs remains less understood and requires further exploration [20]. The interplay between viruses and host cells involves both host-derived and virus-encoded miRNAs, contributing significantly to our understanding of gene regulation at the host-pathogen interface [26]. One of the primary functions of v-miRNAs is the regulation of viral gene expression, a mechanism especially prominent in viruses that undergo both latent and lytic phases during their replication cycle. During latency, the virus remains in a dormant state, with only a restricted subset of its genome being transcribed. While protein synthesis is largely suppressed, viral RNAs, including V-miRNAs and their precursors, continue to be actively expressed and detected [28].

HPV-encoded miRnas

Several studies have shown that certain viruses, including multiple genotypes of HPV, are capable of producing miRNA-like molecules [29]. These small regulatory RNAs are believed to play critical roles in the oncogenic processes associated with viral infections [30]. Given these observations, it is compelling to explore whether HPV genomes encode miRNAs with comparable functional properties.

The identification of putative HPV-associated microRNAs has relied on a combination of computational predictions and experimental investigations. One of the earliest bioinformatic studies proposed that HPV genomes could encode microRNAs, predicting three candidate miRNAs within the long control region (LCR) of HPV16 and five candidates within the LCR, as well as one candidate within the E5 gene, of HPV18 [31].

Subsequently, in 2013, Qian et al. reported one of the first experimental attempts to identify HPV-associated microRNAs using next-generation sequencing (SOLiD4 platform), followed by RT-qPCR and in situ hybridization. This study identified nine candidates small RNAs, with four of five selected candidates detected in HPV16-positive human tissues or epithelial cell lines [32]. In 2016, Virtanen et al. reported the detection of four candidate HPV16-associated miRNAs in cervical samples, including formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues and HPV16-positive cell lines, using real-time PCR; however, the low expression levels limited their potential utility as reliable biomarkers [33]. Notably, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples have been the most frequently used source for the detection of viral microRNAs [20]. Viral miRNAs have been identified in a variety of pathological specimens, including normal squamous epithelium, cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) grades 1 to 3, squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma in situ, adenocarcinoma, and HPV-positive cell lines [32].

Overall, the absence of validation by independent deep small RNA sequencing and Northern blot analysis necessitates cautious interpretation of these findings. In 2024, further studies reported the presence of two putative HPV16-associated microRNAs in HPV16-positive cervical cancer tissues and cell lines using RT-PCR, ChIP-seq, and RNA-seq – based approaches [34,35].

In parallel, an American study introduced a microRNA discovery strategy termed microRNA Discovery by forced Genomic Expression (miDGE), which identified papillomavirus-derived miRNAs from chaffinch leg lesions and reported highly probable miRNA candidates from human papillomaviruses HPV17, HPV37, and HPV41, with selected candidates validated by Northern blot analysis. While these results support the ability of certain cutaneous papillomaviruses to encode functional miRNAs, their relevance to high-risk mucosal HPVs remains unclear [29].

In addition, functional studies based on plasmid-mediated overexpression of a putative HPV16-derived microRNA, HPV16-miR-H1, in HPV-positive head and neck squamous cell carcinoma cell lines suggested oncogenic effects through modulation of the PTEN/AKT pathway; however, such approaches do not demonstrate endogenous miRNA expression during natural infection [36].

Several computational analyses, including comprehensive phylogenetic studies by Weng et al., further predicted numerous candidate pre-miRNAs across high-risk HPV genotypes, with HPV16 and HPV18 showing the highest number of predicted candidates. Despite these extensive predictions, the majority lack direct experimental validation [20].

Collectively, reported studies have described a limited number of putative HPV-associated microRNAs across different HPV genotypes. Although the detection of these small RNAs remains technically challenging, several investigations have reported candidate miRNAs derived from HPV, including five from HPV16 and single candidates from HPV38, HPV68, HPV45, HPV6, HPV17, HPV37, and HPV41) [20,32,33,35]. Notably, sequencing of HPV-infected cells revealed 45, 1203, and 6161 reads mapped to HPV16-miR-H1, HPV16-miR-H2, and HPV16-miR-H6, respectively, highlighting their presence and potential functional relevance (Table 2) [20,32,33,35].

Several studies have investigated the potential expression of HPV-encoded microRNAs; however, this issue remains highly controversial. Pfeffer et al. initially reported, based on computational analyses of the HPV18 genome, the theoretical possibility of viral miRNA expression. However, subsequent experimental investigations have failed to support this hypothesis [37]. Comprehensive analyses of microRNA expression in human cells during both latent and productive phases of the HPV31 replication cycle revealed the cloning and identification of more than 500 host-derived microRNAs, yet no HPV31-specific microRNA was detected [38]. Similarly, extensive small RNA cloning efforts in HPV16-positive CaSki cells did not identify any HPV16-derived microRNAs. These negative findings indicate that the integrated HPV16 genome in CaSki cells does not appear to produce viral miRNAs [39]. In parallel, application of the miRDeep2 pipeline to multiple small RNA sequencing libraries consistently identified hundreds of annotated host miRNAs per library, while no HPV-derived miRNA candidates were detected high-risk HPV types [29].

In contrast to other DNA viruses known to encode miRNAs [40], these findings suggest that high-risk HPVs are unlikely to express canonical viral miRNAs [29]. Taken together, these data highlight a discrepancy between computational predictions, RT-qPCR-based reports, and large-scale experimental studies, underscoring the need for further rigorous validation.

Gene targets and functional implications of HPV-Encoded miRnas

Theoretically, viral microRNAs (V-miRNAs) can influence host gene expression through at least three distinct mechanisms: 1-V-miRNA-induced degradation of host mRNAs, 2-V-miRNA-mediated translational repression of host mRNAs, and 3-host miRNA mimicry, where viral miRNAs (v-miRNAs) mimic host cellular miRNAs in order to regulate gene expression (Figure 2) [28].

Three distinct mechanisms regulate host gene expression through viral microRNAs.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are important modulators of gene expression that affect vital cellular functions as apoptosis, differentiation, and proliferation [41]. By binding to the 3’ untranslated regions (3’UTRs) of messenger RNAs, miRNAs can suppress gene expression [35]. This regulatory mechanism high-lights their significant role in oncogenesis and tumour progression [41].

In cervical cancer, HPV-encoded miRNAs have been shown to modulate host gene expression, contributing to cancer initiation and progression [42]. Notably, HPV-encoded miRNAs have been shown to downregulate the viral oncogenes E6 and E7 by targeting transcriptional enhancer factor-1 (TEF-1) [32,43], a process that may induce cell cycle arrest in HPV-infected cells. This regulatory mechanism allows for cell cycle normalization, thereby facilitating the persistence of HPV infection. Such indirect interactions suggest that HPV-derived miRNAs contribute to tumorigenesis by modulating critical signalling pathways implicated in host cell transformation [30]. To predict and analyse these gene targets, bioinformatics tools such as TargetScan, DAVID, and Gene Ontology (GO) are indispensable [20]. These tools enable the identification of miRNA – mRNA interactions, offering insights into specific genes targeted by viral miRNAs. Additionally, they help categorize the biological functions and pathways affected by these targets, including signalling cascades relevant to cancer development. By using such platforms, researchers can map the cellular pathways influenced by HPV-encoded miRNAs, identify potential oncogenic or tumour-suppressive effects, and explore therapeutic opportunities for targeting these pathways [44].

HPV-derived miRNA exhibits dual targeting capacity, influencing both viral and host genomic elements. For viral targets, HPV16-miR-H1 was found to target two sites in the E5 gene and two in the L1 gene, whereas HPV16-miR-H2 targets one site in the locus control region (LCR) and another in the L1 gene. With respect to human genomic targets, HPV16-miR-H1 was identified to target 137 genes, and HPV16-miR-H2 was found to target 176 genes (Figure 3) [32].

HPV16-derived miRNAs are key modulators of diverse cellular pathways, influencing critical processes such as nucleotide metabolism, cell migration, adhesion, muscle cell function, and the regulation of mitogen-activated protein MAP kinase signalling [20]. Among these, HPV16-miR-H1 has been specifically associated with the regulation of actin filament bundle assembly, transcriptional activity, receptor binding, ion transport, and cell fate determination [20,35]. Additionally, HPV16-encoded miRNAs influence immune system regulation. miR-H1-1 inhibits both T-cell activation and immune system development by targeting BCL11A, CHD7, ITGAM, RAG1, and TCEA1 [30,32]. Similarly, miR-H2-1 modulates immune responses by targeting key proteins involved in T-cell activation (PKNOX1, SP3, and XRCC4) and immune system development (JAK2, PKNOX1, SP3, XRCC4, and FOXP1) [30,32].

HPV16-miRNAs also contribute to cell differentiation and apoptosis. HPV16-miR-2 predominantly targets genes involved in muscle cell differentiation, cell death, and apoptotic pathways [20]. Moreover, HPV16-miR-H1 and HPV16-miR-H6 modulate gene expression extensively, with miR-H1 downregulating 31 genes and upregulating 25 (44.6%), while miR-H6 downregulates 30 genes and upregulates 36 (54.5%) [35]. These findings suggest a key regulatory role of these miRNAs in cervical carcinogenesis, particularly by upregulating the expression of host genes associated with oncogenic pathways [35]. Notably, four genes – FGFR2, ABCA12, CCDC7, and LOC105370532—were consistently upregulated by both HPV16-miR-H1 and HPV16-miR-H6, highlighting their potential role in HPV-mediated oncogenesis [35]. HPV16-miR-H6 exerts broad regulatory effects by targeting genes involved in cell adhesion, transcription, mRNA processing, BMP signalling, MAPK cascade, and cell differentiation [35]. It is also associated with defence response to viruses, innate immune response, cell division, proliferation, migration, and adhesion. Furthermore, it influences major oncogenic and immune-related pathways, including HPV infection, viral carcinogenesis, p53 signalling, RIG-I-like receptor signalling, and cell cycle regulation [35].

Among the genes upregulated by HPV16-miR-H6 through targeted enhancers are: HSP90AA1, LFNG, CACYBP, EEF1E1, GADD45G, DNAJB1, GFRA3, GCNA, CAPN12, DYNLT4, HSPA1A, HSPA7, SMIM26, CENPNP1, BMS1P4, LINC01629, SEPTIN4-AS1, and ARF4-AS1 [35]. miRNAs encoded by HPV16 have a crucial role in the development of malignant transformation as well as the course of viral infection [45]. These miRNAs target multiple signalling pathways, significantly impacting host cellular functions. Among them, HPV16-miR-1 has been extensively studied, with its gene targets identified across different regulatory networks (Figure 4). Notably, STAT5B, a predicted target of HPV16-miR-1, is involved in the Jak-STAT, chemokine, and ErbB signaling pathways [20]. These pathways are essential for immune response modulation, as cytokines exert their effects through the Jak-STAT cascade [46]. Moreover, HPV16-miR-1 has been implicated in cancer-associated signalling pathways, including the p53 pathway, through its regulation of STAT5B [27]. Recognized as the ‘guardian of the genome’, p53 is a central regulator of cell differentiation, DNA damage response, and cell cycle progression [47]. Additionally, HPV16-miR-1 influences other critical pathways, such as the TGF- receptor and MAPK signalling cascades [35]. In addition, HPV16-miR-2 has been shown to regulate key oncogenic signalling pathways (Figure 5), notably those associated with prostate cancer and various other malignancies. Specifically, it modulates the MAPK pathway by targeting PDGFRA [35]. Furthermore, HPV16-miR-H6 has been associated with alterations in the MAPK cascade and BMP signalling pathway (Figure 6) [35]. The ability of HPV16-miR-1 and HPV16-miR-2 to interfere with host defence mechanisms is underscored by their interactions with key molecules within these signalling pathways. This highlights their potential role in immune evasion and tumour progression, further emphasizing the oncogenic potential of HPV16-derived miRNAs [35].

Therapeutic potential of HPV-encoded miRnas

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) have emerged as promising therapeutic agents, either alone or in combination with conventional treatments. Their combination with chemotherapy has demonstrated enhanced antitumor efficacy, broader tumour targeting, and the potential to overcome drug resistance. Multiple studies have demonstrated that the combined use of miRNAs and chemotherapeutic agents produces synergistic effects, leading to significantly enhanced therapeutic outcomes compared to the use of either modality alone. While chemotherapy effectively slows cancer progression, resistance inevitably arises through multiple mechanisms, necessitating novel approaches to counteract this limitation [48].

The regulatory function of miRNAs in gene expression was first demonstrated by Brown et al., who employed miRNA-mediated repression to control transgene expression and reduce off-target effects following systemic administration of lentiviral vectors [49]. MiRNAs have become known as potent regulators of cellular gene expression since this crucial discovery. Furthermore, naturally occurring and differentially expressed miRNAs have been harnessed to influence viral replication cycles, thereby offering promising new strategies for the development of antiviral therapies [50]. In the field of oncolytic virotherapy and vaccine development, miRNA-regulated viral vectors have been engineered to control transgene expression in specific tissues, cell lineages, or differentiation states. The modification of tropism in replication-competent oncolytic viruses (OVs) has garnered significant attention, underscoring the therapeutic potential of miRNA-guided viral targeting strategies [51].

Both host- and virus-derived miRNAs have emerged as promising targets for antiviral therapies at three distinct regulatory levels: (i) the genomic level within the nucleus, where epigenetic modifications can influence both host and viral genomes; (ii) the precursor level, involving the targeting of pri-miRNAs and pre-miRNAs within the nucleus or cytoplasm; and (iii) the mature miRNA level, where functional miRNAs act in the cytoplasm to regulate gene expression [28]. Given that miRNA profiling in healthy and virus-infected cells does not directly reveal target interactions, therapeutic strategies targeting host or viral miRNAs may be more effective than focusing on unidentified mRNAs. However, considering that a single miRNA can regulate 100–200 transcripts, broad-spectrum miRNA inhibition may lead to unintended effects, making virus-specific miRNAs a more targeted and viable approach [28,52].

Their capacity to regulate microRNA expression and activity relies on RNA- and DNA-based molecules. According to their mechanisms of action, these approaches can be classified into four main categories:(i) miRNA mimetics, which restore depleted miRNA levels; (ii) antago-miRs, which specifically counteract individual miRNAs; (iii) target protectors, which prevent miRNA – mRNA interactions by shielding transcripts; and (iv) artificial miRNAs (amiRNAs), which are engineered for specific gene silencing [28,53,54]. MiRNA mimetics are designed to restore the function of downregulated or lost miRNAs and have already demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in cancer treatment [55]. An experimental study using miRNA inhibitors in HPV16-positive SiHa cells showed that inhibition of HPV16-miR-H1 and HPV16-miR-H6 altered gene expression, enhancer-associated histone modifications, and reduced cell migration, suggesting a potential modulatory role in oncogenic pathways, pending further validation [35].

In contrast, antagomiRs present a promising antiviral strategy because of their ability to selectively inhibit virus-encoded miRNAs. Antisense oligonucleotides exert their effects by either binding to miRNA precursors in the cytoplasm, thereby interfering with miRNA biogenesis, or by base-pairing with mature miRNAs to reduce their functional availability. The therapeutic potential of antago-miRs a class of chemically modified antisense molecules has been validated in several preclinical models, including murine studies. Antago-miRs directed against viral miRNAs involved in latency regulation have the potential to enhance antiviral therapy by decreasing viral persistence and promoting immune recognition of viral antigens [56]. Inhibition of HBV-miR-2 using antagomirs suppressed tumour-associated gene programmes and reduced hepatocellular carcinoma progression in mouse models, as shown by transcriptomic analyses highlighting decreased angiogenesis, cell migration, and proliferation. These findings suggest that targeting viral miRNAs may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for HBV-associated disease, pending further validation [57].

Another promising strategy involves ‘target protectors’, which are designed to compete with miRNAs for specific binding sites on mRNAs. By selectively blocking the interaction between a miRNA and a single target transcript, while preserving its interaction with others, target protectors offer a more precise approach to miRNA-based interventions. Initially applied in zebrafish to study gene regulation, they are now being explored as antiviral agentparticularly in cases where host miRNAs support viral replication, such as hsa-miR-122 in hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection [58].

Additionally, artificial miRNAs (amiRNAs), engineered from precursor templates and delivered into cells via vectors, have shown effective gene-silencing capabilities in experimental human cell line studies, further expanding the landscape of miRNA-based therapeutics. As antiviral agents, amiRNAs have been successfully employed to induce virus resistance in plants, suggesting potential applications in human virology. Theoretically, a miRNA can be designed for any viral transcript, but their efficacy requires careful optimization to ensure proper processing and specificity. Advances in vector design, incorporating modifications to natural miRNA precursor sequences, continue to enhance a miRNA-based therapeutic strategies [59].

Overall, miRNA-based therapeutics represent a rapidly advancing frontier with significant potential in both cancer treatment and antiviral strategies. By leveraging their ability to regulate key cellular and viral processes, these strategies offer innovative solutions for combating cancer, overcoming drug resistance, and developing targeted antiviral interventions [48]. Future research should focus on optimizing miRNA delivery systems, minimizing off-target effects, and expanding clinical applications to harness the full therapeutic potential of miRNAs [27]. Overall, the limited number of validated studies highlights a significant gap in our understanding of HPV-miRNAs in cervical cancer, underscoring the need for further experimental investigation.

Conclusion and future perspective

HPV-encoded microRNAs are key regulators of viral replication, pathogenicity, and the modulation of host immune responses. In cervical cancer, these viral miRNAs influence key biological processes and signalling pathways involved in tumour progression, making them promising therapeutic targets. Furthermore, HPV-miRNAs have shown promise as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers and have become useful instruments for evaluating HPV infectivity and replication status. A deeper understanding of their mechanisms of action could enable their use in the early diagnosis of HPV-associated cancers. However, it is essential to validate findings from in silico analyses and bioinformatics approaches through comprehensive experimental studies to confirm their clinical relevance.
