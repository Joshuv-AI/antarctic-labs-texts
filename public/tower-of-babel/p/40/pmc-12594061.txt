Comments on study titled: “Exploring adjunctive continuous theta burst stimulation for treatmentresistant auditory hallucinations in schizophrenia: Insights from a case series”
Authors: Yukta Tayal, Sristi Lakshmi, Sai K. Tikka
Published: 2025

Dear Sir,

There is an ongoing quest for promoting repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) as a potential option for treating auditory verbal hallucinations (AVH) in patients with schizophrenia. The recent most meta-analysis by Ye et al. (2024)[1] that report “no advantage” of adjuvant continuous theta-burst stimulation (cTBS) came as a roadblock for this quest. However, the case-series by Mukherjee et al. (2025),[2] that report significant improvement in auditory hallucinations in treatment resistant schizophrenia cases offers a renewed hope. However, we would like to comment on some critical aspects of this case series.

cTBS was considered while they were treated with clozapine in all the three cases. The case vignettes give reader a sense that the cases were not only “treatment-resistant,” but also “clozapine-resistant” (this term gets reflected in the keywords list too). However, “clozapine-resistance,” which is defined by failure of “an adequate trial of clozapine is in turn defined by plasma concentrations of more than 350 ng/ml, a minimum treatment duration of 8–12 weeks,”[3] does not seem to have been fulfilled. Plasma concentrations are not reported in any of the cases. While the authors do not report duration of treatment with clozapine in two cases, it is only 20 days in the third. We wonder if they could be best described as “partial responders to clozapine.”[4] In fact, as the dosage and duration of antipsychotics used earlier (also their names in case-3) are not reported, it is even difficult to ascertain the “treatment-resistant” status of the three cases. The case narrative would benefit from more thorough documentation of prior and the current medication regimens.

Authors state that no adverse effects occurred in any of the three cases. While cTBS is generally well tolerated, serious adverse events such as seizures have been reported earlier, even from India.[5] With the fact that clozapine reduces the seizure threshold, making the specific mention of seizures becomes important for this case-series. In fact, we would like to suggest the authors to report the tool/checklist they used to assess the adverse effects, as safety reporting is paramount for off-label use of treatment procedures. We also wonder if a baseline electroencephalography is essential before a trial of rTMS in clozapine treated cases.

A couple of other aspects also need further clarification. One, justification for not increasing the dose of clozapine further than 350 mg/day despite “some response” in cases 1 and 2, rationale for opting electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) augmentation in case 3 before the trial of cTBS unlike cases 1 and 2. Lack of clarification on these aspects points towards heterogeneity in the practice standards used in choosing cTBS in these cases.

In conclusion, we commend the authors for exploring a novel intervention in a clinically challenging population.
