The (in)Significance of Establishing Accurate Extracellular Vesicle Nomenclature
Authors: Sunyoung Ham, Luize G Lima, Andreas Möller
Published: 2025

The Importance of Extracellular Vesicle Nomenclature Standardisation

For the past decade, the field of extracellular vesicle (EV) research has vastly expanded (Bazzan et al. 2021). Our knowledge of EV biogenesis, their various roles in intercellular communication, and significant contributions to disease initiation and regulation has quickly developed (Möller and Lobb 2020, Kalluri and LeBleu 2020). The burgeoning of EV research is notable in the rapid parallel advancement of appropriate, cutting‐edge isolation and analysis techniques (Qiu et al. 2023, De Sousa et al. 2023). Whilst there is a consensus amongst the field on certain aspects of EV function and biology, one crucial, trending topic of controversy is the significance of EV nomenclature.

Accurate and appropriate naming is paramount within and outside the scientific community for several reasons. Firstly, researchers must be able to refer to the same entity properly to ensure that the same phenomenon is evaluated in their specific fields, as well as across fields, to allow legitimate comparisons and improvement. Iterative publications of MISEV guidelines and similar initiatives over the years have enabled significant progress (Welsh et al. 2024). Within the past decade, researchers used over 25 names for EVs, often describing the same entity across different diseases or source materials, with different labels (Choi et al. 2015). Precise designation of EVs is not only a matter of scientific uniformity, but also of scientific integrity. If research groups isolate the same EV but use different terms for it, determining whether the findings are providing deeper insights into a certain topic or contradicting each other can be difficult. In 2023, an average of ∼170 publications per week were deposited with PubMed in the EV field, increasing to about 195 in 2024, a trend that is likely to continue. Thus, it is possible that repeated work has been published without cross‐referencing, and that this happens not by ignorance or deceit, but by a difficulty of finding related studies due to discrepancies in EV naming. The nomenclature of EVs was clearly and unambiguously defined in MISEV2018 (Théry et al. 2018) and more recently in MISEV2023 (Welsh et al. 2024). The MISEV guidelines, not only for researchers in the EV field but across disciplines, provide a unified and comparable platform.

Using accurate and specific EV terms is also important for early‐career researchers and those new to the field. As numerous publications show, EVs obtained by current experimental methods are not EVs generated by a single mechanism, but rather a mixture of EVs secreted by multiple mechanisms (Di Bella 2022). Hence, a precise understanding of the EV source material used helps to design appropriate experiments and conduct accurate research. MISEV2023 provides an appropriate framework by defining the terms for EV subpopulations and the requirements for providing basic but sufficient information on all aspects, including source material, enrichment of EVs and characterisation undertaken (Welsh et al. 2024).

The Wide Popularity of the Term ‘Exosomes’

Despite the guidelines proposed, the term ‘exosome’ is often used by academic scientists, those in the industry and the public. As of September 2025, there are 493 clinical trials registered at the online database ClinicalTrials.gov using the term ‘exosomes’, and 309 trials using the term ‘extracellular vesicles’ (ClinicalTrials.gov 2024). Similarly, more than 70 companies are developing products in the EV field, and their marketing strategies and promotions often highlight that their products contain ‘exosomes’. Additionally, this terminology is commonly used in their pipeline and investor information. Therefore, the question is why the term ‘exosome’ is still, more than 5 years after MISEV2018, so vastly popular (Figure 1).

The first evidence of the existence of EVs emerged as early as the 1940s, when Chargaff and West observed that subcellular fractions of human plasma contained a precipitable factor that facilitated the generation of thrombin (West et al. 1946). In 1967, with the emergence of more powerful electron microscopy techniques, Wolf was able to demonstrate that this factor, obtained from ultracentrifugation of platelet‐free plasma, consisted of small vesicles derived from the cell membrane of activated platelets, which he described as ‘platelet dust’ (Wolf 1967). However, it was not until the 1980s that a more specific understanding of EV research started off. This initial rise in interest in EV biology coincided with the publication of pioneering papers that began to apply the term ‘exosomes’ to denominate the vesicular identities they were describing. Trams and colleagues first mentioned it in 1981, stating that ‘exfoliated membrane vesicles may serve a physiologic function; it is proposed that they be referred to as exosomes’ (Trams et al. 1981). Few years later, the term ‘exosomes’ was used to identify intraluminal vesicles released into the extracellular environment through the fusion of multivesicular bodies (MVB) with the plasma membrane (West et al. 1946), a mechanism that had been previously described by two seminal papers in the EV field (Trams et al. 1981, Johnstone et al. 1987). From this point on, the term ‘exosome’ became popular, and these initial studies were followed by a vast amount of work on ‘exosomes’ attempting to convince scientists of the existence and relevance of these nanosized vesicles.

Tragically, Dr. Eberhard Trams, the person who originally coined the name ‘exosomes’ to identify cell‐derived exfoliated vesicles, which could be comparable to the nowadays so called ‘plasma membrane‐derived EVs’ (Trams et al. 1981), was killed in a plane crash just 1 year after his publication, cutting short his pioneering work on EVs (Macdonald and Salem Jr. 2023). On the other hand, Dr. Rose Johnstone, who used the term to specifically refer to MVB‐derived vesicles, published another dozen papers, consolidating herself as the person known for exosomes’ discovery. It is therefore reasonable to speculate that these circumstances strongly contributed to the wide association of the name ‘exosome’ to endocytic vesicles. Semantically, however, the prefix ‘exo‐’ is a root derived from Greek that means ‘outside’ or ‘external’, while the noun suffix ‘‐some’ can be described as ‘denoting a portion of a body, especially a particle of a cell’ (Oxford English Dictionary 2025). In the context of cell‐derived particles, the name ‘exosome’ could therefore be interpreted as particles outside a cell, and therefore used as a synonym for the term ‘extracellular vesicles’ (EVs), defined by MISEV2023 as ‘particles that are released from cells, are delimited by a lipid bilayer, and cannot replicate on their own (i.e., do not contain a functional nucleus)’ (Théry et al. 2018).

Although we have now defined exosomes as a subtype of EVs, more specifically, small EVs (sEVs) originating from the endosomal system, in truth, most publications and commercial entities using the ‘exosome’ term have not demonstrated that these are indeed derived from MVBs. Moreover, the measured diameter of a given particle is related to the specific characterisation method applied, with different techniques such as TRPS, NTA and DLS usually providing divergent measurements for the same sample (Varenne et al. 2016). Also, there is no clear consensus on size cut‐offs to distinguish small from large EVs, and commonly used enrichment methodologies, including ultracentrifugation and size exclusion chromatography, are unable to isolate restricted particle size ranges (Visan et al. 2022). Thus, using operational terms such as small and large to inconsistently define a population of EVs is limited, and, more importantly, publications that used the term ‘exosomes’ should presumably be regarded as general EV papers.

Finally, a large proportion of academics, industry and interested members of the public use the term ‘exosomes’ to generally identify EVs, whether that be for name recognition, traditional or simplistic reasons. Interestingly, artificial intelligence (AI) states to the question ‘What does EV mean?’ that, although ‘EV can refer to several different things depending on the context’, the most common meaning is always identified as ‘Electric Vehicle’, which is also observed when one scans the internet for the same question using popular search engines such as Google (GPT‐4o mini 2025). If AI reflects popular results, the abbreviated form EV may not be an attractive marketing term for the extracellular vesicle industry to reach the general public. Even a refined question such as ‘What does EV mean in the scientific context?’ provides the answer ‘EV often stands for electron Volt’ (GPT‐4o mini 2025), indicating that the term EV, although accepted by our selective community, is still unfamiliar to the lay audience and could also be misleading to other broad scientific groups.

What is next?

For the reasons outlined above, authors may choose to use the term ‘EV’ or ‘exosome’ in their work. Besides the thought and effort put in by the EV community to unify the EV terminology (Witwer and Théry 2019), these terms are still used interchangeably (Figure 1). In addition, it would be hard to judge the research quality just based on the terminology used. To develop the EV field, we believe that it is more advantageous to focus more on the characteristics of the isolated particles than on the name.

So how could the nomenclature of EVs evolve from here? More recently, technologies designed for the characterisation of single EVs have advanced, mostly using flow cytometry‐ and microscopy‐based approaches (Görgens et al. 2019, McNamara et al. 2022). The speed and accuracy with which we can comprehensively evaluate the heterogeneity of the isolated EVs was unthinkable a few years ago and subsequently restricted to very few groups with access to highly specific technologies and skill sets, as well as to significant amounts of money. The current analysis of the heterogeneity of EV preparations, however, is still not capable of attributing specific biological, physiological or physical phenotypes to EV types, which limits establishing a specific nomenclature.

The next logical step in this technical evolution is single EV sorting approaches, with pilot technology platforms exploring this area already. The sorting, like the current single EV evaluation, will be based on specific characteristics, like the presence or absence of any protein, lipid, nucleic acid and/or metabolite capable of being molecularly tagged. So how might this additional information impact on how we name the EVs?

Turning to the field of immunology for guidance, we might discover parallels to our EV naming conundrum. Historically, T and B cells were named based on the lymphoid organs in which they were first discovered. When the function of the thymus gland was discovered through thymectomy experiments in mice, immunologists described two types of cells: thymus‐derived lymphocytes and non‐thymus‐derived, antibody‐producing lymphocytes (Mitchell and Miller 1968, Miller and Mitchell 1967). In parallel, studies in chicken models demonstrated that the bursa of Fabricius, an organ analogous to the thymus present in birds, was involved in antibody production (Dent and Good 1965). At that time, the cells were then named T cells and B cells, as a short form of Thymus‐derived and Bursa‐derived cells, respectively. The nomenclature remained, but these cell types eventually branched into many subsets as different marker proteins for T and B cell subsets were described with knowledge and technical advances (Springer 1990). This coincided, and was based upon significant progress in the technology for analysing cells. In the late 1960s, first impulse‐cytophotometers were commercialised (Picot et al. 2012), and fluorescence‐activated cell sorting, which purified a subpopulation of cells, was developed in the 1970s (Picot et al. 2012). In 1979, categorising the T cell population into CD4‐positive and CD4‐negative T cells to reflect the presence of the CD4 subsequently developed into the definition of two functionally different T cell types, CD4+/CD8− T cells and CD4−/CD8+ T cells (Springer 1990, Reinherz et al. 1979). These T cells are also commonly named according to their function, with CD4+ T cells also known as T helper cells, which are involved in helper functions, and CD8+ T cells known as cytotoxic T cells, given their role in cell‐killing activities. Nowadays, these T cell populations are further categorised by expression of different surface proteins and transcription factors (Figure 2A). The increasing understanding of immune cell function and biology over the past decades has resulted in current publications readily using 5 or more identifying markers to specify the accurate cell lineage identity (Figure 2A). In contrast, newspapers or similar lay publications usually apply generic terms such as ‘T cells’ or ‘immune cells’ (Cox 2022).

Overview of T cell terminology as an analogy for a potential extracellular vesicle nomenclature. (A) Schematic of diverse T cell subsets. Lymphoid progenitor cells develop from hematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow. These lymphoid progenitors migrate to the thymus and subsequently develop to thymocytes. When these cells recognize either the self‐MHC class II or the self‐MHC class I, they undergo T cell maturation to either CD4+ or CD8+ cells, respectively. At this stage, the simple separation of T cells into these two cell subsets is sufficient to determine cell populations with different functions. However, depending on the type of immune response triggered by a given agent, CD4+ helper T cells may further polarise into numerous subpopulation, including Th1, Th2 or Th17, while CD8+ cytotoxic T cells polarise into Tc1, Tc2 or Tc17 and others. This process results in a classification of T cells by phenotyping of multiple antigen markers and transcription factors. (B) Schematic of EV secretion and separation based on protein and lipid abundance. Cells release EVs through several processes. Plasma membrane‐derived vesicles are secreted by membrane budding due to cytoskeleton changes near the plasma membrane. MVB‐derived vesicles are generated by fusion of the MVB with the cell surface and subsequent release of heterogeneous populations of vesicles into the extracellular space. While these various secretion pathways are being elucidated, phenotypical studies on single EVs are underway to determine the particular molecular contents of EVs (including lipids and protein markers). A nomenclature of the presence or absence, in this example of a protein A, the relative abundance of a protein B and the presence or absence of a specific lipid might be used to relate a specific EV phenotype to functional roles or their bioactivity.

Translating immune cell terminology to heterogeneous EV populations may be key in ensuring widespread uniformity in EV nomenclature. Like T cells, which can be identified by markers, EVs will eventually be categorised by their molecular content as well as biological function. We envision an evolution of a nomenclature consensus for EVs based on the presence or absence of certain proteins, lipids, or other features (Figure 2B). In addition, EVs with a certain marker composition might be attributed to specific function(s). With an improved understanding of the biology and function of EVs, significant technical advances and breakthroughs have been achieved. The field is already separating bulk EV populations, for example, by immune‐ or affinity‐precipitation, and describing vastly different EV content and/or function specific to those subpopulations (Hosseinkhani et al. 2020, Kowal et al. 2016, Nakai et al. 2016).

Therefore, we should boldly embark on implementing a description of the type(s) of EVs that we are working with, ascribing functional characteristics specific to a selected EV subpopulation. The current, rapid technological evolution will soon allow us to overcome the above‐mentioned current problems, and by initiating a wider discussion on the evolution and the significance of accurate EV nomenclatures, will allow our field, and our publications, to advance with hugely improved reproducibility, integrity and transparency.
