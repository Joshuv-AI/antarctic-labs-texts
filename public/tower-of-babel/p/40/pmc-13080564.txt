Ventricular Arrhythmias Associated With Long QT Syndrome Type 2 and Anomalous Right Coronary Artery
Authors: Courtney Kenyon, Eiad Habib, Ramzi Ibrahim, Mahmoud Abdelnabi, Christopher Kanaan, Mayank Sardana, Elizabeth Chan

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with congenital long QT syndrome (LQTS) are at increased risk for sudden cardiac death.Case SummaryWe present the case of a young woman with a history of syncopal episodes triggered by emotional stress and menstruation who experienced recurrent ventricular arrhythmias requiring implantable cardioverter-defibrillator shocks. She was diagnosed with LQTS type 2 and found to have an anomalous right coronary artery from the left coronary cusp with malignant features.DiscussionThis clinical case highlights the complex interplay between LQTS type 2, sex hormonal influences, and an anomalous right coronary artery in a young female patient with recurrent ventricular arrhythmias.

History of Presentation

Our patient is a 38-year-old woman who presented to the hospital for a 24-hour history of worsening shortness of breath, palpitations, and lightheadedness occurring at rest and with minimal exertion. The patient had reported feeling extremely unwell due to these symptoms, and shortly thereafter experienced 2 implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) shocks that prompted presentation to the emergency department. On physical examination, she was in no acute distress. Vital signs were notable for blood pressure 101/56 mm Hg, heart rate 82 beats/min, oxygen saturation 100% on room air, and temperature 36.3 °C. Cardiovascular examination revealed a regular rate and rhythm, and a grade 1/6 systolic murmur was heard loudest at the apex in the fifth intercostal space. Past Medical History

The patient reported a longstanding history of syncopal episodes dating back to her teenage years, initially labeled as seizures. These episodes were particularly prevalent during menstruation, pregnancy, and lactation. In addition, she recalled experiencing syncopal episodes whenever she was startled, such as when her morning alarm would go off. Approximately 10 years before presentation, she was diagnosed with long QT syndrome (LQTS) and underwent ICD implantation. She did not have any genetic testing performed. She had experienced many ICD shocks in the past. Years ago, she underwent placement of a progesterone intrauterine device (IUD), after which she had a period of 6 years without any ICD shocks. She reported no known family history of cardiac arrest or seizures. Her brother had died at the age of 26 years, presumably because of drug and alcohol use, although he did have recurrent syncopal spells as a child. Her current medication regimen included nadolol 40 mg once daily, and she had a progesterone IUD that was recently placed 3 weeks earlier, after her prior one expired a year ago.

Differential Diagnosis

Given the patient's history of LQTS presenting with ICD shocks, broad differential diagnosis included ventricular tachycardia (VT)/ventricular fibrillation related to prolonged QT interval and torsades de pointes, ischemia, electrolyte imbalances, drug toxicity, or infection.

Investigations

Lab tests revealed a hemoglobin level of 14.5 g/dL (normal 11.6-15 g/dL), a white blood cell count of 4 × 109/L (normal 3.4-9.6 × 109/L), creatinine 0.6 mg/dL (normal 0.59-1.04 mg/dL), sodium 133 mmol/L (normal, 135-145 mmol/L), potassium 3.8 mmol/L (normal 3.6-5.2 mmol/L), magnesium 1.7 mg/dL (normal 1.7-2.3 mg/dL), troponin <6 ng/L (normal ≤10 ng/L), N-terminal pro–brain natriuretic peptide 2938 pg/mL (normal <160 pg/mL), and thyroid-stimulating hormone 1.95 mIU/L (normal 0.3-4.2 mIU/L). A 12-lead electrocardiogram revealed atrial-paced rhythm at 75 beats/min in a pattern of bigeminy and a prolonged QTc interval of 540 milliseconds with broad-based T waves (Figure 1). A transthoracic echocardiogram showed normal left ventricular systolic function with an ejection fraction of 58% without regional wall motion abnormalities. Management

In the acute setting, the patient received intravenous potassium and magnesium replacement. Intentional atrial pacing was increased from 75 to 90 beats/min. Repeat electrocardiography showed improved QTc duration at a higher pacing rate (Figure 2). Her nadolol was switched to propranolol 40 mg 3 times daily to provide an additional QT shortening effect by attenuation of the late sodium current, Propranolol was chosen over nadolol because of its additional late sodium current–blocking and membrane-stabilizing properties, which can provide greater QT shortening and antiarrhythmic benefit in LQTS type 2 (LQT2). In addition, the patient was started on mexiletine 200 mg 3 times daily because of breakthrough cardiac events despite β-blocker therapy. A coronary angiogram was performed to rule out ischemic heart disease, which showed no obstructive coronary artery disease but did reveal an anomalous right coronary artery (RCA) from the left coronary cusp with an interarterial course and a slit-like orifice. A coronary computed tomography angiogram was performed for further evaluation, confirming the anomalous aortic origin of the RCA off the left sinus of Valsalva with an interarterial course. There was an acute angle takeoff, a slit-like ostium, proximal elliptical-shaped narrowing (3 × 1 mm, compared with 3 × 2 mm distally), and a 16-mm intramural segment, suggestive of higher-risk anatomical features for potential ischemia (Figures 3 and 4). A functional stress was not performed acutely, given that the patient had electrical instability related to LQT2. A nuclear stress test was planned as an outpatient to assess for a separate ischemic etiology for the ventricular arrhythmias. Cardiothoracic surgery reviewed the patient's case. With ventricular arrhythmia and a subsequent readmission for a chest pain episode, they cardiothoracic surgery team the patient high risk and recommended to proceed with surgical correction of the anomalous coronary artery without functional testing. The patient subsequently underwent successful unroofing of the anomalous RCA, tricuspid valve repair, and patent foramen ovale closure. Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient did very well postoperatively. Genetic testing revealed a positive result for congenital LQT2, caused by a KCNH2 mutation c.1745G>C, R582P. Her children also underwent genetic testing and were found to have the same mutation; they are currently undergoing further cardiac evaluation. The patient also underwent successful left cardiac sympathetic denervation surgery shortly thereafter, and mexiletine was discontinued. Since the procedure, she has not experienced any recurrent VT/ventricular fibrillation episodes for 21 months.

Discussion

This clinical case highlights a complex interplay with LQT2, anomalous RCA off left sinus of Valsalva, and sex hormonal influences in a young female with recurrent ventricular arrhythmias. Both LQT2 and anomalous RCA are associated with increased risk of sudden cardiac death via ventricular arrhythmias, but the underlying mechanisms, triggers, and management strategies differ. Anomalous RCA-related arrhythmias are typically ischemic and exertion related.1 In contrast, LQT2 arrhythmias are due to channelopathy-induced repolarization abnormalities, with genotype-specific triggers such as emotional stress and auditory stimuli.2 In our case, it was challenging to discern which of the 2 conditions was the trigger for the patient's recurrent VT. Hence, both LQT2 and anomalous RCA treatment were pursued with shared patient decision-making, yielding a good clinical outcome.

Prior studies have shown that variations in endogenous sex hormones affect ventricular arrhythmia risk in female patients with LQT2 during their lifetime. In female patients with LQT2 assessed throughout their menstrual cycles, higher progesterone levels and progesterone-to-estradiol levels were associated with shorter QTc intervals, not seen in healthy control participants.3 During the postpartum period, there is also low progesterone to estrogen levels in addition to oxytocin and prolactin, which prolong ventricular repolarization. As a result, the postpartum period increases susceptibility to ventricular arrhythmias in LQT2.3 Lastly, menopause is associated with a marked increase in cardiac events in LQT2, independent of oral estrogen therapy. It has been proposed that the loss of progesterone and of androgen levels (testosterone and dihydrotestosterone) during and after menopause accounts for this arrhythmia vulnerability.4

Exogenous sex hormones in oral contraceptives also affect ventricular arrhythmia or syncope risk in patients with LQT and especially in LQT2 genotype. In a large registry of patients with LQTS, progestin-only oral contraceptive demonstrated a 2.8-fold increased risk of cardiac events of syncope, aborted cardiac arrest, and sudden cardiac death.5 Estrogen-only and combined estrogen/progestin contraceptives were not associated with a statistically significant increased risk of cardiac events in LQTS. Cardiac event risk was significantly attenuated with concomitant β-blocker therapy with progestin-only oral contraceptive in LQTS. Of all the LQT genotypes, patients with LQT2 demonstrated the highest risk of cardiac events with progestin-only contraceptive; however, β-blocker exerted a protective effect in these patients with LQT2.

An additional future study of optimal contraceptive selection and patients with LQT2 is needed, recognizing prior observations that fourth-generation antiandrogenic progestins are associated with modestly longer QT intervals compared with first- and second-generation progestins with shorter QT intervals.6

Our patient with LQT2 demonstrated a period of 6 years without ventricular arrhythmias with Mirena IUD placement and β-blocker. During the subsequent period which her IUD had expired, she had 3 ICD shocks. She had a new progesterone Mirena IUD recently placed 3 weeks before our hospitalization. We speculate that in this patient's case, long-term use of progesterone IUD (containing levonorgestrel, a second-generation progestin) with β-blocker may have potentially mitigated ventricular arrhythmia risk.

Management of this case was consistent with current guidelines pertaining to the anomalous coronary artery and LQTS. The 2018 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology Guideline for the Management of Adult with Congenital Heart Disease supports surgical intervention for anomalous RCA from left sinus with ischemic symptoms or ventricular arrhythmias and summarizes higher risk anatomical features for potential ischemia.7 In high-risk LQTS patients with recurrent ICD shock despite β-blocker, the 2017 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology Guideline recommends intensification of medical therapy and/or left cardiac sympathetic denervation, in addition to genetic testing of patients and first-degree relatives.8

Conclusions

We present the case of a young female patient who experienced recurrent ventricular arrhythmias in the context of the anomalous RCA and congenital LQTS, and highlight the impact of sex hormones on LQT2.
