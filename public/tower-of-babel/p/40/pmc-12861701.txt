Investigating the cardioprotective effects of Nigella sativa oil on chronic antibiotic use on the myocardium of the left ventricle of male BALB/C mice
Authors: S Saka Olusola, A Komolafe Omobola, A Enye Linus, A Saka Alice, S Ayegbusi Olateju, E Arayombo Babatunde, Lauretta A Frederick
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe abuse of antibiotics is a significant factor in the emergence of antibiotic resistance and the development of several diseases in the modern world. These were conducted to provide information on the combined impact of chronic antibiotic exposure and Nigella sativa oil (NSO) on the morphology of the left ventricle of the heart of mice.40 adult male BALB/c mice weighing about 28–32 kg, divided into 5 groups, were used for this research. Group A was given normal saline, and Group B mice received 1000 mg/kg/of ampicillin. Groups C mice received 2 ml/kg of NSO, Group D were given 1000 mg/kg of ampicillin + 2 ml/kg of NSO, and Group E mice received 1000 mg/kg pre-exposed to ampicillin + 2 ml/kg of NSO. The rats were sacrificed, and the heart tissue was harvested and stored in sample bottles containing 10 % neutral buffer for histological and immunohistochemical studies.The results showed that there was a significant difference in superoxide dismutase and catalase activity in group B when compared with the control group (p = 0.0002, p = 0.0047, respectively). The photomicrograph of the control mice showed a regular arrangement with clear striations of myocardial fibres and centrally placed nuclei. There was a distortion of the regular arrangement of the myofibrils and disoriented nuclei in the ampicillin-only group.The study concluded that chronic ampicillin exposure induces significant myocardial damage, affecting both the histological integrity and functional capacity of the heart.Keywords: Nigella sativa, Histology, Immunohistochemistry, Left ventricle, Mice, Antibiotic

Introduction

Antibiotics have revolutionized modern medicine [1]. They facilitate necessary treatments and procedures and are crucial for treating infectious disorders. However, despite this success, their continued use in the 21st century is imperiled by two orthogonal challenges [2].

Antibiotic resistance seriously threatens the advancements made during the antibiotic era. This resistance is caused by a combination of natural resistance in some bacterial species (which existed before antibiotics were discovered), genetic mutations in microbes, whereby one species develops resistance from another, and selection pressure from antibiotic use that gives mutated strains a competitive advantage. Suboptimal antibiotic doses, especially from misuse of antibiotics, help in the stepwise selection of resistance [3]. Irrespective of their drug–target interactions, bactericidal antibiotics (ATBs) can cause common oxidative damage in bacteria, leading to the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and damage to DNA, proteins, and lipids, which results in cell death [4]. As an example, the interaction of aminoglycosides with ribosomes can cause mistranslation and abnormal folding of membrane proteins, leading to the production of toxic peptides and subsequent activation of the bacterial envelope stress response, oxidative stress generation, and ultimately cell death [5]. Antibiotics have cardiotoxic properties. Certain antibiotics, including β-lactams, have been implicated in mitochondrial dysfunction in cardiac cells, leading to oxidative stress and potential myocardial damage [6].

Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), Vancomycin-Resistant Enterococci (VRE), Penicillin-Resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae (PRSP), and Multiple-Drug-Resistant Gram-Negative Bacilli (MDRGNB) are a few examples of important resistant infections worldwide. One of the most widely used beta-lactam antibiotics among the more than 200 that are used to treat infections is penicillin (PEN) [7]. Penicillins, the first drugs developed to treat diseases caused by pathogenic bacteria, are still widely prescribed [8,9]. 95 and 81 years have passed since the discovery of penicillin by Fleming and its widespread clinical use, but it is still one of the most commonly prescribed antibiotics in human society. Penicillin's first impact was immediate and significant after 75 years of clinical usage [10]. Its clinical application revolutionized the treatment of infectious diseases, its widespread production revolutionized the pharmaceutical industry, and its discovery fundamentally altered the drug discovery process [11]. The success of penicillin production in Great Britain and the United States overshadowed the serendipity of its production and the efforts of other nations to produce it [12].

Nigella sativa (NS), also known as black seed, belongs to the Ranunculaceae plant family. The seeds and oil of NS have been used for centuries worldwide to treat various illnesses. Nigella sativa exhibits a broad spectrum of biological effects and therapeutic benefits, including anti-diabetic, anti-cancer, immunomodulatory, analgesic, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, spasmolytic, bronchodilator, renal protective, gastro-protective, antioxidant, and neuroprotective properties [13].

Administration of NS improved myocardial fibrosis in an inflammation-induced fibrosis model, and it acts through altering the oxidative/antioxidative balance and increasing antioxidative enzymes [14]. More studies are needed to clarify the beneficial effects and the underlying mechanisms of NS seed extract on myocardial fibrosis after common clinical cardiovascular diseases, especially myocardial infarction [14,15]. It also lowers heart rate and improves endothelial function and cardiac contractility [16]. Therefore, the current study focuses on the effects of Nigella sativa oil on the left ventricle of male BALB/c mice following long-term exposure to antibiotics.

Methodology

Animal care and management

Forty (40) healthy adult male BALB/c mice, weighing 28–32 g, were obtained from the Animal Research Centre, Afe Babalola University, Ado-Ekiti (ABUAD), Ekiti State, Nigeria. The age of each male BALB/c mouse was 84 days (12 weeks). The mice were contained at the Animal Research Centre of Afe Babalola University, Ado-Ekiti, and were housed in five (5) mice/cage under suitable temperature of 25–29 °C, 12-h cycles of light and darkness, full aeration which was enhanced by wire gauzed cage properly partitioned into four chambers and roomy enough to allow for proper ventilation and free movement within it. They were allowed to acclimatize for one week. After one week of acclimatization, the mice were fed with normal pelleted feed and given clean water in plastic water bottles.

Determination of blood pressure

Blood pressure of Adult Male BALB/c Mice was taken using a CONTEC 08A VET sphygmomanometer. The mice were placed in upright position with the forelimbs extending above the middle finger and the thumb; The proximal part of the cuff (pin head) was inserted to the inflation hole on the blood pressure monitoring device and the distal part cuff (3–5 cm) was wrapped around the left forelimb of the rat, the blood pressure monitor was adjusted to fit into the range of cuff attached and at the push of the start button, the machine inflated and deflated automatically displaying blood pressure the values on screen. These values were further processed for analysis.

Ethical consideration

Following the National Institute of Health's (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (since 1985), all experimental procedures were carried out by the Department of Human Anatomy, Faculty of Basic Medical Science, College of Medicine and Health Science, Afe Babalola University, Ado-Ekiti, Ekiti State, Nigeria, with approval number ABUADHREC/010/05/2024/2025.

Administration of ampicillin

250 mg of ampicillin was dissolved in 10 ml of distilled water in a sample vial, and the mixture was well mixed by gently shaking. Groups B, D, and E were force-fed 1000 mg/kg body weight of ampicillin orally via oral gavage throughout the first three weeks.

Administration of Nigella sativa oil

Nigella sativa oil distributed by TRIQUETRA HEALTH LLC, LITHIA, FL 33547. 2 mg/kg body weight of Nigella sativa was administered via the oral route to groups C, D, and E. The route of administration was oral through oral gavage using a cannula and syringe.

Administration of normal saline

0.5 ml of normal saline was administered to each animal in the control group (group A) only. The route of administration was oral through oral gavage using a cannula and syringe.

Sacrifice and tissue collection

At the end of the experiment, the mice were anesthetized with a single ketamine hydrochloride (100 mg/kg). The blood samples were collected by cardiac puncture and stored in 5 ml plain (no anticoagulant) tubes and allowed to clot for 20–30 min at room temperature, spun for 15 min to obtain serum, and carefully transferred the clear serum into labeled tubes without disturbing the clot, which was stored at 2–8 °C for the estimation of biochemical parameters. The heart was excised and fixed in 10 % formal saline for histological studies.

Histological analysis and Histochemical staining

Staining

The staining methods we used for the histological studies were Hematoxylin and Eosin (H & E) staining.

Hematoxylin and Eosin staining

Tissues were fixed and processed for paraffin wax embedding. The procedure of Drury and Wallington (1980) was adopted. All tissues were dehydrated through ascending grades of alcohol by immersion for 2 h each. Dehydrated tissues were cleared in xylene for 2 h. The tissues were infiltrated in two changes of molten paraffin wax at 56 °C in the oven for 1 h each and finally embedded in paraffin wax using plastic embedding molds, smeared with glycerin so that paraffin-blocked tissues can be separated easily from the molds after embedding. Paraffin-blocked tissues were trimmed and mounted on cassettes for sectioning on a rotary microtome. Sections of 5 μm thickness were obtained. The sections were spread in a warm water bath at 40–45 °C and collected on clean glass slides. The slides were dried on a drying plate at 40 °C overnight to enhance adherence and stored in a slide rack for later staining to be done [18].

Immunohistochemical (IHC) evaluation

The heart slices were processed, and paraffin embedded was sectioned. The slides were baked in the oven at 50 °C for 30 min, placed for 10 min in xylene, and rehydrated for about 10 min each in different concentrations of alcohol. The slides were then rinsed in distilled water and placed in hot citric acid solution (antigen retrieval) at pH 7.0 for 40 min, heated at 90 °C till it cools down to room temperature. They were then rinsed with distilled water and Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) a total of three times for 5 min each. The tissue areas on the slide were encircled using PAP pen and then returned to 1X PBS, after which the slides were incubated in H2O2 for 30 min at room temperature to block endogenous peroxidase activity, followed by normal goat serum for 10 min at room temperature. The slides were rinsed with PBS in between the incubations. They were incubated in the primary antibody solution (rabbit anti-interleukin-1) primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the slides were rinsed 4 times in 1X PBS for 5 min each and incubated again, but with biotinylated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody for 60 min at room temperature. Lastly, the slides were rinsed in 1X PBS for 5 min and later incubated with streptavidin solution for 30 min at room temperature, rinsed in PBS, followed by DAB solution for 20 min, and counterstained in hematoxylin solution for about 10 min. After that, they were dehydrated in different concentrations of alcohol for 10 min, cleared in xylene, and then cover-slipped using Dibutylphthalate polystyrene xylene (DPX) [19].

Biochemical assays

The biochemical analyses were done to discern the activities of oxidative stress markers (catalase and superoxide dismutase). This was done by collecting blood from the hearts of mice and allowing it to clot at room temperature. It was then centrifuged for 15 min at 3000 r/min. Supernatant or serum was used to analyze the measurement.

Analysis of antioxidant enzyme activity

Antioxidant enzymes help protect against the harmful effects of oxidative stress, and their activity varies with age and dietary nutrients such as fats and vitamins. These antioxidants interfere with antibiotics by significantly reducing their necrotic effects, due to their being highly sensitive to the presence of molecular oxygen.

Superoxidase dismutase (SOD) assay

About 10 % of all the heart tissues were prepared in 150 mm phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and centrifuged at about 12,000g for 30 min at 4 °C. The protein level in the left ventricle homogenate was measured using bovine serum albumin as a standard. The supernatant was processed for total Cu-SOD, Zn-superoxide dismutase (SOD), and Mn-SOD. The reaction was then stopped by adding freshly prepared Griess reagent, and absorbance was measured at 543 nm. SOD activity is expressed as specific activity (units/mg protein) [20].

Catalase activity assay

The indirect activity of catalase was analyzed using hydrogen peroxide as the substrate. The supernatant of 10 % homogenates of the left ventricle tissue in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was processed for the assay. Optical density was read at 570 nm, and the decrease in hydrogen peroxide was calculated. The standard curve was calibrated with varying concentrations of 0.2 mm H2O2 in PBS [21].

Photomicrography

An Olympus binocular microscope with a 5.1 megapixel MV550 camera was used to visualize the stained section. This was linked to a computer running a picture capture and analysis software. The system was calibrated to obtain high clarity and resolution.

Quantification of staining intensity

Image analysis and processing for Java (ImageJ) was used to analyze and quantify interleukin-1 Immunohistochemistry. Imported RGB images were converted to grayscale images on ImageJ. The software quantifies staining intensity by measuring the pixel value of each pixel in grayscale images following the threshold of areas of staining activity and converting the pixel value to brightness value or gray value. The values obtained were then analyzed statistically.

Statistical analyses

All the statistical analyses were performed using ANOVA (analysis of variance), and the differences between groups were evaluated by a post hoc Tukey test with the aid of GraphPad Prism Software 9.0.0 (121). The outcome of the statistical analyses is represented using a bar chart and graphs, with error bars representing the mean ± SD (standard deviation). The threshold for a significant difference was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

This study was conducted to investigate the therapeutic effects of Nigella sativa oil on the heart following chronic antibiotic exposure in male BALB/c mice. Nigella sativa, a black seed extract, has been shown to possess cardioprotective, antioxidant, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and hepatoprotective properties [22]. Superoxidase dismutase is a potent antioxidant enzyme that helps protect against oxidative stress [23,24]. It was observed that there was a significant reduction in the superoxidase dismutase (SOD) activity of groups A (Control), C (NSO), and D (ABT + NSO) animals, which were administered normal saline, Nigella sativa oil, and ampicillin with Nigella sativa oil as post-treatment, respectively, compared to groups B (ABT) and D (ABT/NSO) animals (ABT), which were administered ampicillin antibiotic and ampicillin with Nigella sativa oil simultaneously, respectively. This research follows Metwally & Soliman [25]. This study also shows that there was a significant difference in catalase activity in group B (ampicillin-only group) compared to group A (control), according to Biswas & Gotz [26], who reported that exposure to prolonged doses of antibiotics, such as pyocyanine and ampicillin, resulted in increased intracellular levels of superoxide dismutase and catalase.

Histological findings also revealed that the morphology of the myocardial cells in the left ventricle of Group B appears to have lost their characteristic arrangement and exhibited cellular disorganization (H & E staining). In the present study, examination of myocardial sections showed that ampicillin administration induced histological changes in all layers of the myocardium, involving different types of myocardial fibers, with disorganized striations, disoriented nuclei, and evidence of myocardial damage. These results align with previous findings that antibiotics can cause pathological and structural alterations in cardiac tissue, leading to severe myocardial damage [27].

The immunohistochemical findings highlight the effects of ampicillin administration and Nigella sativa oil on myocardial inflammation. Interleukin-1 (IL-1), a pro-inflammatory cytokine, was assessed across experimental groups to evaluate inflammatory responses to the heart. The control group showed a normal cytokine activity. However, the ampicillin-only group exhibited a significant increase in IL-1 expression, suggesting heightened myocardial inflammation. This aligns with reports that excessive cytokine release contributes to cardiomyocyte apoptosis, fibrosis, and ventricular dysfunction. In contrast, myocardial sections from the Nigella sativa-treated group displayed reduced IL-1 reactivity, approaching normal levels. This suggests that Nigella sativa possesses anti-inflammatory properties, potentially mitigating antibiotic-induced cardiac damage [28,29]. Previous studies have shown that chronic inflammation plays a key role in cardiovascular diseases, including heart failure [30]. The bioactive compounds in Nigella sativa, such as thymoquinone, may help regulate IL-1 expression, reducing oxidative stress and inflammation [31]. These findings suggest that Nigella sativa oil may serve as a cardioprotective agent, offering potential therapeutic benefits in preventing antibiotic-induced myocardial damage [32].

This study found that the mean arterial blood pressure of rats in group B increased significantly compared to the control group. High-dose antibiotic treatment may have caused cardiovascular damage [6]. The finding supports the idea that damage to cardiac tissue is linked to increased blood pressure [33,34].

Conclusion

The results of this study revealed that chronic ampicillin exposure induces significant myocardial damage, affecting both the histological integrity and functional capacity of the heart. Increased inflammatory responses, indicated by elevated interleukin-1 (IL-1) reactivity, suggest the presence of cardiac stress and potential ventricular dysfunction. However, the administration of Nigella sativa oil demonstrated a protective effect, counteracting the toxic impact of ampicillin on the heart.
