Evaluating the performance of different machine learning algorithms based on SMOTE in predicting musculoskeletal disorders in elementary school students
Authors: Sara Manoochehri, Maryam Zamani, Maryam Afshari, Ali Reza Soltanian, Zohreh Manoochehri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMusculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) are a major health concern for children. Traditional assessment methods, which are based on subjective assessments, may be inaccurate. The main objective of this research is to evaluate Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique(SMOTE)-based machine learning algorithms for predicting MSDs in elementary school students with an unbalanced dataset. This study is the first to use these algorithms to increase the accuracy of MSD prediction in this age group.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted in 2024 on 438 primary school students (boys and girls, grades 1 to 6) in Hamedan, Iran. Random sampling was performed from 12 public and private schools. The dependent variable was the presence or absence of MSD, assessed using the Cornell questionnaire. Given the imbalanced nature of the data, SMOTE-based techniques were applied. Finally, the performance of six machine learning algorithms, including Random Forest (RF), Naive Bayes (NB), Artificial Neural Network (ANN), Decision Tree (DT), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Support Vector Machine (SVM) was compared based on five SMOTE family techniques, including SVM-SMOTE, SMOTE-NC, ADASYN, Borderline SMOTE, and the SMOTE method, for predicting musculoskeletal disorders in primary school students.ResultsThis study analyzed 438 students, with 28 (6.39%) having musculoskeletal disorders (MSD). Machine learning models were tested on both imbalanced and SMOTE-balanced data. Before SMOTE, sensitivity was low (e.g., DT: 18%), but after applying SMOTE, it improved significantly (DT: 85%). Area Under the Curve (AUC) for RF and XGBoost increased from 65 to 99%. Among SMOTE-based techniques, SMOTE-NC achieved the highest accuracy for XGBoost (93.65%), RF (93.41%), ANN (92.80%), SVM (90.24%), and DT (86.58%). Borderline-SMOTE yielded the highest accuracy (96%) for XGBoost and RF. RF and XGBoost performed best overall. Key predictors of MSD were regional facilities, BMI, and gender.ConclusionThe findings of this study demonstrate the effectiveness of SMOTE in addressing class imbalance and improving the accuracy of MSD prediction among students. Overall, RF and XGBoost outperformed other algorithms. Additionally, key factors influencing MSD included regional facilities, body mass index (BMI), and gender, highlighting the significance of environmental and physiological factors in the development of this disorder.Keywords: Random forest, Support vector machine, Naïve bayes, Artificial neural networks, Decision tree, Extreme gradient boosting, Imbalanced data

Introduction

Musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) are any type of tissue damage to the musculoskelet system and nerves that causes a disruption in their function. These disorders affect a wide range of joints, muscles, ligaments, tendons, peripheral nerves, and blood vessels. Childhood and adolescence are particularly important in the development of the musculoskelet system [1]. Physical problems during these years can serve as predictors of irreversible impairments in adulthood, as bones and muscles develop early in life [2]. Preventing these disorders in childhood may delay entry into a cycle of recurrent episodes and reduce the prevalence of adult skeletal disorders [3]. Research shows that about 53% of teens have experienced muscle fatigue, numbness, back pain, shoulder pain, and neck pain at least once during their school years and about 15% have experienced persistent musculoskeletal pain at least once a week [4]. These disorders can lead to an increase in the absence of students from school, learning disabilities, physical health vulnerability, and an increase in disability and negative changes in the physical appearance of the body [5, 6].

MSDs can be caused by acquired, individual, or congenital risk factors. Most acquired risk factors are largely preventable. These include poor ergonomic conditions, heavy backpack loads, improper benches, poor posture while sitting, insufficient or excessive physical activity, excessive use of electronic devices, sports injuries, obesity, and adolescence [7].

Early detection and management of MSDs is critical to preventing their progression and minimizing their impact on children’s health. Traditional approaches to MSD risk assessment and management often rely on subjective assessments or self-reporting, which may lack accuracy and timeliness [8].

On the other hand, many researchers have accepted the effectiveness of machine learning algorithms in predicting various diseases [9]. The simplest type of prediction problem is two-state classification, which is used to distinguish people with a disease from people without a disease [10, 11].Various classification methods are used in data mining and machine learning (ML), including Random Forests (RF), Decision Tree (DT), Support Vector Machines (SVM), Naive Bayes (NB), Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), etc [12]. However, when faced with imbalanced data, which is often the common in medical studies, the algorithms introduced may not be appropriate, because they are biased towards the majority classes and this makes them provide high prediction accuracy for the majority class and misclassify the minority class samples [13]. In such situations, methods to solve the imbalance problem are used first, and then ML algorithms are used for prediction. Among the various methods used to address the imbalance problem are methods based on SMOTE, which include SVM-SMOTE, SMOTE-NC, ADASYN, Borderline SMOTE, and SMOTE method [14].

Based on our research, this is the first study to specifically use a variety of machine learning algorithms based on SMOTE to increase the accuracy of MSD prediction in elementary school students.On the other hand, previous studies on musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) have mainly focused on adults or specific occupational groups at risk.This study uniquely focuses on elementary school students, a population often overlooked in research on MSD despite the increasing prevalence of these issues due to increased sedentary behavior and poor ergonomics in school environments.

Early identification of students at risk can lead to timely interventions and potentially reduce the long-term effects of MSDs. The results of this research have the potential to influence educational health policy by providing evidence-based recommendations for school ergonomics and physical activity programs aimed at reducing the incidence of skeletal disorders among students.

Therefore, the main objective of this study is to comprehensively compare the performance of six machine learning algorithms including RF, NB, ANN, KNN and SVM based on five SMOTE family techniques, namely SVM-SMOTE, SMOTE-NC, ADASYN, borderline SMOTE, and SMOTE method for predicting musculoskeletal disorders in primary school students. In this study, in addition to comparing the performance of machine learning algorithms, we also investigated the impact of risk factors associated with musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs). Identifying and understanding these risk factors is critical to preventing and reducing the prevalence of these disorders.

Materials and methods

Study design

In this cross-sectional study, the study population consisted of primary school boys and girls (grades 1 to 6) attending elementary schools in Hamadan, one of the western cities of Iran. For data collection, the elementary schools in Hamadan province were categorized into two educational districts (districts 1 and 2) based on the division of the General Directorate of Education. Schools were then selected from each district using simple random sampling, considering their type (public or private). Subsequently, in the selected boys’ and girls’ schools, one class from each grade (grades 1 to 6) was randomly chosen. Within each class, students were selected through simple random sampling, and the required data were collected from the participants.

Participants

In this study, conducted in 2024, a total of 438 elementary school boys and girls (grades 1 to 6) from Hamadan were examined. These students were randomly selected from 12 schools in the city. Students with disabilities, fractures, musculoskeletal deformities, motor disorders, or those attending special education schools were excluded from the study.

Response variable

The response variable in this study was the presence or absence of musculoskeletal disorders in students, which is assessed using the Cornell questionnaire. The Cornell Musculoskeletal Discomfort Questionnaire is an appropriate tool for assessing the severity of musculoskeletal discomfort. It provides self-reported information on the presence and intensity of pain and discomfort in 12 body regions, including the neck, left and right shoulders, upper back, right and left upper arms, lower back, right and left forearms, left and right wrists, hips, left and right thighs, left and right knees, right and left lower legs, and right and left feet. To define the binary outcome variable, (MSD: Yes/No), we adopted a threshold-based classification. Specifically, students with a total CMDQ score of 10 or higher were classified as having musculoskeletal disorders (“Yes”), and those with a score below 10 as not having the disorder (“No”). This cutoff was informed by the study of Mongkonkansai et al. (2024), who suggested that CMDQ total scores above 10 indicate severe discomfort and are clinically relevant for identifying individuals with MSDs [15].

Predictive variables

The predictor variables in this study include students demographic and background factors, such as sex, age(year), weight (kg), height (cm), place of residence (urban/rural), regional facilities (low, moderate, well), grade(1,2,3,4,5,6), income status (well, moderate, low), physical activity, having cellphone, having computer, having TV, do playing, backpack weight, way of reaching school, backpack carrying situation, backpack carrier and the backpack selector was collected using a researcher-made questionnaire. These variables were selected based on a comprehensive review of prior research, expert consultation with professionals in school health and ergonomics, and practical considerations relevant to the elementary school context.

Data sources and measurement

students’ weight was measured using a digital scale and recorded in kilograms, while their standing height was measured in centimeters using a stadiometer. Additionally, the weight of students’ backpacks was randomly measured on a weekday using the same scale and recorded in kilograms.

Statistical methods

In this study, various machine learning methods including RF, SVM, NB, DT, ANN, and XGBoost were used to predict musculoskeletal disorders. Additionally, since the response variable was imbalanced between the two classes (with disorder and without disorder), SMOTE-based techniques were applied to balance the class distribution. In this study, in addition to comparing the performance of machine learning algorithms, we also investigated the impact of risk factors associated with musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs).

Decision tree

The decision tree (DT) is one of the simplest and most widely used methods for classification and regression problems. By learning decision rules from data features, it constructs a model to predict the target variable. This model consists of nodes and branches and involves the processes of splitting, stopping, and pruning.There are three types of nodes in a decision tree:1) A decision node (root node) splits the training data into two or more subsets. 2)An internal node represents a decision point within the tree and connects to child nodes.3)A leaf node (terminal node) represents the final outcome and has no outgoing branches.The advantages of a decision tree include robustness to noise, ease of interpretation, the ability to handle both numerical and categorical data, and the capability to manage missing values. However, its main drawback is its tendency to overfit and introduce bias, especially when working with small datasets [16].

Random forest

Leo Breiman was the first to propose the Random Forest (RF) technique. This algorithm is a popular ensemble learning method for regression and classification problems. It generates multiple decision trees using subsamples of the dataset. Each decision tree produces an outcome, and the final result is determined by averaging for regression and majority voting for classification. The advantage of the Random Forest (RF) method over other data mining techniques is that it requires defining only two parameters: (1) the number of trees in the forest and (2) the number of predictor variables at each decision tree node. The most important feature of RF is its high performance in measuring variable importance, helping to determine the role of each variable in predicting the response variable [17].

Support vector machine

Vapnik was the first to propose Support Vector Machines (SVM), which are based on statistical learning theory. The primary goal of SVM is to find a specific linear model that maximizes the margin of the hyperplane. The separation between classes is maximized by increasing the margin of the hyperplane. Support vectors are the training points closest to the maximum margin boundary.Thus, classification is achieved by maximizing the margin between two data classes and mapping variable vectors onto a high-dimensional plane. SVM can classify both linear and nonlinear observations. It employs a kernel function to transform nonlinear input into a linear form in a high-dimensional feature space, enabling linear separation when data is not inherently linearly separable. This has led to the proposal and adoption of various kernel functions for SVM, including sigmoid, linear, radial, and polynomial kernels. SVM is a flexible method as it allows the selection of an appropriate kernel function. To enhance performance, we utilized the radial basis function (RBF) kernel in this study [18].

Naïve Bayes

The Naïve Bayes (NB) classifier is a simple method based on strong independence assumptions and Bayes’ theorem. It assumes that all predictor variables are conditionally independent of each other. The NB approach aims to provide a classifier that is fast, easy to understand, and straightforward. By computing the probability of an object belonging to a particular category, the NB classification model assigns samples to classes. The class with the highest posterior probability is chosen as the object’s class, as Bayes’ formula calculates the posterior probability based on the prior probability of the object.The advantages of the NB approach include ease of use, high performance, the ability to make probabilistic predictions, and minimal training data requirements. Additionally, it is sensitive to irrelevant attributes and performs remarkably well even when the independence assumption is violated. However, it is computationally intensive, especially for models involving a large number of variables [19].

Artificial neural network

An artificial neural network (ANN) consists of an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer, inspired by the functioning of neurons in the human brain. This machine learning approach is highly effective in classification and pattern recognition. The learning process involves identifying patterns and relationships in data and improving through experience.Neurons (nodes) are the fundamental units of each layer. While they are not connected within the same layer, they are fully connected between adjacent layers, with each connection assigned a specific weight. In a feedforward neural network, data flows from the input layer to the hidden layers and finally to the output layer.Activation functions play a crucial role in learning complex relationships between inputs and outputs. In this study, the sigmoid function, a commonly used nonlinear activation function, was employed [20].

Extreme gradient boosting

The gradient-boosted decision tree technique forms the foundation of the XGBoost algorithm, which is an improved version of it. In 2016, Chen and Guestrin introduced a method to address the overfitting of traditional boosting algorithms, developing a scalable tree-boosting system to overcome long training times. XGBoost is constructed by combining a strong classifier with a weak base classifier. At each stage of the training process, the next classifier optimizes the objective function using the residual error of the base classifier. Additionally, this technique can handle missing variables, reduce classification errors, limit overfitting, and shorten training times while constructing the final model [21].

Approaches to handling class imbalance and synthetic minority over‑sampling technique (SMOTE)

Class imbalance arises when one class has significantly fewer instances than another, which can lead to biased classification performance. The imbalance ratio (IR) is commonly used to quantify this issue.Broadly, methods for addressing class imbalance fall into two categories: (1) Data-level approaches, which modify the training data to balance class distribution. These include oversampling (e.g., SMOTE), undersampling, and hybrid techniques.(2) Algorithm-level approaches, which modify the learning algorithm to better handle the minority class, such as cost-sensitive learning, ensemble methods with balanced weightings, or modified loss functions. In this study, we adopted data-level techniques due to their flexibility and compatibility with standard classifiers. Among oversampling methods, SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) is one of the most widely used techniques. Instead of simply duplicating minority samples, SMOTE generates synthetic examples based on k-nearest neighbors, helping reduce overfitting.However, SMOTE has known limitations, such as generating ambiguous samples near class boundaries or overfitting in noisy regions. To address these issues, several SMOTE variants have been developed, including: Borderline-SMOTE: focuses on samples near decision boundaries, SMOTE-NC: supports both numerical and categorical features, SVM-SMOTE: uses SVM-defined boundaries for generating synthetic points.

These enhanced versions allow for more effective handling of class imbalance, particularly in datasets with complex feature distributions, as in the case of our health survey data. The selected SMOTE techniques in our study were chosen based on their reported strengths in the literature and their suitability for mixed-type predictor variables [14].

Software and performance criteria

All SMOTE-balancing techniques used in this study were implemented using Python software version 3.10.6 and the “imbalanced-learn” package. Additionally, R software version 4.1.1 was used to implement all ML method analyses. Packages such as “nnet” for ANN “, “xgboost” for XGBoost, “e1071” for SVM, “naivebayes” for NB, “randomForest” for RF and variable importance (VIMP) in the RF, “rpart” for DT, and “SHAPforxgboost” for SHAP value were used.Several evaluation metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, geometric mean(G-means), area under the curve (AUC), F1-score, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV)were used to assess the predictive performance of machine learning models. A cross-validation strategy was employed. the data was randomly split into 70% training and 30% testing subsets and this process was repeated 100 times. SMOTE-based oversampling techniques were applied only to the training data to address class imbalance, ensuring that the test data remained unaltered and independent. The reported performance metrics (accuracy, AUC, F1, etc.) are averages from the 100 test set evaluations. The hyperparameters listed in Table 1 were tuned for each machine learning algorithm using a grid search strategy with five-fold cross-validation, applied exclusively to the training data. Candidate values for each parameter were chosen based on standard practices and relevant literature. These values were refined through an iterative process that evaluated model performance metrics. The optimal parameters were selected based on the highest average performance across the folds. This approach ensures transparency and supports the robustness of model comparisons.

This strategy was chosen to address the extreme class imbalance (1:14 ratio) and is commonly used in similar scenarios to ensure sufficient representation of minority cases during model training.

A key observation from Table 3 is the significant discrepancy between specificity and sensitivity in all ML models before balancing. In the original dataset, most classifiers exhibited high accuracy (≥ 86%) except for Naive Bayes (NB). The specificity values were notably high for all algorithms except NB, indicating a bias towards the majority class. After applying SMOTE, all methods except NB maintained high accuracy and specificity (≥ 86%).

Balancing the dataset led to improved classifier performance, with increased sensitivity and AUC values across all algorithms. Before SMOTE, sensitivity was significantly lower; for instance, the DT classifier had a sensitivity of only 18%, which increased to 85% after applying the SMOTE-SVM technique. Additionally, the G-mean scores improved for all models after SMOTE application. Initially, the G-mean was 30% for DT and ranged between 0 and 30% for other models. The F1 score, which varied between 0 and 14% in the unbalanced dataset, exceeded 96% for all models post-SMOTE.

Among SMOTE-based techniques, SMOTE-NC achieved the highest accuracy for XGBoost (93.65%), Random Forest (93.41%), ANN (92.80%), SVM (90.24%), and DT (86.58%). Borderline-SMOTE yielded the highest accuracy (96%) for XGBoost and Random Forest. Except for NB, all models had an accuracy above 80% before and after SMOTE.

In the original dataset, the highest AUC achieved was 65%, whereas applying SMOTE led to an AUC of 99% in the RF and XGBoost models. Comparing NPV across ML algorithms, RF and XGBoost achieved an NPV above 90% using SMOTE techniques. Notably, RF had the highest value in all SMOTE methods except SMOTE-NC, while XGBoost reached 95% in the Borderline-SMOTE method. Figure 1 visualizes the AUC of different models before and after applying SMOTE. The results indicate that RF and XGBoost, when used with SMOTE-Borderline, SMOTE, and SMOTE-Adasyn, achieved an AUC of 99%, whereas SMOTE-NC and SVM-SMOTE achieved 98%, 97%, and 63%, respectively. Overall, RF and XGBoost outperformed other ML models across all evaluation metrics.

Discussion

In most classification problems, training or testing data are class-imbalanced, meaning the number of observations in each class is not equal. This imbalance creates challenges in classification, significantly impacting the learning process. Class imbalance typically results in poor predictive accuracy for the minority class, as classifiers tend to favor the majority class, leading to most new samples being classified into the dominant category. In such a situation, the evaluation of classifier performance is also subject to criticism [13]. One approach to addressing this issue is the use of SMOTE-based methods.

In the present study, the performance of several ML methods (ANN, SVM, NB, XGBoost, DT, RF) was examined to predict MSDs among elementary school children. A key characteristic of the analyzed data was its severe imbalance, suggesting that conventional ML methods might not perform well in handling such data. Therefore, the performance of these methods was compared with SMOTE-based ML approaches.

Based on the results of this study, the performance of all ML models improved after applying SMOTE techniques, according to sensitivity, AUC, G-Means, and F1 score metrics. Similar findings have been reported in most previous studies [22–24].For example, in a study conducted by Abdelaoui, which examined the impact of SMOTE on the performance of several ML models using a diabetes dataset, the results showed that although the models achieved satisfactory overall accuracy before SMOTE, significant improvements were observed in recall, precision, and the area under the ROC curve after applying SMOTE [22].

In the present study, In Fig. 1, the orange curve for the Decision Tree shows a distinct inflection point, where the slope changes notably before and after the point. The distinct inflection point in the Decision Tree (DT) ROC curve (orange) arises due to the nature of DT’s decision-making process. Decision Trees make binary splits based on feature thresholds, resulting in discrete predictions that can lead to abrupt changes in the ROC curve, manifesting as an inflection point where the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity shifts significantly. This is particularly pronounced in imbalanced datasets or after SMOTE application, where the DT may prioritize certain thresholds that separate the minority class more effectively, causing a noticeable slope change. In contrast, the Random Forest (RF) curve exhibits a smoother shape, unlike the step-function behavior seen in other methods. This is because RF is an ensemble method that aggregates predictions from multiple decision trees, each trained on random subsets of data and features. This averaging process smooths out the discrete jumps typical of individual decision trees, resulting in a more continuous ROC curve.

In the present study, although most performance evaluation metrics of ML models improved after applying SMOTE techniques, their specificity slightly decreased. A similar observation was made in the study conducted by Kivrak et al., where sensitivity and F1 score improved after applying SMOTE, but specificity decreased across all classifiers [25].

In the present study, despite the fact that the accuracy of all ML models before and after applying SMOTE techniques was above 80%, the accuracy of the NB model was not satisfactory. In a study conducted by Yang et al., which utilized five baseline algorithms as well as SMOTE-XGBoost for predicting heart disease, the accuracy of the NB model was lower compared to RF, KNN, DT, and XGBoost models [24]. A fundamental issue with using the NB method is that it assumes all features used to describe a sample are conditionally independent given the class of that sample. When this assumption is violated—which is often the case with real-world data—classification accuracy decreases due to “information double-counting” and the elimination of interactions [26].

In this study, among all SMOTE techniques, SMOTE-NC had the highest accuracy for all ML models except NB. This strong performance of SMOTE-NC could be attributed to its ability to effectively manage datasets containing both nominal (categorical) and continuous features. This capability is particularly valuable for many real-world datasets, where mixed data types are common. In such cases, traditional SMOTE methods, which primarily focus on continuous data, may not perform optimally [27, 28].

Overall, among all the techniques used in this study, the RF, XGBoost, and ANN models demonstrated superior performance. Similarly, in a study conducted by Hassanzadeh et al., SMOTE-based RF, XGBoost, and ANN models outperformed other ML algorithms in predicting in-hospital mortality for trauma patients [23].

Additionally, given the strong performance of RF among the ML methods mentioned, thise technique was also used to identify the most important variables influencing MSDs. Based on the SHAP value plot derived from the RF model, the five most significant factors affecting the prediction of MSDs in students were regional facilities, BMI, gender, grade, and computer ownership. As mentioned earlier, the most significant variable in predicting MSDs in this study was regional facilities. Overall, MSD prevalence was lower in schools located in well-developed areas. Access to ergonomic study environments is one of the features related to regional facilities that can reduce the risk of developing MSDs. For example, a study found that students who used ergonomic furniture reported fewer musculoskeletal complaints compared to those who lacked access to such facilities [29]. This highlights the importance of environmental factors in the occurrence of MSDs. Based on the results, another important variable in predicting MSDs was BMI, such that an increase in BMI was associated with a higher occurrence of MSDs. This finding is consistent with the study conducted by Tantawy et al., which demonstrated that the prevalence of MSDs was higher among students with a higher BMI [30]. This relationship may be explained by the fact that increased weight places additional stress on the musculoskeletal system, thereby increasing the risk of injury to this system [31, 32]. Gender, age, and grade were also identified as significant variables in predicting MSDs. According to the descriptive statistics, there was a significant difference in gender distribution between healthy students and those with MSDs, with a higher proportion of affected students being female. According to the results of studies, the overall prevalence of MSDs was significantly associated with gender and age [33]. Additionally, these disorders were significantly more common among students in higher grades [34]. Finally, prolonged computer use, a common factor in students’ lives, especially in the context of remote learning and digital education, has been linked to various musculoskeletal complaints, particularly in the neck, shoulders, and back [35]. Using a desk and chair while working with a laptop, utilizing external keyboards to reduce shoulder strain, implementing proper ergonomic methods, and performing short exercises have been recommended to prevent MSDs in individuals who use computers for extended periods [36]. Therefore, focusing on these factors—identified with high accuracy using SMOTE-based ML techniques—can significantly reduce the prevalence of these disorders among students.

Limitations

One of the limitations of the present study is that the analyzed data were obtained through a self-reported questionnaire, which typically introduces recall bias in such studies. Our study was no exception to this issue. Also, the results of this study might not apply to children in other age groups or geographical areas because it was limited to one city (Hamadan) and primary school pupils.

Conclusion

This study utilized SMOTE-based machine learning methods to enhance the accuracy of predicting musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) in students. The application of SMOTE effectively addressed the class imbalance challenge and improved the performance of models, particularly RF and XGBoost. These innovative approaches enable the early identification of at-risk students, which, from a clinical perspective, can lead to the implementation of preventive interventions.

Clinically, key factors such as BMI, gender, age, and school environmental conditions were identified as significant predictors of MSDs. High BMI and poor ergonomic conditions were associated with an increased risk of MSDs, highlighting the need for targeted interventions in educational settings. The integration of advanced machine learning techniques with a deeper understanding of clinical risk factors can assist healthcare professionals and educational authorities in designing effective programs to reduce the prevalence of MSDs. These findings emphasize that improving physical conditions and educating students on proper posture and carrying techniques can significantly reduce the clinical burden of these disorders.
