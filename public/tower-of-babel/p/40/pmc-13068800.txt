Inhibitory effect of transfer factor on infection with the novel variant strain of infectious bursal disease virus in chickens
Authors: Kongda Zhu, Shuang Chang, Pengfei Zhao, Chengyi Che, Zhihao Ren, Wenping Cui, Yixin Wang, Peng Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe novel variant strain of infectious bursal disease virus (nVarIBDV) has emerged as one of the immunosuppressive viruses severely threatening the poultry industry. Even with the presence of certain levels of maternal antibodies in some flocks, infection with nVarIBDV cannot be prevented. Non-specific immunostimulants are wildely used in clinical practice to enhance the prevention and control of this disease. To explore the potential of chicken transfer factor (TF) in suppressing nVarIBDV infection,a total of 210 SPF chicks were randomly divided into 7 groups in this study. The nVarIBDV infection was administered at two different ages (7 and 14 days post-hatch), and the TF treatment was applied at two dosage levels: low dose (0.25 mL per chick) and high dose (0.5 mL per chick). Viral load of nVarIBDV was detected via qRT-PCR at various time points, and the intervention effect of TF on nVarIBDV infection was evaluated through dynamic monitoring of body weight and analysis of bursal organ development index. The results showed that SPF chicks were infected with nVarIBDV at different ages (7 days and 14 days) significantly suppressed body weight gain and induced bursal atrophy. The application of TF could effectively alleviate the nVarIBDV-induced growth retardation and bursal atrophy, while also markedly inhibiting viral replication in the bursa of Fabricius. Moreover, the inhibitory effect was positively correlated with the dosage of TF used. This study provides a new technical supplement for improving the prevention and control of nVarIBDV, while also offering valuable insights for research on antiviral immune regulation in poultry.Keywords: Infectious Bursal Disease Virus, Novel Variant Strain, Transfer Factor, Immunosuppression

Introduction

Infectious bursal disease (IBD) is an acute and highly contagious disease, characterized by immunosuppression, which is caused by the infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV), once an outbreak occurs, the virus spreads rapidly within flocks, exerting a devastating impact on poultry health and farming profitability (Fan et al., 2020; Myint et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2020). IBDV is classified into two serotypes, with only serotype I being pathogenic to chickens. Strains of serotype I can be further classified into classical (cIBDV), virulent or very virulent (vvIBDV), and variant (varIBDV) strains(Jackwood and Saif, 1987). Since the 1990s, variant strains of IBDV have remained in a state of latent circulation, during which they have undergone continuous and gradual mutation (Aliyu et al., 2021; Aliyu et al., 2022). In recent years, the novel variant strain of (nVarIBDV) has re-emerged and become prevalent in China and several other countries. Unlike directly lethal strains, nVarIBDV is not immediately fatal but primarily causes severe immunosuppression (Thai et al., 2022).

Transfer Factor (TF), a low-molecular-weight substance extracted from lymphocytes, functions by transferring immune sensitivity information to T lymphocytes, thereby enhancing the body’s immune response(Qin et al., 2017). Currently, it has found extensive application in treating immunosuppressive diseases, allergies, and autoimmune disorders in poultry (Ascher et al., 1974; Lawrence et al., 1955). TF is also frequently used as an immunostimulanat added to vaccines, significantly enhancing their immunogenic effects. A substantial body of experimental and clinical evidence has demonstrated that transfer factor has achieved remarkable research progress and practical application effects in livestock and poultry research, significantly educing the incidence and mortality of poultry diseases (Miao et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2022). Focusing on the recent upward trend in IBD prevalence, this study investigated the role of transfer factor in inhibiting nVarIBDV, thereby providing a new reference for improving the prevention and control of nVarIBDV infection.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

The animal care and use protocol was approved by the Shandong Agricultural University Animal Care and Use Committee (SDAUA-2016-002). All the experimental animals of this study were cared for and maintained throughout the experiments strictly following the ethics and biosecurity guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shandong Agricultural University.

Strains and reagents

The clinically isolated nVarIBDV strain IBDV-SD-1208 (EID50=105.17/0.2 mL), a newly identified variant of genotype A2dB1 recently emerging in China, was kindly provided by Associate Professor Wei Kai from the College of Veterinary Medicine at Shandong Agricultural University (Liu, 2023).

The TF was produced and supplied by Shandong Sinder Technology Co., Ltd. Each milliliter of the D-ribose oral solution contains a polypeptide content of ≥1 μg and a nucleotide content of ≥30 μg.

The commercial chicken-specific TF oral solution was produced and provided by Shandong Sinder Technology Co., Ltd. The preparation process was as follows: frozen chicken spleens were homogenized in sterile saline at a ratio of 1:5 (w/v) in an ice bath, and centrifuged at 8,000 r/min at 4°C for 30 min. The supernatant was collected, sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 μm filter, and then dialyzed against saline at 4 °C for 24 h using a 3.5 kda dialysis bag. The dialysate was collected and refiltered through a 0.22 μm filter. Ensure that key quality control standards are met, including a peptide content of ≥1 mg per mL of oral solution, a nucleotide content (calculated as D-ribose) of ≥30 μg, and a free amino acid content of ≥ 1.45 mg.

Animal experiment design

A total of 210 one-day-old specific pathogen-free (SPF) chicks were randomly divided into seven groups, with 30 chicks per group. The groups were designated as follows: High-dose TF+7d nVarIBDV, Low-dose TF+7d nVarIBDV, 7d nVarIBDV only, High-dose TF+14d nVarIBDV, Low-dose TF+14d nVarIBDV, 14d nVarIBDV only, and negative control. Chickens in the High-dose TF+7d nVarIBDV and High-dose TF+14d nVarIBDV groups received 0.5 mL of transfer factor per chicks daily from day 1 to 3 of age, whereas those in the Low-dose TF+7d nVarIBDV and Low-dose TF+14d nVarIBDV groups were given 0.25 mL of transfer factor per chicks from day 1 to 3 of age. At 7 days of age, the High-dose TF+7d nVarIBDV group, Low-dose TF+7d nVarIBDV group, and 7d nVarIBDV group were intranasally inoculated with 10⁵·¹⁷ EID₅₀ of IBDV-SD-1208 virus solution. At 14 days of age, the High-dose TF+14d nVarIBDV group, Low-dose TF+14d nVarIBDV group, and 14d nVarIBDV group were intranasally inoculated with 10⁵·¹⁷ EID₅₀ of IBDV-SD-1208 virus solution. The Negative control group was treated with an equal volume of sterile normal saline.

Determination of indicators for animal experiments

SPF chicks in each group were weighed at 7, 14, 21, and 28 days post-infeciton with the IBDV-SD-1208 strain. One each of these time points, six SPF chicks were randomly selected from each group for body weight measurement and necropsy. After necropsy, the bursa of Fabricius was collected and weighed. The Bursa-body Index (BBIX) and bursa of Fabricius development index were calculated respectively. Bursa of Fabricius development was assessed using two indices: BBIX calculated as the bursa-to-body weight ratio of the experimental group divided by that of the blank control group; and the absolute development index (mg/g), defined as bursa weight (mg) divided by body weight (g). The Bursa of Fabricius development index was defined as the organ’s weight (mg) divided by the chicks body weight (g), expressed as mg/g. At 7 and 14 days post-infection (dpi) with the IBDV-SD-1208 strain, six SPF chicks per group were necropsied and the bursal of Fabricius were collected. Viral RNA was extracted from the bursa of Fabricius using a viral RNA extraction kit (OMEGA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The replication of IBDV in the bursa of Fabricius was detected using the established real-time quantitative PCR method. Specifically, a reaction mixture was prepared containing 10 μL of SYBR Green Pro Taq, 0.4 μL of IBDV-F, 0.4 μL of IBDV-R, 2 μL of template cDNA, and 7.2 μL of ddH₂O. The reactions procedure consisted of initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60°C for 30 s. Melting curve analysis was performed using the instrument's default program. The standard curve was defined by the equation y = -0.3133x + 10.921 (R² = 0.9942).

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 5 software. Statistical analyses included T-tests (or non-parametric tests), One-way and two-way ANOVA (or non-parametric/mixed -model equivalents, as appropriate). A P-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

TF alleviated the body weight loss of SPF chicks caused by nVarIBDV infection

At 7, 14, 21, and 28 days post-inoculation with nVarIBDV at 7 days of age, the body weight of the nVarIBDV-infected control group remained the lowest (Fig. 1A). The weight was significantly lower than that of the blank control group at 21 and 28 days post-infection (P < 0.01), indicating a substantial inhibitory effect of nVarIBDV infection on chick growth. Similarly, the same pattern was observed at 7, 14, 21, and 28 days post-inoculation with nVarIBDV at 14 days of age (Fig. 1B). Specifically, beginning at 14 days after nVarIBDV inoculation, the body weight of the infected control group was significantly lower than that of the blank control group (P < 0.01), and no natural mortality was observed in any group throughout the experimental period, thus excluding the interference of non-experimental factors on body weight measurement indices.The body weights of infected chickens pre-treated with TF in each group were higher than those in the nVarIBDV infection control group (without TF treatment), indicating that TF use effectively alleviated the suppressive effect of nVarIBDV infection on body weight gain in chickens. This effect was more significant when a higher dose of TF was used. For example, at 21 and 28 days post-inoculation with nVarIBDV at 7 days of age, the body weight of the High-dose TF+7d nVarIBDV group was significantly higher than that of the nVarIBDV infection control group (7d nVarIBDV group) (P < 0.01), while no significant difference was observed compared with the Negative control group (P > 0.05, Fig. 1A). Similarly, at 28 days post-inoculation with nVarIBDV at 14 days of age, the body weight of the High-dose TF+14d nVarIBDV group was significantly higher than that of the 14d nVarIBDV group (P < 0.01), while the Low-dose TF+14d nVarIBDV group also showed a significantly increase in body weight compared to the 14d nVarIBDV group (P < 0.05, Fig. 1B). All the above results indicated that nVarIBDV infection affected the growth and development of SPF chicks, whereas the treatment with TF at both dosage levels alleviated the inhibitory effect of nVarIBDV infection on body weight gain of SPF chicks, with the alleviating effect being positively correlated with the dosage used.

TF alleviated the bursa of fabricius atrophy of SPF chicks caused by nVarIBDV infection

At 7, 14, 21, and 28 days post-inoculation with nVarIBDV at 7 days of age, the bursa of Fabricius development index in SPF chicks of the nVarIBDV infection control group remained the lowest (Fig. 2A) and was significantly lower than that of the blank control group (P < 0.01), indicating a strong suppressive effect of nVarIBDV infection on bursa of Fabricius development. Similarly, following inoculation at 14 days of age, the bursa of Fabricius development index in the nVarIBDV-infected group was also markedly reduced compared to the blank control group at 14, 21, and 28 days post-infection (P < 0.01, Fig. 2B).

Bursa of Fabricius development index of SPF chickens in each group after infection with nVarIBDV. (A) Infected at 7 days of age, (B) Infected at 14 days of age. Different symbols in the column diagrams indicate statistically significant differences(ns P>0.05;*: P<0.05; **: P<0.01; ***: P<0.001; ****: P<0.0001).

When the BBIX < 0.7, the bursa of Fabricius was considered atrophic. Regardless of Whether nVarIBDV was inoculated at 7 or 14 days of age, the BBIX in all nVarIBDV-only infected groups remained consistently below 0.7, indicating a state of bursal atrophy (Fig. 3A, Fig. 3B). The atrophy of the bursa of Fabricius induced by nVarIBDV infection was also visually apparent. Necropsy findings further confirmed severe bursal atrophy in nVarIBDV-infected chicks compared to the blank control group-both at 28 days post-inoculation in chickens infected at 7 days of age (Fig. 3C) and at days post-inoculation in those infected at 14 days of age (Fig. 3D). However, pre-treatment with TF significantly alleviated the atrophy of the chicken bursa of Fabricius caused by nVarIBDV infection, and this protective effect was better with a higher dose of TF. For example, at 28 days post-inoculation with nVarIBDV at 7 days of age, the high-dose TF treatment group exhibited a significantly higher bursa of Fabricius development index than the nVarIBDV infection control group (P < 0.05), and the BBIX remained consistently above 0.8. At 28 days post-inoculation with nVarIBDV at 14 days of age, the bursa of Fabricius development index of the high-dose TF-treated infection group was significantly higher than that of the nVarIBDV infection control group (P < 0.05), while no significant difference was observed compared with the blank control group. The BBIX values in the high-dose TF-treated infection group remained consistently above 0.7, indicating that high-dose TF could effectively alleviated the adverse effects of nVarIBDV infection on bursal development in SPF chicks at different ages.

TF inhibited the replication of nVarIBDV in the bursa of fabricius of spf chicks

As shown in Fig. 4A, at 7 days post-inoculation, no significant difference was observed in the viral load in the bursa of Fabricius between the Low-dose TF+7d nVarIBDV group and the 7d nVarIBDV group (P > 0.05). However, the viral load in the High-dose TF+7d nVarIBDV group was significantly lower than that in both the 7d nVarIBDV group (P < 0.001) and the Low-dose TF+7d nVarIBDV group (P < 0.01). At 14 days post-infection, the viral loads in the bursa of Fabricius in both the High-dose TF+7d nVarIBDV group and Low-dose TF+7d nVarIBDV groups were significantly lower than those in the 7d nVarIBDV group (P < 0.0001). As shown in Fig. 4B, at 7 days post-infection, no statistically significant difference in viral load was observed in the bursa of Fabricius between the High-dose TF+14d nVarIBDV and Low-dose TF+14d nVarIBDV groups (P > 0.05). However, both the High-dose TF+14d nVarIBDV and Low-dose TF+7d nVarIBDV groups exhibited significantly lower viral loads in the bursa of Fabricius compared to the 14d nVarIBDV group (P < 0.05). A significantly higher viral load was detected in the bursa of Fabricius of the 14d nVarIBDV group at 14 days post-infection, compared to both the High-dose TF+14d nVarIBDV group (P < 0.001) and the Low-dose TF+14d nVarIBDV group (P < 0.01). Thease results demonstrated that TF suppresses nVarIBDV replication in the bursa of Fabricius of SPF chicks in a dose-dependent manner.

Discussion

Infectious bursal disease (IBD) is an acute, highly contagious, and immunosuppressive disease caused by IBDV (Tian et al., 2025). Research has revealed antigenic differences between nVarIBDV and vvIBDV strains, with antigenic variation and mismatch being major contributors to vaccine failure (Fan et al., 2020). This not only underscores the urgency of developing new vaccines against nVarIBDV (Yu et al., 2021) but also highlights the need to explore effective measures for controlling its continuously evolving strains of IBDV.

Transfer Factor (TF), as known as the transmission factor, is a small-molecule polynucleotide peptide produced by lymphocytes that mediates cellular immune function (Wang et al., 2006). It is capable of transferring both specific and non-specific cell-mediated immune responses from a donor to a recipients (Pineda et al., 2005), thereby enhancing the immune function of the organism. TF has a good inhibitory effect on chicken infectious anemia virus (CIAV), Newcastle disease virus (NDV), avian influenza virus (AIV), and other viruses(Duan et al., 2025; Miao et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2022). Compared with CIAV and NDV which exhibit relatively low mutation rates and have well-established commercial vaccines available for prevention and control- IBDV displays significant variability (Miao et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2022). The continuous emergence of nVarIBDV often compromises the protective efficacy of traditional vaccines, posing persistent challenges to the poultry industry. Our study confirms that TF exerts a significant inhibitory effect against variant IBDV, suggesting its potential as an effective approach for preventing or controlling the continuously evolving IBDV. These findings offer new perspectives and technical support for tackling the prevention challenges posed by viral variants.

Based on the analysis that TF could effectively alleviate the inhibition of weight gain in SPF chicks infected with nVarIBDV, we futher discovered-through observaton of the bursal development index, bursal weight index, and gross morphological changes in the bursa of Fabricius-that infection with nVarIBDV leads to impaired development and atrophy of the bursa of Fabricius, regardless of whether the infection occurs at an early age (7 days) or at a susceptible age (14 days). However, use of low-dose TF could alleviate the damage caused by nVarIBDV infection to the bursa of Fabricius, while high-dose TF can further alleviate the pathological injury to this immune organ induced by nVarIBDV infection.

This study also examined the viral load of IBDV viral load in the bursa of Fabricius of SPF chicks. At 7 and 14 days post-infection with nVarIBDV at 7 days of age, the bursal viral load in the Low-dose TF + 7d nVarIBDV group (infected and treated with low-dose TF) was higher than that in the High-dose TF + 7d nVarIBDV group, but lower than that in the 7d nVarIBDV group. At 7 and 14 days post-infection with nVarIBDV on day 14, the bursal viral load in the Low-dose TF + 14d nVarIBDV group (which received a low dose of TF and was challenged) was higher than that in the High-dose TF + 14d nVarIBDV group but lower than that in the 14d nVarIBDV group. These results indicated that TF significantly inhibited the replication of nVarIBDV in the bursa of Fabricius of SPF chicks, and the inhibitory effect was positively correlated with the dosage used.

In conclusion, TF can effectively alleviate the pathogenic damage of nVarIBDV to SPF chicks, which is specifically manifested in reducing the inhibition of weight gain, relieving bursal atrophy, and inhibiting viral replication in the bursa of Fabricius, and this effect is positively correlated with the dosage of TF used. Combined with the known immunological characteristics of TF and relevant research reports, its potential mechanism of action against nVarIBDV can be speculated as follows: first, enhancing the cellular immune response mediated by T lymphocytes, promoting the activation and proliferation of immune cells, and improving the body's ability to recognize and clear the virus(Miao et al., 2025); second, inducing the secretion of antiviral cytokines such as interferons, and inhibiting nVarIBDV replication by regulating the body's immune signaling pathways(Yu et al., 2021); third, protecting the structural integrity of immune organs such as the bursa of Fabricius, maintaining the normal immune function of the body, and alleviating virus-induced immunosuppression. In practical breeding applications, as a commercial oral solution, TF has good feasibility: it is convenient to administer and meets the needs of large-scale breeding, has no toxic and side effects, and does not interfere with existing IBD vaccines, which can specifically solve the problem of decreased protective efficacy of traditional vaccines caused by nVarIBDV. Based on the results of this study, it is recommended to start administering TF to chicks at 1-3 days of age in practical applications to achieve early protection; at the same time, optimize the administration dosage, and the high dose of 0.5 mL per chick is preferred to obtain more significant virus inhibition and damage alleviation effects. On the basis of practical application, we can further explore whether TF can synergize with IBD maternal antibodies or IBD vaccine antibodies to cope with IBDV infection, so as to provide a more comprehensive scheme for the prevention and control of IBDV infection.
