The Matador Bug and Several Related Leaf-footed Bugs (Hemiptera: Coreidae) Accumulate Cyanogens From their Host Plants
Authors: Colin R Morrison, Brian E Sedio, Lawrence E Gilbert, Justin C Havird, Ummat Somjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCyanogenic compounds provide potent defenses to herbivorous insects, yet few species are known to acquire them from their food. Matador bugs, Bitta alipes (Hemiptera: Coreidae), have brightly colored signals that deter avian predators and are specialists of cyanogenic Passiflora plants. It is unknown whether these insects accumulate cyanogenic compounds. We document the first evidence of cyanogen accumulation in matador bugs. However, the presence and concentration of cyanogens depended on the Passiflora species from which insects were feeding. Further, three related species of inconspicuous Coreidae, and their Cactaceae and Aquifoliaceae host plants, also contained cyanogenic compounds. In all coreid species, cyanogen concentrations detected would make them unpalatable, or even lethal, to vertebrate predators. We report the first evidence of cyanogenic compounds in the Coreidae, doubling the number of species known to accumulate cyanide in the Hemiptera. Cyanogen accumulation may be more common amongst hemipterans than previously thought, prompting studies to examine the mechanisms of secondary chemical accumulation and evolution of aposematism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10886-026-01730-6.Keywords: Bitta alipes, Aposematism, Inconspicuous coloration, Passiflora, Toxicity, Synthesis, Cyanogen accumulation

Introduction

Animals from diverse clades have evolved multi-component defenses against predators (Exnerová et al. 2023; Hamalainen et al. 2024; Kikuchi et al. 2021; Rojas et al. 2017). In aposematic species for example, both warning coloration and toxicity can act synergistically to facilitate rapid learning of prey avoidance by predators (Caro and Ruxton 2019; Speed and Ruxton 2005). However, the ability to accumulate toxic compounds and the production of conspicuous warning signals are distinct processes that are likely to evolve asynchronously (Kikuchi et al. 2021).

The accumulation of toxic compounds in the body may occur upon metabolism of diets, and may provide some level of protection from predators, yet this can occur passively in many species and may not be associated with conspicuous anti-predator signals (Lindström 1999). Indeed, recent studies from moths (Nokelainen et al. 2024) and frogs (Coleman et al. 2025; Tarvin et al. 2024) reveal that many toxic species may not have conspicuous features that signal unpalatability. However, once an organism is chemically defended, co-evolutionary processes between predators and prey may lead to enhanced anti-predator signals (Kikuchi et al. 2021), but not always (Wang 2011). For example, signals associated with Batesian mimicry complexes such as conspicuous coloration, morphology or behavior that are associated with toxic prey may facilitate predator learning to avoid those prey items (Drinkwater et al. 2022; Umbers et al. 2019). While Batesian mimicry examples abound (Kikuchi et al. 2021), there are also brightly colored, non-toxic species without sympatric models (Betzner et al. 2025; Pfennig and Mullen 2010; Winters et al. 2018). The anti-predator effects of highly conspicuous and chemically defended organisms are the focus of many studies, yet understanding the toxicity of less conspicuous related species may allow us to uncover the evolutionary processes that shape multicomponent anti-predatory behavior.

Cyanogenic chemical compounds trigger aversive taste responses. Toxic hydrogen cyanide gas is released when tissues are disrupted; this gas is considered a potent and effective signal that prevents or deters consumption (Smiley and Morrison 2020). Yet a diverse group of arthropod taxa have evolved mechanisms to detoxify cyanide from their food including mites (Dixit et al. 2022; Wybouw et al. 2014), white flies (Easson et al. 2021), butterfly and moth larvae (Morrison et al. 2023; van Ohlen et al. 2016), millipedes and centipedes (Zagrobelny et al. 2018b). Further, some arthropods have evolved the ability to defend themselves against natural enemies by accumulating cyanogenic compounds from their food into their tissues via sequestration and de novo synthesis (Engler-Chaouat and Gilbert 2007; Pinheiro de Castro et al. 2019; Zagrobelny et al. 2007b, 2018a). Despite the relatively high diversity of insects relative to other groups, cyanogenic compounds have been documented in only three insect orders, with most cases documented in Lepidoptera, and few cases in Coleoptera and Hemiptera (Table 1).

A small number of insect herbivores specialize exclusively on cyanogenic glucoside-rich Passiflora (Passifloraceae) (Benson et al. 1975; Morrison 2023; Pinheiro de Castro et al. 2019; Smiley and Morrison 2020). Passiflora specialists that have been assayed for cyanide release have been found to assimilate cyanogenic glucosides via combinations of direct sequestration and de novo synthesis via precursors present in host plants (Engler-Chaouat and Gilbert 2007; Pinheiro de Castro et al. 2019). For Heliconius butterflies that specialize on Passiflora, cyanogenic glucoside sequestration reinforces their aposematic coloration as an effective signal to deter avian predators (Chai 1990; Finkbeiner et al. 2014). Yet very little is known about other Passiflora specialist insects, particularly in the order Hemiptera which have been relatively understudied. For example, only four species from three genera in the order Hemiptera (comprising approximately 100,000 species) are known to accumulate cyanogenic compounds (Aldrich et al. 1990a, b; Braekman et al. 1982; Kamo et al. 2012).

Multiple species of leaf-footed bugs (Hemiptera: Coreidae) are specialists on Passiflora, yet no studies to date have examined the presence of cyanogenic compounds in their tissues. Specifically, the matador bug, Bitta alipes (Hemiptera: Coreidae: Aniscocelini) (= Anisoscelis alipes, see Leavengood Jr et al. 2024; Fig. 1), specializes on cyanogenic Passiflora shoots, fruits, and flowers (Schaefer and Mitchell 1983). The brightly-colored flags on their hind legs serve as warning signals directed towards avian and arthropod predators, and they are an emerging model to study the dynamics of aposematism relative to other life history traits in hemimetabolous insects (Evans-Blake et al. 2026; Longbottom et al. 2022; Rubin et al. 2024). Indeed, Aldrich (1988) remarked on the aposematic nature of matador bugs and proposed that they may have cyanogenic compounds that may act in synergy with their warning coloration, but no studies have pursued these speculations.

While matador bugs are avoided by birds (Rubin et al. 2024), many Coreidae species are consumed by avian predators (Beal 1915, 1918; Chapin 1925; Schlee 1986). Moreover, many coreids do not possess obvious, or conspicuous, coloration to a human observer. Instead, they are often non-conspicuous or blend into their surroundings (Reguera and Gomendio 1999). However, dull or inconspicuous coloration does not mean that an organism lacks chemical defenses, which are common in the Coreidae (Aldrich 1988; Aldrich and Yonke 1975; Eliyahu et al. 2012; Prudic et al. 2008; Remold 1963; Waterhouse and Gilby 1964; Whitehead et al. 2014).

We assayed cyanide release as a proxy for cyanogenic compound content in the matador bug, and three closely related species from four tribes in the subfamily Coreinae to assess if the ability to accumulate cyanogenic compounds is specific to the matador bug, present more broadly in the subfamily, or absent from all species sampled. The three other species measured here are not known to be aposematic and do not specialize on known cyanogenic host plants. Thus, we hypothesized that the matador bug, as a Passiflora specialist, would accumulate cyanogenic compounds, but closely related apparently non-aposematic species would not. The three related species were Chelinidea vitteger (Chelinideini), Narnia femorata (Anisoscelini), and Acanthocephala terminalis (Acanthocephalini) (Fig. 1). Chelinidea vitteger and N. femorata specialize exclusively on prickly pear cactus (Opuntia spp., Cactaceae), except for one population of N. femorata which also feeds on thistle (Cirsium, Asteraceae; Cirino et al. 2024). Yaupon (Ilex vomitoria, Aquifoliaceae) is the host plant on which A. terminalis was collected for this study, although it feeds on many hosts from several families across its distribution and individuals can consume multiple plant species in their lifetimes (McPherson et al. 2011). All Coreidae species studied here feed by piercing host plant tissues with their rostrum to extract sap or other nutrient-containing fluids. Given that these three non-conspicuously colored species do not specialize on cyanogenic host plants, we predicted that they do not accumulate cyanogenic compounds.

Materials and Methods

Matador bug adults and nymphs were collected at the La Selva Biological Reserve throughout 2022. La Selva is a protected tropical wet forest in Heredia Province, Costa Rica operated by the Organization for Tropical Studies (10.430334, –84.007149; 35 to 150 m.a.s.l.). There are 13 species of Passiflora present at the reserve (O. Vargas, personal communication), seven of which are known matador bug host plants in Costa Rica and Panamá (P. ambigua, P. auriculata, P. biflora, P. costaricensis, P. oerstedii, P. quadrangularis, and P. pittieri) (Gilbert and Morrison personal observations). Putatively non-cyanogenic species in the Coreinae were collected in south and central Texas in 2024. All samples were collected alive, were starved for 24 h so that plant material could pass through their digestive systems, and frozen at −32 °C until extraction at The University of Texas at Austin, Brackenridge Field Laboratory. Multiple bugs were assayed for some Passiflora species. Voucher specimens from the same collection of each coreid species assayed for cyanogenic compounds were deposited in the University of Texas at Austin Insect Collection (Table S1 for accession numbers and metadata). Host plants for the non-aposematic coreid species (Opuntia engelmannii for N. femorata and C. vittiger, Ilex vomitoria for A. terminalis) were collected at the same time as the putatively non-cyanogenic coreid species, freeze dried, and assayed for cyanide release.

Extraction followed a published protocol (Arias et al. 2016) that is based on the Lambert procedure (Lambert et al. 1975) which uses cyanide release from tissue samples as a proxy for content of cyanogenic compounds in the tissue; the procedure cannot differentiate the identity of cyanogenic compounds present in samples. Whole-body bug samples were homogenized in 4 mL of 0.1 M phosphoric acid and shaken at 100 rpm for 1 h (bug dry mass range = 10–332 mg). The solution was filtered through Pasteur pipettes with cotton filters. The filtrate was mixed with 2 mL of 5 M sulfuric acid and boiled in a water bath for 1 h to hydrolyze the cyanogenic compound(s) for release of hydrogen cyanide. Next, 5 mL of ice-cold 5 M sodium hydroxide was added to the hot solutions to convert hydrogen cyanide into sodium cyanide (NaOH + HCN → NaCN + H2O). Reactions were allowed to proceed for 1 h, in an ice bath. After removing the samples from the ice bath, 125 μL of the reaction were mixed with 875 μL of 0.2 M phosphate buffer, pH 6, followed by 400 μL of N-chlorosuccinimide solution. Samples were allowed to oxidize for 20 min. Finally, 1.6 mL of a chromogenic pyridine-barbituric acid solution was added to the samples to cause color change. Sample absorbance optical density was measured at 580 nm on a DU-520 UV/Vis spectrophotometer (Beckman; Indiana, USA) in 1 mL cuvettes against a blank sample that only contained the reagents used in the reaction; four technical replicates were run per sample and averaged. Cyanogen content of the samples was quantified using a serial sodium cyanide calibration curve with a concentration gradient ranging from 0 to 5 μg NaCN/mL (R2 = 0.97–0.98); 0.01 μg NaCN was the limit of detection; sodium cyanide analytical standard was provided by Sigma Aldrich (Missouri, USA). To assay cyanogenic compound content of the non-Passiflora host plants, we analyzed ~ 50 mg tissue samples from N = 7 O. engelmannii and N = 6 I. vomitoria individuals collected from sites in Goliad and Travis County Texas where the coreids that we sampled were feeding. We express results as cyanide released per dry mass of tissue samples (µg/mg) and as molar equivalents (µmol/g) using the molecular weight of cyanide (CN⁻, 26.02 g/mol).

We use the term ‘sequester’ here to describe the passing of chemical compounds directly across the gut wall where they are stored in specialized cells, tissues or glands and the term ‘synthesis’ to define the process of internally producing chemicals, though the precursor compounds may have been acquired from the diet (Beran and Petschenka 2022; Opitz and Müller 2009); we refer to both processes generically with the term ‘accumulation’ to convey that chemical compounds are present in an organism, regardless of the mechanism(s) that led to their accumulation. We use ‘non-aposematic’ to refer to species lacking conspicuous, high-contrast coloration to human observers, rather than as a quantitatively defined signal trait. Data visualization was conducted in R version 4.1.1 (R Core Team, 2025) using the package ggplot2 (Wickham and Wickham 2016), data wrangling was done with the package dplyr (Wickham and François 2015).

Results

The whole-body cyanogen concentration of matador bugs varied considerably among individuals collected from six Passiflora host species (Fig. 2B). Notably, no cyanogenic compounds were found in any matador bug individuals collected from P. ambigua (N = 8), yet significant cyanogen concentrations were found in individuals collected from the other five Passiflora host plants. The average concentration of the matador bug on host plants where it accumulated cyanogenic compounds was 0.40 μg/mg ± 0.11 standard error (S.E.), and it was 0.17 μg/mg ± 0.08 S.E. if individuals feeding on P. ambigua were included.

Whole-body cyanogen colorimetric analysis revealed variation in concentration across four species of the Coreidae sampled: Acanthocephala terminalis (N = 3), Bitta alipes (N = 14); Chelinidea vittiger (N = 3), and Narnia femorata (N = 3) A. Colorimetric analysis revealed cyanogens in yaupon (Ilex vomitoria), the host plant for A. terminalis and prickly pear (Opuntia engelmannii), the host plant for N. femorata and C. vittiger C. Large dots with bars represent means and standard errors

The average cyanogen concentrations for the non-Passiflora feeding species were 0.37 µg/mg ± 0.17 S.E. for A. terminalis, 0.50 µg/mg ± 0.22 S.E. for C. vittiger, and the highest concentration, 0.76 µg/mg ± 0.08 S.E., was found in N. femorata (Fig. 2A). The non-Passiflora feeding species had a higher concentration of cyanogenic compounds in their tissues, on average, compared to the matador bug (Fig. 2A). Despite small sample numbers, the large S.E. values reported above show that intraspecific variation was considerable among the non-Passiflora feeding coreids. Opuntia engelmannii, the host plant for N. femorata and C. vittiger, had an average cyanogen concentration of 0.54 µg/mg ± 0.13 S.E. (N = 7; Fig. 2C). Ilex vomitoria, the host plant that A. terminalis was collected on, had an average concentration of 1.42 µg/mg ± 0.33 S.E. (N = 6; Fig. 2C). Discussion

All arthropods with cyanogenic compounds in their tissues ultimately derive it from their food and most of them are known to acquire it from cyanogenic host plants (Pinheiro de Castro et al. 2019; Zagrobelny et al. 2018a). Some insects directly sequester cyanogenic compounds from their host plants, but growing evidence suggests that many both sequester and metabolize host-derived cyanogens which are subsequently re-synthesized into novel cyanogenic compounds (Engler-Chaouat and Gilbert 2007; Pinheiro de Castro et al. 2019; Zagrobelny et al. 2008). The matador bug is the first documented case of cyanogenic compound accumulation in a species of Coreidae, supporting our hypothesis that it can accumulate the molecule from its cyanogenic Passiflora host plants. This finding is consistent with the avoidance of these insects by wild insectivorous birds and provides a biochemical basis for the observation that naïve birds learn to avoid matador bugs (Rubin et al. 2024).

Unexpectedly, all other coreid species investigated contained cyanogenic compounds in their tissues at comparable levels to matador bugs. Also unexpectedly, their host plants contained cyanogens. The I. vomitoria and O. engelmannii cyanogen concentration range of 0.3–2.4 µg/mg reported here were lower than most Passiflora species and Lotus (Fabaceae) species that are used by Heliconiinae or Zygaena caterpillars to acquire cyanogenic compounds, but present at comparable magnitudes (e.g., 0.1–10.0 µg/mg) (Engler-Chaouat and Gilbert 2007; Morrison et al. 2023; Smiley and Morrison 2020; Zagrobelny et al. 2007a). To our knowledge, this is the first report of cyanogenesis in the Cactaceae and Aquifoliaceae. These findings demonstrate that these coreid species accumulated cyanogenic compounds from their host plants by direct sequestration and/or de novo synthesis using precursor compounds that they consumed. The discovery of three non-aposematic coreids accumulating cyanogens across three tribes within a subfamily suggests that cyanogenic compound acquisition may be shared more broadly across the Coreidae phylogeny. This discovery more than doubles the number of Hemiptera genera known to accumulate cyanogenic compounds from three to seven and lays a foundation for future studies to investigate the precise mechanism(s) of cyanogen accumulation in matador bugs, other Passiflora specialist coreids including putative ‘wasp mimics’ (e.g., Holhymenia), and coreids from other subfamilies which feed on multiple host plants. Future studies employing standard liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry instruments, with internal cyanogenic compound standards (e.g., linamarin, prunasin, dhurrin), would be able to infer the mechanism of cyanogen accumulation from host plants (e.g., Sculfort et al. 2020).

Accumulation of cyanogenic compounds from host plants is generally understudied in hemipterans. The ability was only known from the Aphidae (Kamo et al. 2012), and Rhopalidae (Aldrich et al. 1990a, b; Braekman et al. 1982; Davis and Nahrstedt 1985; Kamo et al. 2012; Nahrstedt 1988) until now. Cardiospermin is a cyanogenic glucoside synthesized by Leptocoris isolata (Rhopalidae) from fruit of its host plant Allophylus cobbe (Sapindaceae), which does not contain cardiospermin (Nahrstedt 1988). Future studies may use dissection and chemical analyses of hemolymph, scent glands, and other body tissues to identify where coreids store cyanogens and which specific compounds, sequestration or synthesis pathways are used to co-opt plant chemical compounds for defense.

The matador bugs collected from P. ambigua had no detectable cyanogens, unlike those collected from five other host species. Passiflora ambigua is known to be cyanogenic, thus the lack of detectable cyanogenic compounds in matador bugs is most likely reflective of host-plant dependent accumulation. Yet, the reason for the lack of cyanogen accumulation in matador bugs on P. ambigua, and not those collected from other Passiflora species examined, remains an open question for future studies. Population-level variation in toxicity can disrupt predator foraging behavior because prey palatability is unpredictable (Hossie et al. 2021; Prudic et al. 2019). Similar intraspecific variation exists in Heliconiinae butterfly and Zygaena moth species that accumulate cyanogenic glucosides (Chai 1990; Morrison et al. 2023; Zagrobelny et al. 2007a), as well as a diversity of insects from other orders (Agrawal 2004; Pasteels et al. 1995; Sword 1999). We predict that variation in toxin accumulation is common in Coreidae species. One N. femorata population exists entirely on thistle, which is the first observation of this species consuming and reproducing on a non-Opuntia host (Cirino et al. 2024). Comparing cyanogen content of the thistle-feeding N. femorata population which we predict does not contain cyanogenic compounds, with Opuntia-feeding populations would illuminate the extent of intraspecific variation in cyanogen synthesis possible for hemipterans. Similarly, the generalist A. terminalis, which feeds on host plants in many plant families across the eastern U.S.A. (McPherson et al. 2011), offers another opportunity for population-level cyanogenic compound accumulation study. Opuntia species, I. vomitoria, and Centaurea (another thistle genus) contain N-containing alkaloids which may be precursors for coreid cyanogen synthesis (Morrison and Sedio, unpublished data), possibly in addition to sequestration. It is also possible that these species accumulate secondary defensive compounds from host plants besides cyanogens. Thus, the Coreidae provide an exciting model to understand insect chemical ecology within populations that vary in host plant usage, and across species on the generalist-specialist diet spectrum.

The cyanogen concentrations we measured in coreids (0.1–1.0 µg/mg) are relatively low compared to Heliconius butterflies, Zygaena moths, and other arthropods which accumulate cyanogenic compounds (1.0–10.0 µg/mg), but are well within a range that can be unpalatable, or lethal, to naïve vertebrate predators (Pinheiro de Castro et al. 2019; Zagrobelny et al. 2018a). Intraspecific variation in cyanogenic compound concentration and warning coloration may influence predator learning. Birds attack and consume apparently dull-colored coreid species in Leptoglossus and Acanthocephala (Beal 1915, 1918; Chapin 1925), but avoid aposematic matador bugs (Evans-Blake et al. 2026; Rubin et al. 2024). In addition, European blackbirds prefer non-aposematic Heteroptera species (Coreidae, Pentatomidae, Pyrrhocoridae) over aposematic ones (Schlee 1986). Variation in palatability among individuals with certain color or morphological phenotypes may make it difficult for some visual predators to learn which individuals to avoid attacking (Skelhorn and Rowe 2006; Taylor et al. 2016). Other chemical compounds in Coreidae which are exuded from thoracic or abdominal glands in some species may contribute to their unpalatability (Aldrich and Yonke 1975; Waterhouse and Gilby 1964).

While conspicuous warning signals are often associated with chemical defense (Kikuchi et al. 2021), our results emphasize that toxicity can occur in the absence of overt aposematic coloration (Rowland et al. 2017). We interpret cyanogen accumulation in these inconspicuous coreids as a general defensive trait that may reduce predation risk irrespective of visual signaling. The absence of strong warning coloration does not preclude defensive value, particularly if predator responses depend on learning, sampling frequency, and community context (Rowland et al. 2017; Skelhorn and Rowe 2006), rely on cues other than color (Rojas et al. 2018), or if chemical defenses function in combination with other traits (Hamalainen et al. 2024). A more detailed evaluation of the evolutionary drivers of this pattern is beyond the scope of this study, but our findings highlight the need to consider chemically defended yet visually inconspicuous species in broader discussions of predator–prey interactions.

Arthropod predator–prey interactions are rarely determined by the presence of one chemical compound, but rather complex mixtures of compounds which act synergistically to deter or mitigate natural enemies (Richards et al. 2015; Wetzel and Whitehead 2020). More characterization of non-model arthropod chemical composition will provide a deeper understanding about how dietary variation affects resource-consumer-enemy dynamics (Abdala-Roberts et al. 2019; Sedio 2017). The unexpected results reported here indicate that more hemipterans, and insects in general, may be defending themselves with food plant-derived cyanogenic compounds than thought previously. We advocate for chemical surveys of more non-aposematic arthropods, along with host plants not known to be cyanogenic, because the paradigm in which an organism’s palatability spectrum correlates with its degree of warning coloration is more nuanced than typically appreciated (Coleman et al. 2025; Despland 2005; Drinkwater et al. 2022; Dyer 1995; Lehmann et al. 2014; Medina et al. 2020; Nokelainen et al. 2024; Pinheiro de Castro et al. 2019; Tarvin et al. 2024; Umbers et al. 2017).
