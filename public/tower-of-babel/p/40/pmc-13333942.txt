High-temperature electrical performance and interface adhesion performance of SiC-based Pt thin-film RTDs
Authors: Ziyan Fang, Xudong Fang, Chen Wu, Hao Sun, Yong Zhang, Zhongkai Zhang, Bian Tian, Hai Yuan, Prateek Verma, Ryutaro Maeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeveloping high-performance SiC-based platinum thin-film resistance temperature detectors (RTDs) capable of stable operation over 800 °C in air, with superior electrical performance and interfacial mechanical stability, is crucial for improving the high-temperature measurement accuracy of silicon carbide pressure sensors. Therefore, this study investigated the influence of adhesion layer materials and annealing conditions on their high-temperature electrical performances, including the temperature coefficient of resistance (TCR), consistency across multiple tests, electrical stability, and adhesion strength to the SiC substrate. More importantly, two different types of protective layers were introduced to suppress Pt film agglomeration at high temperatures and improve the high-temperature stability of the RTDs. The results showed that Pt thin-film resistors with tantalum (Ta) and aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) adhesion layers maintained functionality in air up to ~855 °C, demonstrating good linearity and high TCR. At 800 °C, the resistance drift rates of Pt thin-film RTDs with Ta and Al₂O₃ adhesion layers were 0.538%/h and 4.159%/h, respectively. With the addition of a yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) protective layer, these rates decreased to 0.291%/h and 0.146%/h, significantly enhancing stability. The critical load (Lc₂) of the Pt thin-film on the SiC substrate with a Ta adhesion layer was 45.65 mN, which decreased to 11.96 mN after annealing. In contrast, for the Pt thin film with an Al₂O₃ adhesion layer, the critical load (Lc₂) increased from 13.13 mN to 16.46 mN after annealing. The investigation of electrical performance and interface adhesion performance at high temperature in this work provided a reliable combination of adhesion layers for developing SiC-based temperature and pressure integrated sensors applied in extreme environments. Subject terms: Sensors, Structural properties

Introduction

Silicon carbide (SiC) high-temperature pressure sensors have been studied due to their excellent mechanical and electrical properties at elevated temperatures in extreme environments. They have high potential to be applied in aerospace, aircraft engines, oil industries, etc. However, significant signal drift limits measurement accuracy. Integrating a temperature sensor on the SiC pressure sensor chip enables real-time temperature monitoring and compensation. Moreover, integrating the temperature sensor with the pressure sensor on one chip allows for simultaneous monitoring of both temperature and pressure parameters, which facilitates the integration and miniaturization of multi-sensors. Platinum (Pt) resistors, due to their excellent high-temperature oxidation resistance, good linearity, and compatibility with MEMS processes1–3, are preferred here for integrating on SiC pressure sensor chips to monitor the temperature at elevated temperature.

The application of Pt film temperature resistors at elevated temperatures was reported in some studies4,5. However, the fabrication methods used in this work are incompatible with MEMS processes, making it impossible to develop integrated, miniaturized chip-level sensors. Moreover, the Pt film RTDs produced in these studies feature micron-scale thick films, which exhibited different high-temperature electrical properties compared to the nanometer-scale Pt thin films fabricated using MEMS processes. While the research on temperature detection using nano-scale Pt thin films has primarily focused on low-temperature applications. Schössler et al. used Pt as the resistor material to fabricate microheaters based on a silicon (Si) wafer and tested their long-term stability in the range of 140-290 °C6. However, the performance at higher temperatures remains unknown. Shao, Lei et al. fabricated a Pt thin-film RTD on a nickel-based Hastelloy strip, with the temperature up to 550 °C7. Huang, Mi-Mi et al. used MEMS technology to fabricate Pt thin-film temperature sensor chips on a Si substrate with titanium (Ti) as the adhesion layer and evaluated their electrical performance over a temperature range of −30 °C to 150 °C. The temperature coefficient of resistance (TCR) of the Pt thin-film temperature sensor reached 3.14 × 10⁻³/°C after annealing, the hysteresis of 0.2% FS and an accuracy of 0.32% FS8. Kandpal, Manoj et al. developed a chromium oxide (Cr₂O₃) intermediate layer as an adhesion layer to fabricate a Pt thin-film RTD, which exhibited a TCR of 2.855 × 10⁻³/°C and an accuracy of 0.17% within 0-150 °C9. These representative studies demonstrate that the application of Pt thin-film RTDs in low-temperature fields is well established. However, their use in high-temperature (>600 °C) environments remains limited. One major limitation is the poor high-temperature stability of commonly used adhesion layers such as titanium (Ti) and chromium (Cr). To address this issue, researchers have proposed high-temperature-resistant metals, including tantalum (Ta), hafnium (Hf), zirconium (Zr), and iridium (Ir), as well as metal oxides like titanium dioxide (TiO₂) and Cr₂O₃, as adhesion layer materials for Pt thin films. Ciftyürek Engin et al. investigated the microstructure of Pt films with different adhesion layers (Ti, Ta, Zr, and Hf) at 800 °C and 1200 °C; however, the electrical performance of Pt films at high temperatures was not examined10. Ababneh A. et al. studied various adhesion layers, including Ti, Ta, aluminum nitride (AlN), aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃), and TiO₂, as well as their cross-sectional morphology after high-temperature treatment at 800 °C11. Their findings suggested that Al₂O₃ and TiO₂ hold potential for high-temperature applications12.

The microstructure of Pt thin films with high-temperature-resistant metals and metal oxides as adhesion layers has been preliminarily evaluated in some studies; however, their electrical performance at high temperatures remains unknown. The electrical and mechanical properties of SiC-based Pt thin-film RTDs at 800 °C and above in air are critical to their reliability in extreme environments and are influenced by the adhesion layer material and annealing conditions. To develop a SiC-based Pt thin-film RTD suitable for applications at 800 °C and above in air, this study investigated the effects of adhesion layer materials and annealing conditions on the high-temperature electrical performance, including TCR, three-test consistency, and high-temperature stability of the RTD. To suppress the agglomeration of Pt films at high temperatures, two different types of protective layers, aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) and yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ), were introduced, and their effects on improving the high-temperature performance of Pt thin-film RTDs were investigated. Additionally, the impact of the adhesion layer on Pt film-substrate interface adhesion was assessed, laying the foundation for enhancing the reliability of SiC-based Pt thin-film RTDs.

Materials and methods

The Pt thin-film resistors fabricated on the SiC substrate consisted of a multilayer thin-film system, including an insulating layer, an adhesion layer, and a Pt layer, and were designed with a serpentine structure featuring a 10 µm wide resistor strip. These resistors were fabricated using the MEMS process, as shown in Fig. 1. The substrates were double-side polished (CMP), n-type 4H-SiC wafers (TankeBlue Semiconductor Co., Ltd.) with a thickness of 350 ± 15 μm, a resistivity of 0.015–0.025 Ω cm, and a root-mean-square (RMS) surface roughness of 0.2 nm (see Supplementary Fig. S3). The adhesion layers used in this study included titanium-titanium nitride (Ti -TiN), TiO₂, Ta, and Al₂O₃. Since the metal adhesion layers (Ti-TiN, Ta) and the semiconductor adhesion layer (TiO₂) are conductive, a 300 nm silicon dioxide (SiO₂) layer was deposited on the SiC substrate by Plasma-Enhanced Chemical Vapor Deposition (PECVD) to achieve electrical isolation from the SiC substrate, as shown in Fig. 1a1. For the highly insulating Al₂O₃, it was directly deposited onto the SiC substrate, as shown in Fig. 1, flow 2. Figure 1b1, b2 show the photolithographically patterned resistor structure, with negative photoresist Rol 7133 used to form an inverted T-shaped cross-section. Magnetron sputtering was then used for the adhesion layer/Pt thin film deposition without breaking the vacuum. The purity of the sputtering targets (Ti, TiN, TiO₂, Ta, and Al₂O₃) was 99.99%. The DC magnetron sputtering current for Ti was 0.5 A, the RF magnetron sputtering power for TiN was 220 W, the RF magnetron sputtering power for TiO₂ was 235 W, the DC magnetron sputtering current for Ta was 0.3 A, the RF magnetron sputtering power for Al₂O₃ was 150 W, and the DC magnetron sputtering current for Pt was 0.3 A. The argon gas flow rate was 60 sccm during sputtering. Regarding the film thicknesses, during the initial preparation of the samples (High-Temperature Electrical Performance of Pt Thin-Film Resistors: Optimization of Adhesion Layer Materials and Annealing Atmosphere), the deposition parameters were empirically set with target thicknesses of approximately 30 nm for the adhesion layers and 300 nm for the Pt film. Notable variations in the actual thickness of the adhesion layers were subsequently observed among the different materials. To ensure accuracy in the subsequent quantitative analysis, precise thickness measurements were performed for the key systems. The measured thicknesses were 90.313 nm/306.17 nm for Ta/Pt and 8.36 nm/284.39 nm for Al₂O₃/Pt (The Effect of Annealing Temperature on TCR and Repeatability of the Pt Thin-Film RTDs). All analyses correlating performance with mechanisms in this study are based on these measured thickness values.

The process flow diagram of SiC-based Pt thin-film RTDs

Finally, acetone was used for ultrasonic stripping, followed by rinsing the wafer with deionized water, as shown in Fig. 1c1, c2. The processed wafer was diced into individual chips, as shown in Fig. 1d, and then annealed in a tube furnace, as shown in Fig. 1e. The prepared SiC-based Pt thin-film RTDs were then tested at high temperatures with the setup shown in Fig. 2. A programmable high-temperature furnace (SXC-2-13G, Hangzhou Blue Sky Instrument Co., Ltd., China) was used for building the high-temperature testing environment. An external thermocouple monitored the temperature of the furnace in real-time. The resistance and temperature were recorded using the DAQ 6510 data acquisition and multimeter system (DAQ 6510, Tektronix, USA).

Result and Discussion

High-temperature electrical performance of pt thin-film resistors: optimization of adhesion layer materials and annealing atmosphere

In this study, Pt thin-film RTDs were fabricated with four different adhesion layers: Ti-TiN, TiO₂, Ta, and Al₂O₃. Since stress is inevitably induced during the Pt thin film growing process, high-temperature annealing is necessary. However, the adhesion layers are highly sensitive to the annealing atmosphere, leading to significant variations in the high-temperature performance of Pt thin-film resistors. Therefore, optimizing the annealing atmosphere is essential. These four groups of samples were annealed at 950 °C under different atmospheres, with their corresponding sample numbers listed in Table 1. Subsequently, their high-temperature resistance performance was evaluated.

The corresponding actual temperatures were approximately 20 °C, 122 °C, 238 °C, 343 °C, 446 °C, 548 °C, 650 °C, 753 °C, and 855 °C. Figure 3a shows the variation of the normalized resistance of the Pt resistors as a function of temperature and time. A resistor was considered damaged when its resistance suddenly increased to infinity (exceeding the measurement range of the multimeter system: 10 µΩ–120 MΩ)13. From Fig. 3a, it can be seen that the resistance of the Ti–TiN–Ar sample tends to infinity at 828 °C, indicating the resistor failure. However, at 546 °C, the resistance begins to decrease, and as the temperature increases further, there is no significant rise in resistance, suggesting that the resistor loses its sensitivity to temperature. Similarly, the resistance of the TiO2-Ar sample tends to infinity after 692 °C. However, at 546 °C, the resistance decreases, and its electrical performance starts to degrade. The resistance of the Ti-TiN-N2 sample tends to infinity after 821 °C. The Ti-TiN-Air sample shows a decrease in resistance after 752 °C, and the resistance maintains almost unchanged with further heating. Therefore, although the Ti-TiN-Air sample does not fail after 752 °C, it loses its temperature sensitivity. Unlike the aforementioned samples, the TiO₂-Air, TiO₂-N₂, Ta-N₂, and Al₂O₃-Air samples continue to function normally at 855 °C. Owing to the temperature limitation of the conductive silver paste used for signal lead-out, these samples were not tested at higher temperatures. Therefore, their maximum operating temperature is at least 855 °C. Figure 3b shows the temperature-resistance curves of the Pt thin-film resistance samples. Among the TiO₂-Air, TiO₂-N₂, Ta-N₂, and Al₂O₃-Air samples, the TiO₂-Air, Ta-N₂, and Al₂O₃-Air samples exhibit the best linearity. The temperature-resistance data from Fig. 3b were extracted and subjected to linear fitting using the least squares method, after which the temperature coefficient of resistance (TCR)14 was calculated. Figure 3c compares the TCR and maximum working temperature of the samples. The maximum working temperature of the TiO₂-Air, TiO₂-N₂, Ta-N₂, and Al₂O₃-Air samples was identical at 855 °C. However, the Ta-N₂ and Al₂O₃-Air samples exhibited higher TCR values than the TiO₂-Air and TiO₂-N₂ samples. Therefore, we deduced that Pt thin-film RTDs with Ta and Al₂O₃ as adhesion layers, annealed in nitrogen and air, respectively, exhibited the best high-temperature electrical performance. Their maximum working temperature exceeded 855 °C, while also demonstrating higher sensitivity and better linearity. Therefore, in the subsequent experiments, Pt thin-film RTDs with these two combinations were used.

The effect of annealing temperature on TCR and repeatability of the Pt Thin-Film RTDs

The kinetic evolution of Pt film particles and the interlayer thermal diffusion between the adhesion layer and the Pt film under high-temperature atmospheric conditions will directly determine the repeatability and sensitivity of the Pt thin-film RTDs. To optimize sensitivity of the Pt thin-film RTDs and improve the consistency of multiple tests and high-temperature stability, the effect of annealing temperature on the electrical performance of the Pt thin-film RTDs was studied. The changes in electrical performance with temperature were revealed from the perspective of surface thermodynamic evolution and interlayer thermal diffusion in the Pt film. The Ta-N₂ and Al₂O₃-Air samples were annealed at temperatures of 650 °C, 750 °C, 850 °C, and 950 °C, respectively, and the corresponding sample numbers are listed in Table 2. Subsequently, temperature-resistance (R-T) tests were conducted on these samples. Each sample was tested three times, and the average TCR value is annotated in the figures. Notably, the resistance value of the Ta-N2(950°C) sample increased to the order of megaohms after the second test, indicating device failure. The TCR and maximum working temperature of the Ta-N2 series samples are compared, as shown in Fig. 4b. It can be observed that the TCR increases with the annealing temperature. The R-T curves of the Al₂O₃-Air series samples have been reported previously in our earlier work15. Figure 4c shows the comparison of TCR for the the Al₂O₃-Air series samples, revealing that the TCR tends to decrease as the annealing temperature increases. To elucidate the mechanism by which annealing temperature affects the TCR of Pt thin-film RTDs, it is necessary to comprehensively consider the evolution of Pt film grains at high temperatures and the diffusion dynamics of Pt atoms with adhesion layer atoms.

The evolution of Pt thin-film particles at high temperatures is closely related to film stress and the diffusion of surface atoms. The stress induced in the film by Ar ion bombardment during sputtering is released after high-temperature annealing, which reduces film defects16. Additionally, as the temperature increases, the Pt film grains undergo recrystallization and grain growth, leading to a reduction in grain boundaries. The decrease in defects and grain boundaries weakens grain boundary scattering, thereby reducing the resistivity of the Pt film and increasing the TCR. The high surface-to-volume ratio of the Pt film results in capillary instability, causing surface atom diffusion17, which is influenced by temperature, annealing atmosphere, the adhesion between the Pt film and the substrate, and impurity atoms18. The diffusion of Pt surface atoms alters the film morphology and, in severe cases, causes film agglomeration, leading to the formation of voids and islands. As shown in Fig. 5, particles appear on the Pt film surface, and the film gradually becomes rougher with the temperature rising. Although numerous particles were formed, only a few representative ones are marked in the figures for clarity. Holes started to appear after annealing at 950 °C. In contrast, in the Al₂O₃/Pt combination, voids began to form as early as 750 °C, and they grew larger with increasing annealing temperature, eventually leading to the formation of discontinuous islands. This phenomenon was studied in our previous work15. The formation of voids reduces the TCR of the Pt thin-film RTDs and, in severe cases, degrades its high-temperature electrical stability.

The interfacial diffusion kinetics between Pt film atoms and adhesion layer atoms is also a factor affecting the TCR. Figure 6a, b show the depth profiling by Auger Electron Spectroscopy (AES) of Ta/Pt before and after annealing in nitrogen at 950 °C for 1.5 hours, while Fig. 6c and 6d show the AES depth profiling of Al₂O₃/Pt before and after annealing in air at 950 °C for 1.5 hours. From Fig. 6a, b, the element distribution between the Pt and Ta layers was distinct before annealing. However, Ta atoms were detected within the Pt layer after annealing, indicating that Ta had diffused into the Pt layer. Figure 6c, d show that no Al atoms are detected in the Pt region, and no Pt is detected in the Al₂O₃ region. This suggests that there is no element diffusion between Pt and Al₂O₃, which was characterized in our previous study by Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)15. Figure 4d presents the comparison of TCR for the Ta-N₂ and Al₂O₃-Air series samples at different annealing temperatures, where the TCR values are the average of three tests. Since the Pt film thickness varied between the Ta-N₂ and Al₂O₃-Air series, and the thickness of the Pt film directly affects the TCR19, the TCR per unit thickness is defined here for comparison, i.e., TCR divided by the Pt film thickness (t), as shown in Fig. 4d. The TCR of the Al₂O₃/Pt combination was higher than that of the Ta/Pt combination. This indicates that the interlayer diffusion of Ta atoms into the Pt layer introduced impurity defects, reducing the TCR of the Pt thin-film RTDs. Additionally, the presence of Ta impurity atoms suppressed the migration of Pt grain boundaries, significantly slowing down the Pt agglomeration process and reducing the formation of voids18, which is described in Fig. 7. This explains why, compared to Al₂O₃/Pt, the formation of voids in the Ta/Pt combination occurred at a higher temperature.

In summary, the TCR of the Ta/Pt combination exhibited an increasing trend as the temperature rose. This is due to the competing effects between the recrystallization and grain growth of the Pt film and the partial diffusion of Ta atoms into the Pt film, which suppressed the surface diffusion of Pt atoms. In contrast, the TCR of the Al₂O₃/Pt combination decreased with the temperature. This is attributed to the combined effects of the recrystallization and grain growth of the Pt film, as well as the enhanced surface diffusion of Pt atoms at higher temperatures.

As shown in Fig. 4a, the Ta-N2(950 °C) sample reaches its maximum TCR but fails after the second test. We deduced that the optimal annealing temperature for the Ta/Pt combination was 850 °C, at which the TCR was 2.741 × 10⁻³/°C, with good consistency across three tests. For the Al₂O₃–Air series samples, an annealing temperature of 650 °C was identified as optimal, yielding both a higher TCR and better test consistency. At this temperature, the TCR reached 2.845 × 10⁻³/°C.

Evaluation of high-temperature stability and performance optimization of Pt thin-film RTDs

In practical application environments, the electrical stability of Pt thin-film RTDs at high temperatures determines durability and temperature measurement accuracy. As identified in “The Effect of Annealing Temperature on TCR and Repeatability of the Pt Thin-Film RTDs”, annealing at 850 °C in N₂ for Ta/Pt and at 650 °C in air for Al₂O₃/Pt was determined to be optimal in terms of TCR and measurement consistency. Thus, the Ta/Pt and Al₂O₃/Pt combinations prepared under these conditions were selected as test samples. These samples were subsequently held in air at 500 °C, 600 °C, 700 °C, and 800 °C for 1 h, and their resistance values were recorded. The test results are shown in Fig. 8a, d. A smaller αd indicates greater resistance stability at that temperature. The resistance drift rates of the two sample groups after holding for one hour at each temperature were calculated, as annotated in Fig. 8a, d. The resistance drift of the Al₂O₃/Pt combination was greater than that of the Ta/Pt combination, especially at 800 °C. This is primarily because the Al₂O₃/Pt combination underwent severe high-temperature agglomeration at 800 °C. Therefore, although the diffusion of Ta impurities into the Pt layer reduced the TCR of the Pt thin-film RTDs, it effectively suppressed Pt agglomeration and thereby enhanced the electrical stability. A quantitative comparison under their respective optimal conditions (Table 3) clearly reveals this trade-off: the 850 °C-annealed Ta/Pt system achieves an approximate 7.7-fold enhancement in stability (resistance drift rate reduced from 4.159%/h to 0.538%/h) at the cost of about a 3.7% reduction in TCR (from 2.845×10⁻³/°C to 2.741 × 10⁻³/°C). This quantitative relationship confirms that performance optimization via controlled Ta diffusion is not only feasible but highly advantageous. For integrated SiC sensors intended to operate above 800 °C, long-term stability becomes the paramount factor determining reliable operation. Unpredictable resistance drift leads directly to sensor failure, whereas the minor, predictable variation in TCR can be effectively compensated for through pre-calibration. Consequently, sacrificing a limited amount of TCR to achieve a substantial gain in stability is both rational and necessary for this application. The results of this study indicate that the 850 °C annealing process is key to attaining this optimal performance balance, establishing a clear and reproducible engineering process benchmark for the fabrication of related high-temperature sensor devices.

To further optimize the high-temperature electrical stability, measures must be taken to suppress Pt thin-film agglomeration, as well as high-temperature oxidation and volatilization. Therefore, a protective layer was introduced. Al₂O₃, as an excellent high-temperature-resistant metal oxide21, possesses good oxidation resistance22 and even maintains good insulation properties at 1000 °C, with an insulation resistance reaching 1 MΩ23,24. Due to these properties, Al₂O₃ is commonly used as a thermal barrier coating for turbine components25. Yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) has a high melting point (2690 °C), stable chemical properties, low oxygen permeability, and excellent ablation resistance26. These characteristics make it suitable for high-performance applications, particularly in aerospace engines27. Therefore, both Al₂O₃ and YSZ were selected as protective layer materials in this study. The thickness of the passivation layer was selected based on the trade-off between high-temperature stability and TCR reported in the literature13,21, which suggests an optimal range of approximately 50-80 nm. Accordingly, Al₂O₃ and YSZ layers were deposited via RF magnetron sputtering at 150 W (60 sccm Ar) and 250 W (20 sccm Ar), respectively. Cross-sectional SEM measurements confirmed that the resulting average layer thicknesses (62.52 nm and 61.42 nm, see Supplementary Fig. S2) fall within this optimal range. These protective layers were deposited on multiple Ta-Pt and Al₂O₃-Pt devices, respectively, which were labeled as Ta-Pt -Al₂O₃-N2(850°C), Ta-Pt-YSZ-N2(850°C), Al₂O₃-Pt -Al₂O₃ -Air(650°C), and Al₂O₃-Pt-YSZ-Air(650 °C). Subsequently, high-temperature stability tests were conducted on these four groups of samples to compare the effects of the two protective layers on the high-temperature stability of Pt thin-film RTDs, as shown in Fig. 8b, c, e, f. The presence of the protective layer significantly reduced the resistance drift rate of Pt thin-film RTDs at high temperatures (600 °C–800 °C). In the range of 600 °C–700 °C, the resistance drift rate of Pt thin-film resistors with an Al₂O₃ protective layer was lower than that of those with a YSZ protective layer. However, at 800 °C, it was higher than that of resistors with a YSZ protective layer. Al₂O₃, as an excellent high-temperature-resistant metal oxide material, exhibits minimal interfacial chemical reactions with Pt below 1200 °C1,28,29. However, in this work, the Pt thin-film resistor with an Al₂O₃ protective layer exhibited greater resistance drift at 800 °C compared to YSZ. This may be due to the increased degradation rate of Al₂O₃ at 800 °C, leading to a loss of structural integrity and a decline in oxidation resistance, as suggested by previous research30. Additionally, the incomplete Al₂O₃ layer may have penetrated into the grooves on the Pt surface, ultimately accelerating the agglomeration of the Pt thin film. Compared to Al₂O₃, YSZ was more suitable for high-temperature applications above 800 °C. This is substantiated by the resistance drift rates: at 800 °C, the Ta/Pt and Al₂O₃/Pt combinations showed drift rates of 0.538%/h and 4.159%/h, respectively. After adding a YSZ protective layer, the rates decreased significantly to 0.291%/h and 0.146%/h, demonstrating its superior stabilizing effect. However, during the heating process from 700 °C to 800 °C, the Pt thin-film RTDs with a YSZ protective layer exhibited some resistance noise signals, as shown in Fig. 8c. Based on literature analysis, this noise is likely attributable to a significant change in the insulation properties of the YSZ protective layer within the 700–800 °C temperature range. For the 8 mol% Y₂O₃-doped YSZ (8YSZ) employed in this study, which is an oxygen-ion conductor, the conductivity increases sharply above approximately 1000 K (~727 °C) due to enhanced oxygen vacancy migration31–33. For instance, it is reported that at 700 °C, the conductivity of 8YSZ is 0.0274 S/m, while at 800 °C, it rises to 3.4 S/m31,32. Although this conductivity remains vastly lower than that of the Pt film (~9.09 × 10⁶ S/m), this steeply varying parasitic conductivity can form an unstable parallel leakage path with the sensor, thereby introducing measurable resistance noise into the signal. For pressure–temperature sensors integrated on SiC chips and operating above 800 °C, the aforementioned resistance noise directly compromises the accuracy and stability of temperature readings, which may consequently interfere with pressure measurements relying on real-time temperature compensation. To enhance sensor reliability, future work may focus on the following optimizations: at the material level, exploring lower doping concentrations (e.g., ~3 mol% YSZ) could achieve lower ionic conductivity across a broad temperature range34; at the structural level, introducing an ultrathin and dense diffusion barrier layer (e.g., Al₂O₃) between the sensing element and the YSZ could be considered; at the system level, adaptive filtering algorithms could be employed for real-time signal processing. These strategies aim to mitigate noise either fundamentally or at the application level, reflecting the potential of this study to extend from fundamental observation toward practical engineering solutions.

The presence of the protective layer improved the overlap of the R-T test curves across three repetitions, indicating enhanced test repeatability of the Pt thin-film resistor. Evaluation of the adhesion performance between Pt thin film and the SiC substrate

The mechanical reliability of SiC-based Pt thin-film RTDs in high-temperature and high-velocity gas flow environments strongly depends on the adhesion performance between the Pt thin film and the substrate. Additionally, the yield of wire bonding for Pt thin-film RTDs is closely related to adhesion performance. Poor adhesion in the Pt thin-film pad makes wire bonding difficult, and even if bonding is successful, the Pt thin-film resistors are prone to detachment under high-temperature gas flow conditions, reducing the application reliability of the device. Therefore, it is essential to evaluate the adhesion of the Pt thin film on the SiC substrate.

Scratch testing, as a semi-quantitative technique, can be used to measure the adhesion performance of various coating-substrate systems35. In this work, adhesion was evaluated using a nano-scratch tester (HYSITRON TI950) equipped with a diamond indenter (tip radius: 4–6 µm, conical angle: 60°). The tests were conducted in force-controlled mode with a ramped load over a scratch length of 250 µm. The lateral force was recorded in real time during scratching. The tested samples included SiC/SiO₂/Ta/Pt and SiC/Al₂O₃/Pt, both in their as-deposited state and after annealing at 950 °C for 1.5 hours (in N₂ and air, respectively). For each of these four conditions, three independent samples were prepared and tested, with three scratch tests performed on each sample. The critical load of the film-substrate system, defined as the normal load at which a sudden increase in lateral force36, was determined from the lateral force versus normal load curves (see Fig. 10). To unambiguously identify the delaminated layer and elucidate the failure mechanism within the multilayer stack, the scratch tracks were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, GeminiSEM 500) and correlated with high-resolution energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping (see Fig. 11). Given the highly consistent mechanical responses and failure modes observed across all replicates for each condition, the detailed analysis presented herein—including representative load-displacement curves and EDS maps—follows the standard practice for critical load determination in multilayer films37–42.

The critical load is the force that causes a typical failure mode. There are two typical critical loads, Lc₁ and Lc₂. Lc₁ refers to the load at which the coating begins to delaminate, while Lc₂ represents the load required for continuous delamination or detachment of the coating. Generally, critical load (Lc₂) is considered the adhesion strength between the film and the substrate42–44. As shown in Fig. 10a, chipping appears at the edges of the scratch track when the scratch length reaches 56.19 µm. EDS analysis of the scratch revealed that Ta was not detected, while Si was detected at 56.19 µm, accompanied by the disappearance of the Pt element, indicating that the substrate had been exposed. Corresponding to Fig. 10a, at a scratch distance of 56.19 µm, a sudden increase occurs in the lateral force, with the corresponding normal force being 45.65 mN. Therefore, the critical load (Lc₂) between the Pt film and the substrate with the Ta adhesion layer in the as-deposited state was 45.65 mN. In Fig. 11b, when the scratch distance reaches 101.09 µm, the film peels off directly. EDS analysis of the scratch track revealed that no Ta element was detected, while the Si element was detected at 101.09 µm, accompanied by the disappearance of the Pt element, indicating that the substrate was exposed. Corresponding to Fig. 10b, at a scratch distance of 101.09 µm, the lateral force increases suddenly, with the corresponding normal force at that point being 11.96 mN. Therefore, the critical load (Lc₂) between the Pt film and the substrate was 11.96 mN after annealing for the Ta/Pt combination. From Fig. 11c, when the scratch distance reaches 115.29 µm, continuous chipping appears along the sides of the scratch track. EDS analysis of the scratch showed that the atomic percentage of Al was 0.43%, indicating that Al was almost absent. Instead, the Si element was detected, suggesting that the substrate had been exposed. Corresponding to Fig. 10c, at a scratch distance of 115.29 µm, the lateral force increases suddenly, with the corresponding normal force at that point being 13.13 mN. Therefore, the critical load (Lc₂) between the Pt film and the substrate with the Al₂O₃ adhesion layer in the as-deposited state was 13.13 mN. Similarly, from Figs. 10d and 11d, it can be determined that the critical load occurs at a scratch distance of 135.54 µm. Therefore, the critical load (Lc₂) between the Pt thin film and the SiC substrate after annealing for the Al₂O₃/Pt combination was 16.46 mN.

From the above analysis, it is evident that for the Ta/Pt combination, the critical load (Lc₂) between the Pt film and the SiC substrate before annealing was 45.65 mN, while after annealing, it decreased to 11.96 mN. As a good adhesion layer material, Ta provides strong adhesion between the Pt film and the SiC substrate in the as-deposited state. However, after high-temperature annealing, the critical load (Lc₂) decreased to 11.96 mN, making the Pt thin film highly prone to detachment from the substrate. The weakening of Ta adhesion is attributed to its oxidation. As shown in Fig. 6a, b, before annealing, a certain amount of oxygen is present in the Ta layer, with an atomic concentration of approximately 50%. After annealing, the atomic concentrations of Ta and O are about 20% and 50%, respectively, indicating that Ta oxidized, with the resulting oxide composition approaching the tantalum pentoxide (Ta₂O₅) equilibrium phase45. The formation of crystalline Ta₂O₅ was confirmed by XRD analysis (Supplementary Fig. S3), which shows a distinct diffraction peak at ~36.1° corresponding to the (116) plane of β-Ta₂O₅. Compared to Ta, Ta₂O₅ exhibits poorer adhesion, which has been demonstrated through a non-quantitative adhesion test method in the literature46. It was established through our bonding experiments that reliable wire bonding cannot be achieved with a critical load (Lc₂) below 11.96 mN (post-950 °C anneal). To overcome this limitation and ensure bonding yield and device reliability, the annealing process was optimized to 850 °C, a condition that consistently yields adhesion strength above this critical threshold. For the Al₂O₃/Pt combination, the critical load (Lc₂) between the Pt thin film and the SiC substrate before annealing was 13.13 mN. This is due to the low oxygen affinity of Pt at room temperature, which results in poor adhesion to oxide ceramic materials such as Al₂O₃. However, after annealing, the critical load (Lc₂) increased to 16.46 mN. One possible reason is that Pt oxidized at the interface between the Al₂O₃ and Pt layers, forming oxides and sub-oxides. The compound PtxO₁-ₓ exhibited strong bonding with Al₂O₃, thereby enhancing the adhesion strength between the Pt film and the substrate47.

Conclusion

This study investigated the influence of adhesion layer materials and annealing conditions on the high-temperature electrical performance of SiC-based Pt thin-film RTDs. The results indicated that the Pt thin-film RTDs with Ta and Al₂O₃ adhesion layers can operate up to 855 °C in air, demonstrating excellent linearity and high TCR, while the annealing temperature affected the TCR of the Ta/Pt and Al₂O₃/Pt combinations differently. The TCR of the Ta/Pt combination increased with annealing temperature, whereas the TCR of the Al₂O₃/Pt combination showed a decreasing trend with the annealing temperature. Compared to the Ta/Pt combination, the Al₂O₃/Pt combination exhibited a higher TCR. This is because Al₂O₃ and Pt form a stable interface, whereas interlayer thermal diffusion in the Ta/Pt combination introduces impurity defects into the Pt film, reducing the TCR of the Pt thin-film RTDs. The optimal annealing temperature for the Ta/Pt and Al₂O₃/Pt combinations was 850 °C and 650 °C, respectively, at which the corresponding TCR was 2.741×10⁻³/°C and 2.845×10⁻³/°C, with good consistency across three tests.

The Pt thin-film RTDs with Ta and Al₂O₃ adhesion layers exhibited resistance drift rates of 0.538%/h and 4.159%/h at 800 °C in air, respectively. With the addition of a YSZ protective layer, these rates decreased to 0.291%/h and 0.146%/h, significantly enhancing stability. Compared to the Al₂O₃ protective layer, the YSZ-coated Pt film RTDs demonstrated superior consistency and a higher TCR. For the Ta/Pt combination, the TCR was 2.411 × 10⁻³/°C with a repeatability error of 3.476% under Al₂O₃ protection, whereas under YSZ protection, the TCR increased to 2.532 × 10⁻³/°C with a reduced repeatability error of 3.368%. For the Al₂O₃/Pt combination, the TCR was 2.573×10⁻³/°C with a repeatability error of 7.096% under the Al₂O₃ encapsulation, while under YSZ encapsulation, the TCR rose to 2.721 × 10⁻³/°C with a lower repeatability error of 2.544%.

The critical load (Lc₂) of Pt thin-film with the SiC substrate using Ta as the adhesion layer was 45.65 mN in the as-deposited state, which decreased to 11.96 mN after annealing. For the Pt thin-film with Al₂O₃ as the adhesion layer, the critical load (Lc₂) increased from 13.13 mN before annealing to 16.46 mN after annealing. Compared to the Ta/Pt combination, the Al₂O₃/Pt combination exhibited enhanced adhesion after high-temperature annealing, demonstrating greater reliability for high-temperature applications.
