Including Vonoprazan With PPIs Challenges the Drug-Interaction Hypothesis
Authors: Ahmed B Shamsulddin
Published: 2026

I read with great interest the study by Nishikura et al1 regarding the effect of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention with an aspirin-free strategy. The authors observed a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular events and mortality (HR: 5.30; 95% CI: 1.82-15.45; P = 0.002) in the PPI group within the aspirin-free cohort. They attributed these adverse outcomes to the attenuation of the antiplatelet effect of P2Y12 inhibitors (specifically clopidogrel) via drug-drug interactions involving CYP2C19.

However, a critical review of Table 1 reveals a substantial heterogeneity in the exposure group that challenges this mechanistic conclusion. In the propensity score–matched cohort for the no-aspirin strategy, 35.5% of the patients in the PPI group (160 of 451) were prescribed vonoprazan.

Vonoprazan is a potassium-competitive acid blocker, distinct from traditional PPIs. Pharmacokinetic studies have demonstrated that unlike omeprazole or esomeprazole, vonoprazan does not significantly inhibit CYP2C19 activity and does not attenuate the antiplatelet function of clopidogrel in the way traditional PPIs are hypothesized to do.2

By lumping vonoprazan users with traditional PPI users, the authors have included a large subgroup (over one-third) for whom the proposed mechanism of harm (CYP2C19 inhibition) is biologically implausible. If the increased cardiovascular risk were truly driven by the attenuation of clopidogrel, the inclusion of 160 patients on a noninteracting drug should have diluted the signal. Instead, the authors report an unexpectedly high HR for death of 5.30.

This extreme mortality signal, persisting despite the high prevalence of vonoprazan use, strongly suggests that the observed association is driven by residual confounding rather than a specific drug interaction. The prescription of potent acid suppression—whether PPI or potassium-competitive acid blocker—likely serves as a marker for unmeasured frailty and severe comorbidities that propensity matching could not fully eliminate.

I urge caution in interpreting these results. To resolve this dilemma, a sensitivity analysis separating vonoprazan users from traditional PPI users, and distinguishing high-interaction PPIs (eg, omeprazole) from low-interaction agents (eg, rabeprazole), is necessary to clarify the true source of risk.3
