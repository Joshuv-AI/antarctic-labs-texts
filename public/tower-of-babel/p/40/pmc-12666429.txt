Signet ring cell cutaneous angiosarcoma with ATM deletion mutation on scalp
Authors: Thuan Huu Nguyen, Jeison De Guzman, Emily Haque Darwish, Markku M. Miettinen, Marcia S. Driscoll
Published: 2025

Introduction

Cutaneous angiosarcoma (CAS) is a rare, aggressive, endothelial-derived malignancy with an estimated 5-year survival rate between 10% and 30%.1 CAS predominantly occurs in patients aged more than 60 years and is often associated with chronic lymphedema or radiation exposure.2 CAS commonly presents on the neck and scalp in variable morphologies, ranging from violaceous macules and patches to ulcerated plaques and nodules.2 Histologically, CAS typically shows irregular and anastomosing vascular channels lined with pleomorphic or mitotic endothelial cells.2 Herein, we present an unusual case of primary CAS with signet ring cell features—a rare variant—accompanied by a deletion of the DNA repair gene ataxia-telangiectasia mutated (ATM).3,4

Case presentation

A 39-year-old female with no prior history of radiation exposure or malignancy presented with 4-month history of an intensely pruritic, skin-colored, shiny, firm, 5 × 6 mm papule with overlying hair loss on the left occipital scalp (Fig 1). The patient reported notable intractable pruritus of the lesion with repetitive scratching episodes resulting in focal alopecia. Dermoscopic examination showed scarred hair follicles with prominent hairpin vessels. A shave biopsy was performed. Histological analysis showed cords and clusters of atypical epithelioid cells, many of which contained signet ring-like cytoplasmic vacuoles (Fig 2). Mitotic activity was noted ≥ 4/10 per high-power field with no necrosis. Immunohistochemically, tumor cells were positive for CD31 and ERG and negative for CAMTA1, CD34, CK7, CK20, FOSB, GATA3, GCDFP, pancytokeratin, and SOX10. Next-generation sequencing of the tissue biopsy specimen using the TruSight Oncology 500 panel identified a 27-codon deletion in the ATM gene (c.3188_3214delTAATGGGAAAAGACTTTCCTGTAAATG, p.Val1063_Asn1071del), with no copy number variants detected. RNA sequencing was negative for gene fusions. DNA methylation profiling using the Sarcoma Classifier5 yielded a high match for angiosarcoma. The final diagnosis was angiosarcoma with signet ring cell features.

Bioinformatic analysis was performed to investigate the functional significance of the deleted sequence. Sequence alignment using the National Center for Biotechnology Information database and motif analysis against an annotated eukaryotic motif database6 revealed that the deletion maps to chain A of the serine-protein kinase ATM and is conserved across many vertebrate species. However, its specific function remains unclear, as it did not correspond to any known annotated motifs.

The patient was referred to the otorhinolaryngology head and neck surgery department for further evaluation. Positron Emission Tomography-Computed Tomography (PET-CT) imaging revealed no evidence of metastatic disease, and surgical excision was recommended. Over a 3-week period, 3 wide local excisions were performed in an attempt to achieve negative margins (Fig 3). Despite these efforts, rare microscopic foci of malignant cells were still present in the third excision specimen. Consequently, adjuvant proton radiation therapy was initiated, as recommended by the multidisciplinary Head and Neck Tumor Board (Fig 4).Fig 3Left occipital scalp defect on POD #6 status post first wide local excision. Approximately 5 months after completing radiation therapy, a PET-CT was performed to assess for residual cancer burden. The results showed no evidence of local recurrence or metastatic disease. Given the aggressive nature of the tumor, we recommended long-term surveillance with frequent full-body skin examinations. As of this writing, it has been over 2 years since the patient’s initial diagnosis. Since her last PET-CT, she has continued to attend her dermatology appointments with no additional imaging surveillance.

Discussion

CAS is a rare and malignant vascular neoplasm. While the etiologies of primary CAS are unknown, secondary CAS is associated with radiation and chronic lymphedema.2 Diagnosis is challenging due to its nonspecific presentations, and biopsy is required to differentiate CAS from other benign and malignant vascular neoplasms. Although standardized treatment guidelines are lacking, a multimodal approach—combining surgical resection with adjuvant chemotherapy and radiation—has been associated with improved survival outcomes.7

We describe a unique case of a young patient with primary CAS presenting as a scar-like papule on the scalp. Due to its rarity, the pathogenesis of CAS remains poorly understood. The underlying mechanism of CAS is thought in part to involve dysregulation of angiogenesis regulation pathways with TP53 mutations and overexpression of vascular endothelial growth factor and RAS signaling pathways.2,8 C-MYC amplification is commonly associated with radiation-induced CAS but is rarely observed in primary CAS. A recent review of 14 studies found that TP53 is the most frequently altered gene in head and neck angiosarcoma, followed by POT1 and CRKL.9

ATM mutations have been identified in angiosarcoma; however, their exact role in CAS remains to be elucidated.9 To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of signet ring variant CAS associated with an ATM gene deletion. ATM is a key regulator of the cellular response to DNA double-strand breaks.10 Somatic mutations are frequently observed across various cancers, with growing evidence suggesting that ATM deficiency increases tumor sensitivity to ionizing radiation and DNA-damaging chemotherapeutic agents.10 Given its role in DNA repair and maintenance of genomic stability, it is plausible that ATM, like TP53, functions in part as a negative regulator of angiogenesis. Thus, its deletion may have contributed to CAS pathogenesis in this patient.

Given the current lack of targeted therapies and the poor prognosis associated with CAS, treatment strategies aimed at key genetic drivers—such as TP53, C-MYC, vascular endothelial growth factor, and ATM—may offer promising avenues for future interventions.2,8, 9, 10 However, further research is needed to elucidate the roles of these molecular alterations in CAS pathogenesis and clinical outcomes.

Our case also highlights the value of molecular characterization in CAS and other rare cutaneous neoplasms. As demonstrated here, genetic testing should be considered when CAS is suspected to enhance our understanding of the pathogenesis of the tumor and guide the development of future therapies. In the meantime, increased awareness of the diverse clinical presentations of CAS is essential to facilitate earlier diagnosis and timely treatment of this aggressive malignancy.
