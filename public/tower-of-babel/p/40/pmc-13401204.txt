Variability in the Diagnostic Criteria for Severe Pertussis
Authors: Pauline Labe, Alexandre Meunier, Pauline Leroux, Mirko Sadi, Carla Rodrigues, Valerie Bouchez, Marion Grimaud, Sylvain Brisse, Jérémie F. Cohen, Julie Toubiana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This systematic review evaluates the variability in terminology and criteria used to define severe pertussis in the published literature.

Introduction

Pertussis, also known as whooping cough, is a worldwide endemic respiratory bacterial infection mainly caused by Bordetella pertussis. Following a period of low incidence during and immediately after the COVID-19 pandemic,1,2 Pertussis has been resurging in several parts of the world, with a peak observed in 2023 to 2024.3 Despite global immunization coverage exceeding 80% of children worldwide,4 pertussis remains a major threat in early infancy,5,6 with over 90% of pertussis-related deaths occurring in infants aged less than 3 months.7 Adults and adolescents are the primary source of transmission to infants,8 reinforcing the importance of preventive strategies such as cocooning and,9,10 above all, maternal pertussis vaccination, which has proven effective in reducing morbidity and mortality among young infants.11 According to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, approximately one-third of infants with pertussis require hospitalization, and 1% of these cases are fatal.12

Pertussis has a variable clinical course. Classically, pertussis progresses through a catarrhal stage, a paroxysmal stage, and a convalescent stage. In some children, the outcome may be severe, with apnea or paroxysmal cough leading to asphyxia, or progressing to severe manifestations such as respiratory failure, pulmonary arterial hypertension, multiple organ dysfunction, and death. Several studies have identified risk factors for disease severity and mortality in infants, including low birth weight, prematurity, young age at cough onset, and an elevated white blood cell count (WBC), generally above 30 000 cells/μL.5,13 However, a standardized definition of severe pertussis is still lacking. Studies may use clinical criteria (eg, apnea) to assess the severity of pertussis, while others use outcome criteria (eg, death or admission to intensive care unit [ICU]). One study14 employed a clinical scale to assess the severity of pertussis disease among children, but this scale lacks validation and has been rarely used.15 Given the gaps in our understanding of the immunological and microbiological determinants of severe pertussis, this lack of standardization represents a barrier to research, clinical management, and implementation of public health interventions.16

Beyond variability in definition, authors use diverse terminology to describe severe forms of pertussis. For example, Berger et al17 defined critical pertussis as pertussis resulting in ICU admission or death. Chantreuil et al18 defined malignant pertussis based on pulmonary hypertension and hyperleukocytosis greater than 50 000 cells/μL, whereas Ganeshalingham et al19 additionally included pneumonia and cardiogenic shock as part of their definition.

The present study aimed to determine whether studies on infant pertussis use highly variable terms and criteria to define severe disease. Our primary objective was to assess the variability in the definition of severe pertussis, focusing on variations in terminology and diagnostic criteria through a systematic review of the literature. We also aimed to evaluate whether the criteria used to define severe pertussis align with established risk factors of poor outcomes in infants.

Methods

Literature Search Strategy and Inclusion Criteria

We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) reporting guideline.20 All data included in this study were publicly available and fully deidentified. Therefore, institutional review board or ethics committee approval and informed consent were not required as the data were derived from previously published sources in accordance with 45 CFR §46. We aimed to identify, analyze, and compare all published studies (including case reports or series, cohort and case-control studies, randomized trials, and reviews) that provided definitions for severe pertussis in infants, given that severe pertussis infections are almost exclusively observed in this population. One author (A.M.) conducted the literature search and data extraction, which was verified by a second author (P.L.). One review author (J.T.) acted as an arbiter in case of discrepancies.

We searched MEDLINE (via PubMed) and Web Of Science from inception to April 30, 2025 (last update), without language restrictions. Details regarding our literature search strategy are shown in the eAppendix in Supplement 1. Studies were selected through an initial screening of titles and abstracts. Then, if the title and abstract were deemed potentially relevant (or in cases of uncertainty), the full text was reviewed.

Data Extraction

For each included study, we extracted the country in which the research was conducted, the year of publication, and the study design (eg, randomized trial, cohort or cross-sectional study, or systematic review). We collected detailed information on the terminology used to describe severe pertussis in infants (eg, severe, malignant, and fatal), along with the specific criteria used in the definitions. These criteria potentially included clinical, biological, radiological, therapeutic, and outcome-based parameters (eg, ICU admission, need for ventilatory support, or need for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation). From studies investigating factors associated with poor outcomes, we extracted risk factors significantly associated with pertussis-attributed death or severity, as identified by univariable or multivariable analyses.

Data Analysis

First, we described the flow of studies in our literature search and provided a narrative summary of the key characteristics of included studies. Second, we analyzed the various terminologies used to designate severe forms of pertussis. Third, we analyzed the criteria used to define severity, grouping them into 4 categories (ie, clinical, biological, radiological, and outcome), and reported them using frequencies and proportions. Additionally, we identified definition criteria corresponding to risk factors associated with poor pertussis outcomes. Analyses were carried out using SPSS version 31 (IBM).

As vaccination status is a commonly studied variable in pertussis research, we identified studies addressing this question (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). Overall, vaccination status did not show any association with the criteria used to define disease severity. Vaccine failure was not included among the defining criteria for severity, nor was severity considered an indicator of vaccine failure.

Criteria Identified as Risk Factors for Poor Outcomes

Twenty-seven of the included studies5,13,16,17,19,21,22,24,26,27,28,36,37,38,39,41,42,45,49,51,53,54,55,56,62,65,82 aimed at identifying risk factors for poor pertussis outcomes. Most studies focused on specific outcomes, such as death (16 studies5,13,17,19,24,26,27,28,36,37,38,45,49,53,65,82) or the need for intensive care (5 studies17,27,36,51,56) (eTable 3 in Supplement 1). Ten studies16,19,21,22,39,41,45,54,55,62 investigated risk factors for disease severity, which was most often defined as a composite outcome combining clinical, biological, and/or prognostic criteria (Table 2). The use of these criteria was highly heterogeneous across studies. Characteristics found to be associated with poor outcomes in infants included younger age (<4 months; 13 of 27 studies5,13,16,17,21,26,27,38,39,41,45,51,65 [48%]), absence of prior pertussis vaccination (11 of 27 studies5,13,16,21,22,26,27,28,38,51,65 [41%]), and prematurity (3 of 27 studies13,16,39 [11%]). Other factors included altered vital signs (eg, tachypnoea and tachycardia; 8 of 27 studies13,17,28,29,36,37,54,65 [30%]), pulmonary hypertension (6 of 27 studies5,13,17,45,53,82 [19%]), respiratory signs (eg, pneumonia, cyanosis, and apnea; 8 of 27 studies13,38,49,51,54,62,65,82 [30%]), and neurological involvement (8 of 27 studies13,17,28,37,39,45,49,65 [30%]), overlapping with those used to define disease severity (Table 2). Among biological factors, leukocytosis was the most frequently reported (17 of 27 studies5,13,16,17,19,24,28,36,37,42,49,51,53,55,56,65,82 [63%]) (eTable 3 in Supplement 1), with 2 studies53,62 showing that rapid WBC growth (>12 000 cells/μL/day) or a lymphocyte and neutrophil ratio less than 1 were associated with increased mortality in infants.

Discussion

Main Findings

In this systematic review, we aimed to specify the concept and definition of severe pertussis and highlight its current variability in the literature. We identified and quantified broad variability in how authors defined severity, often using their own terminology and inconsistent criteria. The terms severe and critical exhibited the most heterogeneous definitions, with criteria ranging from fatal outcome to mild clinical symptoms. These terms also incorporated hospitalization and length of stay as criteria, both of which are highly variable due to differences in health care resources across settings. However, certain criteria recurrently observed in the definitions of malignant and fulminant pertussis, such as respiratory failure, pulmonary arterial hypertension, organ failure, and leukocytosis, were more consistently applied. Among the outcome category, the majority of studies used the ICU admission or death criteria, which appeared in more than a third of the reviewed studies. Definitions also varied depending on the aim of the study (eg, descriptive epidemiological vs identification of severity risk factors or mortality predictors). Studies investigating risk factors for severe pertussis exhibited great heterogeneity in their terminology and definitions. Some criteria used to define severe pertussis also served as risk factors for poor outcomes.

Comparison With Previous Findings

To our knowledge, this is the first study that specifically focuses on definitions of pertussis severity. A previous systematic review of the literature by Machado et al87 explored pertussis severity but did not provide a formal definition for severity, instead focusing on therapeutic outcomes. More recently, a scoping review performed by Guo et al88 concluded that there is a need for standardized diagnostic criteria and the reinforcement of experimental studies to improve the management of affected infants. Préziosi et al14 proposed a severity scale, though it was not widely adopted in the literature. This scale provides a simple method for assessing pertussis severity, but lacks biological criteria, which are often present in severe cases. Gill et al15 modified the Préziosi scale by differentiating cases between infants younger than 6 weeks and those older than 6 weeks, incorporating additional criteria and an outcome criterion (ie, death). Namachivayam et al29 developed a pertussis severity scale that categorized patients into 3 clinical presentation groups: apnea, pneumonia, and seizures. This previous study aimed to determine which clinical presentation type correlated most with fatal outcomes and found that pneumonia was strongly associated with higher mortality, particularly in cases requiring circulatory support. However, the scale did not account for severe manifestations beyond these categories, limiting its ability to capture the full spectrum of severe pertussis presentations.29 The included studies lack well-established radiological and biological criteria for defining pertussis severity. Apart from leukocytosis, no other consistently identified biological factor has emerged as a defining criterion for severity, although severe cases may occur with a normal WBC count.91,92 Imaging criteria are also limited and poorly defined, with standard chest radiographs generally showing nonspecific findings.

Interpretation and Implications for Clinical Practice and Research

There are several possible explanations for the observed variability in pertussis severity definitions in the literature. First, physicians may have different conceptions of severity. In ICU settings, outcome criteria and mortality are more frequently considered, whereas clinical signs such as apnea, cough severity, respiratory failure, and dehydration are more commonly observed in ambulatory care, emergency departments, and conventional hospital wards. Another contributing factor to this variability is the absence of specific clinical guideline statements for monitoring pertussis severity. Context-dependent criteria such as hospitalization, length of stay, and even ICU admission may not accurately reflect disease severity, as ICU admission practices vary across countries and centers; in some settings, infants may be admitted to the ICU briefly for observation rather than due to clinical severity. Regarding biological criteria, tests such as leukocyte counts, imaging for pneumonia, and echocardiography for pulmonary hypertension are not systematically performed. Third, severity definitions depend on the study’s purpose of the study, design, and the population being studied. Case reports typically describe extreme or unusual clinical presentations, whereas case-control and cohort studies may focus on risk factors associated with different levels of severity, which, in turn, vary with patient age; younger children are at the highest risk of requiring intensive care and, ultimately, death.46

Partial overlap exists between the criteria for severe pertussis and risk factors for poor outcomes. Some features used for severity, such as respiratory failure, pulmonary hypertension, leukocytosis, neurological involvement, and ICU admission, also serve as predictors of mortality. Meanwhile, baseline characteristics like young age (<2-4 months), lack of vaccination, prematurity, or bacterial factors present before illness onset are identified as specific risk factors for severe disease. Including variables related to disease progression or treatment, as done in some studies, may confound the association between baseline factors and severity.

A consensus-based, explicit, accurate, and reliable definition of pertussis severity, potentially incorporating a severity scale and mortality predictors, could enhance patient care. Such definitions would enable a more reproducible assessment of severity risk factors, facilitate the identification of high-risk patients requiring specialized care, and support the development of clinical guidelines and standardized patient management protocols. A clear definition is also essential for epidemiological surveillance of pertussis, particularly as new vaccine recommendations, such as maternal pertussis vaccination, are being implemented. In this context, our findings may help define priorities for future consensus work. Such a definition could be structured around 3 domains, clinical, biological, and outcome criteria, which consistently emerged across the literature. Within such a framework, respiratory failure, pulmonary hypertension, leukocytosis, ICU admission, and death may represent core components of severity, whereas more context-dependent criteria, such as hospitalization or length of stay, may require cautious interpretation. Future consensus work should also determine explicit thresholds, particularly for leukocytosis, and clarify the distinction between criteria defining severity and baseline risk factors for poor outcomes. Such a structured approach could provide the development of an operational and internationally applicable definition.

While a harmonized definition of severe pertussis would enhance comparability across studies, it is equally important to recognize that disease severity likely reflects multiple phenotypes shaped by host, bacterial, and outcome-specific factors. Therefore, a universal definition complemented by phenotypic subclassifications and validated through mechanistic studies could both improve standardization and preserve biological interpretability. On the other hand, this definition would also help to improve researchers’ understanding of the underlying pathophysiology of severe pertussis. Pertussis severity may be influenced by both host factors (such as gestational age, birth weight, and vaccine status) and bacterial factors.16,93 For example, the pertussis toxin of B. pertussis contributes to elevated leukocyte levels, often exceeding 100 G/L in most severe cases.94 It remains unclear whether leukocytosis directly contributes to disease severity, pulmonary hypertension, and shock by obstructing the pulmonary vasculature.24 Alternatively, other factors, such as the virulent vaccine antigen protein pertactin, may play a role in disease severity,16 potentially in combination with bacterial superinfection or viral co-infection.53 The heterogeneity of study populations does not facilitate the identification of other shared underlying immunological features that may be triggered by specific bacterial components.

Limitations

Our systematic review has some limitations. We limited our search to only 2 databases (PubMed and Web of Science), which may have excluded relevant studies indexed elsewhere, particularly in non-English (eg, Chinese) databases.95 Additionally, we did not use citation tracking functions like ‘cited by’ in Google Scholar, which may have identified additional relevant studies. Furthermore, some studies may have employed definitions of pertussis severity but failed to report them appropriately, potentially leading to selection bias. Also, we did not include guidelines defining severity, which could have been relevant to review. We identified a limited number of well-designed primary studies, and not all consistently reported the definitions they employed. Unclear and inconsistent reporting may have affected our ability to accurately extract and compare the definitions used across studies. A further limitation is the absence of a formal quality assessment of the included studies. However, given the descriptive nature of our review and the absence of quantitative synthesis (eg, meta-analysis), this was considered acceptable.

Conclusions

In this systematic review of 78 studies, substantial variability in the definition of pertussis severity was observed, in both terminology and diagnostic criteria. This highlighted the complexity of this multifaceted entity. Developing a harmonized definition of severity, with explicit characteristics and thresholds, could improve the quality and comparability of epidemiological, clinical, and translational research. It would also support clinical management and public health interventions that aim to reduce the burden of severe pertussis. A universal definition of pertussis severity could be established, for example, by international experts through consensus approaches such as the Delphi method.
