Exploring Medical Students’ Perceptions of Empathy Training in The Indian Attitude, Ethics, and Communication Curriculum: An Exploratory Qualitative Descriptive Survey
Authors: Shruti Singh, S Kirubakaran, Prashant Kumar Singh, Riya Ranjith Vaidyar, Kye Mon Min Swe, Punit Patel, Dheerendra Kumar Mishra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Empathy is widely recognized as a core component of patient-centered care and an important professional competency for medical graduates. In India, the attitude, ethics, and communication (AETCOM) module was introduced within the competency-based MBBS curriculum to foster empathy, communication skills, and professionalism. However, limited qualitative evidence exists regarding students’ experiences of empathy-focused teaching within this curriculum. This study explored medical students’ perceptions and experiences of empathy-focused AETCOM sessions and their views regarding the teachability of empathy.Methods:An exploratory qualitative descriptive survey was conducted among second-year MBBS students at a tertiary-care medical institute in June 2025. An anonymous online survey containing a single open-ended question—”What are your perceptions and experiences regarding the AETCOM classes focused on empathy?”—was administered following informed consent. Seventy-five students submitted written responses. Responses were reviewed independently by two researchers and organized into descriptive categories representing common patterns in participants’ experiences and perceptions.Results:Four broad categories were identified. Students generally perceived role-plays, simulations, patient narratives, and faculty experiences as valuable learning approaches. Participants described increased awareness of active listening, patient-centered communication, and patients’ emotional concerns. Suggested improvements included greater use of interactive and practice-oriented teaching methods, shorter sessions, and incorporation of emotional resilience and self-care training. A small minority of students expressed reservations regarding the impact of empathy teaching and viewed empathy primarily as an innate personal attribute.Conclusion:Students generally viewed empathy-focused AETCOM sessions positively, particularly when delivered through interactive and experiential approaches. The findings suggest that learner feedback may help inform the ongoing refinement of empathy-focused teaching within undergraduate medical education.

BACKGROUND

Empathy, broadly defined as the ability to understand and respond to another person’s experiences and emotions, is widely regarded as a fundamental component of effective patient-centered care. Higher levels of physician empathy have been associated with improved patient satisfaction, better treatment adherence, enhanced clinical outcomes, and fewer medicolegal concerns.[12] At the same time, concerns have been raised regarding a decline in empathy during medical training, with some studies suggesting that increasing academic and clinical demands may contribute to emotional distancing from patients.[12345]

Historically, empathy was often considered an innate personal attribute. However, a growing body of evidence suggests that empathy can be nurtured through educational interventions and deliberate training.[67] Systematic reviews have reported positive effects of approaches such as communication skill training, role-play, patient narratives, reflective exercises, and experiential learning activities on empathy-related outcomes among healthcare learners.[28910] Consequently, empathy is increasingly viewed not only as a desirable personal quality but also as a professional competency that can be supported through medical education.

In India, the introduction of the attitude, ethics, and communication (AETCOM) module within the competency-based undergraduate medical curriculum represented an important effort to formally address empathy, professionalism, ethics, and communication skills.[11] The module encourages the use of interactive teaching methods, including role-plays, patient stories, case discussions, and reflective learning activities, with the aim of promoting more humane and patient-centered clinical practice.[11]

Early evaluations of AETCOM have generally reported favorable student perceptions, with many learners acknowledging the importance of empathy and communication in patient care.[1112] However, most available studies have relied on quantitative approaches, providing limited insight into how students experience empathy-focused teaching and which aspects of the educational process they perceive as useful or challenging. Understanding these perceptions is important for informing ongoing refinement of the curriculum and ensuring that teaching strategies remain relevant to learners’ needs.

The present study, therefore, explored second-year MBBS students’ perceptions and experiences of empathy-focused AETCOM sessions using a qualitative descriptive approach. By examining students’ written reflections, the study sought to identify teaching approaches perceived as valuable, skills and attitudes associated with empathy training, areas requiring improvement, and differing viewpoints regarding the teachability of empathy

METHODS

Study design and setting

This was an exploratory qualitative descriptive survey conducted in June 2025 among Phase II MBBS students at a tertiary-care medical institute in eastern India. The study explored students’ perceptions and experiences of empathy-focused sessions delivered as part of the Attitude, Ethics and Communication (AETCOM) curriculum. Given the use of a single open-ended survey question, the study was intended to provide a brief descriptive account of student perceptions rather than an in-depth qualitative inquiry.

Participants and data collection

All 125 students in the batch who had been exposed to empathy-related AETCOM sessions were eligible to participate. These sessions included role-plays, patient narratives, classroom discussions, faculty-led reflections, and interactive learning activities. The Google Form link was circulated through the official class communication channel after completion of the empathy-focused sessions. Participation was voluntary, and no incentives were offered.

The first page of the form contained the participant information sheet and consent statement. Students could proceed to the survey only after indicating consent electronically. No names, roll numbers, e-mail addresses, or other direct identifiers were collected. Thus, consent was operationalized through electronic agreement before anonymous response submission.

The survey contained one open-ended question: “What are your perceptions and experiences regarding the AETCOM classes focused on empathy?” A total of 75 students submitted responses, giving a response rate of 60%. Non-respondents were not followed up on to preserve voluntariness and anonymity.

The participant information sheet, consent statement, and survey question are provided as Supplementary File 1.

Research team and reflexivity

The analysis was conducted by two faculty members involved in undergraduate medical education and AETCOM teaching. The researchers’ prior engagement with empathy-related teaching may have influenced interpretation of the responses. To reduce individual bias, both researchers independently reviewed the responses and then discussed the descriptive categories until consensus was reached. The analysis remained close to participants’ written responses and avoided deeper interpretive claims beyond the available data.

Data analysis

Responses were analyzed using a qualitative descriptive approach. Both researchers read the responses repeatedly to become familiar with the data and identify recurring patterns in students’ perceptions. Similar responses were grouped into descriptive categories reflecting common viewpoints. Differences in categorization were resolved through discussion. Illustrative quotations were selected to represent the range of student perspectives while preserving anonymity.

Formal data saturation was not sought or assessed because the study used a single anonymous open-ended survey question. Member checking was also not feasible, as participants could not be re-contacted without compromising anonymity. The findings are therefore presented as exploratory descriptive categories rather than as an exhaustive account of students’ experiences.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee, All India Institute of Medical Sciences, Patna (AIIMS/Pat/IEC/1233). Participation was voluntary, anonymous, and based on electronic informed consent.

RESULTS

A total of 75 of 125 eligible Phase II MBBS students participated in the survey, yielding a response rate of 60%. All respondents had previously attended empathy-focused AETCOM sessions comprising role-plays, patient narratives, classroom discussions, and other interactive learning activities. Responses ranged from brief comments to more detailed reflections. Analysis of the responses identified four broad categories relating to effective teaching methods, perceived learning outcomes, suggested improvements, and reservations regarding empathy training [Table 1].

Synthesis of students’ perceptions and experiences of empathy-focused attitude, ethics, and communication sessions

Many students described interactive teaching approaches as the most valuable aspect of empathy training. Role-plays, simulations, patient narratives, and faculty sharing of clinical experiences were frequently cited as engaging methods that helped students better understand patients’ perspectives and emotions. Several respondents reported that experiential activities were more memorable and meaningful than traditional lecture-based sessions.

Students also perceived that the sessions contributed to the development of communication-related skills and professional attitudes. Commonly reported outcomes included greater attention to active listening, improved awareness of patients’ emotional concerns, and a stronger appreciation of patient-centered communication. Some students described becoming more reflective and mindful of the human aspects of patient care.

Alongside these positive perceptions, respondents identified several opportunities for improvement. Students frequently recommended increasing the use of interactive and practice-oriented activities, including role-plays, case discussions, videos, and real-life clinical examples. Some felt that certain sessions were repetitive or overly theoretical and suggested shorter, more engaging formats. A number of respondents also expressed interest in incorporating topics related to emotional resilience, stress management, and coping with the emotional demands of clinical practice.

A small minority of students expressed reservations about the impact of empathy training. These respondents reported little perceived influence of the sessions on their attitudes or behaviors and viewed empathy primarily as an innate personal characteristic rather than a skill that can be developed through formal instruction.

Overall, student responses suggested that empathy-focused AETCOM sessions were generally perceived positively, particularly when delivered through interactive and experiential approaches. At the same time, participants highlighted opportunities to enhance engagement and address the emotional challenges associated with future clinical practice. DISCUSSION

This qualitative descriptive study explored medical students’ perceptions of empathy-focused AETCOM sessions and provides insight into how learners experience empathy teaching within the competency-based MBBS curriculum. Overall, students described empathy training as valuable and identified several educational approaches that they perceived as helpful for understanding patients’ perspectives. At the same time, they highlighted areas for improvement and expressed differing views regarding the extent to which empathy can be taught.

A prominent observation was students’ preference for interactive and experiential learning methods. Role-plays, simulations, patient narratives, and faculty sharing of real-world experiences were frequently described as engaging and memorable learning experiences. Students perceived these approaches as helping them appreciate the emotional and interpersonal aspects of patient care more effectively than traditional lecture-based teaching. Similar observations have been reported in previous studies, which suggest that experiential and narrative-based educational approaches are well received by learners and may support empathy-related learning.[2891013141516] Students also expressed interest in varied and engaging learning formats. Such preferences are consistent with broader educational literature supporting the use of creative and humanities-based approaches to promote reflection, perspective-taking, and empathic understanding among healthcare learners.[17]

Many students perceived that the sessions contributed to greater awareness of communication practices, particularly active listening, attention to patients’ emotions, and patient-centered communication. Several respondents linked empathy with professional growth and the development of a more holistic understanding of patients. Previous studies have likewise reported favorable learner perceptions of empathy-focused educational interventions and their potential contribution to communication-related competencies.[1718] Students frequently described a shift from viewing patients primarily as clinical cases to recognizing them as individuals with unique experiences and concerns, a perspective that aligns with relational approaches to healthcare that emphasize person-to-person engagement.[19] These perceptions are consistent with the broader goals of the AETCOM curriculum, which seeks to foster ethical, communicative, and professional competencies alongside biomedical knowledge.[11]

Similar favorable perceptions of AETCOM and competency-based medical education have been reported in previous studies from India, supporting the importance of communication and professionalism training within undergraduate medical education.[12]

Another notable finding was the importance students placed on emotional resilience and self-care. Although empathy training is primarily directed toward understanding patients’ experiences, several respondents emphasized the need to learn strategies for managing stress, emotional fatigue, and the psychological demands of clinical practice. Students frequently connected empathy with emotional awareness and professional maturity, suggesting that empathy and self-care may be viewed as complementary rather than competing competencies.[20] Existing literature has similarly highlighted the importance of resilience-building, mindfulness, and reflective practices in supporting healthcare learners’ well-being while maintaining empathic engagement with patients.[2122]

Despite generally positive perceptions, a minority of respondents remained skeptical regarding the impact of formal empathy training and viewed empathy primarily as an innate personal attribute. Similar perspectives have been described previously and reflect continuing debate regarding the relative contributions of personal disposition and educational experiences to empathy development.[23] The coexistence of supportive and skeptical viewpoints suggests that empathy may be influenced by both inherent characteristics and educational experiences rather than representing an exclusively innate or teachable attribute.

Such variation in viewpoints highlights the importance of continuing to explore how learners engage with empathy-focused curricula and which educational approaches they find most meaningful.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is that it captured anonymous written reflections from medical students regarding empathy-focused AETCOM sessions, allowing participants to express both favorable and critical views. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Data were collected from a single institution using a single open-ended survey question, limiting opportunities for in-depth exploration and clarification of participants’ responses. Formal data saturation was neither sought nor assessed, and member checking was not feasible because the anonymous design precluded re-contacting participants. Participation was voluntary, and only 75 of 125 eligible students responded; consequently, the perspectives of non-respondents, including students who may have been less engaged with or dissatisfied by the sessions, are likely to be underrepresented, introducing the possibility of non-response bias. The findings are based on self-reported perceptions rather than objective measures of empathy or behavioral change and should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and descriptive rather than representative of all medical students. Nevertheless, the study provides useful insight into students’ experiences of empathy-focused AETCOM sessions and identifies considerations that may inform future curriculum refinement and evaluation.

CONCLUSION

Medical students generally perceived empathy-focused AETCOM sessions as valuable, particularly when delivered through interactive approaches such as role-plays, simulations, and patient narratives. Participants described perceived benefits in communication, listening, and awareness of patients’ perspectives while also identifying opportunities to enhance engagement through more practical learning activities. Interest in emotional resilience and self-care emerged as an additional area for consideration within empathy training. Although the findings are exploratory and based on self-reported experiences, they suggest that learner feedback may help inform the ongoing refinement of empathy-focused teaching within the undergraduate medical curriculum.
