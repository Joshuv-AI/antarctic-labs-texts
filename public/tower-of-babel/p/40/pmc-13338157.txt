ATP-depleting spreading depolarizations preferentially originate in the somatosensory cortex during in situ energy deprivation
Authors: Karl Schoknecht, Nicolas G. Höck, Felipe Baeza-Lehnert, Jens P. Dreier, Johannes Hirrlinger, Jens Eilers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spreading depolarizations (SDs) contribute to acute brain lesion development. While SDs arise in metabolically compromised tissue, regional determinants of SD-susceptibility remain incompletely understood. Here, we show that SDs preferentially originate in the primary somatosensory cortex (S1) during oxygen-glucose deprivation. Notably, before SD initiation, S1 exhibited a more pronounced decline in ATP compared with other brain regions, suggesting S1-specific metabolic vulnerability as a novel working hypothesis underlying SD initiation.

Spreading depolarizations (SDs) have been associated with lesion progression and worse functional outcomes in clinical conditions such as cerebral ischemia, cerebral hemorrhage, and traumatic brain injury1–3. Their detrimental effects have largely been attributed to a mismatch between energy supply and demand4, in part because energy demand sharply rises following the loss of electrochemical energy stored in ion gradients – a hallmark of SDs5. An adaptive hypermetabolic response in the wake of SDs, likely required to restore ion gradients, has been reported6–11. We recently demonstrated that despite this hypermetabolic response, intracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) transiently declines in neurons during SDs, even in the presence of oxygen and glucose12. Moreover, we found that during oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD), ATP does not decline gradually, but instead, is rapidly depleted by SDs. These findings indicate that, even in a model of severe ischemia, rapid neuronal ATP depletion is a consequence rather than a cause of SDs, supporting the role of SDs for brain injury development and as a promising therapeutic target. While SDs are known to arise in metabolically compromised tissue – possibly triggered by increasing concentrations of extracellular potassium ([K+]o) or glutamate4,13–15 – regional variability in SD susceptibility remains incompletely understood. Elucidating these differences may provide insights into the sequence of events leading to SD initiation. Notably, one in vivo study in mice demonstrated enhanced SD susceptibility in the primary somatosensory cortex (S1) under high [K+]o or anoxia16. Here, we report that preferential SD onset in S1 is preserved in acute brain slices and accompanied by distinct local ATP dynamics.

To study SD onset and propagation alongside neuronal ATP during OGD, we simultaneously recorded transmission-based intrinsic optical signal (IOS), cortical local field potentials (LFP), and ATP sensor signals (Fig. 1a,b). We used acute brain slices from transgenic mice expressing the optical ATP nanosensor ATeam 1.03YEMK in neurons17. A characteristic negative shift of the direct current (DC) potential in the LFP confirmed SD occurrence (Fig. 1c; negativity faces up), while time-series recordings of the IOS (see methods) captured SDs with spatial resolution (Fig. 1d). The IOS increases gradually during OGD and exhibits a distinct SD-associated peak followed by a sharp decline to baseline levels or below (Fig. 1e) resulting from increased light scattering18,19. A systematic analysis of the IOS signal, focusing on the SD-associated IOS peaks, revealed that during OGD, SDs preferentially originated in S1 and subsequently propagated to other cortical areas, including the primary motor cortex (M1; see example in Fig. 2a). Subcortical structures (SUB, pooled data of caudate putamen and thalamus, n = 12 and 4 slices from 10 and 3 mice, respectively) were typically affected secondarily, with a delay of ~6 min in the example shown in Fig. 2b, however, this observation should be interpreted with caution, particularly for the thalamus, given the small sample size. Across experiments, the SD onset site was within the field of view in 16 of 21 brain slices (12 mice). In these 16 slices, SDs most commonly arose in S1, 11 times exclusively first and 3 times in parallel with SUB (Fig. 2c), i.e., with a delay shorter than the temporal resolution of the IOS. Notably, although M1 was within the field of view in 11 slices, SD originated there only once (Fig. 2c), and otherwise arose in S1 in this data subset. When the SD onset site was outside the visual field (n = 5 of 21 slices, 5 mice), the lateral portion of S1 was not fully captured in favor of fully capturing the cortex at the medial wall, containing motor-cortical areas. Nevertheless, the visible part of S1 was reached by SD prior to M1 or SUB in 4 more cases, excluding M1 as the site of SD onset. Remarkably, SDs neither initiated nor propagated within the insular cortex (captured in n = 7 slices, 5 mice; see example in Fig. 1d and Fig. 2a,b), indicating reduced SD susceptibility, which is in line with an in vivo report of reduced SD propagation velocity in this region in rats20. Together, our data emphasize a higher SD susceptibility of S1 compared with other brain regions – not only in vivo16, but also in cortical slices – suggesting features of S1 independent of blood supply underlying this phenomenon. Given that SDs are known to arise when ATP availability is diminished, such as in acute brain injuries13, we analyzed ATP signals preceding SDs, first under baseline conditions, then in OGD, but before SD onset. Baseline ATP signals (YFP/CFP ratio, Fig. 1 b,f and methods) in S1 were lower than in SUB (0.65 [0.63, 0.70] ([interquartile 1 (IQ1), IQ3]) vs. 0.77 [0.70, 0.83], respectively, n = 16 slices, 12 mice, p < 0.01, Wilcoxon test and Bonferroni correction), but not different from M1 (Fig. 2e), the latter suggesting that differences in baseline ATP levels alone cannot account for the higher SD susceptibility of S1. Next, we analyzed the experiments in which SDs originated in S1. Remarkably, already prior to SD onset, ATP signals had declined more in S1 than in adjacent M1 or SUB. Specifically, median ATP signals had similarly declined by 2.7% [1.1, 4.3] in M1 and by 3.1% [1.7, 4,0] in SUB, whereas S1 showed a larger decrease of 6.1% [4.2, 8.8] below baseline (Fig. 2f; n = 8 and 11 slices, 7 and 9 mice, p < 0.05 and p < 0.01 for comparison with M1 and SUB, respectively; Wilcoxon test and Bonferroni correction). The data give rise to the hypothesis that a more pronounced decline in neuronal ATP during OGD in S1 – although the major ATP breakdown due to SDs is yet to come – facilitates SD onset.

The regional focus was motivated by the high prevalence of somatosensory and motor deficits in ischemic stroke, frequently involving primary somatosensory and motor cortices21–23. However, the possibility that other cortical regions, e.g., the frontal association cortex or the visual cortex, are more SD-susceptible cannot be excluded. Notably, during global cerebral ischemia in mice in vivo, SD onset occurred most frequently in S116, suggesting that it may indeed represent the area most prone to SDs in this species.

To understand regional variability in SD susceptibility, age-dependent vascular and metabolic factors emerge as plausible mechanistic determinants. Vascular factors may be particularly relevant in vivo, given their role in substrate supply. Specifically, attenuated cerebrovascular reactivity in aged versus young rats24, as well as reduced vascular density in deep cortical layers, the hippocampal formation, and basal forebrain areas in aging mice25, point to constrained substrate supply that could affect SD-susceptibility. Concurrently, cerebral energy metabolism has been shown to decline with age, to exhibit regional heterogeneity, and to include mitochondrial dysfunction26–28. However, the link between age-dependent metabolic impairment and SD susceptibility may be complex, potentially inverse, as SD thresholds have been reported to increase (not decrease) with age in rat temporohippocampal brain slices29. Collectively, these findings merit thorough future investigations into vascular and metabolic factors influencing SD-susceptibility.

Compared with other brain regions, both reduced ATP production and increased consumption could account for the greater pre-SD decline in neuronal ATP in S1. ATP production may still play a role in OGD despite the breakdown of oxidative energy metabolism, as experiments in acute hippocampal brain slices have shown that glycogen remains a transient energy source, delaying SD onset by several minutes unless glycolysis is simultaneously inhibited30. Accordingly, a reduced availability of glycogen-derived energy-rich substrates to S1 neurons – astrocytes being a known source31 – could underlie the larger ATP reductions preceding SDs.

Alternatively, or in addition, S1 neurons may consume more ATP during OGD, reflecting either greater neuronal density or a possibly higher proportion of highly energy-demanding neurons, such as parvalbumin-positive interneurons32. Neuronal energy demand comprises both activity-dependent and activity-independent processes, including housekeeping functions and maintenance of the resting membrane potential33. Given that neuronal network activity is markedly depressed before SD onset (see Supplementary Fig. 1 and ref. 34), regional variations in ATP consumption – although possibly activity-dependent early during OGD – would likely also reflect activity-independent processes. A limitation of the present experimental setting is that spontaneous neuronal network activity is already reduced under submerged recording conditions35. Finally, returning to the concept of regional heterogeneity, brain-wide comparisons of neuronal counts, including specific subtypes, have shown variable results across studies36, which currently preclude a clearer hypothesis.

Notably, as a potential functional link to increased ATP consumption, in vivo, S1 susceptibility to SDs correlated with accumulation of [K+]o16, which stimulates Na+/K+-ATPase activity37. Conversely, increased [K+]o could result from reduced Na+/K+-ATPase activity due to reduced ATP availability, overall leaving the causal relationship between reduced ATP levels and [K+]o accumulation in S1 unresolved. Independent of ATP supply or Na+/K+-ATPase activity, morphology of the extracellular space (ECS), specifically volume fraction and tortuosity, could also affect [K+]o accumulation38. However, comprehensive comparisons between M1 and S1 are lacking, and a comparison between the thalamus and the sensorimotor cortex showed reduced ECS volume in the thalamus39, favoring thalamic rather than cortical [K+]o accumulation. Thus, while ECS morphology cannot be excluded as a contributing factor to [K+]o accumulation, it is unlikely to account for the heightened SD susceptibility in S1 relative to SUB. Taken together, potential mechanistic targets for ATP reductions in S1 – whether from increased consumption or decreased production – remain inconclusive.

To conclude, we show that an in vivo feature of SDs – their preferential origin in S1 during OGD – is preserved in the acute brain slice preparation. By examining neuronal ATP dynamics, we identified S1-specific metabolic vulnerability, providing a novel working hypothesis to be tested in vivo for the preferential origin of SDs in this region. Elucidating the mechanisms underlying ATP reductions in S1, possibly related to age-dependent vascular and metabolic changes, may lead to novel therapeutic strategies to prevent SDs.

Methods

Animals

Animals were housed and bred in accordance with the German Animal Welfare Act, the European Communities Council Directive (2010/63/EU), the Leipzig University guidelines, and with approval of the local authorities (T05/21-MEZ). Animals were kept in a 12 h/12 h light-dark cycle with free access to food and water. The study was conducted in acute brain slices from 12 adult transgenic mice (median age 30 weeks, 6 females and 6 males) expressing the ATP sensor (ATeam 1.03YEMK)40 under the neuronal promotor Thy1.2 on a C57BL/6 J background (full name: (B6-Tg(Thy1.2-ATeam1.03YEMK)AJhi; MGI:5882597; referred to as ThyAT17). The age of the animals corresponds to a stage in adulthood that, in human equivalent years (age >30)41, aligns with a period of sharply rising stroke incidence42, providing relevance to human stroke, while older relevant age groups were not examined here. Data from seven animals included in this study were previously published12; however, the analyses presented here are distinct and novel.

Acute brain slice preparation

Animals were sacrificed by isoflurane anesthesia and decapitation. Brains were rapidly removed and transferred to ice-cooled artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF). Coronal slices (300 µm thickness) were cut using a vibratome (Leica VT1200S, Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany), incubated in warmed aCSF (35 °C) for 30 min and thereafter stored at room temperature (20–22 °C) at which experiments were performed.

Solutions and drugs

Chemicals were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. The aCSF contained in mM: 125 NaCl, 2.5 or 5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 26 NaHCO3, 1.8 MgCl2, 2 CaCl2, and 20 glucose and was continuously carbogenated (95% O2, 5% CO2; pH 7.4, osmolarity ~305 mOsm). For oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) experiments, equimolar amounts of saccharose replaced glucose, nitrogen displaced oxygen (N2, 95%, 5% CO2; pH 7.4, osmolarity ~305 mOsm), and K+ was elevated to 5 mM to counterbalance constant dilution of accumulating [K+]o, as previously described12,43. Notably, the [K+]o threshold at which SDs occur independent of OGD is substantially higher, i.e., ~15 mM [K+]o44–46.

Electrophysiological recordings

Experiments were performed in an open bath perfusion chamber with slices maintained under submerged conditions (Warner Instruments, Holliston, MA, USA, perfusion rate 6-8 ml/min). Slice movement was prevented by a nylon grid mounted to a U-shaped platinum wire. For electrophysiological detection of SDs, cortical direct current (DC) local field potentials (LFP) were recorded ~50 µm below slice surface with microelectrodes prepared from borosilicate glass capillaries (Science Products, Hofheim, Germany; pipette puller PC-10, Narishige, Tokyo, Japan) The LFP was amplified (EXT-10) and low-pass filtered (3 kHz, LPBF-01GX, both npi electronic, Tamm, Germany) before digitization (CED-1401 Micro3 with Spike2 9.01 software, Cambridge Electronic Design, Cambridge).

Intrinsic optical signal and FRET-based ATP imaging

Brain slices from ThyAT mice were imaged using 2-photon laser scanning microscopy (Ti:Sapphire laser; 810 nm; Mai Tai DeepSee, Spectra-Physics, Milpitas, CA, USA) as previously described12. The ATP sensor reports signals from dendritic, axonal, and somatic neuronal compartments17,47. To detect the onset and propagation of SDs, we captured transmitted light and calculated the intrinsic optical signal (IOS, cf. ‘Data analysis’). The IOS shows a characteristic SD-associated peak18.

To monitor changes in neuronal ATP fluorescence signals, signals were acquired from the Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET)-based ATP nanosensor ATeam 1.03YEMK. The sensor comprises a donor domain (cyan fluorescent protein, CFP), an acceptor domain (yellow fluorescent protein, YFP), and an ATP-binding site40, producing ATP-dependent FRET. The background-corrected YFP/CFP emission ratio provided a semi-quantitative measure of intracellular neuronal ATP, with percentual changes not strictly equivalent to changes in ATP. As previously described12, excitation at 810 nm preferentially excited the donor fluorophore CFP (>60% of its maximal excitation) while minimizing direct YFP excitation (<5% of its maximal excitation; (https://public.brain.mpg.de/shiny/apps/SpectraViewer/) thereby maximizing the contribution of FRET to the YFP signal. Fluorescence was low-pass filtered at 690 nm. CFP and YFP fluorescence were separated by a dichroic mirror (D510 nm), and CFP emission was further isolated with a bandpass filter (median wavelength 480 nm/bandwidth 40 nm, F47-480SG, AHF Analysentechnik AG, Tübingen, Germany). Time-lapse xyz-stacks were acquired using a laser scanning microscope (Olympus Fluoview 10 M, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a 3.32x objective (NA: 0.14, Olympus) for a duration of ~30 min. Stacks consisted of 6-11 individual z-planes (total thickness of 24-44 µm, median: 30 µm) centered around 50 µm below the slice surface acquired at xy resolutions of 512 × 512 to 640×640 pixels (pixel size: 7.5 µm and 6 µm, respectively) and inter-stack time intervals of 20-60 s. Baseline recordings (5-10 stacks, ~5 minutes) preceded OGD induction.

Data analysis

All data were analyzed in Igor Pro 8 (WaveMetrics, Inc, Lake Oswego, Oregon, USA) and Matlab (The MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA, version R2022a). Horizontal motion in the imaging data was corrected using a Fast Fourier Transformation-based registration algorithm48. The IOS was calculated pixel-wise as the percentage change of transmitted light during OGD compared to the averaged transmission before OGD, providing spatial resolution to detect SDs in different brain regions. SDs were identified by their characteristic IOS peak18. Furthermore, SDs were confirmed by cortical recording of the DC-LFP in one location (Fig. 1 a, c).

Analysis of ATP signals, i.e., YFP and CFP emission, involved compression of individual z-planes into maximum intensity z-projections, followed by background correction through subtraction of signals from sensor-free compartments such as the lateral ventricles. Subsequently, ATP maps were generated by pixel-wise calculation of the background-corrected YFP/CFP ratio. Dynamic ATP profiles were then extracted from regions of interest (ROIs) in defined anatomical brain regions (see below). Group summary data represent ATP profiles self-normalized to their baseline average before OGD.

Allocation of brain regions

First, slice positions relative to bregma were determined using a standard mouse brain atlas49, a manual process based on anatomical landmarks, including location, shape, and size of the lateral and, when present, third ventricles; the morphology of the corpus callosum and the cingulum; the appearance of subcortical structures (caudate putamen, thalamus, hippocampus); and cortical thickness. Subsequently, circular ROIs (diameter: ~200 µm) were manually selected in different brain regions, i.e., S1, M1, and subcortical caudate putamen or thalamus, and verified to be located within their anatomical boundaries according to the corresponding atlas sections. Coronal slice positions ranged from −1.5 mm to 0.9 mm relative to bregma.

Data reporting and statistical analysis

This is an exploratory study. Due to the lack of prior evidence, sample sizes could not be accurately estimated and were selected to comply with similar research. 5 slices (from 5 mice) were excluded from analysis of the SD onset location (Fig. 2c) and from ATP analysis (Fig. 2d-f), because the site of SD onset was not within the field of view. In addition, 3 slices (2 mice) that exhibited parallel SD onset in S1 and SUB were excluded from the statistical analysis of SD onset locations to avoid double-counting. Sex distributions across groups are specified in Supplementary Table 1. Sex-dependent differences were not statistically tested.

Data are reported as median and interquartile 1 (IQ1, 25th percentile) and IQ3 (75th percentile). Boxes in boxplots display the median, IQ1, and IQ3 mark lower and upper box limits, whiskers extend to the extrema unless considered as outliers (‘+’, values > 1.5*interquartile range). Data analysis was not blinded. Statistical inference on categorial and numerical data was performed by Fisher’s exact test and the Wilcoxon test, respectively. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Bonferroni post-hoc test. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05. Data will be made available upon reasonable request.
