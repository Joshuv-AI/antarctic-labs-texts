Detecting amyloid–β pathology in subjective cognitive decline using plasma and CSF biomarkers

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundSubjective cognitive decline (SCD) may represent an early stage of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), but biomarker studies in this population are limited. Therefore, we compared the performance of a comprehensive panel of novel cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood biomarkers across different platforms for detecting amyloid-β (Aβ) pathology in SCD.Methods143 individuals with SCD (20% Aβ-positive) from the β-AARC cohort were included. Aβ status was determined by CSF Aβ42/40. CSF and blood biomarkers of Aβ, tau, synaptic function, neurodegeneration, and glial reactivity were measured cross-sectionally.FindingsIn CSF, novel tau variants (p-tau205, p-tau217, p-tau231, p-tau235, NTA-tau) were elevated in Aβ-positive individuals with SCD. In plasma, p-tau217, p-tau217/Aβ42 (across multiple platforms), as well as p-tau181, p-tau181/Aβ42 and Aβ42/40 (platform-dependent) demonstrated strong discriminatory performance and high negative predictive value, but limited positive predictive value because of the low prevalence of Aβ pathology.InterpretationIn conclusion, several plasma biomarkers can exclude Aβ pathology in SCD, while some CSF and plasma markers indicate early AD-related changes. These plasma biomarkers could serve as accessible, cost-effective tools for early AD detection and risk stratification, and expedite the selection of candidates for disease-modifying treatments or preventive strategies.FundingThe research leading to these results has received funding from the 10.13039/100002565Alzheimer’s Drug Discovery Foundation (ADDF) grants #RDADB-201906-2018897 and #201809-2016862. Research in context

Evidence before this study

We conducted a PubMed literature review to identify studies on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood biomarkers in subjective cognitive decline (SCD). Studies specifically addressing SCD, particularly with both Alzheimer’s disease (AD) core and novel biomarkers, are limited. Our study in participants with SCD included a comprehensive panel of CSF and plasma biomarkers reflecting key AD pathophysiological mechanisms.

Added value of this study

Our findings highlight the potential of blood biomarkers for early Aβ pathology detection in individuals with SCD. Plasma p-tau217, p-tau217/Aβ42, Aβ42/40, and p-tau181/Aβ42 demonstrated high accuracy, sensitivity, and negative predictive values for ruling out Aβ pathology in SCD. In CSF, non-core tau species, including p-tau217, p-tau231, p-tau205, p-tau235, and NTA-tau, revealed early changes, also providing insight into AD pathophysiology in early stages.

Implications of all the available evidence

Tau plasma biomarkers could serve as accessible, cost-effective tools for early AD detection and risk stratification. Biomarkers of glial reactivity, neurodegeneration, and synaptic dysfunction are less altered, although they may reflect early pathophysiological processes and may help identify subgroups among individuals with SCD. Future studies are needed across diverse populations and settings to confirm the utility of CSF and blood biomarkers in early Aβ detection and their impact on SCD management.

Introduction

Subjective cognitive decline (SCD) is defined as the perception of cognitive difficulties despite a lack of objective cognitive impairment on formal assessments.1 Individuals with SCD experience a persistent sense of decline in their cognitive abilities, with normal performance on standard neuropsychological tests. While SCD can result from various aetiologies, including non-neurodegenerative conditions, it may also represent an early symptom of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Indeed, individuals with SCD who are positive for core biomarkers of AD in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) have an increased risk of progressing to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia due to AD.2, 3, 4 Identifying individuals without cognitive impairment with SCD and characterising their biomarker profiles is essential for refining early detection strategies and potentially delaying disease progression. This becomes increasingly relevant with the advent of new disease-modifying anti-amyloid-β (Aβ) treatments, which are currently approved in some countries only for MCI and early dementia due to AD.5,6 Ongoing clinical trials are also investigating disease-modifying drugs in individuals without cognitive impairment at risk of AD, including those with SCD.1,7

In individuals with SCD, previous studies have investigated CSF core AD biomarkers8,9 and additional biomarkers of downstream processes, such as CSF Neurogranin, NfL, sTREM2 and YKL40, although often in the context of larger cohorts including MCI and dementia stages.10, 11, 12 In recent years, however, a wider range of CSF biomarkers has been developed to capture early AD pathophysiological mechanisms,13, 14, 15 yet many of these remain underexplored in SCD. Furthermore, the advent of blood-based biomarkers represents a major breakthrough in the AD field, offering a cost-effective and minimally invasive alternative that could streamline the diagnostic pathway, enable timely and accurate diagnoses, and improve access to targeted treatments and quality care. While some studies have examined these biomarkers in individuals with SCD,16, 17, 18, 19 including those from the general population or those seeking medical advice, specific data on SCD remains limited.20, 21, 22 Therefore, a significant knowledge gap exists regarding the performance of novel CSF and blood-based biomarkers for detecting AD pathology specifically in individuals with SCD.

The main aim of this study was to compare the performance of a wide range of CSF and blood-based biomarkers, measured across multiple platforms and targeting both core AD pathology and downstream processes, in individuals with SCD. We evaluated participants from the Barcelonaβeta Brain Research Center’s Alzheimer’s at-risk cohort (β-AARC), a study specifically designed to investigate individuals seeking medical advice for cognitive complaints in primary or secondary care. First, we analysed differences in CSF and blood-based biomarkers between Aβ-positive (A+) and Aβ-negative (A−) individuals with SCD. Furthermore, we assessed the ability of this comprehensive range of CSF and blood-based biomarkers to detect Aβ pathology in individuals with SCD. Finally, we calculated the performance metrics at different cut-offs and discussed the potential clinical applications of CSF and blood-based biomarkers in SCD.

Methods

The β-AARC protocol

β-AARC is an ongoing longitudinal, prospective, and observational study aimed to investigate the factors involved in AD onset and disease progression in non-demented individuals seeking medical advice due to cognitive complaints. The goal is to describe the profile of biomarkers in early AD stages, as well as their capacity to predict underlying AD pathology and future cognitive impairment. Inclusion and exclusion criteria of the β-AARC study.

As of 9 February 2024, 340 individuals were pre-screened (V0). Of these, 207 have been screened on-site (V1). One hundred sixty-six individuals with SCD or MCI underwent a lumbar puncture, and 11 are pending biomarkers results. For the present study, we analysed baseline CSF and blood biomarkers levels of participants with SCD and available AD core CSF biomarkers levels at baseline (V1, n = 143). The study flowchart is presented in Supplementary Fig. S1.

The complete protocol is available in the Supplementary Material. In brief, after pre-screening (V0), eligible participants are invited for an in-person visit at BBRC (V1). This includes a neurological assessment, cognitive and lifestyle questionnaires, blood extraction, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and an extensive neuropsychological assessment to establish the clinical diagnosis of SCD1,23 or MCI.24 Then, participants undergo lumbar puncture and blood extraction for CSF and blood biomarkers. The questionnaires include assessments for SCD, administered to both the participant via the MICOG and the study partner via the SUCOG25; measures of cognitive reserve; lifestyle-related factors; mood-related measures; as well as coping skills, and Personality and Attitude regarding ageing questionnaires.

Basic biochemistry analysis includes haemoglobin, platelets, glucose, creatinine, urea, uric acid, bilirubin, AST, ALT, GGT, ALP, total proteins, albumin, TSH, fT3, fT4, HbA1c, CRP. eGFR was estimated with CKD-EPI.

CSF and plasma collection and processing followed standard procedures. CSF and plasma biomarkers are measured using different platforms and assays, described in detail in the Supplementary Material. In brief, CSF Aβ42, Aβ40, p-tau181, and t-tau were measured using a Lumipulse G600II or a Lumipulse G1200 (Fujirebio Europe N.V., Gent, Belgium). The Roche platform was used to measure CSF Aβ42, Aβ40, α-synuclein, GFAP, IL6, Neurogranin, NfL, p-tau181, S100B, SNAP25, sTREM2, t-tau and YKL40 with the NeuroToolKit (NTK), a panel of exploratory robust prototype assays, on a Cobas® e 411 analyser, e 601 module or e 801 analytical unit (all Roche Diagnostics International Ltd, Rotkreuz, Switzerland). The commercial Simoa ALZpath pTau217 assay was used to measure CSF p-tau217 on a Simoa HD-X instrument. Other non-core tau species in CSF (p-tau205, p-tau231, p-tau235 and NTA-tau) were measured with the Simoa HD-X platform (Quanterix). CSF Aβ42/40, p-tau181/Aβ42 and p-tau217/Aβ42 ratios were calculated using assays from the same platform.

Plasma Aβ42, Aβ40, p-tau181 and p-tau217 levels were measured with a Lumipulse G1200 (Fujirebio Europe N.V., Gent, Belgium). The Roche platform was used to measure plasma Aβ42, Aβ40, p-tau181, p-tau217, GFAP, NfL, SNAP25, and YKL40 with the NTK on an e 411 analyser, e 601 module or e 801 analytical unit.26 The Simoa platform was used to measure plasma Aβ42, Aβ40, GFAP and NfL levels using the commercial Simoa Neurology 4-Plex E Advantage Kit, and plasma p-tau181 using the Simoa pTau-181 Advantage V2.1 Kit. The commercial Simoa ALZpath pTau217 assay was used to measure plasma p-tau217 on a Simoa HD-X instrument.27 Plasma p-tau231 was measured using a previously developed in-house assay at the University of Gothenburg (UGot p-tau231).28,29 Plasma Aβ42/40, p-tau181/Aβ42, p-tau217/Aβ42, and p-tau231/Aβ42 ratios were calculated using assays from the same platform.

The main outcome was A+ status identification, as defined by Lumipulse CSF Aβ42/40 ratio.30 The cut-off for A+ was set at < 0.062.31

For sensitivity analyses, AD pathologic changes were also defined with Roche CSF p-tau181/Aβ42 ratio (Roche Diagnostics International Ltd, Rotkreuz, Switzerland); the cut-off was set at > 0.023.32 A complete case sensitivity analysis including participants with all biomarkers available was also performed (n = 89 for CSF and n = 99 for plasma). We excluded CSF Roche SNAP25, which only had 47 cases.

Ethics

The β-AARC study was approved by the independent ethics committee ‘Parc de Salut Mar’, Barcelona (reference number: 2021/9785/I). Written informed consent was provided by all participants. The study and the manuscript adhere to the 2024 Declaration of Helsinki ethical principles. Further information can be found in Clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04935372).

Statistical analyses

We assessed normality for continuous variables using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Since most variables were not normally distributed, we used the Wilcoxon rank sum test to compare participants with SCD who were A+ and A−. Categorical variables were compared using a chi-squared test (χ2). To establish the percentage of participants with positive plasma Simoa NfL we used the previously published age-specific cut-offs.33

Next, differences in CSF and plasma biomarkers between individuals with SCD who were A+ and A− were analysed using unadjusted and age- and sex-adjusted multiple linear regression models. Effect sizes were calculated with Cohen’s d, and fold of change was computed from the differences in the medians.

To investigate effects of potential confounders, we conducted linear regression analyses, modelling each plasma biomarker as the dependent variable, with age, sex, and each potential confounder as independent variables. Additional models were also adjusted for Aβ status. Linear models assumptions (normality, homoscedasticity, outliers, autocorrelation, multicollinearity) were checked. Regression coefficients were also estimated using non-parametric bootstrap resampling (2000 iterations) to ensure robustness. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to evaluate differences in plasma biomarkers performances in predicting Aβ status in the group with eGFR below vs. above the median.

To assess the ability of CSF and blood biomarkers to discriminate between individuals with SCD who were A+ and A−, we performed logistic regressions and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses, calculating the areas under the curve (AUCs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The AUCs of plasma and CSF biomarkers were compared using the DeLong test across different platforms.34 Optimal biomarker cut-offs were determined using two approaches: (i) single-cutoff, with the highest Youden’s index (sensitivity + specificity − 1) or (ii) two-cutoff, setting the sensitivity at 90% or the specificity at 90%.35 Sensitivity, specificity, accuracy (defined as true cases over total cases), positive (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV), as well as the numbers of individuals falling within the intermediate zone36,37 were computed for each biomarker using these cut-offs.

To evaluate the clinical robustness of plasma biomarkers, we simulated increasing levels of random pre-analytical variability in raw biomarker values by adding Gaussian noise to each observation, with a standard deviation defined by the specified coefficient of variation (CV), range 0%–25%, multiplied by the mean biomarker value across individuals.38 For biomarker ratios, noise was added separately to the numerator and denominator prior to ratio calculation. Only complete cases were included in these analyses. The simulation was repeated 1000 times per CV level, and in each iteration the ROC curve and AUC (with 95% CI) were computed, averaging results across iterations. Each iteration added a different random noise vector, ensuring reproducibility by setting a fixed seed. Biomarkers were considered robust if the mean AUC decreased by less than 10% at an added CV of 15%.

All P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the False Discovery Rate (FDR) at level α = 0.05 (labelled in the text as PFDR).39

All analyses were performed in R (v 4.4.2) and the pROC package was used for the estimation of AUC.

Participants with SCD A+ were older (Wilcoxon rank sum test, P < 0.001) and had a longer symptom duration (Wilcoxon rank sum test, P = 0.03) compared with those who were SCD A−. No differences were found in sex distribution, years of education, family history of dementia, MMSE, M@T, total MICOG or SUCOG, or emotional status (as measured by HADS) between participants with SCD who were A+ and A− (Wilcoxon rank sum test, all P > 0.05).

Participants with SCD A+ exhibited a higher frequency of dyslipidaemia (χ2 test, P = 0.03) and insomnia (χ2 test, P = 0.01) diagnoses compared with those who were SCD A−, and no differences were found in other cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes or BMI. Serum creatinine levels were higher in participants with SCD A− (Wilcoxon rank sum test, P = 0.03), although eGFR did not differ between the two groups. Plasma Simoa NfL was abnormal in 16% (16/103) of A− and in 21% (6/29) of individuals with SCD A+. Detailed clinical characteristics of the participants are provided in Table 2 and Supplementary Table S1.

Fluid biomarkers differences between Aβ groups

A total of 26 CSF and 29 blood-based biomarkers (including ratios) were compared between the SCD A+ and SCD A− groups. CSF biomarkers

All AD CSF core biomarkers were significantly changed in SCD A+ compared with SCD A− (Table 3, Supplementary Fig. S2). Specifically, individuals with SCD who were A+ had lower CSF Roche Aβ42/40 levels, with similar effect size (d = −4.27) of that of CSF Lumipulse Aβ42/40, used as the reference standard in our study. In addition, SCD A+ showed higher CSF p-tau181/Aβ42 ratio (Lumipulse: d = 1.71, Roche: d = 1.50) and p-tau181 (Lumipulse: d = 1.51, Roche: d = 1.22).

Regarding tau species, CSF UGot p-tau231 (d = 2.53), followed by UGot p-tau205 (d = 1.85), ALZpath Simoa p-tau217 (d = 1.75), UGot p-tau235 (d = 1.71), and UGot NTA-tau (d = 1.29), were also increased in individuals with SCD A+ compared with those who were SCD A− (all PFDR < 0.001, Table 3).

Several additional CSF biomarkers reflecting diverse pathophysiological mechanisms were elevated in SCD A+, including markers of neurodegeneration (CSF Roche NfL), synaptic dysfunction (CSF Roche α-synuclein, Neurogranin, SNAP25), and glial reactivity (CSF Roche YKL40) (Wilcoxon rank sum test, all PFDR < 0.05, Table 3). In contrast, no differences were found in CSF Roche GFAP, IL6, or S100B (Wilcoxon rank sum test, PFDR > 0.05, Table 3).

After adjusting for age and sex, the differences observed in CSF biomarkers levels remained significant, except for CSF Roche α-synuclein (PFDR = 0.133), while CSF Roche sTREM2 became significantly elevated in SCD A+ (PFDR = 0.004, Table 3). In the bootstrapping-adjusted models, differences in CSF Roche NfL, sTREM2 and YKL40 were no longer significant.

To allow comparability, a sensitivity analysis restricted to the 89 participants with all CSF biomarkers available was conducted, excluding CSF Roche SNAP25, since it was measured only in 47 participants. The results remain consistent, although the difference in CSF Roche NfL between groups was no longer statistically significant (Supplementary Table S2).

The distribution of CSF biomarkers levels by Aβ status is shown in Supplementary Fig. S2.

Plasma biomarkers

Plasma Aβ42/40 was significantly lower in individuals with SCD A+ compared with those who were SCD A− across all platforms (Table 4, Supplementary Fig. S3), including Lumipulse (d = −1.89), Roche (d = −1.37), and Simoa (d = −1.11). Plasma p-tau217 (Simoa: d = 2.08, Roche: d = 1.93, Lumipulse: d = 1.55), and the p-tau217/Aβ42 ratios (Roche: d = 2.54, Simoa: d = 2.20, Lumipulse: d = 1.90) showed the largest effect size changes, with all significantly increased in individuals with SCD A+. Plasma p-tau181 (Roche: d = 1.61, Lumipulse: d = 1.12, Simoa: d = 0.73) and the p-tau181/Aβ42 ratios (Roche: d = 2.30, Lumipulse: d = 1.78, Simoa: d = 1.21) were also significantly increased in individuals with SCD A+. Plasma Simoa p-tau231 was significantly increased in SCD A+ (d = 0.57), as well as the plasma Simoa p-tau231/Aβ42 ratio (d = 1.24).

Other plasma biomarkers indicative of AD pathophysiological mechanisms were significantly elevated in individuals with SCD A+, including markers of neurodegeneration (plasma Roche NfL), synaptic dysfunction (plasma Roche SNAP25), and glial reactivity (plasma Roche GFAP and YKL40), albeit with smaller effect size differences (Table 4). After age and sex adjustment, the previously observed differences in plasma biomarker levels remained significant; however, the differences in plasma Roche NfL and YKL40, as well as plasma Simoa NfL, did not retain statistical significance in the bootstrap-adjusted analyses (Table 4).

To allow comparability, a sensitivity analysis restricted to the 99 participants with all plasma biomarkers available is presented in Supplementary Table S3. All the previous differences were maintained.

The distribution of values of plasma biomarkers by Aβ status is shown in Supplementary Fig. S3.

Effect of comorbidities in plasma biomarkers

We then examined the potential impact of several relevant comorbidities on blood-based biomarkers levels after sex and age adjustment (Supplementary Table S4). Worse renal function (with lower eGFR estimated with CKD-EPI) was associated with higher levels of plasma Aβ40 (measured by Lumipulse and Roche), Aβ42 (by Lumipulse and Roche) and Simoa p-tau231 (all PFDR < 0.05), though the plasma Aβ42/40 ratio and p-tau231/Aβ42 were not affected by renal filtration (all PFDR > 0.05). Higher plasma p-tau181 (Simoa) and NfL (Roche) levels were also significantly associated with worse renal function (PFDR < 0.05). After additional adjustment for Aβ status, all these associations remained significant (Supplementary Table S5). Remarkably, there was no significant association between renal function and plasma p-tau217 or the ratios p-tau181/Aβ42 and p-tau217/Aβ42 across any platform (Supplementary Table S4).

Regarding hepatic enzymes, higher AST was associated with higher plasma Roche p-tau181 (PFDR < 0.05), but that association disappeared after adjusting for Aβ status (Supplementary Table S5). Higher AST and ALT were associated with increased plasma Roche YKL40 (all PFDR < 0.05), and these associations remained significant after adjusting for Aβ status. No significant associations were observed between other plasma biomarkers and BMI, eGFR, AST, or ALT, with or without adjusting for Aβ status.

All associations that were statistically significant in classical linear models did not retain significance when non-parametric bootstrap inference was applied.

The AUC of p-tau217/Aβ42 ratio was significantly better than that of p-tau217, using either Lumipulse, Roche or Simoa platforms (DeLong test PFDR < 0.001). The robustness analysis revealed that the performance of the plasma Aβ42/40 ratio was more sensitive to added random variation, while the performance of other plasma biomarkers, particularly plasma p-tau217 and plasma p-tau217/Aβ42 ratios, remained more stable (see Supplementary Fig. S4). No significant differences in AUC were observed between participants with eGFR below vs. above the median value of 85.6 ml/min/1.73 m2 for any of the plasma biomarkers (all PFDR > 0.05; Supplementary Table S10).

Using a single-cutoff approach (calculated with the Youden’s index), plasma Aβ42/40, p-tau181, p-tau181/Aβ42, p-tau217, p-tau217/Aβ42 (across all platforms), Lumipulse and Roche Aβ42, Simoa p-tau231, Simoa p-tau231/Aβ42, Roche and Simoa GFAP, Roche and Simoa NfL, and Roche SNAP25 reached a NPV > 90% to rule out Aβ pathology in individuals with SCD (Table 6).

Herein, we also performed three sensitivity analyses. The first one was restricted to the 99 participants with all plasma biomarkers to ensure comparability (Supplementary Table S12). NPVs remained similar, with the highest PPV reached by Simoa p-tau217 (77.8%). In the second analysis we used Roche CSF p-tau181/Aβ42 > 0.023 to define AD (Supplementary Table S13); NPVs were above 90% for almost all plasma biomarkers, but the highest PPV was only 76.7%, reached by Roche p-tau217. In the third analysis, we still applied the Roche CSF p-tau181/Aβ42 ratio to define AD, but used a two-cutoff approach (Supplementary Table S14); this generally resulted in a lower percentage of cases falling within the intermediate zone compared to the same approach with Lumipulse CSF Aβ42/40 to define AD. Discussion

In this study, we investigated 55 CSF and blood-based biomarkers, some across different platforms, in a well-characterised cohort of individuals with SCD. Most biomarkers showed significant differences between individuals with SCD who were A+ and A−. In CSF, beyond core AD biomarkers, substantial changes and high discrimination accuracies were observed for novel tau variants, particularly p-tau217 and p-tau231, but also p-tau205, p-tau235, and NTA-tau. In plasma, the largest magnitudes of change and the highest discrimination accuracies were found for p-tau217, Aβ42/40, p-tau181/Aβ42, and p-tau217/Aβ42. Using a two-cutoff approach with these plasma biomarkers, the number of participants in the intermediate zone was low. Although high-performance biomarkers demonstrated strong sensitivity and specificity, PPVs were modest due to the low prevalence of individuals who were A+ in the SCD population. However, their high NPVs suggest that these biomarkers may be useful for ruling out Aβ pathology in SCD. In addition, while some blood-based biomarkers might be influenced by factors such as renal and liver function or face analytical robustness challenges (i.e., Aβ42/40), p-tau217, p-tau181/Aβ42, and p-tau217/Aβ42 appeared unaffected, highlighting their potential reliability and clinical utility. Finally, CSF and plasma biomarkers of astrocytic reactivity, neurodegeneration, and synaptic dysfunction also exhibited changes in individuals who were A+, albeit of lower magnitude compared with core biomarkers. This suggests that these processes are already ongoing in early stages of AD in SCD.

SCD is highly prevalent, occurring ten times more frequently than MCI.40 Despite being introduced as a well-defined concept a decade ago, relatively little literature has been produced specifically on biomarkers in SCD. A few large prospective cohorts, notably in Amsterdam41 and Cologne,42 include CSF and plasma biomarkers. The scarce literature on biomarkers in SCD may reflect the limited use of lumbar puncture in clinical settings, where SCD is seen as a risk factor rather than a disease. Moreover, without approved treatments for this stage, identifying AD entails challenges for result disclosure, albeit feasible,43,44 keeping its use mostly within research.

Our study includes a large number of CSF and plasma biomarkers in a SCD cohort, capturing multiple pathophysiological mechanisms implicated in early AD. Beyond confirming the expected accuracy of core AD CSF biomarkers, we found that novel tau variants demonstrate comparable or even superior discriminative performance in SCD. Notably, CSF p-tau217 and p-tau231 showed the highest AUC, without significant differences with CSF p-tau181/Aβ42. This aligns with other cohorts findings,45,46 supporting CSF p-tau217 and p-tau231 as early markers of Aβ pathology.45,47 Other tau variants also distinguished individuals with SCD who were A+ from those who were A− with high accuracy in our cohort, achieving AUCs of 0.920 (p-tau205), 0.882 (p-tau235), and 0.819 (NTA-tau). Unlike p-tau217 and p-tau231, these variants may rise later and be linked to more advanced tau deposition in the brain.48, 49, 50, 51, 52

Blood-based biomarkers in SCD have been less explored, particularly in direct comparisons across assays and platforms. In the FACEHBI cohort (200 individuals with SCD, 18% of whom were A+ using amyloid-PET), plasma Aβ42/40 had an AUC of 0.87, slightly inferior than ours.16 In FACEHBI and SCIENCe cohorts, this ratio was associated with progression to MCI at two years and longitudinal cognitive decline on neuropsychological tests.16,17 Giacomucci et al. investigated 28 individuals with SCD in Florence (30% A+ by CSF or amyloid-PET), showing that plasma p-tau181 and NfL discriminated A+ from A− with accuracies of 0.79 and 0.80, respectively.21,53 Similarly, in a cohort of 50 individuals with SCD in Amsterdam (38% A+ by amyloid-PET), plasma p-tau181 accuracy reached 0.83.54

Plasma p-tau217 has emerged as a key biomarker for detecting AD pathology in MCI and dementia stages,55,56 and also in individuals without cognitive impairment.57, 58, 59, 60 Similarly, our findings in SCD show that plasma p-tau217, p-tau217/Aβ42 (across multiple platforms), and p-tau181/Aβ42 and Aβ42/40 (platform-dependent) have high diagnostic accuracy for detecting Aβ pathology. However, probably due to the low prevalence of A+ in SCD (20% here), PPVs are modest, whereas NPVs are high, exceeding 0.90 with both single and two-cutoff approaches. This suggests that these plasma biomarkers, especially with high-sensitivity cut-offs, could be particularly useful for ruling out Aβ pathology in individuals with SCD, while, to date, further tests are needed to confirm it.

Aβ42 inclusion in plasma p-tau181/Aβ42 and p-tau217/Aβ42 significantly improves the AUC, although the difference in AUCs is minimal. Importantly, it also reduces the number of participants in the intermediate zone in a two-cutoff approach. Furthermore, p-tau217, p-tau181/Aβ42 and p-tau217/Aβ42 remain unaffected by renal function. Unlike plasma Aβ42/40, these biomarkers showed robust performance even with up to 20% pre-analytical variability. Despite the good performance of using Aβ42 in the denominator of the p-tau ratios in our cohort, it is important to consider that Aβ42 is sensitive to preanalytical handling due to its adhesive properties and lower robustness. In multicentre cohort studies and clinical practice, even slight differences in sample processing may compromise the robustness of Aβ42 ratios. In such cases, using a single biomarker may offer greater reliability in clinical practice. That said, the PPV of all plasma biomarkers remains insufficient to justify a two-cutoff approach to confirm Aβ positivity without further testing (e.g., CSF or PET).

CSF and plasma biomarkers of glial reactivity, neurodegeneration, and synaptic dysfunction showed changes, though less pronounced than amyloid- and tau-related biomarkers, suggesting that these processes are active in early AD stages in SCD. Our study highlights that certain pathological pathways occur before cognitive decline, while others may not be captured by the tested biomarkers. Notably, glial reactivity, especially involving astrocytes, appears central to early AD, while synaptic dysfunction and neuronal injury suggest potential treatment targets beyond brain amyloidosis. As expected, GFAP performed better in plasma than in CSF.14,61 Although the reasons for this remain unclear, GFAP appears to be less stable in CSF than in blood.62 In addition, biological mechanisms may contribute, including direct release of astrocytic GFAP from perivascular end-feet into the bloodstream at the blood–brain barrier, partially bypassing CSF dynamics. The combination of biomarkers may help identify distinct SCD subgroups and reveal divergent trajectories linked to different pathophysiological pathways.

Determining the appropriateness of testing individuals with SCD for underlying AD pathology remains challenging. The new Alzheimer’s Association criteria state that AD can be detected in individuals without cognitive impairment, but testing should not be performed at the moment,63 while the IWG discourages the use of biomarkers to diagnose AD in SCD.64

Given the absence of disease-modifying treatments at this early stage, testing may still be considered premature. However, ongoing trials, such as AHEAD 3–45,7 could show that early disease-modifying treatments initiation may slow cognitive decline. Additionally, individuals with SCD may want to know their AD biomarkers status for preventive lifestyle interventions and to assess their dementia risk. Although indiscriminate biomarker use in clinical practice raises ethical concerns, testing could be valuable within specialised preventive services, such as Brain Health Services (BHS),65 when biomarkers demonstrate strong performance and results are communicated properly. If plasma biomarker results exceed the cut-off to rule out AD, closer follow-up within specialised services could be offered, additional confirmatory testing conducted, and potential preventive interventions guided, always ensuring clear communication and respect for the individual’s autonomy. Finally, our results may support the use of plasma biomarkers to streamline candidate selection for clinical trials on disease-modifying treatments for AD in SCD.

This study has several strengths. It comprehensively investigates an extensive number of CSF and blood-based biomarkers in a large cohort exclusively comprised of individuals with SCD seeking medical advice. This is relevant, as they represent a key target for early triaging, where NPVs are more meaningful due to low pre-test probability. Our study was conducted in a well-characterised cohort, incorporating core AD CSF biomarkers and accounting for relevant comorbidities, with sensitivity analyses.

However, our study has some limitations. Amyloid-PET and neuropathological confirmation were not available in this cohort, but we used CSF Aβ42/40, which is a widely validated reference standard commonly used in both research and clinical settings. The relatively low prevalence of A+ in this cohort, while expected in SCD, may limit the statistical power. Additionally, the cross-sectional observational design restricts causal conclusions on biomarkers reflecting different pathophysiological mechanisms. Not all biomarkers were available for the entire cohort; however, sensitivity analyses accounted for differences in sample size across assays. Being a single-centre study, the findings require validation in independent cohorts, especially from diverse geographic, ethnic and clinical settings. A+ prevalence may vary depending on endpoint choice and recruitment sources, such as primary care, general neurology practices, or specialised centres, but is expected to remain lower than in MCI or dementia. Thus, the high NPVs and modest PPVs observed here are likely consistent in similar scenarios.

In conclusion, our findings in a well-characterised cohort of individuals with SCD highlight the potential application of CSF and blood-based biomarkers for early AD detection, whether for research purposes or routine clinical practice aimed at preventive interventions such as in a BHS. Plasma p-tau217 and p-tau217/Aβ42 (across multiple platforms), as well as p-tau181/Aβ42 (platform-dependent) demonstrated the highest accuracy for detecting Aβ pathology, with robust performance and high NPVs, making them valuable for ruling out AD in SCD. These biomarkers could serve as accessible, cost-effective tools for early AD detection and risk stratification, while also expediting the selection of candidates for disease-modifying treatments or preventive strategies. Furthermore, specific amyloid and tau-related changes at this stage were observed through different tau species, including p-tau217 and p-tau231. Biomarkers of glial reactivity, neurodegeneration, and synaptic dysfunction were less altered, although they may reflect early pathophysiological processes and may help identify subgroups among individuals with SCD.

Further biomarker validation in diverse populations of individuals with SCD and settings is needed, as well as longitudinal studies evaluating the prognostic value of CSF and plasma biomarkers in early AD stages.
