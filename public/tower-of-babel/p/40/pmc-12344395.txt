Prognostic and Predictive Value of ctDNA for Metastatic Uveal Melanoma: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Mariana Macambira Noronha, Luís Felipe Leite da Silva, Luiz Felipe Costa Almeida, Pedro Robson Costa Passos, Pedro Cotta Abrahão Reis, João Evangelista Ponte Conrado, Valbert Oliveira Costa Filho, Lucas Diniz da Conceição, Mauricio F. S. A. Ribeiro, Samuel D. Saibil, Erick F. Saldanha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMetastatic uveal melanoma (mUM) is a rare disease associated with poor prognosis and limited therapeutic options. Recent studies showed that detecting ctDNA is feasible and can aid treatment decisions for patients with mUM. We systematically searched PubMed, EMBASE, and Cochrane databases for eligible studies published up to May 2025 that included patients with mUM and reported data on the association between ctDNA and survival outcomes (OS and PFS). Statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager 5.4 software. Of the initial 450 records, seven studies met eligibility, including 518 patients with mUM. At baseline, ctDNA positivity was associated with significantly worse PFS (HR 2.34; 95% CI 1.56–3.51; p < 0.01; I 2 = 0%) and OS (HR 3.32; 95% CI 2.09–5.29; p < 0.01; I 2 = 48%). In patients treated with tebentafusp, ctDNA clearance was associated with superior OS (HR 0.19; 95% CI 0.07–0.49; p < 0.01; I 2 = 46%) and any decrease in ctDNA was associated with better OS (HR 0.42; 95% CI 0.22–0.80; p < 0.01; I 2 = 0%). This meta‐analysis underscores ctDNA as a potential predictor of worse survival in patients with mUM, highlighting its potential to refine risk stratification and guide treatment strategies. Trial Registration: International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO): CRD42025638076

Introduction

Uveal melanoma (UM) is the most prevalent and aggressive ocular neoplasm in adults, but remains rare, affecting five individuals per million people worldwide (Ghazawi et al. 2019; Clevenger et al. 2023). UM originates from the extracutaneous melanocytes within the uveal tract of the eye, comprising the choroid (90%), ciliary body, and, less often, the iris (4%), and has a higher prevalence among non‐Hispanic Caucasian individuals (Hu et al. 2005). In contrast to cutaneous melanoma (CM) and its anatomical origin, ultraviolet light is not a risk factor, except for UM arising from the iris, and most patients will be diagnosed with primary UM confined to the eye (Carvajal et al. 2023; Johansson et al. 2020). Despite the early diagnosis and adequate focal treatment of the primary lesion, up to 50% of patients will develop metastatic disease, with an established pattern of hematogenous dissemination often to the liver, but can also include bones, soft tissue, and lung (Tóth et al. 2024; Beasley, Preen, et al. 2023; Switzer et al. 2023). UM accounts for 5% of all melanoma cases and has a distinct genomic profile compared to CM, commonly harboring primary mutations that drive activation of the G protein alpha pathway (GNAQ/11, PLCB4, and CYSTRL2) (Elder et al. 2020; Shain et al. 2019). While immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has changed the landscape of treatment for CM, in patients with metastatic uveal melanoma (mUM), several studies have shown limited response rates with no improvement in overall survival (OS) from ICB therapy (Bertolotto 2021; Yarchoan et al. 2017; Harbour et al. 2010; Karlsson et al. 2020).

Tebentafusp is a novel bispecific T‐cell engager (TCE) that targets glycoprotein 100 (gp100) peptide in the context of HLA‐A02 positive, and anti‐CD3 that redirects polyclonal T‐cells against melanoma cells, and was the first TCE to demonstrate an improvement in OS for patients with mUM and HLA‐A*02:01‐positive, achieving a median OS of 21.6 months and a 32% reduction in the mortality risk. Interestingly, the pivotal study has shown a low objective response (11%) and lack of improvement in progression‐free survival (PFS) for patients undergoing therapy with tebentafusp compared to the investigator's treatment choice (Hassel et al. 2023). However, patients presenting interval disease progression as best overall response also exhibited improved OS, showing a dissociation between conventional radiographic assessment and survival benefit. Hence, efforts to discover biomarkers for improved risk assessment in this selected population of patients undergoing treatment with tebentafusp are crucial (Saldanha et al. 2025).

Liquid biopsy (LB) refers to the analysis of tumor‐derived or tumor‐associated components present in bodily fluids, such as blood, urine, and cerebrospinal fluid, including, but not limited to, circulating cell‐free tumor DNA (ctDNA), extracellular vesicles (EVs), circulating tumor cells (CTCs), circulating endothelial cells (CECs), circulating cell‐free RNA (messenger RNA, miRNAs), tumor‐educated platelets, circulating proteins, immune components, and metabolites (Saldanha et al. 2024; Alix‐Panabières et al. 2023; Siravegna et al. 2019). In solid tumors, the application of LB is evolving rapidly, and in our 2024 report, we showed that although blood and ctDNA remain the most frequently analyzed fluid and analyte, respectively, other analytes are gaining traction, with their use varying by tumor type and clinical setting (Borea et al. 2025). Several studies have suggested the role of ctDNA as a minimally invasive, readily accessible approach for tumor molecular profiling and as a prognostic tool for several solid tumors, including UM, as demonstrated by our group and others (Ricciuti et al. 2021; Kato et al. 2022; Rodrigues et al. 2024; Wong et al. 2023; Cescon et al. 2020). Recent reports have also shown that longitudinal ctDNA dynamic changes may stratify patients who benefit from treatment in a timely fashion, allowing for a more personalized approach (Rodrigues et al. 2024; Leite da Silva et al. 2025).

Despite the recent survival benchmark achieved for patients with mUM treated with tebentafusp, only a subset of patients is eligible for this TCE, and optimal methods for response assessment to this therapy remain elusive. Further, no biomarker that can reliably risk‐stratify and aid clinicians when assessing response to treatment for patients with mUM has been established. This systematic review and meta‐analysis aims to investigate the role of ctDNA as a prognostic and predictive tool for patients with mUM.

Materials and Methods

Our systematic review and meta‐analysis were conducted following the Cochrane Collaboration guidelines and the recommendations of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Table S1A,B; Moher et al. 2009).

Search Strategy

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Cochrane, and Embase in November 2024. The research was last updated in May 2025, and it also included data from conferences from the American Society of Oncology, European Society of Medical Oncology, and American Association for Cancer Research. The following search strategy was applied: (“uveal melanoma” OR “choroidal neoplasms” OR “choroidal melanoma” OR “ocular melanoma” OR “eye neoplasm” OR “uveal neoplasms” OR “iris neoplasms” OR “ciliary body melanoma”) AND (“Circulating Tumor DNA” OR “ctDNA” OR “circulating tumor cells” OR “liquid biopsy” OR “ctDNA clearance” OR “longitudinal ctDNA”). The full search strategy is available in Table S2. Two independent reviewers (M.M.N. and P.R.C.P.) screened titles and abstracts using specialized software for systematic reviews, applying predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies were reviewed by a third investigator (L.F.L.S.), and all conflicts were resolved by consensus.

Eligibility Criteria

The eligibility criteria for study inclusion were as follows: (1) observational or interventional studies; (2) evaluating circulating tumor DNA; (3) in adult patients (≥ 18 years); (4) with histologically confirmed mUM; (5) who have undergone plasma‐based ctDNA analysis at least once. The treatment modality for mUM was not an exclusion criterion. The exclusion criteria included: (1) assessment of liquid biopsy modalities other than ctDNA (e.g., circulating tumor cells and circulating extracellular vesicles) or other fluids (e.g., aqueous humor); (2) case reports, reviews, editorials; (3) studies with insufficient data valuable for analysis; and (4) studies not published in English; (5) there was no restriction on the number of patients in each study.

Data Extraction and Outcomes

Two independent reviewers (M.M.N. and L.F.C.A.) extracted data from each study using a prespecified spreadsheet. The extracted information included the first author, year of publication, intervention, ctDNA analysis method, genomic profiling, time of collection, metastatic site, clinical and pathological characteristics of the patients (number of patients, median age, sex, American Joint Committee on Cancer staging), and hazard ratios of the survival analysis. To avoid data duplication for studies evaluating the same or overlapping patient cohorts, we selected the publication offering the most comprehensive data, characterized by complete methodology, results, and the longest follow‐up. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus with a third reviewer (E.F.S).

The primary objective of this meta‐analysis was to assess the prognostic role of ctDNA detection in patients with mUM. Therefore, the primary endpoints were OS, defined as the time from baseline, according to the definition of each study, to death from any cause, and PFS, defined as the time from baseline to disease progression or death from any cause. The secondary objective was to assess the association between longitudinal ctDNA reduction or clearance in patients with mUM undergoing systemic therapy. Additionally, subgroup analysis was conducted to determine the impact of the ctDNA assay on the outcomes.

Quality Assessment

The risk of bias in the non‐randomized clinical trials was assessed using the Risk of Bias in Non‐randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS‐I) tool (Sterne et al. 2016). The assessment covered the following domains: confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported result. Based on the evaluation, studies were classified as having a “low risk,” “moderate risk,” or “high risk” of bias. The risk of bias for randomized trials was evaluated with RoB 2 (Sterne et al. 2019). Hence, studies were accessed in five domains: (i) randomization method, (ii) deviations from intended interventions, (iii) missing outcome data, (iv) assessment of the outcome, and (v) selection of the reported result, and were categorized as having low, high, or uncertain risk of bias. Two authors (V.O.C.F. and P.C.A.R) performed this evaluation independently, with disagreements resolved through consensus. The likelihood of publication bias was assessed by a funnel plot of the primary outcome using the Egger test.

Statistical Analysis

The pooled outcomes were calculated using hazard ratios for time‐to‐event data and 95% confidence intervals, employing the restricted maximum‐likelihood estimator. The inverse‐variance method was applied for pooling. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran's Q test and I 2 statistics, categorized as low (< 25%), moderate (25%–50%), or high (> 50%). Analyses were conducted using random‐effects models. When hazard ratio values were not provided, individual patient data were obtained by digitizing the Kaplan–Meier curve with the “scanlt” software. The extracted data were then reconstructed using the IPDfromKM software to calculate the hazard ratio values (Liu et al. 2021). Statistical analyses were performed in Review Manager 5.4 software. All tests were two‐sided, with statistical significance set at a p value below 0.05.

Results

A total of 450 articles were identified, including 85 from PubMed, 357 from Embase, and eight from Cochrane. After removing duplicates and reviewing titles and abstracts, the full texts of 18 articles were assessed based on the eligibility criteria. Ultimately, four prospective cohorts and three clinical trials were included in the analysis, involving 518 patients and at least one ctDNA collection time point (Hassel et al. 2023; Rodrigues et al. 2024; Beasley, De Bruyn, et al. 2023; Carvajal et al. 2022; Mariani et al. 2023; Ny et al. 2021; Ramelyte et al. 2024). Baseline ctDNA Detection as a Prognostic Marker

The pooled analysis demonstrated that baseline ctDNA positivity was significantly associated with worse OS (HR 3.32; 95% CI 2.09–5.29; p < 0.01; I 2 = 48%) and PFS (HR 2.34; 95% CI 1.56–3.51; p < 0.01; I 2 = 0%) compared to ctDNA‐negative patients (Figure 3). This OS benefit was consistent across subgroup analyses leveraging different profiling methodologies, NGS (HR 3.22; 95% CI 1.82–5.68; p < 0.01; I 2 = 56%) and ddPCR (HR 3.95; 95% CI 1.37–11.37; p < 0.01; I 2 = 52%) for ctDNA identification. Improved PFS was also observed in ctDNA‐negative patients detected by ddPCR (HR 2.50; 95% CI 1.59–3.95; p < 0.01; I 2 = 0%) (Figure S1).

Assessment of ctDNA Kinetics for Patients Undergoing Tebentafusp

Patients with mUM undergoing tebentafusp therapy with prospective ctDNA collection, and who achieved ctDNA clearance, demonstrated a substantial improvement in OS compared to patients without ctDNA clearance (HR 0.19; 95% CI 0.07–0.49; p < 0.01; I 2 = 46%). Furthermore, tebentafusp treatment also demonstrated that patients with any ctDNA reduction (Carvajal et al. defined at 0.5 log ctDNA reduction and Rodrigues et al. defined as 90% decrease of MAF) exhibited a significant increase in OS when compared to patients who did not achieve ctDNA reduction (HR 0.42; 95% CI 0.22–0.80; p < 0.01; I 2 = 0%) (Figure 4; Rodrigues et al. 2024; Carvajal et al. 2022).

Quality Accessed

Overall, all the studies were considered to have a moderate risk of bias, primarily due to inadequately analyzed confounding factors in key variables, such as differences in patient populations (e.g., tumor burden, prior treatments) and variations in the systemic therapies administered. Also, inconsistencies in the methods and timing of ctDNA assessment were not sufficiently accounted for in the primary study analyses due to limited data. The full analysis is available in Figure S2. Moreover, the funnel plot of OS revealed a slight asymmetry, indicating a potential publication bias (Figure S3).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta‐analysis included seven studies, four prospective cohorts, and three clinical trials encompassing 518 patients with mUM. Our results revealed that the detection of ctDNA for patients with UM in the advanced setting is prognostic. ctDNA detection was significantly associated with worse survival outcomes, OS (HR 3.32) and PFS (HR 2.34), compared to patients without detectable ctDNA at the time of diagnosis of metastatic disease. An exploratory analysis conducted in a subset of patients with mUM and HLA‐A*02:01‐positive undergoing treatment with tebentafusp demonstrated that ctDNA kinetics, defined as clearance (absence of ctDNA detection) or reduction (any decrease in mutant allele frequency [MAF]), was associated with improved survival outcomes, OS (HR 0.19) for clearance and OS (HR 0.42) for decrease. These results suggest that changes in ctDNA levels during tebentafusp therapy can predict a patient's response to treatment, particularly that the depth of response can suggest an increased benefit in survival outcomes, which aligns with data reported elsewhere (Chen et al. 2024).

Patients with mUM have a poor prognosis and limited therapeutic options (Saldanha et al. 2025). Hence, strategies to refine risk assessment can allow personalization of treatment and ultimately improve survival outcomes for this rare population of patients. To this end, liquid biopsy technologies, including but not limited to ctDNA, emerged as a promising minimally invasive and readily available approach for prognosis assessment and prediction of therapeutic benefit across solid tumors in the advanced setting (Finkle et al. 2021; Sivapalan et al. 2023; Anagnostou et al. 2019; Raja et al. 2018; Dickinson et al. 2024; Gracie et al. 2021). Given its bona fide genomic traits, UM is particularly interesting for employing ctDNA assays leveraging somatic genome variations, as demonstrated in previous reports by our group and others (Rodrigues et al. 2024; Sato et al. 2024; De Bruyn et al. 2024). Our meta‐analysis revealed that all included studies leveraged a tumor‐informed ctDNA approach, with four studies using an NGS approach and three focusing on analyzing hotspot genes via ddPCR. The baseline detection of ctDNA was associated with worse survival outcomes, irrespective of the ctDNA detection methods. These results reveal the feasibility and prognostic role of ctDNA detection at baseline for patients with mUM. Likewise, these results can aid treatment decisions for clinicians, as patients diagnosed with mUM have heterogeneous prognoses, such as according to the disease burden (M1a vs. M1c) (Stålhammar et al. 2024). Management of patients with mUM remains largely non‐personalized, with HLA genotyping for selecting patients for tebentafusp therapy, the only available predictive biomarker. Thus, efforts to incorporate a personalized treatment approach that accounts for individual tumor biology are needed. For instance, a recent report demonstrates the importance of molecular characteristics influencing the prognostic and potentially also treatment for patients with mUM harboring SF3B1 mutations, having a marked increase in OS, irrespective of treatment (Del Carpio et al. 2025). Likewise, we recently reported our tertiary referral center approach, highlighting the requirement for patient discussion at a multidisciplinary tumor board and incorporation of NGS ahead of treatment decision, as well as the importance of these patients being enrolled in clinical trials (Saldanha et al. 2025). Therefore, leveraging ctDNA holds promise to assist clinicians in decision‐making, such as determining whether upfront liver‐directed therapy versus systemic therapy should be favored. However, despite promising results, its implementation in routine clinical care is yet limited by key factors such as lack of pre‐analytical factors standardization, heterogeneous analytic accuracy between assays, and validation across prospective randomized clinical trials (Sheriff et al. 2025).

Recently, tebentafusp has become the first treatment to improve OS for patients with HLA‐A*02:01‐positive mUM and is the standard of care approach for this population of patients (Harbour et al. 2010; Dickinson et al. 2024). However, studies investigating tebentafusp for mUM patients revealed a marked dissociation between radiological response by RECIST and OS (Koch et al. 2024). Exploratory analysis of IMC‐202 and IMC‐102 studies showed that patients exhibiting plasma ctDNA reduction derived improved OS, suggesting that ctDNA may predict patients who will derive benefit from tebentafusp (Hassel et al. 2023; Carvajal et al. 2022). Consistent with these findings, our exploratory analysis revealed that the ctDNA kinetics for patients undergoing therapy with tebentafusp was associated with improved OS (HR 0.19) and supports the potential of ctDNA as a surrogate marker for OS. Prospective validation of this association is warranted and will be explored in ongoing clinical trials (NCT06070012).

This meta‐analysis has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. The included studies exhibited heterogeneity in ctDNA detection methods (ddPCR vs. NGS), potentially affecting the pooled results. To address this limitation, we conducted a subgroup analysis stratified by detection method to gauge heterogeneity and employed only random‐effects models. Likewise, the investigation was based on a limited number of studies (N = 7) comprising clinical trials and observational real‐world studies, with an imbalance in study strength as clinical trials generally enrolled more patients than observational studies. Furthermore, the definition of ctDNA positivity and negativity, as well as the threshold for reduction, varied across studies, representing an inherent limitation of this type of analysis. Lastly, the moderate risk of bias in the included studies, due to inadequately analyzed confounding factors (variations in patient populations, differences in treatments administered, inconsistencies in ctDNA assessment methods, endpoint design, and timing), suggests our findings on the association between ctDNA and survival should be interpreted cautiously. Nonetheless, this is the first meta‐analysis to report the role of ctDNA in patients with mUM, comprising a substantial number of patients for this rare disease.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta‐analysis revealed that ctDNA detection is prognostic for patients with mUM. Likewise, exploratory analysis suggests the promising role of leveraging ctDNA kinetics for patients undergoing treatment with tebentafusp. These findings align with mounting evidence in the literature and warrant prospective validation in large, prospective randomized trials.
