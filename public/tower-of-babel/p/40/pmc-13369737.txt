External validity of task-specific metrics for performance assessment in laparoscopic crural repair
Authors: Sofia Garces-Palacios, Alexis Desir, Apoorva Pise, Carla Holcomb, Daniel J. Scott, Ganesh Sankaranarayanan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLaparoscopic hiatal hernia repair is a common foregut procedure, yet high-fidelity simulation tools for training and assessment remain limited. Previously, task-specific metrics (TSMs) were developed and validated at a single institution to evaluate performance during simulated laparoscopic crural repair. This study assessed the external validity and reliability of these TSMs using a broader, international cohort.MethodsParticipants at the 2024 SAGES Education and Innovation Center performed a simulated laparoscopic crural repair using an inanimate silicone model housed in a standard laparoscopic trainer box. Demographic data and surgical experience were collected via pre-survey. Performances were video-recorded and independently assessed by three blinded raters using both the Objective Structured Assessment of Technical Skills (OSATS) and TSMs. Participants were stratified into novice (PGY1–2) or experienced (PGY3–5, fellows, attendings). Inter-rater reliability was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Performance comparisons were analyzed using the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test. Linear regression modeled training level as an ordered predictor, adjusting for simulation exposure. Correlation between OSATS and TSM scores was measured using Spearman’s rank correlation.ResultsThirty-three participants were enrolled; 30 completed the task (attendings: 9; fellows: 3; PGY1–5: 18). 22 were U.S.-trained and 8 internationally trained. Inter-rater reliability was high for both scoring methods (ICC = 0.85; p < 0.001). Experienced participants scored significantly higher than novices on both OSATS (20 vs. 14; p = 0.039) and TSMs (39 vs. 18.5; p = 0.02). In adjusted models, robotic simulation training was independently associated with higher OSATS (β = 4.67, p = 0.021) and TSM scores (β = 15.01, p = 0.024). TSM and OSATS scores were strongly correlated (R = 0.88; p < 0.001).ConclusionsTSMs demonstrated strong external validity and reliability, effectively discriminating between experience levels and supporting their use as standardized assessment tool in surgical education and global training programs.

Introduction

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a prevalent condition that significantly impacts patient quality of life. Hiatal hernias are a key contributor to GERD pathophysiology [1, 2] as they disrupt the anatomical alignment of the lower esophageal sphincter and the diaphragmatic crura, two components that normally work synergistically to maintain an anti-reflux barrier [3]. Surgical correction of gastroesophageal reflux disease includes reduction of the hernia sac, mediastinal mobilization of the esophagus, crural closure, and partial (Toupet) or complete (Nissen) fundoplication [4]. Mastering this procedure requires significant experience with a suggested learning curve of 50 to 200 cases and supervised practice recommended for the first 20 operations to reduce the risk of complications [5, 6]. Simulation-based training and formative feedback are essential to ensure safe, efficient surgical performance [7–11]. The Society of American Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons (SAGES) has a video-based assessment tool for laparoscopic fundoplication [12] that has demonstrated strong content validity and incorporated feedback from both U.S.-based and international surgeons. However, the tool is not specifically tailored for simulation-based training environments. To our knowledge, structured task-specific metrics (TSMs) specifically developed to assess performance in simulated crural repair tasks remain limited.

To address this gap, our group previously developed and validated TSMs for a simulated laparoscopic crural repair using an inanimate silicone model at a single institution [13, 14]. The goal of the present study is to evaluate the external validity of these TSMs by applying them to a diverse cohort of international and U.S.-based surgeons and surgical trainees performing a simulated crural repair at the 2024 SAGES Education and Innovation Center in Cleveland, Ohio.

Materials and methods

Task-specific metric development and validation in a simulated crural repair

A prior study was conducted at the UT Southwestern Medical Center in Dallas, Texas, USA, as part of a National Institutes of Health-funded project for developing a virtual simulator for training in laparoscopic hiatal hernia repair. This led to the development and validation of TSMs for laparoscopic crural repair [14]. These metrics were created by performing a hierarchical task analysis of the laparoscopic hiatal hernia procedure, which involved using a structured methodology that systematically deconstructs surgical procedures into discrete tasks, sub-tasks, and associated motor actions [15, 16]. These metrics were then applied to assess technical performance in a simulated setting and included domains evaluating procedural technique (e.g., suture placement and crural re-approximation), general laparoscopic skills (e.g., bimanual dexterity and flow of operation), and tissue handling (Table 1). The validity of these metrics was established by conducting a study where 33 US-trained surgeons and general surgery residents were recruited from a single medical center and stratified into three groups based on surgical experience and training level: novices (PGY1–2), intermediates (PGY3–5), and experienced (faculty surgeons). Each participant performed a simulated crural repair using an inanimate crural repair model placed into a laparoscopic trainer box. The model was constructed from silicone with an embedded mesh to generate tissue tension, closely replicating the tissue mechanics of the diaphragmatic crura. A foam tube was incorporated to simulate the esophagus passing through the esophageal hiatus, which was intentionally enlarged to reflect clinically relevant crural repair scenarios. The model was mounted on a custom 3D-printed stand angled to reproduce operative positioning with the reverse Trendelenburg position. The entire assembly was housed within an Advanced Training in Laparoscopic Suturing (ATLAS) box trainer (Limbs & Things, Savannah, GA, USA), with a camera fixed to the roof of the box to ensure consistent visualization across all participants (Fig. 1). Performance was video-recorded and independently assessed by two trained raters using both the Objective Structured Assessment of Technical Skills (OSATS) and TSMs. The results of this study demonstrated the discriminant validity and the reliability of TSMs for the assessment of performance of a laparoscopic crural repair with participants at a local (U.S.) institution. Experimental procedure and study participants

This IRB-approved study recruited both local (within the U.S.) and international (outside the U.S.) participants to perform a simulated laparoscopic crural repair and was conducted at a designated station within the Education and Innovation Center during the 2024 SAGES Annual Meeting in Cleveland, Ohio. Prior to participation, individuals accessed a study information sheet via a QR code and provided verbal consent. A pre-survey was administered through REDCap that collected demographic data, including the participants’ level of surgical training, prior experience with laparoscopic hiatal hernia repair, and geographical location of their current training or practice, categorized as either local (within the U.S.) or international (outside the U.S.). Participants then completed a single simulated laparoscopic crural repair using the inanimate silicone model housed within the laparoscopic trainer box described above (Fig. 2). Video recordings were captured with strict anonymity, focusing solely on the operative field and instrument movement. Upon completion, cognitive load during the procedure was assessed using the NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) questionnaire [17], and additionally, a five-point Likert scale [18] survey was distributed to evaluate the participants’ perceptions of the simulator’s utility and realism. After the conclusion of data collection, all videos were reviewed, and performance was assessed using both the OSATS and our previously validated TSMs. Participants were stratified by experience and training level into novice (PGY1–2 s) and experienced (PGY3–5 s, fellows, and attending surgeons) for analysis.Fig. 2Study participant performing a simulated laparoscopic crural repair at the Education and Innovation Center during the 2024 SAGES Annual Meeting in Cleveland, Ohio

Statistical analysis

Of the 30 recorded procedures, a random subset of 10 videos was independently evaluated by three trained graders (SGP, AD, and CH), who consisted of an expert minimally invasive surgery surgeon, a PGY-4 general surgery resident, and a postdoctoral research fellow with extensive experience in video-based performance assessment. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), with values between 0.75 and 0.90 interpreted as indicating good reliability and values above 0.90 as showing excellent reliability [19]. Once acceptable reliability was established, the remaining 20 videos were assessed by SGP. Performance was evaluated using both the OSATS and the TSMs. Total OSATS and TSM scores were calculated by summing all individual item scores. Performance comparisons and simulator perceptions between novice and experienced participants were calculated using the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test, and associations between OSATS and TSM scores were analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. To avoid reliance on categorical groupings, training level was modeled as an ordered predictor and adjusted for simulation exposure using linear regression analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.4.0; R Core Team, 2022) [20].

Results

Demographics

A total of 33 participants were recruited for the study and completed the demographics pre-survey. Overall, 57.6% were male (n = 19) and 42.4% were female (n = 14). Mean age was 35.6 years (SD 7.5; range 24–53). Most participants (81.8%) identified as not Hispanic or Latino. In terms of race, 42.4% identified as Asian, 33.3% as white, 3% as Black or African American, and 21.2% as more than one race. Twenty-five participants (73%) were trained in the U.S., while seven (21.2%) were trained internationally, representing Chile (n = 2), Thailand (n = 2), India (n = 1), Taiwan (n = 1), and Bulgaria (n = 1).

The total cohort included 10 attending surgeons (30.3%) and 23 trainees (69.7%) at the PGY1–PGY5 and fellow levels. Data from three participants were not used for analysis due to problems in video recording. Across the sample, participants demonstrated a wide range of self-reported laparoscopic and robotic case experience across training levels, with increasing case volumes observed among senior trainees and attending surgeons (Table 2). In addition, 66.6% reported prior use of surgical simulation platforms, with slightly higher exposure among U.S.-trained participants (62.5%) compared to those trained internationally (55.6%). Reliability analysis

Inter-rater reliability across the three independent graders was excellent for both metrics. The ICC was 0.85 (95% CI 0.81–0.89; p < 0.001) for TSMs and 0.88 (95% CI 0.80–0.93; p < 0.001) for OSATS.

Global and TSM metrics

In the analysis, participants were initially stratified into novices (PGY1–2 s; n = 8) and experienced (PGY3–5 s, fellows, and attendings; n = 22). Across the cohort, OSATS scores ranged from 7 to 27, while TSM scores ranged from 6 to 60. Experienced group significantly outperformed novices in both OSATS (20 vs. 14; W = 43.5; p = 0.039) and TSMs (39 vs. 18.5; W = 39.5; p = 0.024; Table 3, Fig. 3). In the adjusted TSM model, robotic simulation training was also independently associated with higher performance (β = 15.01, 95% CI [2.19–27.83], p = 0.024), whereas training level and laparoscopic simulation exposure showed positive but non-significant associations (Fig. 5). Workload assessment

In terms of workload assessment, no statistically significant differences were observed between novices and experienced participants across any NASA-TLX domains, including mental (5.5 vs. 6.0), physical (5.5 vs. 5.0), temporal (5 vs. 5), performance (5.0 vs. 4.5), effort (5.5 vs. 6.0), frustration (4.5 vs. 6.0), or overall workload (32 vs. 34; all p > 0.05).

Correlation between OSATS and TSMs

A strong positive correlation was observed between OSATS and TSM scores (Spearman’s ρ = 0.88; p < 0.001). Simulator evaluation

Perceptions of the silicone model and interface were compared by group as well (Table 5). Novices rated the simulator slightly higher for anatomical realism (4 vs. 3 on a five-point scale) and interface realism (4 vs. 3) compared to experienced surgeons. Both groups provided similar ratings for force feedback realism (4 vs. 4) and usefulness (4 vs. 4). A Mann–Whitney U test showed no significant differences between groups for any variable (all p > 0.05), indicating similar perceptions of the simulator across novice and experienced participants. Overall, the simulator was considered useful for practicing a simulated laparoscopic crural repair, though novices tended to perceive higher realism across several domains. Discussion

In this study, we assessed the external validity and reliability of TSMs for a simulated laparoscopic crural repair at the 2024 SAGES annual meeting in Cleveland, Ohio. A total of 30 participants from a diverse cohort representing both U.S.-based and international institutions were included. Notably in this cohort, TSM scores strongly correlated with OSATS scores and effectively discriminated between novice and experienced participants, supporting the applicability of TSMs as a simulation-based assessment tool across diverse training backgrounds. Moreover, despite objective performance differences, the perceived cognitive workload did not vary significantly between groups, suggesting that the observed performance differences were skill-driven rather than task difficulty-driven, reinforcing the construct validity of TSMs. Importantly, these TSMs focus on key procedural elements unique to this operation, including a re-approximation of the diaphragmatic crura with full-thickness and equidistant suture placement, and avoidance of collisions with adjacent vital structures such as the esophagus.

Furthermore, simulation exposure demonstrated an independent association with performance, with greater exposure to robotic simulation associated with higher OSATS and TSM scores. In contrast, laparoscopic simulation exposure and training level showed positive but non-significant associations. Together, these findings indicate that simulation exposure explains additional variance in technical performance beyond experience level alone, highlighting the potential value of simulation-based training for skill acquisition in foregut procedures.

The advantages of the minimally invasive approach in hernia repair have contributed to a marked increase in its adoption in recent years [21, 22]. However, the selection of the operative technique must be individualized, considering both patient factors and the surgeon’s expertise. Importantly, the first 20 cases can shape operative performance and patient outcomes [5, 23–27]. This emphasizes the impact of procedural volume and the steep learning curve to master this procedure. Given these training demands, simulation-based platforms have become essential, offering structured practice and objective performance assessment in a risk-free environment, which accelerates skill acquisition while maintaining patient safety. Similar to our work, previous studies have established the validity and reliability of TSMs across a range of simulated and real-world procedures, including simulated minimally invasive operations such as laparoscopic fundoplication and laparoscopic crural repair, open procedures such as perineal proctectomy, and robotic gastrojejunostomy [13, 28, 29]. While these earlier studies were conducted internally at a single institution, the present study was conducted at the 2024 SAGES annual meeting within the Education and Innovation Center, permitting an opportunity to recruit a broad and diverse cohort, including surgeons and trainees from U.S.-based and international institutions. This setting aligned with one of SAGES’ strategic initiatives, which is to foster international participation and collaboration [30].

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, although a diverse cohort was recruited, the overall sample size was modest (n = 33), and the proportion of international participants was relatively small, limiting the generalizability of findings across broader global contexts. From this sample, data from three participants were not used for analysis due to missing video recordings. Second, the simulated procedures were conducted in a conference setting, where environmental distractions may have influenced participant performance and perceived workload. Third, the cross-sectional design captured performance at a single time point, precluding evaluation of longitudinal skill acquisition, retention, or progression along the learning curve.

Future research should include larger and more balanced cohorts of U.S.-based and international surgeons and trainees, ideally in controlled training environments. This study focused on external validity, and future research should also evaluate cross-cultural validity as well as socioeconomical factors, such as adopting these metrics in diverse settings and languages. In addition, longitudinal studies linking performance in simulated settings to clinical practice and patient outcomes will be essential to fully establish the educational and translational value of TSMs.

Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrated the reliability and the external validity of TSMs for the assessment of performance in simulated laparoscopic crural repair. These findings support the role of TSMs as a standardized assessment tool that can complement traditional training and help address the steep learning curve associated with complex foregut procedures.
