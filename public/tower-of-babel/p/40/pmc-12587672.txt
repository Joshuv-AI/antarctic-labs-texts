‘How nice would it be!’: synergies between pro-choice female physicians and abortion acompañantes in Mexico
Authors: Suzanne Veldhuis, Georgina Sánchez Ramírez, Maribel Campos Muñuzuri, Blair G. Darney
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPro-choice female physicians and abortion acompañantes– feminist activists who provide information and accompaniment during self-managed medication abortions– each contribute to safe abortion access in Mexico. Synergistic collaboration between these actors could improve access to safe and high-quality abortion care. We explored the possibility of synergy between pro-choice female physicians and acompañantes in Mexico.MethodsThis qualitative research is based on feminist epistemology. We used an established framework that describes partner synergy to analyze data from three workshops held with 12 physicians and 13 acompañantes in Chiapas (n = 12), Baja California (n = 9), and Mexico City (n = 4). The workshops sought to foster empathy and reflection on interactions, collaborations, and synergy. Workshop dynamics included sociodrama acting and group discussions.ResultsWe identified a common interest in building alliances and ideas about synergistic collaborations related to abortion care and accompaniment. Additionally, participants expressed an interest in mutual care arising from the recognition of common experiences of gender-based discrimination and violence among participants, which is a clear expression of synergistic collaboration. Our results also suggest that the pro-choice physicians do not see themselves as political actors, in contrast to the acompañantes, for whom their abortion work is deeply political. We describe that combating prejudice and fostering mutual recognition is necessary for successful collaborations, while the power asymmetry between physicians and acompañantes remains the main barrier to synergy. Finally, we determined that the workshop had an awareness-raising effect that could encourage the creation of subsequent alliances.ConclusionsCreating spaces for meeting and exchange may foster collaborations between these actors from different worlds, which can facilitate access to safe abortions.

Background

Historically, physicians have served as abortion gatekeepers whose care takes place under state and institutional control [1, 2]. However, since the 1980 s, with the discovery of misoprostol as a method to self-induce abortion by Brazilian women, abortion seekers are challenging this gatekeeping role [3]. Today, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends medication abortion (MA) with mifepristone and misoprostol or misoprostol alone, including its self-management until 12 weeks, as a safe and effective method [4]. The self-management of MA outside of clinical settings without the involvement of a provider is reshaping the role of medicine by shifting the focus from medical professionals as the sole providers of abortion to a support role and an available resource when needed [4]. Medical professionals have an important role to play in abortion access and care, including procedural abortions, medically or socially complex cases, fetal asystole at more advanced pregnancy durations, treating complications, or simply when a clinical abortion service is preferred.

During the last two decades, acompañantes—feminist activists who provide information and support during autonomous abortions—have become key players in the abortion movement, particularly in Latin America. Their experience in highly vulnerable, criminalized, and stigmatized settings has led to the development of holistic and person-centered accompaniment models, which can serve as valuable examples for clinical services in terms of access and quality of care [5]. Both the scientific literature and activists recognize abortion accompaniment also as a political, autonomous health movement that works through direct action on the edge or outside the law and represents a militant model grounded in the feminist belief that individuals have the right to abortion how, where, when, and with whom they want, free from state or medical control [6–8].

Synergies or collaborations between physicians and acompañantes, who represent different worlds, have the potential to improve access to safe and quality abortions [1, 5, 8]. We define synergy as the feature of a collaboration that may be present to a lesser or greater extent and that combines strengths, perspectives, values, resources, and skills to enhance the effectiveness and impact of such alliances [9, 10]. This occurs especially when different types of knowledge and perspectives are involved, as is the case when bringing together physicians and acompañantes. Paradoxically, diversity is also its greatest challenge [9, 10]. The partnership synergy framework describes how the synergy of an alliance can be expressed in thinking, acting, and its relationship with the broader community [9]. Bringing voices and ideas together can lead to creative, comprehensive, holistic, more practical, and even transformative thinking. It can also strengthen the actions of a partnership. Finally, collaboration can lead to engagement and changes that are supported by and benefit the wider community.

Examples of successful collaborations between physicians and acompañantes are scarce. One well-documented example comes from Argentina, where feminist activists, united in the Socorristas en Red since 2012, worked with health professionals to provide safe abortions in a (at the time) restrictive environment [2, 8]. The activists also provided training to health professionals on the use of misoprostol, which led to an exchange of knowledge that transformed the thinking and approach of allied health personnel. Additionally, activists and health professionals acted together in the Campaña Nacional por el Derecho al Aborto Legal, Seguro y Gratuito (National Campaign for Legal, Safe, and Free Abortion), which achieved the legalization of abortion in all cases up to 14 weeks of pregnancy in 2020. However, how to replicate these effective collaborations in other contexts is unclear.

In Mexico, access to abortion services is still limited despite recent legislative changes following the 2021 and 2023 Supreme Court Decisions on the unconstitutionality of the criminalization of abortion. While abortion in the first trimester is now legal in 22 of Mexico´s 32 states and the recently (2021) implemented Safe Abortion Services offer abortion care in cases of miscarriage or induced abortion depending on local legislation [11], service provision and access remain inconsistent nationwide. Private services are available throughout the country but are inaccessible for most people and not necessarily safe [12].

Meanwhile, hundreds of collectives, organizations, and autonomous acompañantes across the country facilitate access to safe medication abortions and health services when needed [13, 14]. However, there is no overall coordination or articulation with actors within the health sector in Mexico, as seen in Argentina [15]. Likewise, no formal organization of pro-choice activist health professionals existed until the recent formation of the Red de Médicas por el Derecho a Decidir México [Network of Female Physicians for the Right to Decide Mexico] (RMDD) in 2021, whose existence facilitated the current research [16]. The RMDD offers training and peer-support to female physicians whilst advocating for safe, legal, and free abortion.

In this study, we focus on the collaboration between two specific figures that contribute to safe abortion access: pro-choice female physicians and abortion acompañantes. While they share the same gender, power inequality exists among these two dissimilar and diverse possible allies. Physicians represent hegemonic health knowledge and practices; in contrast, acompañantes have subordinate knowledge, and their actions are antisystemic [7, 8]. While acompañantes see the value of collaboration with the formal health system, they are wary of losing autonomy (Jacobson L, Darney BG, Campos Muñuzuri M, Veldhuis S. Balancing autonomy and collaboration: Acompañantes’ perspectives on health system partnerships for medication abortion care in three Mexican states, Forthcoming). As synergy between these actors may improve access to safe and quality abortions, this study aims to explore the ideas about and proposals for synergistic collaboration of both pro-choice female physicians and acompañantes in Mexico.

Methods

Study setting

We conducted this qualitative study in three Mexican states with diverse legal, sociocultural, and political settings: Chiapas, Baja California (BC), and Mexico City. At the time of this study, in Chiapas, abortion was allowed only under narrow exceptions: in case of rape, fetal malformations, and risk to the life of the pregnant person. Safe Abortion Services were recently implemented in five medical units. Several collectives, as well as individual acompañantes, were openly active in the state. In BC, abortion before 13 weeks was decriminalized in November 2021, after which the local network of acompañantes collectives sought collaboration with the medical personnel of the newly instated Safe Abortion Services in two municipalities. Finally, in Mexico City, abortion under 13 weeks was legalized in 2007. Free abortion services are provided in the Legal Interruption of Pregnancy (ILE) clinics. Nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) that offer support and accompaniment to individuals from other states accessing legal abortion clinics are the most visible actors in the capital´s accompaniment movement. Private abortion services are available in all three states, and misoprostol is commonly available in pharmacies across the country without a prescription [14].

Data collection

This study is grounded in feminist epistemology, which starts from the premise that the women participating in the study are creators of scientific knowledge and social change [17]. This study arises from the lead author’s (SV) situated knowledge as a pro-choice physician and abortion activist and aims to promote positive transformation through the creation of knowledge that may facilitate access to safe abortion for all pregnant people [18].

The lead author SV is a cofounder of the RMDD and an allied physician of several Mexican acompañantes, which allowed for intentional sampling: the deliberate selection of physicians and acompañantes with diverse characteristics in terms of age, experience related to abortion, employment, workplace, type of accompaniment or care, and experience with previous interactions. Where possible, we invited acompañantes who participated in a previous study describing the holistic accompaniment model [13]. The inclusion of pro-choice female physicians only in this study was based on the idea that synergistic collaborations are more viable if they involve physicians close to feminist politics and, in addition, share the same gender with the activists. We provided participants with an explanation of the study’s purpose, and all contacted individuals agreed to participate and provided written informed consent. Ethical approval for this study was provided by Comité De Ética Para La Investigación at El Colegio De La Frontera Sur.

The data for this study were drawn from a series of workshops grounded in a gender and health perspective whose purpose was to foster empathy and reflections on interactions, collaborations, and synergy between pro-choice physicians and acompañantes [19]. The workshops did not seek to raise awareness about abortion; we started from the premise that all participants agreed with and were in favor of abortion. SV´s involvement in abortion activism and position in favor of collaboration was explicitly expressed at the beginning of each workshop to foster transparency and trust. The workshop methodology, including a detailed description of the sociodrama dynamic, has been published elsewhere [19]. We collected data from three workshops carried out between November 2022 and March 2023 in Chiapas, BC, and Mexico City. The workshops were recorded and transcribed manually to facilitate analysis.

Data analysis

We recognize our positionality as feminist researchers with a gender and health perspective who are committed to generating and disseminating knowledge that supports universal access to safe abortion. As scientists, we are convinced that collaborations between pro-choice physicians and acompañantes will benefit women and other people who have abortions; therefore, we are interested in identifying possible collaborative scenarios. We ensured data quality through ongoing discussions between the authors during and after each workshop.

We used the partnership synergy framework to create a priori themes and categories and then used a grounded theory approach to conduct an inductive process of open coding. This approach combines a priori themes with the creation of new categories and subcategories throughout the research and analysis [21]. We used Atlas Ti© software to organize the data. Within the core ‘synergistic collaboration’ theme, we built three a priori categories to describe the dimensions of ‘thinking’, ‘acting’, and ‘relationship with the wider community’. These allowed us to analyze both imagined and actual relationships between physicians and acompañantes and evaluate the possible existence of synergistic collaborations. As the workshop was designed to facilitate exchange, awareness, and reflection on collaboration, we set out to analyze these in the a priori theme ‘effects of participating in the workshop’.

In addition to our overarching a priori themes of ‘synergistic collaboration’ and ‘effects of participating in the workshop’, we identified one additional large theme, ‘conditions for collaboration’, which describes what participants considered necessary to achieve and sustain collaborations: combat prejudices, mutual recognition, communication, willingness, horizontality, demedicalization, and interculturality.

Within the ‘synergistic collaboration’ theme, we maintained the a priori categories of ‘thinking’ and ‘action’ and created two subcategories within the latter, ‘new models’ and ‘mutual care’, and revised the third a priori category, ‘relationship with the wider community’, to ‘advocacy’. Within the a priori theme, ‘effects of participating in the workshop’, we confirmed our hypothesis that the workshop had an awareness-raising effect that may foster subsequent collaborations. Our main finding is the shared interest in synergistic collaborations in abortion care/accompaniment.

Synergistic collaboration

Thinking

Several participants expressed an interest in exchanging knowledge, as this would strengthen all: ‘We can […] share our knowledge, train others, to reproduce the movement, making it a living and organized movement’ (L, acompañante, Chiapas).

The acompañantes acknowledged that medical knowledge is useful to them; although ‘medical knowledge is not our basis, we recognize that sometimes we need it, and having someone who supports us in this part is very valuable to us’ (B, acompañante, BC). The acompañantes recognized both types of knowledge as equally valuable and necessary. While physicians recognized the value of the holistic accompaniment model, they did not propose incorporating these ideas into their own model or care but rather considered it to be complementary; they did not consider the acompañante’s knowledge to be as valuable or essential as their medical knowledge.

This subordinating view of acompañantes was highlighted when some participating physicians offered training. Even though the acompañantes are experts in medication abortion—both the medical protocol and the physical, emotional, and logistical aspects—their knowledge was still considered to be limited and not comparable to medical knowledge: ‘I can give everything you need; I am a physician… training… you have my number, write to me, without shame, without fear, I am there’ (T, general physician, BC). In response, the acompañantes emphasized that they would gladly share their experiences and knowledge, seeking a horizontal exchange with mutual benefit. One acompañante urged health personnel to be trained on the issue of abortion:All medical or health personnel [should] receive training on how to perform a medication abortion. I have [accompanied] several people [who went to] institutions where they were given the wrong medication. I see it like this: they believe they are gods and see us [the acompañantes] as these nobodies…. I would love to train them. (T, acompañante, BC)

In this sense, reflections on possible alliances at the level of ‘thinking’ were relatively limited. Although there was mutual recognition that exchanging knowledge and experiences could be useful, they proposed doing so through training (vertical) or exchanges (horizontal). Neither the acompañantes nor the physicians imagined creating something new or transforming their own thinking: each imagined staying in their field: ‘We need to get to know each other, […] each one of us contributes from our area, [we should] have more meetings, talk about cases, give each other tips’ (T, gynecologist, Chiapas).

In this first meeting, the collaborations imagined by participants concerning ‘thinking’ would not be considered synergistic, as they do not lead to new, comprehensive, and transformative ideas. However, for most participants, this was the first meeting with their counterparts. Therefore, it is possible that by concretizing the proposed exchanges over time, changes will occur, and synergistic collaborations in thinking may develop.

Acting

This a priori category describes possible synergy based on findings of the sociodrama and final reflection activities, related to their combined action—both roleplaying and reflections on potential future actions. These dynamics were developed to encourage empathy and to promote collective reflection on collaboration during abortion care and accompaniment, as described in the category ‘new models’. However, the participants also reflected on common action through ‘mutual care’.

New models

All participants recognized the importance and benefit of mutual referrals since they improve access and increase options for those who abort: ‘We can reach places where the hospital cannot reach’ (W, acompañante, BC),I have referred patients to the [acompañante] collectives if they do not want or cannot come here. I am not married to the idea that everyone must come with me. In the end, it is about them having a choice (M, general physician, BC).

I have referred patients to the [acompañante] collectives if they do not want or cannot come here. I am not married to the idea that everyone must come with me. In the end, it is about them having a choice (M, general physician, BC).

The collective creation of the utopian scenarios encouraged brainstorming sessions about what collaboration could look like while providing abortion care or accompaniment. The participants mentioned that there would be more options: people would be able to decide whether to access medical services at any time during the process as well as whether they would like to be accompanied by an activist. They described that the processes could be initiated in a health service, followed by the referral to an acompañante: ‘It would be incredible, to refer a patient from the hospital to you for accompaniment, it would be a very strong alliance’ (T, gynecologist, Chiapas). The acompañante would be able to refer to a physician for check-ups in case of concerns or complications: ‘The ideal would be that when [people undergoing an abortion] are in the hospital, they are told: “The acompañante will accompany you throughout the entire process”, and if there is a complication, [the physician] will take good care of them’ (W, acompañante, BC).

In such a scenario, the alliance would result in personalized and comprehensive care. When reflecting upon the utopian scenario created by participants in Mexico City, which had shown successful collaboration between physicians and acompañantes, the participants noted the following:It was an accompaniment in all spaces. She had support before, during, and after [the abortion], with free medications. This is ideal (…). It was personalized and depended on what the woman needed. She was the one who called us to each of the spaces; she made the decisions, and we [physicians and acompañantes] revolved around what she needed and felt. (R, acompañante, Mexico City)

It was an accompaniment in all spaces. She had support before, during, and after [the abortion], with free medications. This is ideal (…). It was personalized and depended on what the woman needed. She was the one who called us to each of the spaces; she made the decisions, and we [physicians and acompañantes] revolved around what she needed and felt. (R, acompañante, Mexico City)

For the person accompanied, collaborations represented in the sociodrama would result in increased quality of care, in the words of a physician: ‘The human side that you provide, the pampering, we do not come close to this even though we are aware, (…) this [collaboration] allows us to provide more comprehensive care to patients’ (M, general practitioner, BC). The participants in the sociodrama considered that the combination of their actions would also result in a greater perception of security by the accompanied person: ‘As a user [playing the role of the person seeking abortion], I felt very comforted, with the words of both, asking how I am beyond the explanation of the procedure. You feel more confident and prepared’ (N, acompañante, Chiapas).

The acompañantes mentioned that collaboration with allied health providers could reduce the fear and risk of being criminalized or mistreated when accessing health services, as proposed by Yanow et al. (2021): ‘In the case where things may get complicated or they are afraid, being able to tell them to go to the public services (…) we would no longer have to do it in hiding, knowing that we can channel them to you, allied physicians’ (L, acompañante, Chiapas).

The participants acknowledged that collaboration would expand their possibilities of action. In the case of the physicians, because they work in a health system with multiple deficiencies that limit the care they can offer, ‘There are no resources, neither human nor technical, the role you [physicians] have is very important, but it is necessary to collaborate [with acompañantes] because there are limits in your actions’ (L, acompañante, Chiapas), and in the case of the acompañantes because ‘There are limits to our knowledge’ (L, acompañante, Chiapas). One activist from BC, who had already collaborated with allied physicians, mentioned that this also meant ‘a relief because we can offer options; public, private, us, and it does not have to be [only] with us’ (O, acompañante, BC).

Mutual care

The clearest expression of synergy emerged when participants expressed their interest in mutual care: ‘We also need to accompany each other’ (R, acompañante, BC). This interest arose from the participants talking about how being a woman and pro-choice exposes them to stress, stigma, discrimination, and violence, which resulted in mutual recognition and identification, as we describe later. They considered mutual accompaniment and care to be important in sustaining their actions: ‘I need support from all of you to [be able to] continue accompanying, it is very valuable (…) I need this, to have a support network’ (N, acompañante, Chiapas).

This need was expressed by several participants, both physicians and acompañantes, who not only asked for but also offered care, including their workspaces, houses, emotional support, and building a support network: ‘I am committed to friendship, alliances, the smiles, the tears’ (L, general physician, Chiapas).

Advocacy

Acompañantes often spoke about advocacy and how collaboration could enhance advocacy efforts; this is a clear example of synergy in the relationship with the wider community. Some of the activists mentioned that by joining forces, they could influence the health system: ‘[You] are making it possible for things to change within the system. And we, from outside, are pushing…’ (L, acompañante, Chiapas). They imagined concrete collaborations such as those in which the acompañantes would carry out advocacy actions directed at health institutions, which is difficult to do for physicians while they are employed within the system, or that physicians could facilitate the inclusion of acompañantes in institutional decision-making spaces or as beneficiaries of institutional resources. Acompañantes with teaching activities extended invitations to participate in educational spaces. Others proposed jointly influencing medical school curricula or training health personnel on abortion. Only one physician mentioned political advocacy, an acknowledgment of the acompañantes´ advocacy work, rather than a statement about their own advocacy:[We should] not just stay at the level of accompaniment, what you do is very broad; it is much more than giving mife[pristone] and miso[prostol], (…) how to impact public policies, joining forces where each one of us is very important. (N, general physician, Chiapas)

[We should] not just stay at the level of accompaniment, what you do is very broad; it is much more than giving mife[pristone] and miso[prostol], (…) how to impact public policies, joining forces where each one of us is very important. (N, general physician, Chiapas)

Conditions for collaboration

Several participants mentioned the conditions they deemed necessary for building alliances. Both physicians and acompañantes referred to combating prejudices and mutual recognition. In addition, acompañantes emphasized communication, willingness, horizontality, demedicalization, and interculturality as important aspects of successful collaborations.

Although both physicians and acompañantes mentioned that it is necessary to combat prejudices, there was a difference in how they expressed this need. It was important to the physicians for the acompañantes to understand that:Physicians are very demonized, with good reason (…). Obviously, when you start interacting with physicians you are going to have many disagreements and you are going to realize that even the vocabulary is different and they will most likely dislike you, and this is going to cause the relationship to break up… I [hope they can] understand the reason a little. Please have patience and hope that physicians can deconstruct and transform [themselves] and believe that another world is possible. (T, gynecologist, Mexico City)

Please have patience and hope that physicians can deconstruct and transform [themselves] and believe that another world is possible. (T, gynecologist, Mexico City)

Several physicians spoke about their experiences within the institutional health system and the violence they experienced for being women and/or pro-choice. They asked for understanding and empathy from the acompañantes of the limits this imposes on their actions. In contrast, the activists explicitly expressed that ‘As acompañantes, we need recognition of our work, [and for you to know] that it is not necessary to have medical knowledge or a degree to be able to accompany women who exercise their rights’ (D, acompañante, Mexico City).

An acompañante from BC who had interacted with some of the physicians participating in the workshop affirmed the importance of mutual recognition and respect in building new collaborations:The first time we met, we did not know if we wanted to collaborate and refer patients to Safe Abortion Services. Over time, we have learned that [the physician] is a person we can trust and that it is a relationship built on respect, also toward our work as acompañantes, which is what most physicians do not have. (S, acompañante, BC)

These demands reflect the unequal positions that medical personnel and activists occupy in society. Although acompañantes are experts in abortion, society does not recognize them as such. While medical staff do not necessarily have knowledge or experience in abortion care, they are considered (and consider themselves) experts. The participating physicians did not need to request recognition for their work since they are in a position that allows them to assume that this is automatically present.

Both parties committed to working on their prejudices to reduce the gap between the two worlds and facilitate collaboration: ‘I offer openness, not starting from prejudices, listening first, not believing that all acompañantes are cool, nor that all health personnel are terrible’ (R, acompañante, Mexico City).

The acompañantes were very clear about what was required for lasting collaborations. They considered communication to be a central element and mentioned two types of communication: 1) between the physician and the abortion-seeker when referring to an acompañante and 2) the cocoordination of the case, that is, direct communication between the physician and the activist:If [someone] goes to a physician because [they] don’t trust the [acompañantes’] collectives [it is important] to explain why, as a physician, you work with and trust the collectives: explain that it [the abortion] is the same procedure, because sometimes they think that going to the hospital is very different. Also, [communicate with] us: when we refer among ourselves, we let each other know and explain. I don’t give phone numbers to just anyone; I ask if they are available… Communication! Let the chica2 know, from both sides, times and availability, the process, that the work of the collectives is voluntary… (B, acompañante, BC)

Some acompañantes mentioned the importance of demedicalization as a condition for collaboration: ‘I ask the physicians to understand that it is not always necessary to request control ultrasounds; there are times when they [the abortion-seekers] do not have money or resources’ (O, acompañante, BC). The indigenous acompañante mentioned interculturality as central for both physicians and acompañantes: ‘I need […] the ancestral knowledge of medical women to be combined with medical knowledge; this would help a lot to improve the process. May we have the openness to create common systems’ (M, acompañante, Chiapas).

The acompañantes mentioned that these imagined collaborations would first have to be concreted in everyday life and that not everything would be resolved with the alliance between one physician and one acompañante:What we have just done nourishes me a lot. How nice would it be if it were like that! Because it is not like that yet. On one shift [at the hospital], maybe one of our colleagues [allied physicians] will be there, but on another shift, she won’t be there, and we have to send a woman who needs care… It’s not just about physicians but also nursing staff, the security staff… that is why they don’t want to go; they experience so much psychological violence and, on top of it all, end up having a [sharp] curettage. (L, acompañante, Chiapas)

On several occasions, the acompañantes distinguished between the figure of the ‘allied physician’ and the health institutions or physicians in general. While collaboration with institutions may be difficult, ‘with our individual allies there is the possibility of these alliances, I see it as more viable: coconstruction, betting on horizontality’ (B, acompañante, BC).

Notably, the acompañantes from BC, who had already collaborated with some of the physicians who participated in the workshop and with health institutions, emphasized these conditions. This likely led the acompañantes to mention the importance of willingness, clarity about limits, and good communication for building alliances. However, physicians in BC expressed little concern; on the contrary, they mentioned that they are happy with the existing collaborations.

Finally, in a way, both physicians and acompañantes are used to working without collaborating. Although they recognize the possible benefits, one acompañante mentioned the following:I don’t need anything in the sense that we are going to continue getting the job done, with the certainty that if something gets stuck, the network [of acompañantes] and our allied physicians [does not refer to the physicians present at the workshop] are there. Despite the shortcomings and needs, there is nothing that worries me because I trust in the support network that we have been building. (M, acompañante, BC)

Effects of participating in the workshop

An important result of this study is the identification of the role of the workshop in changes in mutual awareness and recognition among the participants in each of the states, thereby possibly laying or strengthening the foundations for future alliances. In other words, the workshops could facilitate collaborations. In the words of several of the participants:This is a first initiative, getting to know each other, putting ourselves in each other’s shoes, at some point I was also the one who said “acompañantes complicate everything”, then after discovering that it is not true, that there are many women who have been accompanied by you, with love, affection, care, integral, not only the medical part… this is already a pretty good first step. (N, general physician, Chiapas)These spaces help us to find our bearings. I did not know the physicians, (…) this way we get to know other realities. I hope there will be more spaces like this. (N, acompañante, Chiapas)

(N, general physician, Chiapas)

These spaces help us to find our bearings. I did not know the physicians, (…) this way we get to know other realities. I hope there will be more spaces like this. (N, acompañante, Chiapas)

In all the workshops, the participants concluded that the main difference between them was that they came from two different worlds. The physicians move within a rigid, hierarchical, androcentric, and medicalized system, which is reflected even in their discourse: ‘The use of words is striking: chica versus patient’ (R, acompañante, BC).

In contrast, acompañantes have greater freedom to create their own flexible, feminist, and demedicalized protocols, which include an emotional accompaniment that physicians lack. This observation was made by both physicians and acompañantes in all three states. In the words of a physician, who commented on the acompañante playing the role of a physician in the sociodrama: ‘You were too nice [in the drama], in no hospital are they going to be that nice! Even the physicians who agree [with abortion] do not give such good treatment; we are much more technical’ (E, general physician, Mexico City).

The sociodrama was designed to foster empathy and reflection on possible interactions, collaborations, and synergy and turned out to be the central awareness-building element within the workshop. Both physicians and acompañantes reported feeling identified by the scenes: ‘I was very moved because I usually deliver medicine in the market, they don’t have money, I identified a lot…[cries]’ (R, acompañante, BC). For the activists, the sociodrama made it tangible how violent and sexist the medical system is for female physicians. At the same time, for some physicians, the sociodrama was the first time they learned of the acompañantes’ work, which resulted in recognition of their actions. For all the participants, acting out a scene based on someone else´s reality resulted in empathy and mutual recognition:They are all admirable… I am just getting to know all these things about acompañantes, I didn’t know about them. I find it very interesting; it seems essential that this knowledge is spread. It is striking that the expressions of recognition differed depending on the source. The appreciation expressed by the acompañantes, as seen in the previous testimony, is primarily recognition of the difficulties that female physicians face when working in a violent system. In contrast, the physicians’ recognition of the acompañantes is for their work itself, of which several of the physicians had no prior knowledge.

The power asymmetry between the two groups is once again evident: physicians may be unaware of the existence of acompañantes, and, strictly speaking, they do not need them for abortion care. However, the acompañantes were very aware of the role of health personnel in abortion and that even when it is self-managed at home, interaction with health services is inevitable in some cases. They knew that they depend on the willingness of those who work in these services to deal with any unforeseen events in the abortion process.

The positive atmosphere during the workshops even allowed for the expression of constructive criticism among the participants. In one of the sessions, the acompañantes gave feedback to a physician on aspects of her action during the consultation. Instead of creating a conflict, the comments were received with gratitude from the physician, who confessed that she had never been able to talk about any of this before this workshop. The openness and trust necessary for this positive interaction were unexpected and hopeful outcomes of the workshop.I already understood, I had not put myself there… I do not do it to cause harm, I thank you infinitely, sometimes we need to put ourselves in the other’s shoes, and the comments you made to me were excellent; it does not bother me, I´m not angry, on the contrary, I thank you. (F, gynecologist, BC)

Finally, the participants identified similarities. As an activist from BC mentioned, ‘[Being] here makes us see everything we share, all this violence unfortunately’ (R, acompañante, BC). It was clear that their gender—being discriminated against and violated for being women—was a shared experience of all participants and that this recognition was a catalyst for creating connections between them:[Let´s] stop thinking that we came to fight because we are fighting shoulder to shoulder; [let´s] leave the label of physicians and acompañantes. In the end, we are all women, and our fight is from women to women. For me, this is important. (T, gynecologist, Chiapas)

Some physicians mentioned that they have had experiences similar to those of the acompañantes since they sometimes accompanied outside of clinical settings: ‘I am a physician, but this does not make me feel very far from your reality, I have accompanied you, like this, at night, on the cell phone… We are not so different’ (L, general physician, Chiapas). Likewise, several acompañantes had worked within public institutions and recognized the type of violence exercised within these spaces.Many of us have also worked at some point within the system; we know what it is like. The system corrupts you, imprisons you, and makes you see other women who can do the same as you as rivals. (M, acompañante, Chiapas)

Importantly, several participants mentioned that they share the same objective:In a concrete utopia, we should see ourselves as we are now, as people, women, and allies, so we can gradually break the tension that has been experienced between physicians and acompañantes. We are on the same earth, and we share the same position; the right to choose. (L, general physician, Chiapas)

This is encouraging, as having a clear common goal can keep a coalition together: the mutual recognition and identification of a common objective form the basis of possible alliances and synergy.

Discussion

The main result of this study is the identification of interest in building alliances based on a common objective: access to safe abortion for all people. Our data suggest that there is potential for synergy—actions that go beyond combining each partner’s actions and may lead to the creation of comprehensive, higher-quality care models. We identified an interest in mutual care arising from the recognition of the gender-based discrimination and violence that the participants face, which is a clear expression of synergistic collaboration. Our results also suggest that the pro-choice physicians do not see themselves as political actors, in contrast to the acompañantes, for whom their abortion work is deeply political. The power asymmetry between physicians and acompañantes remains the main barrier to synergy in collaborations. Finally, we determined that the workshop had an awareness-raising effect that could encourage the creation of subsequent alliances that have the potential to be synergistic.

If the participants’ ideas on collaboration and new models of care were to materialize, there would be synergistic collaborations between two actors who complement each other and therefore have a greater effect or ‘performance’ than what they would achieve individually [9]. Synergy could lead to increased access to a range of options for having a safe abortion, an increased perception of safety and security for abortion seekers, and personalized, comprehensive high-quality care. The participant´s ideas about collaborations in care/accompaniment echo the interactions between allied health professionals and the acompañantes of the Socorristas en Red in Argentina, as they express a recognition of ‘both territories: expertise in dealing with women on the one hand, and, on the other hand, the possibility of prevention, reaction and immediate referral in the event of possible complications’ ([8], p174). However, even in these scenarios, in our study, participants did not describe imagining the disappearance of the limits between both models and health professionals becoming insider activists, as occurred on some occasions in Argentina [8, 22]. Even so, even if collaborations were to be limited to facilitating the referral or channeling of abortion-seekers by acompañantes to medical personnel (although it lacks synergy since it is unilateral), this would be an important step forward, as it guarantees the safety of self-management in agreement with WHO conditions: access to health services when required or desired [4].

The interest in mutual care was a concrete expression of synergistic collaboration, as the participants were not only interested in the results of the collaboration but also in the actual relationship itself, which could lead them to achieve more than what they would if doing so individually [23]. Authors recognize the importance of the quality of interpersonal relationships, including trust and respect, for building synergistic alliances [9, 24]. Furthermore, collective care is a counterhegemonic feminist practice that is ‘a form of symbolic and practical mutual support that transcends the daily and common needs of any person and that leads to protect and support the development of alternative ways of life and individual and collective projects’ ([25], pp42-43). It is based on principles such as horizontality, solidarity, and reciprocity and can be a strategy for reaching a common political objective [26]. Therefore, mutual care can blur the gap between the two worlds.

The physicians’ reflections on alliances related to the wider community, such as advocacy, were limited. This may be due to the emphasis on collaboration during abortion care/accompaniment, through the dynamics of sociodramas, or to the physicians’ limited experience in acting beyond clinical care. The participating physicians mostly conceived their role in providing care, hardly imagining acting in any other way; they do not consider themselves to be political actors. In contrast, the acompañantes understood what the value and impact of joining forces on other issues could be: for acompañantes, abortion is a deeply political issue (Jacobson L, Darney BG, Campos Muñuzuri M, Veldhuis S. Balancing autonomy and collaboration: Acompañantes’ perspectives on health system partnerships for medication abortion care in three Mexican states, Forthcoming). Historically, these kinds of actions have been important points of convergence between physicians and feminist activists, for example, in the legalization of abortion and the implementation of services in the United States pre- and post-Roe, in the Campaña Nacional por el Derecho al Aborto Legal, Seguro y Gratuito in Argentina, and the Causa Justa movement in Colombia [8, 27, 28]. It is promising and necessary to further explore these possible synergies in advocacy actions in Mexico.

The main obstacle to building synergistic collaborations is the power asymmetry that we observed at various moments. While they admired the work of the acompañantes, the physicians did not consider incorporating these learnings into their practices, as they did not describe the acompañantes’ expertise as being equally valuable to their own. The physicians lacked awareness of the existing power inequality, which led them to not recognize the possible difficulties in collaboration. This results in the acompañantes doing the work to overcome these obstacles, adapting to the needs of the physicians, and tolerating the existence of these asymmetries, as they understand the inevitability of contact with medical services and the direct benefits of collaboration for the accompanied people. In contrast, physicians mainly appreciated the work of the acompañantes, as it may support them in theirs. The deconstruction of this asymmetry will require a conscious questioning of their role by physicians, or as Fernández Vázquez and Szwarc mention, ‘The articulation between militants and health professionals [in Argentina] has occurred to a greater extent with those professionals who, although perhaps they do not subvert it, question the hegemonic medical model and support positions closer to community health’ ([8], p174).

The workshop may foster such questioning in addition to the immediate effects of raising awareness and inspiring proposals for collaboration, although we do not know if the ideas and proposals were subsequently concretized. The workshops were carried out among cis women only, which allowed for an environment of trust and sharing of experiences related to their gender. However, the workshop has not been applied in mixed groups or with other types of health personnel (nurses, midwives, for example) or those who hold a more neutral position regarding abortion. We consider it important to investigate the effects of the workshop in other settings to evaluate the possibility of using this methodology as a tool for building alliances that ultimately benefit abortion seekers.

Conclusions

The results of this research suggest that collaborations between female pro-choice physicians and acompañantes have the potential to be synergistic. It is important to create spaces for meetings and exchanges that bring together these actors from different worlds, who share a common objective: to facilitate access to safe and quality abortions for all women and other people who need it.
