Healthcare Development, Relaxed Natural Selection, and COVID‐19 Infection Rates: An Evolutionary Population‐Level Analysis
Authors: Wenpeng You, Brendon Coventry, Arthur Saniotis, Francesco Maria Galassi, Renata Henneberg, Maciej Henneberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimEvolutionary principles offer critical insights into pandemic dynamics but have been largely overlooked in contemporary responses to COVID‐19. Relaxed natural selection, increased global mobility, and lifestyle mismatches have contributed to human vulnerability to infectious diseases in modern societies. This study investigated the relationship between healthcare development, measured by the Biological State Index (Ibs), and cumulative COVID‐19 infection rates across different countries and regions.MethodsData from 189 countries were analysed to assess the association between the Biological State Index and COVID‐19 infection rates, defined as the cumulative percentage of the population contracting COVID‐19. Correlation analyses (Pearson's r and Spearman rho), partial correlations adjusting for aging, economic affluence, urbanization, and vaccination coverage, and multiple linear regressions were performed. Countries were further stratified by income level, development status, and WHO regions to explore group‐specific patterns.ResultsHigher Biological State Index values were strongly associated with higher COVID‐19 infection rates globally (Pearson's r = 0.734, p < 0.001). Partial correlation analyses confirmed that this association remained significant after adjusting for key demographic and economic variables. Multiple regression models identified economic affluence and population aging as strong independent predictors, with the addition of the Ibs further improving model fit. Stratified analyses demonstrated consistently stronger correlations in developing and low‐ to middle‐income countries compared to high‐income and developed nations.ConclusionsHealthcare system advancement is strongly associated with higher reported COVID‐19 infection rates, likely reflecting greater susceptibility, improved detection capabilities, demographic shifts, and broader exposure opportunities. These findings highlight the importance of integrating evolutionary perspectives into pandemic preparedness strategies, recognizing that modern healthcare developments, while reducing mortality, may inadvertently alter infection patterns and pathogen‐host dynamics over time.

Background

Natural selection has long shaped human health and survival by favouring the reproduction of individuals with genetic traits that enhance fitness in specific environments. Acting as the “janitor of the gene pool,” it eliminated deleterious mutations and maintained population resilience. Building on Fisher's concept of reproductive value [1], the Biological State Index (Ibs) quantifies the opportunity for natural selection by integrating mortality and fertility data to estimate the probability that an average individual transmits their genes to the next generation [2, 3]. An Ibs value of 1.0 indicates the theoretical absence of selection through mortality, where all individuals survive to reproductive maturity [3, 4].

Historically, infectious diseases such as smallpox, tuberculosis, plague, and influenza exerted intense selection pressures by removing individuals with weaker immunity [5, 6]. Over centuries, recurring epidemics refined immune defences in surviving populations. However, since the Industrial Revolution, during the second epidemiological transition, medical advances such as vaccination, sanitation, and antibiotic therapy have drastically reduced infectious mortality [4, 7, 8].

An unintended outcome of this progress has been the relaxation of natural selection [9]. Modern medicine now enables individuals with weaker immunity to survive and reproduce [10]. Over roughly five to six generations, this process may have increased the prevalence of alleles associated with reduced immune competence at the population level [3, 11]. Although life expectancy has improved, a subtle erosion of genetic resilience may have occurred [12].

The emergence of COVID‐19 provided a unique opportunity to examine these evolutionary consequences [13]. Populations with long‐standing advanced healthcare systems, reflected in higher Ibs values, may have accumulated greater genetic susceptibility to pathogens, resulting in higher infection rates despite having superior healthcare [14, 15]. Thus, nations experiencing prolonged relaxation of natural selection may paradoxically face increased infection vulnerability.

This hypothesis fits within a broader historical context. Since the Neolithic Revolution, human transitions such as animal domestication, agriculture, and urbanization have heightened pathogen exposure, driving the first epidemiological transition [16]. Modern lifestyles characterised by poor diet, sedentary behaviour, and reduced microbial exposure, contribute to evolutionary mismatch [7, 17]. The Hygiene Hypothesis suggests that highly sanitized environments limit immune system training, weakening innate defences against emerging pathogens.

Globalization has further accelerated pathogen spread through increased mobility and trade, facilitating the rise of zoonotic diseases, including SARS, MERS, Nipah, Ebola, and COVID‐19 [18, 19, 20]. Despite extensive interventions, such as vaccination, lockdowns, and travel restrictions, COVID‐19 continued to evolve rapidly. Non‐sterilizing vaccines reduced mortality but did not prevent transmission and may have favoured the emergence of more infectious variants such as Delta and Omicron [21, 22, 23]. Lockdowns slowed early spread but had limited long‐term impact [21, 23]

Evolutionary theory suggests pathogens tend toward reduced virulence over time [24, 25]; however, the short‐term evolution of highly transmissible variants highlights the complexity of host‐pathogen dynamics when human immunity is shaped by generations of relaxed selection. Modern responses increasingly rely on medical interventions without accounting for these evolutionary implications [26].

There is a growing need to integrate evolutionary perspectives into public health and pandemic preparedness [27]. Understanding how healthcare development, demographic change, and evolutionary history influence susceptibility to infectious diseases is vital for designing sustainable health strategies.

This study employs an ecological design to test the hypothesis that healthcare development, as measured by the Biological State Index (Ibs), is positively associated with cumulative COVID‐19 infection rates globally. By analysing data stratified by income levels, development classifications, and WHO regions, this research aims to clarify how healthcare progress, demographic structures, and evolutionary history interact to shape pandemic outcomes. The findings underscore the importance of incorporating evolutionary principles into public health frameworks to enhance future pandemic resilience.

Material and Methods

Data Sources

The dependent variable in this study is the population‐specific COVID‐19 infection rate, obtained from the World Health Organization (WHO) COVID‐19 dashboard under the metric “Cases – cumulative total per 100,000 population” as of June 28, 2022 [28]. This infection rate represents the cumulative number of confirmed COVID‐19 cases per 100,000 individuals within each population. For clarity and consistency, the term “COVID‐19 infection rate” is used throughout this manuscript to refer to the cumulative proportion of the population that contracted COVID‐19, as reported by the WHO. Although Ibs values were derived from fertility (2008) and mortality (2012) data, these indices represent long‐term, slow‐changing demographic characteristics rather than short‐term variations. Therefore, they remain valid for comparative analyses of recent health outcomes, including COVID‐19 infection rates.

The primary independent variable in this study is “selection opportunity,” assessed using the Ibs. The Ibs values were obtained from previously published sources [29], where they were calculated using fertility data from the United Nations (2008) and mortality data from the World Health Organization (2012) [30, 31]. The Ibs quantifies the probability that an average individual within a population will contribute genes to the next generation. Higher Ibs values reflect a greater likelihood of survival to and through the reproductive age period, primarily due to medical and social advancements. An Ibs of 1.0 indicates the absence of mortality of potentially reproductively active individuals, effectively minimizing natural selection based on deaths of children and young adults during their reproductive life span. As a result, Ibs serves as an indicator of the extent to which modern healthcare and living conditions have reduced natural selection pressures, potentially allowing a broader range of genetic traits, including those linked to immune function, to persist and accumulate across generations [10, 13, 32] rather than being diminished or eliminated.

In addition to the Ibs, several other factors previously associated with COVID‐19 infection rates were included in the analysis as potential contributing variables: population aging, economic affluence, urbanization, and vaccination rate. Given the delayed effects of demographic and socioeconomic changes on COVID‐19 infection dynamics, the variables of aging, economic affluence, and urbanization were backdated by 4 years, using 2018 data.

Aging

Population aging was measured as the percentage of individuals aged 65 years and older (Aging 65 + %) in 2018, sourced from the World Bank database [33]. This measure was included because biopsychosocial functioning typically declines from the age of 65 onward, and individuals in this age group represented a significant proportion of COVID‐19 cases (and deaths) during the pandemic.

Economic Affluence

Economic affluence was measured using Gross Domestic Product per capita at Purchasing Power Parity (GDP PPP), expressed in constant 2018 US dollars, published by the World Bank [34]. Economic affluence has been linked to the availability and quality of healthcare services, including access to COVID‐19 screening, treatment, and preventive measures.

Urbanization

Urbanization was defined as the percentage of the population living in urban areas in 2018, as reported by the World Bank [35]. Urban environments are characterized by a paradoxical combination of higher educational attainment and increased exposure to lifestyle risk factors, such as physical inactivity, reduced social engagement, consumption of nutrient‐poor foods, increased intake of gluten, meat, salt, fat, sugar, and alcohol, and higher levels of air pollution. Additionally, although urban centres often exhibit higher standards of hygiene, the dense population structure increases infection transmission risks, making urbanisation a complex and multifactorial risk factor for COVID‐19 infection.

Vaccination Rate

The COVID‐19 vaccination rate was measured as the percentage of the total population that had received all required doses of a COVID‐19 vaccine as of June 28, 2022, based on data published by the World Health Organization [28]. This metric reflects the proportion of individuals fully vaccinated per 100 people.

Data Preparation

We initially extracted a list of 215 populations with reported COVID‐19 infection rates as of June 28, 2022. Population‐specific data on the selection opportunity (Ibs), aging (percentage aged 65 years and older), economic affluence (GDP PPP per capita) urbanization and vaccination rate were then downloaded and matched to the corresponding populations listed for COVID‐19 infection rates. A complete dataset containing information for 215 countries was compiled and stored in Microsoft Excel for further analysis.

Due to missing estimates for some variables in certain populations, the sample sizes for specific analyses varied between 171 and 189 populations. Each population was treated as an individual study subject, and all available information was included in the analyses.

To explore the generalizability of the relationship between reduced natural selection opportunity and COVID‐19 infection rates, populations were grouped according to several classification systems for subgroup correlation analyses. These included the World Health Organization (WHO) geographic regions, World Bank income classifications, and United Nations common practice classifications. In addition, special regional and cultural groupings were analyzed, including the Asia Cooperation Dialogue (ACD), Asia‐Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC), Arab World, populations with English as an official language the European Economic Area (EEA), the European Union (EU), Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC), the Organization for Economic Co‐operation and Development (OECD), and the Southern African Development Community (SADC).

For these grouped analyses, only populations with available data matching each classification were included. Except for the grouping based on English as an official language, all other population groupings were sourced directly from their respective official organization websites prior to data matching.

Statistical Analysis

Multicollinearity diagnostics, including Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and Tolerance statistics, confirmed the absence of problematic intercorrelations among predictors (VIF range: 1.89–4.49; Tolerance range: 0.22–0.53), thereby satisfying the assumptions required for multiple regression analysis [36]. Following confirmation of these statistical assumptions, the relationships between selection opportunity (Ibs) and COVID‐19 infection rates were examined across different analytical models through a structured four‐step approach [32, 37].

Scatter Plot Visualization

Scatter plots were prepared to explore and visualize the relationship between selection opportunity and COVID‐19 infection rates at the population level. Scatter plots also allowed the assessment of data quality, distribution patterns, and potential curvilinearity. Raw (non‐log‐transformed) data were used to generate the scatter plots using Microsoft Excel 2016.

Before conducting correlation analyses, all variables were logarithmically transformed (ln) to reduce skewness, address potential curvilinear relationships, and improve homoscedasticity in the data distributions.

Bivariate Correlation Analysis

Bivariate correlations, including both Pearson's product‐moment correlation coefficients and nonparametric Spearman's rho, were conducted to examine the strength and direction of associations between selection opportunity, COVID‐19 infection rates, and competing variables. In addition to overall correlations, subgroup bivariate analyses were performed across different population classifications (e.g., WHO regions, World Bank income levels, and UN development groups) to further explore and compare patterns of association between Ibs and COVID‐19 infection rates.

Partial Correlation Analysis

Partial correlation analyses were performed using Pearson's correlation to assess the association between selection opportunity and COVID‐19 infection rates while statistically controlling for the associations of other variables: population aging (Aging 65 + %), economic affluence (GDP PPP), urbanization, and vaccination rate.

Two approaches were used: Each of the five variables (selection opportunity, aging, economic affluence, urbanization, and vaccination rate) was alternately treated as the predictor to assess its independent relationship with COVID‐19 infection rates, while the remaining variables were controlled.Conversely, each variable was alternately treated as the control variable to assess how adjusting for a single factor associated with the relationship between COVID‐19 infection rates and the other independent variables.

Multiple Linear Regression Analysis

Standard multiple linear regression (Enter method: where all independent variables are introduced into the model simultaneously) was conducted to model the association between COVID‐19 infection rates and the predictor variables (selection opportunity, aging, economic affluence, urbanization, and vaccination rate). Regression analyses were performed both with and without the inclusion of Ibs as a predictor to explore how selection opportunity influenced the model's explanatory power and the relationships between COVID‐19 infection rates and other variables.

Subsequently, stepwise multiple linear regression was employed to identify the most significant predictors of COVID‐19 infection rates when selection opportunity was included and excluded, respectively. This allowed further evaluation of the relative importance of each independent variable in associating with COVID‐19 infection patterns.

Bivariate correlations, partial correlations, and multiple linear regression analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All tests were two‐tailed with a predefined alpha level of 0.05, and stronger significance thresholds (p < 0.01 and p < 0.001) were reported where relevant. Regression entry and removal criteria were set at a probability of F to enter ≤ 0.05 and to remove ≥ 0.10. Correlation and standardized regression coefficients, standard errors, and 95% confidence intervals were presented to indicate the strength, direction, and precision of associations. Assumptions of normality, linearity, and multicollinearity were examined and met.

All primary analyses were pre‐specified, while subgroup analyses were exploratory and intended to assess the robustness of the main findings. P values were reported alongside the corresponding statistical tests and data being compared, following standard conventions: p < 0.001 for very small values, to the nearest thousandth for 0.001 ≤ p < 0.01, to the nearest hundredth for p ≥ 0.01, and p > 0.99 for large values. Interpretation emphasized effect sizes and the magnitude and direction of associations rather than statistical significance alone. All analyses adhered to established methodological and reporting standards, including the SAMPL and Assel et al. (2019) guidelines [38].

Discussion

This study provides new evidence that reduced natural selection opportunity, as measured by the Ibs, is independently associated with higher COVID‐19 infection rates at the global level. However, given the ecological and correlational nature of this study, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. In the overall analysis, this positive association remained significant even after adjusting for key sociodemographic and economic factors, including economic affluence, population aging, urbanization, and notably vaccination rates, highlighting the independent contribution of relaxed natural selection to population‐level vulnerability to an infectious disease. In addition, bivariate analyses across different country groupings, including World Bank income classifications, United Nations development categories, and WHO regional divisions, consistently showed that populations with stronger healthcare development, reflected by higher Ibs values, rather paradoxically experienced greater cumulative COVID‐19 infection rates.

The findings align with broader evolutionary research indicating that modern healthcare advances, while greatly improving survival and longevity on the one hand, have also progressively relaxed natural selection pressures. Previous studies have demonstrated that the global rise in non‐communicable diseases, such as cancer [39], type 1 diabetes [3], dementia [10], and obesity [9, 29], are associated with this relaxation of natural selection. In each case, individuals with higher genetic susceptibility to diseases were more likely to survive into the reproductive age and pass on their genetic predispositions to subsequent generations, contributing to increasing disease prevalence at the population level.

Applying this evolutionary framework to infectious diseases, our findings suggest that populations with historically advanced healthcare systems while offering improved treatment and protection, in doing so, may harbour a greater accumulation of genetic variants associated with reduced immune competence [40]. Over multiple generations, as individuals with compromised immune responses survive and reproduce at higher rates due to medical support, the overall genetic resilience of populations to novel infectious threats, such as SARS‐CoV‐2, may become progressively diminished [4]. This interpretation is supported by extensive historical and contemporary evidence demonstrating that host genetic variation plays a crucial role in susceptibility to a range of infectious diseases, including tuberculosis, malaria, HIV, hepatitis, measles, and meningococcal infections [41, 42]. Furthermore, evolutionary studies have shown that human immune responses have been strong targets of natural selection, and historical differences in pathogen exposure have shaped immune gene diversity across populations [42, 43]. As an indication of this, the human immune system has an approximately 4‐billion‐year evolutionary developmental history generating current‐day complexity [44]. Therefore, the strong positive association between selection opportunity and COVID‐19 infection rates observed in this study may partly reflect underlying biological vulnerabilities that have accumulated through evolutionary processes in settings where natural selection has been relaxed. The consistently stronger correlations observed among LMICs compared to high‐income countries, and between developing and developed nations, are complex, but further highlight the potential role of healthcare‐mediated relaxation of natural selection [13]. However, results from smaller subgroups, particularly in regions with limited data availability, should be interpreted with caution, as reduced sample sizes may lower statistical precision and increase variability. In LMICs, where healthcare infrastructure has historically been less advanced, stronger selective pressures may have perhaps ironically preserved higher average levels of immune robustness within the population [45]. In contrast, in developed nations, the widespread availability of medical interventions over multiple generations has allowed individuals with weaker immune systems to survive, reproduce and genetically transmit, gradually leading to an accumulation of genetic variants associated with lower immune competence [4]. Although superior diagnostic capacity, broader surveillance systems, and more complete case reporting in developed countries may partly explain the higher recorded COVID‐19 infection rates, the biological backdrop of reduced immunological resilience likely also contributes to a genuinely higher susceptibility to novel infectious threats [46]. It is also important to acknowledge that higher reported infection rates in developed nations may, at least partly, reflect better testing coverage, diagnostic accuracy, and surveillance infrastructure rather than true differences in biological susceptibility. Together, these factors suggest that both reporting artifacts and underlying biological vulnerabilities must be considered when interpreting differences in pandemic outcomes across different socioeconomic settings [46] and in future responses.

Interestingly, economic affluence remained the strongest individual predictor of COVID‐19 infection rates across all models, but the addition of selection opportunity significantly enhanced the explanatory power of the regression models. This emphasizes that while social determinants such as wealth and healthcare infrastructure influence pandemic outcomes, biological factors rooted in long‐term evolutionary trajectories still also play an independent and important role [47].

Beyond economic affluence, demographic factors demonstrated notable, but comparatively weaker relationships with COVID‐19 infection rates. Population aging, expressed as the percentage of individuals aged 65 years and older, was consistently a positive predictor of infection rates, both independently and after adjusting for other variables. This finding is consistent with previous reports that older people have higher biological vulnerability to infectious diseases due to immunosenescence and comorbidities [48, 49]. However, in our models, the predictive strength of aging was moderate and further diminished when selection opportunity was accounted for, suggesting that demographic aging alone cannot fully explain population‐level differences in infection dynamics.

Vaccination rates showed positive correlations with cumulative infection rates in our bivariate analyses and initial regression models. This counterintuitive association most likely reflects reverse causality, whereby countries experiencing higher infection burdens implemented rapid and widespread vaccination campaigns, rather than vaccination contributing directly to increased infection risk [50]. When early and late phases of the pandemic were examined separately using WHO data (pre‐rollout: 2020–early 2021; post‐rollout: late 2021–2022), infection surges consistently preceded major vaccination drives, further supporting this interpretation. After adjusting for other factors, the independent predictive strength of vaccination rates became marginal, suggesting that while vaccination remains crucial in reducing disease severity and mortality, its association with cumulative infection rates is complex and shaped by multiple contextual and temporal factors. Additionally, variations in non‐COVID‐19 vaccination coverage across countries may have exerted both protective and unintended immunological effects, but insufficient global reporting precluded reliable analysis of these associations.

In contrast, urbanization did not emerge as a significant predictor of COVID‐19 infection rates in any model. Although urban environments theoretically facilitate faster transmission due to higher population density and mobility [51], our findings suggest that urbanization per se does not independently drive cumulative infection rates when other factors such as healthcare access, socioeconomic status, and selection opportunity are considered. Of importance during this recent Covid‐19 pandemic, were lock‐down and lock‐out restrictive behaviour patterns and their regional enforcements, which might have variably influenced population movements. Our finding highlights the need to interpret urbanization effects cautiously and in the broader context of interacting demographic and systemic factors [52].

Taken together, these patterns suggest that while traditional demographic factors contribute to COVID‐19 pandemic dynamics, their explanatory power is limited compared to broader socioeconomic structures and evolutionary biological processes.

Overall, the findings highlight population‐level associations between healthcare development and COVID‐19 infection rates, but causal interpretations are beyond the scope of this ecological analysis.

Strengths and Limitations

This study employed an ecological design using aggregated population‐level data to identify patterns that may not be observable at the individual level. The value of ecological approaches has been demonstrated in prior epidemiological research on cancer, obesity, dementia, and type 1 diabetes [32, 53, 54, 55].

However, several inherent limitations must be considered when interpreting these findings. First, as with all ecological analyses, the results indicate association rather than causation between reduced natural selection and COVID‐19 infection rates. Subgroup analyses with smaller sample sizes may also show greater variability and should be interpreted with caution. Second, the study is subject to ecological fallacy—population‐level associations may not necessarily apply to individuals. Third, the aggregated international data used may contain minor inaccuracies due to differences in collection or reporting practices. Finally, the Biological State Index (Ibs) reflects only postnatal survival and does not account for other evolutionary processes such as gametic selection or intrauterine mortality [56].

Despite these methodological constraints, the consistency and strength of the observed relationships across multiple analytical models provide robust evidence that populations with more relaxed natural selection tend to experience higher COVID‐19 infection rates. These results offer a foundation for future evolutionary and longitudinal studies to further clarify how natural selection shapes human susceptibility to infectious disease.

Public Health Implications

The findings of this study have important implications for public health strategy and pandemic preparedness. While continuous improvements in healthcare systems are essential, integrating evolutionary perspectives on human susceptibility is equally vital for developing sustainable prevention and response frameworks. Applying evolutionary principles to public health planning can guide interventions that are not only reactive to emerging threats but also predictive of biological vulnerabilities shaped by relaxed natural selection.

Recognizing the evolutionary dimensions of population health can strengthen frameworks to address both immediate infectious risks and long‐term immune resilience. The influence of reduced natural selection, though complex, should be considered when designing and funding health policies. Overlooking evolutionary processes that historically enhanced human immunity may inadvertently increase vulnerability to infectious and chronic diseases.

Practical applications include embedding genomic surveillance to track immune diversity and pathogen evolution, adopting vaccination strategies that maintain broad immunological training (e.g., heterologous or booster regimens), and promoting lifestyle and environmental conditions that preserve immune robustness. Evolutionary modelling can further inform how interventions affect pathogen adaptation and transmission, supporting more resilient and adaptive health systems.

Conclusion

This study provides new evidence that reduced opportunity for natural selection, as measured by the Ibs, is independently associated with higher COVID‐19 infection rates at the global level. While economic affluence remained the strongest individual predictor, the inclusion of selection opportunity significantly improved the explanatory power of the models, highlighting the contribution of evolutionary factors to pandemic vulnerability. Bivariate analyses across different country groupings further supported the consistent positive association between selection opportunity and infection rates. These findings suggest that evolutionary processes linked to long‐term healthcare development have played a significant role in shaping population‐level susceptibility to COVID‐19.
