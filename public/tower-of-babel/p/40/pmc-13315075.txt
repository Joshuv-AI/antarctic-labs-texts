Numerical simulation of heat transfer through radiative hybrid nanofluid flow subject to thermal convection past a porous stretching surface
Authors: Humaira Yasmin, Zehba Raizah, Saima Noor, Anwar Saeed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, a rigorous numerical simulation of the hybrid nanofluid (AA7072-AA7075/methanol) flow over a rotating porous stretching surface is presented. Such a configuration has great importance for aerospace thermal management and high-performance engines' cooling. The main aim is to study the synergistic effect of Soret, Dufour effects, thermal radiation, and exponential heat sources on the efficiency of the fluid flow. For centrifugal pumping systems and heat exchangers that have rotational stresses and multi-physical constraints, such a modeling framework is necessary to optimize the system. This study aims to connect theoretical magnetohydrodynamics with the practical applications to cooling applications within the industrial sector by using the Parametric Continuation Method (PCM). From the comparative analysis of current work, it has been noticed that there is a strong correlation between the current and published results, which ensures the authenticity of these results. From the tabular results, it has been observed that the skin friction enhances up to 74.7319% and 62.0497% by varying the A7072 and AA7075 NPs volume friction from 0.01 to 0.03, whereas it drops up to 34.1635% with the variation in the mixed convection factor. The energy transfer rate enhances up to 22.1359% by varying the thermal radiation factor from 0.3 to 0.7, whereas it drops up to 20.0484% and 21.0407% by varying the exponential heat source parameter and Dufour number from 0.1 to 0.5 and 0.2 to 0.6, respectively. The Sherwood number drops by up to 18.6451% by varying the activation energy factor from 1.0 to 5.0, while enhances up to 17.1852% with variation in the rate of chemical reaction factor.Keywords: Nanofluid, Hybrid nanofluid, Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD), Thermal radiation, Stretching sheet, Mixed convection, PCM technique

Introduction

Nanofluid flow signifies advancement in thermal engineering and fluid dynamics, focusing on scattering of nanometer-sized solid particles in conventional base fluids. The fundamental premise is that the incorporation of these nanoparticles, usually at low volume fractions (less than 5%), can dramatically alter the thermophysical properties of the fluid. Most notably, thermal conductivity often experiences a substantial enhancement as revealed by Choi [1], sometimes exceeding a 40% increase, which is not predicted by classical effective medium theory. Nanofluids have been extensively investigated for applications demanding superior heat transfer, including advanced cooling systems for electronics and solar thermal collectors [2, 3]. However, the flow behavior of nanofluids deviates from Newtonian predictions at higher concentrations, exhibiting increased viscosity and potential non-Newtonian characteristics. The hybrid uses of a combination of AA7072 and AA7075 nanoparticles in a methanol base are particularly driven by the fact that they work synergistically in advanced thermal systems. This is because Aluminium alloys (AA series) exhibit excellent corrosion resistance properties and have a high thermal conductivity-to-weight ratio, making them well-suited for lightweight cooling applications in the aerospace and automotive industry. The low freezing point and high volatility of methanol are used as the carrier fluid, especially in heat pipes and electronic cooling systems in extreme environments. When these two specific alloys are mixed together, the effective thermal and electrical conductivities of the fluid can be controlled more precisely than with one type of nanoparticulate system. Yasmin et al. [4] studied double stratification effects on radiative nanofluid flow on an expanding cylinder. Hybrid nanofluid flow extends the concept by employing a combination of two or more distinct kinds of nanoparticles dispersed in a base fluid, aiming to synergistically combine their advantageous properties and mitigate individual limitations as highlighted by Khan et al. [5]. For instance, a hybrid might combine high-thermal-conductivity metallic particles (e.g., silver or copper) with more stable and chemically compatible oxide nanoparticles (e.g., alumina or titania), seeking an optimal balance between enhanced heat transfer, structural stability, and cost-effectiveness. Mahanthesh et al. [6] studied dissipative and Joule heating effects on a 3D nanoliquid flow with the NPs aggregation. Tripathi et al. [7] examined current advancements regarding mono and hybrid nanofluid flow by carrying out experimental work. Research of Mahanthesh et al. [8] indicates that properly formulated Williamson nanoliquid can achieve superior thermal performance compared to single-particle nanofluids, alongside improved stability and reduced susceptibility to sedimentation. Ongoing studies [9, 10] focus on optimizing nanoparticle combinations, understanding the underlying mechanisms of heat transfer enhancement, and addressing practical challenges related to preparation methods, long-term colloidal stability, and the economic viability of large-scale implementation.

Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) is the interdisciplinary field that examines the dynamics of electrically conductive fluids with magnetic effects. These fluids, in turn, interact with the original magnetic field to yield Lorentz forces, which can accelerate, decelerate, or reshape the flow in profound ways. Abdal et al. [11] examined magnetized fluid flow with the effects of Cattaneo-Christov flux theory. Applications of MHD are vast and transformative, ranging from controlling molten metal flows in continuous casting processes and designing efficient liquid–metal cooling systems for advanced nuclear fusion reactors, to explaining astrophysical phenomena like the dynamics of solar flares and the magnetic fields of stars and planets [12–14]. Lone et al. [15] studied MHD fluid flow on a permeable exponential expanding surface with thermo-phoretic particle deposition. In engineering, MHD principles are also harnessed in electromagnetic pumps with no moving parts for transporting corrosive liquid metals, and in boundary layer control for high-speed aircraft to reduce drag. The study of MHD flows is thus vital for advancing both terrestrial technology and our understanding of the universe, providing a framework to manipulate and analyze flows where magnetic and fluid dynamic forces are inextricably linked. Regarding velocity and temperature profiles, the impact of magnetohydrodynamics is substantial and distinct. The imposed magnetic field generally acts as a resistive brake, flattening the velocity profile in the core of a channel flow while creating steep boundary layers, known as Hartmann layers, near walls where viscous forces balance the Lorentz force as examined by Ganji et al. [16]. Thermally, the damping of velocity fluctuations suppresses convective heat transference, often leading to a more conductive-dominated regime. Consequently, temperature profiles become more linear and stratified, with reduced thermal boundary layer thickness. The Joule heating generated by induced currents also adds an internal heat source, which can further distort temperature distributions, potentially creating hot spots and altering overall thermal management strategies in engineering systems [17]. Upreti et al. [18] examined the effects of a magnetic field on radiative nanofluid flow over a wedge.

Thermal radiation introduces a fundamentally distinct mode of energy transfer into fluid flow systems, operating through the emission and absorption of electromagnetic waves by the fluid medium itself, without reliance on a physical medium for propagation. This mechanism becomes critically important in high-temperature environments, as described by Alhazmi et al. [19], and can rival or even dominate conductive and convective heat transfer. The analysis of such flows necessitates modifying the classic energy conservation equation to include a radiative source term that is responsible for scattering radiation within the participating medium [20]. Algehyne et al. [21] discussed computationally the magneto-hydrodynamics fluid flow with bio-convection on a spinning disk. The coupling between radiation and fluid dynamics profoundly alters the thermal boundary layer, as radiative heat transfer can pre-heat or cool fluid particles remotely, effectively communicating thermal information across distances much greater than molecular diffusion would allow. Thermally radiative fluid flow significantly enhances heat transfer. As the radiation parameter increases, thermal profiles augment, indicating improved energy distribution. This effect, demonstrated by Abbas et al. [22], is vital for optimizing systems where radiative heat exchange dominates, like in advanced solar panels. Applications are extensive and vital, including the design of advanced combustion chambers and furnaces, where accurate prediction of radiative heat load on walls is essential for material survival and efficiency [23, 24]. In aerospace engineering for modeling re-entry vehicle aerothermodynamics and spacecraft thermal management, in astrophysics for understanding stellar atmospheres and interstellar clouds, and in advanced manufacturing processes like glass forming and crystal growth. Shakir et al. [25] investigated a nonlinear thermally radiative model for tetra hybrid nanofluid flow on a Riga surface. Their study analyzed the complex relationship among the electromagnetic forces from the Riga plate and enhanced radiative heat transfer within the advanced multi-component nanofluid. Farooq et al. [26] studied computationally the thermally nonlinear radiative fluid flow on a surface with Cattaneo-Christov flux theory using several slip effects.

The study of fluid flow on a stretching sheet represents a cornerstone of boundary layer theory with immense practical significance in modern manufacturing and materials science. This classical problem [27], first systematically analyzed by Crane in 1970. The resulting self-similar solution provides profound insights, revealing that the velocity field is confined to a thin region adjacent to the sheet and decays exponentially to the quiescent free stream. Islam et al. [28] provided a theoretical analysis of fluid flow over an expanding surface. The study employed sinusoidal boundary constraints to model the complex interaction and its effects on the flow dynamics. This paradigm has been extensively generalized to incorporate a multitude of realistic physical effects, including magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) forces, where an applied transverse magnetic field interacts with the flow to create a resistive Lorentz force, thereby altering the velocity profile as examined by Wang et al. [29]. Sohail et al. [30] analyzed the bio-convective flow of a Maxwell fluid on a stretching sheet. The stretching sheet model finds direct and vital uses like the extrusion of polymer sheets, the cooling of metallic plates in a bath, the drawing of plastic films, fiber spinning, and the aerodynamic extrusion of artificial fibers [31, 32]. Its mathematical elegance and practical relevance continue to drive research, with contemporary explorations extending the model to nanofluids, hybrid nanofluids, and flows within porous media, making it an enduring and fertile topic for analyzing momentum and heat transfer in boundary layers driven by surface motion. Madan et al. [33] computationally analyzed MHD Jeffery fluid flow on a permeable expanding sheet. Their study focused on the combined effects of thermal radiation and mass diffusion, detailing resultant modifications to velocity, temperature, and concentration profiles.

Mixed convection flow represents a critical regime in heat transfer where the driven forces of both natural (buoyancy) and forced convection are of comparable magnitude, neither negligible in comparison to the other. This interaction fundamentally alters flow patterns, heat transfer rates, and stability characteristics from what is observed in the pure forms of either mode. Buonomo et al. [34] computationally studied convection in a vertical channel containing a nanofluid. Their model analyzed the complex flow and heat transfer generated by two walls moving in opposite directions, quantifying the interplay between buoyancy, shear, and nanoparticle effects. Ahmad et al. [35] assessed the energy transportation in a hybrid nanoliquid under the mutual effect of heat generation and electric potential. Their analysis focused on entropy generation, incorporating significant nonlinear thermal radiation effects to assess thermodynamic efficiency within the electromagnetic boundary layer flow. These flows are abundant in engineering and environmental systems, including the cooling of electronic components and nuclear reactors, solar thermal receivers, heat exchangers operating at low speeds, atmospheric boundary layers, and ventilation in buildings [36, 37]. Gorai et al. [38] studied mixed convective fluid flow with buoyancy effects on a surface. Such a flow creates complex phenomena like flow acceleration or deceleration, reversal, and the development of recirculation zones, depending on whether the buoyancy force opposes or assists the primary forced flow. In assisting flow, where buoyancy acts in the same direction as the forced motion (e.g., heated surface in an upward stream), it weakens the boundary layer and significantly augments heat transfer. Conversely, in opposing flow, buoyancy acts against the forced stream, leading to a thickened boundary layer, flow separation, and potentially a degradation in heat transfer efficiency. The analysis often involves elaborated boundary layer considerations on various geometries like vertical or inclined plates, cylinders, and channels, and must account for additional complexities like thermal radiation and magnetic fields. Qin et al. [39] studied a computational approach for convective (mixed) non-Newtonian fluid flow by using the Blasius–Rayleigh–Stokes equations. Sreenivasulu et al. [40] examined convective fluid flow with MHD and heat radiation using stable temperature differences past a vertical permeable surface.

In this study, a new approach of utilizing AA7072 and AA7075 aluminum alloy NPs in a methanol-based hybrid nanofluid and exploring their dynamics on a rotating porous stretching surface, is developed. The simultaneous analysis of Soret and Dufour effects with exponential heat source/sink and thermal radiation in a magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) rotating frame is the key novelty of the present study, a configuration which is not fully investigated in literature on heat transfer. Moreover, the numerical framework generated by the application of the Parametric Continuation Method (PCM) is very stable and is able to capture the complex interaction between the viscous dissipation and the cross-diffusion terms. The study defines the specific effect of dual alloy nanoparticles on thermal and concentration profiles, giving original insights into the optimization of cooling efficiency and mass transport in highly precise aerospace and industrial applications.

Solution method

The Parametric Continuation Method (PCM) is a numerical strategy for solving intricate nonlinear equations. It embeds an artificial parameter (p) into the system, enabling a gradual transition from a simple, solvable problem (at p = 0) to the original complex problem (at p = 1) [45]. This path is tracked using a predictor–corrector algorithm. The method is frequently considered superior to alternatives like Newton–Raphson, as it systematically maps solution families, ensures global convergence independent of initial guess accuracy, and successfully navigates turning or bifurcation points where standard iterative techniques fail. Particularly robust for highly nonlinear and unstable systems, the PCM offers key advantages: global convergence independent of initial guess, the ability to handle turning points, bifurcations, and multiple solutions, as well as stability with stiff systems [46]. This versatility makes it a universal tool in engineering and physics, especially for tracing solution branches in bifurcation analysis.

The numerical stability and accuracy of the results are ensured by setting a strict convergence criterion of 10–6 for all the governing residuals. The PCM is particularly selected for this problem because of its global convergence and its ability to provide a robust solution for the problem of rotating coupled equations of Nanofluids that are inherently highly nonlinear, where standard methods such as Newton–Raphson often fail given a poor initial guess [47]. Discussion of results

This investigation examines the heat and mass transfer properties of a hybrid nanofluid (HNF) composed of methanol infused with aluminum alloy nanoparticles (AA7072 and AA7075). The NPs (AA7072 and AA7075) are assumed to be spherical in shape, having a diameter range from 10–100 nm. The volume fraction of these NPs is changed from 0.01 (1%) to 0.1 (10%) in this analysis. The fluid flows across a surface that is both stretching and rotating with Soret and Dufour effects. The effects of thermal radiation, viscous dissipation, and an exponential heat source are used in the work. Magnetic effects are used in the normal direction to the flow system. The main equations of the work have been converted to dimension-free form and then solved through PCM. In the following analysis, the effects of different factors on the velocity, thermal, and concentration fields are explained.

This force acts to flatten the velocity profile , suppresses boundary layer development, and increases the effective viscosity of the flow. Consequently, the flow experiences greater resistance to motion, leading to a substantial deceleration in velocity distribution across the flow on the surface of the sheet. It acts perpendicular to the direction of motion, effectively deflecting fluid particles and disrupting the primary flow. This deflection introduces additional resistance and viscous dissipation, redistributing momentum and suppressing the boundary layer. Ultimately, the intensified rotational effects impede the main flow, causing a deceleration velocity field across the sheet’s surface. This elevation in viscosity strengthens the fluid's resistance to deformation and motion, resulting in greater viscous damping forces relative to inertial forces. Consequently, the flow decelerates, the momentum boundary layer thickens, and the profiles become more suppressed and uniform across the domain. Thermal profiles

The impression of numerous factors on the thermal profiles is explained in Fig. 3a–g. The effect of Eckert number on is explained in Fig. 3a with augmenting manner in the behavior of for growth in . An intensification in causes a rise in due to the enhanced significance of viscous dissipation. The Eckert number represents the ratio of kinetic energy to the enthalpy difference in a flow. As it grows, the work done against viscous forces, which is converted into internal thermal energy, becomes more prominent relative to the heat transfer from conduction. This process acts as an internal heat source, increasing the fluid temperature. Consequently, the conversion of mechanical energy into heat thickens the thermal boundary layer, elevates the temperature distribution, and augments the thermal profile throughout the flow on the surface of the sheet. The effect of nanoparticles concentration on is explained in Fig. 3b with declining manner in the behavior of for growth in . The reduction in with growing is primarily attributed to a substantial enhancement in the effective thermal conductivities of the fluid. While the nanoparticles themselves have high conductivities, their augmented volume fraction greatly improves the fluid's overall ability to transport thermal energy. This enhanced conductive capability facilitates more efficient heat transfer away from the heated surface and throughout the fluid motion, thereby lowering the temperature gradient and reducing the magnitude of the thermal boundary layer. Consequently, the temperature distribution becomes reduced, and the peak temperatures within the thermal profile are suppressed. The effect of radiation parameter on is explained in Fig. 3c with augmenting manner in the behavior of for growth in . An intensification in causes augmentation in due to the intensified contribution of radiative heat transfer relative to thermal conduction. This parameter signifies the strength of thermal radiation within the flow system. As it grows, radiative energy exchange between the fluid, the surface, and the surroundings becomes more dominant. This process effectively adds a source of thermal energy throughout the fluid medium, supplementing thermal transfer. Consequently, the internal energy of the fluid increases, raising the overall temperature distribution , thickening the thermal boundary layer, and resulting in a more noticeable thermal profile . The effect of the heat source on is explained in Fig. 3d with augmenting manner in the behavior of for growth in . The augmentation of with an intensification in results from the direct and positive addition of thermal energy into the flow system. A stronger internal heat source acts as a volumetric energy generation term, continuously supplying heat throughout the fluid medium. This extra energy input elevates the internal energy of the fluid, raising its temperature above the baseline established by boundary conditions and viscous dissipation. Consequently, the rate of temperature increase becomes more noticeable, causing a substantial rise in the temperature distribution, a thickening of the thermal layer, and an augmented thermal profile across the entire surface on which fluid flows. The effect of an exponential heat source/sink on is explained in Fig. 3e with augmenting manner in the behavior of for growth in . The augmentation in with an intensification in occurs due to the net positive contribution of volumetric heat generation to the system's energy balance. When this parameter grows, the non-uniform, temperature-dependent heat source (represented by the exponential function) becomes more intense, releasing additional thermal energy into the fluid at a rate that increases with the local temperature. This acts as a self-reinforcing internal heating mechanism, significantly elevating the fluid's internal energy. Consequently, this leads to a higher overall temperature distribution, a thickening of the thermal boundary layer that causes growth in . The effect of Dufour number on is explained in Fig. 3f with augmenting manner in the behavior of for growth in . The augmentation of with a rise in results from the enhanced influence of a coupled diffusion process known as the Dufour effect. This number quantifies the contribution of mass concentration gradients to the heat flux. As it grows, the energy transferred due to a species concentration gradient (from regions of high to low concentration) becomes more substantial. This cross-diffusion mechanism acts as an additional internal heat source, effectively converting a portion of the diffusive mass flux into thermal energy within the fluid. Consequently, this supplementary heating elevates the fluid's internal temperature, which augments the overall thermal profiles throughout the flow domain on the surface of the sheet. The effect of thermal Biot number on is explained in Fig. 3g with augmenting manner in the behavior of for growth in . The augmentation of with an intensification in results from a shift in the dominant mode of thermal resistance at the boundary. The Biot number compares the internal conductive resistance of the solid body to the external convective resistance from the surrounding fluid. A higher signifies that the internal conductive resistance is more significant than the external convective resistance. This implies that the surface approaches a convective boundary condition, allowing for a more efficient transfer of heat from the surrounding fluid into the material. Consequently, more thermal energy is absorbed internally, causing a steeper temperature gradient within the solid and an intensification in the thermal profiles .

Concentration profiles

The impression of numerous factors on concentration profiles is explained in Fig. 4a–d. The effect of activation energy on is explained in Fig. 4a with augmenting manner in behavior of for growth in . The augmentation of with an intensification in is a direct consequence of the Arrhenius kinetics governing the chemical reaction. Activation energy represents the minimum energy barrier that reacting molecules must overcome for a reaction to occur. A higher makes this barrier more difficult to surmount, thereby slowing down the rate of the destructive chemical reaction. With a diminished reaction rate, the consumption of the reacting species is reduced. This allows the species to accumulate within the fluid medium, leading to higher concentrations, a thicker concentration layer, and an overall augmentation of the concentration profiles throughout the flow domain on the surface of the sheet. The effect of chemical reactivity on is explained in Fig. 4b with declining manner in the behavior of for growth in . An intensification in causes a reduction in due to the accelerated rate of the destructive chemical reaction. Enhanced reactivity signifies a higher kinetic rate constant, which drives a faster consumption of the reactive species within the fluid medium. As the reaction proceeds more rapidly, the mass of the species is converted into products at an increased pace. This heightened consumption depletes the species' concentration more effectively, resulting in a lower concentration distribution, a thinning of the concentration boundary layer that suppresses the concentration profiles throughout the flow domain. The effect of Schmidt number on is explained in Fig. 4c with reducing manner in the behavior of for growth in . The physical interpretation of this phenomenon lies in the relationship between the Schmidt number . An increasing signifies either a more viscous fluid or, more typically in this context, a significantly lower molecular diffusivity for the species in the fluid. A lower diffusivity means the species molecules are less effective at spreading out via random motion against the flow. Consequently, the concentration of the species remains confined to a thinner region near the source or boundary, leading to steeper concentration gradients \and an overall reduction in the concentration profile's spatial extent, as mass transfer is dominated by fluid motion rather than molecular diffusion. The effect of the Soret parameter on is explained in Fig. 4d with augmenting manner in behavior of for growth in . The observed reduction in with an increasing has a clear physical interpretation. The Soret effect, or thermodiffusion, describes the mass flux of a species induced by a temperature gradient. As this effect grows stronger, it drives a more powerful migration of species from hotter to colder regions, or vice versa, depending on the species' thermophoretic properties. Consequently, this thermally-driven flux acts as an additional transport mechanism that actively redistributes the species, opposing and overriding the concentration-driven diffusion that would otherwise lead to a more uniform, broader profile. The result is a sharper segregation of nanoparticles along the thermal gradient, causing a marked depletion of concentration in the high-temperature region and a corresponding accumulation in the colder zone, thus steepening and narrowing the overall concentration distribution .

Conclusions

This investigation examines the heat and mass transfer properties of a hybrid nanofluid (HNF) composed of methanol infused with aluminum alloy nanoparticles (AA7072 and AA7075). The fluid flows across a surface that is both stretching and rotating with Soret and Dufour effects. The effects of thermal radiation, viscous dissipation, and an exponential heat source are used in the work. Magnetic effects are used in the normal direction to the flow system. Limitations and future directions

Limitations: This research works a thorough numerical study of the hybrid nanofluid AA7072-AA7075 with methanol as the base fluid, but it has some limitations. First, the investigation is based on a steady state condition, while the majority of industrial rotating systems consist of unsteady fluctuations that can affect the thermal boundary layer. Secondly, the NPs are assumed to be perfectly dispersed and uniformly spherical, but in real applications, they might, over time, be non-spherical or slightly agglomerated, which may also change the effective conductivity.

Future Directions: Further studies may be conducted based on this model to account for the unsteady flow characteristics or for non-Newtonian base fluids (Power-law or Eyring-Powell model) to better simulate biological or polymer processing applications. The Hamilton-Crosser model could be used to investigate the effect of shape factors, such as cylindrical, platelet, or blade-shaped NPs, and lead to a better understanding of optimization of heat transfer. In addition, using Entropy Generation analysis and the second law of Thermodynamics would be helpful to consider the energy inefficiency and irreversibility of the system. Lastly, the experimental validations would be useful to compare the numerical results obtained in this hybrid nanofluid configuration with experimental data obtained in the lab for further validation of its practical usefulness.
