The effect of surgeon experience on the short-term outcomes of aortic annular enlargement
Authors: Alexander Makkinejad, Barbara CS Hamilton, Robert B Hawkins, Shinichi Fukuhara, Karen Kim, Paul Tang, Himanshu J Patel, Francis Pagani, Gorav Ailawadi, Bo Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo determine whether surgeon case volume influences short-term outcomes after aortic annular enlargement (AAE).MethodsFrom January 2019 to December 2023, 202 patients underwent isolated first-time aortic valve replacement with aortic annular enlargement by 11 surgeons. Two performed ≥25 AAEs per year (high case volume surgeons), whereas the other 9 performed <25 AAEs per year (low case volume surgeons). The short-term outcomes were compared between the high case volume surgeons (n = 126) and the low case volume surgeons (n = 76).ResultsThe groups were similar in age, sex, body surface area, and all measured preoperative comorbidities, including Society of Thoracic Surgeons Predicted Risk of Mortality scores. Preoperative aortic valve mean gradients and aortic valve areas did not significantly differ between the groups. The high case-volume surgeons implanted prostheses that were on average one size larger (size 27 vs size 25). All measured perioperative outcomes including operative mortality were not statistically different between groups. Kaplan-Meier estimation of survival at 4 years was 96.0% among the patients of the high case-volume surgeons and 92.5% among those of the low case-volume surgeons (P = .57). On follow-up echocardiography, patients of the high case-volume surgeons had lower aortic valve mean gradients (7 mm Hg vs 8 mm Hg, P = .005), larger aortic valve areas (2.2 cm2 vs 1.9 cm2, P = .039), and less patient-prosthesis mismatch (6.9% [5/72] vs 26% [11/43], P = .005).ConclusionsAAE can be performed safely by surgeons with less experience with the procedure, but surgeons who perform AAE more frequently implant larger valves with better hemodynamics.Key Words: aorta, aortic valve replacement, aortic root enlargement, aortic annular enlargement

Four-year survival is similar regardless of surgeon experience with annular enlargement.

Central Message

Aortic annular enlargement can be safely performed regardless of surgeon experience with the procedure, but greater-volume AAE surgeons tend to implant larger valves with better hemodynamics.

Perspective

The current literature pertaining to the outcomes of aortic annular enlargement has been variable. Some studies show that annular enlargement is safe, whereas others find that it compromises short-term outcomes. It is unclear whether these discrepancies in short-term outcomes are due to variability in surgeon experience with annular enlargement.

Aortic annular enlargement (AAE) represents an adjunctive procedure that can be used during aortic valve replacement (AVR) to implant a larger aortic valve prosthesis than the native aortic annulus would otherwise be able to accommodate. As it currently stands, there is no randomized controlled trial proving there is a survival benefit of performing AAE. A majority of studies on the matter have demonstrated that AAE can be performed safely,1, 2, 3, 4, 5 whereas a small minority have shown potentially small incremental risk.6, 7, 8 The theoretical benefit of AAE stems from the increased valve area of the larger prosthesis. Studies have consistently shown that AAE improves hemodynamics and decreases the risk of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM), and severe PPM has been shown to be associated with increased mortality after AVR.9,10 Given the theoretical survival benefit associated with reducing PPM, some surgeons argue that given the relative safety of performing AAE, it should be used more routinely for patients undergoing AVR, especially those with a small native aortic annulus.

However, the addition of an annular enlargement adds complexity and time to AVR, an operation with an exceedingly small mortality rate. Given low annular enlargement rates in training programs and limited training options for practicing surgeons, the perceived learning curve can be difficult to overcome. Yet, the impact of surgeon case volume on AAE outcomes is largely unknown. Our study aimed to address the question of whether surgeon experience is a significant determinant of the outcomes of AVR with AAE. We hypothesized that AAE can be safely performed by surgeons even if they are relatively inexperienced with the operation, with satisfactory short-term clinical outcomes and hemodynamics.

Methods

The institutional review board of Michigan Medicine, University of Michigan (HUM00211344, September 18, 2023) approved the study with a waiver of informed consent.

Subjects

From January 2019 to December 2023, 516 patients at Michigan Medicine underwent AVR with AAE by 11 surgeons, of whom 2 performed ≥25 AAEs per year on average, and 9 performed <25 AAEs per year (Table E1). Patients who had undergone previous cardiac surgery and those with any concomitant major procedures were excluded, to yield a cohort of 202 patients who underwent isolated, first-time AVR with AAE. Patients were stratified by surgeons who performed ≥25 AAEs per year (high case volume surgeons, n = 126) and surgeons who performed <25 AAEs per year (low case volume surgeons, n = 76). All AAEs were performed using either Nicks, Manouguian, or Y-incision procedures, the surgical techniques of which have all been well-described in the literature; cases where the surgeon enlarged the aortic root without enlarging the surgical aortic annulus to place a larger prosthesis were not included in the study.11, 12, 13

Data

Data were obtained via the Society of Thoracic Surgeons Data Warehouse from the Department of Cardiac Surgery at the University of Michigan to determine the preoperative, intraoperative, and perioperative variables and supplemented with medical record review. Long-term survival and reoperation data were obtained by medical chart review up to the present day and the Michigan Death Index data through August 27, 2024. Indexed effective orifice area (iEOA) was calculated by dividing aortic valve area (as measured by echocardiogram) by body surface area. PPM was defined by the current guidelines: for patients without obesity, an iEOA <0.85 cm2/m2 was considered moderate PPM and an iEOA <0.65 cm2/m2 was considered severe PPM. For patients with obesity, an iEOA <0.70 cm2/m2 was considered moderate PPM, and an iEOA <0.55 cm2/m2 was considered severe PPM.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are summarized by median (Q1, Q3), and categorical variables are reported as n (%) in frequency tables. Patients were stratified by surgeons who performed high-versus low-volume AAE for univariable comparisons. Continuous variables were compared by Kruskall-Wallis test, whereas for categorical variables the χ2 test or Fisher exact test was used as appropriate. A Kaplan-Meier model was used to estimate survival of the entire cohort with a long-rank test to compare the individual groups. The reoperation rate of the entire cohort was evaluated using a cumulative incidence function, adjusting for death as a competing risk, and the Gray test was used to compare the cumulative incidence of reoperation between groups. All statistical calculations used SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

Preoperative and Demographic Data

Between the patients of the high and low case-volume surgeons, the median age (64 years vs 63 years), number of male patients (42% vs 33%), and median body surface area (2.0 m2 vs 2.0 m2) were comparable. All other measured comorbidities including hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, chronic lung disease, previous stroke, previous myocardial infarction, and preoperative ejection fraction were also comparable between the 2 groups, as were Society of Thoracic Surgeons Predicted Risk of Mortality scores (Table 1). There was no statistical difference between preoperative aortic valve mean gradients (43 mm Hg vs 40 mm Hg) and aortic valve areas (0.8 cm2 vs 0.8 cm2) (Table 2).

Intraoperative Data

Aortic stenosis was the primary indication in 90% of the whole cohort, with no difference between the high- and low-volume AAE surgeons. Most cases in the whole cohort (97%) were elective, with no difference in case status between groups. The greater case-volume surgeons predominantly used the Y-incision technique (80% of cases) to enlarge the aortic annulus, whereas the lower case-volume surgeons tended to use either the Y-incision (49%) or Nicks (45%) techniques. The high case-volume surgeons used larger prostheses (median size 27 vs 25) and had significantly shorter cardiopulmonary bypass times (136 minutes vs 151 minutes) and crossclamp times (106 minutes vs 123 minutes) (Table 3).

Postoperative Data

The incidences of perioperative complications including atrial fibrillation, complete heat block requiring pacemaker, renal failure with new dialysis requirement, stroke, and reoperation for bleeding were all comparable between the 2 groups. Postoperative time to extubation, time in the intensive care unit, and hospital length of stay were also similar between the groups. Operative mortality was 3% among the patients of the high case-volume surgeons and 0% among the patients of the low case-volume surgeons (P = .29, Table 4).

Midterm Outcomes

Kaplan-Meier estimation of survival at 4 years was 96.0% among the patients whose surgeons performed ≥25 AAEs per year and 92.5% among those whose surgeons performed <25 AAEs per year (Figure 1, P = .57). Cumulative incidence of reoperation at 4 years was 0.8% among the patients of the high case-volume surgeons and 1.4% among those of the low case-volume surgeons, which included just one patient in each group requiring reoperation within 1 year, both for endocarditis (Figure 2, P = .67). No patients were completely lost to follow-up in the surveillance period.

Echocardiographic Data

One-year postoperative echocardiographic data had 78% completeness of follow-up and demonstrated that patients of the high case-volume surgeons had lower aortic valve mean gradients (7 mm Hg vs 8 mm Hg, P = .005), larger aortic valve areas (2.2 cm2 vs 1.9 cm2, P = .039), and less incidence of moderate (6.9% vs 23%) or severe (0% vs 2.3%) PPM (moderate/severe: 6.9% vs 26%, P = .005) compared with the patients of the low case-volume surgeons (Table 2).

Discussion

Our study compared the perioperative and midterm outcomes of first-time AVR with AAE between surgeons who perform a high case-volume of AAEs (≥25 AAEs/year) and those who perform a relatively low case-volume (<25 AAEs/year). We found that there were no differences in the perioperative complication rate, including operative mortality. Midterm survival and cumulative incidence of reoperation at 4 years were also not different between the groups. However, the surgeons who performed more annular enlargements on average used large prostheses (median 27 vs 25), and therefore their patients’ mean aortic valve gradients and incidence of PPM were lower (Table 2).

Because there has not been a randomized clinical trial or meta-analysis to prove a long-term benefit of performing AAE, it remains a relatively underutilized procedure nationally. There has been some speculation that existing studies are confounded by being performed at academic medical centers by very high-volume surgeons, and studies demonstrating the safety and effectiveness of AAE may not be generalizable to all cardiac surgeons. In our study, we were able to establish a detectable experience gap between the groups, as the surgeons who performed ≥25 AAEs/year had shorter cardiopulmonary bypass and crossclamp times. Further, they likely were able to enlarge the aortic annulus to a greater degree, given their patients had a larger median prosthesis size (27 vs 25), although this may be confounded by a lack of pre-enlargement annular size measurements and numerically more men in the high-volume surgeon group. Regardless, such a large difference in prosthesis size suggests a true difference in technique and strategy (Table 3).

Despite this experience difference, we found that the short-term outcomes were comparable between the groups. All measured complications including atrial fibrillation, renal failure on dialysis, and return to the operating room for bleeding were not statistically different between the groups. Importantly, the incidence of complete heart block requiring pacemaker implantation was low and similar between both groups, which has often been a concern in AAE due to the proximity of the dissection to the conducting fibers of the heart.14 Operative mortality was 0% among the surgeons who performed <25 AAEs per year, numerically lower than the high volume surgeons (3%), further demonstrating the overall safety of performing AAE. These findings were carried through the follow-up period, as survival remained high and cumulative incidence of reoperation low and equivalent between the groups at 4 years (Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3).

Although there were no clinical differences between the groups in terms of outcomes, there were important hemodynamic differences to highlight. The greater case-volume surgeons placed larger valves on average, which was likely the result of a combination of more liberally performing AAE on patients with larger native aortic annuli, and also a result of more aggressively enlarging the root to accommodate a valve 2 to 4 sizes larger rather than just 1. The high case-volume surgeons more frequently used the Y-incision technique than the low case-volume surgeons (Table 3), which could be either because of the high case-volume surgeons being more comfortable with performing an aggressive annular enlargement or because the surgeons with greater case-volume were more likely to fully buy into the incremental benefits of placing the largest prostheses possible. We therefore argue that the difference in distribution of annular enlargement technique is a natural consequence of the experience differential between the groups. However, we still did include a subanalysis looking at the immediate postoperative outcomes between the groups, including only patients who underwent Y-incision AAE to control for technique, and our results were the same (Table E2). Even when looking only at the cases in which Y-incision annular enlargement was used, the high case-volume surgeons still implanted larger prostheses. Regardless of the reasons, these differences translated into better hemodynamics among the patients of the high case-volume surgeons on follow-up echocardiography, which showed lower mean aortic valve gradients, larger aortic valve areas, and therefore larger iEOAs and less PPM. Severe PPM was almost completely eliminated—only 1 patient of the low case-volume surgeons developed severe PPM. The 6.9% of patients of the high case-volume surgeons and the rest of the 26% of patients of the low case-volume surgeons with documented PPM had only moderate PPM (Table 2).

Limitations

Our study was inherently limited as a single-center, retrospective cohort study. The cutoff of 25 AAEs/year to separate the groups was made arbitrarily on the basis of the distribution of surgeon volumes, although any more granular determinations of volume relationship would require a larger dataset of surgeons. Looking at the data, it was clear that the surgeons with 30 to 40 cases per year could be put in a category separate from most of the remaining surgeons, who performed between 1 and 8 cases per year, but it was difficult to decide where to place the surgeon who performed 16 cases per year (Surgeon 3, Table E1). We ultimately included their cases in the low case-volume group because they still had approximately or less than half the case volume of the high case-volume surgeons. We did perform a comparison between the groups with Surgeon 3's patients excluded from the study, and the immediate postoperative outcomes remained comparable (Table E3). Midterm deaths and reoperations may have been underestimated because of the limitations of the medical record and death index databases for collecting these data. Completion of follow-up for postoperative echocardiograms was also only 78%. In addition, although our study aims to use surgeon case-volume to eliminate the biases that performing these procedures at a high-volume institution could present, it should be noted that because of the high volume of AAE procedures performed at Michigan Medicine, the operating room staff beyond the surgeons, including surgical technologists, circulator nurses, and anesthesiologists, would be more comfortable and competent in facilitating performing the procedure effectively, which could also prevent generalizability of our study to centers which do not perform many AAE procedures.

Conclusions

Surgeons with relatively less experience performing AAE were able to achieve excellent short-term and midterm outcomes after AVR with AAE compared with surgeons who regularly performed AAE. Greater volume surgeons demonstrated increased efficiency with shorter bypass and cross clamp times, along with the ability to implant slightly larger prosthetic valves. Given the high-quality results for both high- and low-volume AAE surgeon groups, experience should not be a limitation in electing to perform AAE for patients who may benefit from having a larger aortic valve prosthesis.
