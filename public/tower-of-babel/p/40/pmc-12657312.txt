Influence of yam-derived flours and fatty acids on the structural characteristics, physicochemical attributes, and in vitro enzymatic digestibility of starch–lipid complexes☆
Authors: Saranchanok Wonglek, Chuchat Jaikaew, Lin Wang, Jinhu Tian, Sunantha Ketnawa, Yukiharu Ogawa, Natthawuddhi Donlao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLauric acid (LA) and myristic acid (MA) were used to prepare yam flour-fatty acid (FA) complexes from purple Dioscorea alata (DAP), white D. alata (DAW), and D. pentaphylla (DP) flours to investigate their structural, physicochemical, functional, and in vitro digestibility properties. FTIR spectra revealed a new peak at 2850 cm−1, indicating stretching vibrations of C—H in FAs. Complexing index (CI) values of the yam flour-MA complexes were higher than those of LA. DAW had the highest CI values for both FAs among the three yam flours. In yam flour-LA complexes, solubility (SOL), water absorption capacity (WAC), and oil absorption capacity (OAC) increased, whereas SOL decreased in yam flour-MA complexes. The complexation of the three flours has resulted in different swelling power (SP) profiles. Complexation of DAP and DP with MA significantly decreased the rapidly digestible starch (RDS) while increasing the slowly digestible starch (SDS) content during in vitro digestion.Keywords: Resistant starch, Slowly digestible starch, Indigenous crops, Food security, Genetic diversity, Rural development, Diabetes

Introduction

Starches are the most widely consumed carbohydrates, serving as a major energy source in the global diet. They are abundant in staple foods such as cereal grains, starchy roots and tubers, legumes, and some immature fruits (Santana & Meireles, 2014). Starch consists of two glucose polymers: amylose, a linear molecule, and amylopectin, a highly branched molecule. The amylose-to-amylopectin ratio varies by botanical source, maturity stage, and growing conditions (Robyt, 2008). Starch is classified based on digestion rate into rapidly digestible starch (RDS), slowly digestible starch (SDS), and resistant starch (RS) (Englyst et al., 1992). RDS is quickly digested, raising blood sugar and glycemic index (GI), while SDS breaks down more slowly, releasing glucose gradually. RS is indigestible and escapes digestion in the small intestine and ferments by gut microbes in the colon, producing beneficial short-chain fatty acids (Bello-Perez et al., 2020). RS is further classified into 5 types: type 1 (physically inaccessible starch), type 2 (resistant starch granules), type 3 (retrograded starch), type 4 (physically or chemically modified starch and starch with non-starch bonds), and type 5 (starch-lipid complex) (Gutiérrez & Tovar, 2021). RS behaves like dietary fiber, and diets rich in RS and dietary fiber have been shown to lower GI and offer various health benefits, including weight management, reduced cholesterol and body fat, improved glucose and insulin regulation, and a lower risk of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) such as cardiovascular disease, obesity, diabetes, and cancer (Cione et al., 2021). Given the increasing interest in RS, the formation of amylose-lipid complexes (ALCs) by the addition of exogenous fatty acids has been extensively studied due to their ability to form RS type-5 during heat processing (Obiro et al., 2012). Several studies have shown that RS type-5 has reduced digestibility, as fatty acids can hinder enzymatic hydrolysis by amylase (Ai et al., 2013; Putseys et al., 2010; Wang & Copeland, 2013). Photinam and Moongngarm (2023) reported that oil addition to corn and rice starches decreased RDS content while increasing SDS and RS fractions. Similarly, Shen et al. (2023) found that rice starch complexed with palmitic acid exhibited lower in vitro digestibility compared to native flour and starch. The complex also formed significantly enhanced the production of short-chain fatty acids, suggesting potential health benefits. Lian et al. (2024) also found that incorporating palm or coconut oil into white rice during cooking slowed glucose release and exhibited potential prebiotic effects. While common sources of starch for ALC formation include wheat, maize, rice, potato, and cassava (Cervantes-Ramírez et al., 2020; Chao, Yu, et al., 2018; Chao, Cai, et al., 2018; Chumsri et al., 2022; Faridah et al., 2021; Kawai et al., 2012; Shen et al., 2023), less commonly studied sources such as lotus, ginkgo (Li et al., 2023), adzuki bean (Wu et al., 2022), banana, and mung bean (Photinam & Moongngarm, 2023) have also demonstrated this capability. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have reported on ALC formation in yam flour, highlighting a significant research gap.

Yams (Dioscorea spp.) belong to the Dioscoreaceae family and are widely distributed across the subtropics and tropics, with over 600 species. In 2021, global yam production reached 75 million tonnes across 8.8 million hectares, with over 90 % produced in West Africa's “yam belt,” including Nigeria, Benin, Ghana, and Côte d'Ivoire (FAOSTAT, 2022). Yams are valued for their ease of cultivation, adaptability, attractive tuber shape, delicate taste, and long postharvest life (Neina, 2021). In Southeast Asia, especially Thailand, yams serve as an alternative carbohydrate source to rice, cassava, maize, and potatoes. However, limited production and low awareness hinder their commercial potential, particularly for yam flour in Thailand.

In addition to their agronomic advantages, yams provide higher essential nutrients than many root crops (Maneenoon et al., 2008; Pusporini & Anggraini, 2016; Shajeela et al., 2011). They grow naturally in forests and are cultivated in some rural areas, particularly among indigenous and tribal populations (Maneenoon et al., 2008; Thapyai, 2004; Thapyai et al., 2017). Fresh tubers are boiled, steamed, or dried into chips, while in West Africa, they are often used for pounded yam (fufu) (Honfozo et al., 2021). Dioscorea spp. is rich in bioactive compounds such as saponins, alkaloids, flavonoids, tannins, phenols, diosgenin, corticosterone, and stigmasterol, contributing to its medicinal and market potential (Mustafa et al., 2018). Their nutritional composition and amylose/amylopectin ratio vary between species, influencing starch digestibility and functional properties (Riley et al., 2004; Riley et al., 2008). Among Dioscorea species, D. alata tubers possess two distinct colors, white and purple. The purple variety, also known as greater yam, derives its colour from anthocyanins, which contribute to significant differences in polyphenol content and antioxidant activities between the purple and white tubers (Wang et al., 2024). These differences extend to their morphology, chemical composition, thermal properties, and techno-functional characteristics (Wonglek et al., 2024). Demand is rising due to their nutritional richness, ease of preparation, and unique taste. In Asian cities, D. alata (greater yam) is increasingly recognized as a health food, with diuretic, immunostimulant, neuroprotective, cardioprotective, anti-hypertensive, hypocholesteric, and anti-diabetic properties (Salehi et al., 2019).

Another underutilized species, D. pentaphylla is native to various Asian regions and the Pacific and is widely used in ethnomedicine (Kumar et al., 2017). As an available wild tuber, it serves as both food and medicine for many rural and tribal communities. Its bioactive compounds contribute to its pharmacological potential, including antibacterial, antioxidant, and anticancer activities (Manjula et al., 2022).

Given the promising potential of yams, it is essential to explore their applications in starch modification. ALCs have been incorporated into food products to enhance functionality. For example, Singh et al. (2014) used ALCs as a substitute for skim milk solids in yogurt, while Thachil et al. (2014) incorporated amylose-rich corn flour in puffed snacks. Mariscal-Moreno et al. (2018) improved tortilla shelf life with ALCs, and Lee et al. (2020) successfully replaced 50 % of shortening in low-fat white bread with a corn starch-stearic acid complex. Recent work by Chao et al. (2024) demonstrated that flours with their natural organization exhibit lower digestibility than depleted or reconstituted flours, underscoring the nutritional advantage of native flours over refined starches. Therefore, in this study we focused on yam flour without removing endogenous proteins and lipids, to preserve its inherent structure, achieve better control of digestibility, and reflect its practical use in food systems.

In this study, medium-chain saturated fatty acids, lauric acid (LA) and myristic acid (MA), were chosen to prepare complexes with three varieties of yam flours: Dioscorea alata-purple (DAP), D. alata-white (DAW), and D. pentaphylla (DP). Their relatively low melting points, high mobility, and saturated structure favor protein–starch–lipid interactions (Chen et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2016; Zheng et al., 2018). Lauric and myristic acids are abundant in common food lipids such as coconut oil, palm kernel oil, and dairy fats, and thus were chosen to investigate how subtle differences in chain length influence complexation. Accordingly, this study aims to investigate how yam flour–FA complexes influence structural, physicochemical, and in vitro digestibility properties, unlocking their potential in the food industry and promoting yam valorization.

Materials and methods

Chemical and flour sample preparation

Dioscorea alata-purple (DAP), D. alata-white (DAW), and D. pentaphylla (DP) from northern Thailand were processed into flour in the food processing laboratory at Mae Fah Luang University in Thailand. The yam flours were prepared according to the methods described by Aprianita et al. (2014) with some modifications. Fresh tubers underwent a series of processing steps: cleaning, peeling, washing, and slicing into 1–3 cm thickness. Subsequently, they were soaked in a 0.5 % w/v sodium metabisulfite solution for 10 min to inhibit discoloration. The sliced samples were tray-dried at 50 °C until the moisture content was below 13 %. The dried chips underwent a milling process utilizing a hammer mill, followed by sifting through a 150 μm mesh. Flour samples were stored in aluminum foil bags prior to subsequent analysis.

Lauric acid (LA), myristic acid (MA), α-amylase from Aspergillus oryzae (Type VI-B, ≥5 units/mg solid), and amyloglucosidase from A. niger (≥260 U/mL) were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Company (St. Louis, MO, USA). All other chemicals used in the study were of analytical grade.

Determination of chemical composition of flour samples

The proximate composition of flour samples, including moisture, protein, fat, ash, and carbohydrate and fiber content, was analyzed according to AOAC methods (2000). Amylose and the total starch content were measured using the amylose/amylopectin test kit (Megazyme, K-AMYL Dublin, Ireland) and total starch assay kit (Megazyme, K-TSTA, Dublin, Ireland).

Preparation of yam flour-FA complexes

The complexes were prepared according to Kang et al. (2022) with some modifications. 10 g of yam flour were mixed with 150 mL of distilled water in a 6-cm breathable glass bottle. The mixture was heated in boiling water with continuous stirring for 1 h. 0.3 g of FAs (LA or MA) were dispersed in 20 g of methanol (≥99.7 %) and mixed with flour paste solution. The paste solution was then continuously heated for another 1 h while stirring. After cooling to RT, the mixture was washed four times with 70 % methanol and centrifuged at 1100×g for 15 min each time. The supernatant was discarded, and the residue was dried in an oven at 40 °C for 12–16 h. The dried samples were then ground into a fine powder using a coffee mill and passed through a 150-mesh sieve. Yam flour paste without FAs was used as a control.

Complexing index (CI) of yam flour-FA complexes

The complex was determined following the procedure of Meng et al. (2014) with modifications. The complex powder (0.1 g) was mixed with 1 mL of distilled water in a 5 mL centrifuge tube for 20 s using a vortex mixer before centrifuging for 15 min at 3000×g. Then, 250 μL of the supernatant was added with 3.75 mL of deionized water and 0.5 mL of an iodine solution (2 % w/w KI and 0.2 % w/w I2 in deionized water) in a 10 mL test tube and mixed well. The visible light absorbance was measured at 620 nm (Jasco V-630 Bio UV–Vis spectrophotometer, Tokyo, Japan). Flour pastes that contained only yam flour were used as a reference. Structural properties

Scanning Electron microscope (SEM)

The granule morphology of all yam flour-FA complexes was studied by SEM (Hitachi S-510, Tokyo, Japan) with a magnification of 1000×.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis

The FTIR spectra of all yam flour-FA complexes were acquired by the FTIR spectrometer (Jasco Corporation FT/IR-4200, Tokyo, Japan). A KBr was mixed with flour samples and then pressed using a manual presser for 1 min. The FTIR spectra were recorded between 4000 cm−1 and 400 cm−1.

In vitro digestibility

The in vitro digestibility of flour-FA complexes was determined according to Kang et al. (2022) with some modifications. 100 mg of flour samples were weighed and suspended with 2.5 mL of distilled water into a 50 mL centrifuge tube. The mixture was cooked in boiling water for 20 min. With constant mixing at 5-min intervals. After cooking, 5 mL of sodium acetate buffer (0.2 M, pH 5.2) and glass beads were mixed into the samples and kept in a water bath at 37 °C for 30 min while shaking gently. A mixture of α-amylase and amyloglucosidase enzymes (120 U/80 U/mL in sodium acetate buffer, pH 5.2) was added to each 2.5 mL sample after incubation period. The tubes were capped and placed into a shaking water bath maintained at 37 °C. 200 μL aliquots were taken from the reaction mixture at 20, 120, and 240 min. The reaction was immediately stopped by mixing it into 3 mL of 70 % (v/v) ethanol. The digesta samples were then centrifuged at 3000×g, 20 °C for 5 min. The glucose concentrations were quantified using the glucose oxidase-peroxidase method and reported as Rapidly digestible starch (RDS) content was the glucose released after digestion within 20 min, slowly digestible starch (SDS) content was the glucose released from 20 to 120 min, and resistant starch (RS) content was the unhydrolyzed starch fraction remaining after 240 min of digestion.

Total phenolic compounds (TPC) and antioxidant properties during in vitro digestion

Total phenolic compounds (TPC)

The total phenolic content of yam flour-FA complexes during digestion was measured using the Folin-Ciocalteu test as explained by Wonglek et al. (2024). Digesta samples (25 μL) were combined with 125 μL of 10 % Folin-Ciocalteu reagent and 100 μL of 7.5 % sodium carbonate (Na2CO3). The mixture was mixed well and allowed to stand at room temperature for 1 h. After incubation, absorbance of the mixture was measured at 765 nm using a microplate reader (ThermoFisher Scientific Multiskan FC, Waltham, United States). Gallic acid was used as a standard, and water was used as a blank. The total phenolic contents were reported as mg GAE (gallic acid equivalent) per g of dry weight of sample.

DPPH radical scavenging activity

The antioxidant activity of flour-FA complexes was determined using the 1,1-diphenyl-1 picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) free-radical scavenging assay described by Wonglek et al. (2024). A 50 μL sample of digesta was mixed with 1.95 mL of 60 μM DPPH solution and was kept in the dark for 30 min at room temperature. After incubation, absorbance of the mixture was measured at 517 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific Multiskan FC, Waltham, United States). Trolox was used as a standard, and methanol was used as a blank. The radical scavenging activity was reported in millimoles of Trolox equivalent (mM TE) per gram of the dry weight of the sample.

Determination of ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP)

The antioxidant activity of yam flour-FA complexes during digestion was measured using the ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) method described by Wonglek et al. (2024). The FRAP reagent was made by mixing 300 mM acetate buffer (pH 3.6) with 10 mM TPTZ in 40 mM HCl and 20 mM FeCl3 in a ratio of 10:1:1. A 0.4 mL of digesta sample was mixed with 2.6 mL of FRAP reagent and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. After incubation, absorbance of the mixture was measured at 595 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific Multiskan FC, Waltham, United States). FeSO4 was used as a standard, and water was used as a blank. The ferric reducing antioxidant power was reported as mM FeSO4 per g of dry weight of sample.

Statistical analysis

All data was analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) at a 95 % significance level. Tukey's test was conducted to separate sample means. The software used was IBM SPSS Statistics 26. Data was presented as means ± standard deviation (SD), and all tests were conducted in triplicate.

The nutritional compositions of DAP, DAW, and DP flours are detailed in Table 1. DAW flour exhibited a higher ability to form complexes with both LA and MA. Moreover, clear distinctions in complex formation were also evident between LA and MA in DAP and DP flours. This superior complexing in DAW is likely due to its significantly lower protein content (4.89 %) and higher fat content (0.42 %) compared to DAP and DP flours, potentially leading to less interference and a facilitated dispersion of added fatty acids by native fats. The presence of protein can diminish amylose-FA complex formation. This occurs through the promotion of a more ordered amylose-FA complex structure, which restricts additional complex formation, particularly when the FA concentration is below amylose saturation (Zhang & Hamaker, 2004). Additionally, protein can form ternary amylose-lipid-protein complexes, where FAs serve as bridges between incompatible amylose and protein molecules (Chao, Yu, et al., 2018; Chao, Cai, et al., 2018; Zheng et al., 2018). In these ternary complexes, the influence of FA complexes on structural order is less significant compared to binary complexes (Wang et al., 2020).

The use of LA and MA enabled of how subtle differences in the length of medium-chain fatty acid influence complex formation. Our findings indicate that the CI value increased with increasing carbon chain length of the FAs. This aligns with Lu et al. (2021), who reported a strong linear correlation (R2 = 0.94) between carbon number and CI value when testing FA ranging from C6 to C18 complexed with debranched starch. This suggests that longer-chain FAs, such as MA, can form complexes more easily than shorter ones like LA in yam flour samples. A similar pattern was reported by Sun et al. (2021) for maize starch, although they found no significant difference between LA and MA.

Previous studies have shown contradictory results regarding the optimal fatty acid chain length. Some suggests that shorter chains disperse better (Kawai et al., 2012), while others note that very long chains can hinder complex formation (Wang et al., 2016). However, our findings support the idea that longer alkyl chains can facilitate stronger hydrophobic interactions and higher complex crystallinity (Siswoyo & Morita, 2002).

In contrast, several studies have reported that shorter-chain FAs form complexes better, with the assumption that their higher dispersibility in gelatinized starch facilitates interaction with amylose and results in more complex formation (Lu et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2016; Zheng et al., 2018). The difference is often explained by the greater steric hindrance of longer-chain FAs, which lowers their critical micelle concentration and water solubility (Lu et al., 2021).

Despite these conflicting findings, our results consistently showed that MA formed more effective complexes with yam flour than LA. This supports the idea that stronger hydrophobic interactions contribute to higher complexation in our system. Discrepancies among studies may arise from preparation methods (e.g., agitation, heating conditions, and fatty acid concentration) and amylose properties (e.g., quantity, chain length, and pre-formed amylose). Amylose fractions with longer chains and higher polymerization degrees tend to form more resistant complexes. Lipid characteristics such as chain length, saturation, solubility, and concentration also influence complex formation, structure, and properties (Wang et al., 2020). However, the overall resistance and thermostability of complexes primarily depend on amylose chain length, with a lesser influence from the FAs chain length (Obiro et al., 2012). At lower lipid concentrations, amylose may form double helices, which may compete with the single helices required for inclusion complexes (Putseys et al., 2010).

Native flours (N) showed an intact, smooth surfaces with oval-oblong-shaped granules. Some residues, possibly protein, fiber, and fat, remained after the flour preparation process. In contrast, the control flour (C) showed rougher surfaces evidently caused by granule disruption during gelatinization (high temperature) and mechanical stirring (shear stress) in the complexation process.

The yam flour-FA complexes exhibited protruding, irregular, elastic material-presumably FA deposits-coating the granule surfaces (red circle). This observation aligns with Sun et al. Effect of FAs on physicochemical properties

Water absorption capacity (WAC) and oil absorption capacity (OAC)

Fig. 4 shows the WAC of native yam flour and FA-treated flours. The ability of flour to absorb and retain water or oil is one determinant factor of the final texture of foods, comminuted meat, meat analogs, and baked goods. The WAC was higher than the OAC in all samples. The higher WAC in the C compared to the N can be attributed to the pre-gelatinization process, which disrupts starch crystallinity and enhances water binding. The hydrophilic groups in gelatinized starch enhances its capability to bind with water, while the porosity of flour facilitates water absorption (Ma et al., 2011). A substantial increase in WAC- by 50.46 % and 94.19 % compared with N flour-was observed in DAP-N and DAW-N, particularly in the LA complexes (p < 0.05). Among all samples, DAP-LA showed the highest WAC (4.48 g/g), while DP-C showed the lowest (1.70 g/g). Significant differences in WAC were found for DAP after complexation with both LA and MA. The WAC of DAW and DP showed similar trends after FA addition, although the addition of MA did not cause a significant change (p < 0.05) in the WAC of DAW and DP compared with the C.

The changes in WAC of the complexes were negatively related to the CI values. As can be observed in DAW-MA and DP-MA, the WAC did not significantly differ from that of the C. The WAC of DAP-LA, DAW-LA, and DP-LA increased by 33.2 %, 18.8 %, and 33.5 %, respectively, when compared to C. This increase may be due to the higher water solubility of LA compared with MA. The uncomplexed LA residues left in the yam flour could be trapped in the interstitial spaces between the amylose helices, thereby enhancing the WAC of the yam flour-FA complexes through their amphiphilic nature. When lipid molecules do not form complexes with amylose, they can instead interact with water or self-associate into micellar structures, which contribute to higher WAC (Tang & Copeland, 2007).

These results differ from some previous studies that reported a decrease in the WAC of ALCs due to hindrance mechanisms. The formation of ALCs reduces the ability of starch to absorb and retain water because FA layer physically blocks water penetration (Dorantes-Campuzano et al., 2022). The exogenous FAs added form an insoluble layer around the granules, preventing the water entry and leaching out of soluble carbohydrates (Sun et al., 2025). However, numerous studies have reported an increase in the WAC of ALCs. Ocloo et al. (2014) reported an increased WAC and OAC in high amylose maize starch complexed with stearic acid, attributed to the amphiphilic nature of the stearic acid. Similarly, Chumsri et al. (2022) found that the WAC, OAC, and SP of rice starch-lipid complex increased due to greater exposure of hydroxyl groups in amylose and amylopectin after gelatinization and ultrasonic treatment. The increase in WAC can also be influenced by hydrophilic constituents with free groups of carbohydrates and proteins. Protein denaturation leads to the uncovering and loosening of hydrophilic groups in yam flours, producing amino acid residues capable of binding with water (Marta & Tensiska, 2017).

The OAC of native yam flour and FA-treated flours is shown in Fig. 4. OAC reflects the interaction between nonpolar amino acids and lipid hydrocarbon chains (Sindhu & Khatkar, 2016). OAC influences mouthfeel, flavor retention, palatability, and shelf life- particularly in bakery and meat products. An increase in OAC was observed after pre-gelatinization of flours. Among alll samples, DP-N showed the highest OAC (2.11 g/g), while DAP-C had the lowest (0.86 g/g). The higher OAC of DP is credited to its higher protein content (Table 1), as oil may remain entrapped within the nonpolar side chains of proteins (Bala et al., 2020). The addition of MA to DAP and DAW flours did not cause significant changes in OAC (p < 0.05) compared with the C. The effects of LA addition on DAP were opposite to that on DAW and DP. The DAP-LA complex showed a significantly higher OAC than other yam flour-FA complexes. Meanwhile, DP showed the highest OAC in C, which significantly decreased (p < 0.05) after complexation with both LA and MA. This reduction may be related to the high protein content of DP flour. During gelatinization, protein denaturation may expos amino acids that initially increase oil binding in DP-C; however, these amino acids can subsequently participate in complex formatio, reducing their availability to bind oil. Chumsri et al. (2022) elucidated that intact amylose–amylose or amylose–amylopectin interactions formed during high-temperature gelatinization, or the limited oil integration into the porous starch network due to the formation of a starch–lipid complex, may explain the OAC reduction.

The addition of LA and MA significantly increased the SP of DP flour, consistent with the CI values. The decrease in SP observed for DAP-LA, DAP-MA, DAW-LA, and DAW-MA with increasing temperature suggests that the complexes formed inhibited starch swelling. This inhibition likely results from the physical barrier and hindrance mechanism from FA, as explained in the effect of FA addition on WAC of the yam flour-FA complex. The results agree with previous reporting a significant decrease (p < 0.05) in SP after lipid addition (Chang et al., 2013; Photinam & Moongngarm, 2023; Wang et al., 2016).

In contrast, the SP of DP flour complexes with FA increased, suggesting that the complexes formed may be less crystalline than those in other flour samples. SP and WAC are correlated because swelling depends on the ability of starch to form hydrogen bonds with water molecules. During gelatinization, hydrogen bonds between starch molecules are disrupted and replaced by hydrogen bonding with water. Below 70 °C, swelling increases as intermolecular hydrogen bonds within the amorphous regions loosen (Marta & Tensiska, 2017). ALCs form helical structures that prevent water penetration, therefore both the amount of ALCs and the degree of starch crystallinity influence the SP of the complexes (Wang et al., 2016).

The SOL of all samples increased with the rising temperature. SOL is influenced by the physical interactions between water molecules and the semi-crystalline parts of starch chains. The glycosidic bonds loosen as temperature rises, leading to the absorption of water by starch granules and resulting in irreversible change of starch, crystallinity loss, granule swelling, leaching out, and solubilization of amylose (Gerits et al., 2015). The complexation of three yam flours-DAP, DAW, and DP-with MA significantly decreased SOL and was lowest among all samples. The addition of LA to DAP and DP increased SOL compared w C because less complexation and the presence of LA increase the solubility of the samples. However, the SOL of DAW-LAshowed a significant decrease at p < 0.05 compared with DAP-LA and DP-LA, resulting from greater complex formation. The results demonstrated that higher CI values correspond to lower SOL of complexes. The coating formed by FAs delays water penetration and inhibits amylose leaching, thereby reducing swelling and further water solubility of starch (Cervantes-Ramírez et al., 2020).

Pre-gelatinization significantly (p < 0.05) increased the RDS and decreased the SDS and RS contents of all yam flour-FA complexes, except for DAP-MA. The higher RS content observed in native flours is because of more stable and crystalline structure of amylose compared with pre-gelatinized flour and ALCs (Shamai et al., 2003). Compared to the control flour, all yam flour-FA complexes showed RDS reductions, with the greatest decrease observed in DAP-MA (approximately 16 %), followed by DAW-MA (10 %), DP-MA (8 %), DAP-LA and DAW-LA (both approximately 7 %), and DP-LA (3 %). These reductions indicate that complexation of yam flour with fatty acids successfully decreased starch digestibility. Our results align with and fall within the range of previously documented studies on starch–lipid complexes. Studies on isolated starch have shown a wide range of RDS reductions, often with more dramatic results than those we observed in whole flour. For example, Królikowska et al. (2022) reported reductions of 3–8 % in cassava starch and 5–7 % in wheat starch when treated with palmitic, stearic, and oleic acid. Similarlt, Photinam and Moongngarm (2023) reported reductions of 15–16 % in corn starch, 10–17 % in rice starch, 12–45 % in banana starch, and 23–99 % in mungbean starch using various cooking oils. Because this study used whole yam flour rather than isolated starch, the samples retained endogenous proteins that physically hinder amylase accessibility to starch granules and promoted the formation of ternary starch–lipid–protein complexes during heating. Meanwhile lipids naturally form amylose–lipid complexes that resist enzymatic breakdown (Chao et al., 2024).

The results showed that the addition of both LA and MA to yam flours significantly increased the SDS and RS content, with the greatest enhancement in the yam flour-MA complexes. The addition of MA increased the RS content by 8.4 % in DAP and by approximately 40 % in DAW and DP flours compared to the C. These findings are consistent with previous studies reported (Ai et al., 2013; Chumsri et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2019; Kawai et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2016). The addition of FA slows the digestion rate of flours by promoting the formation of V-type amylose-lipid complexes, commonly rederred to as resistant starch type 5 (RS), contributing to increase in SDS and RS contents (Ai et al., 2013; Raigond et al., 2015). The inclusion complex formation between yam flour and FAs also restricts starch granule swelling, which reduces enzymatic accessibility and hydrolysis (Kang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2016). Kawai et al. (2012) explained that the amorphous structure of starch are less stable than crystalline amylose and complexes. This makes crystalline amylose and complexes more resistant to hydrolysis, confirmed by the higher thermal stability (Tm) of. In this study, the yam flour-LA complexes exhibited a lower CI value than the corresponding MA complexes; however, they still demonstrated a notable increase in SDS and RS content. This suggests that even less-ordered complex formation can effectively hinder enzymatic hydrolysis and enhance the resistant starch fraction.

Effects of FAs on total phenolic content (TPC) and antioxidant activities

The total phenolic content (TPC), DPPH scavenging ability (DPPH), and ferric reducing power (FRAP) of yam flour-FA complexes in each starch fraction are shown in Fig. 5. The DPPH and FRAP assays are widely used to analyze the free radical scavenging ability and overall antioxidant potential of bioactive compounds. Following pre-gelatinization, the TPC of DAP, DAW, and DP flours decreased significantly (p < 0.05) from 1.32, 0.84, and 0.37 GAE mg/g DW to 0.34, 0.43, and 0.21 GAE mg/g DW, respectively, in the RDS fraction. The thermal degradation of phenolic compounds due to heating was more pronounced in DAP flour than in DAW and DP flours, likely because of its initially higher phenolic content.

Numerous phytochemicals and bioactive compounds have been reported in both D. alata and D. pentaphylla, for example, phenolics, phenols, flavonoids, tannins, and oxalates (Obidiegwu et al., 2020). Among these, DAP possesses anthocyanins such as cyanidin and peonidin, which are responsible for purple flesh colour and contribute to its higher TPC and antioxidant activity compared to DAW and DP flours (Adomėnienė & Venskutonis, 2022). The intensity of the purple colour was directly proportional to phenolic content in purple yams (Cornago et al., 2011). However, the anthocyanins present in DAP flours are sensitive and degraded by heat during pre-gelatinization. As anthocyanins contributed to radical scavenging abilities, both DPPH scavenging ability and ferric reducing power decreased accordingly. A significant correlation was observed between TPC and DPPH radical scavenging activity (R2 = 0.837, p < 0.01) and between TPC and reducing power (R2 = 0.931, p < 0.01) in the RDS fractions, agreeing with findings reported by Cornago et al. (2011).

D. alata tubers are known to be rich in polyphenols, with allantoin, phenols, and flavonoids identified as major contributors to their strong antioxidant activity (Wang et al., 2022). D. pentaphylla tubers contain diosgenin-a phytosteroid sapogenin-responsible for antimicrobial and antioxidant activities (Kumar et al., 2018)

The TPC content of all yam flour-FA complexes was significantly higher (p < 0.05) than that of C. During the complexation process, FAs possibly effectively entrap these phenolic compounds, protecting them from thermal and mechanical degradation. Wang et al. (2025) reported that polyphenols (quercetin, curcumin, and resveratrol)-although not naturally forming complexes with amylose,-can be entrapped within amylose in the presence of LA. The ternary complexes formed presented better antioxidant activity, lower digestibility, and controlled release of content. Aside from that, bioactive compounds can form starch-phenol inclusion complexes with amylose via hydrophobic interactions or hydrogen bonding, which may inhibit enzymatic hydrolysis of starch and attenuate starch breakdown (Riess et al., 2022). Previous studies on quercetin–Tartary buckwheat starch complexation demonstrated similar effects, including increased crystallinity, enhanced thermal stability, and reduced digestibility (Li et al., 2020). These findings are consistent with our observation that amylose–lipid complexes and possible starch–phenol interactions can jointly contribute to slower starch hydrolysis and higher SDS and RS content in yam flour systems. This allows the application of amylose-lipid complexes as delivery vehicles for bioactive phenolics with sustained starch breakdown and potentially lowered glycemic index. Overall, there are no significant differences (p > 0.05) were observed between yam flour complexes formed with LA and MA.

The increased DPPH radical scavenging activity and ferric reducing power observed in our sample align with the findings of Chumsri et al. (2022). They reported that rice starch complexed with various FA chain lengths (C4, C12, C18, and C18:2) led to increased DPPH radical scavenging activity, with the effect varying based on the specific fatty acid. This enhancement was attributed to the single-helical structure of amylose-fatty acid complexes, which exposes more hydroxyl groups and contributes to increased antioxidant activity. Our results showed both TPC and antioxidant activities of the flour samples gradually increased with digestion time. This is due to the hydrolysis of bound phenolic compounds and their subsequent release from the flour matrix under acidic conditions and digestive enzymes during digestion (Gullon et al., 2015).

The higher DPPH radical scavenging activity compared to ferric reducing power observed in all samples was explained by the number of thiol-containing peptides from yam's major protein, dioscorin, which exhibits antioxidant properties (Tsai et al., 2013). However, the FRAP assay does not enable determining thiol antioxidants, according to the acidic conditions used in this method prevent thiols from reacting (Ivanova et al., 2015).

Conclusions

This study showed that three yam flour varieties, DAP, DAW, and DP, have the ability to form complexes with FA. MA showed superior ability to form complexes with all yam flours compared to LA. DAW effectively formed complexes with both MA and LA. The formation of yam flour-FA complexes altered granule morphology, physicochemical properties, and digestibility. SEM micrographs and FTIR spectra confirmed the formation of V-type amylose complexes. The addition of LA tended to increase WAC, SP, and SOL of the complexes, while MA showed the contrasting effects. The incorporation of FA reduced RDS content while increasing SDS and RS contents. The addition of MA increased RS content by approximately 8 % in DAP and 40 % in DAW and DP. The yam flour-FA complexes also showed better TPC, DPPH radical scavenging activities, and ferric reducing power during digestion compared to control samples.

Overall, the incorporation of ALCs into carbohydrate staple foods increases RS content, offering potential benefits for individuals with metabolic syndrome. These findings help to understand the application of yam flour in carbohydrate-containing processed foods like noodles, tortillas, bread, and snacks with lipids in the formulation. The ALCs can be applied as carbohydrate-based fat replacers in food products. Yam flour could be a promising raw material for lipid complexation with further application in the functional food and pharmaceutical industries.
