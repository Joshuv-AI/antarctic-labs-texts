Pilot validation of a whole-blood mRNA expression-based diagnostic system for early-stage pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma: a single-center case–control diagnostic accuracy study
Authors: Masaki Miyazawa, Hajime Takatori, Masaki Nishitani, Noboru Takata, Akihiro Seki, Kouki Nio, Noriho Iida, Tetsuro Shimakami, Taro Yamashita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) has a poor prognosis because most cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage. We previously developed a whole-blood messenger RNA (mRNA) expression–based diagnostic system using 56 genes, but its performance in very early-stage PDAC remains unclear. This single-center prospective pilot study evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of the mRNA-based system in patients with stage 0–I PDAC. Among 253 patients diagnosed between 2020 and 2024, ten with stage 0 or I disease were included. One hundred and four healthy controls were analyzed for comparison. Whole blood samples were analyzed by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction, and a pre-specified locked algorithm generated an mRNA diagnostic score. Serum carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9) was evaluated using a cut-off of 37 U/mL. The mRNA-based system detected 9 of 10 early-stage PDAC cases (sensitivity 90.0%; 95% CI 55.5–99.8%), whereas CA19-9 detected 1 of 10 (10.0%; 95% CI 2.5–44.5%). Specificity was 70.2% for the mRNA-based system and 97.1% for CA19-9. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.84 and 0.66, respectively. These exploratory findings suggest that whole-blood transcriptomic signatures may improve diagnostic classification of highly selected patients with stage 0–I PDAC. Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the most lethal malignancies, with a 5-year relative survival rate of only 13% for all stages combined1. Early detection is crucial to improving patient outcomes, but most PDAC cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage2. According to the Japan Pancreas Society registry, the 5-year survival rate exceeds 80% for patients with carcinoma in situ or tumors smaller than 10 mm. However, stage 0 and stage I PDAC cases account for only 0.7% and 2.3% of all PDAC, respectively3. Thus, improving the detection rate of very early PDAC is a key requirement for meaningful survival gains.

PDAC is thought to develop through a multistep progression from precursor lesions, particularly pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PanIN), to invasive carcinoma4,5. This stepwise model provides a rationale for detecting PDAC at a preinvasive or very early invasive stage, when curative treatment is more likely to be feasible. A simulation model estimated that progression from PanIN 1 to PDAC takes approximately 33.6–35.3 years, whereas progression from PanIN 3 to PDAC takes approximately 11.3–12.3 years, suggesting that there may be a substantial time window for early detection5. However, in clinical practice, diagnosing PDAC within this window remains challenging.

In current clinical practice, carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9) is the most widely used tumor marker for PDAC. However, its sensitivity for stage 0–I disease is insufficient, and its specificity is compromised by elevation in other gastrointestinal malignancies and benign conditions6,7. The development of simple, minimally invasive, and accurate biomarkers could increase resection rates and reduce mortality. Previously, our multicenter study developed a novel diagnostic system for PDAC based on an algorithm corresponding to the messenger RNA (mRNA) expression levels of 56 genes in whole blood measured by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)8. The clinical utility of this system was confirmed in test and validation cohorts including patients with various stages of PDAC, with a sensitivity of 73.6% and a specificity of 64.7% for healthy controls (HCs). However, the cohort included few patients with early-stage PDAC, leaving the value of this system for early detection unverified. To address this evidence gap, we designed a pilot diagnostic accuracy study to evaluate the clinical performance of this mRNA-based system in patients with stage 0 and I PDAC. This study aimed to provide preliminary, hypothesis-generating data on the diagnostic performance of this assay in a highly selected stage 0–I PDAC cohort.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This was a single-center, prospective observational pilot study conducted at Kanazawa University Hospital. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review board of Kanazawa University Hospital (approval No. 2016 − 202). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, including both PDAC patients and HCs, prior to enrollment.

The study was designed as an exploratory diagnostic accuracy investigation and reported with reference to the STARD 2015 guidelines9.

Patient selection and setting

Between February 2020 and March 2024, 253 patients diagnosed with PDAC at Kanazawa University Hospital were assessed for eligibility. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria:

Ten patients fulfilled these criteria and were enrolled as the early-stage PDAC cohort. Patients with other concomitant malignancies, active inflammatory diseases, or severe systemic illness were excluded (Fig. 1).

The control group comprised 104 HCs without known active malignancy or pancreatic pathology, recruited from individuals undergoing routine health examinations. Controls with abnormal imaging or laboratory findings suggestive of pancreatic disease were excluded.

Imaging evaluation and tumor staging

All PDAC patients underwent contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT), contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS) as part of routine clinical assessment. Tumor diameter was measured by EUS. Tumor stage was assigned according to the UICC TNM classification, 8th edition. When a pancreatic mass was identifiable on EUS, EUS-guided fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) was attempted. In cases in which no mass was detected on EUS or a definitive pathological diagnosis could not be obtained by EUS-FNA, serial pancreatic juice aspiration cytologic examination (SPACE) was performed with endoscopic nasopancreatic drainage (ENPD). Ultimately, all PDAC patients underwent surgical resection, and the final diagnosis was confirmed by histopathological examination of the resected specimens.

Blood sampling and CA19-9 measurement

Peripheral blood samples for mRNA analysis were collected at the time of PDAC diagnosis, prior to any systemic therapy. Whole blood (2.5 mL) was drawn into PAXgene Blood RNA tubes (PreAnalytiX, Hombrechtikon, Switzerland) and stored according to the manufacturer’s instructions until RNA extraction. For CA19-9, serum samples were collected at the same time point and analyzed using the Atellica IM CA19-9 assay (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). A cut-off value of 37 U/mL was used to define CA19-9 positivity. In PDAC patients with obstructive jaundice, biliary drainage was performed prior to blood sampling, and CA19-9 was measured after the total bilirubin level had decreased to < 3.0 mg/dL.

mRNA-based diagnostic system

The mRNA-based diagnostic system used in this study was developed through previous transcriptomic and biostatistical analyses8,10–13. Briefly, gene expression profiling of whole-blood cells from patients with digestive system cancers and non-cancer controls identified a set of genes whose expression levels were consistently altered in PDAC. A panel of 56 immune-related genes was ultimately selected based on discriminative performance in prior work8,11.

Total RNA was extracted from PAXgene samples, cDNA was synthesized using Panrexa® (KUBIX, Ishikawa, Japan). Gene expression of 56 immune-related candidate genes was quantified by RT-PCR using Rotor-Gene Q MDx 5plex HRM (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). A Sum-value > 0 was interpreted as positive for PDAC; a value ≤ 0 was considered negative. This threshold was defined in a prior multicenter study based on receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis comparing PDAC patients and HCs8. The present pilot study used this locked algorithm and cut-off without modification to avoid overfitting.

Statistical analysis and sample size considerations

Hierarchical clustering analysis was performed on the expression profiles of 56 genes from 104 HCs and 10 PDAC patients using GENESIS software version 1.8.1 (Institute for Genomics and Bioinformatics, Graz University of Technology, Graz, Austria). Complete linkage clustering with the uncentered Pearson correlation distance metric was applied to visualize gene expression patterns. Each cell in the resulting heatmap represents the expression level of a given gene in an individual sample. Color coding indicates relative expression levels, with red and green denoting high and low expression, respectively, as shown on the scale bar representing fold changes in gene expression.

Group comparisons between PDAC and HCs were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. For the evaluation of diagnostic performance, sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve (AUC) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for both the mRNA-based system and CA19-9. Correlation between mRNA-based scores and CA19-9 levels was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Given the rarity of stage 0–I PDAC even in high-volume centers3,6, no formal a priori sample size calculation was undertaken. Instead, this study was explicitly designed as an exploratory pilot investigation, aiming to characterize the diagnostic signal and estimate performance parameters with acknowledgement of the resulting wide CIs and limited precision. The small number of stage 0–I PDAC patients also implies that estimates of sensitivity, specificity, and AUC may be unstable and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive, in line with methodological guidance on pilot diagnostic accuracy studies9.

Among the 10 patients with PDAC, four had a history of other malignancies, including ovarian cancer, endometrial cancer, prostate cancer, and hepatocellular carcinoma. All four patients were in the postoperative follow-up period, had no evidence of recurrence of the previous malignancy, and had not received postoperative chemotherapy. Pancreatic abnormalities were incidentally detected during follow-up CT after surgery for the previous malignancy in all four patients. Therefore, none of these patients had active concomitant malignancy at the time of blood sampling.

Among the 10 patients with PDAC, two had stage 0 carcinoma in situ without a visible mass on imaging, while eight had stage I disease with measurable pancreatic tumors. In the eight patients with identifiable tumors on EUS, tumor diameters ranged from 5 to 19 mm. EUS-FNA yielded a pathological diagnosis in six of these eight patients, whereas in the remaining two patients, EUS-FNA was non-diagnostic and malignancy was confirmed by positive cytology obtained through SPACE. In the two patients with stage 0 disease and no detectable mass on EUS, focal main pancreatic duct stenosis with upstream dilatation and/or focal pancreatic atrophy prompted further evaluation by SPACE. SPACE was performed in both cases; cytology was positive in one patient, while the other showed negative cytology. Despite negative cytological findings, surgical resection was undertaken in this patient based on comprehensive clinical assessment and patient preference. Ultimately, all patients underwent curative-intent surgical resection, and the diagnosis of PDAC was confirmed histopathologically using the resected specimens.

Diagnostic performance of the mRNA-based system and CA19-9

Using the predefined positivity threshold (Sum-value > 0), the mRNA-based system correctly classified 9 of 10 early-stage PDAC patients as positive, yielding a sensitivity of 90.0% (95% CI 55.5–99.8%). In contrast, only 1 of 10 patients had CA19-9 ≥ 37 U/mL (sensitivity, 10.0%; 95% CI 2.5–44.5%). Thus, the mRNA-based system demonstrated substantially higher sensitivity than CA19-9 for stage 0–I PDAC (Fig. 3A). Specificity for distinguishing PDAC from HCs was 70.2% (95% CI 60.4–78.8%) for the mRNA-based system and 97.1% (95% CI 91.8–99.4%) for CA19-9. ROC analysis showed an AUC of 0.84 (95% CI 0.74–0.94) for the mRNA-based system, compared with 0.66 (95% CI 0.46–0.86) for CA19-9 (Fig. 3B), indicating superior overall discrimination by the mRNA-based assay.

For CA19-9, the conventional clinical cut-off of > 37 U/mL was initially applied. Additional ROC-based analysis identified an alternative cut-off value of 12.4 U/mL. This lower threshold improved sensitivity from 10.0% to 60.0%, but reduced specificity from 97.1% to 77.9%. Despite the gain in sensitivity, the overall diagnostic performance of CA19-9 remained inferior to that of the mRNA-based system. In contrast, the cut-off for the mRNA-based system was predefined based on prior ROC analyses and was not re-optimized.

We further evaluated diagnostic performance using a predefined combined mRNA/CA19-9 score. This combined model showed a sensitivity of 60.0%, specificity of 93.3%, and AUC of 0.815, indicating improved specificity compared with the mRNA-based system alone but reduced sensitivity.

Next, we compared the performance of each model under matched specificity and sensitivity conditions. At a matched specificity of approximately 70.2%, the sensitivity was 90.0% for the mRNA-based system, 80.0% for the combined score, and 60.0% for CA19-9. Conversely, at a matched sensitivity of approximately 90.0%, the specificity was 70.2% for the mRNA-based system, 64.4% for the combined score, and 28.8% for CA19-9.

No significant correlation was observed between mRNA-based scores and CA19-9 levels (Spearman’s r = − 0.029, P = 0.76), suggesting that these biomarkers capture different biological dimensions of PDAC. Exploratory analyses of associations between clinical background factors and mRNA-based system positivity are shown in Supplementary Table S2. Except for age (P = 0.04), no statistically significant associations were observed. Because the PDAC group had a significantly higher prevalence of family history of PDAC than HCs, we additionally examined the relationship between family history and mRNA-based system positivity. Among all 114 participants, five had a family history of PDAC; two were positive by the mRNA-based system, and both were PDAC patients. Among the 104 HCs, three had a family history of PDAC, and all three were negative by the mRNA-based system. Although this analysis was limited by the small number of participants with a family history, these findings did not suggest that mRNA-based system positivity was driven solely by family history of PDAC.

To provide additional context regarding the representativeness of the early-stage PDAC cohort, we analyzed a subset of 46 patients with stage II–IV PDAC for whom mRNA data were available (stage II, n = 10; stage III, n = 11; stage IV, n = 25). Stage-stratified sensitivities, including those of the stage 0–I cohort, are summarized in Supplementary Figure S3. CA19-9 positivity was 40.0% (4/10) in stage II, 90.9% (10/11) in stage III, and 84.0% (21/25) in stage IV. The corresponding positivity rates for the mRNA-based system were 50.0% (5/10), 63.6% (7/11), and 72.0% (18/25), respectively. The combined mRNA/CA19-9 score showed positivity rates of 70.0% (7/10), 90.9% (10/11), and 88.0% (22/25), respectively. Overall, the positivity rates in the stage II–IV subset were 76.1% (35/46) for CA19-9, 65.2% (30/46) for the mRNA-based system, and 84.8% (39/46) for the combined score.

Discussion

The prognosis of PDAC remains poor, largely due to difficulty in early diagnosis14. Risk factors include family history, diabetes, chronic pancreatitis, intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (IPMN), smoking, and alcohol, but most PDAC arises without clear risk factors15. PDAC often does not exhibit specific symptoms in the early stages, and by the time symptoms appear, many patients are already inoperable. Various imaging modalities, including CT, MRI, and EUS, are used to detect early-stage PDAC, which is still operable. However, stage 0 PDAC, in particular, often lacks tumor mass formation. In such cases, only indirect findings, such as changes in the main pancreatic duct or focal pancreatic atrophy, provide important diagnostic clues16. In Japan, EUS-FNA has excellent diagnostic accuracy for the pathological diagnosis of pancreatic solid lesions, but its performance decreases to 83.2% for tumors < 20 mm in diameter17. When no mass is detected, SPACE is often performed with ENPD in cases where PDAC is suspected, with a reported diagnostic accuracy of approximately 74.3%18. Consequently, some early-stage PDACs remain pathologically unconfirmed preoperatively, creating an unmet need for highly sensitive complementary biomarkers.

Traditional serum tumor markers are simple and minimally invasive. CA19-9 is widely used as a traditional tumor marker for PDAC, but as was the case in our study, its sensitivity is insufficient for early-stage disease. In previous studies of early-stage PDAC, serum CA19-9 elevation has been reported in only approximately 27–50% of patients6,7, although the exact assay thresholds may vary across studies. Furthermore, individuals who genetically express non-sialylated Lewis blood group antigens do not express CA19-9 at all19. Other serum markers such as CEA, DUPAN-2, and Span-1 show even lower diagnostic rates6,20.

Recently, several blood-based and urine-based biomarkers for PDAC have been investigated. However, the definition of “early-stage PDAC” varies among studies, and many reports have evaluated stage I–II or resectable PDAC rather than stage 0 disease. Apolipoprotein A2 isoforms have shown sensitivities of 47.4% for stage I PDAC and 16.7% for stage 0 PDAC20,21, and a next-generation sequencing-based microRNA (miRNA) model combined with CA19-9 demonstrated a sensitivity of approximately 60% for stage 0 or I PDAC22. A urinary exosomal miRNA assay also demonstrated a sensitivity of around 90% for stage I or IIA PDAC23. Other approaches, including thrombospondin-2 (THBS2), PAM4-based immunoassay, and rapid magnetic nanosensor assay have shown encouraging diagnostic performance for stage I–II, or resectable PDAC24–26. In contrast, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA)-based approaches may have inherent limitations for detecting very early-stage PDAC because ctDNA shedding is strongly influenced by tumor burden. For example, Jiang et al. reported that ctDNA was detected in only 53.8% of patients with stage I PDAC, whereas the detection rate increased with disease stage27. Thus, although several circulating biomarkers have shown potential for stage I or resectable PDAC, robust evidence for detecting stage 0 disease remains limited.

In this single-center, prospective pilot diagnostic accuracy study, we evaluated a whole-blood mRNA-based diagnostic system in a cohort of patients with stage 0 or I PDAC. The key finding is that the mRNA-based system detected 9 of 10 early-stage PDAC patients, corresponding to a sensitivity of 90.0%, whereas CA19-9 identified only one patient (10.0%). The AUC of the mRNA-based system (0.84) also exceeded that of CA19-9 (0.66), highlighting its superior overall diagnostic discrimination in this setting. On the other hand, CA19-9 showed superior specificity compared to the mRNA-based system (97.1% vs. 70.2%). These findings indicate that the mRNA-based system and CA19-9 provide complementary diagnostic information. The mRNA-based system consistently demonstrated superior sensitivity across matched conditions, whereas CA19-9 provided high specificity. The combined mRNA/CA19-9 score improved specificity at the expense of sensitivity, suggesting that different clinical contexts may require different diagnostic strategies.

Compared with the previous study by Sakai et al.8, which reported an overall sensitivity of 73.6% and specificity of 64.7%, with a sensitivity of 78.6% in the stage I–II subset, the present study showed a sensitivity of 90.0% and specificity of 70.2% in patients with stage 0 or I PDAC. Although this comparison should be interpreted with caution because of differences in study design, patient population, and the small number of early-stage cases in the present cohort, these findings may suggest that the mRNA-based system retains, or may even show higher, sensitivity in very early-stage PDAC. Further validation in larger, independent cohorts is warranted to determine its diagnostic performance across different disease stages.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess this mRNA-based system specifically in carcinoma in situ, providing rare data on biomarker performance at the earliest stages of PDAC. While direct cross-study comparisons should be made cautiously, our findings suggest that peripheral blood gene expression profiling may complement conventional tumor markers, particularly in patients with minimal tumor burden. This mRNA-based system is blood-based, minimally invasive, and can be performed using standard RT-PCR platforms.

Our previous study has demonstrated a correlation between peripheral blood gene expression changes and systemic inflammatory responses in PDAC11. The 56 genes comprising this mRNA-based system are largely immune-related, consistent with the notion that PDAC is involved in both local and systemic immune responses8,11. The results of this study support the possibility that even small PDAC lesions may induce local inflammation and immune alterations, which is reflected in peripheral blood transcriptome signatures. Our previous studies have suggested that monocytes/macrophages and CD4 + T cells play an important role in shaping the tumor microenvironment in early-stage PDAC, and capturing these immune responses in peripheral blood provides a diagnostic mechanism using this mRNA system11. This approach differs from conventional protein biomarkers, such as CA19-9, which are highly dependent on tumor burden and secreted protein levels28.

In the additional stage II–IV subset, CA19-9 and the combined score showed higher overall positivity rates than the mRNA-based system, particularly in stage III–IV disease. In contrast, the mRNA-based system showed relatively consistent positivity across stage 0–I and stage II–IV disease, supporting the possibility that this assay may capture host-response signatures that are not solely dependent on tumor burden. However, because mRNA data were available only in a subset of the entire PDAC cohort, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and do not eliminate the possibility of selection bias.

Moreover, because this immune-related whole-blood mRNA signature may reflect systemic host responses rather than PDAC-specific tumor-derived signals, its disease specificity remains uncertain. This panel has not been systematically evaluated in other malignancies or inflammatory conditions, and therefore cross-reactivity with non-PDAC cancers or non-malignant inflammatory diseases cannot be excluded. Furthermore, because this algorithm was developed as a weighted diagnostic classifier rather than a mechanistic feature-importance model, the biological contribution of each individual gene remains to be clarified in future studies.

From a clinical perspective, the moderate specificity (70.2%) of the mRNA-based system represents an important limitation, particularly if used as a stand-alone diagnostic test. In screening or population-based settings, false positives could lead to unnecessary anxiety and subsequent excessive tests. It is also unclear whether some mRNA-positive HCs in this study harbor subclinical pancreatic malignancy or simply reflect background variation. Although HCs had no imaging or laboratory findings suggestive of PDAC or related pancreatic diseases, systematic evaluation for occult malignancies in other organs, chronic inflammatory conditions, or other immune-modifying disorders was incomplete, and longitudinal follow-up data for mRNA-positive HCs were unavailable. Therefore, mRNA positivity among HCs may reflect non-specific systemic immune activation rather than preclinical PDAC. Future studies including disease-control cohorts and longitudinal follow-up of mRNA-positive individuals are required to clarify the disease specificity and predictive value of this assay.

The PDAC group had a higher prevalence of family history of PDAC than HCs, raising the possibility that hereditary predisposition could influence the performance of the mRNA-based system. However, the results of exploratory analyses do not suggest a clear dependence of mRNA positivity on family history alone. Nevertheless, the number of participants with a family history was very small, and germline mutation status was not systematically assessed. Therefore, whether hereditary predisposition or germline variants influence the performance of this assay remains unclear and should be evaluated in future studies involving genetically defined high-risk cohorts.

At present, the most appropriate role for this assay may be as a complementary tool in well-defined clinical scenarios rather than as a general screening test. Potential applications include:

(i)individuals with strong PDAC risk factors (e.g. familial PDAC, hereditary pancreatitis, hereditary pancreatic cancer syndrome, long-standing new-onset diabetes, chronic pancreatitis)15,20,29;

individuals with strong PDAC risk factors (e.g. familial PDAC, hereditary pancreatitis, hereditary pancreatic cancer syndrome, long-standing new-onset diabetes, chronic pancreatitis)15,20,29;

(ii)patients with indirect imaging findings (e.g. main pancreatic duct changes, focal atrophy) but no detectable mass16; and.

patients with indirect imaging findings (e.g. main pancreatic duct changes, focal atrophy) but no detectable mass16; and.

(iii)cases in which EUS-FNA or SPACE fail to provide a definitive pathological diagnosis17,18.

cases in which EUS-FNA or SPACE fail to provide a definitive pathological diagnosis17,18.

In such situations, a highly sensitive blood-based test could guide intensified surveillance, additional imaging, or surgical decision-making. However, this study should be interpreted as a case–control diagnostic accuracy study in a highly selected cohort, rather than a screening or early detection study. Establishing a diagnostic algorithm combining this mRNA-based system with laboratory and imaging tests may improve diagnostic stratification of selected patients with suspected resectable-stage PDAC.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center case–control diagnostic accuracy study with a small number of stage 0–I PDAC cases, resulting in wide CIs for estimates of sensitivity and AUC. Although the observed sensitivity was high, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of diagnostic utility. Second, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed because of the extreme rarity of PDAC detected at stage 0 or I, even in high-volume centers. Accordingly, all eligible stage 0–I patients diagnosed over a four-year period were prospectively enrolled, and the study was designed as an exploratory pilot investigation. Third, the use of HCs rather than clinically relevant disease controls, such as patients with chronic pancreatitis, IPMN, pancreatic cystic lesions, or individuals at high risk because of genetic predisposition or family history, substantially limits the generalizability of the findings to real-world diagnostic settings. In particular, because the mRNA-based system reflects immune-related whole-blood gene expression, false-positive results may arise from non-PDAC malignancies, inflammatory conditions, or other systemic immune alterations. This mRNA panel has not been systematically evaluated in other malignancies or inflammatory diseases; therefore, cross-reactivity with non-PDAC cancers or non-malignant inflammatory conditions cannot be excluded, and disease specificity remains insufficiently established. In addition, although HCs had no findings suggestive of PDAC or related pancreatic diseases on health-checkup evaluations, systematic screening for occult malignancies in other organs or chronic inflammatory conditions was incomplete, and longitudinal follow-up data for mRNA-positive HCs were not available. Fourth, this study represents an internal evaluation of a previously developed diagnostic system rather than an independent external validation study. Although the diagnostic algorithm and cut-off were predefined and not re-optimized in the present cohort, validation in independent multicenter cohorts is required. Fifth, our algorithm was developed as a weighted diagnostic classifier rather than a mechanistic feature-importance model. Therefore, the relative biological contribution of each gene remains to be clarified in future studies. Sixth, potential confounding factors, including subclinical inflammation, comorbidities, and concomitant medications, may influence peripheral blood gene expression, although no clear associations beyond age were detected in this small sample. Finally, this study did not evaluate cost-effectiveness or logistical issues related to implementing RT-PCR-based assays in routine practice. Future studies should include larger numbers of early-stage PDAC cases, appropriate disease-control cohorts, longitudinal follow-up of mRNA-positive controls, and external validation of the diagnostic algorithm before clinical implementation.

In conclusion, this pilot prospective study demonstrates that the mRNA-based diagnostic system shows substantially higher sensitivity than CA19-9 for the detection of early-stage PDAC, including carcinoma in situ. Although limited by sample size and moderate specificity, these findings provide preliminary evidence that peripheral blood transcriptomic signatures may capture early tumor-associated immune alterations not detectable by conventional tumor markers. Future multicenter studies with adequately powered cohorts, disease control groups, and refined diagnostic algorithms are warranted to confirm diagnostic accuracy, optimize specificity, and define the most appropriate clinical contexts for implementation. Ultimately, integration of this mRNA-based system into existing diagnostic strategies may improve diagnostic evaluation of selected patients with suspected early-stage PDAC and ultimately contribute to improved patient outcomes.
