OPRM1 A118G gene polymorphism and interindividual differences in postoperative nausea and vomiting induced by IV tramadol in patients undergoing femur fracture surgery- A pilot study
Authors: Geetanjali T. Chilkoti, Pankaj Dixit, Neha Tawar, Brijesh K. Mishra, Ashok K. Saxena, Sanjna Tiwari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Aims:The opioid receptor mu receptor 1(OPRM1) gene codes the human μ-opioid receptor, which is a major target for opioids. The OPRM1 A118G polymorphism leads to inter-individual variation in the susceptibility to postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV). This study explored the OPRM1 A118G gene polymorphism and inter-individual differences in PONV induced by postoperative IV tramadol in patients undergoing femur fracture surgery.Material and Methods:The present prospective observational study was conducted following IEC approval, prospective CTRI registration, and written informed consent. Patients of ASA PS I/II undergoing femur fracture surgery under subarachnoid block were included. Fifty patients of ASA I and II, aged 18–60 years, of either sex, undergoing isolated femur fracture surgery and receiving IV tramadol postoperatively were included. Two milliliters venous sample was withdrawn for gene polymorphism study preoperatively. Incidence of PONV and comparison of PONV score, total rescue antiemetic consumption, postoperative NRS pain score, and total analgesic consumption were compared between different genotypes in the first 24 h postoperatively.Results:The prevalence of the wild homozygous allele (AA) was 56%, and mutant-G alleles, i.e., AG and GG, were 36% and 8%, respectively. PONV incidence was significantly higher in the AA allele than in mutant-G alleles i.e. 89% vs 7%; odd’s ratio is 0.214 (P value = 0.000097), reflecting the protective role of the G allele in patients’ predisposition to PONV. Patients with AA allele of OPRM1 A118G required significantly more rescue antiemetic in the first 24h postoperatively.Conclusion:A significantly lower incidence of PONV and lower consumption of rescue antiemetics was observed among patients with mutant G alleles of OPRM1 A118G.

Introduction

Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) is a common complication following surgery, and the incidence ranges between 30%–80%.[1] Recently, the inter-individual difference in PONV incidence has been attributed to individuals’ genotype.[2] This individual genotype predisposes some patients to PONV despite similar risk factors.[34]

The use of opioids for perioperative pain management is known to cause PONV.[5] Opioids are the most common analgesics used for moderate to severe pain management in the perioperative period.[6] Opioids exert their effects by binding to the μ-opioid receptor (MOR), which is encoded by the opioid receptor mu(μ)1 (OPRM1) gene. One of the most prevalent genetic variations in this gene is the A118G single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP), which can influence the function of MOR.[5] This variation in the OPRM1 A118G-SNPs contributes to individual differences in opioid use and the associated side effects, including PONV.

Few studies have investigated the PONV incidence related to OPRM1 gene polymorphism.[4678] Few studies have reported the protective effect of the mutant G allele of the OPRM1 A118G gene on the occurrence of PONV.[47] In contrast, a study by Zhang et al.[5] did not attribute the inter-individual difference in PONV to the OPRM1 A118G polymorphism in patients receiving IV fentanyl for postoperative pain management.

Most of the studies have evaluated the association between OPRM1 gene polymorphism and PONV induced by intrathecal morphine,[2] IV fentanyl,[5] or IV remifentanil.[7] However, among opioids, tramadol is commonly used for postoperative pain management following orthopedic surgeries, but it has not been evaluated in this context.

Race and ethnic diversity determine genetic variation[2]; therefore, therefore mentioned findings cannot be extrapolated to other populations. On literature search, we did not find any study evaluating the association of OPRM1 A118G gene polymorphism with the occurrence of tramadol-induced PONV. In addition, no such study has been undertaken in the Indian subcontinent. We hypothesize the protective role of the mutant G allele of the OPRM1 A118G gene in the prevention of tramadol induced PONV following femur fracture surgeries in the Indian population. With this background, the present pilot study was undertaken to explore the influence of OPRM1 A118G polymorphism on the occurrence of PONV induced by postoperative IV tramadol in patients undergoing femur fracture surgeries. The primary objective was to assess PONV scores amongst different OPRM1 A118G genotypes, and the secondary objectives were to assess rescue antiemetic consumption, numerical rating scale (NRS) pain and, rescue analgesic consumption between different OPRM1 A118G genotypes.

Material and Methods

The present observational pilot study was undertaken following approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee–Human Research (IEC-HR) and prospective CTRI registration (CTRI No. 6433 at www.ctri.nic.in dated-03/09/2021), and was conducted from October 2021 to March 2022. The study was carried out in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013) and adhered to established good clinical practice standards. Patients aged 18–60 years, of either sex, with American society of Anaesthesiologists physical status (ASA-PS) I or II, undergoing isolated femur fracture surgeries under subarachnoid block (SAB) with an Apfel score ≥1 were included. Apfel score ≥1 was required for inclusion in the study, as one risk factor—postoperative opioid use (i.e., IV tramadol)—was present in these patients as per institutional protocol. Patients were excluded if they were pregnant, had BMI >35 kg/m2, experienced nausea or vomiting within 24 h before surgery, received any other antiemetics within the last 24 h, or had known hypersensitivity to 5-HT3 agents. Each participant provided written informed consent.

For premedication, a tablet of alprazolam (0.5 mg PO) was given the night before surgery if the procedure was elective. A 2 ml venous sample was withdrawn for gene polymorphism study in the preoperative room. Standard anesthetic techniques for SAB were adopted in all patients undergoing femur fracture surgery. After the institution of routine ASA-recommended monitoring (i.e., ECG, noninvasive blood pressure, pulse oximetry), the patient was placed either in a sitting or lateral position for the administration of SAB. Following preparation of the back, SAB was administered in the L3–L4/L4–L5 interspace, and inj. bupivacaine 1.8–3.0 ml was administered intrathecally, depending on the patients’ height. Any event of hypotension or bradycardia was noted and managed accordingly.

In the postoperative period, patients were kept under observation for at least 4 h. Hemodynamic variables, i.e., heart rate, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and mean arterial blood pressure, were recorded every hour for four h. PONV were evaluated using a numeric scoring system, i.e., PONV score (no nausea or vomiting = 0; nausea but no vomiting = 1; vomiting once in 30 min or more = 2; persistent nausea >30 min or two or more vomits in 30 min = 3). The PONV score was recorded at various designated intervals, i.e., first 2 h, 2–6 h, 6–12 h, and 12–24 h. If the patient developed nausea for more than 15 min or vomiting in the postoperative period, the anesthesiologist was allowed to administer IV ondansetron. The severity of postoperative pain was assessed using a numerical rating score (NRS) that ranged from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain imaginable) at the end of 2nd h, 6th h, 12th h, and 24th h.

For postoperative pain management, as per the institutional protocol, all patients received injection paracetamol 1g IV every 8h and tramadol 50mg IV every 12h for the initial 2–3 days. If NRS pain score ≥3, IV diclofenac 75 mg was administered as a rescue analgesic. Common side effects of the medication, including drowsiness, dizziness, headache, constipation, flushing, and QT prolongation, were documented.

Genotyping: A 2ml venous blood sample was withdrawn and collected in an EDTA tube and stored at 4°C. From this stored blood, genomic DNA was extracted from unclotted venous blood using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol. A two-step polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was used for the genotyping of the OPRM-1 A118G gene. The first PCR was set up to amplify the region of exon 1 in which this mutation occurs at the 118 nucleotide position.

In the first PCR, the sequence for the forward primer was 5’ AAA GTC TCG GTG CTC CTG GCT-3’, and for the reverse primer, it was 5’-TGG GAG TTA GGT GTC TCT TTG TA-3’. After the first PCR the amplicon was 420 base pairs in size, and this amplified product was used for the second PCR. In the second PCR, two different forward primers were used for the A and G alleles, while the reverse primer remained the same. The forward primer sequences for the A allele were 5’-CAA CTT GTC CCA CTT AGA TGG CA-3’, and for the G allele, it was 5’-CAA CTT GTC CCA CTT AGA TGG CG-3’. Both alleles produced a fragment size of 267 bp. In total, three reactions were setup for a single genomic product to analyze which of the three genotypes (AA, GG, and AG) is present in a DNA sample, and primers were used in three combinations to test the same.

For the thermocycling conditions, DNA was denatured at 94°C for 2 min, followed by 30 denaturation cycles at 94°C for 30 s, primer annealing at 53°C (for the first PCR) and 64°C (for the second PCR) for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 45 s, followed by a final extension for 2 min. The PCR products were analyzed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and each sample was recorded for wild-type homozygous (AA), mutant heterozygous (AG), and mutant homozygous (GG) alleles.

On literature search, we did not find any study evaluating the role of OPRM1 A118G gene polymorphism and interindividual difference in PONV induced by postoperative IV tramadol. For sample size calculation, a moderate correlation between OPRM1 A118G gene polymorphism and the occurrence of postoperative tramadol-induced PONV in patients undergoing femur fracture surgery was considered meaningful. To detect a moderate correlation (r = 0.4) with 80% power and an alpha at 0.05, a sample of 50 patients was required.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 20.0. Data were categorized according to genotype, and allele frequencies were estimated from the observed numbers of each specific allele. The normality of the data was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Data for numerical variables was expressed as means and SD, while categorical variables (age, body weight, gender, duration of study, I.V fluid, ASA physical status) were expressed using the Tukey HSD test. The PONV incidence was analyzed using a Chi-square test. The Tukey HSD test was used to analyze intergroup comparisons between genotype groups for PONV, rescue antiemetic consumption in 24 h, mean NRS, and mean rescue analgesic consumption in 24 h amongst different genotypes at various time intervals. The correlation between NRS pain and PONV scores at various time intervals was analyzed using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sixty patients were assessed for their eligibility in the study; of these, four patients had a BMI >35kg/m2, and two patients had nausea and vomiting 24h before surgery, and thus were excluded [Figure 1]. Among the 54 patients who were enrolled, four patients were excluded as GA was administered either due to the long duration of surgery or inadequate SAB. Finally, 50 patients were included in the study.

The DNA fragment (base pair) produced by the OPRM1 A118G wild-type homozygous (AA) was 193bp. The DNA fragment (base pair) produced by the OPRM1 A118G mutant-type heterozygous (AG) and mutant-type homozygous (GG) was 193bp + 169bp and 169bp + 24bp, respectively.

The prevalence of the wild homozygous (AA) allele of the OPRM1 A118G gene was 56% (n = 28) (minor allele frequency (MAF) = 0.56). The prevalence of the mutant G allele, i.e., AG allele and GG allele, was 44% (MAF = 0.44), with the AG allele and GG allele being 36% (n = 18) and 8%(n = 4), respectively. The genotypic distribution in our study was in accordance with Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium.

Discussion

In the present study, a significantly lower PONV incidence and lower rescue antiemetic consumption were observed amongst patients with mutant G alleles of OPRM1 A118G. However, no difference in NRS pain scores and rescue analgesic consumption was observed.

Opioids are commonly used for pain management during the perioperative period. Out of all opioid-induced side-effects, PONV has been the most common and distressing. Genetic variations, such as SNPs of OPRM1, may be linked to individual differences in opioid sensitivity and the occurrence of PONV.[4]

The major target for opioids is the MOR, which is encoded by the OPRM1 gene. Among over 3,000 polymorphisms in OPRM1, the most commonly studied SNP is A118G, in which the amino acid asparagine at position 40 is replaced by aspartate.[6] Moreover, the frequency of the polymorphism (G allele) varies depending on the population. The global average frequency of the abnormal G allele is approximately 20%.[9]

Various studies have reported that the mutant G allele of OPRM1 118A is associated with a lower PONV incidence compared to the wild homozygous allele (AA).[2467] This genetic variation among individuals is subject to race and ethnic diversity[2]; therefore, the aforementioned findings cannot be extrapolated to other populations.

In the present study, the mutant G allele, i.e., both mutant heterozygous (AG) and mutant homozygous (GG) was observed in 42%, and the MAF was 48% in the Indian population. The genotypic distribution in our study is in accordance with Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. Our findings are in concordance to Zhang et al.[5] which was conducted among the Chinese population to evaluate the influence of the OPRM1 gene on PONV following myomectomy, where the frequency of G allele was observed by 32.4%. Similarly, a 118G allele frequency of 38.6% was observed by Lee et al.[7] in the Korean population undergoing GA with remifentanil.

On literatures search, various studies have reported the protective effect of the mutant G allele of the OPRM1 A118G gene on the occurrence of PONV.[2467] The predisposition to PONV using OPRM1 A118 G polymorphism, with the use of opioids via various routes such as intrathecal morphine,[2]IV fentanyl infusion postoperatively[5], and IV remifentanil in TIVA[7] have been evaluated.

Tan et al.[9] conducted a prospective cohort study in parturients undergoing caesarean section under SAB, with the primary aim of evaluating the influence of OPRM1 SNPs on acute postoperative pain.[2] This study observed a lower incidence of nausea (P value = 0.026) and vomiting (P value = 0.022) in patients with the mutant G allele of the OPRM1 gene. Additionally, a significant association was found between the OPRM1 118G variant and time-averaged pain scores (P = 0.024). Homozygotes for the OPRM1 118G allele used more morphine and experienced higher pain scores compared to carriers of the 118A allele.

Lee et al.[7] evaluated the influence of OPRM1 A118G on PONV and postoperative pain in adult Korean patients. They found that patients with the GG genotype had a lower incidence of PONV (P = 0.002) compared to those with the AA and AG genotypes. No differences in postoperative pain were observed across the various opioid receptor genotypes. The study concluded that the occurrence of PONV varies depending on the OPRM1 A118G gene polymorphism and the anesthetic technique used in patients undergoing general anesthesia with remifentanil.

The recent most meta-analysis by Kong et al[10] concluded that the OPRM1 A118G polymorphism is associated with the risk of postoperative vomiting, but not nausea, pruritus, or dizziness. Amongst these, the mutant-type homozygous (GG) is associate d with a lower incidence of PONV.[11]

Thus, most of these aforementioned studies have affirmed the protective role of the mutant G allele of OPRM1 A118 G for PONV incidence.[2467] However, on the contrary, two studies observed that the G allele has no effect.[512] Zhang et al.[5] evaluated the association of the OPRM1 A118G gene polymorphism with PONV induced by IV fentanyl analgesia in patients undergoing hysterectomy/myomectomy, and they did not observe any significant difference in PONV among patients homozygous for 118A and heterozygous. Similarly, Walldén et al.[12] also did not observe any association between the genetic polymorphism A118G and nausea and vomiting caused by fentanyl. However, they discovered that patients with inhibited gastric motility experienced a higher incidence of PONV. This finding offers an additional explanation for PONV, suggesting that it may be caused by opioid-induced alterations in gastric motility.

In the present study, we observed a significant association between the G mutant allele of OPRM1 118A and PONV incidence. In addition, in the present study, we have used more standard assessment of PONV using the PONV score along with rescue antiemetic consumption at various time intervals.

In the present study, the calculated odds ratio or relative risk for PONV is 0.214 (P value < 0.01), which shows the protective role of the G allele on the occurrence of PONV following the use of IV tramadol and postoperative analgesia. Among the related studies, three studies have evaluated the relative risk of PONV with the G allele of OPRM1 118A. The reported odds ratios by Sugino et al.[4] in a meta-analysis is 10.5 (P value = 0.04), and 1.30 by Ren et al.[6] (P value ≤ 0.01). All these studies have observed the protective role of OPRM1 A118G on the occurrence of PONV.

The difference in rescue antiemetic requirement was observed to be significantly higher in wild-type homozygous individuals only when compared to the mutant homozygous allele (GG)(P value = 0.015). Among all related studies, only Lee et al.[7] have evaluated rescue antiemetic consumption among different OPRM1 118A genotypes, and they did not observe any significant difference among the different genotypes.

Most of the studies have concluded that the OPRM1 A118G polymorphism is associated with difference in interindividual pain perception among different OPRM1 genotypes, among which wild mutant homozygous (GG) is associated with higher pain perception compared to wild-type homozygous (AA).[261113141516] However, we did not observe any significant difference in the NRS pain score or rescue analgesic consumption among the different genotypes. This could be attributed to the small sample size and ethnic background differences. Although we observed a trend toward higher mean rescue analgesic consumption and higher pain scores in the mutant G allele, but it was not significant.

The study has a few limitations. First, the present preliminary data have been obtained from a limited cohort from a single center. Second, genetic variation is influenced by race and ethnic diversity, meaning the findings of this study may not be applicable to individuals from other racial or ethnic backgrounds. Third, other genes, such as COMT, CYP2D6, MC1R, ABCB1, and KCNJ6, may also play a role in regulating PONV sensitivity, potentially confounding the results of this study.[2]

Conclusion

We noted a marked reduction in the incidence of PONV and a decrease in the need for rescue antiemetic consumption amongst patients with mutant G alleles of OPRM1 A118G; however, no significant trend was observed in NRS pain scores and rescue analgesic consumption. We observed a significant protective role of the mutant G allele on the predisposition to the development of PONV among patients receiving IV tramadol for postoperative pain management following fracture femur surgeries.

The finding of the present study is a small step toward the future; wherein the widespread use of basic OPRM1 A118G polymorphism testing via inexpensive PCR–restriction fragment length polymorphism (PCR–RFLP) genetic tests may help in formulating individualized PONV management protocols—especially in high-risk populations. Larger-scale multicentric trials are needed to validate the findings of the current study.
