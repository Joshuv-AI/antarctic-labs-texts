Review of Handbook of Statistical Methods for Precision Medicine (edited by Eric Laber, Bibhas Chakraborty, Erica E.M. Moodie, Tianxi Cai, and Mark van der Laan)
Authors: Kristin A. Linn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Handbook of Statistical Methods for Precision Medicine is an edited volume that compiles recent methodological advances in the field. Authored and edited by leading statisticians, the book emphasizes mathematical rigor and theoretical development over applied examples. While this focus makes the volume a valuable resource for researchers seeking a deep understanding of statistical foundations in precision medicine, it may be less accessible to practitioners looking for direct guidance on implementation.

Precision medicine research has advanced rapidly over recent decades, propelled by an explosion of biomedical data, dramatic improvements in computing power, and the ubiquity of personal data-collection technologies such as smartphones and smartwatches. Seminal contributions to the field include the Sequential Multiple Assignment Randomized Trial (SMART) design (Murphy 2005), which formalized experimentation for developing optimal adaptive treatment strategies, and Just-In-Time Adaptive Interventions (JITAIs) (Nahum-Shani et al. 2016), which leverage real-time data to deliver context-aware, personalized treatments. Estimation frameworks for optimal dynamic treatment regimes, such as Q-learning (Murphy 2003), A-learning (Schulte et al. 2015), Outcome Weighted Learning (Zhao et al. 2012), and many others, have promised to be a catalyst for tailoring medical and behavioral care over time. In parallel, advances in causal inference, including targeted maximum likelihood estimation (Van Der Laan and Rubin 2006; Van der Laan et al. 2011) and doubly robust estimators (Bang and Robins 2005), have been driven in part by precision medicine goals. These tools have primarily been applied in secondary data analyses to illustrate how treatments could be tailored to patients for chronic disease and mental health management over time, but for the most part, translation into new standards of care has been minimal.

In the preface, the editors claim that the book is widely accessible to statisticians, epidemiologists, and clinicians. I wholeheartedly disagree with this claim. Most chapters require the reader to have advanced training in statistical inference, and several chapters require more than a surface-level understanding of causal inference, machine learning, or reinforcement learning. Although largely unavoidable, the notational burden in many of the chapters may be intimidating yet conquerable to some readers, while prohibitory to others. Accessibility of the material is not aided by occasional errors in the equation references (particularly Chapters 9 and 13). I believe that this book will be useful to methodological researchers in statistics, causal inference, and machine learning who are interested in pushing the boundary of methods for precision medicine. Only a few chapters include or provide a link to code for implementing the methods, whereas a large number of chapters have appendices dedicated to mathematical derivations and proofs. To see widespread adoption of the methods in this book, additional work will need to be done to translate the more advanced concepts to applied statisticians like me—or to the agentic models that may soon take our jobs. In what follows, I summarize the three sections of the book.

Part I: Study Design for Precision Medicine

Part I of the book begins with a chapter on adaptive designs. At first glance, I found the topic of adaptive designs to be an unexpected choice for the opener of the book. Having been involved in the design and analysis of several SMART designs, I have found myself expounding the distinctions between adaptive designs and adaptive interventions to collaborators on many occasions. As I made my way through the material, I appreciated this chapter’s comprehensive introduction to adaptive designs for goals such as optimal dose finding, interim analyses and rules for early stopping, adaptive enrichment for targeted patient recruitment, and adaptive randomization for dropping or adding treatment arms, all of which are presented through the lens of precision medicine. The authors also cover the basics of basket and umbrella trials. Considerations for inferential validity (e.g., Type I error control) are presented but balanced with descriptions of real-world adaptive trials to illustrate key concepts. Chapter 4 of Part I reviews how dose-finding in phase I-II trials can be framed as a multi-stage decision problem where the goal is to jointly optimize efficacy and toxicity.

Chapters 3 and 6 continue to synthesize methods for adaptive trials and sequential decision problems. Chapter 3 presents methods for computing and applying adaptive randomization probabilities to SMART designs. These methods use information accrued from an early set of trial completers to estimate Q-functions at each stage of the trial. Then, the randomization probabilities are modified based on the estimated Q-functions and other pre-specified design parameters so that future patients who enroll in the trial are more likely to be assigned to the most effective treatment sequences. Chapter 6 presents Thompson sampling (TS) as another framework for guiding patients toward more effective treatments during a trial through randomization probabilities that adapt to the accumulation of information on patient responses to treatment. This approach can be viewed as precision medicine in the sense that the expected number of treatment successes is optimized during the trial. However, precision medicine is perhaps more salient when the authors of Chapter 6 detail how TS for contextual bandits can be applied to learn about and leverage heterogeneous treatment effects to more deeply tailor treatment assignments during a trial. Chapter 6 also provides details on applying TS for more precise treatment allocation during multi-stage trials (i.e., SMARTs) and settings with infinite horizons. Familiarity with Markov decision processes may be helpful to readers interested in these latter topics.

Chapter 2 of Part I describes methodology for designing and implementing a small n SMART design (snSMART). The snSMART design leverages ideas from traditional SMART designs to improve efficiency in rare disease research, where small sample sizes inhibit scientific questions from being answered. The authors present two models for analyzing data from an snSMART with a binary outcome: the log-linear Poisson joint stage model and the Bayesian joint stage model. They also discuss design extensions that allow for removal of inferior treatment arms after interim analyses. The authors of Chapter 2 conclude with a short demo of their R package, snSMART, available on GitHub, illustrating its functions for sample size determination and analysis of data from an snSMART design. Their efforts to include implementation details will be appreciated by applied analysts.

Readers who trained in statistics or biostatistics programs may find the agent-based modeling techniques detailed in Chapter 5 to be less familiar than the precision medicine methods discussed in other chapters. When applied to clinical research, agent-based modeling is a simulation framework that aims to generate realistic patient-level features and outcomes. The data simulated by an agent-based model can then be used to optimize clinical trial designs or assist in drug development, among other applications. If possible, agent-based models employ existing data from real patients in a relevant setting to calibrate or learn parameters for the data generative models. Statisticians will notice parallels with simulation studies for assessing the operating characteristics of a clinical trial design, plasmode simulations, or perhaps imputation methods for missing data. I thought the subsection on pair-copula construction was nicely presented and, in addition to its utility for agent-based modeling, might be of interest more broadly to graduate students who need to generate realistic multivariate data for Monte Carlo simulation studies.

Part II: Estimation of Optimal Treatment Strategies

The number of frequentist methods that have been proposed to estimate optimal treatment strategies has dwarfed that of Bayesian methods. The first chapter of Part II reviews familiar frequentist models for estimation of static and dynamic treatment regimes (e.g., Q-learning, structural nested mean models) but then gives high-level sketches of algorithms from a Bayesian perspective for obtaining posterior samples of optimal treatments and parameters indexing the corresponding models. Chapter 7, the first of Part II, uses concrete examples to illustrate that optimal treatment estimation strategies often rely on backward recursive techniques to account for mediated effects of earlier-stage treatment effects through intermediate variables. Chapter 7 might be one of the most accessible chapters in this section for applied analysts and epidemiologists.

Chapter 8 explores how measurement error in covariates, treatment, or outcome may lower the quality of optimal treatment estimation in one- and two-stage contexts. The chapter also highlights a more subtle issue—that measurement errors in tailoring variables may lead to sub-optimal treatment of future patients even when the optimal treatment strategy is known. Application of regression calibration during model training or obtaining replicates of tailoring variables to use for both estimation and deployment are demonstrated to partially mitigate problems caused by measurement error. More broadly, the author of Chapter 8 cautions that the total effects of various measurement errors on precision medicine are complex in practice and likely require “bespoke” handling.

In Chapter 9, the authors begin by introducing the standard two period difference in difference (DiD) design where treated and control groups are observed in a pre-treatment period and a post-treatment period. This design is popular in economic and health policy research and has recently received much attention in the methodological statistics and econometric literatures, where practical considerations such as staggered rollouts of policies over time have inspired new estimands and corresponding estimators (Sun and Abraham 2021; Goodman-Bacon 2021; Callaway and Sant’Anna 2021; Athey and Imbens 2022). Unlike optimal treatment strategies which provide a recommended treatment to any individual patient that presents with relevant clinical information, the target estimand of Chapter 9 is the average treatment effect on the treated, conditional on a set of covariates. Continuing the focus on causal estimands, Chapter 10 offers a review of nonparametric parameter estimation and corresponding efficiency bounds. I find that I get more out of this chapter every time I read it, but to fully grasp the intuition it offers and be able to apply the tools presented, I’d need to brush up on foundational semiparametric theory building blocks (Van der Vaart 2000; Tsiatis 2006). Despite my knowledge gaps, I found the strategies for deriving influence functions easier to follow than other materials on this topic, particularly the “tricks” the author provides on page 219, which according to the author have not been described elsewhere in the literature. Thus, this chapter may be valuable to graduate students and other researchers looking for an introduction to the types of functional estimation techniques that have recently proliferated in the causal inference literature.

Learning good estimators for conditional average treatment effects is the goal in Chapter 11, where a good estimator is defined as one that minimizes the worst-case Bayes risk over a set of prior distributions for the joint distribution of covariates, treatment, and outcome (i.e., Gamma-minimax). A couple of algorithms are proposed that pit an estimator against a prior distribution from which data are drawn, allowing each to iteratively modify itself based on the behavior of the other. Framing optimal treatment learning as the solution to a two-player adversarial game is a marked deviation from more established estimation strategies. However, it seems like a promising direction due to the ability to leverage developments in machine learning over the last decade. The authors discuss construction of prior classes from which to sample data for learning and desirable properties of the proposed class of estimators. While some familiarity with the theory and implementation of neural networks might benefit the reader of Chapter 11, the availability of code on the author’s GitHub page (and its reliance on popular libraries such as TensorFlow and Pytorch) promises to facilitate application and extension of the methods.

Chapter 12 demonstrates how random effects in mixed effects models can be utilized to learn deeply personalized treatment policies while also accounting for correlated longitudinal patient data. The random effects are framed as unobserved patient information that may inform individualized treatment rules along with the fixed effects. Mobile health engagement provides a motivating application, where tradeoffs exist between aiming to optimize short-term outcomes versus longer-term outcomes.

I was initially excited for the overview of bandit algorithms in Chapter 13 but soon found myself disillusioned by the focus on theoretical results and the use of measure theoretic notation. I found a disconnect between the authors’ stated hope that the reader “…appreciates the relevance of bandit algorithms to mobile health and precision medicine” and the absence data examples. No attempts were made to translate notation, theoretical results, or algorithms to concrete data settings. Theoretically inclined readers may enjoy this treatment of bandit algorithms, as it seems rigorous and comprehensive. I would have loved to see a more accessible chapter on this topic—perhaps one that aimed to minimize the regret of applied researchers not utilizing bandit algorithms for precision medicine.

Part III: Precision Medicine in High Dimensions

Part III of the book offers a collection of modern approaches to precision medicine when the data available are high dimensional. Variable selection may improve the performance and adoption of data-driven optimal treatment strategies through increased statistical efficiency and encouragement of simple treatment rules. Chapter 15 is perhaps the most accessible of the entire book as it provides high-level intuition for the link between optimally treating individuals and overall population benefit, including in constrained resource settings. This chapter may have been a better choice for the opener of Part III as the authors demonstrate why it is important to consider multiple covariates at a time rather than studying heterogeneous treatment effects in a one-covariate-at-a-time manner. Several chapters in Part III review established methods that have been used in lower-dimensional problems such as Q-learning, A-learning, g-estimation, outcome weighted learning, and value search. After a review of dWOLS, Chapter 14 covers tailoring variable ranking techniques, enforcement of “strong heredity” where a variable selection method selects an interaction if and only if the main effects are also selected into the model, and approaches to selecting variables for both the outcome and confounder models when learning optimal treatment strategies.

Chapter 16 gives assumptions needed for nonparametric identification of optimal treatment regimes in the context of unmeasured confounding that rely on a binary instrumental variable (IV). The authors briefly survey the literature on more recent developments for individualized treatment rule estimation based on proximal causal inference, where the IV may be continuous. I felt this chapter would have benefited from more discussion of practical considerations, such as the problems posed by weak IVs.

Chapter 17 gives a nice introductory overview to polygenic risk score construction and estimation. Polygenic risk scores aim to quantify genotypic associations with disease and may play an increasing role in treatment discovery and treatment personalization. An important consideration in polygenic risk score construction is whether and how to incorporate information about linkage disequilibrium (LD). The authors review several popular methods and categorize them as either methods that directly model and account for LD or methods that impose sparsity in some way (e.g., regularization term, spike and slab prior).

The book closes with Chapter 18 on post-selection inference for precision medicine methods. Post-selection inference methods account for both uncertainty in the selection of the dimension of a model’s parameter space as well as in the parameter estimates for the selected model. This is in contrast to classical statistical inference methods that only reflect uncertainty in the latter. The authors of Chapter 18 first present a convenient re-expression of the usual form of the outcome model used in estimation methods such as Q-learning that permits non-parametric confounding control through estimation of nuisance functions using cross-fitted machine learning procedures. Then, taking the Lasso as a concrete model selection procedure, they provide details of two post-selection inference methods in the context of precision medicine: the Polyhedral method and the UPoSI method. Each provide confidence regions for the parameters indexing individualized treatment rules in the single-stage setting. In a simulation study, the authors show that the UPoSI method is highly conservative—perhaps enough to be disfavored in practice—while the Polyhedral method’s performance varies across different target metrics. I personally struggled through the derivations and theorems in Chapter 18, particularly those related to the UPoSI method. I believe applied statisticians like me will first need to study the theoretical basis of seminal post-selection inference works (Lee et al. 2016; Tian and Taylor 2017) before attempting to apply these methods to further the goals of personalized medicine.

Conclusion

Handbook of Statistical Methods for Precision Medicine presents a wide-ranging collection of emerging methodological research on study design, modeling, estimation, and statistical inference for advancing data-driven approaches to personalized medicine. The volume will be most valuable to researchers with a strong foundation in advanced statistical inference who are interested in contributing to the methodological development of precision medicine. Those seeking more introductory or applied perspectives, including clinicians, applied scientists, or early-stage graduate students in (bio)statistics, may find other resources better suited to their needs. As the field of precision medicine continues to evolve, there is a growing need for researchers who can bridge the gap between methodological innovation and practical implementation within collaborative, interdisciplinary science.
