HSPA8 globally modulates transcriptome profile and PI3K/AKT signaling pathway to facilitate malignant progression phenotypes of triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Wenting Xu, Yongtao Li, Hu Wang, Chenguang Zhang, Miaomiao Zhang, Jianghua Ou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) represents the most aggressive subtype of breast cancer. HSPA8 is a chaperone protein involved in the proper folding or degradation of many proteins that are implicated in various types of cancers, while its role in TNBC remains unclear.MethodsIn this study, we extensively explored the cellular function and molecular targets of HSPA8 in TNBC cells by performing small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated knockdown, followed by cytological assays and transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq).ResultsThe results of HSPA8 expression levels across distinct breast cancer subtypes indicated that TNBC exhibits higher HSPA8 expression. Then, cytological experiments demonstrated that knockdown of HSPA8 significantly inhibited TNBC cell proliferation, invasion and migration, and induced apoptosis. Mechanistically, RNA-seq showed that HSPA8 globally regulates gene expression and alternative splicing (AS), particularly modulating expression of genes in PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, and AS of genes in protein phosphorylation. Finally, we experimentally confirmed that knockdown of HSPA8 significantly suppressed the activation of the PI3K/AKT axis and AKT phosphorylation in TNBC cells.ConclusionsOur findings highlight an important pro-oncogenic role of HSPA8 in TNBC progression, mediated in part through transcriptomic remodeling and activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. These results may inform the development of novel targets and strategies for future targeted therapy in TNBC.

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the most common malignant tumors among women worldwide and a leading cause of cancer-related deaths. 1 Among different molecular subtypes, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) harbors the highest degree of malignancy, accounting for approximately 15-20% of all breast cancer cases, and exhibits a high heterogeneity and invasive potential clinically, as well as an unfavorable prognosis. Owing to limited therapeutic options and the presence of treatment resistance, TNBC patients face higher risk of recurrence and metastasis.2–4 Several reports have reviewed potential targets and key signaling pathways involved in TNBC therapeutics, such as Notch, Hedgehog, Wnt/β-catenin, and TGF-β signaling pathways.5–7 Despite some understanding of these pathways, treating TNBC remains challenging due to its high heterogeneity and complexity. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop novel molecular targets and explore more precise therapeutic strategies for more effective and sustainable TNBC management.

Heat shock protein family A member 8 (HSPA8) is a molecular chaperone involved in numerous cellular functions, including chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA), protein folding and transport, ubiquitin-proteasome degradation, ATPase cycle, and antigen presentation. 8 HSPA8 has been implicated in the pathogenesis of multiple diseases. In oncology, it is highly expressed in many cancers and closely associated with tumorigenesis and progression. For instance, high HSPA8 expression predicts an adverse outcome in acute myeloid leukemia, 9 serves as a potential prognostic marker for bladder cancer, 10 and functions as a crucial host factor modulating HBV replication and ferroptosis in liver cancer. 11 Beyond cancer, HSPA8 dysregulation has also been documented in neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease, where it is involved in protein aggregate and clearance, 12 and in cardiovascular conditions, influencing cardiac remodeling. 13 These evidences underscores the important role of HSPA8 across diverse pathological contexts, including cancer.

Accumulating studies indicate that HSPA8 is essential for cancer cell survival, functioning as a key regulator of protein homeostasis and a participant in several oncogenic signaling pathways that drive tumor development, invasion and metastasis. In hepatocellular carcinoma, HSPA8 upregulates PHDLA2 through the co-activating transcription factor ETV4 to enhance cancer cell growth in vitro and in vivo. 14 In ovarian cancer, HSPA8 promotes the degradation of CLPP protein by regulating its stability, thereby increasing cisplatin resistance. 15 In colon cancer, HSPA8 interacts with CAV1 in a chaperone-dependent manner, which promotes CMA-dependent degradation of CAV1, consequently releasing β-catenin into the nucleus and activating the Wnt pathway, leading to metastasis and progression of cancer cells. 16 Notably, targeting HSPA8 was shown to activate the VAV1/ERK pathway, promoting autophagy and apoptosis in TNBC cells. 17 Furthermore, our previous work confirmed a significant correlation between HSPA8 expression and clinical prognosis of TNBC, suggesting its potential value as both a diagnostic biomarker and a promising therapeutic target. 18 However, the influence of HSPA8 in TNBC remains unclear, and its underlying molecular mechanisms require further investigation.

In this study, we sought to investigate the functional role of HSPA8 in TNBC progression and identify its downstream molecular targets and key signaling pathways. To test this, we silenced HSPA8 expression in TNBC cells and combined cytological experiments with whole transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) to confirm the influence of HSPA8 suppression on cellular malignant progression and its potential molecular mechanisms. Our findings provide new insight into the mechanism by which HSPA8 promotes TNBC development and further support its potential as a predictive biomarker and therapeutic target for TNBC patients.

Materials and methods

Clinical data retrieval

The expression profiles and clinical-pathological information of breast cancer patients were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas Breast Invasive Carcinoma (TCGA-BRCA) dataset, comprising 117 TNBC cases, 1001 non-TNBC case, and 113 non-tumor breast tissue samples. For survival prognosis analysis, we used the Kaplan-Meier plotter (kmplot.com) platform, which integrates gene expression data and clinical follow-up information from multiple public databases. The TNBC subtype classifier was applied to filter patient cohorts specifically for TNBC.

Cell culture and transfections

All cell lines (MCF10A, MCF-7, HCC38, SK-BR-3, MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468) have been authenticated and purchased from Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China), and are free from mycoplasma contamination. Cell line identities and RRIDs are as follows: MCF10A (RRID:CVCL_0598), MCF-7 (RRID:CVCL_0031), HCC38 (RRID:CVCL_1267), SK-BR-3 (RRID:CVCL_0033), MDA-MB-231 (RRID:CVCL_0062), and MDA-MB-468 (RRID:CVCL_0419). All cells were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2. HCC38 and MDA-MB-231 were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium. MCF-7, SK-BR-3 and MDA-MB-468 were maintained in DMEM medium. MCF10A cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 medium. All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco), 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin (Hyclone). For siRNA transfection, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3×105 cells per well 24 hours prior to transfection to reach 50-60% confluence. Transfections were conducted using Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX transfection reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with a final siRNA concentration of 30 nM. A non-targeting scrambled siRNA (siNC) was used as a negative control. Medium was replaced with fresh culture medium 6 hours post-transfection. Knockdown efficiency was assessed by Western blot analysis 48 hours post-transfection, followed by all subsequent functional assays. HSPA8-specific and siNC duplexes were synthesized by Gemma (Suzhou, China). The siNC sequences were as follows: sense 5′-UUC​UCC​GAA​CGU​GUC​ACG​UTT-3’ and antisense 5’-ACG​UGA​CAC​GUU​CGG​AGA​ATT-3’. The sequences of HSPA8 siRNA were as follows: sense 5′-GCC​AUG​ACA​AAG​GAU​AAC​ATT-3’ and antisense 5′-UGU​UAU​CCU​UUG​UCA​UGG​CTT-3’.

RNA isolation and reverse transcribed quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using Trizol agent (Ambion) following the manufacture’s protocol. Then the mRNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme), and the Hieff®qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen) and Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System were applied for RT-qPCR. Amplification conditions were as follows: 95˚C for 10 min, 40 cycles of denaturing at 95˚C for 15 sec and annealing and extension at 60˚C for 1 min. Each sample had three technical replicates. The concentration of each transcript was then normalized to GAPDH and mRNA level using 2- ΔΔCT method to analysis. For alternative splicing (AS) events, we used a boundary-spanning primer for the sequence encompassing the junction of constitutive exon and alternative exon and an opposing primer in a constitutive exon in detecting alternative isoforms. Comparisons were performed with the paired Student’s t-test by using GraphPad Prism software (v.8.0). The primers used for RT-qPCR were as follows: CD38 (Forward: GTA​TCC​TGG​TCC​TGA​TCC​T; Reverse: AGT​GTA​CTT​GAC​GCA​TCG), PPP2R2D (Forward: AAA​GCC​GCC​CTC​ATT​CTA; Reverse: GCA​GCA​TTC​TGT​TGT​GGT​A), RDH16 (Forward: CAA​GCA​GGA​CTT​CGT​GAC​CA; Reverse: GGA​GAT​GCA​GTA​GCC​TCC​AC), IL4R (Forward: CGT​GAC​CTA​CCT​AGA​ACC; Reverse: GTG​GTG​TTA​TAG​CAC​TGA​G), GAPDH (Forward: GGT​CGG​AGT​CAA​CGG​ATT​TG; Reverse: GGA​AGA​TGG​TGA​TGG​GAT​TTC), PPP2R2D (M/AS-Forward: CAA​CGA​CTT​CCA​GTG​GTG​CTT​CT; M-Reverse: ATG​ATG​TCC​GCT​TCG​GCC​AC; AS-Reverse: GGC​TTT​TAT​TCT​TCG​GCC​AC), MALAT1 (M/AS-Forward: TGA​GGA​AGG​AAA​AGA​TAA​AA; M-Reverse: TTT​TCT​TCG​CAT​TTT​TTA​CT; AS-Reverse: TTT​TAA​ATA​CAT​TTT​TTA​CT).

Western blot

Total protein was extracted using ice-cold RIPA buffer (Proteintech, China) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma, USA). Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein (35 μg per lane) were then separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred into 0.45 µm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). The PVDF membranes were then blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 1h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibody, followed by an incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody. Protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Beyotime, China), and the semi-quantifications of protein expression were analyzed using ImageJ software. The antibodies used in the experiment include: HSPA8 (Ab51052, 1:1000, Abcam, RRID:AB_880538), GAPDH (60004-1-Ig, 1:10000, Proteintech, RRID:AB_2107436), PI3K p110β (20584-1-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech, RRID:AB_10734439), AKT (10176-2-AP, 1:1000, Proteintech, RRID:AB_2224574), p-PI3K p85/p55 (Tyr467, Tyr199, Tyr464) (PA5-121306, 1:1000, Thermo, RRID:AB_2914878), p-AKT (Ser473) (4060, 1:2000, CST, RRID:AB_2315049). GAPDH was used as the loading control for normalization.

Cell proliferation assay

The transfected HCC38 cells were collected and inoculated into 24-well plates at a density of 0.5×105 cells per well. After incubation for 0, 24, 48, and 72 hours at 37°C with 5% CO2, 10 μl of CCK-8 solution (HY-K0301, MCE) was added to each well and incubated for another 2.5 hours. Cell proliferation was detected by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm (OD450) using a microplate reader (ELX800, Biotek).

Apoptosis assays

Apoptosis was detected using the Annexin V-APC/7-ADD Apoptosis Detection Kit (40304ES60, Yeasen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, transfected HCC38 cells were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 1.8×105 cells per well and harvested 48 h post-transfection by trypsinization, washed twice with cold PBS, and then resuspended in 1X Binding Buffer. Subsequently, 5 μl of Annexin V-APC and 10 μl of 7-ADD were added to 100 μl of the cell suspension, gently vortexed, and incubated for 15 min at room temperature and protected from light. After incubation, 400 μl of 1X Binding Buffer was added to each tube, and the samples were analyzed on a BD FACSCantoTM II flow cytometer within 1 hour. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.9.0).

Invasion and migration assays

Cell invasion and migration were examined by transwell assays using 24-well chambers with 8-µm pores (Corning). For invasion assays, the upper chambers were pre-coated with matrigel (356234, BD Biosciences), while migration assays used uncoated chambers. Cells were seeded in the upper chambers in serum-free medium with an invasion density of 0.6×105 and migration of 0.4×105, and then the lower chamber was supplemented with medium containing 600 μl 10% FBS. After 24 hours, cells on the upper surface of the membrane were removed with a cotton swab. Cells that had invaded or migrated to the lower surface were fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099, Beyotime) and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (C0121, Beyotime) for 30 minutes. The invaded and migrated cells were observed and counted under inverted microscope at 200× magnification (MF52-N, Mshot) in five random fields using an automatic cell counter (Countstar Rigel S5).

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 5×105 cells per well and cultured in petri dishes to 90% confluence. A straight scratch was made in the monolayer using a sterile 10µL pipette tip. Debris was removed by washing three times with PBS. Cells were then cultured in fresh serum-free medium for 24 hours. Images of the scratched area were captured at 0 and 24 h using an inverted microscope (MF52-N, Mshot) at 40× magnification in three random fields for each experimental setting. The wound area was measured using the ImageJ software and the percentage of wound closure was calculated relative to the 0 h time point.

RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted from the cells transfected with siHSPA8 and siNC and subjected to RNA sequencing by Wuhan Ruixing Biotechnology Co. Ltd. Specifically, for each sample, total RNA was subjected to mRNA capture using VAHTS mRNA capture beads (N401, Vazyme), and strand-specific RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal V8 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (NR605, Vazyme), and qualified libraries were applied to the DNBSEQ-T7 System for 150 nt paired-end sequencing.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and AS analysis

Raw reads from RNA-seq were firstly pre-processed by FASTX-Toolkit (v.0.0.13, https://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx_toolkit) to remove adapters and low-quality bases to obtain clean reads. Subsequently, clean reads were mapped to the GRCh38 reference genome using HISAT2 (v.2.2.1), 19 where uniquely mapped reads were used for Fragments Per Kilobase of exon model per Million mapped fragments (FPKM) calculation. The reads count files were analyzed for gene expression quantification using DESeq2 (v.1.30.1), 20 and DEGs were screened using a fold change (FC) of ≥ 2.0 or ≤ 0.5 and false discovery rate (FDR) of < 0.01 as the cutoff threshold.

For AS analysis, we used ABLas (v.0.2) program to systematically identify the AS events by calculating the splicing junction reads based on the mapping results. 21 We classified the AS events into ten types according to their splicing pattern. For HSPA8-regulated AS events (RASEs), we used Student’s t-test to calculate the difference and statistical significance of AS ratios between siHSPA8 and control groups. After that, a FDR < 0.05 and ratio difference > 0.15 were set as the thresholds for RASE prediction. The genes associated with RASEs were considered as HSPA8-regulated AS genes (RASGs).

Functional enrichment analysis

To sort out functional categories of genes, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) were identified using the clusterProfiler package (v.4.12.6). 22 Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the fgsea program (v1.16.0), 23 and the enriched canonical pathways were derived from the Reactome pathway database.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with at least three replicates per group. Student's t-test was used to analyze differences between groups, and p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

HSPA8 is highly expressed in TNBC and is closely associated with poor prognosis

To determine the expression pattern of HSPA8 in TNBC, we downloaded mRNA expression profiles of breast cancer patients from the TCGA-BRCA dataset, including 117 TNBC tissue samples, 1001 non-TNBC tissue samples, and 113 non-tumor tissue samples. Differential expression analysis revealed HSPA8 expression was significantly elevated in breast cancer (Figure 1(A)). Moreover, also investigated the expression of HSPA8 in various breast cancer cell lines in vitro, and found that compared with normal MCF10A breast cells, HSPA8 expression levels were significantly increased in breast cancer cells, with even higher expression in TNBC cell lines, particularly HCC38 (Figure 1(B)). These results suggest that HSPA8 overexpression may have a profound impact on TNBC progression. Additionally, based on Kaplan-Meier plotter analysis of TNBC patients, we observed that high HSPA8 expression was associated with a trend towards poorer overall survival (Figure 1(C)), suggesting a potential link with adverse clinical outcomes. HSPA8 promotes malignant progression of TNBC cells

To investigate the function of HSPA8 in TNBC cells, we selected the HCC38 cell line with the highest HSPA8 expression, and used HSPA8-specific siRNA to knockdown its endogenous expression in the cells. Western blot analysis confirmed that HSPA8 protein expression levels were significantly decreased by approximately 30%–40% in siHSPA8-treated cells (Figure 2(A)). The cytological results indicated that HSPA8 knockdown significantly inhibited HCC38 cell proliferation and induced apoptosis (Figure 2(B) and (C)). Meanwhile, the invasion and migration ability of HCC38 cells were also substantially impaired upon HSPA8 silencing (Figure 2(D), (E) and (F)). Molecular characteristics and functional pathways associated with HSPA8 expression

To overview the role of HSPA8 in the pathogenesis of TNBC, we additionally performed RNA-seq experiment to explore the downstream targets of HSPA8. After obtaining the expression levels of all genes, principal component analysis (PCA) showed a clear gene expression profile difference after HSPA8 knockdown (Figure 3(A)). In addition, a total of 587 DEGs were identified, of which 350 were up-regulated and 237 were down-regulated (Figure 3(B)). These DEGs exhibited high similarities within the same group but showed significant differences between groups (Figure 3(C)). To characterize the role of HSPA8 in TNBC cells, we carried out the GO analysis and the results showed that up-regulated DEGs were mainly enriched in extracellular matrix/structure organization, while down-regulated DEGs were significantly associated with multiple critical biological processes, including wound healing, positive regulation of cell adhesion, response to virus, regulation of leukocyte migration and epithelial cell differentiation (Figure 3(D) and (E)). To unveil the possible regulatory oncogenic pathways, we subsequently employed GSEA to analyze all expressed genes rather than just the DEGs. Based on the detailed pathway information provided by the Reactome database, we found that inhibiting HSPA8 could considerably activate ERBB2 and EGFR signaling in cancer and inhibit signaling pathways such as AKT1/E17K, NOTCH1, ALK, and WNT. Interestingly, although substantial inhibition of PI3K/AKT signaling was also observed, constitutive signaling by aberrant PI3K was activated (Figure 3(F)), likely reflecting the complexity of HSPA8 in regulating PI3K/AKT signal transduction. Notably, AKT phosphorylates targets in the cytosol was strikingly suppressed (Figure 3(G)), potentially representing the core mechanism by which HSPA8 interferes with the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. This suppression hindered the normal phosphorylation modification process of AKT on its downstream targets, and ultimately might have multiple inhibitory effects on the biological behaviors of cancer cells such as proliferation, survival, migration, and invasion.

HSPA8 modulated the AS profile associated with protein phosphorylation

Alternative splicing can alter the function of protein products and contributes to the progression of multiple cancers. 24 Thus, we systematically investigated the AS profile changes between siHSPA8 and NC samples. By using the ABLas program, 21 we identified hundreds of HSPA8-regulated AS events (RASEs) with p-value < 0.05 and ratio difference > 0.15 as thresholds (Figure 4(A)). Intron retention (IntronR), alternative 5′ splice sites (A5SS), and alternative 3′ splice sites (A3SS) were the top RASE types, suggesting that HSPA8 has the potential to modulate global AS profile. By analyzing the HSPA8-regulated AS genes (RASGs), we found that most RASGs contained only one RASE, while 175 RASGs also had more than one RASEs (Figure 4(B)). Meanwhile, we also analyzed the variation of splicing ratios in these two groups, and found that most RASE types showed similar standard deviation except the exon skipping (ES) and A3SS&ES (Figure 4(C)), indicating that HSPA8 can modulate the splicing ratio of ES events. Finally, we explored the enriched pathways for RASGs. Apoptosis, transcriptional regulation, and protein phosphorylation were enriched for GO biological process (BP) (Figure 4(D)), suggesting that the protein phosphorylation process, which is important in cancer progression, 25 may be changed by HSPA8 in TNBC cells. To validate the AS changes identified by RNA-seq, we selected two AS events in genes directly involved in PI3K/AKT signaling: an A3SS event in PPP2R2D and an A5SS event in MALAT1. RT-qPCR showed that HSPA8 knockdown significantly altered the splicing patterns of these two genes (Figure 4(E)). HSPA8 is involved in TNBC progression through activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling axis

Based on the above findings of the functional pathway enrichment analysis, we hypothesized that HSPA8 might play a key role in regulating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway by protein phosphorylation process, which encouraged us to further examine the effects of HSPA8 expression on the PI3K/AKT signaling axis. We first examined the expression levels of several genes that have been reported to induce PI3K/AKT activation, including PPP2R2D, CD38, RDH16, and IL4R. The results showed that HSPA8 knockdown significantly reduced their mRNA expression in tumor cells (Figure 5(A)), consistent with the RNA-seq data. Next, we focused on evaluating the activation status of the PI3K/AKT pathway using Western blot analysis. The results confirmed that HSPA8 knockdown significantly inhibited PI3K protein expression and phosphorylation, and also observed a significant suppression of phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT). The decreased ratio of phosphorylated protein/total protein further indicated a substantial downregulation of the activation level or functional status of this signaling axis (Figure 5(B)). Discussion

TNBC is a highly heterogeneous subtype of breast cancer, and is one of the most difficult malignancies for treatment due to the lack of effective clinical therapeutic targets. HSPA8 has emerged as a novel biomarker of tumor progression in many human cancers. 26 Our previous investigation showed that HSPA8 had a higher expression level in high-risk TNBC patients, suggesting its possible role in mediating adverse outcomes. 18 Furthermore, another study found that the absence of HSPA8 inhibits the growth of most tumor cells including TNBC and reduces cell invasion and migration capacity, 27 consistent with our findings. Overall, in the present study, we explored the important role and underlying mechanism of HSPA8 in TNBC pathogenesis, and suggest that HSPA8 promotes cancer cell growth and malignant progression, potentially through the regulation of downstream gene expression, alternative splicing, and particularly, the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, highlighting its promise as a novel therapeutic target in TNBC.

HSPA8 is a versatile molecular chaperone protein whose overexpression has been linked to diverse oncogenic activities and signaling pathways,28–30 often functioning as a cancer driver. Nevertheless, the downstream signaling pathways regulated by HSPA8 that maintain the tumorigenic potential of TNBC remain largely elusive. Recent effort has demonstrated that both HSPA8 and AKT form part of an important oncogenic pathway in breast cancer, and that targeting or interfering with this pathway is probably an effective strategy for improving breast cancer therapy efficiency. 31 The role of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in TNBC has been extensively studied, and it is widely recognized that activation of this pathway dramatically confers the ability to regulate TNBC cell growth, survival, proliferation, metabolism, angiogenesis and motility regulation, and is a compelling target for the development of novel anticancer therapies.32–34 Notably, we found that in TNBC cells, HSPA8 may inhibit the degradation of PI3K protein, and increases AKT phosphorylation, perhaps through regulating expression of other molecules across the PI3K/AKT axis, which in turn affects the activation of AKT pathway.

Meanwhile, we performed RNA-seq experiment and found that HSPA8 can profoundly regulate transcriptome profile, including gene expression and AS. For gene expression, we observed that siHSPA8 significantly regulated the expression levels of genes involved in PI3K/AKT pathway, indicating a possible explanation for HSPA8 in modulating TNBC progression. In addition, we observed that many AS events were enriched in genes involved in protein phosphorylation, which may represent an additional layer of regulation by HSPA8 beyond transcriptional control. These observations indicate that HSPA8 appears to have a greater effect on AKT, and as previously reported the regulation of AKT activity by HSPA8 may be an indirect consequence of compensatory increases via other critical mediators not necessarily dependent on upstream PI3K protein. 35 While we validated selected AS events, we acknowledge that comprehensive validation of all identified RASEs and the functional consequences of these splicing changes warrants further investigation, particularly their direct impact on protein phosphorylation and AKT activity.

In our study, we also observed that HSPA8 knockdown leads to the repression of key molecules such as PPP2R2D, CD38, RDH16, and IL4R, as well as splicing alterations in PPP2R2D and MALAT1, which are similarly known to be important regulators involved in the PI3K/AKT signaling axis.36–39 Besides, CD38 is also an immunosuppressive molecule, inhibition of which may elicit strong anti-tumor immunity that prevents tumor growth and metastasis. 40 Inhibition of PPP2R2D, a regulatory subunit of protein phosphatase 2A, markedly enhances cytotoxic function in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, and affects DNA repair efficiency, thereby suppressing tumorigenesis. 41 RDH16 encodes a retinol dehydrogenase involved in retinoic acid metabolism, and the high expression level of RDH16 has been significantly correlated with a high risk of developing breast cancer. 42 IL4R has been shown to be upregulated in many epithelial cancers, and its mediated signaling is essential for support tumor growth, with evidence suggesting that IL4R signaling could be a strong driving force for pro-metastatic phenotypes of TNBC. 43 Together, these results revealed the possible relationship between HSPA8 expression and the PI3K/AKT signaling axis associated with pro-tumor progression, as well as a potential mechanism for HSPA8 in TNBC development.

While this study provides novel insights into the role of HSPA8 in TNBC, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our mechanistic investigations primarily relied on in vitro experiments using a single TNBC cell line and a single siRNA construct, lacking validation from in vivo xenograft models or further clinical cohort analysis, which restricts the generalizability of our findings and a more comprehensive understanding of HSPA8 pathophysiological function within the intact tumor microenvironment. Future validation using subcutaneous or orthotopic xenografts of HCC38 or additional TNBC lines with stable HSPA8 knockdown, as well as patient-derived xenograft models that better recapitulate tumor heterogeneity, would be valuable to confirm the tumor-suppressive effects observed in vitro. Second, although RNA-seq data suggest that HSPA8 influences genes related to protein phosphorylation through the regulation of alternative splicing, this hypothesis has not been directly verified experimentally, for instance, via reporter-based splicing assays or proteomic analyses. Furthermore, the precise molecular targets through which HSPA8 regulates the PI3K/AKT signaling axis, particularly its direct clients as a molecular chaperone, remain incompletely elucidated. Third, although many HSPA8 inhibitors have been shown to exert some antitumor activity, only two molecules, 15-deoxyspergualin and P140 peptide, have been evaluated in patients, and both were developed primarily for autoimmune indications. 44 To date, no HSPA8-targeting drug has been approved for cancer therapy. This highlights a major translational gap: despite the well-established oncogenic functions of HSPA8 in TNBC and other solid tumors, the therapeutic potential of HSPA8 inhibition in cancer remains largely unexplored in the clinical setting. Finally, this study did not explore the potential crosstalk between HSPA8 and other known TNBC-related signaling pathways, such as Notch. These limitations highlight directions for future research, including validating the observed phenotypes in in vivo models, identifying direct substrates via protein interactome profiling, positioning HSPA8 within broader signaling networks, and ultimately evaluating the therapeutic efficacy of HSPA8-targeting agents.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings indicate that elevated HSPA8 expression in TNBC is associated with oncogenic phenotypes and that its knockdown suppresses PI3K/AKT signaling, correlating with inhibited cell growth and reduced malignant progression in vitro. These results contribute to a better understanding of the functional role and possible molecular regulatory mechanism of HSPA8 in TNBC, and also provide a strong rationale for repurposing or refining HSPA8-targeting strategies toward TNBC treatment. Future studies will elucidate more details about HSPA8 function and tumorigenesis and highlight its potential application in TNBC targeted therapy.
