Etiology of bloodstream infections in adult diabetic patients from a single tertiary hospital in southern Vietnam: a retrospective study
Authors: Le Buu Chau, Dang Van Tri, Vo Truong Quy, Ranjit Tiwari, Quynh Phan, Truong Thi My Kieu, Duong Van Tho, Le Minh Khoi, Nguyen Thi Nhu Quynh, Rohat Muhyadeen Haji, Nguyen Thanh Dung, Nguyen Tien Huy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDiabetes mellitus (DM) is a significant risk factor for the development of bloodstream infections (BSIs), particularly in settings with a high burden of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). However, data availability in countries with low to middle incomes remains scarce.ObjectivesTo describe the clinical characteristics, etiology, and AMR patterns of BSIs in patients with DM.MethodsAn observational study was conducted on 99 adult diabetic patients with confirmed BSIs admitted to a tertiary hospital in southern Vietnam from January 2022 to May 2024. Information about clinical, laboratory, and microbiological findings was collected from electronic medical records.ResultsThe median age was 60 years, and 55.6% were female. The most common foci of infection were urinary tract infections (19.2%) and deep abscesses (10.1%), while multiple infectious foci were identified in 16.2% of cases. Escherichia coli (53.5%), Klebsiella pneumoniae (15.2%), and Burkholderia pseudomallei (10.1%) were the predominant pathogens. Resistance to third-generation cephalosporins (3GCs) was observed in 64.7% of E. coli and 26.7% of K. pneumoniae isolates evaluated. Empirical antimicrobial treatment most commonly involved β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (51.5%) and carbapenems (33.3%). Intensive care was required for 20.2% of patients, and one fatality was reported.ConclusionsBSIs in diabetic patients in southern Vietnam are predominantly caused by gram-negative bacteria with a high rate of AMR, particularly to 3GCs. These findings highlight the need for early diagnosis, rational empirical therapy, and enhanced AMR monitoring to improve outcomes.

Introduction

Bloodstream infections (BSIs) are severe clinical conditions that may progress to sepsis and multi-organ failure [1, 2]. Estimates suggest that between 113 and 204 new cases of BSIs occur annually per 100,000 population in several developed countries [3]. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a growing global concern, with an estimated 1.27 million deaths directly attributable to drug-resistant BSIs [4].

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a major global health challenge and contributes to an increased risk of infection. Individuals with DM are 1.4 times more likely to develop BSIs compared with non-diabetic individuals [5]. Hyperglycemia and diabetes-related physiological changes impair host defenses, resulting in heightened susceptibility to infections [6].

Mortality rates among diabetic patients with BSIs range from 6.8% to 11.1% [7, 8]. Infections caused by antimicrobial-resistant pathogens have been associated with poorer outcomes in this population [8]. Furthermore, diabetic individuals overall have significantly higher odds of mortality compared with non-diabetic individuals (odds ratio [OR] = 1.91; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.76–2.06) [9].

Despite the evidence of increased risk, comprehensive data on the etiology and antimicrobial resistance patterns of BSIs in diabetic patients remain scarce, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, such as Vietnam. The purpose of this study was to describe the etiology and antimicrobial resistance patterns of BSIs in adult diabetic patients at a tertiary infectious diseases hospital in southern Vietnam.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

An observational retrospective study was performed at the Hospital for Tropical Diseases (HTD) in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam, from January 1, 2022 to May 31, 2024. HTD is a 550-bed tertiary care center specializing in infectious diseases and lacking surgical facilities. Most patients are admitted with febrile illnesses or are referred from other healthcare facilities in southern Vietnam for the evaluation and management of suspected or confirmed infectious diseases.

Criteria for selecting the participants were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) a confirmed diagnosis of bloodstream infection (BSI); and (3) a documented diagnosis of DM. Patients were excluded if they had suspected blood culture contamination [10] or a known HIV-positive status. Epidemiological data, clinical characteristics, laboratory findings, and microbiological results were extracted from electronic medical records.

Case definitions and laboratory procedures

BSIs were diagnosed when one or more organisms were isolated from at least one blood culture bottle and clinical signs of infection, such as fever (> 38 °C), chills, cough, or dysuria, were present. In cases, where patients experienced multiple episodes of BSIs during a single hospitalization, only the initial episode was considered for analysis. In addition, when patients had multiple hospital admissions with a confirmed diagnosis of BSIs during the study period, data from only their first admission were included in the analysis.

A pathogen was considered recognized if it did not match any organism on the National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) common commensal list [10]. Exceptions were made for organisms identified in both bottles of a blood culture set, or those isolated from a single bottle with clinical signs of infection, such as abscess or valvular vegetation.

DM was defined as a condition that met one of the two criteria: a pre-existing self-reported or previously documented diagnosis, or a newly diagnosed condition identified during the current hospital stay. All diagnoses were made according to the criteria issued by the Vietnam Ministry of Health [11] which are consistent with those recommended by the American Diabetes Association [12].

One set of blood cultures, consisting of one aerobic and one anaerobic bottle, was collected for each patient within the first 48 h of admission to HTD, with 8–10 mL of blood inoculated into each bottle. Bacterial identification was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization–time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI–TOF MS) on a Bruker instrument (USA). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was conducted utilizing either the VITEK 2 Compact system (bioMérieux, France) or the disk diffusion method. AMR was interpreted under the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [13, 14]. Not all antibiotics were tested for every isolate. Third-generation cephalosporin (3GC) resistance was defined as being not susceptible to at least one tested 3GC.

For specimens suspected of B. pseudomallei, enhanced Biosafety Level 2 (BSL-2 +) practices were implemented to mitigate the risk of exposure for laboratory workers, particularly in low-resource settings, such as Vietnam. BSL-2 + practices were defined as the implementation of Biosafety Level 3 procedures within a Biosafety Level 2 facility [15–17]. Cultures were placed in zip-lock bags to prevent cross-contamination. All manipulations involving aerosol generation (such as pipetting, streaking, and plating) were performed within a Class II Biological Safety Cabinet (BSC) equipped with a high-efficiency particulate air filter to ensure controlled airflow. Laboratory workers were provided with appropriate personal protective equipment, including N95 respirators, double gloves, fluid-resistant gowns, and protective goggles. Upon completion of each work session, the surface of the BSC was disinfected with 70% ethanol. All laboratory waste was sterilized using an autoclave to ensure complete inactivation of micro-organisms [16, 18, 19].

The foci of infection were determined clinically by the attending physician or inferred from microbiological evidence, defined as isolation of the same pathogen from another clinical specimen. The foci were categorized into the following groups: urinary tract infection, respiratory tract infection, deep abscess, skin and soft tissue infection, other, and unknown.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis was performed using categorical variables with frequency and percentage, while continuous variables were presented with mean and standard deviation (SD) or median, range and interquartile range (IQR) based on normality of data distribution. The Skewness and Kurtosis test was used for testing the normal distribution of continuous variables. Data management and statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17 (StataCorp LLC, USA).

Multivariable model selection for the 3GC resistance among 66 cases of monomicrobial BSIs caused by E. coli and K. pneumoniae was guided by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), which balances model fit and parsimony and is suitable for smaller sample sizes. Laboratory parameters were excluded from the multivariable modeling process, because they primarily reflect disease severity rather than underlying predictors of antimicrobial resistance. Therefore, only demographic characteristics, comorbidities, and relevant exposure history (such as antimicrobial use within the preceding 90 days) were retained as candidate predictors. Competing models were evaluated using the gvselect module in Stata, and based on AIC comparisons, the final multivariable model included age, gender, and antimicrobial use within 90 days. Model performance was assessed using McFadden’s R2, the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported, and a two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Multivariable logistic regression was conducted as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating analysis to assess potential associations between clinical factors and 3GC resistance, rather than to infer causality.

Ethical statement

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee for Biomedical Research of the Hospital for Tropical Diseases, Vietnam (Approval No. 3574/QD-BVBND, dated 1/11/2023). Since the study was a retrospective study based on existing medical records, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee. All data were anonymized before data analysis to ensure confidentiality and privacy.

Results

From January 1, 2022 to May 31, 2024, among 104 hospitalizations with positive blood cultures recorded from 101 diabetic patients at HTD, including three patients with two separate admissions each. Two patients were excluded: one with a coagulase-negative Staphylococcus isolate without a confirmed focus of infection, and one with an unidentified Streptococcus isolate. Three patients had repeat admissions during the study period; for these patients, only data from the first admission were included in the analysis. Antimicrobial resistance profiles of pathogen isolates

A total of 53 cases yielded E. coli isolates, and AST was performed for 51 of them. For the remaining two cases, AST could not be completed due to cost-related issues after the patient transfer. K. pneumoniae was isolated in 15 cases, all of which underwent AST. In total, 66 AST results were included in the analysis (Table 4). None of the E. coli isolates exhibited intermediate susceptibility to any of the tested 3GCs. Among the 15 K. pneumoniae isolates, one case was resistant to cefotaxime and showed intermediate susceptibility to ceftriaxone. As a result, 3GC-resistant E. coli was identified in 64.7% (33/51) of cases, while 3GC-resistant K. pneumoniae was detected in 26.7% (4/15) of cases. Notably, one E. coli isolate exhibited extensive resistance to cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, cefepime, levofloxacin, amoxicillin/clavulanate, piperacillin/tazobactam, ertapenem, and meropenem, but remained susceptible to gentamicin and imipenem. In the patient with a polymicrobial BSI involving E. coli and K. pneumoniae, the E. coli isolate was resistant to amoxicillin/clavulanate, cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, and gentamicin, exhibited intermediate susceptibility to levofloxacin, but remained susceptible to piperacillin/tazobactam and all tested carbapenems. Empirical antimicrobial treatment and clinical outcomes

The most commonly used empirical antimicrobial agents were β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, administered to 51.5% of patients (51/99), with piperacillin/tazobactam accounting for the majority (49/51). This was followed by carbapenems (33.3%; 33/99) and ceftriaxone (10.1%; 10/99).

During hospitalization, 20.2% (20/99) of patients were transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU). One critically ill patient was discharged against medical advice and subsequently died. In addition, nine patients were transferred to other hospitals for drainage procedures or surgical interventions; clinical outcome data following transfer were unavailable.

The fatal case involved a 31-year-old female who had been newly diagnosed with type 2 DM approximately 4 months prior, but had not initiated treatment. On the second day of illness, she was transferred to the ICU exhibiting fever, dyspnea, and hypotension. Septic shock was the initial diagnosis, and empirical treatment with piperacillin/tazobactam was promptly initiated. Chest X-ray revealed right lung consolidation, while urinalysis was unremarkable and urine culture was negative. Blood cultures yielded K. pneumoniae, which was susceptible to all tested antimicrobials. Despite appropriate antimicrobial therapy, the patient developed severe respiratory failure requiring endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation. She also developed acute kidney injury and metabolic acidosis, necessitating continuous renal replacement therapy. Her pneumonia was further complicated by bilateral pneumothorax, for which chest tube insertion was performed. After 26 days of intensive care without clinical improvement, the family elected to withdraw treatment. The patient was discharged upon request and subsequently died.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis of factors associated with 3GC resistance among 66 cases of monomicrobial BSIs caused by E. coli and K. pneumoniae

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis among 66 cases of monomicrobial BSIs caused by E. coli and K. pneumoniae, an increase in age (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 1.05, 95% CI 1.00–1.10; p = 0.040) and the utilization of antimicrobial agents within the previous 90 days (aOR = 15.92, 95% CI 1.57–161.40; p = 0.019) were independently associated with 3GC-resistant isolation. Gender showed a non-significant inverse association (aOR = 0.37, 95% CI 0.11–1.20; p = 0.098). The model’s AIC was 82.39, and it demonstrated a good overall fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow χ2 = 8.49; p = 0.39), acceptable discrimination (AUROC = 0.75), and moderate explanatory power (McFadden’s R2 = 0.18).

Discussion

Patients in this study demonstrated an age distribution skewed toward individuals over 50 years, and a predominance of female participants. It is estimated that over 246 million adults aged 30 years and older are living with DM in the Southeast Asia region. A meta-analysis estimated the prevalence of DM and systemic hypertension in Vietnam to be 6.0% and 25.0%, respectively [20]. Consistent with these findings, systemic hypertension was the most common comorbidity, present in 45.5% of patients.

Furthermore, 16.2% and 20.2% of patients had a history of hospitalization and antimicrobial use within the preceding 90 days, respectively. These proportions appear to be broadly comparable to those reported by Lee et al., with corresponding rates of 15.7% and 10.0% [21]. Indeed, patients with DM are prone to infectious complications [6], which may result in more frequent exposure to antimicrobials.

Metformin was found to be the most commonly prescribed antidiabetic agent prior to admission, reported in 75.8% of patients, followed by sulfonylureas and insulin. This treatment pattern is consistent with both international and national guidelines, which recommend metformin as the first-line treatment for patients without acute hyperglycemic complications [22].

A meta-analysis done by Liang et al. demonstrated a significant association between prior metformin use and reduced mortality among diabetic patients (OR = 0.59; 95% CI 0.43–0.79; p = 0.001). However, the limitation of this analysis was the lack of data regarding the proportion of patients with microbiologically confirmed BSIs [23]. Although previous studies have explored potential associations between antidiabetic therapy and clinical outcomes, this study did not evaluate antidiabetic medications as predictors of antimicrobial resistance; therefore, no conclusions can be drawn regarding their role in 3GC resistance. In our cohort, patients were using metformin, sulfonylureas, or insulin prior to hospitalization; however, we did not assess whether baseline health status or prior hospitalization risk differed across these groups. This may introduce confounding, as metformin is contraindicated in patients with severe renal impairment (estimated glomerular filtration rate < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2) [24]. Therefore, individuals who are able to take metformin generally have better baseline renal function and overall clinical stability compared with those requiring insulin therapy. In addition, several studies have suggested that metformin exhibits weak antimicrobial activity and may enhance bacterial susceptibility to agents such as tetracyclines through inhibition of efflux pumps and increased intracellular antibiotic concentration. Nevertheless, we were unable to identify evidence supporting a mechanistic link between metformin use and reduced resistance to 3GCs. Thus, without ensuring comparability of baseline health status between treatment groups, no firm conclusion can be drawn regarding the potential protective effect of metformin against 3GC resistance in this study.

The median blood glucose level on admission was 252 mg/dL (IQR: 182–368). Patients with DM are known to be more susceptible to BSIs [5]. Conversely, infections can exacerbate hyperglycemia through various mechanisms, including increased secretion of stress hormones, elevated production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and direct invasion and destruction of pancreatic beta cells [6]. Several investigations have explored the relationship between admission blood glucose levels and mortality in septic patients; however, existing literature does not support an association [25, 26].

The Sysmex platform provides the immature platelet fraction, immature granulocyte count, and the Intensive Care Infection Score, all of which have shown potential utility in early detection of sepsis [27]. More recently, Beckman Coulter analyzers have introduced the monocyte distribution width, which has demonstrated good diagnostic performance for sepsis in emergency and critical-care settings [28]. Although these biomarkers were not available for most patients in this study, their wider adoption in Vietnam could facilitate earlier identification of bloodstream infections among diabetic patients and warrants further evaluation in future research.

In this study, urinary tract infections and deep abscesses represented the predominant foci of infection. Similarly, de Laroche et al. reported that urinary tract infections accounted for as many as 60.8% of cases, while intra-abdominal infections represented only 7.1% [29]. In contrast, a study focusing exclusively on K. pneumoniae BSIs found that intra-abdominal infections were the most common, accounting for 33.6% of cases [30]. These findings suggest that the frequency of infection foci may vary considerably across studies, likely influenced by the underlying microbiological etiology. Moreover, this may be partially explained by the predominance of female patients, compounded by the focus of this study on individuals with DM, a population known to be at elevated risk for urinary tract infections [6].

To date, blood culture remains the standard procedure for pathogen identification in patients with suspected BSIs. However, the diagnostic yield of this method is influenced by multiple factors, among which obtaining an adequate blood volume per culture bottle is considered the most critical [31]. In routine clinical practice, the collection of two or three blood culture sets is commonly recommended [32]. Lee et al. reported BSI detection rates of 75.5% and 86.6% when one and two culture sets were obtained, respectively, corresponding to an absolute gain of 11.1% of diagnostic sensitivity [33]. Conversely, a multivariable analysis by Lin et al. suggested that the number of blood culture sets was not independently associated with culture positivity (OR = 0.74, 95% CI 0.50–1.12) [34].

With an overall blood culture positivity rate of approximately 10.0% [35, 36] and an estimated cost of USD 25 per blood culture set, achieving an absolute sensitivity gain of 11.1% would require about 100 additional blood culture sets. This corresponds to an incremental expenditure of USD 2500 to identify one additional BSI case. In comparison, Vietnam’s annual gross domestic product per capita in 2024 was roughly USD 4700 [37]. Collectively, these findings suggest that routinely performing additional blood culture sets may not be a cost-effective strategy in this resource-limited setting.

This study revealed a prevalence of E. coli and K. pneumoniae to be 53.5% and 15.2%, respectively. This finding is consistent with several previous studies [29, 38]. However, other studies have reported S. aureus as the predominant pathogen [8, 39]. The distribution of micro-organisms may be affected by the underlying characteristics of the study population. Reported risk factors for S. aureus BSIs include HIV infection, particularly among intravenous drug users, and patients receiving hemodialysis [40].

In addition, B. pseudomallei (10 cases) and S. suis (1 case) were also identified in this study. This finding is consistent with previous data from an observational study in northern Vietnam [38]. In contrast, most other studies did not report the presence of these two pathogens [8, 29]. These discrepancies may be attributed to regional differences in epidemiological patterns, geographic distribution, and exposure risks [2]. According to Gassiep et al., cases of B. pseudomallei infection, also known as melioidosis, are primarily found in tropical and subtropical regions [41]. In Vietnam, the reported isolation rate of B. pseudomallei ranges from 3.4% to 10.2% [42]. In this study, this rate was 10.1%, which is higher than in some previous reports, possibly due to the study population consisting of diabetic patients, one of the major risk factors for melioidosis.

This study found a relatively high prevalence of 3GC-resistant Enterobacterales, with resistance rates of 64.7% in E. coli and 26.7% in K. pneumoniae. Reported rates of 3GC-resistant E. coli in other studies vary widely, ranging from 19.7% to 91.0%, depending on the study population and geographical setting [43–45]. In the United Kingdom, a prevalence of 13.4% was reported for 3GC-resistant Klebsiella spp. [46]. These findings suggest that 3GC resistance is generally more prevalent in E. coli than in K. pneumoniae. Nonetheless, the prevalence of 3GC resistance in this study may have been underestimated due to variability in 3GC susceptibility testing related to routine laboratory workflows.

In addition to Enterobacterales, S. aureus also posed a significant resistance concern. Among seven S. aureus isolates, six (85.7%) were identified as MRSA, indicating a high burden of resistance in gram-positive pathogens as well. In comparison, MRSA accounted for 27.8% to 31.6% of S. aureus BSIs [29, 38]. This discrepancy is likely due to this study focusing specifically on patients with DM, a population that may carry a higher burden of resistant pathogens. In summary, these findings emphasize the importance of implementing antimicrobial stewardship programs and selecting empiric antimicrobial regimens that are informed by local resistance patterns to improve clinical outcomes.

This study reported one fatal case of BSI caused by drug-susceptible K. pneumoniae. Several risk factors for in-hospital mortality among patients with K. pneumoniae BSIs were identified by Roach et al., including ICU admission, hematologic malignancy, respiratory tract infection, and inappropriate initial antimicrobial therapy [30]. The fatal case in this study shared some of these risk factors, as the patient was transferred directly to the ICU and had pneumonia as the identified focus of infection. Despite receiving appropriate empirical antimicrobial treatment, the patient's clinical condition progressively deteriorated and did not improve.

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis of 66 cases of monomicrobial BSIs caused by E. coli and K. pneumoniae, increasing age and prior exposure to antimicrobial agents within the preceding 90 days were found to be independently associated with the isolation of 3GC-resistant organisms. These findings appear to be consistent with previous reports. According to Huang et al., higher age (aOR = 1.016, 95% CI 1.003–1.029; p = 0.015) was significantly associated with the risk of infection due to antimicrobial-resistant strains after adjustment for serum creatinine and albumin levels [8]. Although the association with age was relatively modest, it is biologically plausible and may partially reflect the cumulative effects of healthcare exposure, comorbidity burden, and age-related alterations in host immunity and microbiota composition [47, 48].

In addition, this study found that recent antimicrobial use was associated with 3GC resistance (aOR = 15.92, 95% CI 1.57–161.40; p = 0.019). This observation also seems to support previous findings by Chopra et al., who reported that prior exposure to antibiotics such as cefepime or β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations was associated with an increased risk of BSIs caused by extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing pathogens [6, 49]. Indeed, inappropriate antimicrobial use—such as incorrect indications and suboptimal adherence to treatment regimens—may generate selective pressure that potentially contributes to the emergence of novel resistance mechanisms and facilitates the spread of existing resistance determinants across bacterial populations in environmental, animal, and human reservoirs [50]. This finding emphasizes the importance of carefully considering prior antibiotic exposure when selecting empirical therapy.

This study presents several limitations. Its retrospective, single-center design may restrict the generalizability of the findings and potentially introduce biases related to information collection and referral processes. The limited sample size, particularly concerning the analysis of 3GC resistance, diminishes the statistical power and results in less precise estimates of effect. AST was not performed uniformly across all isolates, and the resistance mechanisms were not characterized through molecular methods. Moreover, there was a lack of detailed data regarding illness severity, glycemic control, and the appropriateness of prior antimicrobial therapy, which may contribute to residual confounding. In addition, clinical data following transfer were not collected, potentially limiting the comprehensive assessment of patient outcomes. To validate and expand upon these findings within similar tropical environments, prospective multicenter studies employing standardized microbiological and molecular techniques are necessary.

Conclusions

Gram-negative bacteria, notably E. coli and K. pneumoniae, were the predominant pathogens observed in diabetic patients with BSIs at a tertiary care center specializing in infectious diseases in southern Vietnam. A high prevalence of AMR was observed, with 3GC-resistant E. coli being particularly notable. Empirical antimicrobial treatment primarily involved β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations and carbapenems. The frequent requirement for intensive care and the occurrence of severe complications, including one fatal case, emphasize the significant clinical burden of BSIs in this high-risk population. Factors such as increasing age and recent antimicrobial use appeared to be independently associated with 3GC-resistant strains in diabetic patients with BSIs caused by E. coli and K. pneumoniae. The findings of this research provide insights into the crucial need for early diagnosis, timely and appropriate empirical antimicrobial therapy, and ongoing surveillance of local AMR patterns to enhance clinical outcomes in diabetic patients with BSIs.
