Single-cell long- and short-read transcriptomics sheds light on high-altitude adaptation in the porcine cerebral cortex
Authors: Yongcheng Chang, Bofang Duan, Hailong Huo, Xia Zhang, Wan Lin, Feidi Wen, Yiming Guo, Lingxiang Wu, Fengcai Zou, Jinlong Huo, Guiying Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe cerebral cortex is highly sensitive to oxygen availability and thus serves as an ideal model for studying neural adaptation to hypoxia. Here, we integrated long-read (Oxford Nanopore) and short-read (Illumina) single-cell RNA sequencing to systematically characterize the cellular composition and transcriptional landscape of the cerebral cortex in high-altitude Diqing Tibetan pigs (DT, ~3200 m) and low-altitude Diannan small-ear pigs (DSE, ~500 m). Single-cell analysis identified nine major cortical cell types and revealed pronounced transcriptional divergence between the breeds. In DT, multiple genes associated with high-altitude hypoxia adaptation and energy homeostasis, including NKAIN2, CTNNA3, ZFP36, and HSP40 family members, were significantly upregulated. Functional enrichment highlighted mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and energy metabolism pathways, suggesting metabolic reprogramming under chronic hypoxia. Key transcription factors, including SOX17, NKX6-2, ETV4, and SPI1, displayed cell-type-specific activity in Endo, Oligo, and Micro. Cell-cell communication analysis showed DSE with broader intercellular connectivity, whereas DT exhibited more focused and stronger signaling interactions. Integration of long-read sequencing further identified numerous novel, cell-type-specific transcripts, expanding porcine cortical transcriptome annotation. Collectively, these findings provide a comprehensive framework for understanding cellular and molecular adaptations of the cerebral cortex to prolonged high-altitude hypoxia.Subject terms: Molecular neuroscience, Genomics

Introduction

The cerebral cortex, constituting one of the most intricate and evolutionarily advanced structures of the mammalian brain, serves as the principal substrate for higher-order cognitive functions, including language, perception, decision-making, learning, and motor planning. At the cellular level, the human cerebral cortex contains approximately 16 billion neurons, accounting for over 80% of the brain’s total neuronal complement, thereby underscoring its structural predominance [1]. Architecturally stratified and functionally specialized, the cortex exhibits a precisely organized, multilaminar configuration, which furnishes the neuroanatomical foundation for the diverse cognitive and behavioral capacities of the brain [2]. Cortical development and functional specialization are orchestrated through the dynamic integration of intrinsic genetic programs and extrinsic signaling cues, processes that collectively shape neuronal identity, connectivity, and circuit assembly [3]. Moreover, the evolutionary expansion and gyrification of the mammalian cortex, being tightly coupled to escalating circuit complexity [4], underscore its central role in cognition and behavioral regulation [5].

The porcine brain shares remarkable similarities with the human brain in terms of anatomy, developmental trajectory, and physiology, particularly with respect to brain volume, cortical lamination, and the proportion of white matter to cortex, surpassing traditional laboratory rodents such as mice and rats in these aspects [6–8]. Moreover, the extended period of brain development in pigs, including neuronal migration and synaptic maturation, closely parallels that of humans [9], conferring distinct advantages for investigating human cognitive functions, neurodegenerative disorders, and neural adaptive mechanisms. Notably, in studies of high-altitude hypoxia adaptation, pigs offer a unique translational model due to their large body size and metabolic profile, which more faithfully recapitulate the systemic effects of hypobaric hypoxia on brain tissue [10, 11]. Consequently, utilizing pigs as an experimental model not only facilitates detailed analyses of neural structure and function but also provides a robust and translatable platform for studying mechanisms underlying high-altitude hypoxia adaptation. The Diannan small-ear pig (DSE) and the Diqing Tibetan pig (DT), both indigenous to Yunnan Province, China, inhabit the southern lowlands (~500 m) and the northwestern high-altitude plateau (~3200 m), respectively. Adapted to hot and humid conditions, the DSE is distinguished by tender meat quality, substantial adipose deposition, and pronounced environmental resilience [12, 13]. By contrast, the DT exhibits enhanced cardiopulmonary capacity and remarkable tolerance to high-altitude hypoxia [14, 15]. Having undergone prolonged natural selection under sharply contrasting ecological pressures, these two indigenous pig breeds have evolved distinct adaptive strategies, thereby furnishing a powerful comparative framework for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying neural adaptation to high-altitude hypoxia.

High-altitude hypoxia exerts widespread effects on mammalian brain tissue, including impaired oxygen supply leading to dysregulated energy metabolism, altered angiogenesis, changes in neuronal excitability, and elevated oxidative stress [16, 17]. At the molecular level, hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) are recognized as central regulators of the cellular response to low oxygen, modulating processes such as angiogenesis, glycolysis, and cell survival [18]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the expression of certain HIF-related genes in the brains of high-altitude-adapted animals is altered to accommodate limited oxygen availability [19]. Furthermore, high-altitude hypoxia may influence intercellular communication between neurons and glial cells, synaptic plasticity, and myelination, thereby potentially affecting cognitive function and behavior [20]. Despite the anatomical and functional advantages of the porcine brain, its cortical cellular heterogeneity, transcriptomic architecture, and intercellular communication networks under high-altitude hypoxia remain poorly characterized at single-cell resolution, limiting mechanistic insights [21]. Single-cell RNA sequencing offers a powerful approach to address this gap.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has emerged as a transformative technology for resolving cellular heterogeneity within complex tissues. In contrast to conventional bulk RNA sequencing (bulk RNA-seq), which aggregates transcriptional signals across heterogeneous cell populations and obscures intercellular variability, scRNA-seq enables high-resolution transcriptomic profiling at the single-cell level [22–25]. By delineating discrete cellular subpopulations and reconstructing their cell-type-specific transcriptional programs, scRNA-seq has substantially refined our understanding of tissue architecture and functional specialization [26]. Currently, mainstream short-read platforms, exemplified by Illumina sequencing, deliver high accuracy and throughput; however, they are limited in their ability to fully resolve transcript isoforms and alternative splicing events [27]. In contrast, long-read technologies, such as Oxford Nanopore sequencing, directly capture full-length transcripts, thereby enabling more precise characterization of isoforms and transcript variants [28, 29]. Consequently, integrating short- and long-read sequencing strategies within a unified single-cell framework not only quantifies differential gene expression with high fidelity but also elucidates splicing complexity and transcript architectural diversity, thereby providing a more comprehensive and structurally resolved molecular landscape for functional studies of the cerebral cortex.

In this study, we aimed to investigate cortical adaptation to high-altitude hypoxia by integrating long- and short-read single-cell RNA sequencing in pigs. Specifically, we sought to characterize cortical cell populations and major cell types, examine transcriptional differences between lowland and high-altitude populations, explore potential alterations in cell–cell communication, and improve the annotation of the porcine cortical transcriptome. This work is intended to provide a comprehensive cellular and molecular framework for understanding high-altitude hypoxia adaptation in the cerebral cortex.

Materials and methods

Animals

Cerebral cortex samples were collected from 18-month-old female DT and DSE obtained from the Diqing Tibetan Pig Breeding Farm in Xianggelila and the Diannan Small-ear Pig Breeding Farm in Xishuangbanna, Yunnan Province, China, respectively. For each breed, three biological replicates (n = 3 per group), each representing an individual animal, were included in this study. Standard veterinary surgical procedures were performed to collect cerebral cortex specimens. After removal of the meningeal tissue, the middle region of the cerebral cortex was transversely sectioned to obtain small samples of cortical parenchyma. The tissue samples were preserved at 4 °C in MACS Tissue Storage Solution (Miltenyi Biotec, Cologne, NW, Germany) for subsequent cell isolation.

Single-cell suspension preparation

Single-cell suspensions from the cerebral cortex of DT and DSE (Sus scrofa) were prepared using the Miltenyi Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Cortical tissues were washed twice with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) to remove residual storage solution and then minced into ~2-3 mm³ fragments. Enzymatic dissociation was performed under mild conditions following the manufacturer’s protocol. The resulting cell suspension was passed through a 40 μm nylon cell strainer and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The cell pellet was resuspended in ice-cold complete medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin). To remove red blood cells, the suspension was incubated with Red Blood Cell Lysis Buffer (Miltenyi Biotec) on ice (4 °C) for 10 min. Cell counting was performed using a Countess II FL Automated Cell Counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) after mixing with trypan blue. The proportion of cell aggregates was automatically calculated by setting the cell size range (4-30 μm) and circularity threshold (0.7-0.9), ensuring that the aggregation rate remained below 5% (4.5% in this study) for single-cell resolution. Cell viability was assessed using the trypan blue exclusion method, with a viability of 98.0%. Finally, dead cells with compromised membranes were removed using the Dead Cell Removal Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Single-cell RNA-seq library construction

Single-cell RNA-seq libraries were generated from cerebral cortex-derived single-cell suspensions of DT and DSE (Sus scrofa) using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v3.1 (10× Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cell viability (>90%) and concentration (800-1200 cells/μL) were assessed using 0.4% trypan blue exclusion and a Countess II Automated Cell Counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cell suspension, reaction mixture, and barcoded gel beads were co-encapsulated into Gel Bead-in-Emulsions (GEMs) using the Chromium Controller (10× Genomics). Following GEM generation, cell lysis was performed to release RNA, and residual enzymes and primers were removed by incubation with Dynabeads MyOne SILANE magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at room temperature for 5 min. The amplified cDNA libraries were quantified using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with a High Sensitivity DNA Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Illumina single-cell sequencing

Single-cell transcriptome libraries derived from the cerebral cortex of DT and DSE (Sus scrofa) were subjected to short-read sequencing on the Illumina platform. Raw sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger (v7.1.0, 10× Genomics) for adapter trimming and quality control. Trimmed FASTQ files were aligned to the pig reference genome (Sscrofa11.1, Ensembl release 112) to assess mapping accuracy, read quality, and gene expression levels. Low-quality cells, unmapped reads, multi-mapped reads, and PCR duplicates were further filtered out. Gene transcript counts were quantified based on unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) and cell barcodes, providing the basis for downstream gene expression and functional analyses.

Nanopore single-cell sequencing

Quality-controlled cDNA amplification products were subjected to end repair and 3′ adenylation using the NEBNext Ultra II kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) for Oxford Nanopore single-cell sequencing. Sequencing adapters were ligated using the SQK-LSK114 ligation sequencing kit (v1.0.3; Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK), followed by purification with AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) to remove excess adapters. The final library was quantified using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and then loaded onto a PromethION R10.4.1 flow cell (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) for real-time single-molecule sequencing.

Illumina short-read data preprocessing

Raw Illumina short-read sequencing data were processed using the Cell Ranger software package (v8.0.1; 10x Genomics). The raw sequencing files (FASTQ format) were mapped to the Sus scrofa reference genome (Sscrofa11.1, Ensembl release 114) through the cellranger count pipeline. This workflow included several steps carried out in sequence, such as barcode and UMI identification, alignment of reads to the reference genome using the STAR spliced aligner, and construction of a gene-cell UMI count matrix. The pipeline ultimately generated filtered feature-barcode matrices, which were subsequently used for all downstream analyses.

Nanopore long-read data preprocessing

Nanopore long-read sequencing data were preprocessed using the FLAMES pipeline. Cell barcodes and UMIs were identified and assigned to long reads using the match_cell_barcode module, which enables accurate barcode and UMI detection. Transcriptome analysis was subsequently performed with the sc_long_pipeline.py module, which aligns reads to the Sus scrofa reference genome (Sscrofa11.1, Ensembl release 114) using Minimap2, and detects and quantifies transcript isoforms according to splice site compatibility. The pipeline ultimately produced a cell-by-transcript count matrix that was used for downstream analyses.

Cell clustering and annotation

To annotate and characterize cell populations in the cerebral cortex of DT and DSE (Sus scrofa), cell clustering and annotation analyses were performed using Seurat (v4.3.0) [30]. The raw count matrix was first subjected to quality-control filtering to remove low-quality or abnormal cells, using commonly applied single-cell criteria, including cells with abnormally high mitochondrial gene expression or unusually low numbers of detected genes, as well as genes with extremely low expression across cells. Potential doublets or multiplets were subsequently identified and removed using scDblFinder (v1.16.0) [31] to improve the dataset’s reliability. The filtered gene expression matrix was then normalized using the NormalizeData function in Seurat, followed by the identification of highly variable genes for downstream dimensionality reduction and clustering analyses. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using RunPCA, and dimensionality reduction was conducted with RunUMAP. Cell-cell neighborhood relationships were calculated using FindNeighbors, and cell clusters were subsequently identified with FindClusters. Marker genes for each cluster were determined using FindAllMarkers. Finally, functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was performed using clusterProfiler (v4.6.0) to investigate the biological functions of distinct cerebral cortex cell populations.

Correlation analysis among cell types

To assess transcriptional similarity between cell types across samples, average gene expression profiles were calculated using the AverageExpression function in Seurat by grouping cells according to sample identity and annotated cell type. Genes shared between the DSE and DT datasets were retained, and pairwise correlations between cell types across the two samples were computed based on their average expression profiles using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. The resulting correlation matrix was visualized as a heatmap using the pheatmap package, with correlation coefficients displayed in each cell.

Differential expression and functional enrichment analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between DSE and DT were identified for each cell type using the FindMarkers function implemented in Seurat (v4.3.0) [32]. For each cell type, significantly upregulated and downregulated genes were ranked by absolute log2 fold change, and the top five genes in each category were selected for downstream visualization. Functional enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler R package. Both Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method. Enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways with adjusted p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. This strategy enabled the identification of cell-type-specific transcriptional signatures and provided insights into the biological processes underlying high-altitude hypoxia adaptation in the cerebral cortex.

Gene regulatory network analysis

To characterize transcription factor–mediated gene regulatory relationships in the porcine cerebral cortex and to explore potential regulatory differences between DT and DSE, gene regulatory networks (GRNs) were constructed using pySCENIC, mapping porcine genes to their human orthologs. The analysis comprised three main steps. First, during GRN inference, co-expression relationships between transcription factors (TFs) and candidate target genes were calculated to construct a TF–target interaction network, which was then used to identify potential regulatory modules. Second, in the motif enrichment analysis step, co-expression modules were refined using RcisTarget by assessing TF-binding motif enrichment, retaining only TF-target relationships supported by DNA-binding evidence to reduce false positives. Finally, regulon activity was quantified at the single-cell level using AUCell. AUCell computes an area-under-the-curve (AUC) score to evaluate the enrichment of each regulon’s target genes within a cell-specific ranked expression profile, generating a regulon × cell AUC matrix for subsequent visualization and comparative analyses.

Inference of cell-cell communication

To characterize how intercellular signaling interactions contribute to functional differences between DT and DSE, we applied CellChat (v1.6.1) to infer ligand-receptor interactions among annotated cell populations. Porcine genes were first converted to human orthologs to enable the use of the curated human interaction database. Separate communication networks were constructed for DSE and DT groups based on normalized single-cell expression data. Significant ligand-receptor pairs were identified, and interaction probability and strength were quantified. Communication networks were analyzed at both the interaction and signaling pathway levels, and information flow analysis was performed to compare pathway activity between groups. Sender-receiver analysis further evaluated the relative signaling output and input capacities of each cell type.

Isoform detection and quantification

To accurately resolve transcript isoform diversity and capture full-length transcript structures associated with molecular differences between DT and DSE, BLZAE (v2.0.7) was used to identify adapter and barcode positions, generating reliable barcodes [33]. To ensure precise assignment of barcodes and unique molecular identifiers (UMIs), barcode-UMI sequences from long-read data were cross-validated and corrected based on cell annotations derived from short-read data. Validated reads were aligned to the pig reference genome (Sscrofa11.1) using minimap2 (v2.24), then polished and assembled into consensus isoforms. Redundant isoforms were removed, and degraded 5′ isoforms were filtered using cDNA-Cupcake, yielding a high-quality set of full-length isoforms suitable for downstream analyses.

Isoform classification and filtering

To systematically classify transcript isoforms and ensure data quality for comparative analysis between DT and DSE (Sus scrofa), SQANTI3 (v5.2.0) was used for isoform classification and quality control, with the identified isoforms compared against the reference genome [34]. SQANTI3 classified isoforms and extracted relevant classification information, while ComplexUpset (v1.3.3) was applied to visualize isoform distributions across different cell types. Based on the classification results, isoforms were categorized as follows: full splice match (FSM), with splice junctions fully matching reference annotations; incomplete splice match (ISM), containing only a subset of reference splice junctions; novel in catalog (NIC), representing new combinations of known splice sites; novel not in catalog (NNC), containing at least one novel splice site; and fusion isoforms, spanning multiple gene loci. To ensure structural integrity, isoforms with noncanonical splice sites, potential nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) targets, or 3′ ends deviating by more than 100 bp from annotated polyadenylation sites were removed. Following this stringent filtering, a high-confidence isoform catalog was obtained for downstream functional analyses.

Results

Single-cell transcriptomic landscape of the cerebral cortex across distinct pig breeds

To systematically characterize the cellular composition and molecular features of the cerebral cortex in DSE and DT, we performed high-resolution single-cell transcriptomic profiling using the 10× Genomics platform (Fig. 1a). Following stringent quality control, normalization, data integration, and dimensionality reduction implemented using Seurat v4.3.0, we obtained a robust and high-quality single-cell transcriptomic dataset. The integrated UMAP projection revealed a substantial overlap between DSE and DT cell distributions (Fig. 1b), thereby confirming effective batch correction and seamless data integration. We classified all cells using unsupervised clustering and visualized them with Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Thirteen distinct clusters were identified according to their unique transcriptional profiles (Supplementary Figure 1a). Given that comprehensive cell-type specific markers for the porcine cerebral cortex remain incompletely defined, cluster annotation was performed by mapping porcine protein-coding genes to their human homologs and by integrating curated marker information from related mammalian cerebral cortex single-cell studies [21, 35–40] and the Human Protein Atlas, thereby enhancing annotation confidence. Based on this integrative strategy and widely accepted marker genes, we divided these cell populations into nine canonical cell types, including microglia (Micro), endothelial cells (Endo), oligodendrocytes (Oligo), astrocytes (Astro), smooth muscle cells (SMC), inhibitory neurons (In), pericytes (Peri), excitatory neurons (Ex), and oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) (Fig. 1c). The proportional distributions of these cell populations across breeds are illustrated in Fig. 1d, with detailed statistics provided in Supplementary Table S1. Notably, Micro predominated in both breeds, followed by Oligo and Endo. DT exhibited a modestly increased Micro fraction, whereas DSE displayed relatively higher proportions of Endo and Astro, thereby suggesting subtle yet discernible breed-specific differences in cellular composition. Absolute cell counts across clusters and annotated cell types further corroborated these proportional shifts, confirming inter-breed heterogeneity at the cellular level (Supplementary Figure 1b-c). To evaluate transcriptional conservation, we computed pairwise gene expression correlations between corresponding cell types in DSE and DT (Fig. 1e; Supplementary Table S2). Remarkably, correlation coefficients exceeded 0.95 for all matched cell types, indicating a high degree of global similarity in transcriptional profiles between the two breeds. Notably, this high global correlation reflects overall similarity in expression patterns, whereas differential expression analysis highlights localized, gene-level variations that may contribute to functional divergence. Conserved expression patterns of canonical cell-type-specific marker genes are summarized in Fig. 1f and Supplementary Table S3, whereas representative markers defining each major cell type are depicted in Fig. 1g. Taken together, we establish the first single-cell-resolution atlas of the cerebral cortex in two indigenous pig breeds, demonstrating that despite quantitative differences in cellular abundance, the overall cellular composition and transcriptional programs remain highly conserved across breeds, underscoring the intrinsic molecular stability of the porcine cerebral cortex.

Cortical cell-type transcriptional divergence in DSE and DT

To delineate molecular divergence in the cerebral cortex between DSE and DT, we systematically compared cell-type-specific transcriptional profiles across major cortical cell types, revealing pronounced breed-specific regulatory features within the nervous system. The top ten DEGs per cell type, identified using the FindMarkers function, are summarized in a volcano plot (Fig. 2a), highlighting the cellular heterogeneity of the cerebral cortex. Notably, representative upregulated genes in DT included NKAIN2, CTNNA3, ZFP36, and HSP40, whereas TPT1, SLC39A10, MYH11, and PRKG1 were among the prominently downregulated genes compared with DSE. The complete list of DEGs for each cell type is provided in Supplementary Table S4.

KEGG pathway analysis revealed that genes upregulated in the DT cerebral cortex were preferentially enriched in oxidative phosphorylation entries, as well as neurodegenerative disease-related entries, including Huntington’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, prion disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Additional enriched entries included chemical carcinogenesis—reactive oxygen species and diabetic cardiomyopathy. In contrast, downregulated genes were primarily enriched in immune, inflammatory, and infection-related entries, as well as entries involved in cellular signaling and endocytic processes, such as chemokine signaling, T cell receptor signaling, and endocytosis (Fig. 2b; Supplementary Table S5). GO biological process analysis indicated that upregulated genes were mainly concentrated in energy metabolism-related entries, including oxidative phosphorylation, aerobic respiration, cellular respiration, aerobic electron transport chain, mitochondrial ATP synthesis-coupled electron transport, ATP synthesis-coupled electron transport, ATP biosynthetic process, and purine ribonucleotide and nucleoside triphosphate biosynthetic processes. Conversely, downregulated genes were primarily associated with intracellular material transport and vesicle-mediated transport entries, as well as entries related to cytoskeleton organization, small GTPase-mediated signal transduction, and protein polyubiquitination, including cytoplasmic transport, nucleocytoplasmic transport, and actin filament organization (Fig. 2c; Supplementary Table S6). Taken together, these enrichment patterns suggest that, under high-altitude hypoxia, the cerebral cortex may undergo adaptive metabolic reprogramming by enhancing mitochondrial energy metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation while selectively regulating intracellular transport, cytoskeletal dynamics, and signaling in response to high-altitude hypoxic stress.

To further dissect the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms underlying these differences, we applied pySCENIC to calculate transcription factor (TF) regulon activities across cell types and identified the top five TF regulons within each cell type in both breeds. In DSE, the most active TF regulons included TEAD4, SPI1, MAFB, SP1, and CEBPA (Fig. 2d); whereas in DT, the predominant high-activity regulons comprised MAF, SPI1, SOX17, ETV4, and NKX6-2 (Fig. 2e). These transcription factors exhibited pronounced cell-type-specific activity patterns; for instance, SPI1 and MAF showed elevated activity in Micro, SOX17 was strongly active in Endo, and NKX6-2 was enriched in Oligo.

Cell-cell communication differences in the cerebral cortex of DSE and DT

To investigate how intercellular communication contributes to cortical functional differences associated with high-altitude hypoxia adaptation between DT and DSE, we systematically analyzed ligand-receptor interaction networks using CellChat (Fig. 3a-b; Supplementary Figure 2a). Differential interaction analysis revealed substantial remodeling in both the number and strength of intercellular interactions between the two groups. Separate reconstruction of the communication networks showed that the DSE group exhibited a more extensive and complex connectivity structure (Fig. 3c), whereas the DT group displayed a relatively reorganized network topology (Fig. 3d). Quantitative comparison revealed that DSE exhibited a substantially greater number of interaction pairs than DT (3611 vs. 2349). In contrast, DT showed stronger average interaction strength than DSE (0.053 vs. 0.047), indicating a trade-off between interaction number and strength (Supplementary Figure 2b). This suggests that DSE forms a more diffuse and redundant communication network, while the high-altitude DT cortex adopts a more centralized and efficient strategy with fewer but stronger interactions. Such a configuration may enhance signaling robustness and coordination under hypoxic conditions, thereby facilitating adaptation to high-altitude hypoxia. At the signaling-pathway level, we identified 81 signaling pathways that mediate cortical intercellular communication, including 70 shared pathways, 10 specific to DSE, and 1 specific to DT (Fig. 3e). These results suggest distinct strategies for pathway utilization: DSE engages a broader range of signaling pathways, while DT concentrates signaling through fewer, but stronger and more focused, routes, reflecting clear differences in communication breadth and efficiency between the two conditions. Input/output modeling further analyses revealed that the contribution structure of distinct cell populations across pathways was reshaped between the two groups (Supplementary Figure 2c-d). Integration of the two-dimensional sender/receiver distribution further demonstrated functional specialization among key cellular populations, showing that Micro and Endo acted predominantly as signal senders (output nodes) in DSE, whereas Astro and Peri functioned mainly as receivers (input nodes) (Fig. 3f); by contrast, DT exhibited markedly elevated Oligo output capacity, while Astro retained a primarily receptive role, indicating a shift in dominant communication drivers under different pathological states (Fig. 3g). Consistently, pathway-level analyses of specific cell populations showed that signaling pathways, including NCAM, PSAP, PTN, NRG, JAM, and CLDN, exhibited distinct alterations between DSE and DT (Fig. 3h-i). Overall, these results indicate distinct communication topologies in the two breeds, with DSE establishing a broadly connected signaling network characterized by extensive pathway engagement, whereas DT adopts a comparatively more concentrated network configuration with higher per-interaction strength and selective pathway intensification.

Comparative isoform profiling between DSE and DT in the cerebral cortex

To assess whether transcript isoform diversity contributes to molecular differences associated with high-altitude adaptation between the cerebral cortices of DSE and DT, we employed Oxford Nanopore long-read sequencing to generate high-confidence full-length transcriptomes. Following stringent quality filtration and redundancy collapse, 81,965 non-redundant high-quality isoforms were resolved, representing 12,513 annotated genes. Leveraging the pig reference genome annotation (Ensembl v111), isoforms were hierarchically categorized into five categories: full splice match (FSM, 59.3%), incomplete splice match (ISM, 11.3%), novel in catalog (NIC, 10.8%), novel not in catalog (NNC, 18.0%), and other types (0.6%) (Fig. 4a; Supplementary Table S7). Notably, the proportional distribution of major isoform categories was highly consistent between DSE and DT, indicating a conserved transcript structural landscape across breeds. Isoform length distributions were highly similar between DSE and DT, with largely overlapping curves (Fig. 4b). Likewise, the peak positions of CCS read length distributions were closely aligned in both breeds, further demonstrating consistent sequencing performance and data quality (Fig. 4c). Structural analysis further revealed that transcripts in both DSE and DT were predominantly composed of reference-matched isoforms, isoforms containing at least one novel splice site, and intron retention isoforms, with comparable proportions across categories (Fig. 4d-e; Supplementary Table S8), highlighting the striking similarity in splicing patterns between the cerebral cortex of the two pig breeds. At the gene level, the transcriptomic architecture was similarly constrained: over 80% of genes generated one to five isoforms, whereas fewer than 5% produced more than five isoforms, with no significant inter-breed differences detected (Fig. 4f; Supplementary Table S9), reflecting comparable transcriptomic complexity. Importantly, compared with FSM isoforms, NIC and NNC isoforms were generally shorter, possessed reduced coding sequence (CDS contained fewer exons (Fig. 4g), and open reading frame (ORF) lengths (Fig. 4h) and contained fewer exons (Fig. 4i), revealing a substantial repertoire of compact novel transcripts in the cerebral cortex of both breeds. Collectively, these findings provide a critical foundation for refining porcine cortical transcriptome annotations and offer valuable insights into potential functional divergence between breeds.

Long-read single-cell transcriptomics reveals isoform diversity across cell types

To further determine how isoform diversity at single-cell resolution contributes to cell-type-specific functional specialization associated with adaptive differences between DSE and DT, we applied long-read sequencing to accurately resolve full-length transcript structures. This approach overcomes the limitations of conventional short-read sequencing in reconstructing complete isoforms and enables unbiased identification and quantification of transcript variants. Using this strategy, we systematically reconstructed the isoform landscape of the porcine cerebral cortex at single-cell resolution. UMAP visualization of the integrated long-read dataset revealed nine transcriptionally distinct cell types, including Endo, SMC, Peri, Micro, Oligo, Astro, En, In, and OPCs (Fig. 5a). This high-resolution cellular framework provided the basis for dissecting isoform diversity within the cortical microenvironment. The structural resolution afforded by long-read sequencing further enabled accurate isoform classification and revealed substantial heterogeneity in isoform number and composition across cell types (Fig. 5b; Supplementary Table S10). Isoform abundance varied significantly across structural categories. Full splice match (FSM) isoforms exhibited the highest expression levels and were significantly enriched compared with incomplete splice match (ISM), novel in catalog (NIC), and novel not in catalog (NNC) isoforms (all adjusted P < 0.001). ISM isoforms showed intermediate expression levels, which were significantly higher than those of NIC and NNC isoforms. Notably, no significant difference was observed between NIC and NNC isoforms. Overall, these results indicate a gradient of isoform abundance across transcript categories, with annotated full-length isoforms being more highly expressed than novel or partially matched transcripts (Fig. 5c). At the gene level, most genes contained only one or two isoforms, whereas a limited subset produced more than five isoforms, potentially contributing to the fine-tuned regulation required for neuronal function. Notably, the distribution of isoform counts showed no significant difference between DSE and DT (Fig. 5d; Supplementary Table S11), supporting the conserved nature of isoform mechanisms within the porcine cerebral cortex. Further gene-level analyses revealed an uneven distribution of isoform numbers in both DSE and DT: While the majority of genes exhibited limited transcript diversity, a subset displayed extensive isoform complexity. To systematically evaluate isoform sharing across cell types, we employed UpSet analysis to visualize major intersections among isoform sets (Fig. 5e). A minimum intersection threshold (min_size = 30) was applied to retain only intersections comprising at least 30 isoforms. The results indicated that most high-abundance isoforms were shared across multiple cell types, suggesting extensive transcript reuse and coordinated regulatory programs within cortical networks. In contrast, only a small fraction of isoforms exhibited strict cell-type specificity, reflecting refined transcriptional specialization. Collectively, these findings reveal that the isoform repertoire of the porcine cerebral cortex follows a “high-abundance shared/low-abundance specific” organizational principle, highlighting both the conserved transcriptional framework and the presence of cell-type-specific alternative transcriptional activity within the porcine brain.

Discussion

The cerebral cortex, serving as the principal hub for higher-order cognition in mammals, orchestrates complex neural processes through its diverse cellular composition and intricately organized circuitry [41]. Owing to its exceptionally high metabolic and oxygen demands, the cerebral cortex remains particularly susceptible to high-altitude hypoxic stress [42]. Notwithstanding substantial advances in transcriptomics, conventional approaches remain insufficient for resolving cell-type-specific responses within the structurally and functionally heterogeneous cortex [43]. To surmount these limitations, here we integrated long-read (Oxford Nanopore) and short-read (Illumina) single-cell RNA sequencing technologies, thereby enabling a multidimensional dissection of high-altitude hypoxia adaptation in the cerebral cortex. Short-read sequencing, offering high accuracy and throughput, establishes a robust framework for precise cell-type delineation and quantitative gene expression profiling at single-cell resolution [27, 44]. Furthermore, the depth and coverage afforded by short-read sequencing allow for precise quantification of transcript levels, facilitating quality assessment and refinement of single-cell transcriptomic data [45]. In contrast, long-read sequencing, capturing full-length transcripts, enables accurate annotation of transcription start and termination sites while uncovering previously unannotated novel transcript isoforms [28, 29, 46]. Synergistically integrating these complementary technologies, we achieved a high-resolution cell-type identification to in-depth transcript structural analysis, thereby elucidating the cell-type-specific molecular architectures that underpin cortical high-altitude hypoxia adaptation.

Differential gene expression analysis revealed that the cerebral cortex of DT and DSE exhibits differences across multiple key molecular networks, highlighting adaptive transcriptional remodeling under long-term high-altitude hypoxia. In DT, differentially expressed representative genes, including NKAIN2, CTNNA3, ZFP36, and members of the HSP40 family, were elevated. Yang et al. found that NKAIN2 is associated with adipogenesis, energy homeostasis, and hemoglobin regulation, and its upregulation in DT may reflect coordinated optimization of metabolism and oxygen delivery under high-altitude hypoxia [47]. The increased expression of CTNNA3 suggests enhanced cell adhesion, vascular homeostasis, and inflammatory pathways, with its differential methylation also linked to vascular diseases [48]. Enhanced expression of HSP40 family members reflects active protein homeostasis systems, indicating adaptive remodeling of stress-response pathways under prolonged hypoxia and cold conditions [49, 50]. High expression of ZFP36 may contribute to neural homeostasis by regulating the stability of inflammation-related mRNAs, consistent with observations in yak lung tissue under high-altitude hypoxia [51]. In contrast, TPT1, SLC39A10, MYH11, and PRKG1 were relatively more highly expressed in DSE. TPT1, a conserved homeostatic regulator, primarily supports cell survival and basal stress buffering [52–54]; SLC39A10 maintains zinc ion homeostasis to support mitochondrial function and oxidative metabolism efficiency [55–57]; while differential expression of MYH11 [58] and PRKG1 [59] suggests regulation of vascular smooth muscle contraction and the NO-cGMP signaling pathway. Overall, the DT cerebral cortex does not simply activate classical hypoxia pathways; instead, it reinforces structural support and stress-buffering mechanisms while rebalancing vascular and metabolic networks, suggesting multi-level transcriptional remodeling. This coordinated pattern of structural prioritization and energy homeostasis may constitute a key molecular basis for maintaining central nervous system function in DT under long-term high-altitude hypoxia.

Enrichment analysis revealed that the DT cerebral cortex under high-altitude hypoxia exhibits transcriptional remodeling centered on enhanced mitochondrial function. Specifically, KEGG pathways related to oxidative phosphorylation and multiple neurodegenerative diseases, including Huntington’s, Parkinson’s, Alzheimer’s, Prion diseases, and Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, were significantly enriched, with core genes concentrated in mitochondrial electron transport chain complexes and ATP synthase subunits [60], suggesting that this enrichment likely reflects an enhancement of the oxidative phosphorylation system rather than activation of pathological processes. Long-term high-altitude adaptation is not associated with simple suppression of aerobic metabolism but involves modulation of mitochondrial function to maintain metabolic homeostasis [61]. Consistently, GO biological process analysis showed significant enrichment of oxidative phosphorylation, aerobic respiration, electron transport chain, and ATP synthesis-coupled electron transport pathways, accompanied by upregulated ATP biosynthesis and nucleotide metabolism [62], indicating systemic upregulation of the “oxidative phosphorylation-ATP production” axis. Given the brain’s high energy demand, with synaptic transmission and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase-driven ion homeostasis accounting for the majority of energy expenditure [63, 64], these enriched pathways likely support sustained neuronal activity. Notably, enrichment of the “chemical carcinogenesis-reactive oxygen species” pathway suggests coordinated regulation of redox homeostasis. Elevated respiratory chain activity is typically accompanied by increased ROS production [65], and mitochondrial quality control mechanisms, including mitophagy, are critical for maintaining functional integrity [66], suggesting that DT cortex enhances antioxidant defenses and mitochondrial quality surveillance to buffer oxidative stress. In contrast, pathways related to immune response, inflammation, infection, chemokine signaling, and T cell receptor signaling were downregulated, and given the high energetic cost of immune activation [67], suppression of these pathways likely optimizes energy allocation toward core neural functions. Downregulated GO pathways were further enriched in cytoplasmic transport, nucleocytoplasmic transport, vesicle-mediated transport, and actin filament organization, with intracellular transport dependent on ATP-consuming molecular motors [68] and actin remodeling also ATP-dependent [69], suggesting selective reduction of structural turnover and intracellular logistics under high-altitude hypoxia to enhance energy efficiency. Collectively, these results indicate that the DT cerebral cortex under high-altitude hypoxia does not merely reduce metabolic activity but reinforces mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and energy supply while suppressing inflammatory and certain high-energy structural processes, achieving metabolic reprioritization and functional module reorganization. This mitochondria-centered adaptive network likely underpins the long-term stability of neural function at high altitude.

Under high-altitude hypoxia, the brain must preserve neural function and structural integrity despite sustained hypoxic stress. Our regulon analysis reveals that in the cerebral cortex of DT, key transcription factors regulating Endo, Oligo differentiation, and myeloid gene programs are markedly activated, reflecting an adaptive reinforcement of supportive cellular networks under high-altitude hypoxic stress. In vascular regulation, SOX17 [70], a critical determinant of arterial endothelial fate, exhibits increased activity, suggesting enhanced arterial-specific differentiation and endothelial regulation in DT cortex. Concurrently, ETV4, known to participate in high-altitude hypoxia-induced transcriptional programs [71], shows upregulated regulon activity, further indicating an amplified transcriptional response to high-altitude hypoxia. Regarding neural structural maintenance, NKX6-2 [72], which drives oligodendrocyte differentiation and myelination, displays elevated activity consistent with its role in preserving myelin and long-term axonal integrity [73], pointing to strengthened myelin-related programs in DT cortex. At the immune microenvironment level, SPI1, a core regulator of Micro and macrophages [74], shows enhanced activity, suggesting altered myeloid transcriptional programs, consistent with the established roles of Micro in neuroinflammation and microenvironment modulation [75, 76]. Collectively, these transcriptional changes are concentrated in supportive cell types—including Endo, Oligo, and Micro—that are critical for CNS development and functional maintenance [77]. By contrast, DSE exhibits a relatively conserved regulon landscape, predominantly maintaining immune homeostasis and basal transcriptional programs [78]. Taken together, under prolonged high-altitude hypoxia, DT cortex displays a reinforced transcriptional network in vascular regulation, myelin maintenance, and immune microenvironment programs, highlighting substantial adaptations in structural support systems.

Cell-cell communication analysis revealed pronounced differences between the cortical networks of DSE and DT, with DSE exhibiting widespread connectivity and DT showing fewer but stronger interactions, likely reflecting long-term adaptations to high-altitude hypoxia, where DT may maintain brain function by preferentially reinforcing a limited set of critical pathways rather than relying on extensive but weaker connections. High-altitude hypoxia has been shown to disrupt oxidative homeostasis and induce inflammatory responses in cortical regions, indicating that low-oxygen environments impose physiological stress on neural cells [79], and to alter neuronal activity and functional connectivity, suggesting that oxygen deprivation can reshape cortical network topology in a manner consistent with the differences observed between DSE and DT [80]. Long-term acclimatization further drives structural and functional remodeling, supporting the notion that environmental stress selectively modulates intercellular communication patterns [81]. At the cellular level, Oligo exhibited enhanced output in DT while Astro largely remained a signal receiver, whereas Micro and Endo were the primary signal senders in DSE, reflecting a functional specialization that likely represents an adaptive reorganization under hypoxic pressure to sustain critical neuro-metabolic and information-transmission processes, consistent with reports that hypoxia dynamically modulates microglial behavior and neurovascular interactions [82]. Collectively, these observations suggest that differences in cortical cell–cell communication networks reflect divergent adaptive strategies to distinct oxygen environments, with DT achieving efficient brain function maintenance through selective reinforcement of key pathways and core cell signaling, and DSE retaining extensive connectivity to preserve flexibility in responding to environmental perturbations, providing mechanistic insight into how high-altitude hypoxia drives cortical network remodeling and functional adaptation.

Long-read sequencing further revealed the complexity of transcript structures in the pig cerebral cortex. The results showed that the overall transcript architecture of DT and DSE was highly conserved, indicating a high consistency in their core transcriptional regulatory framework and suggesting that both breeds exhibit similar transcript composition and isoform structural features in the cortex. Moreover, a large number of previously unannotated novel transcript isoforms were identified in both breeds, substantially enriching the pig cortical transcriptome. These isoforms mainly consisted of NIC and NNC types, the majority of which displayed low expression levels and pronounced cell-type specificity [34, 83]. These findings highlight the extensive transcript diversity in the pig cortex, and the newly identified isoforms may serve as valuable genetic resources, providing a foundation for further investigation of breed-specific transcript-level regulatory differences.

Conclusion

In this study, we provide a single-cell and transcript structure-resolved view of the porcine cerebral cortex under distinct environmental conditions, offering insights into neural adaptation to high-altitude hypoxia. Despite overall conservation in cellular composition and global transcriptional patterns between DSE and DT pigs, we identified substantial divergence in regulatory programs across multiple layers. High-altitude adaptation in the DT cortex is characterized by enhanced mitochondrial oxidative metabolism and suppression of energy-intensive processes, suggesting a strategy of metabolic reprioritization to sustain neural function. At the regulatory level, these changes are primarily concentrated in supportive cell populations, including Endo, Oligo, and Micro, while cell–cell communication analysis reveals a shift toward a more focused and efficient signaling network, indicating network-level optimization under chronic hypoxia. By integrating short- and long-read single-cell sequencing, this study captures both gene expression dynamics and transcript isoform diversity, thereby expanding the annotation of the porcine cortical transcriptome. Collectively, these findings establish a multi-layered framework for understanding cortical adaptation, highlighting coordinated regulation across metabolic, cellular, and network levels. Further studies incorporating multi-omics approaches, functional validation, and cross-species comparisons will help to refine the regulatory mechanisms and broader biological significance of hypoxia adaptation.
