A systematic review of surgical treatment of reverse shoulder arthroplasty associated acromial and scapular spine stress fractures
Authors: Ashley Isenberg, Jonathan Gagnon, Daniel Maunder, Scott Huff, Anand M. Murthi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCurrent treatment options for acromial and scapular spine fracture (ASF) after reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) include nonoperative immobilization or surgical fixation, but optimal patient selection for each option remains unclear. We performed a descriptive analysis of operative vs. nonoperative treatment to identify patterns in fracture type, functional outcomes, and operative timing.MethodsWe reviewed 11 studies reporting nonoperative and operative management of post-rTSA ASFs. Data were extracted based on fracture subtype, union rate, time to surgery, indication for surgery, and functional outcomes. We assessed heterogeneity in study design, baseline reporting, and sample size when making interstudy comparisons. Risk of bias was evaluated in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute method.ResultsEleven studies with a total of 88 surgically managed fractures were identified. Across 8 studies reporting union rates, the operative group achieved higher radiographic union (range, 69%-100%) than the nonoperative group (30%-79%), though only one study of acute Levy II/III patterns treated within 6 weeks of fracture demonstrated statistical significance. However, most studies reported that patient-reported outcomes were similar between the groups. In addition, surgical management was associated with a notable complication profile, including hardware failure and the need for secondary procedures. In most studies, surgical treatment was performed for fractures that did not respond to an initial trial of immobilization and persistent pain. Outcome measures varied and were often reported without baseline values. Small operative cohorts across all studies limited statistical power.ConclusionIn the studies included in this review, Levy type III fractures were treated surgically at a higher rate than other fracture types due to poor function and pain. Surgical management of ASFs was largely reserved for patients who failed nonoperative treatment. Improvement of functional outcomes in surgical intervention compared to nonoperative treatment was found only in acute surgical intervention. No statistically significant difference was found when comparing delayed surgical intervention and nonoperative treatment. Surgical management was associated with significantly higher union rates than nonoperative management. However, functional improvements were largely modest and comparable to nonoperative management in delayed cases, and the benefits of surgery must be weighed against a high complication rate. Double plate fixation was the predominant surgical technique for ASF after rTSA. The use of the Levy classification system and assessment of uniform functional outcomes in future studies would allow for stronger analysis of the comparative impacts of surgical and nonoperative treatment of ASF after rTSA.

Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) was initiated with Paul Grammont's 1985 design of the reverse shoulder prosthesis,8 with U.S. Food and Drug Administration in 2003. The indications for rTSA have broadened substantially over the past decade with improvements in prosthesis design and better patient outcomes. Originally rTSA was reserved for elderly, low-demand patients with rotator cuff arthropathy or as a salvage procedure after failed primary arthroplasty.5 Today, rTSA is used in the treatment of primary glenohumeral osteoarthritis, proximal humerus fracture, and inflammatory arthritis.2 rTSA accounted for 74% of total shoulder arthroplasties in 2020, compared to just 31% in 2011.12 In addition, the number of surgeons performing rTSA in the United States increased 112% from 2016 to 2020.20

rTSA is associated with post-operative complications, including acromial and scapular spine fracture (ASF). Generally, the rTSA prosthesis shifts the center of rotation inferomedial and lengthens the arm, leading to an increased deltoid moment arm with an increase in the mechanical advantage of the deltoid and reduced reliance on the rotator cuff for shoulder abduction and rotation.4 Consequently, tensioning the deltoid increases the strain at its origin along the acromion and scapula, elevating the risk of developing ASF.21 Lateralizing the glenoid is a technique that has evolved to avoid scapular notching and improve free range of motion (ROM) in rTSA. However, lateralized glenoid design implants have been associated with a higher rate of acromial fractures compared with medialized designs.11 It is well documented that patients with ASF experience worse functional outcomes than those who do not develop ASF after rTSA.17,26 Although there is some heterogeneity across studies reporting the frequency of ASF after rTSA, the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) Complications of Reverse Shoulder Arthroplasty Multicenter Research Group determined a cumulative stress fracture incidence of 4.1%.1

Conventionally, ASFs have been treated nonoperatively with sling immobilization and physical therapy.10 In 2011, an ASES survey found that 75% of surgeons prefer to treat ASF nonoperatively.13 More recently, surgical intervention has gained popularity after reports of poor outcomes after nonoperative treatment.3,10,27,32 As the utilization of rTSA continues to rise, the debate over nonoperative vs. surgical management of these fractures will increase. Since 2022, 6 studies have directly compared the 2 treatment modalities, offering new insight into this management question.3,17,24,27,29,33 In addition, several recent reports have evaluated outcomes of surgical management alone. Together, these studies create an opportunity to assess the role of surgical management of ASF after rTSA.

The purpose of this systematic review is to characterize the current literature on the surgical management of ASF following rTSA. Specifically, we aimed to examine indications for surgery, the rates of surgical management stratified by fracture type, timing of intervention (acute vs. delayed), surgical techniques utilized, rates of fracture union, functional outcomes, and reported complications. The primary objectives were to analyze operative rate by fracture type, the influence of timing of surgery on outcomes, and union rates for operative vs. nonoperative management. We hypothesize that surgical management of ASF may be utilized for specific indications and is associated with better clinical outcomes and higher union rates compared to nonoperative treatment. We also anticipate that certain fracture types (eg, Levy Type III) are more likely to be managed operatively due to their impact on deltoid function. Finally, we hypothesize that acute surgical intervention may lead to superior functional outcomes compared to delayed reconstruction due to earlier stabilization and preservation of shoulder function.

Methods

Literature search

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. Literature search and screening details are shown in Fig. 1. No protocol was published prior to the start of this review. A comprehensive search was conducted using Ovid MEDLINE, Web of Science, Embase, and CENTRAL without restriction on publication date. A set of preliminary search terms related to “acromion,” “scapular spine,” “fracture,” “stress,” and “reverse shoulder arthroplasty” were tested in MEDLINE initially, followed by Embase, CENTRAL, and Web of Science. Study selection

Eligible studies included comparative studies, randomized controlled trials, observational studies, and case series. Case reports were excluded due to their high risk of selection bias and limited generalizability. Risk of bias was evaluated in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute tools for case-controlled studies, cohort studies, and case series, and bias visualizations were created using the web-based “robvis” tool.22 Although systematic reviews were excluded from analysis, references in those studies were screened for additional eligible studies.

Studies were included if they examined patients who developed ASF following rTSA. ASF arising from an etiology different from rTSA and acromial stress reactions were excluded. Eligible studies had to specify operative management for ASF following rTSA. In addition, eligible studies had to report quantitative outcome data. No restrictions were placed on study setting, primary rTSA indication, or follow-up duration. Cadaveric studies and non-English language publications were excluded.

Screening

Two authors (A.I. and J.G.) independently performed title and abstract screening and full-text review. Conflicts were resolved by consensus with a third author (D.M.). The first search was performed on March 6, 2025.

In addition to the 8 studies identified in the primary database search, we conducted a manual search of the reference lists of all included studies and relevant review articles to identify additional eligible studies not captured in the initial search.

Data extraction

Data were independently extracted by 2 reviewers (A.I. and J.G.) using a standardized Excel spreadsheet. Extracted demographic data included lead author, year of publication, study design, level of evidence, total number of participants, total number of fractures, mean age, sex, mean follow-up duration, fracture subtype or location, operative treatment, mean time to diagnosis, and diagnostic confirmation methods. The primary outcomes extracted were operative technique, time to treatment, fracture union, and pain and functional outcomes after fracture in patients who received operative treatment compared to those who received conservative treatment. The functional outcomes examined in this study included ASES Standardized Shoulder Assessment Score, Subjective Shoulder Value (SSV), Constant–Murley (CM), ROM and visual analog scale (VAS) for pain.

Statistical analysis

A chi-square test on pooled event counts was performed to determine if there was a statistically significant association between fracture type and operative rate and to compare union rates between operative and nonoperative management. Significance was set at P < .05. Analyses were performed using Excel.

Results

Reported outcomes included pain scores (VAS), functional assessments such as the ASES score, SSV, and Constant Score (CS), as well as measures of ROM (eg, forward flexion, abduction, external rotation). Several studies also reported radiographic union rates and time to healing.

Only 6 studies incorporated a comparison group of patients treated with nonoperative management. There was considerable heterogeneity across the studies in terms of fracture classification, surgical techniques, outcome measures, and follow-up duration, which limited direct quantitative comparisons.

Indications for surgical management

Across the series of studies examining open reduction internal fixation (ORIF) for ASF after rTSA, operative fixation was generally reserved for patients who either failed an initial course of nonoperative management or presented with persistent, debilitating symptoms. In the cohorts reported by Marigi et al,19 Yu et al,33 Moore et al,24 and 2 of the Hess et al14 3 ORIF patients, every fracture was first managed nonoperatively. Only fractures that failed to achieve radiographical union or were associated with continued lack of function and serious unremitting pain went on to ORIF. Schenk et al27 acknowledged that their retrospective design precludes clear insight into each surgeon's rationale for choosing surgery, and Kriechling et al17 similarly noted that indications varied by surgeon preference rather than by a standardized protocol. In the smaller case series by Toft et al,28 the indication for ORIF was “debilitating pain” unresponsive to nonoperative measures, and Wahlquist et al30 also reported surgery only in patients whose pain and functional loss persisted beyond the acute period. However, Bauer et al3 enrolled only fractures treated acutely (within 6 weeks), all of which were Levy type IIa/IIb/IIc or III, focusing on early intervention for more extensive or unstable fracture patterns. Together, these studies demonstrate that much of the existing literature on operative management of ASFs following rTSA has been conducted in patients that have failed nonoperative management and thus have delayed surgical intervention. The report by Bauer et al was the only study that assessed acute surgical intervention.

Rate of surgical management depending on fracture type

Post-operative ASFs were classified in accordance with either the Levy or Crosby classification system. However, there is little overlap between the respective classification systems, preventing direct comparisons. In this review, 3 studies utilized the Crosby system, with a type I fracture corresponding to the anterior acromion, type II indicating acromial fractures posterior to the acromioclavicular joint, and type III representing fractures of the scapular spine.16,27 In 7 studies, 231 fractures were classified by the Levy system: 55 type I fractures involving a portion of the anterior and middle deltoid, 95 type II fractures corresponding to the entire middle deltoid region and a portion of the posterior deltoid region, and 70 type III fracture indicating involvement of the entire middle and posterior deltoid region.3,17,19,23,24,30,33 The remaining 2 studies made no note of fracture classification.28,29

Out of the 231 fractures classified according to the Levy system, 59 (25%) underwent surgical intervention. When stratified by fracture type, operative rates were 10% for Levy type I, 25% for Levy type II, and 35% for Levy type III (Fig. 5). Chi square analysis revealed a statistically significant difference in the surgical management rate, with Levy type III fractures more frequently managed operatively compared to type I and type II fractures (χ2 = 5.48, P = .019).Figure 5Distribution of operative and nonoperative management by Levy fracture type.

A smaller cohort of 62 fractures were classified in accordance with the Crosby system, 12 type I, 29 type II, and 21 type III. Among those fracture types, operative rates were not significantly different (25% type I, 24% type II, and 47% type III; χ2 = 3.432, P = .18).

Functional outcomes of surgical vs nonoperative management

Six studies reported functional outcome data directly comparing operative vs. nonoperative management of post-rTSA stress fractures. Moore et al,24 Schenk et al,27 Kriechling et al,17 Kennon et al,16 and Wahlquist et al30 found no statistically significant differences in functional outcomes measured between operative and nonoperative patients. Bauer et al1 was the only study that reported statistically significant improvement in measured outcomes in the surgical cohort compared to the nonoperative group. There was marked heterogeneity among these studies in both the outcome measures chosen and the study designs employed, making cross-study comparisons challenging. For example, Bauer et al1 reported VAS pain, ASES, SSV, and forward elevation and found a significant improvement in the acute ORIF group compared to the nonoperative group in SSV (90 vs. 67, P = .009), ASES (82 vs. 60, P = .001), and forward elevation (128 vs. 98, P = .025). However, they did not provide any baseline values. Schenk et al27 measured CS, SSV, and ROM (flexion, abduction, external rotation), with author-noted bias toward operating on more severe fractures, and found no statistically significant differences between operative and nonoperative treatment. Kriechling et al17 similarly assessed CS, SSV, pain, and ROM with acknowledged selection bias and abduction strength and reported that both groups reached similar outcome measures for the SSV, CS, ROM, and abduction strength. Moore et al24 reported ASES, SSV, VAS, and ROM but did not report preoperative benchmarks. That study also found no statistically significant difference in outcomes between operative and nonoperative patients in ASES score.

Acute vs. delayed surgical intervention after fracture

Overall, the time to surgical intervention after fracture incidence was variably reported in studies comparing operative and nonoperative management. Although not the primary outcome of interest in any of the included articles, acute vs delayed intervention was reported in selected patients. Recently, Bauer et al3 investigated the use of acute ORIF vs. nonoperative management in a larger cohort of 16 fractures treated surgically and 58 fractures treated nonoperatively. The operative group was limited to patients receiving surgery within 6 weeks after fracture. This was the only comparative study that noted significantly better pain scores, SSV, ASES, and forward elevation for the surgically managed group compared to nonoperatively managed patients.

Kriechling et al17 reported a mean time of 15 weeks from fracture to surgery in 11 patients. Similarly, Tran et al29 observed a mean duration of 28 weeks from symptom onset to surgery in a cohort of 11 patients. In a smaller study, Schenk et al27 reported a more acute intervention, with 7 fractures undergoing surgery at an average of 2 weeks from fracture diagnosis. In addition to the 7 acutely operated fractures, Schenk et al27 described 4 nonoperatively treated fractures that failed and were later operated on at an average of 63 weeks after fracture diagnosis. They noted a healing rate of 4/7 (57%) for the acute intervention group and 1/4 (25%) for the failed nonoperative treatment group. Hess et al14 described an initial nonoperative treatment course followed by delayed ORIF in 2 patients and acute ORIF after fracture diagnosis in 1 patient. Although all patients reported better pain scores and functional outcomes, only the acute ORIF patient returned to prefracture function and pain levels as assessed by SSV, CM, forward elevation, and abduction.

Wahlquist et al30 compared the timing of surgical vs. nonoperative treatment in a case report of 5 patients. In the 3 surgical interventions, 1 patient underwent ORIF within 1 month of fracture, and 2 underwent ORIF at 9 months and 12 months after fracture diagnosis. All patients treated surgically achieved fracture union, but none returned to prefracture function levels as assessed by the Neer score.

It is worth noting that several investigations, including Moore et al,24 enrolled only those patients in the operative cohort who had previously undergone and not responded to nonoperative management, without accounting for these outcomes in the nonoperative group. Bauer et al3 was the only study that limited their operative cohort to fractures that were acutely treated with surgery (within 6 weeks of injury).

Union rates after operative compared to nonoperative treatment

Across the 9 studies that reported union data, union occurred in significantly more operatively vs. nonoperatively managed fractures (81% vs. 61%, respectively; χ2 = 8.92, P = .0028), indicating a statistically significant difference in favor of operative management. In each study, operative fixation of ASF after rTSA demonstrated higher rates of radiographic healing than nonoperative management, with the notable exception of Moore et al,24 who observed a lower union rate in the surgical group (71%, 5/7) compared to the nonoperative group (75%, 9/12). Of the 7 operatively treated patients, 5 had Levy type I acromial fractures. The authors reported that patients with Levy type I acromial fracture had poorer healing than patients with other subtypes, regardless of treatment modality (operative vs. nonoperative). The authors did not specify the fixation method used for each patient but stated that ORIF using tension band constructs with plates, Kirschner-wires, and cables were used for patients that did not show callus formation following nonoperative treatment. Bauer et al,3 Schenk et al,27 and Wahlquist et al30 all reported higher union rates in surgically treated patients compared to nonoperatively managed patients. Kriechling et al17 reported a higher union rate in the surgically managed group (81%, n = 9/11) compared to the nonoperatively managed group (44.8%, 13/29) and noted a 100% union rate after surgical fixation in patients with Levy type III fracture. Primary indication for reverse total shoulder arthroplasty and stress fractures

The underlying reasons for the initial rTSA varied in the series of patients who underwent surgical fixation of ASF after rTSA. In the study by Hess et al,14 2 ORIF patients had rotator cuff tears and 1 had rotator cuff arthropathy. In the 16 rTSA ORIF patients in the study by Yu et al,33 10 patients had rotator cuff arthropathy, 2 had osteoarthritis, 1 had an isolated rotator cuff tear, 1 had failed fracture fixation, 1 had avascular necrosis, and 1 primary indication listed as “other.” Toft et al28 reported 2 fractures in patients whose arthroplasty was done for rotator cuff arthropathy, 2 with rotator cuff tears, and 1 with failed fracture fixation. Wahlquist et al30 similarly described 2 cases after failed cuff repair and 1 following primary cuff tear. Schenk et al2 reported 6 ORIF patients that underwent rTSA due to rotator cuff tear and 1 due to osteoarthritis. Marigi et al,19 Tran et al,29 Kriechling et al,17 and Bauer et al3 did not report the primary indication for rTSA.

Complications

Complications were discussed for 54 surgically managed fractures in 6 studies.3,14,17,28,30,33 The complication profile for operative treatment was notable, consisting of mechanical failures such as screw loosening and glenoid loosening, as well as biological complications including secondary fractures. Furthermore, several patients required subsequent surgical interventions for plate removal or scar débridement, and one instance of pneumothorax was reported. Discussion

In this systematic review, surgical management of ASF was mainly indicated for patients who had already failed conservative management. Although one study by Bauer et al3 suggested that acute surgical intervention within 6 weeks of a diagnosed ASF might improve functional outcomes compared with nonoperative management, it is important to note that this study lacked preoperative baseline patient-reported outcomes. In contrast, most of the literature indicates that delayed surgery conferred no benefit over nonoperative treatment in terms of functional scores. Fractures treated surgically achieved union at a significantly higher rate than those treated nonoperatively. Levy type III fractures were treated operatively at a statistically higher rate than other fracture types. A few studies used the Crosby classification; however, among those studies, type III fractures were treated operatively at a nonsignificant higher rate than type I or type II fractures. The higher rate of surgical intervention for Levy type III fractures may be related to more severe functional limitations associated with these fractures compared to Levy type I and type II. Merlin et al23 reported worse functional outcomes prior to any intervention among Levy type III fractures in a cohort of 7 post-operative ASFs. This finding is supported by Boltuch et al6 and Walch et al,31 who note worse ASES, SST, and forward flexion for Levy type III fractures compared to other fracture groups. In the 2023 meta-analysis of nonoperatively managed ASF after rTSA by Cui et al,10 outcomes for Levy type I fractures were comparable to patients without fractures, whereas patients with Levy type III fracture experienced worse outcomes than the nonfracture cohort.

A systematic review by Patterson et al26 found no overall statistical advantage for ORIF over nonoperative management of ASFs following rTSA. Similarly, the current review did not detect significant differences in pooled pain or functional scores between treatment modalities, but both the Patterson et al study and the current study found a higher union rate in the operative cohort. Despite these similarities, Patterson et al14 focused primarily on comparing patients without fractures to those with ASF. Outcomes after acromial fractures were inferior to those in patients without fractures, with ASES, CS, VAS, Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation, and SST all lower in patients with fractures vs. patients without fractures and also before and after the development of the fracture within the same patient. In addition, Patterson et al26 only examined a total of 20 patients that received operative treatment across 7 different studies, whereas the current review included 88 patients with operative treatment. Patterson et al14 reported that type III fractures were more likely to be treated operatively. However, that study examines smaller patient cohorts more extensively and focuses on specific fracture subtypes and timing of intervention. Rather than relying solely on aggregate P values, the current study identifies patterns suggesting when surgical fixation may be advantageous. These observations should inform the design of future studies aimed at pinpointing which patients are most likely to have improved functional outcomes from ORIF.

The findings of this review suggest that although surgical fixation is a reliable method for achieving radiographic union, its impact on clinical function appears modest. Surgeons should counsel patients that operative management carries a substantial risk of complications without a superior functional result compared to nonoperative immobilization, particularly in chronic or delayed settings.

Only the Bauer et al3 study, which uniquely enrolled only those acromial fractures managed acutely (within 6 weeks of injury) and excluded any patients who had first undergone a trial of nonoperative care in the surgical cohort, demonstrated statistically significant improvements in function and ROM compared to the nonoperative group. Generally, the operative management cohort in all other studies were composed of patients that initially failed nonoperative management, and no significant differences in functional outcomes were reported in those studies. This may serve as a confounding variable and further indicates the need for further study of acute operative management. The allocation of failed nonoperative patients to the surgical arm may result in artificially favorable outcomes in the nonoperative group. Considering only the outcomes of patients that underwent surgical repair after failed nonoperative treatment in the surgical cohort could harm the internal validity of direct comparisons between nonoperative and operative treatment pathways. Acute ORIF should continue to be explored in future studies, as it allows a stronger comparison between the 2 treatment modalities to better elucidate the relationship between the functional outcomes of surgical and nonoperative management.

In addition, heterogeneity in fracture classification may further impact the ability to derive impactful conclusions on ASF management after rTSA. Given that the Levy classification system has greater interobserver reliability than the Crosby classification, we propose that the Levy system is used in future studies to promote greater homogeneity in the study of ASFs and allow for robust review and possible meta-analysis.25 Future studies that report CM, ASES, SST, pain, and Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation outcome measures with larger sample sizes and fractures classified in accordance with the Levy system would allow for a direct comparison of surgical and conservative management. This would allow surgeons to better distinguish between treatment plans for their ASF patients.

In the current study, dual plate fixation was significantly more used than a single plate technique. A 2022 review of ASF fixation constructs by Cassidy et al7 reported that dual plate fixation was more commonly used for Levy type III fractures than for more lateral Levy type I and II fractures.7 Overall, they investigated 17 fractures, finding dual plate techniques for 8 fractures, and single plating for 9 fractures, noting union in 14 out of 15 studied fractures. With a lack of direct comparison of functional outcomes between fixation techniques in the studies included in both reviews, it is difficult to determine if an optimal fixation technique exists. Biomechanical studies have demonstrated that the dual plate technique is a stronger fixation construct, but there currently is a paucity of functional clinical outcomes stratified by fixation technique.15 Further studies should investigate functional outcomes between single and dual plate fixation.

A major limitation of this review is the small sample sizes and the retrospective nature of these studies. Larger sample sizes with unified outcome measures would be necessary to perform a meta-analysis of surgical management compared to operative management. Currently, meta-analysis is attainable for nonoperative management compared to nonfracture groups, as demonstrated by Cui et al10

Conclusion

In the studies included in this review, Levy type III fractures were treated surgically at a higher rate than other fracture types due to poor function and pain. Surgical management of ASFs was largely reserved for patients who failed nonoperative treatment. Improvement of functional outcomes in surgical intervention compared to nonoperative treatment was found only in acute surgical intervention. No statistically significant difference was found when comparing delayed surgical intervention and nonoperative treatment. Surgical management was associated with significantly higher union rates than nonoperative management. However, functional improvements were largely modest and comparable to nonoperative management in delayed cases, and the benefits of surgery must be weighed against a high complication rate. Double plate fixation was the predominant surgical technique for ASF after rTSA. The use of the Levy classification system and assessment of uniform functional outcomes in future studies would allow for stronger analysis of the comparative impacts of surgical and nonoperative treatment of ASF after rTSA.
