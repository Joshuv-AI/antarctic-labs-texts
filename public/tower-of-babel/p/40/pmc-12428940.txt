Metabolism-driven posttranslational modifications and immune regulation: Emerging targets for immunotherapy
Authors: Gujie Wu, Xiaofei Fan, Lin Cheng, Zongwei Chen, Yanjun Yi, Jiaqi Liang, Xiaolong Huang, Na Yang, Jiacheng Yin, Weigang Guo, Yiwei Huang, Shanye Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The interplay between cellular metabolism and immune regulation is central to immune function and disease progression, revealing notable therapeutic opportunities. Upon activation, immune cells undergo metabolic reprogramming to meet heightened demands for energy and biosynthesis, reshaping regulatory networks across epigenomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic layers. Metabolite-derived posttranslational modifications (PTMs) serve as pivotal mechanisms integrating metabolic intermediates with immune signaling pathways. Beyond classical acetylation, diverse nonacetyl PTMs—including lactylation, succinylation, malonylation, palmitoylation, and myristoylation—modify histone and nonhistone proteins, regulating gene expression, protein stability, subcellular localization, enzymatic activity, and protein-protein interactions. Advances in mass spectrometry and bioinformatics now enable precise characterization of these PTMs, uncovering their broad roles in immune regulation. This review summarizes current progress in immunometabolism and explores future directions such as mechanistic studies, combination strategies, and clinical applications. Metabolite-driven PTMs critically connect metabolism to immune regulation, suggesting promising therapeutic approaches for cancer, autoimmune disorders, and inflammatory diseases.

INTRODUCTION

The rapidly growing field of immunometabolism investigates how metabolic reprogramming and the accumulation of metabolic intermediates influence immune regulation across diverse immune cell subsets, tumor microenvironments (TMEs), and systemic metabolic networks (1). Upon activation in inflammatory contexts, immune cells often adopt metabolic profiles that resemble those of malignant cells, relying predominantly on aerobic glycolysis to fulfill the heightened energy demands associated with immune activation. This metabolic phenotype is characteristic of effector T cells (Teff cells), including T helper 1 (TH1) and TH17 subtypes, M1 macrophages, natural killer (NK) cells, and dendritic cells. In contrast, a metabolic shift favoring oxidative phosphorylation (OxPhos) is generally associated with anti-inflammatory responses, such as those observed in M2 macrophages and regulatory T cells (Treg cells) (2–4). Recent advancements in metabolomics have facilitated the precise mapping of metabolite alterations across various immune contexts, including infection, inflammation, autoimmunity, and cancer (5–8).

As our understanding of immunometabolic regulation deepens, it is increasingly clear that metabolic alterations extend far beyond their traditional roles in energy production and biosynthesis. Metabolites function as multifaceted regulators of immune cell behavior, influencing various layers of cellular control—including the epigenomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolic landscapes (9). In the context of epigenetic regulation, metabolites act as essential cofactors and cosubstrates for chromatin-modifying enzymes, thereby shaping gene expression programs (10). At the transcriptomic level, certain metabolites affect RNA metabolism through mechanisms such as riboswitch-mediated sensing and posttranscriptional modifications (11, 12). Moreover, metabolites can modulate protein activity by engaging in noncovalent interactions, including competitive inhibition and allosteric regulation (13). Some metabolites exert regulatory effects independently of classical metabolic pathways by directly modifying proteins posttranslationally. These emerging roles underscore the significance of metabolites as integral signaling molecules in the immune system beyond their canonical metabolic functions.

Metabolite-derived posttranslational modifications (PTMs) have emerged as key regulatory mechanisms that integrate metabolic reprogramming with immune function. PTMs are defined as covalent modifications of amino acid residues in proteins following translation, most of which are enzyme-catalyzed, and are typically classified into two categories: histone modifications and nonhistone modifications. While histone PTMs have been extensively studied in the context of epigenetic regulation, nonhistone PTMs play an equally crucial role in regulating protein stability, subcellular localization, enzymatic activity, and molecular interactions. Phosphorylation was the first PTM to be identified, with descriptions dating back to 1906 (14). The discovery of histone acetylation in 1964, which established a direct link between gene transcription and protein modification, substantially advanced the field of PTM research (15). With the advent of cutting-edge technologies, such as mass spectrometry–based proteomics and stable isotope labeling, more than 600 distinct types of PTMs have now been identified (16). In immune cells, a variety of PTMs derived from metabolic intermediates has been found. Classical metabolism-related PTMs such as acetylation [derived from acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA)] and methylation (dependent on S-adenosylmethionine) have been comprehensively reviewed elsewhere (17). Here, we focus on underexplored, metabolically driven PTMs that exemplify the direct cross-talk between metabolic reprogramming and immune signaling, including glycolysis-derived lactylation, tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle–derived malonylation and succinylation, lipid metabolism–derived palmitoylation and myristoylation, and amino acid–derived modifications. Collectively, these PTMs reveal how metabolic states directly program immune cell function, laying the foundation for mechanistic breakthroughs and therapeutic innovations in immunometabolism.

In this review, we provide a comprehensive overview of current insights into immunometabolism, with a particular focus on metabolic adaptations that occur at specific stages of immune cell development and activation. These dynamic, stage-specific changes in metabolites lay the biochemical foundation for subsequent protein modifications, which ultimately modulate immune cell function. We emphasize recent developments in the study of metabolic PTMs that arise from immunometabolic pathways and contribute to immune response regulation. Last, we explore the potential therapeutic applications of targeting the interplay between metabolism and PTMs, particularly in the context of immune and inflammatory diseases, and provide a forward-looking perspective on future research directions in this field.

OVERVIEW OF IMMUNOMETABOLISM

Macrophages

Macrophages are highly versatile innate immune cells, which can be broadly categorized into two major subtypes: M1 and M2. M1 macrophages, or pro-inflammatory macrophages, are activated in inflammatory contexts through signals such as Toll-like receptor (TLR) engagement, bacterial components like lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and interferon (IFN) signaling. Conversely, M2 macrophages—alternatively activated or reparative macrophages—are typically induced in TH2-dominated environments by interleukin-4 (IL-4), IL-10, or IL-13 (18). Because of their inherent plasticity, macrophages are capable of integrating a wide array of environmental signals, resulting in a functional spectrum that spans from M1 to M2 phenotypes in vivo (19). Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) constitute the most abundant immune cell population within the TME, exhibiting a heterogeneous range of functions from immunosuppressive to antitumorigenic (20).

Metabolically, M1 polarization is characterized by an early increase in both aerobic glycolysis and mitochondrial respiration, accompanied by elevated glucose uptake and accumulation of TCA cycle intermediates (21). A key node in this rewiring is citrate, which is exported from mitochondria and converted into acetyl-CoA by adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP)–citrate lyase (ACLY), serving as a substrate for histone acetylation and transcriptional activation of inflammatory genes (22). As activation progresses, M1 macrophages further enhance glycolytic flux, engage in de novo fatty acid synthesis, and activate the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). Up-regulation of acetyl-CoA carboxylase 1 (ACC1) promotes the generation of malonyl-CoA, facilitating protein malonylation and contributing to pro-inflammatory gene expression (23). During sustained activation, the TCA cycle becomes disrupted because of down-regulation of isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) and succinate dehydrogenase (SDH), leading to intracellular accumulation of citrate and succinate (24). Succinate, in particular, stabilizes hypoxia-inducible factor–1α (HIF-1α) and reinforces inflammatory signaling. Succinylation of mitochondrial proteins is also enhanced, further modulating macrophage effector functions (25). A metabolic hallmark of late-stage M1 activation is the induction of reverse electron transport, wherein electron flow from ubiquinol back to complex I generates reactive oxygen species (ROS). This process, while bypassing canonical OxPhos, amplifies antimicrobial and inflammatory responses (26).

In contrast, M2 macrophages preferentially rely on OxPhos as their principal energy source. While glycolysis plays a supportive role, fatty acid β-oxidation (FAO) and mitochondrial respiration are critical for M2 polarization and function (27, 28). Notably, inhibition of pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1) enhances the conversion of glucose-derived pyruvate into acetyl-CoA via pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH), reinforcing OxPhos and promoting M2 differentiation (27).

T cells

T cells are classified into two main subsets: CD4+ TH cells and CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs). Upon activation, T cells undergo notable metabolic reprogramming to fulfill the bioenergetic and biosynthetic demands associated with rapid proliferation and effector function. This metabolic transition involves a shift from oxidative metabolism, which predominantly relies on FAO and pyruvate oxidation, to anabolic pathways such as glycolysis, glutaminolysis, and the PPP (29, 30). Recent studies suggest that these metabolic changes are not merely passive by-products of activation but play an active role in shaping T cell differentiation and functional outcomes (31).

In their naïve state, both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells maintain a hypometabolic profile, predominantly relying on OxPhos for energy, using pyruvate and FAO as key substrates. Upon antigenic stimulation, CD4+ TH cells differentiate into Teff and Treg cells. Effector subsets, including TH1, TH2, and TH17 cells, undergo a pronounced metabolic shift toward an anabolic state, primarily driven by the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway. Conversely, Treg cells preferentially use adenosine 5′-monophosphate (AMP)–activated protein kinase (AMPK) signaling, bypassing mTOR to maintain metabolic homeostasis. In activated Teff cells, both glycolysis and OxPhos are up-regulated, with glutamine oxidation in the TCA cycle enhancing OxPhos capacity, while FAO and pyruvate oxidation are down-regulated (30). This increase in glycolysis is driven by the elevated expression of glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) and key enzymes in the glycolytic pathway, while mitochondrial biogenesis supports the enhanced OxPhos (32). Notably, although OxPhos is essential for the initial activation of T cells, sustained glycolysis is indispensable for the effector functions of Teff cells, including cytokine production (33). In addition to the well-characterized phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt/mTOR pathway, Teff differentiation also involves transcriptional reprogramming, mediated by the up-regulation of Myc and HIF-1α.

In contrast, Treg cells primarily rely on FAO and OxPhos for energy production. These cells do not depend on mTOR or HIF-1α but exhibit high levels of phosphorylated AMPK and express the lineage-defining transcription factor Forkhead box P3 (Foxp3) (34). Restricting glycolysis and favoring a shift toward OxPhos are crucial for the differentiation and function of Treg cells, underscoring the distinct metabolic requirements that govern the functions of Teff and Treg cells (6, 35). Similarly, upon activation, CD8+ T cells undergo a metabolic shift toward aerobic glycolysis, driven by GLUT1, mTOR, and Myc. However, memory CD8+ T cells adopt a distinct metabolic profile, primarily relying on FAO and mitochondrial OxPhos for energy. Intriguingly, sustained glycolysis during activation impairs the formation of memory T cells, suggesting that a return to oxidative metabolism is essential for the establishment of long-lived memory phenotypes (36).

NK cells

NK cells are innate lymphocytes that play a critical role in the immune defense against microbial infections and tumor cells. Unlike adaptive lymphocytes, NK cells can exert cytotoxic activity without prior sensitization, primarily through the engagement of activating receptors and ligands expressed on target cells (37). Upon activation, NK cells eliminate targets by releasing cytolytic granules containing perforin and granzymes, secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ and tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α), or by activating death receptor pathways (38).

In their resting state, NK cells exhibit a hypometabolic profile and rely predominantly on OxPhos for energy production. Activation triggers a robust metabolic reprogramming characterized by a marked up-regulation of both glycolysis and OxPhos. This shift is largely dependent on mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signaling and is associated with increased glucose uptake and enhanced aerobic glycolysis (39). Simultaneously, mitochondrial respiration and OxPhos capacity are elevated, accompanied by increased mitochondrial mass (40). Pyruvate produced through glycolysis is converted into acetyl-CoA, which fuels the citrate-malate shuttle to generate reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) for OxPhos within mitochondria, while also serving as a substrate for acetylation reactions and fatty acid biosynthesis in the cytoplasm (41). Activated NK cells primarily depend on glucose as their metabolic substrate and do not substantially engage glutaminolysis or use glutamine-derived carbon through the TCA cycle (42). The role of fatty acids as an energy source in NK cells remains incompletely understood; however, evidence suggests that excess lipid accumulation impairs NK cell function, indicating that lipid metabolism must be tightly regulated (43).

Emerging evidence highlights the importance of metabolic adaptation in NK cell education (also known as licensing), a developmental process essential for functional maturation and self-tolerance. Educated NK cells exhibit higher expression of glucose transporters and increased glycolytic rates compared to their uneducated counterparts, while the OxPhos capacity remains relatively unchanged (40, 44). These findings underscore the role of glycolytic metabolism in shaping the effector potential and responsiveness of NK cells.

B cells

Precursor B cells, including B1 and B2 subtypes, originate in the bone marrow or fetal liver and undergo a series of sequential developmental stages in peripheral lymphoid organs before fully maturing in response to antigen stimulation (45). This entire developmental process is tightly regulated by complex metabolic programs. Key lineage-defining transcription factors, such as paired box protein 5 (PAX5) and Ikaros family zinc finger 1 (IKZF1), not only control the expression of B cell–specific genes but also shape the metabolic profile of B cells to meet biosynthetic and energetic demands at each stage (46).

Before entering the proliferative S phase, B cells primarily rely on glycolysis for energy production. During the S phase, the PPP is activated to provide ribose-5-phosphate necessary for nucleotide biosynthesis. This proliferative phase is marked by increased consumption of essential amino acids and elevated lactate production, reflecting heightened metabolic activity. Glutamine serves as a critical carbon source, particularly supporting immunoglobulin synthesis (47). Following antigen stimulation, B cells undergo substantial metabolic reprogramming. Their metabolism shifts prominently toward aerobic glycolysis to support the energetic and anabolic requirements of rapid cell growth, clonal expansion, and antibody production (48). Activation of the B cell receptor (BCR) substantially enhances glucose uptake and accelerates glycolytic flux, leading to increased lactate generation (49). In addition, stimulation with LPS promotes intracellular fatty acid synthesis, which supports cell proliferation and up-regulation of plasma cell differentiation markers such as CD138 and B lymphocyte–induced maturation protein 1 (Blimp-1) (50). Costimulation with CD40 ligand (CD40L) and IL-4 induces pronounced metabolic stress, resulting in the accumulation of ROS. In this context, glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3) acts as a crucial metabolic stress sensor. GSK3 negatively regulates c-Myc–dependent cell growth, mitochondrial biogenesis, glycolysis, and ROS production, thereby alleviating metabolic pressure and protecting B cells from oxidative damage (51).

Neutrophils

Neutrophils are the most abundant innate immune cells and serve as the first line of defense against infection by rapidly migrating to affected tissues to initiate potent antimicrobial responses. Their effector functions include chemotaxis, phagocytosis, production of ROS, secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and mediators, and the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) through a process known as NETosis (52). These immune functions are tightly regulated by distinct metabolic pathways that govern neutrophil development, migration, and activation.

The differentiation of hematopoietic stem cells into mature neutrophils—termed granulopoiesis—demands high energy input to support cytoplasmic expansion and nuclear remodeling (53). During early differentiation stages, neutrophil-committed progenitors and precursors rely heavily on mitochondrial oxidative metabolism, including FAO, the TCA cycle, and OxPhos, to generate ATP (54). As neutrophils mature, mitochondrial activity gradually diminishes, and glycolysis becomes the dominant energy source. Mature neutrophils primarily depend on glycolysis to sustain key effector functions such as chemotaxis, ROS production, apoptosis, and NET formation. In addition, the PPP and purinergic signaling pathways are essential modulators of neutrophil responses. The PPP provides reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH), which fuels NADPH oxidase activity responsible for the bulk of ROS generation critical to microbial killing. Chemotaxis, in particular, is driven by autocrine purinergic signaling that begins with a burst of mitochondrial ATP release followed by sustained ATP production through glycolysis (55). To maintain energy homeostasis under stress or glucose-limited conditions, neutrophils can also use intracellular glycogen stores through glycogenolysis. This glycogen cycling ensures continued support for their antimicrobial functions even in metabolically challenging environments. GLYCOLYSIS-DERIVED PTMS IN IMMUNE CELLS

Lactate and lactylation

l-Lactate has traditionally been regarded as a waste by-product of glycolysis, but emerging evidence highlights its notable physiological roles, such as serving as a fuel for the Krebs cycle (56). Activated immune cells, which have a high demand for glucose, exhibit the Warburg effect, resulting in the production of large amounts of l-lactate (57). In 2019, Zhao and co-workers (58) found a previously unidentified histone PTM called lysine lactylation (originally Kla, but hereafter KL-la), which is induced by l-lactate. Notably, the direct metabolite l-lactyl-CoA—hypothesized to be the lactyl group donor in this modification during Zhao and co-workers’ study—was first detected and quantified in mammalian cells and tissues using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry in 2020 (59), validating its existence and providing a critical metabolic basis for l-lactylation. Subsequently, two isomers—N-ε-(carboxyethyl)-lysine (Kce) and d-lactyl-lysine (KD-la, originally called lactoylation)—were identified, formed through uncatalyzed reactions with glycolytic by-products S-D-lactoylglutathione (D-LGSH) and methylglyoxal (MGO), respectively (60, 61). Existing research mainly focuses on KL-la, which is the primary lactylation isomer and the only one dynamically regulated by glycolysis (62, 63). This review focuses on lysine l-lactylation.

l-Lactylation, particularly histone l-lactylation, is catalyzed by lysine acetyltransferase (KAT) enzymes, including P300 (59). Two recently identified lactyl-CoA synthetases, acetyl-CoA synthetase 2 (ACSS2) and guanosine triphosphate (GTP)–specific SCS (GTPSCS), generate l-lactyl-CoA, which supplies the lactyl group (64, 65). Class I histone deacetylases (HDAC1 to HDAC3) and SIRT1 to SIRT3 have been identified as the “erasers” of l-lactylation; notably, HDAC1 and HDAC3 play a site-specific deacylation role in cells (66). Enzyme activities involved in glycolysis and mitochondrial metabolism influence l-lactylation by modulating endogenous l-lactate levels. Moreover, l-lactylation of glycolytic enzymes under hyperglycemic conditions can be interpreted as a feedback mechanism to suppress glycolytic flux. l-Lactylation is expected to fill the theoretical gap between l-lactate and cellular phenotypes. Aberrant l-lactylation has now been implicated in cancer, fibrosis, sepsis, cardiometabolic diseases, and neurodegenerative disorders (67–70).

Lactylation in macrophages

The recent discovery of l-lactylation provides previously unrecognized insights into the implications of Warburg metabolism in macrophages. In the early stages, glycolysis is required for pro-inflammatory gene expression; however, in later stages, the main product of glycolysis, l-lactate, induces histone l-lactylation of reparative genes, which is crucial for the resolution of inflammation and maintenance of homeostasis (71). Zhang et al. (58) were the first to identify histone l-lactylation in bacterially challenged macrophages and found that increased l-lactylation during the late phase of M1 polarization drives the expression of M2-like genes, including wound healing–related arginase 1 (ARG1). A subsequent study confirmed that l-lactate–derived l-lactylation was involved in the transition from inflammatory to reparative macrophage phenotypes (72). The deficiency of B cell adapter for PI3K (BCAP) down-regulated the glycolytic enzyme phosphofructokinase-3 (PFKFB3) and mTORC1 signaling, which attenuated aerobic glycolysis and l-lactate production, further decreasing histone l-lactylation of reparative genes in macrophages. Providing exogenous l-lactate successfully rescued inducible histone l-lactylation and the reparative transition (72).

In addition to histone modification, l-lactylation has also been identified in nonhistone proteins. Pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) has been recognized as a critical determinant of the Warburg effect in pro-inflammatory macrophages (5). PTMs have been shown to regulate the structural and functional properties of PKM2, including its enzymatic activity and subcellular localization. A recent study found that l-lactate–derived l-lactylation at the K62 site of PKM2 suppressed its tetramer-to-dimer transition, enhancing its pyruvate kinase activity and decreasing its nuclear distribution. l-Lactylation–activated PKM2 inhibited glycolysis, up-regulated the expression of ARG1, and down-regulated inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), thus facilitating the pro-inflammatory–to–reparative transition of macrophages (73).

Microglia, tissue-specific macrophages in the central nervous system and retina, shift to the M1 phenotype under pathological stimulation (74). In Alzheimer’s disease, histone H4 lysine 12 l-lactylation was elevated in Aβ plaque–adjacent microglia, primarily enriching at the promoters of glycolytic genes, thereby increasing glycolytic activity and l-lactate production. This glycolysis/H4K12la/PKM2 axis forms a positive feedback loop, exacerbating microglial dysfunction and facilitating Alzheimer’s disease pathogenesis (68). Moreover, H3K18la directly stimulates nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signaling by facilitating binding to the promoter regions of Rela (p65) and NF-κB1 (p50) in senescent microglia, elevating the levels of senescence-associated secretory phenotype components such as IL-6 and IL-8 (75).

In the autoimmune uveitis model, elevated l-lactate production and pan-lysine l-lactylation level were confirmed, and p300-catalyzed Yin-Yang 1 (YY1) l-lactylation substantially contributed to microglia activation, migration, and proliferation both in vitro and in vivo (76). A previous study demonstrated that hyper–l-lactylated YY1 in microglia enhances FGF2 transcription and promotes retinal neovascularization (77). Meanwhile, l-lactylation is involved in the communication between microglial metabolism and astrogenesis. Mechanistically, H4K12la promotes the transcription of leucine-rich repeat containing 15 (LRRC15), which interacts with CD248 to trigger the Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducers and activators of transcription 3 (STAT3) pathway, further regulating astrogenesis (78). In spinal cord injury (SCI), H4K12la enhanced programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) transcription in microglia, thereby facilitating microglial scar formation and SCI repair (79). Correspondingly, single-cell RNA sequencing revealed the neuroprotective effects of l-lactylation in the subacute phase of SCI. Administration of l-lactate elevated l-lactylation levels and suppressed the pro-inflammatory phenotype of microglia, thus improving locomotor recovery (80).

In tumors, l-lactate accumulation is a hallmark of the TME, which has been recognized as a prominent immunosuppressive factor enhancing M2 polarization. l-Lactylation can be derived from both glucose-dependent endogenous and exogenous l-lactate. High l-lactylation levels in TAMs have been confirmed in two mouse models of cancer, supporting the idea that this modification plays a vital role in the alternatively activated profile of TAMs (59). In glioblastoma, a population of macrophages exhibits high GLUT1 levels induced by tumor-derived factors, which activate the protein kinase RNA-like ER kinase (PERK)–activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) axis. Intracellular l-lactate–driven histone l-lactylation promotes IL-10 expression and suppresses T cells (81). To summarize, recent advances in l-lactylation have substantially improved the theoretical framework of l-lactate–mediated effects on macrophages.

Lactylation in T cells

l-Lactate, a glycolytic metabolite, has been recognized as a crucial signaling molecule that exerts immunosuppressive effects on T cells, influencing their survival, differentiation, and cytokine production (82). l-Lactylation has emerged as a notable mechanism explaining how l-lactate accumulation in the microenvironment affects T cells. In tumor-specific CTLs from KRAS-mutant cancers, l-lactate–derived histone l-lactylation promotes the transcription of circular RNA of ataxin 7 (circATXN7), which binds to the NF-κB p65 subunit, inhibiting its nuclear localization and thereby enhancing sensitivity to activation-induced cell death (83). Gu et al. (84) proposed that l-lactate could promote the development and function of Treg cells through l-lactylation of K72 in MOESIN, which enhances transforming growth factor–β (TGF-β) signaling via TGF-βRI. Furthermore, the application of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) inhibitors was shown to enhance the efficacy of anti–PD-1 therapy in hepatoma mouse models.

A recent study demonstrated that l-lactate treatment induced a robust phenotypic switch of TH17 cells to Treg cells. In l-lactate–treated TH17 cells, increased H3K18 histone l-lactylation was observed in genes associated with T cell receptor signaling, including NF-κB and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways, particularly at the proximal promoter region of Foxp3 (85). In l-lactate–treated or glioma stem cell–cocultured CD4+ T cells, histone H3K18 l-lactylation elevated the expression of CCR8, disrupting the Treg/TH17 balance. In addition, l-lactylation–mediated up-regulation of ectonucleotidases CD39 and CD73 contributes to the accumulation of adenosine, a key immunosuppressive factor (86).

Lactylation in other immune cells

A recent study indicated that metformin reduced H3K18 l-lactylation and decreased ROS levels, which attenuated neutrophil recruitment in a zebrafish model of inflammation (87). In addition, ablation of heterotrimeric guanine nucleotide–binding protein–coupled receptor 37 (GPR37) in tumors reduced the production of chemokines CXCL1 and CXCL5, thereby preventing neutrophil recruitment in the TME of colorectal cancer liver metastasis (88). In tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells (TIMs), l-lactate accumulation in the TME strongly up-regulated the expression of methyltransferase 3 (METTL3) via H3K18 l-lactylation (89). METTL3 catalyzes N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification of JAK1 mRNA, promoting its translation and subsequent phosphorylation of STAT3, thus contributing to the immunosuppressive functions of TIMs (90). Notably, two l-lactylation sites in the zinc-finger domain of METTL3 are essential for its ability to capture target RNA. The H3K18 l-lactylation-ROS loop has been identified as a mechanism exacerbating neutrophil recruitment in zebrafish under light conditions (91). Histone l-lactylation enhances the transcription of the duox gene, leading to ROS production, which in turn elevates l-lactylation, creating a positive feedback loop (92). TCA CYCLE–DERIVED PTMS IN IMMUNE CELLS

Malonyl-CoA and malonylation (Kma)

Malonyl-CoA is converted from acetyl-CoA by ACC for the synthesis of fatty acids or cholesterol and also serves as a substrate for transferring malonyl groups to lysine residues. Malonylation was first detected in 2011 as an evolutionarily conserved and dynamic PTM in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes (93). This modification shifts the charge of lysine from +1 to −1, thereby altering the protein structure and interfering with electrostatic interactions (94). Kma is presumed to be catalyzed by acetyltransferases KATs, as in other acylations, although research on its specific modifying enzymes remains limited. In a comprehensive analysis of 18 known deacetylases, including HDAC1 to HDAC11 and SIRT1 to SIRT7, only SIRT5 exhibited notable lysine demalonylase activity (77, 93). The intracellular concentration of malonyl-CoA is controlled by several metabolic enzymes, including ACC, propionyl-CoA carboxylase (PCC), fatty acid synthase (FASN), and malonyl-CoA decarboxylase (MCD) (95).

Kma has been reported to participate in diverse physiological processes, including inflammation, angiogenesis, and metabolic disorders (23, 96). It has been proposed that increased Kma is associated with compromised mitochondrial function and reduced fatty acid oxidation, contributing to the development of malonic aciduria (97). In type 2 diabetes, elevated Kma levels have been identified, and the malonylated proteins are enriched in glucose and fatty acid metabolism pathways, echoing previous findings of accumulated malonyl-CoA in skeletal muscle samples from patients with type 2 diabetes (98). In endothelial cells, malonylation of mTOR inhibits its kinase activity, thereby impeding angiogenesis and neovascularization (96). Elevated malonylation levels have also been detected during the late activation phase of macrophages, where Kma on glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) promotes its dissociation from mRNA, thereby facilitating the translation of pro-inflammatory genes (23). In conclusion, these studies reveal a close association between malonylation and metabolic reprogramming in various physiological and pathological contexts.

Malonylation in macrophages

In LPS-stimulated macrophages, GAPDH undergoes malonylation at K213, which enhances its glycolytic activity while attenuating its RNA binding capacity. The dissociation of GAPDH from TNF-α mRNA facilitates transcript translation, whereas the released GAPDH reengages in glycolysis, collectively contributing to a dual mechanism driving the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-6 (23). A recent study demonstrated that treatment with a sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2i) alleviated LPS-induced macrophage inflammation by suppressing Kma. Mechanistically, the SGLT2i elevated ketone body levels and down-regulated ACC1 expression, resulting in reduced malonyl-CoA availability and diminished GAPDH malonylation (99). In parallel, He et al. (100) reported that SIRT5 interacts with DDX3 and demalonylates it, thereby activating the TBK1-IRF3 signaling axis and enhancing type I IFN production to bolster antiviral innate immunity.

Malonylation in other immune cells

Although studies on Kma in T cells remain limited, available evidence highlights its potential relevance in immune regulation. In endothelial cells, silencing of FASN or treatment with malonyl N-acetylcysteamine (malonyl-NAC) induces mTOR malonylation at K1218, impairing mTORC1 kinase activity (96). Given the pivotal role of mTOR signaling in T cell metabolism and function, this modification merits further investigation in T cell–specific contexts. In autoimmune disease, integrated proteomic and malonylomic analyses of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) identified several differentially malonylated proteins associated with the NETosis pathway, implicating Kma in neutrophil-mediated inflammation and RA pathogenesis (101).

Succinyl-CoA and succinylation (Ksuc)

Succinyl-CoA is a crucial metabolic intermediate in the TCA cycle, generated from the catabolism of odd-chain fatty acids, which are converted to propionyl-CoA before being further metabolized into succinyl-CoA, along with certain branched-chain amino acids. Lysine succinylation is a PTM defined as the partial transfer of succinyl-CoA to lysine residues, first reported in 2011 (102). Similar to Kma, Ksuc results in a two-unit charge shift from positive to negative. On the one hand, succinylation can occur as a spontaneous, nonenzymatic process, regulated by the concentration of succinyl-CoA and the pH value (103). On the other hand, enzymatic activities of succinyltransferases and desuccinylases add complexity to the regulation of Ksuc. Mitochondrial α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex (KGDHC) mediates the succinylation of mitochondrial proteins through its E2k succinyltransferase activity (104). KAT2A (also known as GCN5) serves as a histone H3 succinyltransferase, coupled with KGDHC in the nucleus (105). In addition, carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A) has been identified as a lysine succinyltransferase (106). Furthermore, typical acyltransferases such as p300/CREB, histone acetyltransferases (HATs), and HBO1 have been shown to succinylate H3K122 in HepG2 cancer cells (107). Both SIRT5 and SIRT7 exhibit desuccinylase activity; SIRT7 specifically desuccinylates histone Ksuc, while SIRT5 displays desuccinylation across all cellular compartments (77).

Ksuc has a substantial impact on gene expression and protein functions across a wide range of biological processes. A series of proteomic studies using mass spectrometry has established a comprehensive landscape of succinylated substrates in the cytoplasm, mitochondrion, and nucleus. Notably, these proteins are largely enriched in metabolic pathways, including the TCA cycle, lipid biosynthesis, β-oxidation, amino acid catabolism, and OxPhos (106, 108). These findings provide strong evidence that succinylation is closely linked to metabolic processes and warrants further investigation.

Succinylation in macrophages

Because of a reduction in succinate dehydrogenase activity in the TCA cycle, succinate accumulates during the later stages of macrophage activation, leading to elevated levels of succinylation, particularly in M1 macrophages. In LPS-stimulated macrophages, several metabolic enzymes, including GAPDH and malate dehydrogenase, have been found to undergo succinylation, although the functional consequences remain unclear (25). PKM2 is succinylated at K311, which impairs its enzymatic activity and promotes its nuclear translocation by facilitating the tetramer-to-dimer transition. Wang et al. (109) demonstrated that the loss of SIRT5 results in hypersuccinylation of PKM2 and an increase in IL-1β production, with SIRT5-deficient mice exhibiting increased susceptibility to dextran sulfate–induced colitis.

PKM2 has also been found to be succinylated at K498 in tumor cells, which enhances its enzymatic activity (110). This seemingly contradictory effect may be attributed to the domain organization and functional compartmentalization of modification sites. K311 is located at the interface of the catalytic and regulatory domains of PKM2, where it likely inhibits enzyme activity by disrupting the substrate binding pocket and promoting tetramer-dimer conversion by disrupting hydrogen bonding. In contrast, K498 is situated within the C-terminal regulatory domain, where it may enhance enzyme activity through allosteric activation and stabilization of the enzyme’s conformational state.

Furthermore, while elevated succinylation promotes pro-inflammatory responses in M1 macrophages, it has also been suggested to support M2 polarization and reduce inflammation. Mechanistically, α-ketoglutarate (αKG) inhibits SIRT5-dependent desuccinylation of the PDH complex, thereby maintaining its activity and promoting OxPhos. This, in turn, supports M2 polarization and alleviates inflammation and organ injury in a sepsis mouse model (96).

Succinylation in T cells

A recent study by Wu et al. (111) revealed that succinylation plays a critical role in the predisposition of CD4+ T cells to differentiate into tissue-resident memory T cells (Trm cells) in RA. Mechanistically, the accumulation of mitochondrial succinyl-CoA leads to the succinylation of bromodomain containing 2 (BRD2). Succinylated BRD2 impairs the transcription of proteasomal subunit Psmb5, resulting in dysregulated proteasome-mediated protein degradation. This, in turn, causes elevated Hobit protein levels, contributing to abnormal Trm cell differentiation in RA. PKM2 is another key target of succinylation, with contrasting effects on its nuclear translocation depending on the modified site. It was shown that nuclear PKM2 promotes TH1/TH17 polarization by inducing STAT3 phosphorylation, thereby facilitating IL-17 transcription (112).

Fumarate-succination

Succination refers to the modification of cysteine thiol groups by fumarate, resulting in the formation of 2-(succino)cysteine (2SC) (113). This fumarate-mediated modification was initially observed in diabetes and obesity (114) and later confirmed in fumarate hydratase–deficient renal cell carcinoma, where accumulated fumarate caused metabolic and epigenetic rewiring (114). Since its discovery, succination has been found to occur widely on proteins involved in all major cellular processes across various cellular compartments. Several metabolic enzymes, including GAPDH, alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH), and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PDH), have been shown to undergo succination at functionally important cysteine residues, leading to reduced enzymatic activities (113). In fumarate hydratase–deficient renal cell carcinoma, succination of iron regulatory protein 2 (IRP2) inhibits its activity, disrupting iron homeostasis (115). In addition, proteins involved in the organization and maintenance of mitochondrial DNA, such as transcription factor A, mitochondrial (TFAM), TWINKLE, and DNA polymerase subunit gamma (POLG), have been found to be succinated, which interferes with mitochondrial DNA replication and maintenance (116). Compared to other acylations, research on succination remains in its early stages. The modifying mechanisms and functional implications are still largely unclear, and the measurement of stoichiometry and profiling of succination remain technically challenging. However, recent advances in isotopic tandem orthogonal proteolysis activity–based protein profiling offer an indirect quantitative approach to profile protein succination (117).

Succination in macrophages

Dimethyl fumarate (DMF), a fumarate derivative, is an immunomodulatory drug approved for the treatment of psoriasis and multiple sclerosis (118). Studies have shown that DMF succinates the catalytic cysteine of GAPDH in murine macrophages, T cells, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients with multiple sclerosis who were treated with DMF. In macrophages, succination of GAPDH inhibits its enzymatic activity, thereby reducing glycolytic flux and impairing IL-1β production (119). In addition, both exogenous DMF and endogenous fumarate have been shown to succinate gasdermin D (GSDMD), contributing to their anti-inflammatory effects. Caspase cleavage of the GSDMD N-terminal domain causes plasma membrane perforation and the release of danger-associated molecular patterns, triggering pyroptosis and inflammatory responses. In bone marrow–derived macrophages treated with LPS, S-(2-succinyl)-cysteine modification on GSDMD prevents its interaction with caspases, inhibiting the subsequent pyroptotic process (120).

Succination in T cells

In naïve CD4+ T cells, DMF, a fumarate derivative, induces glycolytic blockade by succinating GAPDH, thereby inhibiting its enzymatic activity and reducing glycolytic flux. This impairment leads to decreased differentiation of TH1 and TH17 cells, as well as reduced production of IFN-γ and IL-17 (119). Notably, DMF-induced inactivation of GAPDH selectively dampens the function of highly glycolytic Teff cells while sparing oxidative Treg cells and naïve T cells, highlighting immunometabolism as a potential therapeutic target. Furthermore, the discovery of fumarate-induced succination suggests an intrinsic negative feedback loop regulating immune responses. Itaconate and 2,3-dicarboxypropylation

Itaconate is one of the most abundant intermediates of the Krebs cycle in classically activated macrophages, resulting from citrate accumulation and up-regulation of cis-aconitate decarboxylase (CAD) (24). Known as an anti-inflammatory metabolite, itaconate has recently been identified as a cysteine modifier, with this modification referred to as 2,3-dicarboxypropylation (2,3-DCP) (121). The cell-permeable itaconate derivative 4-octyl itaconate (4-OI) alkylates several cysteine residues on Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1), destabilizing it and thereby protecting nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (NRF2) from KEAP1-induced degradation. As a result, the anti-inflammatory transcription factor NRF2 initiates antioxidant responses and inhibits the production of certain pro-inflammatory cytokines (121). Researchers have proposed that endogenous itaconate can also induce 2,3-DCP on a number of proteins, including LDHA, annexin A1, and IFN-γ–inducible lysosomal thiol reductase (GILT) (121). A thiol-reactive probe was subsequently developed to provide a comprehensive profile of endogenous itaconate-derived cysteine modifications in macrophage lysates (122). This was followed by the creation of a biorthogonal itaconate-alkyne probe for chemoproteomic profiling of itaconation in living macrophages. In this screen, numerous immune targets of 2,3-DCP were identified (123). Qin et al. reported that 2,3-DCP on C24 of GAPDH had no effect on its enzymatic activity, whereas another study showed that this modification on C22 reduced glycolytic activity and further lowered pro-inflammatory cytokine production (124). A recent study has enabled the quantification of S-2-succinocysteine and 2,3-dicarboxypropylcysteine in macrophages by mass spectrometry, a method that will facilitate further exploration of these immunometabolite-mediated PTMs in other isolated cell types or tissues (125).

LIPID METABOLISM–DERIVED PTMS IN IMMUNE CELLS

Palmitic acid-palmitoylation

Palmitoylation, first reported by J. Folch in 1951, is one of the major types of lipidation (126). This PTM occurs through thioester linkages of 16-carbon palmitic acid to cysteine residues, increasing the hydrophobicity of targeted proteins and enhancing protein-lipid bilayer interactions. Palmitoylation is classified into S-palmitoylation, N-palmitoylation, and O-palmitoylation on the basis of the type of linkage, with S-palmitoylation being the most extensively studied (127). The modification is catalyzed by palmitoyl acyltransferases (PATs), which are referred to as zDHHC enzymes because of their conserved catalytic domain, the zinc finger Asp-His-His-Cys (ZDHHC). Depalmitoylation is carried out by acyl protein thioesterases (APTs) and α-β hydrolase domain–containing 17 proteins (128). Palmitoyl-CoA, the donor molecule, is generated by cytosolic acyl-CoA synthetases (ACSs). Palmitoylation plays a key role in protein sorting, localization, stability, protein-protein interactions, and enzymatic activity (129).

Palmitoylation in macrophages

Palmitoylation has been shown to play a critical role in macrophage-mediated innate immune responses. The nucleotide oligomerization domain–like receptors 1 and 2 (NOD1/2) are crucial pattern recognition receptors that respond to peptidoglycans associated with microorganisms (130). In macrophages, S-palmitoylation of NOD1/2, catalyzed by ZDHHC5, is required for their membrane localization to bacterium-containing endosomes and activation of downstream NF-κB and MAPK signaling (131).

The palmitoylation of NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) by ZDHHC7 is essential for activating the NLRP3 inflammasome. In contrast, ZDHHC12-mediated palmitoylation of C841 occurs after NLRP3 activation, terminating inflammasome signaling (132). ZDHHC5 was also found to palmitoylate NLRP3 at the leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domain, with ABHD17A responsible for its depalmitoylation (133). Notably, phenylpyruvate binds to palmitoyl-protein thioesterase 1 (PPT1), suppressing depalmitoylase activity, which leads to increased palmitoylation of NLRP3 and enhances inflammatory responses in diabetic wounds (134). Given that FASN generates intracellular palmitic acid (PA) from acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA, FASN inhibitors have been shown to exert anti-inflammatory effects by down-regulating palmitoylation in macrophages (135). A recent study confirmed that pharmacological or genetic depletion of FASN dampens NLRP3 activation (136).

The fatty acid translocase CD36 is the first well-studied scavenger receptor regulated by S-palmitoylation. In macrophages, CD36 is responsible for recognizing and internalizing disease-associated ligands, such as oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL). CD36 was initially reported to be palmitoylated on both N- and C-terminal cytoplasmic tails (137). It has since been shown that S-palmitoylation modulates CD36’s endoplasmic reticulum (ER) processing, trafficking through the secretory pathway, and incorporation into lipid rafts, all of which are required for fatty acid uptake (138). Palmitoyl acyltransferases ZDHHC4 and ZDHHC5 function at different subcellular locations to control this modification, and depletion of ZDHHC4/5 disrupts CD36-dependent fatty acid uptake (139). Consistently, CD36 palmitoylation mutants show decreased efficiency in oxLDL uptake (138).

The ER-localized protein selenoprotein K (Selk) is a crucial cofactor for maintaining the stable, localized expression of palmitoylated CD36 in macrophages, contributing to foam cell formation and atherogenesis (140). Selk was found to interact with ER-localized ZDHHC6 via the SH3/SH3 binding domain, suggesting an alternative or parallel pathway for regulating CD36 S-palmitoylation (141). In patients with nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, CD36 S-palmitoylation levels were elevated, and the blockade of CD36 palmitoylation ameliorated fatty acid metabolic disorders and suppressed inflammation in vitro and in vivo, offering a potential therapeutic strategy for liver steatosis (142). In addition, macrophage ATF3 can increase ZDHHC4/5–mediated palmitoylation at C3, C7, C464, and C466 of CD36, which attenuates hepatocyte steatosis and fibrogenesis in hepatic stellate cells, thus preventing the progression of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (143).

S-palmitoylation was involved in the phagocytic defects following HIV-1 infection. In uninfected phagocytes, ZDHHC20 palmitoylated viral toxin Tat on C31, which enabled Tat accumulation via increasing its affinity to phosphatidylinositol (4,5)-bisphosphate within the plasma membrane, thus impeding the phagocytosis and neurosecretion (144). Similarly, S-palmitoylation controls the intoxication, lipid raft localization, and oligomerization of anthrax toxin and aerolysin (145).

The stimulator of IFN genes (STING) is a critical immunoregulatory protein, particularly regulated by palmitoylation. Abrogating STING palmitoylation substantially impairs type I IFN production (146). Down-regulation of STING palmitoylation has been implicated in the immune-evasion mechanisms of Staphylococcus aureus (147). Wang et al. (148) demonstrated that STING palmitoylation promotes its trafficking from the ER to the Golgi, activating downstream signaling and facilitating the secretion of pro-inflammatory and profibrotic cytokines. This exacerbates metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis and inhibits energy expenditure. The itaconate derivative 4-OI can directly alkylate STING at C91, blocking STING palmitoylation and oligomerization (149). H-151, a potent STING palmitoylation antagonist, inhibits STING and further suppresses the development of choroidal neovascularization and fluorescent leakage from neovessels (150).

A recent study showed that PPT1 regulates systemic autoimmunity by removing S-palmitoylation from C258 and C265 of TLR9 in lysosomes, promoting the secretion of TNF by macrophages in systemic lupus erythematosus (151). In addition, S-palmitoylation facilitates macrophage pyroptosis by promoting GSDMD membrane translocation and pore formation. In a septic mouse model, inhibiting GSDMD palmitoylation reduced pyroptosis and IL-1β release, alleviating organ damage and improving survival (152).

Palmitoylation in other immune cells

During T cell receptor engagement, palmitoylation of the linker for activation of T cells (LAT) regulates its recruitment to the immunological synapse and its localization within glycolipid-enriched microdomains. Defective LAT palmitoylation has been identified as an upstream event in the induction of T cell anergy (153). Notably, palmitoylation also plays a critical role in stabilizing the key T cell immune checkpoint molecule, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1). ZDHHC3 mediates palmitoylation of PD-L1 at the C272 residue within its transmembrane domain, thereby preventing its ubiquitination and subsequent lysosomal degradation. Both pharmacological inhibition of palmitoylation using 2-bromopalmitate and genetic silencing of ZDHHC3 reduce PD-L1 palmitoylation, leading to a marked enhancement of T cell–mediated antitumor responses (154). While current immune checkpoint blockade therapies target PD-L1 at the cell surface, intracellular reservoirs of PD-L1 and its recycling to the plasma membrane have emerged as potential mechanisms of therapeutic resistance. In this context, targeting PD-L1 palmitoylation represents a promising strategy for overcoming resistance and enhancing immunotherapeutic efficacy.

Moreover, the dynamic palmitoylation-depalmitoylation cycle has been shown to govern TH17 cell differentiation by potentiating STAT3 activation, a pivotal transcriptional regulator in this lineage. ZDHHC7 catalyzes S-palmitoylation of STAT3 at C108, promoting its membrane localization and subsequent phosphorylation. Conversely, APT2 (also known as LYPLA2) mediates the depalmitoylation of phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3), thereby facilitating its nuclear translocation. Notably, pharmacological inhibition of either ZDHHC7 or APT2 effectively suppresses TH17 differentiation and ameliorates inflammatory bowel disease symptoms in vivo (155).

In neutrophils, TLR signaling—mediated via myeloid differentiation primary response protein 88 (MYD88)—relies on palmitoylation by ZDHHC6 at C113, a modification critical for IL-1 receptor–associated kinase 4 (IRAK4) recruitment and downstream signaling activation. Inhibition of ZDHHC6-mediated palmitoylation effectively dampens TLR/MYD88 signaling in response to LPS stimulation (156). In B cells, palmitoylation of human germinal center–associated lymphoma protein (HGAL) targets it to membrane raft microdomains, where it promotes BCR activation while concurrently suppressing chemoattractant-induced cell motility (157). Intriguingly, palmitoylation also contributes to the negative regulation of BCR signaling through the F-box protein 10 (FBXO10)–HGAL axis. Upon BCR engagement, FBXO10 undergoes rapid palmitoylation and translocates to the plasma membrane, where it ubiquitylates and facilitates the degradation of HGAL (158).

Myristic acid and myristoylation

Myristoylation is an evolutionarily conserved lipid modification first identified in the bovine brain in 1982 (159). It involves the attachment of the long-chain fatty acid myristic acid to the N terminus of glycine or lysine residues, a process catalyzed by N-myristoyltransferase (NMT) (160). In most instances, myristoylation occurs cotranslationally, where it is added to nascent proteins following the removal of the methionine initiator by methionine aminopeptidase 2 (MetAP2). However, in certain contexts, such as during apoptosis, myristoylation occurs posttranslationally (161). In mammalian cells, two isozymes of NMT, NMT1 and NMT2, exhibit functional specificity and differential substrate preferences (162). The canonical myristoylation at the NH2-terminal glycine is considered irreversible, while myristoylation on the Nε-side chain of lysine residues can be displaced by sirtuins and HDACs (163). Notably, SIRT6 exhibits demyristoylase activity that surpasses its deacetylation function (164).

Similar to palmitoylation, myristoylation substantially influences cellular functions such as intracellular trafficking, lipid-protein interactions, protein stability and degradation, and signal transduction. Dysregulated myristoylation has been linked to a variety of diseases, including cancer, infectious diseases, metabolic disorders, and autoimmune diseases (161, 165).

Myristoylation in macrophages

In macrophages, myristoylation of the TRIF-related adaptor molecule directs its localization to the plasma membrane, where it facilitates TLR4 signaling in response to LPS stimulation (166). Similarly, endonuclease-exonuclease-phosphatase family domain containing 1 (EEPD1/KIAA1706) is a direct transcriptional target of liver X receptors, which enhance cellular cholesterol efflux in macrophages by regulating the abundance of ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1). Myristoylation at the N terminus is critical for its targeting to the plasma membrane (167). Moreover, N-myristoylation of ARF1 inhibits innate antiviral immune responses against herpes simplex virus–1 infection by suppressing STING-induced IFN responses in macrophages while promoting STING-triggered autophagy, suggesting a therapeutic strategy for modulating STING activation (168).

Myristoylation in T cells

In synergy with palmitoylation, myristoylation regulates the transmission of TCR signals by modulating the subcellular localization of key molecules, including Fyn, Lck, and the myristoylated alanine-rich C-kinase substrate (MARCKS) (169, 170). T cell–specific deletion of NMT1 and NMT2 results in defective TCR signaling and impairs thymocyte development, especially during the transition from DN3 (CD25+CD44−) to DN4 (CD25−CD44−) stages (170). Paradoxically, reduced myristoylation has been shown to promote T cell hyperactivation in RA, although this phenomenon is not observed in other autoimmune diseases. In CD4+ T cells isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells of patients with RA, decreased NMT1 expression impairs AMPK activity, leading to enhanced differentiation of TH1 and TH17 cells and exacerbated synovial tissue inflammation in a murine arthritis model (171). AMINO ACID–DERIVED PTMS IN IMMUNE CELLS

Glutamylation, a modification derived from glutamate, regulates various immune effector mechanisms in macrophages. Cyclic guanosine 5′-monophosphate (GMP)–AMP synthase (cGAS), a key sensor of cytosolic DNA, catalyzes the synthesis of cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP), which activates the adaptor protein STING and triggers antiviral responses (172). Polyglutamylation of cGAS by the enzyme tubulin tyrosine ligase like 6 (TTLL6) impairs its DNA binding ability, while monoglutamylation catalyzed by TTLL4 attenuates its synthase activity. The carboxypeptidases cytosolic carboxypeptidase 5 (CCP5) and CCP6 hydrolyze the monoglutamylation and polyglutamylation of cGAS, respectively. Deficiency in CCP5 or CCP6 leads to heightened susceptibility to DNA viruses, underscoring the critical role of these modifications in immune defense (173).

Citrullination, also known as deimination, is a PTM in which peptidyl arginine deiminases (PADs) irreversibly convert arginine residues into citrulline, a nongenetically encoded amino acid (174). The PAD family consists of five isoenzymes (PAD1 to PAD4 and PAD6), each exhibiting tissue-specific expression patterns and distinct substrate preferences (175). Under normal physiological conditions, PAD activity is relatively low, but in pathological contexts, citrullination is widely observed in structural proteins, including vimentin, keratin, filaggrin, and core histones (H1, H2A, H3, and H4) (174). Dysregulated citrullination is implicated in the pathogenesis of numerous autoimmune diseases, cancers, and metabolic disorders (176). Notably, immune complexes containing citrullinated peptides have been shown to stimulate cytokine production in macrophages and promote neutrophil proliferation. However, the full spectrum of citrullination’s functional role in immune cells remains an area of active investigation.

CURRENT CHALLENGES AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

Research on therapeutic agents against metabolic PTMs

Accompanied by deepening insights into the relationship between protein modifications and diverse diseases, noteworthy advances have been made in the development of PTM-targeted therapeutic agents. In general, these therapeutic strategies can be categorized into two categories: interventions targeting modifying enzymes and regulatory molecules or targeting the modified proteins themselves. Notably, the former has made more substantial progress, as interfering with a protein is relatively simpler than targeting a single amino acid.

To date, a number of targeted agents—such as kinase agonists/inhibitors, histone deacetylase inhibitors, and histone methyltransferase inhibitors—have demonstrated notable efficacy in cancer therapy (177). In addition to broad-spectrum agents like HDAC inhibitors, emerging studies have begun to explore the therapeutic potential of metabolic PTMs in specific disease contexts. Here, we summarize recent advancements in targeting these modifications across various diseases, with a primary focus on tumors (Table 1). On the one hand, these studies have driven the development of previously unreported inhibitors targeting protein modifications; on the other hand, they underscore the value of integrating global metabolic dynamics into PTM-targeted therapeutic frameworks to enhance combinatorial treatment strategies.

Significance of metabolic PTMs in immunotherapy

Metabolic PTMs have emerged as critical regulators of immunotherapy efficacy and resistance. Palmitoylation of PD-L1 by ZDHHC3 stabilizes its expression, while inhibition of ZDHHC3 by benzosceptrin C induces lysosomal degradation, thereby synergizing with anti-CTLA4 therapy (178). In hepatocellular carcinoma, inhibition of the depalmitoylase PPT1 by ezurpimtrostat enhances anti–PD-1 immunotherapy by increasing major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) expression on hepatocellular carcinoma cell surfaces and promoting lymphocyte infiltration (179). Similarly, DC661, another PPT1 inhibitor, activates naïve T cells and enhances T cell–mediated cytotoxicity in vitro, demonstrating the potential of this therapeutic combination in immunotherapy (180). Moreover, targeting STING palmitoylation disrupts its oligomerization and downstream signaling, offering a previously unknown approach for treating cGAS-STING–mediated autoimmune diseases (149).

Recent studies indicate that reprogrammed lactate metabolism in T cells plays a complex role in antitumor immunity, with a potential dual effect that requires further investigation. The lactate-rich TME facilitates Treg recruitment, which enhances Treg immunosuppression through PD-L1 up-regulation (181). Depletion of MCT1 hinders lactate uptake in tumor-infiltrating Treg cells, restoring sensitivity to PD-1 blockade immunotherapy and potentially improving clinical outcomes (182). In non–small cell lung cancer, lactate enhances apolipoprotein C2 (APOC2) lactylation at K70, stabilizing it and triggering the release of free fatty acids, which leads to Treg accumulation and anti–PD-1 resistance (183). On the other hand, recent findings confirm that lactate enhances the stemness of CD8+ T cells by inhibiting HDAC activity, increasing TCF7 expression. Notably, CD8+ T cells pretreated with lactate effectively inhibit tumor growth upon adoptive transfer in colorectal cancer, non–small cell lung cancer, and melanoma mouse models. These findings underscore the dual role of lactate in promoting antitumor immunity while also suggesting its potential for immunotherapy sensitization (10).

Superiority and challenges of targeting PTMs

The pivotal role of PTMs in modulating biological processes and mediating responses to environmental stimuli has sparked notable interest in their potential applications in modern drug discovery. Targeted protein modification refers to a broad range of approaches designed to induce specific modifications on a protein of interest. One prominent example is proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) (184). Compared to traditional small-molecule inhibitors, PROTAC-based targeted degradation offers several distinct advantages, including reversibility, event-driven activity, the ability to target previously “undruggable” proteins, and overcoming conventional drug resistance (185). Advances in PROTAC-mediated protein ubiquitination have opened previously unexplored avenues for harnessing the autophagy-lysosomal system for therapeutic purposes. Beyond protein degradation, the toolkit for modulating proteins of interest has expanded to include selective phosphorylation, dephosphorylation, acetylation, and O-GlcNAcylation, all of which represent promising therapeutic targets. Phosphorylation-targeting chimeras (PhosTACs), for instance, focus on extensive substrate dephosphorylation by recruiting Ser/Thr phosphatases. This unique mode of action provides targeted function gain with greater selectivity and longer effective period (186).

Despite these remarkable advancements, challenges remain. The functional profiles of PTM omics are still not fully understood, particularly with regard to the competitive or antagonistic interactions between different PTMs. Furthermore, the phenomenon where PTMs on different residues of the same protein exert opposing effects complicates the development of targeted interventions. For example, succinylation at K311 of PKM2 attenuates its enzymatic activity and promotes nuclear translocation (109), whereas succinylation at K498 has the opposite effect (110). Current therapeutic agents still face notable pharmacokinetic challenges, including issues related to selectivity, feasibility, and potency. Moving forward, innovations in this field are expected to redefine the paradigm of drug discovery.

CONCLUSIONS

Over the past decade, the rapidly evolving field of immunometabolism has provided profound insights into the role of metabolites in the complex regulation of immune cells. Current evidence highlights metabolite-derived protein modifications as key intermediaries linking metabolism and immune function. While targeting these metabolic PTMs holds notable promise for cancer treatment, the development of related therapies remains in its early stages. By elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying PTM-driven immune regulation, researchers can design previously unknown cancer therapies that capitalize on the unique metabolic profiles of immune cells at specific stages. Future research is urgently needed to explore synergistic combination therapies and develop innovative analytical and therapeutic strategies. The targeting of PTMs holds considerable promise for the treatment of cancer, autoimmune diseases, and metabolic disorders in the near future.
