Proteome and Metabolome Profiling of Anticoagulant Disorders Induced by Familial Protein S Deficiency
Authors: Caiping Zhang, Yimin Zhang, Binjie Liu, Yongxv Chen, Yue Xie, Huiting Huang, Zhongqiu Liu, Zhuoru He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Protein S deficiency (PSD) is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by congenital thrombophilia. Studies on PSD are limited yet, resulting in a lack of clarity about molecular changes during abnormal coagulation. Proteomics and metabolomics analyses were conducted on the plasma of PSD patients based on liquid and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC- and GC–MS). Differential proteins and metabolites of PSD were then filtered by univariate statistical analysis and subjected to network analysis using the ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA) platform. The proteome and metabolome of PSD were obviously disturbed, and the biological pathway of coagulation and complement cascades was the most affected. During PSD, overall levels of anticoagulant protein decreased and negative regulation of thrombin production was reduced, causing the formation of fibrin clots and platelet aggregation. Furthermore, 9 differential proteins correlated significantly with protein S, comprising A2M, AGT, APOE, FGG, GPLD1, IGHV1–69, CFHR5, CPN2, and CA1. The biological networks suggested that the pathways of acute phase response, FXR/RXR activation, serotonin receptor signaling, and p70S6K signaling were associated with PSD, indicating an interaction disorder of inflammatory immune and lipid metabolism. The findings may contribute to knowledge of available functional molecules and biological pathways of familial PSD and help with treatment improvement. Data are available via ProteomeXchange with identifier PXD055111 and MetaboLights with reference number MTBLS2653.

Introduction

Protein S is a glycoprotein encoded by PROS1 in humans, with 653 amino acids and a molecular weight of 70,690.1 In plasma, S circulates in two forms. Approximately 60% of protein S is bound noncovalently to complement regulatory protein C4b-binding protein, whereas the remaining 40% is free.2 Free protein S has anticoagulant activity and serves as a cofactor to activate protein C (APC), which subsequently deactivates both factor V (F5) and factor VIII (F8).3,4 F5 and F8 are important cofactors in prothrombinase (PTase) and tenase (Xase) complexes.5 PTase comprises the enzyme factor X (F10) and the cofactor F5, whereas Xase is composed of the enzyme factor IX (F9) and its cofactor F8.6 Protein S has been found to directly bind F10, F5, and F8 and then shows autonomous APC-independent anticoagulant activity.7 Therefore, a deficiency in protein S easily triggers loss of these natural anticoagulant properties, resulting in unchecked thrombin generation, favoring thromboembolism.

Protein S deficiency (PSD) is regarded as an autosomal dominant genetic disorder in humans, with only approximately 18% of patients having no family history. The occurrence of familial PSD is reported to be 0.03–0.13% in the general population.8 In the population with venous thrombosis, the prevalence of PSD is approximately 2–13%, with 28 years being the average age of first onset.9 Moreover, approximately 56% of cases are spontaneous, and the principal clinical manifestations of PSD patients are deep vein thrombosis (almost 90%), thrombophlebitis, and pulmonary embolism. In rare cases, thrombosis of the axillary, mesentery, and anterior sagittal sinus vein may also be observed. In general, PSD can be clinically diagnosed by examining the activity of plasma protein S, and a plasma level of less than 60% can result in venous thrombosis. Therefore, it is often inevitable that people with familial PSD need to receive anticoagulant therapy.

Currently, patients with PSD are usually treated with warfarin anticoagulant or thrombolysis in the acute stage of thromboembolism, followed by oral anticoagulant to prevent thrombosis recurrence. However, PSD patients carry a greater risk of bleeding during anticoagulation treatment due to frequent relapses caused by congenital thrombophilia.10 More research on PSD-induced anticoagulant disorders should be conducted to identify alterations in plasma biomolecules in PSD patients, providing more promising points for available clinical treatment strategies. It has been found that protein S is likely to have biological functions other than anticoagulation, such as stimulating phagocytosis of apoptotic cells and localizing complement regulatory activity.2,11−13 Nonetheless, as a rare disease, procuring samples from PSD patients is often challenging, thus leading to fewer reported studies.

In this study, we met patients with familial PSD in the course of clinical treatment. Plasma from 7 males of families was collected and subsequently subjected to proteomics and metabolomics analyses. Based on the outline of detected proteins and metabolites, this research focused on functional molecules and biological interaction disorders in familial PSD to discover new mechanisms of PSD-induced anticoagulant disorders and provide new insights into protein S and new points for PSD clinical therapy development. Methods and Materials

Chemicals and Reagents

Tridecanoic acid, four n-alkanes (including decane, dodecane, pentadecane, and nondecane), and iodoacetamide (IAA) were obtained from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Methoxyamine hydrochloride, N-methyl-N-(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide (MSTFA), trimethyl chlorosilane (TMCS), and dithiothreitol (DTT) were all purchased from TCI (Shanghai, China). Tamoxifen, reserpine, cholic acid-2,2,4,4-d4 (CA-D4), trifluoroacetic acid (TCA), and tetraethylammonium bromide (TEAB), and DTT were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Ammonia solution was acquired from Tianjin Kemiou Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Tianjin, China). Ammonium acetate, Pierce BCA kit, and trypsin were bought from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Shanghai, China). An indexed retention time (iRT) kit was bought from Biognosys (Cambridge, MA, USA). HPLC grade formic acid, acetone, methanol, and acetonitrile were ordered from Merck & Co. (Billerica, MA, USA). Deionized water was purified using a Milli-Q system (Millipore, MA, USA).

Study Subjects and Plasma Collection

Plasma samples from four patients with inherited PSD (n = 4) and three controls with nonPSD (NPSD, n = 3) were collected from the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine. In particular, these seven people were related and belonged to the same family (Table 1). The study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine with the number NO.K[2021]008. In addition, all the plasma samples with EDTA anticoagulant in our study were collected in the morning from 07:00 to 09:00 a.m. after an overnight fast to eliminate dietary disturbances. After collection, all plasma samples were separated following centrifugation at 8000g for 10 min at 4 °C and stored at −80 °C before analysis.

Sample Pretreatment of Proteomics

After thawing, plasma samples were centrifuged at 12,000g at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube, and the total protein concentration was determined with a BCA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The same concentration of protein was taken from each sample, and the volume was adjusted using the lysate to be equal. Then, 20% TCA was slowly added to protein precipitation at 4 °C for 2 h. Centrifugation at 4500g for 5 min was conducted; the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was washed and precipitated by precooled acetone 2 or 3 times. After being dried, the pellet was added to 200 mM TEAB and mixed by ultrasound. Trypsin was then added to the mixture at a 1:50 trypsin-to-protein mass ratio for the first digestion overnight. Finally, the mixture was treated with 5 mM DTT for 30 min at 56 °C and then alkylated with 11 mM IAA for 15 min at room temperature in the darkness. A 300 extend C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm; Agilent, CA, USA) was utilized for peptide fractionation. Briefly, peptides were first separated with a gradient of 8–32% acetonitrile (pH 9.0) over 60 min and then combined into 48 fractions. The combined fractions were then added to iRT peptides according to the manufacturer’s instructions and lyophilized by vacuum centrifugation for subsequent analysis.

LC–MS/MS Analysis of Proteomics

The peptides were resuspended in 0.1% formic acid and loaded onto a homemade reversed-phase (RP) analytical column (15 cm × 75 μm) with an EASY-nLC 1200 ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA). Mobile phases A (0.1% formic acid in 2% acetonitrile) and B (0.1% formic acid in 90% acetonitrile) were used for the RP gradient. The gradient was set at a constant flow rate of 400 nL/min for 120 min. The peptides were then subjected to a Q-Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA) equipped with a Nanospray Flex source. The electrospray voltage applied was set to 2.0 kV. The m/z scan range was 385–1200 for a full scan, and intact peptides were detected in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 120,000. mass spectrometry (MS)/MS of peptides were acquired by the approach of data independent acquisition (DIA), and the fragments were detected in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 15,000. The 70 most intense peaks in MS were fragmented with higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) with a collision energy of 27%. MS/MS spectra were obtained with an automatic gain control target of 5 × 105. The fixed first mass was set as 200 m/z.

Database Search and Differential Proteins

DIA data was processed on Skyline (version 4.1.0) after inputting the spectral library, and the parameters of iRT were set to “Prediction”. The precursor ion charge of “Transition” was set to 2, 3, and 4, and the product ion charge was set to 1 and 2. Moreover, six ions with the highest intensity in the spectral library were used for peptide quantification. Then, DIA data were imported and filtered by false discovery rate (FDR) using the mProphet algorithm. The result of protein quantitation was finally obtained using the MSstats R package. Abundance of proteins were logarithm transformed to base 2 and then carried out univariate statistical analysis between PSD and NPSD to obtain P values. The FDR value of each differential protein was also calculated using P values. The proteins with P < 0.05 and FDR < 0.1 were considered significantly different.14,15

Sample Pretreatment of GC–MS-Based Metabolomics

The extraction of metabolites was performed according to a protocol, with some modifications.16 Briefly, 150 μL of methanol and 10 μL of tridecanoic acid (0.2 mg/mL in methanol) were added to a 1.5 mL Eppendorf centrifugation tube containing 50 μL of thawed human plasma. The mixture was vortexed for 1 min and incubated at 4 °C for 10 min, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min. After that, 100 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a new vial and dried under vacuum at room temperature for 1 h. Quality controls (QCs) were produced by merging equal amounts of the supernatant from each sample and were treated in the same way as the samples. For gas chromatography (GC) derivatization, the dried extracts of samples and QCs were first added to 30 μL of methoxyamine hydrochloride (15 mg/mL in pyridine) for 16 h at 25 °C for oximation, followed by the addition of 30 μL of MSTFA (containing 1% TMCS) for 1 h at 25 °C for silanization. Finally, 10 μL of a mixture consisting of four n-alkanes (decane, dodecane, pentadecane, and nonadecane, 0.3 mg/mL each) was added prior to GC injection for the calculation of the retention index (RI).

GC–MS Analysis of Metabolomics

The plasma metabolomics was carried on a 7890B-5977A GC–MS system (Agilent, CA, USA) with a HP-5MS capillary column (5% phenylmethylsiloxane: 30 m × 250 μm, 0.25 μm; Agilent, MA, USA). The helium carrier gas flow rate was set at 1 mL/min. The temperature program was set to 70 °C initially, held for 4 min, rose to 320 °C at 15 °C/min, and then held for 4 min. The auxiliary temperature was set to 280 °C, and the injector temperature was set to 250 °C with a 1 μL injection volume in split mode (4:1 split ratio). Besides, the electron ionization source temperature was kept at 230 °C. Mass spectra were acquired with electron ionization mode (70 eV) in the full scan mode (m/z 45–800), and solvent delay was set at 4.5 min. Each sample was detected with three replicates. QCs were inserted at every six injections.

Data Processing of GC–MS and Differential Metabolites

Raw mass spectra of QCs were obtained in “.d” format, and deconvolution and feature extraction were conducted using Unknowns Analysis (version 10.1) on a MassHunter Workstation (Agilent, CA, USA) (detailed parameters are shown in Supporting Information Table S1). After that, the result of feature extraction was outputted in the “.cef” format and then was inputted to QuantAnalysis (version 10.1, Agilent, CA, USA). The “.cef” format file was treated as a feature extraction method for the batch process to yield a data matrix of the feature area for each injection (detailed parameters are shown in Supporting Information Table S1). The features with missing values of >20% were excluded from the data matrix. All feature areas were logarithmic transformed and then subjected to univariate statistical analysis to obtain P values. Meanwhile, the FDR value of each differential metabolite was also calculated. The features were thought to be significantly different between PSD and NPSD when P < 0.05 and FDR <0.1.17,18 Next, the potential differential features were identified by comparison of mass spectra with those of the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST 14), with a similarity of more than 70%. In addition, the abundances of four n-alkanes were used to calculate the RI for each feature. The RIs of differential features were then matched with RIs of standard references on the database Human Metabolome Database (HMDB, https://hmdb.ca/) for further identification.

Sample Pretreatment of LC–MS-Based Metabolomics

The extraction method for LC–MS analysis was described in previous work, with some modifications.19 Briefly, after the mixture was thawed, 50 μL of plasma was added to 200 μL of methanol for protein precipitation. The mixture was vortexed for 3 min and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min. Next, 180 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a new vial, and QC consisted of merging equal amounts of the remaining supernatant from each sample, followed by drying under a vacuum at room temperature for 2 h. The dried extracts of samples and QCs were redissolved with 100 μL of 50% methanol containing 500 nM reserpine and CA-D4 in reversed-phase liquid chromatography (RPLC) or tamoxifen and CA-D4 in hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC). The mixture was vortexed for 3 min and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm at 4 °C for 30 min before LC injection.

LC–MS Analysis of Metabolomics

Untargeted plasma metabolomic analysis was performed using a 1290 UPLC system (Agilent, CA, USA) coupled to 6540 Q-time-of-flight (TOF) MS (Agilent, CA, USA), operated in positive and negative ionization modes. We used two kinds of columns for metabolite separation, RPLC and HILIC, respectively.20 A Waters BEH C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters, MA, USA) was used for reverse phase separation. The column temperature was set as 50 °C. Plasma metabolites were separated with a 31 min gradient at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. Mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid in H2O, and mobile phase B was 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The gradient was set as follows: 0–1 min, 2% solvent B; 1–25 min, 100% solvent B; 25–30 min, 100% solvent B; 30–31 min, and 2% solvent B. On the other hand, a Waters XBridge BEH Amide column (2.1 × 100 mm, 5 μm; Waters, MA, USA) was used for hydrophilic interaction phase separation. The column temperature was set at 40 °C. Plasma metabolites were separated with a 13 min gradient at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min under mobile conditions. The mobile phase A was 95% H2O and 5% ammonium acetate (pH = 9 with ammonia), and mobile phase B was 95% acetonitrile and 5% H2O. The gradient was set as follows: 0–1 min, 99% solvent B; 1–6 min, 30% solvent B; 6–8 min, 30% solvent B; 8–8.1 min, 99% solvent B; 8.1–13 min, and 99% solvent B. The electrospray ionization (ESI) conditions were as follows: capillary voltage 3500 V, gas temperature 320 °C, gas flow rate 8 L/min, nebulizer 35 psig, sheath gas temperature 350 °C, sheath gas flow rate 11 L/min, nozzle voltage 1500 V, fragmentor 175 V, skimmer 65 V, TOF mass range: 50–1200 Da, and scan rate 4 spectra/s.

Data Processing of LC–MS and Differential Metabolites

Raw mass spectra of QCs were obtained in the “.d” format, and feature extraction was performed by qualitative analysis (version B.07.00) on a MassHunter Workstation (detailed parameters are shown in Supporting Information Table S1). The result of feature extraction was outputted in the “.cef” format and was treated as a feature extraction method to input QuantAnalysis for the batch process, obtaining a data matrix of feature area for each injection (detailed parameters are shown in Supporting Information Table S1). Then, the features with missing values of areas of >20% were excluded from the data matrix. All feature areas were logarithmic transformed and then subjected to univariate statistical analysis to obtain P values. Then, the same as in GC–MS, the FDR value of each differential metabolite was calculated. The features with P < 0.05 and FDR < 0.1 were thought to be significantly different between PSD and NPSD.21 Next, significantly differential features were further identified by matching the databases in terms of the exact mass composition, the goodness of the isotopic fit, and the MS/MS fragments. The involved databases were Metlin, HMDB, and lipid maps (https://lipidmaps.org/).

Enrichment, Correlations, and Interaction Networks Analysis

Enrichment analysis of differential biomolecules was performed by the “function analysis” module on the platform OmicsShare (https://www.omicshare.com/). The top enriched pathways were mapped and visualized using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Mapper (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/mapper/). The correlation of differential biomolecules was analyzed by modules “Big_Corrplot”, “Corrplot”, and “Line_Regression” using the platform HIPLOT PRO (https://hiplot.com.cn/). The interaction network of differential biomolecules was generated with the ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA) platform (version 1.0, QIAGEN, DUS, Germany). IPA is a web-based software application (http://www.ingenuity.com) that identifies networks of interactions between biomolecules of interest underpinned by published reports.22,23 A new core analysis was first constructed, and the form with UniProt IDs or HMDB IDs, FDR value, and logarithmic fold change was then uploaded to IPA for network analysis. Relationships between differential biomolecules were searched based on the Ingenuity Pathway Knowledge Database, and networks including relevant biomolecules were generated. Furthermore, with the results of IPA, the global biological pathways involved in the development of PSD were speculatively determined based on the KEGG database (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html).

Statistics

All data are expressed as the mean ± the standard deviation (SD). Univariate analysis was conducted using SPSS software (version 22.0, IBM, IL, USA) with the Student’s t test to obtain P values. Simca-p (version 14.1, Umetrics, Sweden) was used to perform principal component analysis. The FDR value was obtained from the adjusted P value of FDR correction by the Benjamini–Hochberg method. RIs of differentially abundant metabolites in GC–MS were based on the formula of nonisothermal Kovats retention indices.24 The flowchart and biological pathway were drawn with Microsoft Visio (version 2010, Microsoft, WA, USA). Diagrams were generated by Prism (version 6, GraphPad Software, CA, USA), Illustrator (version 2019, Adobe, CA, USA), the online platform HIPLOT PRO, and E Venn (http://www.ehbio.com/test/Venn/).25

Results

Proteome and Metabolome Profiling of PSD and NPSD Plasma

We conducted plasma proteomics and metabolomics analysis of 7 volunteers, 3 of whom had NPSD and 4 of whom had PSD. In the proteomics results, most of the peptides were distributed in the 7–20 amino acid range, which was in accordance with the general rules of trypsin enzymolysis and higher-energy C-trap dissociation (HCD) fragmentation (Supporting Information Figure S1). Therefore, the distribution of peptides identified by MS met the QC requirements. In addition, the MS spectral errors of most peptides were less than 10 ppm, which conforms to the high-precision characteristics of Orbitrap MS. This result indicated that the quality accuracy of the mass spectrometer was within the normal range, thus assuring that the qualitative and quantitative analyses of proteins would not be affected by excessive quality deviation (Figure 2A).

In the metabolomics results, we set QC samples to monitor the stability of raw data, and the results showed that the relative SD (RSD %) of IS in all QC samples was less than 20%, which met the data stability requirements of the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for MS detection of metabolomics (Figure 2B).16 In GC–MS detection, the RSD % of RIs (decane, dodecane, pentadecane, and nondecane) and IS tridecanoic acid of QC samples (n = 8) were 10.40, 6.81, 4.51, 14.64, and 11.75%, respectively. In LC–MS detection, the IS RSD % of QC samples were as follows: reserpine (RPLC, ESI+, n = 9) 4.65%, CA-D4 (RPLC, ESI–, n = 9) 3.49%, tamoxifen (HILIC, ESI+, n = 10) 2.48%, and CA-D4 (HILIC, ESI–, n = 8) 9.75%. Besides, the PCA scatter plot showed that coefficients of variation of the feature area of QC samples were within the ±2SD range, indicating good instrument reproducibility during the entire batch analysis (Supporting Information Figure S2). On this basis, PLS-DA analysis was performed on all detected proteins and metabolites, revealing an obvious variation in the overall profile of the proteome and metabolome of plasma from people with NPSD and PSD (Figure 2C).

Moreover, enrichment analysis of all detected proteins and metabolites showed that the detected proteins were mainly distributed in complement and coagulation cascades and immune-related signaling pathways (Figure 2D). The metabolites are mainly involved in metabolism (Figure 2E), with most of them distributed in the metabolism of amino acids, cofactors, carbohydrates, and lipids (Figure 2F). The results suggested the main types of biological information that can be obtained from the proteomics and metabolomics approaches in this study.

Biomolecules with Abnormal Changes in the Plasma of PSD

A total of 9473 protein features were detected by proteomics analysis, with 177 proteins possessing a P value of less than 0.05 and an FDR of less than 0.1, 60 of which were significantly upregulated and 57 obviously downregulated. Among them, F2, APOE, GSN, C9, and SERPIND1 were the top 5 upregulated proteins; ITIH3, APOD, LCP1, C2, and CFH were the top 5 downregulated proteins (Figure 3A and Supporting Information Table S2). Moreover, metabolomics analysis revealed 5323 MS features in total, of which 35 metabolites had a P value below 0.05 and FDR below 0.1, with a high matching percentage of more than 70% in GC–MS and 85% in LC–MS through the NIST and Metlin databases, respectively (Supporting Information Table S3). Among these 35 metabolites, 11 were significantly increased and 24 were notably decreased. When the differentially abundant metabolites were ranked by log2(fold change), the top 5 with increased levels were sulfoglycolithocholate (HMDB0062801), fructose (HMDB0000660), epidermin (HMDB0034777), α-tocotrienol (HMDB0006327), and aminomalonic acid (HMDB0001147). The top 5 with decreased levels were for 3-hydroxybutyric acid (HMDB0000011), malonic acid (HMDB0000691), p-cresol (HMDB0001858), taurodeoxycholic acid (HMDB0000896), and ethyl 3-oxobutanoate (HMDB0031216) (Figure 3B).

To assess the contribution of these differential biomolecules to the differentiation of NPSD and PSD, clustering heatmap analysis was conducted, and the results showed that the different biomolecules can well classify NPSD and PSD (Figure 3C). It is suggested that these biomolecules show obvious abnormal changes in PSD. Further study of the enriched biological pathways of differential biomolecules is helpful to reveal the pathologic metabolic mechanisms related to thrombotic tendency in PSD. To assess the contribution of these differential biomolecules to the distinction between NPSD and PSD, clustering heatmap analysis was conducted, and the findings showed that differential biomolecules could effectively classify NPSD and PSD (Figure 3C). It is suggested that these biomolecules underwent remarkable abnormal changes in the body during PSD. Further investigation into the enriched biological pathways of differential biomolecules will be helpful for revealing the pathological metabolic mechanisms related to thrombosis susceptibility in PSD.

Most Enrichment Signaling Pathway by Differential Biomolecules in PSD

On the basis of differential biomolecules composed of 177 proteins and 35 metabolites, enrichment pathway analysis was performed to focus on important abnormal biological networks in PSD. However, by using the KEGG Mapper online platform, only 47 proteins and 13 metabolites had K numbers in the KEGG database. However, the biological functions of biomolecules without K numbers are not yet clear, hindering enrichment pathway analysis. Therefore, 60 differentially abundant metabolites were used for KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Supporting Information Table S4). The results showed that PSD differential biomolecules were mainly enriched in the “Complement and coagulation cascades” pathways, with 25 proteins mapped in total, hinting at abnormal coagulation and immune signaling networks in PSD. The next pathways were “Staphylococcus aureus infection” and “Coronavirus disease-COVID-19”, with 12 and 11 differential proteins involved, respectively, suggesting that PSD might be associated with abnormal changes similar to microbial infection or autoimmune mechanisms (Supporting Information Figure S3). In particular, in the “complement and coagulation cascades” pathway, clinical ELISA and proteomics showed that the activity and level of protein S were significantly reduced in PSD (Table 1 and Figure 4A).

Furthermore, levels of most anticoagulation proteins that inhibit F2 (also known as thrombin) production in the coagulation cascade pathway were significantly decreased in PSD plasma. The absence of protein S, which made it impossible to inhibit F5 and F8’s catalytic conversion of F10 to F2, resulted in the large production of F2, that is, fibrinogen, which was activated. The abnormal changes above pushed plasma coagulation into hyperactivity toward thrombosis in PSD (Figure 4A left). In addition, we found that the complement cascade pathway was significantly affected in PSD, especially with regard to increasing levels of key proteins in the alternative pathway, which was independent of antibodies and could promote processes such as inflammation and apoptosis (Figure 4A right). Overall, the results showed that in the “complement and coagulation cascades” pathway, a total of 8 and 17 proteins were up- and downregulated (Figure 4B); F2 (thrombin) and C5 were the most elevated and degraded proteins, respectively (Figure 4C). This result suggests that in PSD, proteins in the “complement and coagulation cascades” pathway are impacted by the deficiency of protein S to varying degrees. Therefore, it is necessary to further analyze the correlation between protein S and differential proteins in not only this but also other pathways to investigate differential proteins sensitive to the level of protein S and to reveal the molecular mechanism of PSD.

Interaction Pathway Analysis of Biological Associations among Differential Biomolecules, Protein S, and Coagulation

To investigate the relationship between all of the differential biomolecules and protein S to uncover the biological changes caused by PSD, correlation analysis was performed for the 212 differential biomolecules and protein S described above (Supporting Information Figure S4). As shown in Figure 5A, there were 9 differential proteins that showed a significant correlation with protein S. Among them, six proteins, IGHV1–69, A2M, CFHR5, CPN2, AGT, and FGG, correlated positively with protein S, with P values less than 0.05 and coefficients of determination (R2) greater than 0.3, which indicated a significant and good correlation (Figure 5B–D). Three proteins, APOE, CA1, and GPLD1, correlated negatively with protein S, with P values less than 0.05 and R2 values less than −0.3, signifying a significant and good correlation (Figure 5B,C). The P values and R2 are shown in Supporting Information Table S5.

Based on previous analysis, A2M and CFHR5 are proteins of “complement and coagulation cascades” in the top enrichment pathway (Figure 4). Inhibition of their expression would promote coagulation, which is consistent with the body characteristics of thrombosis caused by PSD. However, other correlating proteins are not involved in this pathway, suggesting that PSD might also affect other biological pathways. Consequently, pathway analysis and molecular interaction network analysis for these 9 differentially expressed proteins and protein S were conducted using the IPA platform. In canonical pathway analysis, protein S-related differential proteins are mainly involved in the pathways “coagulation system”, “internal and external prothrombin activation”, “acute phase reaction”, and “LXR/FXR/RXR activity” (Figure 6A, Supporting Information Table S6). The results of the molecular interaction network further showed that these 9 differentially expressed proteins and protein S could be divided into 3 interaction networks, among which 8 proteins, PROS1, A2M, AGT, APOE, FGG, GPLD1, IGHV1–69, and CFHR5, had a high interaction possibility and were concentrated in the top network (Figure 6B and Supporting Information Table S7). The diseases and functions mainly involved in the top network are cardiovascular disease, hematological disease, and hereditary disorder. Discussion

As a rare disease with limited research, familial PSD is characterized by a tendency toward venous thrombosis. It is currently thought that the anticoagulant dysfunction caused by PSD is mainly due to the decreased level of protein S, which reduces inhibition of F5 and F8 regarding conversion of F10 to F2 (thrombin), rendering plasma more prone to coagulation.26 Patients with PSD generally rely on warfarin for anticoagulant relief after onset, but prolonged use of warfarin anticoagulant therapy can lead to an increased risk of bleeding; there are however no other therapeutic improvement options.27 Use of big data analysis to study abnormal alterations is conducive to understanding the biological profile of thrombosis caused by PSD as a whole, which can facilitate better comprehension of the novel mechanism of thrombosis induced by PSD and provide potential interaction pathways and targets for the development of antithrombotic treatment approaches.

In this study, plasma was collected from 7 volunteers with familial connections, of whom 4 were patients with familial PSD and the remaining 3 NPSD controls (Table 1). The proteomics and metabolomics of the PSD patients and NPSD controls were detected and analyzed. The results showed that the proteomic and metabolomic profiles of PSD patients were significantly different from those of the NPSD controls (Figure 2C). A total of 177 differential proteins and 35 differentially abundant metabolites were screened, of which 47 proteins and 13 metabolites are known to be involved in biological pathways (Figure 3C). According to KEGG enrichment analysis, 25 of the 60 different biomolecules are distributed in the “complement and coagulation cascades” pathway, accounting for the largest percentage of the number of enriched biomolecules in the pathway and indicating the most obvious abnormal changes in the coagulation and complement cascade signaling pathways in PSD patients (Figure 4A). Through overall analysis of the biomolecular changes in this pathway, the level of anticoagulant protein in PSD was found to be significantly reduced, but the complement system was abnormally promoted, suggesting that the humoral immune function is disturbed in PSD.

To further reveal the molecular mechanism and biological relationship of coagulation and immune dysfunction in PSD, we conducted a correlation analysis between protein S and all different biomolecules. The results showed that 9 different proteins correlated significantly with protein S, including A2M, AGT, APOE, FGG, GPLD1, IGHV1–69, CFHR5, CPN2, and CA1 (Figure 5A). Canonical pathway and molecular interaction network analyses were performed with the IPA platform for protein S and these 9 differential proteins (Figure 6). According to the results of canonical pathway analysis, protein S (PROS1), A2M, and FGG are involved in activation of thrombin, thereby promoting the coagulation system, as shown in Figure 7.

Moreover, A2M, FGG, and AGT are all engaged in pathways of acute phase response signaling. In other words, A2M and FGG provide a possible molecular link between prothrombin activation and acute response signaling in PSD. Additionally, FGG and GPLD1 are implicated in the serotonin receptor signaling pathway. AGT is involved in not only the LXR/FXR/RXR pathway with APOE but also the p70 signaling pathway with IGHV1–69. Therefore, with the above pathways as the main contents, combined with the molecular interaction network (Figure 6B) and known canonical pathways based on KEGG and IPA (Figure 7), the overall hypothesized pathway of abnormal changes in coagulation and immune function in PSD is illustrated in Figure 8.

The solid line indicated the direct action, and the dashed line indicated the indirect action. The lines were colored differently to distinguish overlapping lines. In PSD, the decrease in the anticoagulant protein S (PROS1) and A2M leads to an increase in the conversion of coagulation factor F10 to F2 (thrombin), which promotes enzymatic hydrolysis of fibrinogen (FGG) to fibrin monomer and cross-linked to form a stable fibrin clot.28 The increase in F2 and decrease in the complement regulator CFHR5 result in amplified transformation of complement component C3 into the membrane attack complex and proinflammatory C5a, promoting cell lysis and degranulation chemotaxis. An increase in the fibrin clot, cell lysis, and degranulation chemotaxis gives rise to an inflammatory response and immune dysfunction, activating the transcription factors NFκB and STAT3. This in turn promotes transcription of F2, C3, C9, and other components, further increasing plasma concentration.29,30

Furthermore, platelets store serotonin in dense particles, and upon coagulation, they release serotonin, which acts as a vasoconstrictor to help maintain the balance of plasma coagulation and hemostasis.31 In PSD, the decrease in fibrinogen FGG might reduce the expression of the platelet surface transporter SLC6A4 through the Gp2b3a receptor, thereby decreasing the transport of serotonin into dense particles. The particles remain in the plasma to promote coagulation or bind to the HTR2A receptor on the platelet surface to induce the release of dense particles, thus resulting in platelet aggregation. In addition, increased levels of GPLD1 in PSD might be regulated by serotonin. The increase in GPLD1 increases DAG levels for activation of the calcium signaling pathway, which, in turn, activates NFkB signaling.

Despite the decrease in AGT levels that comprise HDL in PSD, levels of APOA1 and APOE, as well as the metabolites deoxycholic acid (HMDB0000626) and androsterone sulfate (HMDB0002759) that activate their upstream regulatory protein NR1H4, were found to be significantly increased, suggesting that cholesterol metabolism and bile acid synthesis in PSD might be hyperactive.32,33 Furthermore, AGT and myo-inositol, which are the upstream regulatory molecules of IP3, showed significantly reduced levels, thus forming negative feedback regulation of the calcium signaling pathway. In addition, both upregulation of F2 and downregulation of IGHV1–69 might affect PI3K signaling to regulate the degree of inhibition of BAD by RPS6K and consequently affect the cell survival status. In PSD, the increase in F2 was greater than the decrease in IGHV1–69, suggesting a greater possibility of promoting apoptosis.

In summary, by utilizing omics technology, we conducted analysis of alterations in the plasma proteome and metabolome of patients with PSD, providing data support for revealing a novel molecular mechanism of familial PSD in rare diseases and proposing new ideas for its treatment. However, because no appropriate animal model of familial PSD has been developed, further exploration of the potential biopathway is limited. To further investigate the molecular mechanisms of PSD, animal models of familial PSD should be developed and studied in the future to establish a research method basis.

Conclusions

This study suggests that the proteome and metabolome of PSD show obvious disturbances, with the most abnormal changes occurring in the “coagulation and complement cascade” pathway. In PSD, the overall level of anticoagulant protein decreases, weakening the negative regulatory effect on thrombin production and resulting in the formation of fibrin clots and platelet aggregation. Among the differential biomolecules of PSD, 9 proteins were found to correlate significantly with protein S, including A2M, AGT, APOE, FGG, GPLD1, IGHV1–69, CFHR5, CPN2, and CA1. Taking these protein S-related proteins as the main hubs, our analysis revealed that in addition to the “coagulation and complement cascade” pathway, PSD significantly affects other biological pathways, including the pathways of “acute phase response”, “FXR/RXR activation”, “serotonin receptor signaling”, and “p70S6K signaling”, indicating interaction disorders among coagulation, inflammatory immune, and lipid metabolism in PSD.
