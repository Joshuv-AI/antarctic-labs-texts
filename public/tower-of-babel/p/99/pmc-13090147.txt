A One Health view of antimicrobial resistance in North Africa: Meta-analysis of ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae across sectors
Authors: Oussama Abi, Imane Adoud, Asmaa Dihmane, Hind Boudarf, Fatima Azzahra Lahlou, Meryem El Jemli, Samy Iskandar, Idrissa Diawara, Zakaria Belrhiti, Lhousaine Balouch, Mohamed Kettani Halabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntimicrobial resistance is a growing global health threat, and extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing Enterobacterales exemplify a critical challenge. Because these organisms circulate across human, animal, food, and environmental reservoirs, a One Health perspective is essential for understanding their epidemiology. However, comprehensive regional syntheses remain limited in North Africa.MethodsThis systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence from 182 studies published between 2000 and 2024 on ESBL-producing Enterobacterales across North Africa within a One Health framework. We analyzed epidemiological data from human, animal, food, and environmental sources, as well as reported resistance genes, mobile genetic elements, transferability patterns, and clonal diversity associated with ESBL dissemination in the regionResultsIn the human sector, the pooled prevalence estimate (PPE) of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in clinical infections was 32.9%, with a higher burden in healthcare-acquired infections (37.1%) than in community-acquired infections (20.1%). ESBL carriage was also frequent at admission (34.1%) and increased after hospitalization (51.1%). Among non-human sectors, the PPE was 10.9% in animals, 3.73% in food, and 40.7% in environmental sources. blaCTX-M was the predominant β-lactamase gene family, with blaCTX-M-15 as the leading subtype. Non-β-lactam resistance genes were also frequently reported, indicating broader multidrug resistant backgrounds. Genetic drivers of dissemination included insertion sequences, integrons, and diverse plasmid backgrounds, particularly IncF plasmids. Transferability experiments showed frequent horizontal transfer of ESBL associated resistance, often with co-transfer of non-β-lactam resistance determinants. MLST data identified multiple high-risk clones, including Escherichia coli ST131, ST10, ST405, and ST617, and Klebsiella pneumoniae ST101, ST147, and ST15ConclusionESBL-producing Enterobacterales are widely disseminated across interconnected human, animal, food, and environmental compartments in North Africa. Their regional epidemiology is shaped by a high human burden, substantial environmental reservoirs, frequent multidrug resistant backgrounds, mobile genetic elements, horizontal gene transfer, and the circulation of multiple clonal lineages. These findings highlight the need for strengthened and integrated One Health surveillance in North Africa.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) remains one of the most urgent global health threats and has been recognized by the World Health Organization (WHO) as one of the top ten challenges facing humanity [1]. In 2019, bacterial AMR was directly responsible for an estimated 1.27 million deaths and contributed to an additional 4.95 million worldwide [2]. Among resistant pathogens, Enterobacterales producing extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs), particularly those resistant to third-generation cephalosporins, represent a significant challenge due to their widespread dissemination and high resistance levels. These pathogens remain among the WHO's bacterial priority pathogens in urgent need of new antibiotic development [3]. The emergence and spread of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales are largely driven by anthropogenic factors, particularly the inappropriate and excessive use of antimicrobials in humans, animals and agriculture [4]. This is especially concerning for β-lactam antibiotics, which are the most widely used class in clinical practice [5]. The dissemination of β-lactamase enzymes, mainly ESBLs alongside resistance to other antimicrobial classes such as aminoglycosides, trimethoprim and quinolones, has contributed to the alarming rise of multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains [6], [7]. In recent years, ESBL-producing Enterobacterales have been increasingly reported across diverse ecological compartments, including humans, animals, food, and environmental sources [6]. Their spread is facilitated by both horizontal and vertical transmission of resistance genes, often mediated by mobile genetic elements (MGEs) such as plasmids, insertion sequences, transposons, and integrons, which enable dissemination across bacterial populations and interconnected reservoirs [8]. Because these interconnected reservoirs are central to the epidemiology of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales, the One Health framework provides an essential perspective for understanding their regional dissemination [9], [10]. In North Africa, this issue is of particular concern given the growing burden of antimicrobial resistance, the close interactions between human, animal, food, and environmental sectors, and the unequal development of surveillance systems across the region. However, despite the public health importance of this threat, no comprehensive synthesis has yet examined the epidemiology, resistance backgrounds, and dissemination mechanisms of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in North Africa within a One Health framework. This systematic review and meta-analysis was therefore conducted to synthesize the available evidence on the burden of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales across human, animal, food, and environmental sources in North Africa, and to describe the associated resistance genes, mobile genetic elements, and clonal diversity reported in the region.

Methodology

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis was performed in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items For Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses) guidelines and registered in PROSPERO database (registration number: CRD42024620843). The registered protocol covers both extended-spectrum β-Lactamase-producing Enterobacterales (ESBL-E) and carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPE). The present manuscript focuses exclusively on ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in North Africa.

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science to identify eligible studies reporting ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in North Africa. Search terms combined concepts related to extended-spectrum β-lactamases, Enterobacterales, antimicrobial resistance and the North African region. The search covered publications from 1 January 2000 to 25 March 2024. The detailed search strategy is provided in the Supplementary Material.

Study selection

Titles and abstracts were screened independently by three authors (OA, IA and HB) using predefined eligibility criteria. Full texts of potentially relevant articles were then assessed in detail. Any disagreement during the selection process was resolved through discussion with a fourth author (AD).

Studies were eligible for full text assessment if they reported data on extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing Enterobacterales from human, animal, food and environmental sources in North African countries (Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya and Egypt) and were published in English or French.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they: (i) investigated extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing Enterobacterales (ESBL-E) in at least one of the predefined One Health sectors (human, animal,food and environment) in North Africa. (ii) were published in English or French, and (iii) provided sufficient data for calculation of ESBL related proportions.

Studies were excluded if they: (i) were reviews, conference abstracts, case reports or book chapters. (ii) were conducted outside the predefined North African region, (iii) used unclear or insufficient methods for ESBL detection, or (iv) did not provide sufficient data for calculation of ESBL related proportions.

Data extraction

Data were extracted independently by two authors (OA and IA) using a standardized extraction form and compiled in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet for further analysis. The extracted variables included author, country, study period (when the exact study period was not reported, it was estimated as the two years preceding the publication year), sector/source, method used for ESBL detection, sample type, total number of isolates/samples, number of ESBL positive isolates/samples, and bacterial species identified. Molecular data were also extracted when reported, including ESBL genes, non β-lactam resistance genes, mobile genetic elements (MGEs), plasmid replicon types, conjugation or plasmid transferability data, and clonal lineages. All extracted data are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Quality assessment

The quality of each study was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tool for analytical cross-sectional studies [11], consisting of eight questions: (1) Were the criteria for inclusion in the sample clearly defined? (2) Were the study subjects and the setting described in detail? (3) Was the exposure measured in a valid and reliable way? (4) Were objective, standard criteria used for the measurement of the condition? (5) Were confounding factors identified? (6) Were strategies to deal with confounding factors stated? (7) Were the outcomes measured in a valid and reliable way? (8) Was appropriate statistical analysis used? Each question was scored as 0 (no) or 1 (yes). Studies scoring 6–7 were considered to have a low risk of bias, scores of 4–5 indicated a moderate risk of bias, and scores below 4 indicated a high risk of bias. Low-quality studies were not excluded, but their potential limitations were considered in the interpretation of the results. The checklist is presented in the Supplementary Material.

Data synthesis

Meta-analysis was performed using Jamovi version 2.3.28. Pooled prevalence estimates (PPEs) were calculated using a random-effects meta-analysis of proportions with inverse-variance weighting. Forest plots were generated with 95% confidence intervals.

Analyses were conducted separately according to sectors in order to avoid combining non-comparable outcomes. For human clinical studies, pooled prevalence estimates were calculated as the proportion of ESBL positive Enterobacterales among the total recovered Enterobacterales, while for colonization/carriage studies, pooled prevalence estimates were based on the proportion of ESBL positive individuals screened among the total number examined. For non-human sectors, pooled prevalence estimates were primarily calculated as the proportion of ESBL positive samples among the total samples examined.

Subgroup analyses were performed according to country, sector, sample type and time periods. To better explore temporal patterns, studies were grouped into 5-year periods (2000–2005, 2006–2010, 2011–2015, 2016–2020 and 2021–2024).

Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q test and the I2 statistic, with values greater than 75% considered indicative of substantial heterogeneity. Publication bias was explored using funnel plots, Kendall's rank correlation test, and a regression test for funnel plot asymmetry.

Molecular data were synthesized separately from the main epidemiological analyses. For frequently reported resistance genes, proportions were calculated as the number of ESBL positive isolates carrying a given gene among the total ESBL positive isolates tested for that gene. Other molecular variables were summarized descriptively according to consistency of reporting across studies.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The systematic search of Scopus, PubMed and Web of Science identified a total of 9168 records. After removal of 3348 duplicates, 5820 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 4641 were excluded for not meeting the predefined eligibility criteria. ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in human sector

Human studies represented the largest proportion of the included literature and covered both clinical infections and colonization/carriage. In total, 128 studies reported ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in clinical infections, including both healthcare-acquired and community-acquired infections, while 9 studies provided data on colonization/carriage.

Across clinical infection studies, the pooled prevalence estimate (PPE) of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales was 32.9% (95% CI: 28.7–37.2). When stratified by infection source, the prevalence was higher in healthcare-acquired infections (HAI), with a PPE of 37.1% (95% CI: 32.5–41.6), whereas community-acquired infections (CAI) showed a lower PPE of 20.1% (95% CI: 12.0–28.2). Colonization/carriage studies also showed a high burden of ESBL carriage in human populations, the pooled estimate of ESBL carriage at admission was 34.1% (95% CI: 8.3–59.9), while a higher estimate was observed after hospitalization (51.1%, 95% CI: 28.5–73.8). At the country level, the pooled prevalence of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in human clinical infections varied markedly across North Africa. The highest overall prevalence was observed in Egypt (53%, 95% CI: 47.3–58.8), followed by Libya (33.4%, 95% CI: 16.9–50.0), Algeria (25.6%, 95% CI: 15.8–35.4), Morocco (19.2%, 95% CI: 13.1–25.4), and Tunisia (18.6%, 95% CI: 11.9–25.3). Over time, the overall prevalence increased steadily, rising from 18.7% (95% CI: 8.4–29.0) in 2000–2005 to 61.8% (95% CI: 47.7–75.9) in 2021–2024, the estimate for this last period was based on five studies, four of which were from Egypt, which may explain the particularly high prevalence observed both for Egypt and for the overall North African estimate. ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in non-human sectors

A total of 28 studies reported ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in animal sources across North Africa. The pooled prevalence estimate (PPE) in the animal sector was 10.9% (95% CI: 5.4–16.4), with reported prevalence ranging from 0.6% to 69.0%, both the lowest and highest rates were observed in chicken samples. At the country level, the highest PPE was observed in Egypt (17.0%, 95% CI: 4.5–29.5), followed by Tunisia (6.8%, 95% CI: 0.8–12.7) and Algeria (5.8%, 95% CI: 2.0–9.6), whereas no eligible animal studies were identified from Morocco and Libya. Across study periods, the PPE was 13.5% (95% CI: 2.1–26.1) in 2011–2015 and 9.9% (95% CI: 5.0–14.8) in 2016–2020. By animal species, chicken and broiler samples showed a PPE of 11.2% (95% CI: 2.5–19.9), while cattle samples showed a PPE of 8.3% (95% CI: 4.1–12.5) (Table 3). Prevalence rates in other animal species were reported in single studies and included camels (1.7%), wild birds (2.0%), sheep and goats (6.9%), wild boars (28.2%), and cats and dogs (35.7%).

From food sources, 9 studies reported ESBL-producing Enterobacterales across North Africa. The pooled prevalence estimate (PPE) in the food sector was 3.7% (95% CI: 2.0–5.5), prevalence rates ranged from 0.6% in chicken meat to 17% in sheep meat, indicating variability across food types and study settings. At the country level, Egypt was the only country with sufficient data for meta-analysis, with a PPE of 5.1% (95% CI: 3.1–7.0). Only two studies from Tunisia and one study from Morocco were available, reporting prevalence rates of 1.5% and 2.5%, respectively. No eligible food studies were identified from Algeria or Libya (Table 3). Most food studies were conducted on food products of animal origin, particularly meat and milk samples, whereas the Moroccan study reported ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in vegetables.

The environmental sector represented the highest burden among the non-human sectors. The pooled prevalence estimate (PPE) of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales was 40.7% (95% CI: 21.7–59.8). By environmental source, hospital surface samples showed a PPE of 38.9% (95% CI: 11.2–66.7), while waste and treated water samples showed an even higher PPE of 73.9% (95% CI: 54.2–93.6) (Table 3). Among the 13 environmental studies, hospital surface investigations were conducted in Algeria (n = 4), Tunisia (n = 2), and Egypt (n = 2), whereas wastewater studies were reported only from Algeria (n = 3). Single studies also reported ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in ambulances surfaces from Egypt (7.3%), groundwater samples from Morocco (13%), and cockroaches considered as an environmental vector from Tunisia (19.1%).

Resistance genes in ESBL-producing Enterobacterales

Molecular characterization of β-lactamase determinants among ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in North Africa showed a clear predominance of blaCTX-M detected in 89.1% of tested isolates. Other frequently reported β-lactamase families included blaOXA (69.3%), blaTEM (54.2%), and blaSHV (43.9%) (Table 4). This distribution pattern was broadly consistent across sectors, countries, and study periods, with blaCTX-M remaining the dominant β-lactamase family throughout. At the subtype level, blaCTX-M-15 was by far the most common variant, detected in 78.2% of tested isolates, other subtypes included blaTEM-1 (65.5%), blaOXA-1 (64.3%), blaCTX-M-1 (40.0%), blaSHV-12 (12.5%), and blaCTX-M-14 (9.2%). Resistance genes among ESBL-producing Enterobacterales.

Non β-lactam resistance genes were also frequently reported among ESBL-producing Enterobacterales (Table 4). Among aminoglycoside resistance genes, aac(3)-II (51.6%), aphA (47.1%), and aadA (44%) were the most frequently detected genes, whereas armA and rmtB were less common. For plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance, aac(6′)-Ib-cr was the predominant determinant (54.8%), followed by qnrS (26.6%), qnrB (25.1%), and qnrA (13.1%). Tetracycline resistance genes were also commonly reported, particularly tet(A) (53.3%) and tet(B) (43.5%). Among sulfonamide and trimethoprim resistance determinants, dfrA (66.3%), sul2 (62.3%), and sul1 (49%) were the most frequently detected. The plasmid-mediated colistin resistance gene mcr-1 was detected in 19.1% of tested isolates, while additional phenicol resistance determinants, including floR, cmlA, and cat, were also reported (Table 4).

Genetic drivers of resistance dissemination

Genetic drivers associated with ESBL determinants were described in 38 studies across the human, animal, food, and environmental sectors. Among mobile genetic elements, class 1 integrons were the most frequently identified, occurring in 333 of 458 tested isolates (72.7%), whereas class 2 integrons were less common and were detected in 74 of 292 tested isolates (25.3%). Where integrons structures were further characterized, cassette arrays carrying multiple resistance determinants were described, most often including aadA and dfrA variants, and in some studies additional genes linked to aminoglycoside, trimethoprim and sulfonamide resistance. Several studies also reported blaCTX-M genes within genetic environments involving ISEcp1, while orf477 and IS26 were identified less frequently.

Plasmid replicon analysis revealed diverse plasmid backgrounds associated with ESBL-producing Enterobacterales. These plasmids were described mainly in human and animal isolates, but were also reported in food and environmental studies. The IncF family was the most consistently reported and included IncFIA, IncFIB, IncFII, and IncFIIK subtypes. Other replicon families were also identified, including IncL/M, IncA/C, IncH, and IncI, whereas IncN, IncK, IncY, and IncP were reported in a smaller number of studies. In multiple studies, ESBL-producing isolates carried more than one replicon type, indicating multireplicon plasmid backgrounds. These plasmid families were identified in association not only with β-lactamase genes, particularly blaCTX-M variants, but also with additional resistance genes conferring resistance to aminoglycosides and quinolones. In line with these findings, transferability experiments were reported in 32 studies from all sectors, most commonly using conjugation assays. Overall, 466 ESBL-producing isolates were tested, and successful transfer of ESBL genes and associated resistance phenotypes was demonstrated in 291 isolates (62.4%). In most studies, transfer involved blaCTX-M-positive isolates, particularly those carrying blaCTX-M-15, and multiple studies also reported co-transfer of additional resistance genes to aminoglycosides, quinolones, tetracyclines, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and chloramphenicol. Few studies further showed that isolates with negative conjugation results were still transferable by electroporation, indicating the presence of non-conjugative but transferable plasmids, whereas a smaller number of studies (n = 3) reported unsuccessful transfer experiments or chromosomal localization of ESBL genes.

Clonal diversity and major clonal lineages

Clonal diversity was reported in 35 studies mainly from the human sector, using different typing methods including PFGE, ERIC-PCR and RAPD. 18 studies (51.4%) described high clonal diversity and polyclonal populations, while 12 studies (34.3%) reported mixed patterns combining marked diversity with smaller clonal groups, indicating that most ESBL-producing isolates belonged to genetically heterogeneous populations, a pattern consistent with horizontal dissemination of ESBL genes. Only few studies (n = 4) identified dominant clones or closely related profiles, suggesting that local expansion also contributed to ESBL spread in some settings particularly in human isolates, but it was also reported in animal isolates.

MLST data from 30 studies further refined the clonal epidemiology by showing a broad diversity of sequence types (STs) among ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in North Africa, with 47 Escherichia coli STs and 55 Klebsiella pneumoniae STs reported. Among E. coli, ST131 was the most common and widely distributed clone. It was reported in 13 studies from Egypt, Tunisia, Algeria, and Morocco, mainly in human clinical isolates from both hospital and community settings, and was also identified in cattle in Tunisia and chickens in Algeria, supporting its circulation beyond the human clinical compartment (Fig. 5). Other sequence types of epidemiological importance were also identified among recognized multidrug resistant clones. ST405 was reported in four studies from Egypt, Algeria, and Tunisia and was detected in hospital isolates, cattle, and wastewater, while ST167 appeared in three studies from Algeria and Tunisia in human, animal, and food isolates, including raw milk. ST410 was reported only once, in a community colonization study from Libya, and ST648 was identified in a single study from Tunisia in cockroaches. ST38 was reported in two studies from Libya and Tunisia, in community colonization and human urinary isolates. ST10 and ST617 were also detected across multiple sectors, ST10 was reported in seven studies from Egypt, Algeria, and Tunisia and was found in human clinical isolates, wastewater, raw beef meat, cattle, and camels. ST617 was reported in four studies from Tunisia, Morocco, and Algeria and was detected in human urinary isolates, raw milk, and wastewater. In Klebsiella pneumoniae, ST101 was the most frequently reported clone, appearing in five studies from Tunisia and Libya. It was found mainly in human hospital clinical isolates and was also detected in cattle in Tunisia. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides a comprehensive One Health synthesis of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales across human, animal, food and environmental sources in North Africa. Beyond prevalence, this study brings together the resistance backgrounds and dissemination mechanisms that shape the regional epidemiology of these organisms. By combining epidemiological findings with molecular data on resistance genes, mobile genetic elements and clonal diversity, this study offers a broader understanding of how ESBL-producing Enterobacterales are disseminated across interconnected reservoirs in the region.

In the human sector, the pooled prevalence estimate of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in clinical infections was 32.9%, positioning North Africa as a region of a high ESBL-E burden. This overall estimate remains higher than those reported from Saudi Arabia (26.4%) [12], and Nepal (29%) [13], but lower than estimates from East, Central and Southern Africa (38%) [14], and Pakistan (40%) [15]. Within clinical context, a clearly higher burden was reported in healthcare-acquired infections (37.1%), compared to community-acquired infections (20.1%), a pattern consistent with findings from East, Central and Southern Africa, where HAI and CAI estimates were 41% and 34%, respectively [14]. Additional regional comparisons reinforce this trend, a study across East African hospitals reported a PPE of 42% [16], while a nationwide survey of 30 hospitals in China estimated 46% [17]. In North Africa, Egypt showed the highest PPE (53%), aligning with elevated burden previously reported, although lower than the 60% PPE reported in a prior Egypt meta-analysis [18], likely because of differences in study scope and included data sets.

The carriage findings add an important dimension to this burden. ESBL carriage was already frequent at admission (34.1%) and increased further after hospitalization (51.1%), suggesting that hospitals in North Africa act not only as sites of infection but also as amplifiers of intestinal colonization, this is clinically relevant because colonized patients may serve as reservoirs for onward transmission and may also be at increased risk of subsequent infection [19], [20]. The specimen distribution further refines this picture, although urine represented the largest share of ESBL-positive human isolates, highlighting the important contribution of urinary tract infections to the overall clinical burden, the pooled proportion was higher in blood isolates (56.3%). These findings indicate that ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in the region are not confined to colonization or lower severity infections, but are also well represented among invasive disease, in line with reports of a high ESBL burden in bloodstream and other invasive clinical isolates from Ethiopia [21].

ESBL-producing Enterobacterales extend beyond human clinical setting, into animal, environmental and food sectors, reflecting the One Health paradigm [22]. Among the non-human sectors, environmental sources showed the highest burden, followed by animals, whereas food had the lowest PPE. The high prevalence in environmental sources, particularly in hospital surfaces and in wastewater is consistent with previous evidence identifying healthcare environments and wastewater systems as important reservoirs of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales [23], [24], [25]. In the animal sector, the pooled estimate was lower than the global, African and Asian prevalence estimates for ESBL-E.coli reported in broader One Health analyses [10], [26], poultry and cattle still emerged as the principal animal hosts in North Africa, consistent with earlier reports highlighting their epidemiological importance in ESBL-E dissemination [27], [28]. These findings remain particularly relevant in light of the continued use of antibiotics in livestock and poultry production, which contribute to the selection and persistence of resistant strains [29]. The food chain on the other hand is a critical pathway contributing in this transmission cycle, although food samples showed the lowest pooled estimate, the detection of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in meat, milk and vegetables points to the food chain as a potential interface linking animal production systems, environmental contamination and human exposure [30]. Similar concerns have been raised in studies from China where ESBL-producing Enterobacterales were frequently recovered from retail food products [31], and in a global study showing contamination of vegetables [32]. Even at lower prevalence, it remains epidemiologically important because it provides an additional route for dissemination outside direct healthcare exposure.

At the molecular level, the resistance profile was dominated by blaCTX-M, with blaCTX-M-15 as the leading subtype, followed by blaTEM and blaSHV, a distribution in line with the global epidemiological shift toward CTX-M enzymes, which have become the predominant ESBL family in many regions of the world [33]. A similar predominance of blaCTX-M, particularly blaCTX-M-15 has been reported in Europe [34], [35], the United States [36], and Brazil [37], indicating that North Africa follows the same broader molecular pattern. The predominance of blaCTX-M-15 is particularly important because this enzyme has repeatedly been linked to successful epidemic plasmids and high risk Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae clones, which likely contributes to its persistence and wide regional spread [33], [38]. In addition to β-lactamase genes, ESBL-producing isolates frequently carried non-β-lactam resistance genes affecting aminoglycosides, quinolones, tetracyclines, sulfonamides, and trimethoprim, showing that ESBL production in the region is commonly embedded within broader multidrug resistant backgrounds, this co-resistance profile is epidemiologically important because it was documented across human, animal, food, and environmental sectors, suggesting that the circulation of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in North Africa is sustained by wider resistance gene assemblages. The detection of mcr-1 in isolates from non-human sources further adds to this concern, as it points to the presence of colistin resistance within ESBL-producing backgrounds beyond the clinical setting. Although reported in a limited number of studies, its detection in food producing animals, food products, and environmental samples is significant because it highlights the co-occurrence of resistance to extended-spectrum cephalosporins and resistance to one of the last-resort drugs used against multidrug resistant Gram-negative infections [39].

This molecular pattern raises important questions regarding the genetic mechanisms behind it. Multiple studies identified blaCTX-M-15 within genetic environments involving insertion sequence ISEcp1 and less frequently IS26, highlighting their importance in the mobilization and spread of resistance determinants [40]. Plasmid replicon analysis showed that ESBL genes circulated within diverse plasmid backgrounds, most consistently within the IncF family. Despite their relatively narrow host range within Enterobacterales, they are recognized as major vehicles for blaCTX-M-15 and often carry additional resistance determinants, including genes associated with aminoglycoside and quinolone resistance [38], [41]. Integrons add a further layer to this genetic landscape through their recognized role as genetic platforms able to capture, integrate, and express gene cassettes, thereby facilitating the accumulation and organization of multiple resistance determinants [42], [43]. In this review, class 1 integrons were frequently reported, whereas class 2 integrons were less common, and the associated cassette arrays contained different resistance genes, these findings support the important role of mobile genetic elements in shaping the regional epidemiology of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales and in sustaining multidrug resistant phenotypes across sectors. The transferability findings also point in the same direction supporting a major contribution of horizontal gene transfer to the spread of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in North Africa, successful transfer was documented in a large proportion of tested isolates and involved not only ESBL genes but also the co-transfer of resistance genes to other antibiotic classes including aminoglycosides, quinolones and tetracyclines. Previous studies have shown that the international spread blaCTX-M genes has been driven by plasmid mediated horizontal transfer, often facilitated by associated mobile elements and shared plasmid backbones [44], [45]. The clonality findings provide a complementary perspective on this dissemination pattern by showing polyclonal populations and mixed patterns combining marked diversity with smaller local clusters, suggesting that the regional spread of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales involves multiple clonal lineages. MLST data from included studies refined this picture by revealing multiple STs among both Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae. Among Escherichia coli, ST131 stood out as the most widespread clone, this deserves a particular attention because ST131 is a globally disseminated high-risk ExPEC clone that has been closely linked to blaCTX-M-15, quinolones resistance and the successful dissemination in both hospital and community settings [46], its global spread has been attributed not only to antimicrobial resistance, but also to the combination of resistance and virulence associated genes that enhance persistence and pathogenicity [46]. The repeated detection of ST10, ST405 and ST617 across sectors and countries is also significant, ST10 is recognized as a widespread clone with clear One Health relevance, having been discovered from humans, animals, food and environmental sources, often with broad resistance profiles including ESBL and mcr-type genes [47]. ST405 has also been linked to the wider international spread of CTX-M producing E.coli, and is considered a common extraintestinal pathogenic clone associated with globally disseminated ESBLs, particularly blaCTX-M-15 [48], more recent reports shows that some ST405 have also acquired carbapenem resistance and enhanced virulence potential [49]. ST617 also appears to be part of this wider epidemiological landscape, having been reported from both human and animal sources and linked to CTX-M-producing multidrug resistant backgrounds, which supports its relevance within a One Health framework [50]. A similarly important pattern was observed for Klebsiella pneumoniae, in which ST101, ST147 and ST15 were the main reported clones. ST101 has been described as an emerging high-risk clone with extensive drug resistance and broad ecological distribution, some reports have confirmed its occurrence of carbapenem resistance, colistin resistance and virulence genes [51], [52]. ST147 is likewise recognized as a major high-risk clone, frequently associated with carbapenemase production and plasmids combining resistance and virulence determinant [53]. ST15 is also of the same importance as it is an expanding international clone linked to hospital outbreaks, multidrug resistance, carbapenemases acquisition and virulence associated genes that increase its pathogenic potential [54].

This review shows that ESBL-producing Enterobacterales are widely disseminated across interconnected human, animal, food, and environmental compartments in North Africa, with a particularly high burden in clinical infections and environmental sources. The presence of non-human reservoirs, the predominance of blaCTX-M, especially blaCTX-M-15, the contribution of mobile genetic elements, and the circulation of multiple clones. These findings should nevertheless be interpreted in light of some limitations, including substantial heterogeneity across included studies, unequal representation of countries and sectors, and the fact that detailed molecular, transferability, and clonality data were available only for subsets of studies. In addition, some non-human compartments, particularly in certain countries, remain underrepresented. Despite these constraints, the consistency of the main epidemiological and molecular patterns across sectors supports the need for strengthened and integrated One Health surveillance in North Africa.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides a comprehensive One Health overview of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in North Africa and highlights their dissemination across human, animal, food, and environmental sectors. The findings indicate a high burden in the human sector, environmental contamination, and continued circulation in animal and food sources, confirming that ESBL epidemiology in the region extends far beyond clinical settings alone. At the molecular level, the predominance of blaCTX-M, the frequent coexistence of additional resistance determinants, the involvement of mobile genetic elements, and the presence of multiple high-risk clones, together indicate that dissemination is sustained by both horizontal gene transfer and clonal expansion. These findings underscore the urgent need for stronger regional One Health surveillance, improved antimicrobial stewardship, and closer integration of clinical, veterinary, food, and environmental data to better understand and contain the spread of ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in North Africa.
