Navigating uncertainty during non‐invasive prenatal testing: Genetic counselors' experiences of supporting autonomous reproductive decision‐making
Authors: Chanelle Warton, Danya F Vears
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere is limited understanding of how healthcare professionals understand and apply the concept of reproductive autonomy in their clinical practice with non‐invasive prenatal testing (NIPT). Existing research indicates potential misalignment between theoretical understandings of reproductive autonomy and its operationalization by healthcare professionals in clinical practice. Despite genetic counselors (GCs) having unique expertise and experience in prenatal genetic counseling, there is a dearth of research regarding their understanding and practices relating to reproductive autonomy. To address this gap, we explored GCs' NIPT clinical practice, particularly focusing on their conceptualization and facilitation of autonomous reproductive decision‐making. Semi‐structured interviews were undertaken with 23 GCs experienced in providing NIPT in Australia. Data were analyzed using inductive content analysis. GCs viewed their role as facilitating reproductive genetics decision‐making and supporting patients to navigate the uncertainty inherent to prenatal screening, including phenotypic uncertainty. Uncertainty was viewed by GCs as an inherent feature of prenatal genetic testing, but not one that was incompatible with reproductive autonomy. Diverse views were expressed regarding the scope of conditions available via NIPT. Cost was viewed as a source of inequity, with all GCs supporting some level of public funding for NIPT.Keywords: decision‐making, ethics, genetic counselling, non‐invasive prenatal testing, qualitative, reproductive autonomy

What is known about this topic

Prenatal genetic counseling is recommended to facilitate autonomous decision‐making about non‐invasive prenatal testing (NIPT). Concerns have been raised as to whether current prenatal genetic counseling approaches adequately support decision‐making and whether autonomous choice can be achieved in light of both feasibility constraints and the uncertain implications of genetic information.

What this paper adds

There is a gap between current genetics education that emphasizes non‐directive counseling and the practice of genetic counselors which, for some, better aligns with a reproductive deliberation approach. Genetic counselors are uniquely positioned to facilitate autonomous reproductive decision‐making in light of the uncertain genetic information arising from NIPT and further training may be required for non‐genetics health professionals.

BACKGROUND

Autonomous reproductive decision‐making has been used to justify the availability and expanded application of non‐invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) since its implementation into clinical practice in 2011 (Ravitsky, 2017). In the prenatal context, the reproductive autonomy rationale proposes that the offering of NIPT supports individuals to manage their pregnancies in alignment with their personal goals and values (Ravitsky, 2017). This rationale typically underscores self‐determined and independent choice free from external influences (Beauchamp, 1989; Jamal et al., 2020; Zeiler, 2004), operationalized through practices such as the provision of informed consent, frameworks for individual decision‐making and non‐directive counseling (Holmes et al., 2025). However, such conceptions of autonomy are subject to increasing critique for their inadequate recognition of the social context in which reproductive decisions are made (Holmes et al., 2025). Relational autonomy, that which recognizes the interdependent and socially embedded nature of individuals, has thus been emphasized as a conceptual alternative. Within this conception, social contexts are viewed to shape an agent's beliefs and desires, their capacity for autonomy and their ability to act on autonomous desires in the context of their social environment (Mackenzie & Stoljar, 2000). In the context of prenatal testing, this increased recognition of the social context often involves associated increased recognition of the influence and involvement of healthcare professionals in prenatal testing decisions. Various models have been proposed as a means of operationalizing relational conceptions of autonomy in prenatal clinical practice, including shared decision‐making (Rantanen et al., 2008) and reproductive deliberation (Warton et al., 2023).

Despite the centrality of autonomy in the prenatal setting, there is limited exploration of how healthcare professionals who provide prenatal care understand and apply this concept in their practice. How healthcare professionals understand and subsequently operationalize reproductive autonomy in their practice is likely to influence patient decision‐making. In particular, genetic counselors (GCs) occupy a unique position in the offering of NIPT. It is crucial to understand GCs' unique perspective given their training in facilitating autonomous decision‐making and their experiences in supporting decision‐making about complex prenatal screening results. Despite their unique expertise and experience, there has been no specific examination of GCs' understanding and practice of supporting reproductive autonomy.

Amidst concerns of increasing routinization of NIPT globally (Horn, 2022; Young, 2025), there is a need for timely consideration of how genetic counselors understand and apply reproductive autonomy in their prenatal genetic counseling practice. Moreover, there is a marked absence of any research pertaining to the role of GCs in the provision of NIPT in Australia (Warton & Vears, 2025a). This paper thus addresses two gaps in current literature: (1) the dearth of research examining GCs' understanding and application of reproductive autonomy in the provision of NIPT and (2) the absence of insight into the views and practice experiences of GCs' provision of NIPT in Australia. This paper reports on the clinical practice of GCs with NIPT in Australia, with particular focus on their conceptualization and facilitation of autonomous reproductive decision‐making.

METHODS

This paper reports on a qualitative interview study that aimed to explore GCs' views and experiences of NIPT in Australia. Ethics approval for this study was granted by the Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee (#35590).

Recruitment

Recruitment utilized convenience sampling, which supports researchers to include individuals who may be hard to reach due to limited availability (Etikan et al., 2016). To be eligible for this study, participants were required to hold current professional registration as a GC and be currently or previously involved in the provision of NIPT in Australia. Sampling strategies included advertisements in the Human Genetics Society of Australasia newsletter and emails to participants of a previous survey who indicated their willingness to be contacted for participation in future research (Johnston et al., 2024). A snowball sampling strategy was also employed whereby participants were asked to share study details with potential participants. A participant explanatory statement accompanied all recruitment advertisements and email invitations.

Interview procedure

Semi‐structured interviews were conducted via Zoom across two rounds: between August to September 2023 and May to June 2024. Interviews were video recorded and subsequently transcribed by CW. Verbal consent was provided by participants at the outset of each interview. Data collection was concluded based on information power. Information power is used for appraisal of qualitative data in health sciences research as an alternative to data saturation. Similar to the use of power calculation in quantitative research, the relative information power of a qualitative sample helps to determine sample size on the basis of the study aim, sample specificity, use of theory, quality of dialogue and variation (Malterud et al., 2021).

A semi‐structured interview guide (Appendix S1) was developed to understand participants' views and professional practice experiences with NIPT. The interview guide was developed by researchers with relevant expertise in genetic counseling, bioethics and qualitative research methods. Interview questions explored participants' pre‐ and post‐test involvement with NIPT, their conceptualization of reproductive autonomy, their understanding and application of non‐directiveness and their perspectives on test scope.

Data analysis

Inductive content analysis was used to analyze interview data. Inductive content analysis is commonly used with text‐based data, such as interview transcripts, employing a close reading of texts to infer codes from the data as opposed to analysis using a predetermined content list (Vears & Gillam, 2022). Coding was an iterative process whereby transcripts were initially coded for higher‐level content categories with subcategories codes being ascertained through subsequent readings. All transcripts were coded by CW in NVivo 14 (QSR International). Six transcripts were co‐coded by DV (Coulston et al., 2025). Codes were compared and discrepancies resolved through discussion to refine coding categories and enrich the analysis. While inductive content analysis can be undertaken by a single coder, a second coder was used for some transcripts in this study to increase nuance and richness of interpretation (Coulston et al., 2025).

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

Interviews were conducted with 23 GCs from across Australia (see Table 1). Interview duration ranged from 35 to 91 min with an average duration of 55 min. The majority of participants had more than 10 years of clinical practice experience (n = 13, 56.5%). Most participants were employed in private health services (n = 14, 60.9%), which included both for‐profit and non‐for‐profit organizations. Participants were based across 7 of Australia's 8 states and territories. While the majority (n = 21, 91.3%) of GCs worked with NIPT in a pre‐test capacity, most (n = 15/21, 71.4%) had infrequent pre‐test involvement in limited circumstances such as only for patients with a known translocation or chromosomal variation. All (n = 23/23, 100%) GCs worked with NIPT in a post‐test capacity. The nature of participants' involvement with NIPT is further described in a corresponding article (Warton & Vears, 2025b).

Genetic counselors' experiences with reproductive autonomy in the prenatal setting

Conceptualization of reproductive autonomy

GCs largely articulated their understanding of reproductive autonomy through the methods they used to operationalize it in clinical practice, including offering information and the availability of real decisional pathways. Aligning with traditional conceptions of autonomy, many GCs viewed information provision and independent decision‐making as features of autonomy. While some GCs emphasized the need to provide pregnant people with all information, others suggested that the information provided should be material to the individual and their pregnancy

It really is about giving people enough information so that they can contextualize their own risk and then make a decision that is appropriate for them. So trying to make sure that, if they're choosing a test or not choosing a test, they know why they're doing that and what the implications of that are and therefore what the implications of those results are. (P21)

To me, it means that you've had the opportunity to receive the pieces of information that are going to make a difference at a point when it's going to make a difference…. (P13)

Several GCs noted reproductive autonomy that supporting autonomous decision‐making encompassed not only information provision but also included supporting individuals to determine whether and what kinds of information they received.

… they have a right to decide… what genetic testing they want to do, how far they will go with that. The information that's provided, do they want to know that? It is always, as far as I'm concerned, a patient's right to choice. (P6)

Some GCs identified reproductive autonomy through the potential for informed choice and meaningful decision pathways, particularly in the context of post‐test counseling.

For me, it just means the choice about whether you want to continue a pregnancy or end a pregnancy. (P2)

Reproductive autonomy, to me, means that a woman can decide what she wants to do in terms of her family planning and I guess we usually talk about it in a more narrow definition, in terms of what they want to do in terms of prenatal testing or continuing a pregnancy or stopping on pregnancy. So I think it refers to women being able to make their own decisions in all aspects of family planning. (P23)

Supporting reproductive autonomy in practice

Examples provided by GCs of supporting reproductive autonomy in practice often involved supporting autonomous decision‐making in light of complex patient circumstances or decisional opposition. GCs generally reported supporting autonomous decision‐making to be rewarding, albeit also resulting in perceived pressure to always practice at their best. GCs often raised examples where they supported patients with complex personal circumstances to navigate significant post‐test decision‐making in the absence of a clinically superior option. This pertained both to decisions to continue or terminate a pregnancy as well as whether to pursue subsequent diagnostic testing

… they wanted a greater understanding of the test so we went through the ins and outs of NIPT… and the couple were wanting additional testing… they wanted sort of the most comprehensive level of information available… And the couple, I mean they had young children, and they felt that having a child with Down syndrome was not something that they would be willing to take on or manage, so they elected to terminate the pregnancy. (P1)

Two GCs gave examples of promoting reproductive autonomy through advocating for patient choice against perceived pressures or opposition. This included supporting patient decision‐making where other healthcare professionals questioned patient choice, but also where counselors supported patient choice despite personal discomfort with the decision.

I met a young girl, she would have been in her twenties, and she was not in a relationship at the time, but she was pregnant and she received a high risk NIPT result for Trisomy 21… She really felt that she would be very capable of supporting a child with special needs and whatever that child needed she would be able to work with that… and there were a lot of conversations and doctors around being like, ‘Is this okay? Should this be happening?’ and I really tried to advocate for this girl… I really advocated for her and said, ‘People will tell you that you should have more testing and you don't have to do that. You can, if you want, to know 100% for sure, but you don't have to.’ And so she ended up not doing that. Her baby was delivered, tested after birth, and found to have Trisomy 21 but was doing really well. (P8)

The majority of GCs viewed their experiences supporting autonomous reproductive decision‐making to be a rewarding experience that contributed to their role satisfaction.

It overall feels good that you're helping a couple through a really difficult and tricky time of their life, and knowing that they're making the best decisions for them is also good to see, and, from an outsider's perspective, knowing that you can provide a little bit of support to help them through that time. (P1)

While many identified their role in supporting decision‐making to be a privilege, this corresponded with a perceived pressure to provide quality, comprehensive genetic counseling.

…sometimes I think you put pressure on yourself to be doing the absolute best that you can and you want to feel like you're really helping people. But sometimes it's hard to know whether you are because of the situation that you see that they are in. (P11)

Understandings and applications of (non‐)directiveness

GCs varied in their understandings and alignment with non‐directive counseling. While no GCs identified their practice as wholly directive, some GCs echoed critiques of non‐directive counseling approaches and noted being directive in terms of the decision‐making process. Non‐directiveness was understood as a means of supporting autonomous reproductive decision‐making. GCs described non‐directiveness as providing options and exploring potential pathways in alignment with patient needs and values. Many participants described their approach to prenatal counseling for NIPT as non‐directive.

I think I'm fairly non‐directive. I don't sit right back and go, ‘It's your choice.’ I do try really hard to work with people to understand, from a values base, what's really important to them and work around those other things… So I would say I'm non‐directive, but exploratory… (P14)

I will say to [patients] ‘I'm not here to tell you what you should and shouldn't do. A genetic counselor should be non‐directive in their support’. (P6)

Some GCs' conceptualization of non‐directiveness aligned with traditional conceptions of non‐directiveness in genetic counseling which emphasized the primary role of the GC as neutral information provision.1

… when I think non‐directive, I think that I should be just giving them the information and they make their own choice. (P7)

… non‐directive is just providing all the options and letting the patient say which option they want or none of the options. It's not telling a patient which direction to follow, it's just providing information and letting the patient decide for themselves. (P2)

However, others explicitly acknowledged critiques of the traditional non‐directive‐directive binary, questioning whether this conceptualization appropriately reflected counselors' actual practice and expertise and whether some level of directiveness was required to adequately support autonomous decision‐making.

I feel like [the terms directive and non‐directive] are probably not that helpful in describing genetic counselling anymore. I think historically we talked about non‐directiveness as being really important because genetic counseling did arise in these prenatal contexts and we were trying to really push back against previous eugenic practices. I think it was a necessary step in genetic counseling to differentiate ourselves from harmful practices, but I would argue that we are neither directive nor non‐directive… I still think that in prenatal we engage people and share decision making to support them to reach a conclusion that works for them and just providing options and calling that non‐directive is not representative of the nuanced discussions that we have. (P5)

Aligning with this divergence from traditional conceptualizations of non‐directiveness, many GCs identified elements of their practice as directive. GCs often made the distinction between being directive in the decision‐making process (i.e., recommending a care pathway based on their understanding of the patient's values and goals) and being non‐directive in the decisional outcome (i.e., whether or not a patient chose to pursue the recommended care pathway).

I would see myself as very directive about the processes people go through, the important things to think about… I'm not directive about an outcome or decisions that people make…. We're not directive in an outcome, what decisions they make I'm not invested in, but I'm very invested in the process. (P9)

Directive in that way of saying, ‘These are the facts of the situation,’ but not directive in terms of decision making processes. (P17)

Directiveness in the decisional outcome was understood by some as aligning more closely with a paternalistic or medical model of supporting decision‐making. No GCs identified their practice as wholly directive. Most GCs described at least one instance in which they would or did have the desire to be more directive. All GCs expressed this desire in relation to the decision‐making process rather than the decisional outcome. The primary circumstance in which GCs desired to be more directive was where patients decided to end pregnancies based on NIPT results alone. In such instances, some GCs expressed concerns that the immediate emotional response of patients to positive NIPT results may cloud understandings of the accuracy of the test or mildness of the condition.

I can think of some awful examples where people have wanted to or have terminated a pregnancy based off of an NIPT result and that doesn't sit well with me… I'm thinking of one particular couple, they were feeling very distressed by that news and really concerned about some of the features of Klinefelters and me, as the counsellor was reiterating, ‘This is a screening result and we don't know that this is true. We really strongly recommend confirming this if you are thinking about requesting a termination.’ They went ahead and had a termination without confirming it… That's difficult. I feel like you're trying to support these people and do the best for them and it feels a little bit out of your hands sometimes… But ultimately it's their decision and can't really do anything about that. (P8)

GCs also reported increased directivity about specific tests that were more appropriate for individual patients, particularly where GCs had relevant knowledge of the benefits of different tests or where they were aware of other healthcare professionals providing patients with conflicting advice.

There are times where I will be a little bit directive but I think that comes with experience… If a patient says to me that they're on Thyroxine, for example, and they have said that they're going to choose the Generation test, I might say to them ‘That actually might not be the best test for you because with their test, they have no wriggle room for noisy DNA.’ They'll just fail it whereas the VCGS Percept test, they know the noise that's going to happen if patients are on drugs like Thyroxine. That would be a case where I would be a little bit more directive… So I wouldn't say ‘You do this test’, but there's certain ways of wording things to be a little more directive without giving it any instruction. (P6)

Yes, particularly when you know one of their other managing doctors might be giving conflicting feedback or advice to our mutual patient. That's when I would be more directive. Instead of acknowledging and outlining the differences of those perspectives, outlining my rationale for being directive towards a particular diagnostic test over another one. (P1)

Supporting patients through (mis)information and uncertainty

A key theme in this study was the perceived expertise of GCs in managing the uncertainty inherent in prenatal screening, including uncertainty regarding the clinical presentation of a result. This expertise was often raised in contrast to examples of correcting misinformation and the management of uncertainty by non‐genetics professionals. Aligning with the goal of supporting autonomous decision‐making, GCs approached post‐test counseling by identifying the knowledge, response and position of the individual patient to facilitate decision‐making in accordance with individual goals and values. Identifying patient understanding of the test and test results was viewed as particularly essential when providing post‐test counseling for those to whom they did not provide pre‐test counseling. Many GCs felt the content and process of pre‐test counseling provided by other healthcare professionals was opaque

I don't know what's being discussed with the doctor, really. (P10)

I feel that it's kind of a black box at the moment as to what's happening in practice with that pre‐test counseling, particularly from the GPs and the obstetricians. I feel like I would have more of a sense of what is talked about when a genetic counselor is providing pre‐test counseling, because I know what education they receive in their qualifications, for example. (P5)

Many GCs reported that pre‐test counseling influenced their post‐test counseling approach. Several GCs reported correcting misinformation from other healthcare professionals about the accuracy of the test and the severity of the condition tested as well as navigating instances where patients were not aware they had used NIPT or had received unexpected results.

Sometimes there's been some miscommunication or misinterpretation by the patient of what the doctors told them. I think a lot of, and genetic counseling roles in general, particularly in this space, is around sometimes working back from that and providing a bit more information… (P17)

I feel like sometimes the consent process, if it's not through a genetic counselor, isn't really there, and then patients are a little bit more surprised when the results come through. Of course, you're going to be shocked and devastated sometimes by the result that you hear, but I feel that that's amplified when people haven't even had a proper consent to NIPT testing. (P2)

Misinformation about test accuracy or detected conditions or misuse of NIPT alongside other prenatal tests was often perceived to result in a significant emotional burden for patients. While GCs noted that they were unlikely to see all patients who decided to terminate, some reported instances in which misinformation influenced a patient's decision to terminate their pregnancy.

Somebody who has heard that there's an anomaly and have almost made that gut reaction to stop the pregnancy or that very quick decision, which is obviously fine, but I think being told that it's a significant condition adds weight to their decision… if we're potentially saying, ‘Yeah, but it could be milder than that,’ that can be hard… the patient would prefer to continue to hear what they've heard in the first place. (P21)

Several GCs also reported experiences of providing post‐test counseling subsequent to NIPT being used inappropriately alongside other prenatal tests, such as without first conducting a viability scan or following NIPT with combined first trimester screening.

We started adding more information about the importance of that 12–13 week anatomy scan because so many people thought NIPT replaced it and the fact is it absolutely does not so we made a really big push for that… What's the point of paying me, at that time, $800‐$900 for a test if you don't have an ongoing pregnancy? What's the point of paying me this amount of money if actually we see something so concerning you need to go straight to CVS? (P19)

Post‐test counseling thus often included a reiteration of test characteristics and limitations. Post‐test counseling also included discussion of the expected clinical presentation of a given result. Several GCs emphasized uncertainty as a unique feature of genetic testing and one that considerably complicated reproductive decision‐making. Some GCs viewed their expertise in navigating this uncertainty with patients as a point of difference between themselves and other healthcare professionals.

Very, very rarely are we dealing with something that we can be really exact about. Most of the time, it's uncertainty, it's percentages, chances. People really struggle with it and it makes their decision making really hard. I guess you could argue, though, they're going to have uncertainty either way, whether NIPT is there or not, it's just another step. (P20)

That it's a hard space, and you're not always going to get it right. That's probably something that is a little foreign to a lot of other health professionals. I think that there's so much that's right and wrong or clearer cut in other disciplines and, with genetics, we're really used to working with that uncertainty. (P14)

Despite their unique expertise in navigating uncertainty in the prenatal genetic setting, many GCs acknowledged that pre‐test counseling could be competently provided by primary care providers who ordered the test. GCs emphasized confidence and sufficient genetics education as factors for determining the quality of pre‐test counseling providers rather than specific professions.

I feel like we're moving towards it being much more routinely offered in general practice. If we can get GPs to feel really confident in providing that pre‐test counseling and informed consent, that would be great. (P18)

I think that [an understanding of NIPT] probably trumps profession… I think NIPT, in an ideal world, all the information should be delivered by someone confident in delivering that information and has a good awareness of NIPT, too. But I know that not all GPs who order NIPT are across, or very, very familiar with the testing. (P1)

Offering NIPT

Choice of NIPT provider

Relationships with laboratories were the primary factor reported to influence the choice of NIPT provider. Benefits of an established relationship with a laboratory included access to further explanation and additional interpretation in instances of complex test results.

I'll probably nearly always go to [named laboratory]. The reason I would go there is that I know some of the scientists there, I have relationships with them, I know that I can ask them questions and get the answers that make sense to me, which then supports me to be better at what I do. That's where I'd go for all those reasons. If I had the relationship with someone else, I'd go to someone else, right? (P14)

… faith in the lab and our ability to communicate with the lab and love from the lab. So there are some labs where the impression I get is that they just churn out a report without too much thought or comment. (P3)

Other factors that influenced the choice of NIPT provider included the quality or perceived utility of reports, particularly when testing based on known parental risk factors such as X‐linked conditions or known translocations. Patient demand, cost and logistical concerns, such as proximity to the lab or pathology collection schedules, were also factors.

Cost

The prohibitive cost of NIPT for some people was raised as a source of inequity and as a limiting factor in patient decision‐making about using NIPT. Cost limited the reproductive options available, both encouraging some to opt for lower cost prenatal tests and impacting whether healthcare professionals offered the test to patients.

We see a lot of people who don't have any testing because they can't afford it. Or they go on to have diagnostic testing, because they can't afford [NIPT]. (P12)

[NIPT is] not offered publicly, but it provides a dilemma in that, if you've got an increased risk, for free, you could put your pregnancy at risk or you can pay to have a test. There's obviously an inequity there as well.2 (P21)

While cost was viewed as a challenge to patient autonomy due to creating inequitable access, it was simultaneously viewed as a protective factor against routinization by some GCs. Cost was viewed to prompt patients to consider whether the prenatal testing was something that was important to them in their pregnancy.

It's tricky, because removing cost could level the playing field in terms of accessing that test but then it becomes something that people just automatically offer. You remove cost and you almost are taking away that decision‐making process for someone to say, ‘Oh, should I? I'm paying this cost! What information am I going to get?’… Cost, it's a barrier, but you're also spreading that line between making it routine and losing potentially that more involved decision making process because people have to pay money to have the test. (P17)

However, GCs did not view this protective element as sufficient reason to continue offering NIPT on a user‐pays basis. While GCs supported public funding for NIPT, views on how public funding should be implemented varied.

I wish it was on Medicare. I wish every woman had access to it if she wanted… It might be good to make it medicare funded under certain situations like with older parents and older mothers, or after a first trimester screen that come back positive, something like that, put some caveats on it. (P12)

I firmly believe that genetic screening is much better placed in a government funded screening program model, not a medicare funded test. The direction that the government seems to have been going down with genetic screening and prenatal and preconception is to fund Medicare item numbers. The problem with that is that it literally only funds the test and there's no service that wraps around it. So there's no funding for extra genetic counselling to support people with the results… (P11)

Views on the scope of NIPT

As opposed to restricting the availability of some uses of NIPT, several GCs believed how NIPT is used should be determined by individuals in consultation with their healthcare professional. This view was largely motivated by the belief that challenges arose from inadequate pre‐test counseling rather than some inherent feature of screening for a condition.

It's an interesting question, because part of me thinks that maybe it would be better to restrict NIPT to the common chromosome aneuploidies… But then another part of me– like when I was pregnant, I went and had the full genome NIPT because I wanted it and I wanted all the information. So no, I don't think it should be restricted. I think it needs to have appropriate pre‐test consent. (P5)

In a perfect world, every woman would sit down with her provider and talk about the scope of testing and tick the things that they're concerned about and not the other ones. (P3)

Some GCs additionally questioned the feasibility of implementing restrictions on the scope of NIPT in light of current genetics practice.

NIPT's out of the bag now, so I don't think we can shove the genie back in, unfortunately. (P9)

The horse has already bolted… I'm like ‘Look, it's going to happen. They're going to stick everything that they can on them’ and it's just my job to be prepared for that and to be practising those conversations. (P13)

In contrast, some GCs suggested that possible uses of NIPT should be restricted prior to being offered to patients.

I think there does need to be some kind of gatekeeping about what we offer, that it's not just a free‐for‐all, because, and I suppose it's more applicable maybe in fetal exome or something like that, because there needs to be some responsibility with the information that's provided to people in the context of where they're at in the pregnancy and what issues are seen in a pregnancy. (P9)

I would think that in a public system, at least, which is what I work in, that what the limit or options are could feasibly be determined by healthcare professionals. But what a person should then have should probably be up to them. So, this is the pool from which you can choose, but it shouldn't be all or nothing. (P4)

Some GCs who supported restrictions to NIPT questioned whether the test should be used to screen for conditions with milder phenotypes. Some GCs questioned whether the availability of screening for milder phenotypes reflected women's desires to have healthy children and acknowledged that screening offerings often reflected societal values around disability and the use of prenatal testing.

I don't think that we have a genuine conversation in society about disability, and everyone gets onto this treadmill of prenatal testing and NIPT… Women will say, ‘I want to have a healthy baby.’ Girls with Turner syndrome are generally reasonably healthy, some people with Down syndrome are pretty healthy. There's a disconnect between what's being pitched and what is being requested by the woman. (P3)

Several GCs raised concerns about potential future applications of NIPT, particularly in relation to screening for conditions or characteristics that were not perceived to impact the health of a future child.

… I have seen some of the discussions around NIPT and have seen some of the things about ‘as much information as possible’ or adult onset conditions or traits, which kind of horrify me a bit. (P4)

In the future I'd hate to see it be used for selecting intelligence or skin color or things like that. (P10)

Where some level of restriction was supported, GCs ultimately felt that a range of healthcare professionals and laboratory staff should be involved in the decision. A small number of GCs also suggested the involvement of people with lived experience of testing and/or the genetic condition and ethicists.

DISCUSSION

This is the first study to focus on GCs' role in and understanding of reproductive autonomy in provision of NIPT. GCs viewed their role as facilitating reproductive genetics decision‐making and supporting patients to navigate associated uncertainty. Uncertainty was viewed by GCs as an inherent feature of prenatal genetic testing, but not one that was incompatible with reproductive autonomy. GCs perceived their expertise as enabling them to facilitate autonomous reproductive decision‐making about NIPT in light of such uncertainty. However, many acknowledged the need for additional education and support for other healthcare professionals in this area.

GCs in this study demonstrated a sound understanding of reproductive autonomy in the context of their professional roles. While GCs expressed a shared conceptualization of reproductive autonomy as supporting people to make timely and informed decisions about their pregnancies, there were variations in how this concept was applied in their clinical practice. Many GCs described their counseling approach as non‐directive and emphasized the neutral and comprehensive provision of information. As understood by counselors in this study, non‐directive counseling emphasizes value‐neutral information provision as a means of supporting reproductive decision‐making. However, non‐directive counseling has been subject to critique for inadequately supporting comprehension of the information provided and for inadequately recognizing the social context in which reproductive decisions are made (Dive, 2017; Dive & Newson, 2018; Hodgson & Spriggs, 2005). Questions have also been raised as to whether neutrality can be maintained in clinical practice (Garcia et al., 2008; Hertig et al., 2014; Scott et al., 2024; Spiegel, 2026). Many GCs were cognizant of these critiques and diverged from a strict traditional non‐directive approach in their practice. GCs distinguished between being directive in the decision‐making process (e.g., recommending a care pathway based on their understanding of the patient's values and goals) and being non‐directive in the decisional outcome (i.e., whether or not a patient chose to pursue a given care pathway). This better aligns with alternative approaches to prenatal genetic counseling, such as reproductive deliberation (Warton et al., 2023). Reproductive deliberation aims to recognize the relationality of the clinical encounter through allowing GCs to be responsive to patient requests for value judgments while maintaining the decisional authority of the pregnant person (Warton et al., 2023). The nuanced implementation of GCs' value judgments suggested under reproductive deliberation and enacted in practice by GCs in this study is important for facilitating patient comprehension in light of the increasing quantity and complexity of genetic information returned via NIPT. However, as NIPT is increasingly provided by other health professions without specialist genetic counseling education, there is a need for further research to understand whether and how the value judgments of other professionals should be integrated into the provision of NIPT. The findings of this study demonstrate differences in the operationalization of reproductive autonomy in genetic counseling practice, particularly around whether to adhere to a non‐directive approach. Given compelling critiques in existing literature of non‐directive counseling reflected by some GCs in this study, training curricula should be designed to reflect novel approaches to prenatal genetic counseling.

Uncertainty emerged as a theme across GCs' experiences providing prenatal genetic counseling for NIPT. Uncertainty was identified as arising from the inherent nature of NIPT as a screening test, thus indicating the likelihood of a genetic condition as opposed to confirming a diagnosis, as well as the potential phenotype of a detected condition. Challenges in post‐test decision‐making for patients arising from this uncertainty were also recognized. GCs viewed supporting patients to navigate this uncertainty as a key part of their role in facilitating autonomous reproductive decision‐making. GCs viewed some uncertainty as inherent to prenatal genetic testing and as something they were equipped to navigate in order to facilitate autonomous reproductive decisions. These findings contrast with existing research that reports a tension between prenatal testing provision and healthcare professionals'3 desires for certainty (Drysdale et al., 2024). Healthcare professionals in existing literature viewed the uncertainty arising from prenatal testing as negating meaningful reproductive decision‐making (Drysdale et al., 2024). The contrast between the findings in this study and those in the existing literature suggests that the perceived impact of (un)certainty in reproductive decision‐making is linked to profession. Where prenatal genetic counseling is provided by a GC, this alleged tension between reproductive autonomy and NIPT use dissipates. This is further reflected in the views expressed by some GCs in this study who recognized their expertise in navigating uncertainty as being distinct from typical scenarios encountered by medical colleagues. Existing literature additionally reports that patients may expect reassurance from prenatal testing (Long et al., 2023; van der Meij et al., 2022; van Schendel et al., 2017) which may be challenged by uncertain or unexpected results (Bowman‐Smart et al., 2019; Drysdale et al., 2024). However, GCs in this study reported patient expectations for reassurance and the return of unexpected results were absent where pre‐test counseling was provided by a GC. Healthcare professionals have a significant role in managing or establishing patient expectations during pre‐test counseling. Reproductive decision‐making in light of uncertain information may be challenging for patients. However, uncertainty need not be considered a necessary constraint on reproductive autonomy. Rather, through acknowledging uncertainty as an inherent feature of prenatal genetic testing, healthcare professionals can be upskilled to adequately facilitate autonomous choice around uncertain genetic information arising from NIPT.

Despite variations in whether GCs supported restrictions to the conditions detectable via NIPT, these views appeared to be largely motivated by a shared goal of supporting reproductive autonomy. Some GCs opposed restrictions and suggested that which conditions were screened via NIPT should be determined by the individual pregnant person in consultation with their healthcare professional. Those who supported this view suggested that issues arising from NIPT were not due to an inherent feature of screening a given condition but rather insufficient pre‐test counseling and raised concerns about unduly limiting the choice of pregnant people. Other GCs viewed restricting the scope of NIPT as a necessary means of supporting autonomous patient decision‐making. This view was largely motivated by a perceived misalignment between the expressed interests of pregnant people in screening for conditions that significantly affected the health of their child and their choice to screen for mild conditions. GCs suggested that insufficient pre‐test counseling resulted in choices discordant with patient values and questioned whether informed consent could be truly achieved in light of the increasing scope of NIPT. This echoes sentiments in some existing research that support limiting the scope of NIPT to that where informed consent can be achieved (Kater‐Kuipers et al., 2018). It may also reflect bioethical debate around whether some level of non‐coercive paternalism, such as pre‐emptive restrictions on conditions available via NIPT, may be appropriate in the context of genomic sequencing technologies (Koplin et al., 2022). The diverse opinions expressed by participants highlight a perceived tension between supporting reproductive autonomy and feasibility constraints in the current clinical application of NIPT. The range of genetic conditions available via NIPT does not necessarily inhibit autonomous reproductive decision‐making. However, supporting autonomous decision‐making where there are no restrictions to scope requires sufficient training and resourcing of the healthcare workforce. The feasibility of achieving this resourcing on a national level remains a point of tension. Irrespective of whether restrictions are enacted, findings from this study support the prioritization of reproductive autonomy in policies determining such restrictions.

Aligning with findings in existing literature (Bowman‐Smart et al., 2019; Johnston et al., 2024; Lewis et al., 2016; Long et al., 2023; McKinn et al., 2022; Pynaker et al., 2025), the cost of NIPT was viewed as a source of inequity. Cost was perceived to constrain the reproductive options available for some individuals through both encouraging patients to select lower‐cost prenatal tests and discouraging healthcare professionals from offering NIPT where they perceived it to be beyond a patient's financial capacity. Whether NIPT should receive public funding has been the subject of ongoing academic debate. Integrating NIPT into public healthcare systems has raised concerns about routinization and pressure to test and terminate for funded conditions (Bunnik et al., 2020a; Iacobucci, 2018; Schmitz, 2020; Vanstone et al., 2018, 2019). Moreover, it has been suggested that there is a misalignment between the goal of prenatal screening to support reproductive autonomy and typically funded healthcare services that are aimed at prevention or treatment (Schmitz, 2020). Others suggest that private payment for NIPT perpetuates social injustice for those who cannot afford it (Bunnik et al., 2020a, 2020b; Pynaker et al., 2025). Public funding is thus argued to support informed decision‐making, conferring medical utility through providing the option to decide not to have a child with a given condition (Bunnik et al., 2020b). Additionally, equitable public funding for NIPT should address intersectional factors that impact access, including increasing access for those living in rural or remote areas (Luke et al., 2022; Tweneboa Kodua et al., 2022) and offering culturally responsive services (Filoche et al., 2017; Luke et al., 2022). Although GCs in this study acknowledged cost as a potential safeguard against routinization, they felt that this did not outweigh concerns of inequity and highlighted that this safeguard did not apply to patients for whom the cost of testing was negligible. GCs interviewed supported public funding in the provision of NIPT. GCs varied in their views about whether funded NIPT should be available to all pregnant people and whether it should be limited to specific conditions, such as those where NIPT was perceived to have medical utility. GCs additionally varied in their support of whether funding should be implemented as a Medicare rebate for NIPT alone or as part of a screening program with wraparound support services. While much academic debate is focused on whether NIPT should be funded, GCs presumed a right to publicly funded NIPT and instead questioned how such funding should be implemented in order to best support autonomous reproductive decisions.

Limitations

Low generalizability is often considered a limitation of qualitative research such as this. While generalizability is an important quality criterion in research, we aim to present data in this paper that has high transferability. Transferability is supported through providing detailed descriptions of participant data and context which supports subsequent appraisal, abstraction and application of information by readers facilitated by a contextual understanding of the data. While two researchers were involved in the analysis and interpretation of data, several researchers with diverse expertise provided feedback on iterations of the interview guide.

CONCLUSION

NIPT is an increasingly ubiquitous component of prenatal care. Correspondingly, prenatal genetic counseling is being provided by an increasing range of healthcare professionals. GCs hold unique expertise in facilitating autonomous reproductive decision‐making. While reproductive autonomy is well articulated by GCs, there is a gap between current genetics education that emphasizes non‐directive counseling and the practice of GCs which, for some, better aligns with reproductive deliberation. GCs are particularly well equipped to facilitate autonomous prenatal decision‐making in the context of uncertain genetic information. While some research suggests there is a tension between reproductive autonomy and genetic uncertainty, the findings in this study indicate this tension may arise from incongruous medical training as opposed to an inherent feature of NIPT. Additional resources and training are required to ensure healthcare professionals without genetic counseling backgrounds are supported to adequately navigate uncertainty in the prenatal genetic counseling process. Scope of conditions screened remains a point of tension; however, decisions about restrictions to scope should be motivated by supporting the reproductive autonomy of the pregnant person. The cost of NIPT remains a source of inequity, and public funding is supported as a means of addressing this. Public funding for wraparound services that include healthcare professional education and training could mitigate some of the challenges currently arising from the provision of NIPT. Alongside the continued development and clinical availability of NIPT, there is a need to standardize genetic counseling training and develop policy responsive to concerns of inequity, including cost and test scope.
