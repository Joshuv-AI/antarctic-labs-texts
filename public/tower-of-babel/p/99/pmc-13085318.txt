Association between prenatal opioid exposure and long-term neurodevelopmental disorders among children: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Hey-ran Choi, In-Ae Song, Tak Kyu Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExisting evidence on the long-term effects of prenatal opioid exposure on child development remains inconsistent, often limited by short follow-up and insufficient control for maternal clinical indications. We investigated whether maternal opioid use during pregnancy is associated with an increased hazard of long-term neurodevelopmental disorders (LNDD) in children, utilizing an extended 13-year follow-up.MethodsThis retrospective, population-based cohort study used the National Health Insurance Service database in South Korea, including all live births between 2011 and 2014 (n = 1,553,505). Children were followed until December 31, 2023. Maternal opioid prescriptions were categorized by trimester and duration (1–6 days vs. ≥ 7 days). To address confounding by indication, propensity score (PS) matching (1:10 ratio) and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were employed, adjusting for maternal psychiatric history and pain-related clinical indications.ResultsIn the matched cohort (n = 133,147), the incidence of LNDD was 10.53 per 1,000 person-years (PY) in the opioid-exposed group and 9.26 per 1,000 PY in the non-exposed group. Prenatal opioid exposure was associated with a 14% increased hazard of total LNDD (hazard ratio [HR] 1.14; 95% CI 1.08–1.20; P < 0.001). Subtype analysis showed the strongest association for intellectual disabilities (HR 1.33; 95% CI 1.16–1.53; P < 0.001). The hazard was more pronounced with first-trimester exposure (HR 1.17; 95% CI 1.06–1.28; P = 0.001) and longer durations of use (≥ 7 days: HR 1.17; 95% CI 1.09–1.24; P < 0.001).ConclusionsPrenatal opioid exposure is associated with a significantly increased hazard of LNDD, particularly intellectual disabilities, independently of maternal psychiatric and pain conditions. This hazard was particularly pronounced following first-trimester exposure and longer durations of use, with intellectual disabilities showing the strongest association. These findings, based on long-term follow-up into early adolescence, emphasize the need for cautious opioid prescribing during pregnancy and developmental surveillance for exposed offspring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06714-9.

Introduction

The use of opioid analgesics during pregnancy has significantly increased over the past two decades, paralleling the broader rise in opioid prescriptions globally [1, 2]. In South Korea, while the prevalence of opioid use remains lower than in Western countries, prescriptions for chronic non-cancer pain among reproductive-aged women have been steadily increasing [3], raising public health concerns regarding its safety during pregnancy [4, 5]. Opioids readily cross the placenta, and prenatal opioid exposure during critical periods of fetal development may disrupt normal neurodevelopmental processes [4]. Animal studies and emerging human data suggest that prenatal opioid exposure interferes with neuronal differentiation, synaptogenesis, and neurotransmitter systems in the developing brain [6–8]. Consequently, prenatal opioid exposure has been associated with immediate neonatal complications, including preterm birth, low birth weight, and neonatal abstinence syndrome [9].

Despite accumulating evidence, few studies have examined the long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes of prenatal opioid exposure. These discrepancies often arise from variations in study designs; for instance, traditional cohort studies frequently report increased risks, whereas more recent studies utilizing sibling-matched designs suggest that these associations may be largely explained by shared familial environments or underlying maternal clinical indications rather than the opioid exposure itself [10, 11]. Furthermore, inconsistencies stem from the limited follow-up durations in many studies—often restricted to early childhood—which may fail to capture disorders that manifest during school age or early adolescence.

A recent nationwide study in South Korea by Kang et al. [10] reported increased neuropsychiatric risks but noted that these associations were attenuated in sibling-matched analyses [10]. However, that study had a relatively limited follow-up period for children born in later years and focused on broad neuropsychiatric outcomes. To address these gaps, we conducted a nationwide cohort study with an extended follow-up of up to 13 years (through 2023). By utilizing a Cox proportional hazards model to account for varying follow-up durations and strictly controlling for maternal clinical indications—such as chronic pain and psychiatric comorbidities—while excluding neonatal mediators to avoid over-adjustment bias, we aim to provide a more refined understanding of the long-term developmental safety of prenatal opioids in an Asian population.

Methods

Study design, setting, and ethical statement

This population-based cohort study followed the STROBE reporting guidelines [12]. It was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1975, revised in 2008) and relevant institutional and national regulations. The study was approved by the institutional review board (approval No. X-2407-910-902) and by the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS; approval No. NHIS-2025-02-1-010), which also authorized access to the NHIS database. The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design and use of deidentified data.

Data source

In South Korea, the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) is the sole health insurance provider. Enrollment is mandatory for all Korean citizens and for foreign residents living in the country for more than 6 months. NHIS members pay premiums, and government support is provided based on disease severity. The NHIS database includes comprehensive information on prescription medications, medical procedures, diagnoses, socioeconomic factors, and mortality [13]. Diagnoses in the NHIS database are recorded using the Korean Standard Classification of Diseases (KCD), which is a modified version of the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10). Although more than 95% of physicians practice privately, their clinical activities are regulated by the government regarding prescriptions, treatments, and service fees. As a result, the likelihood of underreporting for prescriptions or diagnoses is considered very low, as all clinical activities are strictly monitored and linked to the national reimbursement system. All relevant KCD codes were included in this study.

Study population

Birth data from all deliveries in South Korea between January 1, 2011, and December 31, 2014, were collected. Newborns were initially identified, and maternal records linked to each newborn were subsequently retrieved. The children were followed from their date of birth until December 31, 2023, the date of diagnosis of LNDD, or death, whichever occurred first. Cases with incomplete delivery-related information were excluded to maintain the completeness and reliability of the dataset.

Opioid exposure (opioid group and non-opioid group)

We classified newborns into the opioid group if their mothers were exposed to any type of prescribed opioid analgesic during pregnancy. To estimate the pregnancy period, we referred to previous studies and calculated the Last Menstrual Period (LMP) by back-calculating from the delivery date [11, 14]. Specifically, for deliveries associated with preterm birth (identified by KCD codes O60 or P07), the LMP was estimated by subtracting 35 weeks (245 days) from the delivery date. For all other deliveries, 39 weeks (273 days) were subtracted to represent the midpoint of full-term gestation [14, 15]. This approach has been widely adopted in maternal-fetal research using administrative claims data to minimize misclassification of exposure timing. In the non-opioid group, mothers with a history of opioid prescriptions within 90 days prior to the estimated LMP were excluded due to the potential risk of opioid exposure. The opioid group was further categorized based on the timing of opioid prescriptions into first trimester exposure (1–12 weeks from LMP), second trimester exposure (13–27 weeks from LMP), and third trimester exposure (28–40 weeks from LMP). To evaluate a potential dose-response relationship, we also stratified exposure by duration: 1–6 days and ≥ 7 days, which served as a proxy for cumulative exposure levels.

Study outcome (LNDD)

The primary study outcome was the occurrence of LNDD in the offspring. Children were followed from birth until the date of LNDD diagnosis, death, or the end of the study period (December 31, 2023), whichever occurred first. To address potential selection bias, children who died before a diagnosis were censored at their date of death rather than being excluded from the cohort. LNDD was defined using KCD codes F70–F98. In South Korea, the registration of these diagnostic codes is required for patients to access government-funded financial support and specialized educational services for developmental disabilities. This institutional requirement ensures a high positive predictive value and diagnostic validity for LNDD codes within the NHIS database. Consistent with previous literature, we classified LNDD into three sub-domains: intellectual disabilities (F70–F79), disorders of psychological development (F80–F89), and behavioral/emotional disorders with onset usually in childhood (F90–F98) [15, 16].

Collected covariates

Covariates were collected for both newborns and mothers to account for potential confounding. Maternal demographic variables included age at delivery, household income, employment status, and residential area.

To rigorously address potential confounding by indication, we collected detailed maternal clinical characteristics recorded using the KCD. Baseline health status was assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). Furthermore, we included maternal psychiatric history (e.g., mood disorders, anxiety, and sleep disorders) and potential clinical indications for opioid prescriptions (e.g., dorsopathies, arthropathies, and injuries/trauma) identified within one year prior to the estimated LMP. This ensure that these maternal health factors represent baseline status prior to the index pregnancy-related exposure. We also collected pregnancy-related conditions, such as complications of the placenta, labor, and delivery. The specific KCD codes used to define these maternal conditions are detailed in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as counts with corresponding percentages, and continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations (SDs). To address potential baseline differences between the opioid and non-opioid groups, propensity score (PS) matching was performed. PS matching, a widely used method for reducing confounding in observational studies, was conducted using the nearest neighbor algorithm at a 1:10 ratio without replacement, applying a caliper width of 0.2 [17]. A 1:10 matching ratio was chosen to maximize the utilization of the large non-opioid pool while maintaining the robustness of the opioid group samples. PSs were estimated through logistic regression models incorporating all maternal demographic and clinical baseline covariates (e.g., CCI, psychiatric history, and pain-related indications). Notably, neonatal conditions identified as potential mediators (e.g., preterm birth, low birth weight) were excluded from the PS estimation to avoid over-adjustment bias. The balance of covariates before and after matching was assessed using the absolute standardized mean difference (ASD), with a value < 0.1 indicating adequate balance.

Considering the varying follow-up durations and the time-to-event nature of neurodevelopmental diagnoses, Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were conducted in the PS-matched cohort to compare the incidence of LNDD. For each child, the time-at-risk was defined from the date of birth until the date of first LNDD diagnosis, death, or the end of the study period (December 31, 2023), whichever occurred first. Incidence rates were expressed as the number of events per 1,000 person-years (PY). The results were presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The proportional hazards assumption was verified by testing the Schoenfeld residuals.

Multivariable Cox regression modeling was also performed in the entire cohort as sensitivity analyses. These analyses aimed to: (1) assess the consistency of findings between the PS-matched and entire cohorts; (2) evaluate the dose-response relationship based on the total duration of opioid prescriptions (1–6 days vs. ≥7 days); and (3) determine whether the association varied based on the timing of opioid exposure (first, second, or third trimester). Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, all of which were < 2.0. By utilizing the Cox model, we addressed potential selection bias related to mortality during follow-up by treating deaths as censored observations rather than excluding them. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

Between January 1, 2011, and December 31, 2014, a total of 1,821,452 newborns from 1,231,523 mothers were recorded in South Korea. Among these, 10,637 mothers and 267,947 newborns were excluded due to incomplete delivery records. Thus, 1,553,505 newborns from 1,220,886 mothers were initially screened for analysis. Among them, 12,130 newborns were classified into the opioid group based on maternal exposure to prescribed opioid analgesics during pregnancy. Of the remaining 1,541,375 newborns, 125 were excluded because their mothers had a history of opioid prescriptions within 90 days prior to the estimated LMP, leaving 1,541,250 newborns in the non-opioid group. PS matching was then performed at a 1:10 ratio, resulting in a final matched cohort of 133,147 newborns (12,120 in the opioid group and 121,027 in the non-opioid group). A flow diagram of the study population selection process is presented in Fig. 1. Table 1 presents the comparison of baseline characteristics between the opioid and non-opioid groups before and after PS matching. After matching, all ASDs for covariates were less than 0.1, indicating that an adequate balance between the two groups was achieved in the PS-matched cohort. The mean follow-up period for assessing LNDD was 12.0 years (standard deviation [SD], 1.15 years) among all newborns included in this study.

Analyses in the PS-matched cohort

Table 2 presents the results of the analyses before and after PS matching. Discussion

In this nationwide, population-based cohort study conducted in South Korea, we found that prenatal exposure to opioid analgesics was associated with a 14% increased hazard of LNDD in offspring. This association remained consistent across various sensitivity analyses, including stratification by the duration and timing of opioid exposure. Notably, our findings remained robust even after strictly adjusting for maternal clinical indications—such as chronic pain and psychiatric comorbidities.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies that have reported an association between prenatal opioid exposure and adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes [18]. A recent nationwide cohort study by Kang et al. [10] in South Korea also demonstrated an increased risk of neuropsychiatric disorders, although their sibling-matched analysis suggested that shared familial factors might partly explain the observed associations [10]. However, our study provides several critical advancements that address the limitations of prior research. First, we extended the follow-up period until December 31, 2023, ensuring a longitudinal observation of up to 13 years. This extended duration is crucial for capturing neurodevelopmental disorders that often manifest more clearly during school age or early adolescence, whereas earlier studies were often limited to early childhood. Second, while sibling analyses are powerful for controlling unmeasured familial confounding, they can be limited by reduced statistical power and “carryover effects.” By utilizing a much larger PS-matched cohort and directly adjusting for the specific clinical indications for opioid prescriptions—such as maternal psychiatric history and chronic pain syndromes—we have provided complementary evidence that the association between opioid exposure and LNDD persists independently of these major confounders.

Several biological mechanisms may explain the observed association. Opioids readily cross the placenta and disrupt critical processes of fetal brain development, such as neuronal proliferation and synaptogenesis [6]. Animal studies have demonstrated that prenatal opioid exposure alters neurotransmitter systems, including dopaminergic and serotonergic pathways, which are essential for cognitive and emotional regulation [9]. In addition, opioids may impair placental function and induce chronic intrauterine hypoxia, further compromising fetal neurodevelopment [19]. These mechanisms suggest that both direct neurotoxic effects and indirect effects mediated through placental dysfunction may contribute to the increased risk of LNDD observed in offspring.

Importantly, the hazard of LNDD varied depending on the timing of opioid exposure, with the greatest hazard observed for exposures during the first trimester (HR, 1.17), followed by the second (HR, 1.15) and third trimesters (HR, 1.06). These findings suggest that not only the duration but also the timing of opioid exposure during pregnancy are critical determinants of long-term neurodevelopmental risks in offspring. The heightened vulnerability during the first trimester may reflect the critical periods of neuronal proliferation and migration occurring at that stage, as supported by previous studies [10, 18]. Recent animal and human studies have further demonstrated that prenatal opioid exposure disrupts brain microstructure, alters neurite morphology, and reduces regional brain volumes, underscoring the particular sensitivity of early fetal brain development to opioid-related insults [7, 20].

When dividing LNDD into three subtypes, we observed that prenatal opioid exposure was associated with the highest hazard increase for intellectual disabilities, followed by disorders of psychological development and behavioral and emotional disorders usually occurring in childhood and adolescence. The stronger association between prenatal opioid exposure and intellectual disabilities may be explained by the particular vulnerability of early neurodevelopmental processes. During the first trimester, neuronal proliferation, migration, and synaptogenesis occur at a rapid pace, forming the structural and functional foundation for higher cognitive functions [7, 20]. Opioids readily cross the placenta and can disrupt these critical processes, impairing the development of brain regions such as the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, which are essential for intellectual and cognitive abilities [6]. Animal studies have shown that prenatal opioid exposure leads to abnormal hippocampal development and deficits in learning and memory, providing biological plausibility for the observed stronger association with intellectual disabilities [8]. These mechanisms suggest that early disruption of brain network formation may have more profound and irreversible effects on intellectual functioning compared to later-emerging domains such as behavioral or emotional regulation.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, opioid exposure was determined based on prescription records, and actual medication adherence during pregnancy could not be confirmed. Second, detailed information on the specific types and dosages of opioid analgesics was not available, limiting our ability to perform stratified analyses based on these factors. Third, although we adjusted for numerous covariates and performed PS matching, the possibility of residual or unmeasured confounding cannot be completely ruled out. In particular, we were unable to fully account for the potential effects of other teratogenic exposures during pregnancy, such as alcohol, tobacco, or concomitant medication use. Finally, while our use of the Cox proportional hazards model effectively addressed time-varying diagnosis patterns and accounted for mortality through censoring, administrative data may still under-capture subclinical cases.

Conclusions

In conclusion, prenatal exposure to opioid analgesics was associated with an increased hazard of LNDD in offspring in this nationwide, population-based cohort study. The hazard was particularly significant following first-trimester exposure and longer durations of use, with intellectual disabilities showing the strongest association. These findings, derived from an extended 13-year follow-up, emphasize the need for cautious opioid prescribing during pregnancy and heightened clinical awareness of potential long-term neurodevelopmental hazards.
