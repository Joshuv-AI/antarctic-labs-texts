Neurological complications of Zika virus and their impact on quality of life: a narrative review
Authors: Amber Tiemens, Lambertus Franciscus Wolters, Adrianne Marije de Roo, Gerard Timmy Vondeling, Juan Carlos Jaramillo, Adriana Tami, Maarten Jacobus Postma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundZika virus (ZIKV), known for its alarming health consequences, is primarily transmitted by infected Aedes spp. mosquitoes but can also spread through sexual contact and from mother to child during pregnancy. While most infections are mild or asymptomatic, ZIKV has been linked to severe neurological disorders, such as Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS) in adults and congenital Zika syndrome (CZS) in infants. While the substantial economic burden and the range of these disorders have been described, the potential long-term effects and the impact on quality of life (QoL) remain inadequately understood, presenting challenges for both public health and clinical care.Main bodyThis narrative review describes the broader spectrum of neurological complications associated with ZIKV infection and explores their impact on the QoL of patients and caregivers, aiming to deepen the public and clinical understanding of the topic, which is crucial for developing effective prevention and treatment strategies. Beyond the established links to GBS and CZS, ZIKV has been associated with other neurological complications, such as encephalitis and myelitis, highlighting the complex clinical course of the disease. These neurological outcomes can lead to lifelong disabilities, profoundly affecting patients’ and caregivers’ QoL. Mothers of children with CZS may experience heightened anxiety, depression, and overall reduced QoL, while many individuals with GBS struggle to regain the QoL they had pre-illness.ConclusionsThe limited availability of high-quality data, primarily relying on case reports, highlights the need for more comprehensive research to establish clear causal links and better understand the long-term consequences of ZIKV-related neurological complications. Prioritizing research on long-term effects, management, and prevention strategies is crucial to improving patient outcomes and guiding future clinical care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41182-026-01013-6.

Introduction

Once considered a mild tropical disease, Zika virus (ZIKV) infection, has emerged over the last decade as a serious global health threat capable of causing devastating neurological and developmental disorders [1, 2]. While ZIKV is primarily transmitted through infected Aedes spp. mosquitoes, it can also spread through sexual contact and from mother to fetus during pregnancy [3]. About 80% of people infected with ZIKV remain asymptomatic, while those who do exhibit symptoms—such as fever, rash, conjunctivitis, muscle and joint pain, and headache—typically experience these for 2–7 days [4, 5].

In adults, the infection can lead to a range of neurological disorders, such as Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS), myelitis, and encephalitis, affecting both the central nervous system (CNS) and the peripheral nervous system (PNS) [6–8]. Particularly concerning is how ZIKV infection during pregnancy can cause congenital Zika syndrome (CZS) in the fetus, leading to severe developmental complications. The 2015 Zika outbreak in Brazil highlighted these outcomes, with a surge in GBS cases and a significant rise in microcephaly among newborns, particularly in the northeastern region, where nearly 3000 suspected cases of microcephaly were reported by December that year [6, 9, 10]. This outbreak and the resulting increase in neurological disorders not only represented a significant clinical burden but also a substantial economic burden to the affected countries. Over a 10-year period, the costs for the Brazilian government were estimated at US$129.0 million for severe CZS and US$86.5 million for moderate CZS, while severe GBS cases in 2016 were estimated to cost nearly US$12 million for that year alone [11, 12]. In addition, the association between ZIKV infection during pregnancy and microcephaly prompted the WHO to declare it a Public Health Emergency of International Concern, thereby accelerating research into the virus’s effects and public health measures to control its spread [13].

Although the exact mechanisms behind ZIKV’s neurodegenerative effects remain unclear, studies have identified potential pathways. One involves ZIKV triggering an immune response that releases pro-inflammatory cytokines, causing CNS inflammation and neurological damage [14]. ZIKV can also cross the blood–brain barrier, allowing immune cells and inflammatory molecules to infiltrate the brain, worsening damage [14, 15]. Moreover, ZIKV has been shown to directly infect human neural progenitor cells, disrupting neurodevelopment and regeneration processes [16]. Understanding these pathways is crucial for preventing long-term neurological damage and guiding treatment approaches.

As there are currently no specific antiviral therapies approved for ZIKV infection, symptom management remains a primary focus in clinical settings. Preventative strategies currently rely largely on mosquito control and avoiding exposure in endemic areas. In parallel, several vaccine candidates are under investigation in clinical trials [17–19]. Continued research and funding are needed to further evaluate and develop effective preventive and therapeutic strategies against the disease.

The long-term neurological complications of ZIKV infection highlight the critical need for preventive measures and early detection, particularly for vulnerable groups, such as pregnant women, immunocompromised individuals, and residents of areas with active transmission. While several reviews have examined ZIKV-related complications, primarily focusing on CZS and GBS [7, 20, 21], a comprehensive review of the broader spectrum of neurological complications and their impact on quality of life (QoL) is still lacking.

This narrative review aims to fill this gap by giving an overview of the neurological complications associated with ZIKV infection and evaluating their impact on QoL for both patients and caregivers. Building on previous research that has identified key neurological effects and potential pathogenic mechanisms of ZIKV, this study provides an overview of the broader spectrum of neurological outcomes as a framework for understanding the disease’s complexity and how these complications affect both individuals and their families. This broader perspective is essential, because the burden of neurological disability often extends beyond the patient, significantly impacting the emotional and socioeconomical well-being of those providing care. By increasing understanding of the far-reaching impact of neurological complications associated with ZIKV, we hope to raise awareness further, support the development of effective public health strategies, improve clinical management, and foster targeted interventions to mitigate the devastating effects of ZIKV.

Methods

A narrative review was conducted to summarize existing knowledge and provide clinical and scientific context, rather than to address a narrowly defined research question. Literature searches were performed by two reviewers between August and September 2024. Searches were conducted in MEDLINE In-Process (PubMed.com), Embase, and MEDLINE (using Embase.com).

Initial searches focused on neurological manifestations associated with ZIKV infection, including studies on epidemiology, clinical manifestations, risk factors, severity, and prognostic indicators. The PubMed search strategy is provided in the Supplementary Materials. Studies were excluded if they did not report neurological manifestations linked to (laboratory-confirmed) ZIKV infection, were published in languages other than English, or consisted of non-scientific literature (e.g., editorials or opinion pieces).

Subsequent searches aimed to identify studies to assess the impact of these neurological complications on QoL. The search strategy used to assess the impact of GBS is presented in the Supplementary Materials, and similar search strategies were applied to other ZIKV-associated neurological complications. Studies were excluded if they did not report QoL-related outcomes, were published in languages other than English, or consisted of non-scientific literature.

No formal systematic review methodology was applied. Study selection was guided by the relevance of available evidence to the objectives of this review, with particular emphasis on recent and frequently cited studies. Titles and abstracts were screened independently by two reviewers, followed by full-text assessment of potentially relevant studies. Disagreements regarding study inclusion were resolved through discussion and reviewer consensus.

Congenital disorders

Congenital Zika syndrome

CZS is the most widely recognized neurological complication of ZIKV infection, encompassing several birth defects and developmental abnormalities in infants born to mothers who contracted ZIKV during pregnancy. This study follows the definitions of CZS and birth defects used in the source studies, though these definitions may vary. Vertical transmission, occurring in 20–70% of maternal infections, can occur at any stage of pregnancy, regardless of whether the mother is symptomatic or not [28, 29, 33, 99].

The estimated incidence of CZS among children born to ZIKV-infected mothers is 5%. For instance, a study of 6799 ZIKV-infected pregnancies in the United States found a 4.6% prevalence of Zika-related birth defects [36]. Similarly, a prospective cohort study by Hoen et al. reported neurological or ocular birth defects in 7.0% of cases, with microcephaly occurring in 5.8% [31]. However, some studies have reported even higher rates. For instance, Rosado et al. identified an 11.4% risk of CZS in Brazil, while Freitas et al. reported an 8% incidence in Rio de Janeiro specifically [30, 34]. Notably, the highest risk for birth defects was linked to ZIKV infections during the first trimester [31, 100]. Multiple studies have also noted a higher prevalence of CZS in areas with poorer socioeconomic conditions [34, 101].

Extensive evidence details the wide range of symptoms and challenges faced by children with CZS. Besides microcephaly, motor impairment is a significant concern, as evidenced by Melo et al., who reported that 81% of patients suffer from severe motor difficulties, which is further illustrated by the high prevalence of hip dysplasia among affected individuals [42, 48]. Sleep disturbances affect 27.4% of patients, and language deficits are also common [43, 50]. Oral manifestations, such as the absence of lip sealing and malocclusion, are present in all children with CZS-related microcephaly, with 79.1% experiencing dysphagia [39, 40, 44]. Eye abnormalities, particularly in children born with microcephaly, are frequently observed [45]. Furthermore, 66.7% of the children exhibit urological sequelae and bladder-bowel dysfunction [46]. Epilepsy is another major concern, with a cumulative incidence of 71.4% within the first 24 months, and seizures constitute a significant complication in infants during the first 4 months of life [22, 23]. Even 8 years after the ZIKV epidemic, children with CZS continue to experience severe functional limitations affecting mobility, vision, and feeding abilities, underscoring the long-term impact of this condition [41].

Neurodevelopmental outcomes in children prenatally exposed to ZIKV without postnatal symptoms of CZS

While CZS has been the most recognized outcome of prenatal ZIKV exposure, emerging research suggests that the virus can also cause long-term and delayed neurological effects in children, even those born without obvious signs of CZS. Martinez et al. found that children exposed to ZIKV in utero had a higher prevalence of visual impairment, abnormal visual function, and contrast sensitivity compared to non-exposed children, though no significant differences in visual acuity were observed [102]. Another study reported that 54.5% of children exposed to ZIKV in utero had eye anomalies, compared to 18.2% of unexposed children, suggesting a potential risk for visual impairment [30]. Interestingly, Valdes et al. reported no differences in visual reception during the first year of life between ZIKV-exposed infants and their non-exposed peers, implying that the ophthalmologic impact of ZIKV exposure may emerge later in development [103].

Evidence regarding the neurodevelopment of ZIKV-exposed children without CZS is mixed. Max et al. found no significant differences in early learning composite scores at 36 and 48 months between ZIKV-exposed and unexposed children in Nicaragua [104]. Similarly, da Silva et al. found that children without microcephaly who were prenatally exposed to ZIKV are not at considerably increased risk of neurodevelopmental impairment in the first 42 months of life [105]. In Colombia, however, differences in neurodevelopmental outcomes between prenatally exposed children without CZS and their unexposed controls were identified [106]. While motor development appeared comparable to that of their peers, ZIKV-exposed children demonstrated lower adaptive mobility skills and lower emotional regulation function.

Building on this, a cross-sectional study in Puerto Rico found significantly lower receptive language scores in the group exposed to ZIKV in utero compared to the unexposed group, although no significant differences were found in total cognitive scores between groups [103]. A case–control study in the United States found that while most prenatally ZIKV-exposed children generally show favorable developmental progress at 4–5 years of age, there were some differences in motor skills, mood and behavior, executive function, and school readiness [107]. A meta-analysis underscored the possible adverse effects of ZIKV exposure on neurodevelopment. Language delay was particularly common, occurring in 29.7% of children in the pooled analysis, with 8.4% of these children having moderate to severe delays. Motor and cognitive delays were also noted, but less commonly, with 6.5% and 11.5%, respectively [108].

While many children exposed to ZIKV in utero who do not develop CZS exhibit typical development in several areas, subtle differences in vision, adaptive skills, emotional regulation, and specific cognitive domains, such as receptive language, suggest that ZIKV exposure may still have significant neurodevelopmental effects.

Acquired disorders

Guillain–Barré syndrome

GBS is an autoimmune disorder causing progressive bilateral weakness and a loss of deep tendon reflexes due to peripheral nerve damage, often triggered by an infection [109]. The link between ZIKV infection and GBS is supported by multiple studies documenting increased incidence rates during ZIKV outbreaks, along with consistent clinical manifestations. Early evidence of this link comes from a case–control study by Cao-Lormeau et al., conducted in 2013 and 2014 in French Polynesia. Of the 42 patients diagnosed with GBS, 97.6% had a prior ZIKV infection, compared to 35.7% in the control group [62]. Furthermore, a significant rise in GBS cases in Mexico coincided with the introduction of ZIKV, and a positive correlation was found between the two, with an average of 1.93 GBS cases occurring for every 1000 estimated ZIKV infections [51, 52]. In Martinique, a fourfold increase in GBS incidence was found during the ZIKV epidemic [53]. A meta-analysis has found GBS incidence rates ranging from 0.62 in Mexico to 9.35 per 100,000 in Martinique, while a mathematical model suggested approximately 2.0 reported GBS cases per 10,000 ZIKV infections [55, 56].

Common symptoms of ZIKV-associated GBS include hyporeflexia, paresthesia, limb weakness, reduced or absent reflexes, and facial weakness [53, 57, 58]. Two studies reported that neurological symptoms typically begin 5–8 days after the onset of infectious symptoms [53, 57]. Interestingly, evidence suggests a more severe clinical profile for ZIKV-associated GBS compared to other GBS cases [60]. One study found more frequent cranial nerve impairment, sensory deficits, and autonomic nerve dysfunction in ZIKV-associated GBS compared to Campylobacter jejuni-associated GBS [59]. In addition, another study showed that patients with ZIKV-related GBS had worse olfactory function compared to those with non-ZIKV GBS, with this difference persisting up to 2 years after the acute phase [61].

Encephalitis, meningoencephalitis, and encephalopathy

ZIKV infection has been associated with several brain-related conditions, including encephalitis, meningoencephalitis, and encephalopathy. Encephalitis refers to the inflammation of the brain tissue, while meningoencephalitis refers to the simultaneous inflammation of both the brain (encephalitis) and its protective membranes, the meninges [110]. Encephalopathy broadly describes any disorder of the brain that alters its structure or function. Although cases of ZIKV-associated encephalitis appear sporadic, they have been observed in both children and adults [71–73, 76]. One particularly severe case involved a patient who initially showed mild symptoms such as rash and joint pain but rapidly developed severe neurological symptoms leading to death [74]. ZIKV-induced meningoencephalitis has also been observed in previously healthy adults. Among those hospitalized, symptoms such as hemiplegia, acute confusion, and severe headaches were consistently observed [77–79]. In addition, a case of ZIKV-related encephalopathy was noted, characterized by confusion and seizures [80]. Notably, all cases of encephalitis, meningoencephalitis, and encephalopathy identified in this review appeared to arise during the acute phase of infection, typically developing within a few days or weeks of the initial ZIKV infection. While existing evidence suggests a link between ZIKV and these conditions, further research is needed to establish a causal relationship.

Myelitis and related disorders

Myelitis, an inflammation of the spinal cord, can disrupt normal communication between the brain and the body and has been associated with ZIKV infection, along with related conditions, such as acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM) and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD). A systematic literature review examined cases of acute transverse myelitis following ZIKV infection, identifying nine articles that included 20 subjects [86]. The study identified several case reports, including: a 15-year-old girl who developed acute myelitis with lesions in her cervical and thoracic spinal cord, a 23-year-old man who experienced longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis following a ZIKV infection, and a 38-year-old woman who experienced paraparesis progressing to flaccid paralysis [83–85]. ADEM has been linked to ZIKV by a study by Ferreira et al., in which two patients out of 151 cases with neurological symptoms linked to ZIKV were diagnosed with ADEM. Additional case reports support this connection, including a Multiple Sclerosis patient with an ADEM-like episode, a woman with tetraplegia and ADEM post-ZIKV infection, and a man with movement disorders following ADEM linked to ZIKV infection [87–89].

Although rare, both NMOSD and optic neuritis have been reported in association with ZIKV infection. One case involved a previously healthy 35-year-old Brazilian man who was hospitalized with urinary retention, proximal paraparesis, and paresthesia in his lower limbs and face, with MRI findings consistent with NMOSD [91]. In addition, bilateral optic neuritis was observed in two cases—a 49-year-old woman and a 4-year-old boy—who both developed symptoms 2–3 weeks after a febrile illness suspected to be ZIKV [92].

Even though evidence is scarce, these case reports on myelitis suggest a potential link between ZIKV and neurological manifestations. Myelitis appears most closely linked to the acute phase of ZIKV infection, as all cases identified in this review occurred within 3 weeks of the initial infection.

Cerebrovascular complications

Cerebrovascular complications, such as stroke, cerebral aneurysm, and vasculitis, affect the brain’s blood vessels, potentially leading to brain damage or dysfunction. A prospective observational study by Brito Ferreira et al. found that 5% of 41 individuals infected with ZIKV experienced a stroke or transient ischemic attack within a median of 119 days after infection, a higher rate than in the general population [93]. Supporting this, case reports include a 10-month-old boy who suffered a stroke during acute ZIKV infection and an infant with intrauterine ZIKV exposure who had chronic ischemic cerebral infarction at 16 days [94, 95]. While the exact mechanisms remain unclear, these findings suggest an increased risk of cerebrovascular complications in ZIKV-infected individuals, although the available evidence remains limited.

Sensory polyneuropathy

Sensory polyneuropathy is a neurological disorder characterized by damage or dysfunction of multiple peripheral nerves responsible for sensory functions, resulting in symptoms ranging from mild discomfort to severe pain and loss of sensation [111]. The first reported case in 2016 described a patient with acute ZIKV infection who experienced reversible sensory polyneuropathy, characterized by sensory disturbances and nerve conduction abnormalities [97]. Further evidence for this association is provided by a prospective study, which reported a case of chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, and a case series, where four out of five patients with reversible sensory polyneuropathy had a recent infection with either ZIKV or the chikungunya virus [81, 96]. While a link between ZIKV and sensory polyneuropathy is suggested, the limited number of cases makes it difficult to draw definitive conclusions.

Sensory neuronopathy

Sensory neuronopathy is a rare neurological disorder that involves damage or dysfunction of the sensory neurons located in the dorsal root ganglia, affecting sensory transmission from the peripheral nerves to the CNS. This results in unique patterns of sensory loss and symptoms that can be widespread and asymmetric [112]. Martinez et al. reported an illustrative case which underscores the potential link between ZIKV infection and neuronopathy in a 42-year-old Brazilian patient who displayed key characteristics of sensory neuronopathy 3 weeks after ZIKV symptoms [98]. To date, this is the only reported case of ZIKV-related sensory neuronopathy.

Impact of Zika-related neurological conditions on quality of life

While the range of ZIKV-related neurological complications is documented, a comprehensive understanding of their impact on QoL is limited, leading to an underestimation of the burden Zika imposes on patients, caregivers, and society. As Zika continues to pose public health challenges, exploring the complex impact of its neurological manifestations is crucial for enhancing patient care and improving long-term outcomes. The Supplementary Materials summarize the characteristics of the included studies, including evidence domains, QoL instruments, and study populations.

Congenital Zika syndrome

Children with CZS often experience severe and debilitating conditions, including feeding and walking problems, which result in a high level of dependency on caregivers. Numerous studies have examined the impact of CZS on QoL for affected children and their caregivers, while others have assessed its broader burden using Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs).

In Brazil, the DALY burden of CZS was estimated at 30,027 DALYs between 2015 and 2020 [101]. Moreover, the global burden of ZIKV from 2010 to 2019 was 44,000 DALYs on average per year, with CZS representing the most significant portion of Zika’s overall burden [113]. In addition, a study estimated that microcephaly associated with Zika in Colombia caused a loss of 10,609 DALYs during the 2015–2016 epidemic period [114].

In the context of health-related quality of life (HRQoL), Miranda et al. reviewed studies focused on microcephaly and identified only one study specifically focused on the condition [115]. This study utilized the Health Utilities Index 3, with primary caregivers acting as proxy respondents. The adjusted marginal disutilities for microcephaly, compared to optimal health, ranged from − 0.820 to − 0.745, indicating significantly reduced HRQoL in affected children. Following the emergence of CZS in Brazil, its link to cerebral palsy was recognized. A cross-sectional study in Brazil assessed QoL in 193 children with cerebral palsy resulting from CZS. Caregivers completed the Caregiver Priorities and Child Health Index of Life with Disabilities (CPCHILD) questionnaire, which revealed that activities related to positioning, transferring and mobility had the greatest impact on QoL [116].

Rego Filho et al. emphasized the challenges faced by caregivers of children with CZS in Brazil [117]. The study revealed that 71.7% of caregivers reported a mild burden, with 26.7% experiencing moderate levels. Caring for a child with a neurological disorder often increases marital and financial stress, leading to depressive symptoms, psychiatric disorders, anxiety, and musculoskeletal pain in mothers. Araújo et al. evaluated the QoL of mothers of children with CZS-induced microcephaly in Brazil and found a high prevalence of musculoskeletal pain, with moderate to high intensity [118]. Mothers with pain had significantly lower QoL scores across multiple domains, such as functional capacity, physical aspects, and pain.

The long-term effects of CZS on maternal well-being were further assessed during a 5-year follow-up study assessing the QoL of mothers of children with CZS [119]. The results showed consistently low QoL scores over time, with no significant changes in depressive symptoms or mental health throughout the study period. These ongoing mental health challenges were likely tied to caregiving demands, social isolation, occupational changes, and the stigmatization associated with the condition.

Most individuals with CZS following the 2015–2016 outbreak are still young, so QoL studies focus on their caregivers. Findings show a profound and lasting impact of CZS on both affected children and caregivers, and the urgent need for targeted support.

Guillain–Barré syndrome

GBS is a disorder that affects the peripheral nerves, leading to sensory disturbances, muscle weakness, and, in severe cases, paralysis. These symptoms can have profound and lasting consequences for individuals. Research has shown the substantial health burden posed by GBS and underscored its far-reaching implications for public health. A study conducted in Brazil has highlighted a significant increase in GBS incidence, mortality, and DALYs from 2014 to 2016, correlating with the ZIKV epidemic [120]. Persistent disability and depression have been identified as long-term outcomes for ZIKV-associated GBS [58]. A general review of HRQoL in GBS showed mixed results across various timeframes post-disease onset. Initial QoL and physical health were poor, with noticeable improvement within the first year. Longitudinal studies indicated improvements in mental health and QoL scores during the first year, while subsequent studies showed little further improvement beyond 2 years. GBS patients consistently reported lower QoL across multiple assessment tools, with common impairments in physical function, daily activities, and psychosocial domains [115]. Factors predicting poorer long-term HRQoL in GBS patients include older age, delayed hospital admission, cranial nerve involvement, and treatment factors [121]. Since pain is a serious manifestation of GBS, affecting both acute and chronic stages, Papri et al. studied its prevalence, characteristics, and impact on QoL in 644 GBS patients in Bangladesh. Pain was reported by 71%, with higher pain intensity in women, older patients, and those with severe forms of GBS. Acute pain significantly affected self-care, usual activities, and anxiety/depression, while chronic pain impacted anxiety/depression, highlighting its negative effect on QoL [122]. Overall, QoL improves significantly in the first year after GBS onset, but long-term outcomes vary, requiring further research to address knowledge gaps.

Myelitis and related disorders

ZIKV has been linked to myelitis and related disorders, including ADEM, and, in rare cases, optic neuritis and NMOSD. Numerous studies have highlighted the complex, negative effects of NMOSD on QoL, with fatigue, pain, and depressive symptoms being common among patients [123]. In addition, pediatric-onset NMOSD can result in disability and poor academic performance over time, particularly impacting emotional functioning [124]. NMOSD patients also experience a significant reduction in vision-related QoL, mostly affecting health perception, general vision, driving ability, and role difficulties. Overall, their QoL is markedly lower than that of healthy controls [125].

Compared to NMOSD, the impact of myelitis and other myelitis-related disorders associated with ZIKV infection has been less studied. Nevertheless, a cross-sectional study by Toussaint–Duyster found that post-ADEM children reported significantly higher fatigue levels compared to healthy peers and scored lower across various Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory scales, except for emotional functioning, with a notable correlation between fatigue and reduced HRQoL [126]. Due to limited evidence, further research is needed on the impact of ZIKV-associated myelitis-related disorders.

Encephalitis, meningoencephalitis, and encephalopathy

ZIKV infection has been linked to cases of encephalitis, and, in some instances, meningoencephalitis and encephalopathy. Research shows that these conditions impact both physical health and social well-being. For instance, Ramanuj et al. found that encephalitis survivors consistently scored lower in all HRQoL domains compared to the general population, with those aged 15 and older with infectious encephalitis reporting the lowest scores [127]. Another study also highlighted that encephalitis patients experienced significantly lower short-term HRQoL and reduced workability, along with neurocognitive deficits in the long term [128]. Yokota et al. specifically examined autoimmune encephalitis, which may in some cases be triggered by ZIKV, noting that while most patients had favorable neurological outcomes, nearly half faced lasting sequelae that negatively impacted their social QoL [72, 129]. Although no specific studies were found on encephalopathy's impact on QoL, research revealed that caregivers of children with developmental and epileptic encephalopathy experienced significant variability in their social care-related QoL, often struggling with self-care and participation in enjoyable activities [130]. These findings highlight the broad and persistent impacts of encephalitis and related conditions.

Cerebrovascular complications

Cerebrovascular complications, such as stroke, transient ischemic attacks, and chronic ischemic cerebral infarction, though weakly associated with ZIKV infection, have a profound impact on QoL. Ramos-Lima et al. studied 131 ischemic stroke patients and found that over half had moderate to severe disability, with lower QoL strongly associated with worse functional status and greater clinical severity [131]. Similarly, a review of studies on young stroke survivors highlights that initial functional outcomes and psychological factors, such as fatigue and depression, heavily influence QoL [132]. Importantly, the burden extends beyond the patients. Tiwari et al. showed that stroke significantly affects the QoL of caregivers, who often face stress, financial concerns, and lifestyle adjustments [133], underscoring the broad, multifaceted impact of cerebrovascular complications on both patients and caregivers.

Discussion

This study highlights that the burden of ZIKV infection extends beyond its typically mild acute presentation and includes a spectrum of neurological complications associated with substantial long-term impairment in daily functioning and QoL for both patients and caregivers.

CZS remains the most well-documented and severe consequence of maternal ZIKV infection, underscoring the substantial risk the virus poses to the developing fetal nervous system. Children affected by CZS experience a wide array of neurodevelopmental impairments, encompassing microcephaly, cognitive and motor deficits, seizures, and sensory impairments, all of which place a significant burden on both the patients and their caregivers [22, 23, 41, 42, 46, 48]. A recent meta-analysis of individual patient data from 18 studies, comprising 9568 pregnant individuals and 9608 newborns, reported that severe primary microcephaly was more common in ZIKV-positive pregnancies (1.5%) than in ZIKV-negative ones (0.3%), with a relative risk of 4.5 [134]. More broadly, prenatal ZIKV exposure has been associated with heterogeneous developmental outcomes, ranging from typical development to significant impairments in visual, neurodevelopmental, and language domains [30, 102–108]. This heterogeneity emphasizes the critical need for comprehensive, long-term monitoring of all children exposed to ZIKV, irrespective of their initial presentation.

In adults, ZIKV has been clearly linked to GBS, with outbreaks consistently correlating with increases in GBS cases [51–53, 62]. In the short term, patients may experience a rapid onset of symptoms (such as weakness and sensory disturbances), leading to hospitalization and significant healthcare utilization [53, 57, 58]. The long-term impacts of ZIKV-associated GBS, including olfactory dysfunction and persistent neurological deficits, point to the complexity of its clinical course and the need for better management strategies [61].

Evidence linking ZIKV to rarer neurological conditions—including encephalitis, meningoencephalitis, encephalopathy, myelitis, ADEM, NMOSD, optic neuritis, cerebrovascular complications, and peripheral neuropathies—remains limited. Yet, some carry the potential for severe or fatal outcomes [74, 83–85]. These findings suggest that the neurological effects of ZIKV may extend beyond the established associations with CZS and GBS, highlighting the need for continued clinical awareness and further research into the broader neurological spectrum associated with ZIKV infection.

While previous reviews [7, 8] have similarly outlined a range of neurological conditions associated with ZIKV, a distinguishing feature of this study is its assessment of their impact on QoL. The study shows that GBS greatly affects patients' QoL. While many patients experience partial normalization of HRQoL within the first year, a substantial proportion continue to face long-term disabilities, underscoring the need for improved care and further research [58, 115, 122]. While several studies have examined acute transverse myelitis following ZIKV infection, no studies were identified on its impact on QoL. Furthermore, although conditions such NMSOD, ADEM, encephalitis, and cerebrovascular complications are known to impair QoL, existing data primarily come from non-ZIKV contexts [123–125, 127, 128, 131, 132].

Neurological complications due to ZIKV place a significant emotional and physical burden on caregivers, including persistent physical, emotional, and social challenges. This impact is particularly evident among mothers of children with CZS, who report significantly higher levels of anxiety, depression, and overall reduced QoL when compared to those with healthy children [117–119]. Caregivers often face the burden of psychological distress and financial strain, as well as other potential difficulties, further complicating their ability to provide adequate care. Future research should focus on addressing existing knowledge gaps, particularly regarding long-term outcomes, coping mechanisms, caregiver well-being, and access to support services.

This study has several limitations. First, the scarcity of high-quality evidence for certain conditions necessitated reliance on a small number of case reports. Although these provide valuable insights, we acknowledge that their narrow scope limits our ability to confirm definitive causal relationships between ZIKV and some of the neurological complications reviewed, underscoring the need for more comprehensive and rigorous research to clarify these associations. Second, there is a significant gap in long-term studies assessing the neurological effects of ZIKV infection. Given that the consequences may evolve over time, it is crucial to understand the long-term implications, including potential neurodegenerative risks and persistent symptoms (such as sensory deficits and cognitive decline), to develop effective interventions and improve patient outcomes. Third, while there is ample evidence to underscore the significant impact on patients' and caregivers' QoL, the data are very heterogeneous and largely confined to the more common complications, such as CZS and GBS. Further complicating synthesis, a wide range of approaches used to measure QoL was identified—including DALYs, HRQoL, and other QoL measures using specific scales—limiting the direct comparison across studies. Improved standardization of research methods and an expansion of scope to include less common neurological complications are necessary to provide a comprehensive overview of the full extent of the impact of ZIKV-related neurological complications.

In conclusion, the findings of this review suggest that the burden of ZIKV infection is not adequately captured by its acute clinical manifestations alone. ZIKV infection may lead to a spectrum of neurological complications associated with persistent disability, reduced QoL, and substantial caregiver burden. Collectively, the findings indicate that ZIKV-related neurological disease converges in its downstream effects on functional independence, psychological well-being, and long-term social and healthcare needs—outcomes that, for many complications, remain insufficiently characterized.

Model projections suggested that by 2050, up to 1.3 billion more people may be exposed to environments conducive to ZIKV transmission [135]. Therefore, future research should focus on understanding the long-term neurological impacts of ZIKV infection and assessing how neurological complications affect QoL, especially considering the potentially large number of affected individuals in the future. Advancing this agenda will require improved, methodologically coherent approaches to measuring the human impact of neurological complications linked with the ZIKV infection. As this review demonstrates, QoL evidence across these conditions is fragmented, heterogeneous in terms of measurement, and difficult to compare across studies and populations. To accurately quantify the human impact of ZIKV-related neurological complications, large scale research is first needed to definitively determine the link between ZIKV infection and these complications and to estimate their prevalence. In parallel, a more standardized approach to measuring QoL outcomes is required to enable consistent comparisons across complications and studies. Combining this with accurate estimates of the prevalence of neurological complications would give a comprehensive understanding of the QoL burden associated with ZIKV infection. Gender-related impacts also warrant more consideration, as women often experience higher levels of exposure and vulnerability due to gender norms, such as their traditional roles in collecting and storing water in some low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), which increase contact with mosquito breeding sites [136, 137]. ZIKV can also be transmitted from mother to fetus, leading to severe, lifelong health consequences and placing a disproportionate caregiving burden on women, who already perform about 75% of unpaid care work worldwide [138]. In addition, the risk of vertical transmission imposes an additional burden on women to prevent pregnancy when at risk of infection, a responsibility further complicated in environments characterized by limited availability of healthcare and family planning resources [139]. Greater emphasis should be placed on effective management strategies to support individuals experiencing these complications.

Mitigating the impact of ZIKV on the QoL of infected individuals requires comprehensive implementation of the “WHO Zika Strategic Response Plan”, which underscores the importance of detection, prevention, care and support, research, and coordination. Preventative measures include targeting all life stages of the Aedes mosquito (eggs, larvae, and adults), reducing the risk of transmission through sexual and other potential routes, strengthening collaboration with local governments, promoting community engagement in mosquito control practices, and implementing education and behavior change campaigns [140]. In addition, continued research and innovation in vaccine development remain important for improving public health responses to ZIKV infection.
