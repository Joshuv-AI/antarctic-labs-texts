Comparative Genomic Analysis Identifies FleQ and GcbB as Virulence‐Associated Factors in Pseudomonas syringae pv. tabaci Strains
Authors: Muhammad Taufiq Hidayat, Kei Yoshioka, Takafumi Nishimura, Shuta Asai, Sachiko Masuda, Ken Shirasu, Nanami Sakata, Mikihiro Yamamoto, Yoshiteru Noutoshi, Kazuhiro Toyoda, Yuki Ichinose, Hidenori Matsui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Pseudomonas syringae pv. tabaci (Pta) is an important plant pathogen, which causes wildfire disease in Nicotiana species. However, the genetic basis underlying strain‐level differences in virulence remains largely unresolved. To address this, we performed a comparative genomic analysis between a highly virulent strain Pta6605 and a less virulent strain Pta7375. Despite high overall genome similarity, we identified key single‐nucleotide polymorphisms, including premature stop‐codon mutations in seven open reading frames in Pta7375. Notably, point mutations in two regulatory genes, such as fleQ, which encodes a transcription factor essential for flagellar biogenesis and biofilm formation, and gcbB, which encodes a GGDEF domain‐containing diguanylate cyclase responsible for cyclic dimeric guanosine monophosphate (c‐di‐GMP) synthesis, were implicated in virulence disparity. Functional analyses using deletion and locus replacement mutants in the Pta6605 background revealed that the disruption of fleQ markedly reduced motility, flagellin production, c‐di‐GMP accumulation, biofilm formation and virulence level mirroring the Pta7375 phenotype. The gcbB replacement mutant showed reduced disease symptom development, although c‐di‐GMP levels remained comparable to the Pta6605 wild type. Locus replacement between strains confirmed that a point mutation in fleQ was the primary driver of reduced motility and flagellin expression in Pta7375. These findings indicate that the reduced virulence of Pta7375 is associated with impaired regulation of flagella‐related genes and disruption of the FleQ‐mediated c‐di‐GMP signalling, underscoring the value of comparative genomics in disentangling the complex regulatory networks that govern virulence in plant pathogens.Keywords: comparative genomics, cyclic‐di‐GMP, fleQ, gcbB, Pseudomonas syringae

Introduction

Pseudomonas syringae , a globally significant plant pathogen, comprises over 50 pathovars with distinct host specificities and collectively infects nearly all economically important crops (Xin et al. 2018). Phylogenetically classified into 13 phylogroups, this species exhibits remarkable genetic diversity, reflecting its adaptations to various hosts and environmental niches (Xin et al. 2018). Despite extensive studies on its molecular pathogenicity, the genetic basis of strain‐level differences in virulence, particularly in host colonisation and disease progression, remains poorly understood (Helmann et al. 2019; Lindeberg et al. 2008). Identifying the specific genes driving these variations is challenging because of structural genomic plasticity among strains.

Pseudomonas syringae initiates infection by entering host tissues through stomata or wounds, relying on flagellar motility (O'Malley and Anderson 2021; Xin et al. 2018). Once established, it deploys virulence factors, such as type III effectors, for suppressing plant immunity, along with phytotoxins, exopolysaccharides and biofilm matrix for evading antimicrobial defence (Budil and Lišková 2024; Xin et al. 2018). The bacterial transition from a motile to a sessile lifestyle is tightly regulated by cyclic dimeric guanosine monophosphate (c‐di‐GMP), a bacterial second messenger that modulates biofilm formation, motility and virulence (Wang et al. 2023). FleQ, which has a Rec domain, an AAA+/ATPase σ54 interaction domain, and a DNA‐binding helix‐turn‐helix (HTH) motif, is a transcription factor that plays a central role in controlling bacterial lifestyle (Su et al. 2015). FleQ orchestrates the shift between motility and sessility by inversely regulating the flagellar (planktonic growth) and biofilm‐associated (sessile growth) genes (Oladosu et al. 2024). In Pseudomonas fluorescens and Pseudomonas putida , FleQ governs adhesion proteins, such as LapA and MapA, balancing surface attachment and dispersal (Blanco‐Romero et al. 2018; Pastora and O'Toole 2023). Critically, FleQ interacts with c‐di‐GMP, which fine‐tunes its transcriptional activity; elevated c‐di‐GMP levels repress motility genes while activating biofilm formation (Xiao et al. 2021). However, the functional interplay between FleQ and c‐di‐GMP in plant pathogens remains underexplored, particularly given the redundancy of the GGDEF/EAL‐domain proteins that regulate c‐di‐GMP levels (Nie et al. 2020).

Pseudomonas amygdali pv. tabaci (formerly P. syringae pv. tabaci [Pta]) is a gram‐negative bacterium that causes wildfire disease in tobacco (Kashihara et al. 2022). The genetic basis underlying strain‐level differences in virulence of Pta remains largely unresolved. The dynamic plasticity of plant‐pathogenic bacterial genomes, coupled with extensive mutational variation, often makes the identification of causative genes by comparative genomics challenging. However, we believe that identifying genes that determine the differences in virulence is important for elucidating the control mechanisms of the virulence of plant‐pathogenic bacteria.

In this study, we performed comparative genomics for identifying genes that play important roles in virulence by comparing the genomes of a highly virulent strain, Pta6605, and a less virulent strain, Pta7375. Given the overall similarity in their genome architectures, our investigation centered on analysing single‐nucleotide polymorphisms that may underlie differences in virulence. The primary objective was to assess the roles of two candidate genes, fleQ (involved in c‐di‐GMP signalling) and gcbB (which encodes a protein with a GGDEF domain), in modulating virulence. To achieve this, we conducted functional analyses using deletion and locus replacement mutants to determine fleQ and gcbB contribute to virulence in Pta6605.

Results

Comparative Genomic Analysis Between Pta6605 and Pta7375

To elucidate the causes of the differences in virulence between strains, we investigated the genetic difference in virulence between the highly virulent Pta6605 strain and the less virulent Pta7375 strain. The virulence factors of Pta6605 have been studied, but no reports have been made on Pta7375 due to its low virulence. Pta6605 caused severe symptoms in the host Nicotiana tabacum 'Xanthi' as observed in the flood assay (Figure 1a). Pta6605 demonstrated high motility in both the swimming and swarming assays (Figure 1b,c). In contrast, Pta7375 exhibited reduced virulence in Xanthi in the flood assay (Figure 1a) and displayed significantly low motility (Figure 1b,c). Given the established direct relationship between flagellar function and virulence in Pta6605 (Ichinose et al. 2003), we examined the accumulation of flagellar proteins by western blotting. The results revealed a significant reduction in flagellin levels in Pta7375 compared to that in Pta6605 (Figure 1d). Furthermore, biofilm production was markedly low in Pta7375 (Figure 1e). Comparison of in vitro growth between Pta6605 and Pta7375 strains showed no difference in initial growth, but a clear difference in optical density (OD) was observed at 24 h. By 36 h, both strains reached similar OD values (Figure S1).

To elucidate the genetic determinants underlying the divergent phenotype of Pta6605 and Pta7375, we performed a comparative genomic analysis. Their genomes exhibited 99% overall nucleotide identity and conserved syntenic organisation (Figure 1f). We identified three strain‐specific large insertion events. In addition, we catalogued approximately 350 polymorphic sites within the shared genome (Table 1), comprising 43 nonsynonymous single‐nucleotide variants (SNVs), 39 small insertions/deletions (< 20 bp) and 15 premature stop codons predicted to truncate open reading frames (ORFs). Collectively, these structural and sequence‐level variations were likely to underlie the observed differences in virulence between the two strains. Detailed analysis revealed there are frameshift mutations leading to premature stop codons in seven ORFs of Pta7375 (Figure 1g). These ORFs encode an alpha/beta‐hydroxylase (Pta6605_RS01805), an autotransporter (Pta6605_RS01830), two hypothetical proteins (Pta6605_RS01865 and Pta6605_RS12405), a dehydrogenase (Pta6605_RS07205), a sigma‐54‐dependent Fis family transcription factor (fleQ; Pta6605_RS09960) and a protein containing a GGDEF domain (gcbB, Pta6605_RS17490). Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the GGDEF domain‐containing protein was a homologue of Pseudomonas fluorescens Pf0‐1 GcbB (Newell et al. 2011) (Figure S2).

We focused on two candidate genes, fleQ and gcbB, that are potentially responsible for the reduced virulence of Pta7375. This is because both fleQ and gcbB are considered to contribute to c‐di‐GMP signalling, which functions as a second messenger. fleQ is involved in flagella formation and biofilm production in other Pseudomonas species (Oladosu et al. 2024; Su et al. 2015). In Pta7375, a single‐base insertion mutation in fleQ resulted in a frameshift and a premature stop codon (Figure S3a), disrupting the essential DNA‐binding HTH domain. Similarly, a single‐base substitution in the Pta6605_RS17490 resulted in a stop codon and loss of the GGDEF domain, which is critical for c‐di‐GMP synthesis (Figure S3b). Based on these findings, we generated mutants targeting fleQ and gcbB to investigate whether these genes contribute to the virulence of Pta6605 and to evaluate their roles in bacterial virulence.

Phenotypic Characterisation of fleQ and gcbB Deletion Mutants in Pta6605

To determine whether fleQ and gcbB were responsible for the observed phenotype (motility, biofilm and virulence) in Pta7375, we generated ΔfleQ, ΔgcbB and ΔfleQΔgcbB double mutants in the Pta6605 background. Construction of the ΔfleQ mutant and its phenotypic characterisation, including fleQ and gcbB gene expression and flagellin protein accumulation, is shown in Figure S4. When fleQ and gcbB gene expression was examined in ΔfleQ and ΔgcbB mutants, it was revealed that the deletion of fleQ did not affect gcbB gene expression, and conversely, the deletion of gcbB did not affect fleQ gene expression, indicating that these genes do not regulate each other (Figure S4e,f). The Pta6605 ΔfleQ and Pta6605 ΔfleQΔgcbB mutants exhibited significantly reduced swimming and swarming motility, mirroring the phenotype of Pta7375 wild‐type (WT) (Figure 2a–c). In contrast, the Pta6605 ΔgcbB mutant exhibited little to no effect on motility. Western blot analysis showed a clear flagellin band in the Pta6605 WT but no detectable signal in the ΔfliC mutant (Figure 2d and Figure S4d). The ΔfleQ mutant similarly lacked any accumulation of ΔfliC protein (Figure 2d). In fact, the gene expression of flagella‐related genes including fliC was decreased in the ΔfleQ mutant as compared with Pta6605 WT (Figure S5c).

The Pta6605 ΔgcbB mutant did not affect flagellar protein levels, and the Pta6605 ΔfleQΔgcbB mutant showed a significant reduction, similar to that of Pta7375 WT (Figure 2d). To verify the presence or absence of flagella, microscopic observations were conducted. Consistent with the western blot analysis results, no flagella were observed in ΔfliC, ΔfleQ, ΔfleQΔgcbB mutants and Pta7375 WT, while flagella were confirmed in Pta6605 WT and the Pta6605 ΔgcbB mutant (Figure S6). Additionally, biofilm production was markedly decreased by the Pta6605 ΔfleQ and ΔfleQΔgcbB mutants (Figure S7). Pta7375 WT had significantly lower c‐di‐GMP levels than that of Pta6605 WT (Figure 2g). The Pta6605 ΔfleQ mutant also exhibited lower c‐di‐GMP levels as compared with Pta6605 WT. No significant reduction in c‐di‐GMP accumulation was observed in the Pta6605 ΔgcbB (Figure 2g). Although GcbB is presumed to function as a diguanylate cyclase (DGC), gcbB deletion did not affect c‐di‐GMP levels under this condition. Notably, the Pta6605 ΔfleQΔgcbB mutant displayed a significant decrease in c‐di‐GMP levels, resembling that of Pta7375 WT.

We conducted an inoculation test on N. tabacum 'Xanthi', using a flood assay for assessing virulence (Figure 2f). Disease symptoms were observed in plants inoculated with Pta6605 WT but not with the ΔfleQ mutant or Pta7375 WT (Figure 2f). The ΔgcbB mutant exhibited a slight reduction in virulence compared to that of Pta6605 WT (Figure 2f,h). When virulence was confirmed by performing an infiltration assay, severe disease symptoms were observed in Pta6605 WT and the ΔgcbB mutant, but disease symptoms were suppressed in the ΔfleQ and the ΔfleQΔgcbB mutant (Figure S8). Both ΔfleQ and ΔfleQΔgcbB mutants showed a significant decrease in disease symptoms, similar to those observed in the Pta7375 WT. These results indicate that FleQ is essential for the virulence of Pta6605, and that GcbB also contributes to virulence.

Gene Swapping of fleQ Confirms Its Regulatory Role in Flagellin Production

To compare the effects of point mutations in fleQ and gcbB between Pta7375 and Pta6605, we exchanged the fleQ and gcbB loci between these strains (Figure S9). To assess whether gene replacement affected expression levels, we examined fleQ and gcbB gene expression in the replacement strains (Figure S10). The expression of fleQ showed a tendency to decrease in Pta7375 compared to Pta6605, and a similar trend was observed for gcbB; however, these differences were not statistically significant. These results confirmed that the gene replacement procedure itself did not cause dramatic changes in fleQ and gcbB gene expression levels. The effects of the replacement were investigated by evaluating the swimming ability of each replacement strain. As expected, Pta6605 fleQ 7375 and Pta6605 gcbB 7375 fleQ 7375 exhibited markedly reduced swimming motility compared to that of Pta6605 WT (Figure 3a,b). Pta6605 gcbB 7375 retained swimming motility similar to that of Pta6605 WT. Pta7375 fleQ 6605 and Pta7375 gcbB 6605 fleQ 6605 recovered swimming motility comparable to that of Pta6605 WT. Pta7375 gcbB 6605 did not exhibit similar swimming motility to that of Pta7375 WT (Figure 3a,b). We conducted western blot analysis to confirm whether the replacement mutants changed the production of flagellar proteins. Pta6605 fleQ 7375 and Pta6605 gcbB 7375 fleQ 7375 showed significantly reduced amounts of flagellin, whereas Pta6605 WT and Pta6605 gcbB 7375 produced high amounts of flagellin (Figure 3c). Pta7375 fleQ 6605 and Pta7375 gcbB 6605 fleQ 6605 recovered the high production of flagellin, whereas Pta7375 gcbB 6605 still showed low levels of flagellin similar to that of Pta7375 (Figure 3c). These results indicate that the fleQ mutation in Pta7375 negatively affects motility and flagellar production. Additionally, we sought to identify other genes affected by deletion or single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in fleQ 7375. We scanned the Pta6605 genome for the FleQ‐binding motif (Baraquet and Harwood 2016) and found that at least two flagella‐related genes, flgA and fliL, contain FleQ‐binding motifs upstream near the start codon (Figure S5a,b). Reverse transcription‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) analysis revealed that the expression of flgA and fliL was decreased in both Pta6605 ΔfleQ and Pta6605 fleQ 7375 compared to Pta6605 WT (Figure S5d,e). This result was further supported by the observation that flgA and fliL gene expression was recovered in Pta7375 fleQ 6605 compared to Pta7375 WT. These results indicate that even SNP in fleQ 7375 can disrupt the transcription regulatory function of FleQ, leading to impaired activation of important flagella‐related genes.

The Dual Mutation of fleQ and gcbB Reduces the Virulence of Pta

Because motility and flagellin production were consistent with our hypothesis for each replacement strain, we analysed biofilm formation, c‐di‐GMP levels, and virulence. In the biofilm assay, Pta6605 fleQ 7375 and Pta6605 gcbB 7375 fleQ 7375 replacement strains showed reduced biofilm formation compared to that of Pta6605 WT; Pta6605 gcbB 7375 fleQ 7375 showed the lowest extent of biofilm formation, similar to that of Pta7375 WT (Figure S7). In contrast, Pta7375 fleQ 6605 showed increased biofilm formation at 20 and 30 h compared to Pta7375 WT. However, after 30 h incubation, Pta7375 fleQ 6605 and Pta7375 gcbB 6605 fleQ 6605 did not completely recover the biofilm accumulation to the level of Pta7375. These results suggest that mutations in fleQ play a crucial role in the reduced biofilm formation observed in Pta7375, while additional factors beyond fleQ and gcbB may also contribute to the complete biofilm formation.

Pta6605 fleQ 7375 and Pta6605 fleQ 7375 gcbB 7375 showed decreased amounts of c‐di‐GMP (Figure 4a), which was consistent with biofilm formation. In contrast, Pta7375 fleQ 6605 and Pta7375 fleQ 6605 gcbB 6605 exhibited significantly increased c‐di‐GMP levels, but Pta7375 gcbB 6605 did not (Figure 4a). These results suggest that FleQ functions as a major regulator of c‐di‐GMP levels in Pta.

Next, we tested the alterations, if any, in the virulence of the replacement strains using a flood assay. Development of disease symptoms was reduced in the Pta6605 fleQ 7375, the Pta6605 gcbB 7375 and Pta6605 gcbB 7375 fleQ 7375 strains (Figure 4b). Bacterial populations of Pta6605 fleQ 7375, Pta6605 gcbB 7375 and Pta6605 gcbB 7375 fleQ 7375 were lower than that of Pta6605 WT at 3 days post‐inoculation (dpi) (Figure 4d). In contrast, none of the Pta7375 replacement mutants showed recovery in disease symptom development at an initial concentration of 0.002 optical density value at 600 nm (OD600) (data not shown). When the inoculum concentration was increased to OD600 = 0.08, disease symptom development was observed for the replacement strains Pta7375 fleQ 6605 and Pta7375 gcbB 6605 fleQ 6605 compared to that for Pta7375 WT (Figure 4c). In the Pta7375 replacement mutants, the bacterial population at 3 dpi was higher than in Pta7375 WT. Moreover, Pta7375 gcbB 6605 fleQ 6605 exhibited a relatively high increase in the bacterial population (Figure 4e). Reintroduction of functional fleQ and gcbB into Pta7375 restored motility. However, it only partially rescued virulence, suggesting the involvement of additional genes in virulence control.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to identify the determinants of differences in virulence among plant‐pathogenic bacterial strains that would lead to a relatively deep understanding of the regulation of virulence in plant‐pathogenic bacteria. Through comparative genomic analysis between the highly virulent strain Pta6605 and the less virulent strain Pta7375, we attempted to identify the causative genes of the phenotypic differences between the Pta6605 and Pta7375 strains (virulence, motility, flagellin accumulation and biofilm formation). Comparative genomic analysis found point mutations in two crucial regulatory genes, fleQ and gcbB, in Pta7375. Deletion and replacement mutants revealed essential roles of these genes in regulating motility, biofilm formation and virulence. Our findings indicate that the genetic basis of attenuation of virulence in Pta7375 is linked to the truncation of two pivotal genes, fleQ and gcbB.

FleQ is a well‐studied transcriptional regulator, which controls the balance between motility and biofilm formation in various Pseudomonas species (Blanco‐Romero et al. 2018; Nogales et al. 2015; Oladosu et al. 2024; Xiao et al. 2021). The Pta6605 ΔfleQ mutant led to a marked reduction in both swimming and swarming motility, effectively mimicking the phenotype of the less‐virulent Pta7375 strain. This observation is consistent with previous studies on Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Pseudomonas putida , where FleQ has been identified as a key regulator of flagellar biosynthesis and biofilm development (Blanco‐Romero et al. 2018; Su et al. 2015). We found that deletion of fleQ in Pta6605 and SNP in fleQ of Pta7375 could disturb the production of flagellin protein FliC (Figures 2d, 3c, and Figure S4d). These results indicate that a SNP in the fleQ gene has an impact on the regulatory ability of FleQ. Thus, it is clear that FleQ serves as a regulator of flagellar motility in Pta6605 and Pta7375. These findings are consistent with previous findings (Blanco‐Romero et al. 2018; Matsuyama et al. 2016; Oladosu et al. 2024; Torres‐Sánchez et al. 2023). To understand the molecular basis of FleQ dysfunction in Pta7375, we identified FleQ‐binding motifs upstream of flagella‐related genes (flgA and fliL) using FIMO (Find Individual Motif Occurrences) analysis. Expression analysis confirmed that these genes, along with fliC, were downregulated in both ΔfleQ and Pta6605 fleQ 7375 mutants (Figure S5), indicating that the SNP disrupts FleQ's transcriptional regulatory function.

Domain prediction analysis suggests that the SNP in fleQ7375 introduces a premature stop codon, resulting in loss of the helix‐turn‐helix DNA‐binding domain (Figure S3a). This truncation likely prevents FleQ from activating flagella gene transcription. However, detailed functional characterisation of the truncated FleQ protein and its interaction with the c‐di‐GMP signalling pathway remain to be investigated.

We also identified GcbB as a factor contributing to virulence. GcbB, a homologue of P. fluorescens Pf0‐1, functions as a diguanylate cyclase responsible for c‐di‐GMP synthesis, which may regulate the transition between motile and sessile bacterial lifestyles (Dahlstrom et al. 2018; Newell et al. 2011). GcbB contains CHASE, PAS and GGDEF domains, which are essential for its function in c‐di‐GMP synthesis (Newell et al. 2011; Valentini and Filloux 2016). In Pta7375, a frameshift mutation in gcbB resulted in the loss of the GGDEF domain, which is essential for c‐di‐GMP synthesis (Dahlstrom and O'Toole 2017; Jiménez‐Fernández et al. 2016; Martínez‐Rodríguez et al. 2023; Whiteley and Lee 2015; Xiao et al. 2016). On the other hand, analysis using the ΔgcbB mutant and replacement strains demonstrated that gcbB contributes to disease symptom development and bacterial growth during inoculation. However, under the conditions analysed in this study, no significant effects on motility, biofilm production or c‐di‐GMP levels were observed. Indeed, because multiple proteins containing GGDEF domains exist in the Pta genome, although the gcbB identified in this study plays some role in virulence, more detailed analysis is required to elucidate its specific function. This series of analyses suggests that the fleQ mutation substantially contributes to the reduced virulence of Pta7375 by impairing motility, flagellin production, c‐di‐GMP accumulation and biofilm formation. In contrast, the gcbB mutation appears to cause only a slight reduction in virulence and does not markedly alter most of the phenotypes examined in this study.

FleQ regulates c‐di‐GMP signalling by controlling the expression of diguanylate cyclase (DGC) genes that modulate c‐di‐GMP levels. For example, in P. putida KT2440, fleQ deletion significantly reduced transcription of the highly conserved gcbA gene across Pseudomonas species (Xiao et al. 2016). In vitro experiments showed that FleQ binds to the gcbA promoter region, and this binding is inhibited by increased c‐di‐GMP (Xiao et al. 2016). Therefore, we investigated whether FleQ regulates gcbB gene expression in Pta6605. However, no change in gcbB gene expression was observed in the ΔfleQ mutant (Figure S4f), suggesting that FleQ does not directly regulate gcbB transcription in Pta. Nevertheless, c‐di‐GMP levels were reduced in the ΔfleQ mutant (Figure 2g), suggesting that FleQ may regulate other DGC genes in Pta6605, thereby influencing overall c‐di‐GMP levels.

Beyond the molecular role of FleQ, it is critical to consider why less virulent alleles (Pta7375) would be maintained in field populations. One important driver is human‐mediated selection through roguing practices: by removing plants showing conspicuous disease symptoms (infected by highly virulent strains), growers inadvertently impose negative selection on genotypes that cause prominent lesions. Over time, strains with reduced lesion formation gain a relative fitness advantage because they evade removal (Murray‐Watson and Cunniffe 2023). Although roguing has been studied extensively in the context of viral diseases (e.g., tomato yellow leaf curl virus), the same principle applies to bacterial pathogens: plants with large bacterial lesions are more likely to be pulled, reducing the effective inoculum of aggressive strains and favouring milder or silent infections that escape detection (Murray‐Watson and Cunniffe 2023).

In addition, ecological theory predicts that lower virulence can be advantageous when transmission depends on subtle, chronic colonisation rather than rapid, lesion‐driven dispersal. For example, work in other bacterial systems ( Staphylococcus aureus in Caenorhabditis elegans ) shows that in‐host competition and host defence pressures drive the evolution of reduced virulence over successive passages (Ford et al. 2016). In the case of plant and P. syringae interactions, downregulation of flagellar genes (controlled by FleQ and further modulated by AlgU) has been shown to reduce plant immune activation (via FLS2 recognition of flagellin), thereby conferring a fitness advantage inside the host while simultaneously reducing conspicuous symptom development (Bao et al. 2020). Consequently, strains like Pta7375 that bear fleQ alleles leading to attenuated motility may be selected both by host defences and by agricultural removal practices.

In summary, this study aimed to identify factors responsible for the virulence differences between two strains and identified two genes, fleQ and gcbB, through comparative genomic analysis. This represents a case where the identification of causal genes was facilitated by favourable conditions, including similar genomic structures and a low number of SNPs between the strains. Further analysis using these causal genes and mutants is expected to lead to the elucidation of gene networks involved in virulence regulation.

Experimental Procedures

Bacterial Strains and Growth Conditions

All bacterial strains used in this study are listed in Table 2. Escherichia coli strains were cultured at 37°C in Luria‐Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with appropriate antibiotics, as required. Pta6605 and Pta7375 (Taguchi et al. 2001) were grown at 27°C in King's B (KB) medium supplemented with necessary antibiotics (Table 2). For phenotypic analyses, liquid LB medium supplemented with 10 mM MgCl2 was used to support optimal growth conditions.

Plant Material and Growth Conditions

Nicotiana tabacum 'Xanthi' seeds were sown in a 1:1 mixture of Sakata Super Mix (Sakata Seed Corporation) and vermiculite (Asahi Kogyo Corporation). Plants were maintained under controlled conditions at 28°C with a photoperiod of 16 h of light (90 μmol·m−2·s−1) and 8 h of dark to ensure uniform growth and development for subsequent inoculation assays.

Comparative Genomic Analysis

The complete genome sequences of Pta6605 and Pta7375 were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information and Pseudomonas genome databases (Accession Number AP024464) (Matsui et al. 2021). Pta7375 was originally isolated from tobacco leaves in Nagasaki Prefecture, Japan (Taguchi et al. 2001). Complete genome sequencing of Pta7375 was performed using a PacBio sequencer, as previously described (Matsui et al. 2021). The Pta7375 genome was 6,201,939 bp in length, had an average GC content of 58.0%, and consisted of one circular chromosome (6,201,939 bp). Both genomes were automatically annotated using the DFAST pipeline (Tanizawa et al. 2018). For Pta7375, 5643 protein coding sequences, 66 tRNAs and 16 rRNA (5S, 16S and 23S rRNA) operons were predicted.

Comparative genomic analysis was conducted using Mauve v.2.4.0 with default settings for identifying genomic rearrangements, SNPs and insertion/deletion regions (Darling et al. 2004). Visualisation and confirmation of SNPs and insertion sites were performed using SnapGene (GSL Biotech), facilitating the identification of mutations potentially responsible for phenotypic differences between the strains. We deposited the complete genome sequence in GenBank (Pta7375 accession number AP044048).

Generation of Mutants

To generate both deletion and replacement alleles of fleQ and gcbB, approximately 1.5 kb of genomic sequence immediately upstream and downstream of each target locus was amplified from Pta6605 or Pta7375 by PCR. For deletion constructs, these flanking fragments were first cloned into pHSG396 (TaKaRa) via HindIII and EcoRI sites using the NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix (New England Biolabs), after which the fleQ open reading frame was excised by inverse PCR to produce the ΔfleQ fragment. This fragment was then subcloned into the suicide vector pK18mobsacBN (Kashihara et al. 2022; Schäfer et al. 1994). In parallel, flanking regions for gcbB were assembled directly into NotI‐digested pK18mobsacBN by In‐Fusion Assembly (TaKaRa) to create the ΔgcbB deletion vector. Replacement constructs were built in two steps: (1) full‐length fleQ locus (5549 bp, including flanks) or gcbB (5400 bp, including flanks) loci were amplified and cloned into pHSG396 via HindIII/EcoRI or NotI sites as appropriate, and (2) the resulting inserts were transferred into pK18mobsacBN to yield pK18mobsacBN‐fleQ 6605, pK18mobsacBN‐fleQ 7375, pK18mobsacBN‐gcbB 6605 and pK18mobsacBN‐gcbB 7375. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing before use.

Deletion and replacement alleles were introduced into recipient strains by conjugation with E. coli S17‐1 carrying the appropriate pK18mobsacBN derivative. After overnight mating on King's B agar, transconjugants were selected on KB plates supplemented with 50 μg/mL nalidixic acid and 50 μg/mL kanamycin. To resolve single‐crossover integrants and cure the vector backbone, colonies were counterselected on KB agar containing 50 μg/mL nalidixic acid and 10% (wt/vol) sucrose. Deletion mutants (ΔfleQ, ΔgcbB and the ΔfleQΔgcbB double mutant) were confirmed by colony PCR, while replacement strains were validated by restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis—using Psp1406I for fleQ swaps and SfcI for gcbB swaps (Figure S9). Double deletions or double replacements were generated by repeating the conjugation and selection procedure in a strain already carrying the first allele. A complete list of all strains, plasmids, primers and mutants generated in this study is provided in Table 2 and Table S1.

Phenotypic Assays

Motility Assays

Swimming motility was assessed using MMMF medium (50 mM potassium phosphate [pH 5.7], 7.6 mM (NH4)2SO4, 1.7 mM MgCl2·6H2O, 1.7 mM NaCl, 10 mM fructose and 10 mM mannitol) solidified with 0.3% agar. Overnight cultures of Pta strains were centrifuged at 700 g for 10 min, washed twice with 10 mM MgSO4 and adjusted to an OD600 of 1.0. Aliquots (3 μL) were spotted onto the centre of the agar plates and allowed to airdry. Plates were incubated at 27°C and photographed after 24 and 48 h to evaluate the swimming distance.

Swarming motility was measured using SWM medium [0.5% proteose peptone (Gibco Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0.3% yeast extract (BD Biosciences) and 0.45% agar (BD Biosciences)]. Preparation and incubation conditions of bacteria were identical to those used in the swimming motility assay. Swarming zones were documented after incubation at 27°C for 24 and 48 h. The area of swimming or swarming motility was quantified using Fiji (ImageJ, https://fiji.sc/). First, the image scale was calibrated using a ruler included in the motility assay image. The motility zone was then manually outlined using the Freehand Selection Tool to accurately trace irregularly shaped areas. The selected area was measured using the Analyse > Measure function to obtain the total area in mm2.

Biofilm Formation Assays

Biofilm formation was visualised using crystal violet staining (Shao et al. 2019). Overnight cultures of Pta strains were centrifuged at 700 g for 10 min, washed, and adjusted to an OD600 of 0.1 in fresh KB medium containing 50 μg/mL Nal. Each bacterial strain was cultured in test tubes at 23°C without shaking. After incubation, unattached cells were removed by washing with sterile water. Biofilms were stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min, thoroughly washed with sterile water, and airdried. Quantitative analysis of biofilm accumulation is described in the Appendix S1.

Measurement of Intracellular c‐di‐GMP Levels

Intracellular c‐di‐GMP levels were measured using a c‐di‐GMP ELISA Kit (Cayman Chemical), as previously described (Liu et al. 2022). Pta strains were harvested after 24 h of growth by centrifugation at 13,000 g for 2 min at 4°C, and washed twice with ice‐cold phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). Cells were lysed using a B‐PER Bacterial Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), incubated at room temperature for 10 min, and centrifuged at 13,000 g for 5 min. Total protein concentration was determined using a Quick Start Bradford assay (Bio‐Rad Laboratories). Intracellular c‐di‐GMP concentrations were normalised to the total protein content and expressed as picomoles per milligram of protein.

Flagellin Detection by Western Blotting

Overnight cultures of Pta strains were adjusted to an OD600 of 0.6 with PBS‐T (PBS with 0.05% Tween 20) (TaKaRa). Aliquots (1 mL) were centrifuged at 800 g for 10 min, and cell pellets were resuspended in 60 μL PBS‐T. The samples were mixed with 4 × sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS) loading buffer and heated at 70°C for 20 min to denature proteins. Proteins were separated by electrophoresis on 15% polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes using a Trans‐Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio‐Rad Laboratories). Membranes were blocked and probed with rabbit anti‐flagellin primary antibody (Shimizu et al. 2003) diluted 1:1000 in Can Get Signal Immunoreaction Solution 1 (Toyobo). Goat antirabbit IgG (H+L)‐alkaline phosphatase conjugated (Bio‐Rad Laboratories), diluted 1:10,000 in Can Get Signal Immunoreaction Solution 2, was used as the secondary antibody. Immunoreactive protein bands were detected using CDP‐Star detection reagents (Cytiva) and signals were visualised using a ChemiDoc Touch imaging system (Bio‐Rad Laboratories).

Plant Inoculation Assays

Flood Assay

The flood assay technique was adapted from the previous method (Ishiga et al. 2017). Overnight cultures of Pta strains were centrifuged at 700 g for 10 min and adjusted to an OD600 of 0.002 or 0.08, with sterile distilled water containing 0.025% Silwet L‐77 to facilitate bacterial adhesion. Tobacco plants grown on Murashige and Skoog medium were submerged in 30 mL of the inoculum to ensure complete leaf coverage. Excess inoculum was removed, and plants were airdried for 15 min. Plates were sealed with surgical tape and incubated at 22°C. Disease symptoms were observed and photographed at 4 dpi.

Measurement of In Planta Bacterial Population

At 3 dpi, leaves from each treatment group were harvested and surface‐sterilised by immersion in 70% ethanol. Using a sterile 4‐mm‐diameter cork borer, two circular discs were excised from each leaf and transferred into a sterile tube. The discs were thoroughly homogenised using a sterile pestle. Sterile water (1 mL) was added to each tube, and the homogenate was vortexed to ensure a uniform suspension. Serial 10‐fold dilutions (10−1–10−5) were prepared, and 5 μL of each dilution was spotted onto KB agar plates supplemented with 50 μg/mL Nal. After a 2‐day incubation period at 27°C, colonies were counted, and the bacterial population was determined based on the dilution factor.

Statistical Analysis

The data of motility, biofilm formation, c‐di‐GMP levels and plant inoculation assays data were analysed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's post hoc test with Benjamini‐Hochberg (BH) adjustment for multiple comparisons (α = 0.05). All analyses were performed using R software (v. 4.4.1; https://www.r‐project.org/) and RStudio (v. 2024.04.02+764; https://posit.co/download/rstudio‐desktop/).

This mutation results in the synthesis of truncated GcbB in Pta7375, which, based on domain prediction, loses the GGDEF domain, an important domain for diguanylate cyclase activity required for synthesising cyclic diguanylate (c‐di‐GMP).
