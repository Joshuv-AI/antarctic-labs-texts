Predictors of vaccine hesitancy among medical students at the University of Belgrade
Authors: Aleksandra Nikolić, Sandra Šipetić Grujičić, Afroditi Maria Tanula, Isidora Vujčić, Vuk Marušić, Marta Majcan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVaccine hesitancy has been observed not only among the general public but also among healthcare professionals and medical students, where it may negatively influence future decisions regarding immunization. This study aimed to assess vaccine attitudes and identify predictors of vaccine hesitancy among medical students.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted on a representative sample of 257 medical students from the Faculty of Medicine at the University of Belgrade. Participants completed a demographic questionnaire and the Vaccination Attitudes Examination (VAX) scale. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data, and linear regression analysis was performed to identify potential predictors of vaccine hesitancy.ResultsOverall, most medical students demonstrated positive attitudes toward vaccination, although some concerns and skepticism were also present. Multivariate linear regression analysis identified several significant predictors of anti-vaccine attitudes. A stronger role of religion in students’ lives (b = 0.404, p = 0.004) and enrollment in English-taught programs (b = 0.618, p < 0.001) were associated with higher VAX scores, indicating greater vaccine hesitancy. In contrast, being in higher years of study (b = -0.467, p = 0.001) and being a voluntary blood donor (b = -0.317, p = 0.017) were associated with lower VAX scores, reflecting more positive attitudes toward vaccination.ConclusionAlthough medical students generally hold positive attitudes toward vaccination, persistent concerns and misconceptions remain. These findings underscore the need for targeted educational strategies during medical training to strengthen vaccine confidence. Equipping future healthcare professionals with accurate knowledge and communication skills is essential for fostering public trust and supporting informed decision-making in a complex health landscape.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08028-3.

Background

Vaccination is considered one of the most effective and impactful public health interventions, responsible for significantly reducing the incidence and mortality of numerous infectious diseases worldwide [1, 2]. Beyond controlling vaccine-preventable diseases, immunization also plays a crucial role in reducing disease-related cancers such as cervical and liver cancer, and contributes to broader societal and economic benefits, including healthcare savings and improved health equity [2].

Despite these well-documented benefits of vaccination, vaccine hesitancy has emerged as a growing global concern. Defined as “a delay in acceptance or refusal of vaccination despite the availability of vaccination services” [3], it was listed by the World Health Organization as one of the top ten threats to global health in 2019 [4].

The determinants of hesitancy are complex and multifactorial. The “3 Cs” model developed by the WHO’s Strategic Advisory Group of Experts on Immunization highlights three main drivers: confidence, complacency, and convenience [3]. Confidence involves trust in vaccine safety and efficacy, the healthcare system, and policymakers; low confidence may arise from fear of side effects, institutional mistrust, or belief in conspiracy theories. Complacency occurs when individuals perceive the risk of vaccine-preventable diseases as minimal, leading to reduced motivation for vaccination. Convenience refers to the availability, affordability, and accessibility of vaccination services, as well as the clarity of health communication. Barriers in these areas can limit uptake even among individuals willing to be vaccinated. An expanded model, the Vaccine Hesitancy Determinants Matrix, further categorizes contributing factors into three domains: contextual influences (e.g., political and religious beliefs, media misinformation), individual and group influences (e.g., personal experiences, knowledge, and trust in healthcare providers), and vaccine-specific issues (e.g., side effect concerns, vaccine novelty, or delivery logistics) [3].

While most studies on vaccine hesitancy focus on the general population [5–7], growing evidence suggests that it also affects healthcare professionals [8–10] and future healthcare providers - medical students [11–13]. A recent systematic review found that vaccine hesitancy among healthcare professionals is most often driven by concerns about vaccine safety or effectiveness, followed by time constraints, lack of vaccine-related knowledge, financial costs, and distrust of the pharmaceutical industry [14].

Similar patterns are seen among college students, with misconceptions regarding vaccine safety, efficacy, and necessity [15, 16]. Beliefs in vaccine-related conspiracy theories and distrust of pharmaceutical motives have also been documented among medical students [17, 18]. Although 80% of students rejected such theories, a notable 20% either endorsed at least one or expressed uncertainty about their validity [17]. In addition to conspiracy beliefs, other factors contributing to vaccine hesitancy among medical students include fears of adverse side effects, doubts about vaccine safety, limited access to credible information, and heavy reliance on social media as a primary information source [19].

Vaccine hesitancy among healthcare workers is particularly concerning given that they are in daily contact with patients and play a crucial role in promoting vaccination and addressing concerns. Their personal attitudes significantly influence whether they recommend vaccines, which in turn shapes patients’ trust, perceptions, and ultimately their decision to get vaccinated [8, 20, 21]. Medical students, as future healthcare providers, are in a formative stage of acquiring both scientific knowledge and professional values. This makes them a key target for early educational interventions to build vaccine confidence and counter misinformation.

Understanding medical students’ attitudes toward vaccination is essential for improving vaccine education and addressing potential misconceptions early in their training. This study aimed to evaluate vaccine attitudes and identify predictors of vaccine hesitancy among medical students at the Faculty of Medicine, University of Belgrade.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional survey was conducted in December 2024 on a sample of medical students from the Faculty of Medicine, University of Belgrade. The required sample size was calculated using Epi Info™ version 7.2.4.0, based on an estimated population of 4,000 students (approximately 3,500 studying in Serbian and 500 studying in English). Assuming a 20% prevalence of belief in at least one anti-vaccine conspiracy theory [17], 95% confidence level, 5% type I error, and a design effect of 1, the minimum sample size was estimated at 232 participants. To ensure proportional representation across study years and program language, we applied a stratified sampling approach, aiming for approximately 40 students per year. Within each stratum, students were approached in person during scheduled classes and invited to complete an anonymous online questionnaire via QR code or link. Participation was voluntary, and all students who were present during the designated survey week and consented to participate were included. No replacement was performed for students who were absent, and no individual follow-up (e.g., email reminders) was carried out. A total of 257 students completed the survey, which slightly exceeded the minimum required sample size.

Research tools

Participants first completed a demographic questionnaire developed for the purposes of this study (see Supplementary File 1). It captured age, gender, year of study (1–6), type of study program (Serbian/English), country of origin, and hometown size (less than 10,000 inhabitants/10,000–49,999/50,000–99,999/100,000 –999,999/1,000,000 and more). Additional items included socioeconomic status (good/moderate/poor), presence of chronic conditions, hospitalizations in the past five years, blood donor status, and importance of religion (Very important/Somewhat important/Little importance/Not important at all).

To assess vaccine hesitancy among students, we used the Vaccination Attitudes Examination Scale (VAX), a previously published and validated 12-item instrument [22] (see Supplementary File 1). It evaluates four key dimensions of vaccine hesitancy: mistrust of vaccine benefit, worries about unforeseen future effects, concerns about commercial profiteering, and preference for natural immunity. Each of the four subscales consists of three items. Responses are rated on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). Items in the Mistrust of vaccine benefit subscale are reverse-scored. Higher scores on each subscale and the overall scale indicate greater vaccine hesitancy. The scale has been translated and validated in many languages [23–26], including Serbian [27].

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Linear regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors of vaccine hesitancy, with the total score on the VAX scale as the dependent variable.

To simplify interpretation and ensure adequate statistical power, several categorical variables were dichotomized: Year of study (1st–3rd vs. 4th–6th year, based on differences in clinical exposure), size of hometown (< 1,000,000 vs. ≥1,000,000 inhabitants, since nearly half of participants lived in large cities), socioeconomic status (Good vs. Moderate/Poor, due to the small number of participants reporting poor status, N = 5), and religiosity (Not important vs. Little to Very important, allowing comparison based on any degree of religious influence).

The independent variables included: gender, age, year of study, type of study program, country of residence, size of hometown, socioeconomic status, religiosity, presence of chronic conditions, hospitalization in the previous 5 years, and voluntary blood donor status.

All variables with a p-value < 0.1 in the univariate linear regression were entered into the multivariate model. Before performing linear regression, assumptions of linearity, normality, homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity were checked. Normality of residuals was assessed using Q–Q plots and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Although the test indicated a significant deviation from normality (p < 0.05), visual inspection of Q–Q plots suggested approximate linearity. Given the large sample size and the robustness of linear regression to minor violations of normality, this assumption was considered sufficiently met. Homoscedasticity was assessed through residual-versus-predicted scatterplots, and multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIFs), all of which were below 5.

A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using SPSS software, version 23.

The average age of the students was 21.62 years. Students aged 21 years or younger comprised 53.7% of the sample, while those older than 21 made up 46.3%. Approximately 62% of participants were in the first three years of their studies.

Most students (76.7%) were from Serbia, while 23.3% were international students from various countries in Europe and other regions. The largest proportion of students (44.7%) came from large cities with populations exceeding 1,000,000. The majority of respondents (61.9%) reported having a good socioeconomic status.

In terms of health status, 7.0% of students reported having a chronic condition (e.g., diabetes, asthma), and 14.4% had been hospitalized at least once in the past five years. Voluntary blood donation was reported by 34.2% of participants.

Regarding the role of religion in their lives, 32.3% of students stated that religion is very important, 25.3% indicated it is somewhat important, and 21.0% reported it is of little importance. Another 21.4% considered religion not important at all.

Figure 1 displays the distribution of responses to the VAX scale items. However, high levels of concern were noted in the “Worries about Unforeseen Effects” subscale. For example, 78.7% agreed that undiscovered problems might exist, and 43.6% worried about vaccines causing unforeseen problems in children. Additionally, 41.3% feared long-term unknown effects.

Concerns about commercial profiteering were also present: 29.1% believed pharmaceutical companies benefit disproportionately, 32.4% suspected that vaccination is promoted for financial reasons (with 7.8% strongly agreeing with this statement), and 16% viewed vaccination programs as deceptive (with 5.1% strongly agreeing).

Regarding natural immunity: 29% believed it provides longer protection, 32.3% saw it as the safest form of immunity, and 20.2% viewed natural exposure as healthier than vaccination. 4.7% of students strongly believed that being naturally exposed to diseases is safer for the immune system than vaccination.

Figure 2 shows the mean values and standard deviations of the VAX Scale scores. The overall mean value of the VAX Scale was 2.80 (SD = 1.19). The highest mean was found in ‘Worries about unforeseen future effects’ (M = 3.63, SD = 1.05), indicating greater concern in this domain. The lowest mean was in ‘Mistrust of vaccine benefit’ (M = 2.15, SD = 1.19), suggesting generally high confidence in vaccine efficacy.

Predictors of vaccine hesitancy (linear regression analysis)

Univariate linear regression identified several significant predictors of VAX score: year of study (b = −0.303, p = 0.012), type of study program (b = 0.408, p = 0.001), importance of religion (b = 0.430, p = 0.004), and voluntary blood donor status (b = −0.243, p = 0.050) (Table 2). Students in higher years, and those who were blood donors, showed lower VAX scores. Conversely, higher importance of religion and enrollment in English-language programs predicted higher VAX scores.

Variables with a significance level of p < 0.1 in univariate analysis (year of study, type of study, country of residence, role of religion, and blood donor status) were included in the multivariate linear regression model. The model was statistically significant (F = 10.122, p < 0.001) and explained 17% of the variance in vaccine attitudes (R² = 0.170)(Table 2) . A stronger role of religion in students’ lives (b = 0.404, p = 0.004, 95% CI: [0.127, 0.680]) and enrollment in English-taught programs (b = 0.618, p < 0.001, 95% CI: [0.350, 0.886]) were associated with higher VAX scores, indicating greater vaccine hesitancy. In contrast, being in higher years of study (b = −0.467, p = 0.001, 95% CI: [−0.733, −0.201]) and being a voluntary blood donor (b = −0.317, p = 0.017, 95% CI: [−0.577, −0.058]) were associated with lower VAX scores, reflecting more positive attitudes toward vaccination.

Discussion

This study explored vaccine attitudes among medical students at the University of Belgrade and identified several sociodemographic and behavioral predictors of vaccine hesitancy. A stronger role of religion in students’ lives and enrollment in English-taught programs were predictors of greater vaccine hesitancy. In contrast, being in higher years of study and being a voluntary blood donor were associated with lower VAX scores, reflecting more positive attitudes toward vaccination.

While the majority of students expressed generally positive attitudes toward vaccination, reporting high levels of perceived safety and effectiveness, important concerns remained, particularly regarding the potential for unforeseen side effects and mistrust toward pharmaceutical motives. This duality is consistent with international studies reporting vaccine hesitancy among healthcare students, who, despite their training, may harbor vaccine-related doubts and believe in vaccine conspiracy theories [12, 13, 17, 28]. This is especially significant, considering that today’s students are tomorrow’s healthcare providers. Their personal views not only shape their future clinical behavior but also influence patient decisions. Research consistently shows that healthcare providers with more favorable attitudes toward vaccines are more likely to recommend them, not just to patients, but also to friends and family. Since healthcare workers are among the most trusted sources of vaccine information, their confidence in vaccination plays a pivotal role in improving vaccine uptake at the population level [20, 21, 29].

The mean VAX score in this study (M = 2.80, SD = 1.19) suggests a moderate level of hesitancy within the sample. Among the four subscales, the highest average score was observed for ‘Worries about unforeseen future effects’ (M = 3.63), indicating a prevalent concern among students about unknown or long-term side effects of vaccines. Similar results have been reported in other studies using the same scale, where this subscale also yielded the highest scores: German sample (M = 3.26) [25], U.S. sample (M = 3.28) [22], Turkish sample (M = 3.67) [24], Romanian sample (M = 4.17) [26]. This pattern aligns with global evidence indicating that concerns about future effects are among the primary drivers of vaccine hesitancy. Prior reviews have shown that even medically educated populations may harbor doubts about vaccine safety, particularly when it comes to potential long-term consequences or risks that are not well understood [10, 19].

These concerns must be interpreted within the context of the ongoing ‘infodemic’, defined by the WHO as the overabundance of (mis)information, both online and offline, during disease outbreaks. It can lead to confusion, risky behaviors, mistrust in health authorities, and a weakened public health response [30].

Social media platforms have amplified vaccine-related misinformation, often spreading emotionally charged but scientifically unfounded narratives about vaccine risks [31]. The COVID-19 pandemic serves as a striking example of an infodemic, an overwhelming surge of misinformation and disinformation, that fueled widespread public concern and confusion [31, 32]. Numerous conspiracy theories circulated during this period, particularly regarding the origin, severity, and prevention of COVID-19, including misinformation about vaccines [33]. Notably, a significant proportion of healthcare workers were not immune to these narratives, with studies reporting that some healthcare workers and students endorsed COVID-19-related conspiracy beliefs [17, 34]. This contributed to measurable levels of vaccine hesitancy within the healthcare workforce, undermining efforts to promote vaccine acceptance and public trust [10, 35].

According to our findings, students in the first three years of medical school (preclinical years) were more likely to be vaccine hesitant. Young adults, including medical students, often rely on social media for health-related information [36, 37]. Research shows that individuals who depend on social media for vaccine information are more prone to vaccine hesitancy, as these platforms often circulate content that lacks scientific validity [38]. This risk is particularly high among preclinical students, who may not yet have developed the critical thinking skills needed to evaluate the credibility of scientific sources [39, 40]. In contrast, students in the later years of study were less hesitant, likely due to increased clinical exposure and training. Similar patterns have been observed in other studies, where senior medical students demonstrated lower belief in conspiracy theories and a greater willingness to get vaccinated [41].

In our curriculum, vaccination-related topics are primarily addressed in Microbiology, Immunology, and Epidemiology courses, which are taught during the second and third years. Additionally, Immunology is offered as an elective course. However, since many students in the early years may not yet have completed these courses—or may opt not to take the elective—there is a gap in vaccine-related education during a critical period when students are forming their professional attitudes. Given this, it may be beneficial to integrate basic immunization content earlier in the curriculum and to consider making the Immunology course a required component of medical education.

These findings highlight the need for earlier and more consistent inclusion of vaccine-related content in medical training. Introducing fundamental concepts of immunization, vaccine safety, and public health relevance during the first two to three years, along with training in critical appraisal and media literacy, could help reduce hesitancy. Interactive teaching strategies—such as case-based learning, small-group discussions, and role-play—may be particularly effective [42, 43]. Equipping students with these skills from the beginning and reinforcing them throughout the curriculum is essential for developing well-informed attitudes and preparing future physicians to serve as trusted sources of vaccine-related information.

An important and novel finding in our study was that students enrolled in English-taught programs had significantly higher VAX scores. Being enrolled in an English-taught program emerged as an independent predictor of vaccine hesitancy. This may be influenced by differences in cultural background, previous experiences with healthcare systems, and varying levels of trust in institutions in their home countries. This result aligns with previous research showing that vaccine hesitancy can vary across countries and population groups and is often higher among minority groups [6, 44, 45]. Similar differences have also been reported among student populations [15, 16, 19]. Given that the curriculum is nearly identical for both English- and Serbian-taught programs, this finding highlights the need to consider cultural factors when designing educational interventions. Tailoring the curriculum to address these differences may help reduce vaccine hesitancy among international students.

In our study, religiosity emerged as a strong predictor of vaccine hesitancy. This finding aligns with previous research indicating that higher levels of religious involvement may be associated with stronger beliefs in conspiracy theories [17, 18, 46, 47] and increased vaccine hesitancy [48]. Even among healthcare workers, individual ethnic, cultural, and religious beliefs have been linked to variations in vaccine acceptance. Religious and political beliefs can act as either barriers or facilitators to COVID-19 vaccine uptake, depending on the context and messaging [19].

Conversely, students who identified as voluntary blood donors reported significantly lower levels of vaccine hesitancy. In addition, being a voluntary blood donor emerged as an independent predictor of higher vaccine acceptance. This finding is consistent with research showing that individuals who engage in altruistic health-related behaviors often demonstrate greater social responsibility and prosocial behavior [49, 50]. The factors driving vaccine hesitancy—such as limited access, mistrust in medical institutions, and cultural disconnect—closely mirror those influencing hesitancy toward blood donation [51, 52].

While blood donation is generally associated with positive health behaviors, some studies conducted during and after the COVID-19 pandemic have reported variation in COVID-19 vaccine uptake among blood donors. For instance, a U.S. study found high rates of noncompliance with COVID-19 vaccination among blood donors, mostly driven by vaccine hesitancy, despite this group typically being highly educated, healthy, and having good access to healthcare [53]. Similarly, Canadian data show that while blood donors demonstrated strong acceptance of COVID-19 vaccination during the pandemic, reflecting general population trends [54], post-pandemic uptake declined significantly, especially compared to seasonal influenza vaccination [55]. These findings suggest that even within health-conscious populations, hesitancy toward specific vaccines, such as the COVID-19 vaccine, may persist and should not be overlooked. Although these behaviors may not fully reflect general vaccine attitudes, they highlight the complexity of vaccine decision-making and the importance of distinguishing between general vaccine hesitancy and vaccine-specific hesitancy.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine general attitudes toward vaccination among medical students at the Faculty of Medicine, University of Belgrade using the validated VAX scale. Our findings emphasize the need for targeted educational interventions early in medical training. Addressing vaccine hesitancy in this population is essential not only for shaping students’ own vaccination behaviors but also for strengthening their future roles as trusted sources of information for patients. Incorporating evidence-based vaccine education into the curriculum, encouraging open discussion of concerns, and training students to recognize and counter misinformation may help reduce hesitancy and improve public health communication.

Limitations

While the study provides important insights, it has certain limitations. Its cross-sectional design does not allow for causal interpretations, and the findings may not be generalizable beyond the surveyed institution. Additionally, self-reported data may be influenced by social desirability bias. Since the survey relied on students who were present during scheduled classes in the designated survey week, the sample may not fully represent all medical students, particularly those absent at the time of data collection. To facilitate interpretation and ensure sufficient statistical power, several categorical predictors were dichotomized based on theoretical and practical considerations (e.g., low frequencies in certain categories or meaningful grouping based on clinical exposure). While this approach is commonly used in epidemiological research to simplify models and enhance interpretability, it may reduce sensitivity to more subtle associations. Nevertheless, key strengths include the use of a validated measurement tool (VAX), a representative student sample, and robust statistical analysis. Further research is needed to explore vaccine hesitancy among medical students in greater depth, ideally through longitudinal designs and by including a broader range of psychological, cultural, and contextual factors that may influence attitudes toward vaccination.

Conclusion

Despite overall support for vaccination, this study reveals significant levels of vaccine hesitancy among medical students, particularly related to concerns about long-term safety and mistrust of the pharmaceutical industry. The findings underscore the importance of implementing targeted educational interventions early in medical training, especially for students in the foundational years and those enrolled in English-taught programs. Notably, students with stronger religious beliefs also showed greater hesitancy, mirroring trends observed in the general population, which should be considered when designing tailored strategies. Integrating comprehensive vaccine education into the curriculum, fostering open dialogue about concerns, and building skills to address misinformation are essential steps. These efforts are crucial not only for shaping students’ attitudes but also for preparing them to serve as credible advocates for vaccination in their future clinical practice.
