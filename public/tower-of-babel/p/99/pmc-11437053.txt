A role for event centrality in prolonged grief disorder
Authors: Ruth Brookman, Celia B. Harris, Maja O’Connor
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Having a traumatic or negative event at the centre of one’s identity is associated with adverse psychological outcomes including post-traumatic stress, depression, and prolonged grief disorder (PGD). However, direct investigation of the role of centrality of a bereavement-event in the maintenance of PGD symptoms is scarce and has not compared immediate and long-term changes in event centrality nor examined the nature of the loss. Data from bereaved partners and adult children in The Aarhus Bereavement Study at four time points over 26 months post-loss were included in this study. Participants completed a PGD symptom measure and the Centrality of Events Scale (CES) on each occasion. Results suggest that bereaved partners had higher PGD and CES scores than bereaved adult children at all four post-bereavement time points. Regardless of relationship type, maintaining higher CES scores over time predicted PGD symptoms, over and above initial symptoms. Our findings suggest a risk factor for maintaining PGD symptoms is the continued centrality of the bereavement to ones’ life story and autobiographical memory. This finding links the mechanisms for maintaining PGD symptoms to those involved in other disorders such as post-traumatic stress, with implications for theoretical models of prolonged grief as well as treatment.

Introduction

Bereavement is the state of having suffered the death of a close other, which is a fundamental human experience with significant and difficult life experience which impacts on physical and cognitive health as well as emotional wellbeing e.g.,1,2. Despite the universality of bereavement, both the severity and duration of grief reactions are marked by differences across individuals. While most people adjust to their loss, approximately 10% of bereaved adults will experience prolonged or pervasive grief reactions that significantly impair their daily functioning3,4. Bereavement is also an inherently relational experience, as the subsequent grief affects all the people close to the deceased. Grief is in other words a family matter. Previous studies on the same sample found that, even in adulthood, children are quite affected by their older parent’s grief reaction5. However, little is known about the mechanisms that drive prolonged grief reactions and how these interplay between parents and children. Clarifying the nature of individual differences and risk factors over time, and identifying the mechanisms involved in the development and maintenance of pervasive grief reactions, assists with both the early identification and treatment of those affected. As such, this study investigates the centrality of the loss event in one’s identity as one such explanation.

Prolonged grief disorder

The term Prolonged Grief Disorder (PGD) has been codified and described as a pervasive grief response to the loss of a loved one, characterised by symptoms of intense emotional pain and yearning or pre-occupation with the deceased person (International Classification of Diseases (ICD), Version 11)6 . The severity and duration of symptoms are important when assessing whether a grief response reaches criteria for PGD, given that grief is a normal reaction to loss. For example, both the ICD6 and the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual, 5th edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR)7 stipulate there must be significant functional impairment to the bereaved individual. Also symptom persistence is a key criterion for diagnosis, with prolonged grief equiring persistent symptoms for a minimum of 6 months (ICD)6 or 12 months (DSM-5-TR)7 following the loss. Features of PGD also include intense sadness, identity disturbance, difficulty accepting the loss and finding meaning beyond it, disbelief, and a sense of emotional detachment8.

Several theoretical models of PGD propose that identity disturbance is central to the maintenance of symptoms. These theories include the Cognitive Attachment Model9, the Cognitive Behavioural Model10, and the Meaning Reconstruction Model11. Consistent with these models, the presence of PGD is associated with weaker self-concept clarity12,13, and individuals with PGD show a bias to recall more autobiographical memories linked with the deceased14.

Centrality of events as a risk factor for adverse psychological outcomes

Event centrality has emerged as one possible manifestation of the link between identity and PGD. Event centrality refers to the extent to which a person holds a particular traumatic or stressful event as central to their identity and life story15. The Centrality of Events scale (CES) was initially developed to examine the role of event centrality of the trauma event in the development of PTSD. The theoretical motivator for CES is based in research findings that autobiographical memories–particularly those that are vivid and easily accessible–can provide structure for one’s sense of self and life narrative16. Typically, autobiographical memories are biased towards positive events, which in turn directs the way we view ourselves and our history toward the positive15. However, Berntsen & Rubin16 hypothesised that negative events–particularly when they are stressful or traumatic–may also have a pivotal role in our life story and identity formation. As such, research on event centrality with diverse population groups impacted by stressful events have included, for example, war veterans17, people living with schizophrenia18, and older bereaved adults19. Findings have identified event centrality as a risk factor for adverse psychological outcomes, such as PTSD (e.g.,20,21).

While the extant literature suggests co-morbidity between prolonged grief reactions and PTSD, it also asserts that PGD is a distinct disorder (see4 for a comprehensive review). Further, a systematic review examining the psychological and clinical correlates of CES found high associations between event centrality and both PTSD and PGD as separate psychological conditions15. Interestingly, when comparing different correlates, Gehrt and colleagues15 found larger associations with CES in studies measuring trauma-related distress and conditions that directly involve autobiographical memory, such a PGD. In contrast to recent longitudinal studies examining the role of event centrality in PTSD (e.g.,22), most of the studies examining PGD have been cross-sectional. These cross-sectional studies have supported a relationship between centrality of the bereavement event to one’s identity and life story and the presence of PGD. For instance, Eckholdt and colleagues23 found that centrality of the bereavement event at 4 years post-loss was associated with grief symptoms in elderly bereaved spouses. Bellet and colleagues24 found a similar relationship in a cross-sectional study of undergraduate university students, in which meaning-making moderated a positive relationship between grief symptoms and event centrality.

However, beyond the presence of PGD symptoms, the direct investigation of the role of event centrality in the maintenance of PGD over time is scarce. In one exception, a prospective study by25 found that centrality of the event in a sample recruited within one year of bereavement predicted grief symptoms one year later (see also26). However, it is not clear from these prospective studies whether event centrality remains elevated for those who have high initial scores, and therefore whether it is the initial or current event centrality that is predictive of subsequent PGD. Additionally, no longitudinal research has examined the impact of individual differences associated with the bereavement event, such as the nature of the relationship with the deceased. Previous research has often not examined the impact of relationship27. However, some prior research suggests that in relationships where there is an ongoing merged identity, PGD symptoms following the loss are increased28, suggesting that some types of losses may increase risk of PGD.

Despite the central position of ‘identity’ within bereavement models, and the proposal that event centrality may influence grief reactions23,29, there are limited studies that have directly examined the role of event centrality in PGD over time. However, as the pathology of PGD is reflected in both the presence and persistence over time of grief symptoms, longitudinal research examining factors related to identity disturbance is necessary to identify the mechanisms that contribute to the maintenance of symptoms post-loss. Event centrality may develop and change post bereavement as a factor that influences PGD over time. We addressed this gap in the current study.

The present study

In the current longitudinal study, we aimed to directly examine the role of the event centrality in PGD symptoms over time, and how this might differ depending on the relationship with the deceased. This was addressed through the collection and analysis of self-reported questionnaires by bereaved individuals (partners and adult children) at four key time points (2-, 6-, 11-, and 26-months) post bereavement. Details of the research questions are presented below. Our primary research question was whether the centrality of a bereavement event to one’s identity and life story, both initially and over time, influenced the maintenance of PGD symptoms. We also examined any difference in event centrality.

Method

Participants

Participants for the present study were bereaved individuals who had experienced the death of either a partner or a parent, resulting in participant cohorts of partners and adult children. Partners were married to or in registered same-sex or heterosexual partnerships with the deceased. The bereaved partners were also registered to live at the same address as the deceased. Adult children were defined as biological, adopted, or stepchildren of the deceased, and were identified by the bereaved partner.

Participants completed the reported measures in the context of their broader participation in the longitudinal Aarhus Bereavement Study, a cohort managed by the last author (see30 for full details, broader aims and methods). The Aarhus Bereavement (TAB) study is multi-wave large cohort study examining natural and prolonged grief reactions. National registers in Denmark were used to identify individuals living within the Aarhus metropolitan area who lost a spouse between January 2017 and March 2018. Partners were sent information about the study and invited to participate in the research, approximately one month following the death of their spouse. To recruit the adult child participants, spousal participants were also invited to share information about the research with their children. The TAB study adheres to the General Data Protection Regulation of the European Union [2016/679] and complies with the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2008. Data were collected and managed using the research electronic data capture (REDCap) tool hosted at Aarhus University, Denmark. The study and all experimental protocols were approved by both the Danish Data Protection Agency [registration number: 2015-57-0002-62908-26] and Aarhus University’s institutional GDPR [#266]. When the TABstudy was initiated in 2017, in Denmark, observational research did not require additional approval from the University Human Research Ethics Committee (beyond stated data protection approvals) unless there was an intervention component.

All participants provided written informed consent and received self-report questionnaires for the current study at 4 time points: T1, two-months post-loss; T2, 6 months post-loss; T3, 11 months post-loss; and T4, 26 months post-loss. To accommodate different needs, participants chose between receiving the questionnaire by email or postal service. Participant numbers and characteristics at each time point are presented in Table 1. Measures

Participants completed a range of measures over multiple time points as part of their participation in the larger TAB study. The larger study involved completion of a 24-page survey battery including demographic questions immediately followed by a measure of prolonged grief disorder symptoms. Next, self-reported measures examined mental health (depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder), physical function, rumination, event centrality (page 18), and finally personality trait measures (e.g., neuroticism, positivity). For the purposes of the current research, we analysed data using the following two self-administered questionnaires.

PGD symptoms: the prolonged grief-13 scale

Symptoms were measured using the Prolonged Grief-13 (PG-13)3. The PG-13 is a 13-item scale designed to measure the severity and frequency of PGD symptoms based on the DSM-5-TR7 diagnostic criteria, such as yearning for the deceased, shock, numbness, intense sadness, difficulty accepting the loss, avoidance of reminders of the loss, difficulty engaging in life, difficulty finding meaning and purpose, and identity disturbance. In addition to these 11 items regarding symptoms, one item assesses duration of symptoms, and one assesses functional impairment. The items were rated using 5-point scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (several times a day or overwhelmingly) and scores were summed. Higher scores indicate elevated grief symptoms and a score over 29 is considered to indicate clinical level symptoms (e.g.,30). Example of PG-13 items include: “In the past month, how often have you felt yourself longing or yearning for the person you lost?”. The Cronbach’s alphas for the scale in the present study was 0.90 (T1), 0.91 (T2), 0.92 (T3), and 0.89 (T4).

Event centrality: the centrality of events scale

The Centrality of Events Scale (CES)16 was developed to measure the extent to which an event is central to an individual’s everyday inferences (example item: ‘This event has become a reference point for the way I understand myself and the world’), life story (example item: ‘This event permanently changed my life’), and identity (example item: ‘I feel that this event has become part of my identity’). We used the short-form 7-item version, where participants were asked to rate on a 5-point Likert-type scale from 1 (Totally Disagree) to 5 (Totally Agree), and scores were summed to create a total score with a maximum value of 35. The Cronbach’s alphas in the current study were 0.87 (T1), 0.89 (T2), 0.89 (T3), and 0.90 (T4).

Procedure

Participants received questionnaires by hard copy mail or by email, depending on their nominated preference. They gave informed consent for their participation in the research. Participants completed a battery of questionnaires in their own time at each time point.

Data analysis

To first characterise the sample and examine levels of symptomatology over time, we conducted two separate mixed measures analyses of variance (ANOVAs) for (i) prolonged grief symptoms and (ii) centrality of event, to examine changes across T1-T4 as well as to compare relationship type (parent vs. adult child). We used a 2 (relationship) × 4 (timepoint) design and examined main effects as well as interaction, following up any interaction with pairwise comparisons of relationship type at each time point.

To answer our primary research question, we first conducted a correlation analysis to explore the relationships between PGD symptoms and event centrality at each of the 4 time points. Next, to assess the unique variance accounted for by event centrality and to compare the explanatory power of initial and ongoing CES scores, we conducted a linear regression analysis with the dependent variable of PGD symptoms at the final time point (T4), since a high score here indicates the maintenance of PGD symptoms.

Results

Participant attrition

To determine any potential impact of participant attrition on our findings, since our analyses only utilised data from those who returned questionnaires at all four timepoints, we compared baseline PG-13 and CES scores for participants who completed T4 measures versus those who dropped out (see Table 1). This analysis indicated that those who returned questionnaires at T4 compared to those who had dropped out in the interim had similar T1 scores on the PG-13 and the CES questionnaires, t(1173) = 1.236, p = 0.217 and t(1115) = 1.014, p = 0.311 respectively. Overall, we considered the remaining sample representative in terms of their grief symptomatology.

PGD symptoms and CES over time

First, we examined whether PGD symptoms and CES scores changed over time, and whether initial scores as well as trajectory over time depended on the nature of the relationship to the deceased (Fig. 1). Pairwise comparisons (α = 0.05/4 with a Bonferroni adjustment) confirmed that partners had significantly higher PG-13 scores than children at all time points, all ts > 7.02, all ps < 001. PGD symptoms tended to reduce with additional time post-loss regardless of relationship. Pairwise comparisons (α = 0.05/3 with a Bonferroni adjustment) on the whole sample showed a significant decrease between each successive time point, all ts > 7.59, all ps < 0.001. This analysis confirmed that CES at Time 4 accounted for significant unique variance in the maintenance of PGD symptoms, over and above the effects of baseline symptoms as well as demographic variables (see Tables 3 and 4). Interestingly, in the final model, once we accounted for demographic variables and baseline PG-13 scores, then CES-T1 showed no significant relationship with PG13-T4 (see Tables 3 and 4). To understand the role of gender as a predictor, pairwise comparisons suggested that women had higher scores than men for PG-13 at T1 and for CES at both T1 and T4, all ts > 4.62, all ps < 0.001, although the PG-13 scores of women and men were not statistically different at T4, t = 1.69, p = 0.092. Overall, to answer our primary research question, our analysis suggested that over and above initial grief symptoms, maintained centrality of event accounted for unique variance in prolonged grief symptoms two-years post-loss while initial centrality of event did not.

Discussion

We investigated the role of centrality of the bereavement event to identity and life story in the maintenance of PGD symptoms over time, examining data from bereaved partners and adult children at four time points over 26 months post-bereavement. Our findings suggest that bereaved partners had higher prolonged grief scores and higher proclivity to centralise the bereavement event in their identity and life story, compared to bereaved adult children. Regardless of relationship type (partner or adult child), maintaining this higher centralisation of the bereavement event over time predicted the maintenance of grief symptoms, over and above initial symptoms. Our findings suggest a risk factor for maintaining prolonged grief symptoms over time is the continued centrality of the bereavement to ones’ life story and autobiographical memory.

Role of individual differences

Consistent with previous research, we found that the nature of the relationship between the bereaved and the deceased impacted the severity of grief symptoms. Partners experienced more intense grief symptoms both immediately following the bereavement event, and over time, but the symptom intensity diminished with additional time post-loss at a similar rate regardless of relationship type28,32. We also found that women reported more intense grief symptoms and higher levels of event centrality than did men, reflecting the significant explanatory effect of gender within our regression analysis. This is also consistent with prior research that has identified female gender as a risk factor for prolonged grief33.

The loss of a co-habiting partner is generally found to be a risk factor for PGD (see33 for a systematic review and meta-analysis of risk factors). This may be explained, in part, by the dramatic changes to everyday life following the loss of a co-habiting partner compared to a non-cohabiting parent in adulthood. Bereaved partners are generally confronted with more coping challenges, including the yearning for the deceased, identity disruption, the regulation of emotions, and the management of activities of daily living–all without the support of their partner. The finding that PGD symptoms of the loss event is and remains higher in partners compared to children is therefore an important, but not surprising finding. A novel finding from this study, however, is that centrality of event scores followed a similar trajectory, whereby partners obtained higher event centrality scores at all post-bereavement time points when compared to their adult children. The greater changes to everyday life and coping generally experienced by partners could similarly explain this finding, as these could be functioning to centralise the loss-event more firmly into the bereaved partner’s (versus adult child’s) identity and life story. The intensity of event centrality, however, diminished significantly for both partners and adult children with additional time post-bereavement, although scores on centrality took longer to drop for both groups compared to prolonged grief disorder symptoms, with a significant reduction only seen between 11- and 26-months post-bereavement. The reasons why the intensity of event centrality reduced at a slower rate than PGD symptoms is unknown, highlighting the need for additional longitudinal research to further examine the similarities and differences between these constructs over time.

A systematic review highlighted the importance of autobiographical memory processes for the centrality of one’s stressful or traumatic event, through findings of positive stable correlations between CES and subjective characteristics of the event memory such as vividness and emotional intensity15. That is, negative events that are central to identity and life stories are also more vivid and intense than those negative memories that are less central to the self. It could be, therefore, that autobiographical memories of spousal bereavement events are particularly vivid and/or accessible, due to increased impact on day-to-day experiences and activities meaning that the bereavement event is a more significant practical transition point (see also34). Importantly, our findings indicate that bereaved individuals who have lost a partner are more prone to centralise the bereavement event in their identity and life narrative, which provides a focal point for intervention for at-risk individuals.

Role of immediate and ongoing event centrality

Our primary research question explored whether the maintenance of grief symptoms might be explained by the centrality of the event to one’s identity and life story, and particularly to compare the explanatory power of initial and ongoing centrality. We used a direct measure of centrality of events16 at multiple time points from immediately post-loss up to two years later. We found that event centrality played a key role in PGD symptoms, predicting unique variance in symptom maintenance over and above prior symptom intensity. Interestingly we also found that this role was time specific, with a particular role for maintained high levels of event centrality over and above initial event centrality and the severity of prolonged grief symptoms both initially and over time.

This finding has theoretical implications concerning our understanding of the trajectory of prolonged grief and associated risk factors. According to the “self-expansion model”35,36 individuals incorporate loved ones into their own identity to enhance their self-efficacy and goal achievements. There is research evidence to support this practice by some couples, as this shared identity has positive associations with the quality and constructive maintenance of the relationship37, at least while partners are both alive. In the context of bereavement, however, placing the bereavement-event at the centre of one’s life story, may have negative consequences for coping, as the deceased is no longer an active, living asset/self-efficacy enhancing factor in the daily life of the bereaved. Therefore, it has been argued that an important task of functional grief is–to some extent–to exclude the ‘other-from-self’ through strengthening one’s own identity apart from the loss38. This would involve accepting the finality of the loss–the deceased will never return–and openness to the loss becoming gradually less “central” to one’s life story, such that a bereaved individual ideally would become less reliant on the “loss” as the focal point for their own self-identity. Our findings are consistent with the possibility that those individuals who experience pathological prolonged grief reactions have been less able to reduce the centrality of the bereavement event in their life.

Theoretically, our results support the importance of identity disturbance in PGD whereby centrality of events scores, and particularly T4 scores, predicted the maintenance of grief symptoms over and above initial grief reaction. This suggests that those who have been more successful in the task of “de-centralising the loss” over time and going through the process of building a life story that does not pivot entirely on the bereavement-event, have a lower risk of maintaining prolonged grief reactions. This is consistent with theoretical models that acknowledge identity and memory disturbance as key mechanisms contributing to PGD, which importantly should be considered to inform future PGD research and interventions (e.g.,9,10).

The link between PGD and centrality of event also has clinical implications, as this knowledge can assist with early identification and guide treatment over time for those at risk of developing PGD. For example, the use of the centrality of event scale16 by health professionals immediately following bereavement and then again at 2 years post-bereavement could assist with detection and intervention for PGD. Also, the importance of autobiographical memories can be taken into consideration when designing interventions for people with prolonged grief reactions. For example, considering the role that the subjective features of autobiographical memories, such as vividness and emotional intensity, may have in the maintenance of event centrality over time15. Overall, the association of event centrality with PGD symptoms suggests a risk factor over time, with clinical implications. For example, in the early period following the loss, the bereaved often need to acknowledge the massive impact (centrality) of the death and the deceased on their identity. However, our findings suggest the potential value of strengthening the life story around other people and events, beyond their loss. Narrative therapy is one therapeutic approach that could be helpful in the process of strengthening life stories outside of the bereavement, as it aims to challenge a dominant discourse that may be shaping a person’s life story in unhelpful ways, whereby the therapist helps people to co-author a new narrative about themselves39,40. Studies examining the efficacy of narrative therapy highlight the potential benefits of this approach for PGD, however, the need for continued and rigorous research remains (see41–43).

The longitudinal study design together with a large sample size are a strength, providing us with the opportunity to map the trajectory of both prolonged grief symptoms and event centrality, building on prior prospective research25,26. Initial measures were completed proximally to the loss (~ 2 months) enabling close tracking of functional and pathological reactions to bereavement. There are, however, methodological limitations to consider when interpreting the findings. For example, while our sample was population-based, we do not know of the identified patterns in non-responders or participants who dropped out of the study, particularly since we only focused our analysis on those who had completed measures at every time point. However, our analysis did not identify baseline differences between responders and non-responders. Further, while we employed standardized measures to assess our variables of interest, responses involved self-reported measures which may have contributed the findings, and our survey was brief to minimise the burden on participants (e.g., using the 7-item CES). As such, there are other factors that were not included in the study, that may have influenced the grief journey of participants. For example, the study included only two relationship types to the bereaved (partner and adult child). Future research could consider including a broader range of relationship types when examining the role of event centrality and PGD over time.

This longitudinal study is the first to compare the predictive power of both initial and long-term event centrality in the maintenance of prolonged grief symptoms, and to compare patterns of associations depending on relationship-type. Compared with adult children, bereaved partners experienced higher levels of both prolonged grief symptoms and event centrality at all time points post-bereavement. However, both groups showed decreasing symptoms at a similar rate over time. Importantly, our novel longitudinal findings confirmed a relationship between prolonged grief symptoms and the centrality of events that were time specific, such that continuing to maintain the centrality of the bereavement event over time had more of an impact on prolonged grief symptoms than initial central of the event immediately post-bereavement. This highlights the importance of assessing centrality of loss when determining an individual’s risk for prolonged grief reactions. These findings also have theoretical implications, whereby the task of de-centralising the loss from one’s identity, aligns with PGD models that acknowledge the role of identity and memory disturbance in the development and maintenance of PGD. In this instance, the role of event centrality can not only assist with early identification of individuals at-risk for the development of PGD, but its link with theoretical models and research provides an evidence base to guide treatment planning. Future research could examine whether therapy targeting event centrality is particularly beneficial supporting people to recover from PGD.
