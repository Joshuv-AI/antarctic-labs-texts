Introduction

Background and rationale

Mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (mTLE) is one of the most common focal epilepsy syndromes and a major contributor to drug-resistant epilepsy, with substantial impacts on morbidity, psychosocial functioning and health-related quality of life (QoL).1 2 Pharmacological therapy is the first-line treatment; however, a considerable proportion of patients continue to have disabling seizures despite appropriate antiseizure medication trials.3 Persistent seizures are associated with increased risks of injury, cognitive decline, psychiatric comorbidity and premature mortality, underscoring the need for effective disease-modifying interventions.3

For appropriately selected patients with drug-resistant mTLE, epilepsy surgery provides the highest likelihood of seizure freedom and can reduce seizure-related disability and treatment burden. Traditional open surgery (OS), such as anterior temporal lobectomy (ATL) and selective amygdalohippocampectomy, is an established standard of care and has demonstrated robust seizure outcomes in multiple cohorts.4,6 Nevertheless, OS is inherently invasive and may be associated with perioperative morbidity, longer hospitalisation and recovery, and potential neurocognitive consequences, particularly in domains supported by mesial temporal and adjacent temporal neocortical networks.7 These considerations have driven increasing interest in minimally invasive approaches that aim to reduce surgical burden while preserving seizure control.8 Magnetic resonance-guided laser interstitial thermal therapy (MRgLITT) has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative for mTLE.9 10 MRgLITT delivers laser energy through a stereotactically implanted applicator to produce controlled thermal ablation of epileptogenic mesial temporal structures under real-time MRI thermometry.11 potential advantages include smaller incisions, reduced tissue disruption, shorter hospital stays and faster recovery, with a theoretical benefit for functional preservation due to more focal targeting.12 Early observational studies have demonstrated seizure-free rates ranging from 55.8% to 76.9% at 12 months, with improved tolerability compared with OS.8 13 In a propensity score-matched cohort, seizure freedom did not differ significantly between MRgLITT and OS.13

Despite the growing clinical adoption of MRgLITT, direct high-quality comparative evidence against conventional OS remains limited, resulting in uncertainty when counselling patients, particularly when balancing seizure control against functional outcomes and healthcare utilisation. To address this evidence gap, we designed the Minimally Invasive Neurosurgery for Epilepsy (MINE) trial to compare MRgLITT with conventional OS in patients with drug-resistant mTLE. The trial will evaluate non-inferiority for seizure-free rate at 12 months and will additionally assess patient-reported cognitive and memory outcomes, perioperative safety, antiseizure medication reduction and health-economic outcomes.

Objective

The primary objective of the MINE trial is to determine whether MRgLITT is non-inferior to traditional OS for achieving seizure-free rate at 12 months in patients with drug-resistant mTLE. Secondary objectives are to compare the two interventions with respect to (1) broader seizure outcomes (International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) class distribution, seizure frequency and seizure severity) assessed at 6 and 12 months; (2) cognitive and memory function assessed at 6 and 12 months; (3) postoperative safety outcomes collected from surgery through discharge and at follow-up visits at 6 and 12 months, and (4) healthcare utilisation and health-economic outcomes: length of hospital stay measured during hospitalisation; and reoperation, antiseizure medication reduction and QoL assessed at 6 and 12 months.

Trial design

This protocol was developed in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines.14 The MINE trial is a prospective, randomised, open-label, blinded-endpoint (PROBE), parallel-group clinical trial with 1:1 allocation to MRgLITT or conventional OS. The primary outcome is seizure-free rate at 12 months, and the trial is powered for a non-inferiority comparison.

Methods and analysis

Trial design

Beijing Tiantan Hospital led the MINE trial. All patients considered for enrolment will undergo a comprehensive presurgical evaluation to confirm the diagnosis of drug-resistant mTLE and to determine suitability for epilepsy surgery. Only participants deemed appropriate surgical candidates by a multidisciplinary epilepsy surgery team will be enrolled and randomised. Participants will attend virtual or in-person study visits, complete electronic questionnaires and undergo protocol-specified neuroimaging assessments. The collection of exploratory data during in-person visits will be optional.

Eligible participants will be adults aged 18–65 years with drug-resistant mTLE who have at least one seizure per month, and who have completed a comprehensive presurgical evaluation confirming the diagnosis and determining suitability for epilepsy surgery (box 1). Interventions

Eligible participants will be randomised in a 1:1 ratio to undergo either MRgLITT or traditional OS. Both interventions will be performed by experienced epilepsy surgery teams using standard perioperative care pathways at participating centres.

Participants allocated to the MRgLITT arm will undergo stereotactic laser ablation of the epileptogenic mesial temporal structures under real-time MRI guidance and thermometry. Surgical planning will be performed using preoperative structural and vascular imaging to define the target and to plan one or more trajectories that maximise coverage of the intended mesial temporal ablation zone while avoiding critical neurovascular structures. The procedure will be performed under general anaesthesia. Placement of the laser applicator will be verified with intraoperative imaging prior to ablation, and thermal ablation will be delivered in one or more cycles under continuous MRI thermometry, with additional ablation and/or pullback along the planned trajectory performed as required to achieve the prespecified ablation extent. A postablation MRI scan will be obtained to confirm the ablation zone. The number and configuration of laser trajectories used will be recorded for each participant as part of the procedural details.

Participants allocated to the OS arm will undergo conventional epilepsy surgery with ATL, according to prespecified indications and the treating team’s standard operative approach. In brief, ATL involves resection of the anterior temporal neocortex together with removal of mesial temporal structures. The extent of resection will be quantified using a voxel-based morphometry-derived metric, defined as the ratio of resected temporal lobe volume to total brain volume and will be recorded for each participant as part of the procedural details.

Outcome measures

The outcome measurements will include a comprehensive assessment of seizure control efficacy, brain function protection, surgical safety and health economics, allowing for an evidence-based comparison between MRgLITT and OS for TLE.

The primary outcome of this study is postsurgical seizure-free rate at 12 months, assessed according to the ILAE outcome classification15 (table 2). Seizure freedom will be defined as ILAE Class 1, indicating no seizures, including aura, at the time of follow-up. Seizure outcomes will also be assessed at 6 months as an additional time point.

Sample size

The primary outcome is the 12-month postsurgical seizure-free rate. Based on the investigators’ previously published clinical data,13 the expected seizure-free rate at 12 months is 76.9% for MRgLITT and 76.0% for traditional OS. The trial is designed as a non-inferiority study. The non-inferiority margin was set at an absolute difference of −0.23, informed by patient preference research reporting a minimum acceptable benefit of 23% for choosing a minimally invasive procedure over OS.21

Sample size was calculated using PASS (V.15.0.5) with the following assumptions: one-sided α=0.025, power=80%, 1:1 allocation, expected proportions P1=0.769 (MRgLITT) and P2=0.760 (OS), and an assumed true difference D1 (P1–P2) = 0.009. Under these parameters, a minimum of 50 participants per group is required. Allowing for 15% loss to follow-up, the final planned sample size is 120 participants (60 per group).

Recruitment and screening

Potentially eligible participants will be identified from outpatient clinics and inpatient admissions within the epilepsy surgery programme at participating centres. Candidates will undergo a comprehensive presurgical evaluation as part of routine clinical care to confirm drug-resistant mTLE and to determine suitability for epilepsy surgery. After initial eligibility screening, trained research staff will approach eligible patients, provide written and verbal information about the study, and obtain written informed consent prior to enrolment. Following consent, baseline assessments, reasons for exclusion and reasons for withdrawal prior to randomisation will be documented.

Randomisation and allocation

An independent statistician will generate the randomisation sequence using a computer-generated permuted-block algorithm with 1:1 allocation. Allocation will be implemented via a secure central randomisation system to ensure concealment. After eligibility confirmation and written informed consent, authorised study staff will randomise participants and notify the treating surgical team of the allocated intervention. To minimise predictability, variable block sizes (eg, 4 and 6) will be used and will not be disclosed to investigators or recruiting staff. The randomisation list will be stored securely and will not be accessible to outcome assessors or data analysts.

Blinding

In the PROBE trial, blinding of participants and treating clinicians is not feasible due to the nature of the surgical interventions. However, to reduce assessment bias, postoperative outcome assessments will be performed by independent assessors blinded to treatment allocation, including seizure outcome classification and neuropsychological/patient-reported outcome assessments. Data analysts will remain blinded to group allocation until the primary analysis is finalised. Procedures to maintain blinding will include restricting access to allocation information and separating clinical care documentation from assessment records wherever feasible.

Data collection and management

Data for each participant will be recorded on standardised case report forms (CRFs) by trained study staff. Participants will be assigned a unique study identification number, and identifiable information will be stored separately from study data to maintain confidentiality; identifiers will not appear on CRFs or other study documents used for analyses. Where assessments are conducted by blinded personnel, the assessor role and blinding procedures will be prespecified in the protocol and documented in the trial master file.

A coordinating centre will oversee data management across sites, including training and operational support, routine data quality checks and data validation and cleaning procedures. CRFs are designed to capture key trial variables, including demographics, baseline characteristics, surgical and perioperative details, follow-up assessments and AE reporting. CRFs will be pilot-tested prior to study initiation, and refinements will be implemented as needed to improve completeness and reliability.

Monitoring and oversight

An independent Data and Safety Monitoring Committee (DSMC) will oversee participant safety and trial conduct. The DSMC will comprise three external members (a chair and two members), including at least one biostatistician/methodologist, none of whom will be directly involved in trial delivery. The DSMC will review accumulating safety data at prespecified intervals and may recommend continuation, modification or termination of the trial based on participant safety and protocol adherence. The DSMC will have access to unblinded safety data as required. All SAEs, including deaths, will be reported to the DSMC without delay in accordance with institutional and regulatory requirements.

Between-group comparisons of baseline variables will be performed using Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous data, and χ² tests or Fisher’s exact test for categorical data, as appropriate. Ordinal variables will be compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Harms

AEs and SAEs will be recorded from the time of surgery through the 12-month follow-up using standardised CRFs and review of medical records. All AEs will be assessed by a study clinician for severity and relatedness to the allocated intervention. All SAEs, including deaths, will be reported to the DSMC and the responsible ethics committees without delay in accordance with institutional and regulatory requirements.

Inspections and auditing

The trial may be subject to inspection or audit by the sponsor and/or ethics committees/regulatory authorities to ensure compliance with the protocol and applicable regulations. Any audit will be conducted independently of the investigators.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. The burden of the intervention and follow-up procedures was assessed by the clinical team based on routine practice.

Trial dates

This trial started recruitment in July 2025 and aims to complete follow-up by the end of 2027.

Ethics and dissemination

The study has been approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Beijing Tiantan Hospital (KY2025-073-01). Any protocol amendments will be submitted to the IRB for approval and communicated to relevant stakeholders as required.

Trained research staff will obtain written informed consent from all participants prior to enrolment. Potential participants will receive written and verbal information about the trial and will have the opportunity to discuss study procedures with a member of the research team in person before deciding whether to participate.

Access to data

Access to the trial dataset will be restricted to authorised study personnel, including the principal investigator, coinvestigators, the clinical research coordinator, research assistants and the study statistician, in accordance with institutional policies and data protection requirements.

Dissemination policy

Trial results will be reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for parallel-group trials. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations, and lay summaries will be shared with participants and relevant patient communities where appropriate.

Data sharing statement

De-identified individual participant data that underlie the results reported in this study will be made available on reasonable request. Additional documents, including the study protocol and statistical analysis plan, will also be available upon request.

Discussion

In recent years, MRgLITT has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative for mTLE, with observational studies suggesting seizure outcomes that may be comparable to OS, alongside reduced perioperative morbidity and shorter hospital stays.22 However, prospective randomised trials directly comparing MRgLITT with conventional OS remain limited. The MINE trial is designed to address this evidence gap using a PROBE framework, evaluating seizure-free rate at 12 months together with patient-reported cognitive and memory outcomes, safety and healthcare utilisation. If MRgLITT is shown to be non-inferior to OS for seizure freedom, it could support wider adoption of less invasive approaches and inform shared decision-making for patients who prioritise rapid recovery and functional preservation.

In our review of trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, we identified one completed study (the SLATE trial, NCT02844465), a prospective study investigating stereotactic laser ablation for temporal lobe epilepsy. While SLATE will provide important information on longer-term outcomes after MRgLITT, it does not directly compare MRgLITT with ATL, leaving uncertainty about relative effectiveness. A recent meta-analysis reported that seizure-free rate after MRgLITT may be modestly lower than those after ATL but may be associated with better neurocognitive outcomes, suggesting potential advantages for patients at higher risk of cognitive decline.23 In addition, Wu et al compared outcomes across resective, ablative and neuromodulatory procedures for temporal lobe epilepsy and concluded that MRgLITT may offer favourable cognitive preservation, although seizure recurrence and the need for repeat procedures remain important considerations.24

Recent studies have demonstrated that MRgLITT achieves comparable seizure-free rates to OS, with MRgLITT achieving a rate of 76.9% compared to 76.0% for OS. These findings suggest that MRgLITT may offer a minimally invasive alternative to ATL without compromising seizure control.13 Existing evidence also highlights potential advantages of MRgLITT that warrant rigorous evaluation in randomised settings. MRgLITT enables targeted ablation of mesial temporal structures, which may help preserve networks supporting memory and language. Preliminary reports suggest limited adverse neuropsychological effects following MRgLITT in selected cohorts, whereas ATL involves broader tissue resection and has been associated with declines in verbal and visual memory, including in relation to laterality.25 Reported complication rates and healthcare utilisation may also favour MRgLITT, with some studies suggesting lower rates of major complications and shorter hospital stays compared with open resection.26 Together, these observations support the rationale for a pragmatic comparative trial that evaluates seizure outcomes alongside cognitive, safety and health-economic.

This trial has several methodological strengths. First, randomisation reduces selection bias and supports causal inference regarding comparative effectiveness. Second, blinded endpoint assessment within a PROBE design mitigates outcome assessment bias while maintaining feasibility for surgical interventions. Finally, the prespecified non-inferiority framework reflects clinical decision-making in which a less invasive procedure may be preferred if seizure control is not meaningfully worse, particularly when considering functional outcomes and resource utilisation.

Several limitations should be considered. The primary follow-up duration is 12 months, and longer-term seizure control after MRgLITT may decline over time in some cohorts; extended follow-up will be important to evaluate durability of benefit and identify predictors of long-term outcomes.8 Although this trial is conducted across participating centres with standardised procedures, generalisability to other settings may still be influenced by centre experience and patient selection. Future multicentre studies across broader healthcare systems, along with advances in neuroimaging and predictive modelling, may further refine patient selection for MRgLITT versus OS and improve individualised surgical planning.

In summary, the MINE trial will provide randomised comparative evidence on MRgLITT versus conventional OS for drug-resistant mTLE, integrating seizure control, patient-reported cognitive and memory outcomes, safety and healthcare utilisation. The findings are expected to inform patient counselling, surgical decision-making and guideline development, and to clarify the role of minimally invasive approaches in contemporary epilepsy surgery.
