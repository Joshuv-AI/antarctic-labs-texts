Oxygenated PAHs in Tokyo Bay sediments: distributional contrast with parent PAHs and their effect evaluations with fish embryos
Authors: Kazuki Imamura, Aya Maehara, Shutaro Nakamura, Seiichi Uno, Emiko Kokushi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Oxygenated polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (oxy-PAHs) are a group of PAHs derivatives formed by chemical or microbial oxidation that can negatively affect benthic organisms. However, their distribution in coastal sediments remains poorly understood. In the present study, we examined the distribution and ecological risks of oxy-PAHs in sediments collected from 18 sites in Tokyo Bay, Japan. Concentrations of target oxy-PAHs ranged from 10.2 to 1299 ng/g dry weight, generally exceeding previously reported values. Strong correlations were found between oxy-PAHs and sediment carbon content and between oxy-PAHs and parent PAHs, possibly showing common sources or transformation pathways. Lethal and developmental effects were observed in Java medaka (Oryzias javanicus) embryos exposed to the artificial sediments spiked with two oxy-PAHs (7,12-benz(a)anthraquinone and 1,4-naphthoquinone) found at several locations in Tokyo Bay. Both risk quotient indices exceeding 1 were shown at more than 10 sites, and suggest that these 2 substances may pose potential threats to benthic organisms at a number of sites in Tokyo Bay.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10661-026-15109-3.

Introduction

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), which consist of two or more fused benzene rings, are discharged into the environment through oil spills and exhaust gases generated from the combustion of fossil fuels and organic matter (Miki et al., 2014; Ravindra et al., 2008). Owing to human activities, they ultimately reach coastal ecosystems and settle in sediments in high concentrations, especially near major cities with dense populations (Dai et al., 2022). The attachment of one or more oxygen atoms to PAHs produces oxygenated PAHs (oxy-PAHs), which are generated not only through the combustion of organic matter but also by the photochemical and chemical oxidation of PAHs and microbial degradation (Qiao et al., 2022). Currently, information on oxy-PAHs in the coastal environment is limited (Qiao et al., 2022), with only a few reports on oxy-PAHs distributions in the water column and atmosphere in Japan (Kojima et al., 2010; Kurihara et al., 2005). This means that the level of oxy-PAHs pollution in aquatic sediments remains unknown.

The effects of oxy-PAHs on aquatic organisms have been previously reported. For example, the exposure of Japanese medaka (Oryzias latipes) embryos to oxy-PAHs (acenaphthenequinone, 7,12-benz(a)anthraquinone, 1,4-naphthoquinone and 9,10-phenanethrenequinone) caused malformations in the larvae (Kawano et al., 2017). Developmental and cardiovascular abnormalities in zebrafish (Danio rerio) embryos exposed to benzo[a]fluorenone, 4H-cyclopenta[def]phenanthren-4-one, and 6H-benzo[cd]pyren-6-one have been observed (Cunha et al., 2020). Other effects include genotoxicities in larvae exposed to 7,12-benz(a)anthraquinone (Dasgupta et al., 2014), and abnormalities for protein biosynthesis, mitochondrial functions, neural developments, vascular developments, and cardiac functions in zebrafish (Elie et al., 2015). Notably, some oxy-PAHs may cause toxicity at lower concentrations than the parent PAHs (Cunha et al., 2020; McCarrick et al., 2019).

Recently, the importance of evaluating the effects of chemicals in polluted sediments on benthic organisms has been increasingly recognized (e.g., Leppanen et al., 2024; OECD, 2023). Several research groups have developed an evaluation method using fish embryos, spiking target chemicals into artificial sediments with only a small amount of pore water and without upper-layer water (Barjhoux et al., 2012; Vicquelin et al., 2011). This approach allowed for the evaluation of the effects caused by chemicals that are dissolved in pore water and absorbed by sediments, as the upper water layer, which may have dissolved the chemicals, was not introduced. The toxicities estimated using this method can be used to estimate the risks in field sediments from the distributions of the target chemicals.

In contrast with PAHs, the distribution of oxy-PAHs remains underexplored, although their toxicological profiles are comparable to those of their parent PAHs. In this context, the present study aimed to elucidate the distribution of oxy-PAHs and examine their similarity to parent PAHs in sediments of Tokyo Bay, a coastal region that has been extensively monitored for dioxins, UV filters, and other various pollutants (e.g., Hosomi et al., 2003; Tsui et al., 2015). In addition, the toxicity of oxy-PAHs spiked into artificial sediments was assessed using fish embryos, and their risk distributions in sediments were estimated.

Materials and methods

Target oxy-PAHs

For this study, target oxy-PAHs were 1,4-naphthoquinone (NaQ), benzophenone (BP), 9-fluorenone (FluO), xanthone (Xat), anthrone (AntO), 9,10-anthraquinone (AQ), 2-methylanthraquinone (MAQ), 2,3-dimethylanthraquinone (Dant), benzanthrone (BA), 7,12-benz(a)anthraquinone (BAQ), and 1,4-chrysenequinone (ChrQ) (Fig. 1). NaQ, FluO, AntO, AQ, MAQ, BA, BAQ, and ChrQ were purchased from Tokyo Kasei Kogyo Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). BP, Xat, and Dant were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Co., LLC. (St. Louis, MO, USA). These target oxy-PAHs were selected because they have been reported in previous studies (e.g., Sola et al., 2022; Zeng et al., 2021) and were readily available as commercial standards.Fig. 1Target oxy-PAHs in this monitoring

Collection and sediment treatment

Tokyo Bay lies between two major industrial zones in Japan, with the Keihin Industrial Area extending from Tokyo to Kanagawa prefecture including Yokohama and Kawasaki cities, and the Keiyo Industrial Area, which are mainly Chiba prefecture including Chiba, Funabashi, and Kisarazu cities (Fig. 2). The bay is subject to significant anthropogenic influence arising from heavy vehicle traffic along its western shore expressways and intensive maritime vessel activity within its waters.Fig. 2Locations of sampling sites in Tokyo Bay

Locations of sampling sites in Tokyo Bay

Sediments from 18 sites in Tokyo Bay were collected using an Ekman-Birge sampler (Fig. 2, Table S1) in December 2018. They passed through a 1 mm sieve to remove trash, gravel, shells, and other larger materials. Subsequently, excess water from the treated sediments was removed by centrifugation (760 × g, 10 min), and the sediments were completely dried at room temperature. The dried sediments were stored at –20˚C until further analysis.

Total carbon (TC) measurement

The TC content of the sampled sediments was measured using a CHN Micro Coder (JM10, J-Science Lab Co., Ltd.). Measurements were conducted in duplicate for each site (n = 2), and the concentrations were presented as the mean percentage of dry samples.

Oxy-PAH and PAH analysis in sediments

Analytical procedure for oxy-PAHs

One gram of the dried sediment was ultrasonically extracted with 20 mL of dichloromethane for 15 min, centrifuged at 760 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. This procedure was repeated twice, and the collected extracts were combined. The solution was concentrated until approximately 0.5 mL with a nitrogen stream. Approximately 1.2 g of 3% hydrous silica gel (packed in a 9-inch Pasteur pipette) washed with 10 mL of dichloromethane. The concentrated extract was loaded into the column, and target oxy-PAHs were subsequently eluted with 10 mL of dichloromethane. Copper chips were added to the eluent to remove sulfur components. This was allowed to react for half a day, after which the chips were removed. Naphthalene-d8, anthracene-d10, pyrene-d10, and chrysene-d12 were spiked as internal standards for measured on GC/MS and concentrated to 0.1 or 1 mL using a nitrogen stream. The oxy-PAHs were measured using GC/MS (GC: Agilent 7890 A, MS: 5975 C, Agilent Technologies) equipped with a DB-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 µm film thickness, Agilent Technologies). The measurement conditions for oxy-PAHs, excluding ChrQ, were as follows: 290˚C injector temperature; 80˚C oven temperature maintained for 1 min and increasingly raised to 300˚C at a rate of 5˚C/min, and then maintained 300˚C for 15 min. The temperatures of ion source and quadrupole were 230˚C and 150˚C, respectively. Under splitless mode, a 1 µL solution was injected. With regard to sensitivity, only ChrQ was measured under other conditions for the temperatures of injection and oven in GC. The injector temperature was set to 300˚C. The oven temperature was kept at 60˚C for 1 min, raised to 300˚C at a rate of 30˚C/min, then maintained for 5 min. A 1 µL solution was also injected with the pulsed splitless mode. The mean concentrations of the Σoxy-PAHs were estimated from two subsamples (n = 2) at each site. Before measurements, we confirmed the recovery rates of target oxyPAHs through the analytical procedures using quartz sand spiking each oxyPAHs. The rates ranged from 77 to 133%, except for FluO. The recovery of 133%, which was shown MAQ, slightly exceeds the generally acceptable range. However, that was reproducible, and we decided to allow this in the present study. The recovery of 165% for Fluo; therefore, the concentrations detected in sediments were treated as reference values. Each substance had detection limits ranging from 3.00 to 30.0 ng/g dry weight (Table 1).

Analytical procedures for PAHs

Analysis of the target PAHs, which were the 16 priority PAHs of the US EPA, was conducted using a modified method by Uno et al. (2010). One gram of dried sediment sample was ultrasonically extracted using 20 mL of dichloromethane/hexane mixture (1:1, v/v) and centrifuged at 760 × g for 15 min. This procedure was repeated twice, and the extracts were combined. The concentrated extract was added to 1 M potassium hydrate-ethanol and saponified under 90˚C for 1 h. The solution was washed with Milli-Q water, and the target PAHs were extracted using a dichloromethane-hexane mixture (1:3, v/v). Approximately 1.2 g of 3% hydrous silica gel (packed in a Pasteur pipette) was washed 10 mL of hexane. The concentrated extract was loaded into the column, and the target PAHs were eluted with 10 mL of hexane, followed by 10 mL of 3% acetone–hexane. The eluents were combined and left on the copper chips for half a day. Naphthalene-d8, anthracene-d10, pyrene-d10, and chrysene-d12 were used as the internal standards. PAHs were measured using the same GC/MS and column as for the oxy-PAHs measurements. The injector was set 280˚C. The oven temperature of the column was incrementally increased as follows: 60 °C for 1 min; 60 °C to 190 °C at a rate of 15 °C/min, maintained at 190 °C for 3 min; 190 °C to 220 °C at a rate of 2 °C/min, maintained at 220 °C for 1 min; 220 °C to 300 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min, and then maintained at 300 °C for 20 min. The temperature of the ion source was 230˚C and the quadrupole was 150˚C. The solutions for 1 µL was injected under the splitless mode. The mean PAHs concentrations were estimated from two subsamples (n = 2) at each site. The recovery rates for the analyses ranged from 84 to 121%, and their detection limits ranged from 0.01 to 1.00 ng/g dry weight (Table 2).

Oxy-PAHs risk assessment using artificial sediments

Toxicity tests were conducted using the sea water medaka, Java medaka (Oryzias javanicus), to evaluate the risks of oxy-PAHs on the benthic organisms at each sediment sampling site. Although Java medaka are not benthic fish, their embryos are demersal eggs, and, therefore, we used the embryos as model benthic organisms.

Java medaka culturing and spawning

Java medaka, which have been maintained in Faculty of Fisheries, Kagoshima University, for over two decades, were used in this study. A total of 27 adult Java medaka (male-to-female ratio of 1:2) were maintained in a glass tank (45 cm × 30 cm × 30 cm) containing approximately 30 L of seawater kept at 27 ± 1˚C. The photoperiod was set at 14L:10D. Fish were fed a few times daily with Otohime C1 (Marubeni Nissin Feed Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and live Artemia sp. Approximately 30% of the rearing water was replaced each morning, which coincided with the collection of the spawned eggs. The eggs were incubated at 27˚C for approximately 5 h, and normally developing embryos at the early blastula stage were selected for evaluation.

Oxy-PAHs toxicity evaluation using artificial sediments

In the present study, we selected NaQ and BAQ as the target oxy-PAHs in sediments because they have been previously demonstrated to exhibit significant embryonic toxicity in fish (Kawano et al., 2017). Artificial sediments were prepared according to OECD test guideline 218 (TG218) (OECD, 2023) with slight modifications. The artificial sediments were composed of 73% w/w quartz sand, 20% w/w kaolin, 7% w/w peat, 0.7% w/w CaCO3, and 20% v/w artificial seawater (Lyman & Fleming, 1940). Before mixing, either NaQ or BAQ was dissolved in acetone and spiked to 10% (w/w) of quartz sand prepared in advance. The mixture was homogenized and dried under a draft hood until the solvent completely evaporated. The dried quartz sand was then mixed with the remaining quartz sand and other components to prepare the spiked sediments. The nominal concentrations of NaQ and BAQ in the sediments were 0.33, 1, 3, 9, and 27 µg/g dry weight and 3, 9, 27, 80, and 240 µg/g dry weight, respectively. Their concentrations in the spiked sediments were analyzed as described in the section, “Analytical procedure for oxy-PAHs”. Artificial sediments without BAQ or NaQ spikes were used in the control group.

The embryos were exposed to the oxy-PAHs in the sediments following the methods described by Vicquelin et al. (2011) and Uno et al. (2017). Sediments spiked with NaQ or BAQ were placed in a 30 mm glass petri dish with a height of approximately 5 mm. A polypropylene mesh (approximately 2 mm) was placed on the sediment. Ten embryos were placed in a Petri dish within a mesh reticulation, with half of each embryo buried in the sediment. The dishes were not filled with an upper layer of water, and instead covered with a glass cap and sealed with Parafilm to maintain moisture in sediments. Petri dishes were incubated at 27˚C for 12 days post fertilization (dpf). Subsequently, the embryos were transferred to the wells of a 48-well microplate containing approximately 1 mL of artificial seawater without oxy-PAHs. The microplates were incubated at 27 °C until the third day after hatching. The effects of oxy-PAH exposure to the embryos were primarily evaluated based on abnormalities in hatching larvae and the time needed to hatch. Exposures were conducted in triplicate (n = 3).

Risk assessment

Environmental risk assessments of the oxy-PAHs in the collected sediments were conducted by calculating the Risk Quotient (RQ) using the following equation:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$RQ=MEC/{PNEC}_{sed}$$\end{document}RQ=MEC/PNECsedwhere MEC represents the measured concentrations of NaQ or BAQ in the sediments, and PNECsed denotes the predicted no-effect concentration (PNEC) in the sediments.

The PNECsed was derived from the median lethal concentration (LC50) obtained from toxicity tests using spiked sediments dividing by an assessment factor of 100. According to the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare et al. (2023), when calculating PNECsed, if a chronic toxicity value is available, the no-observed-effect concentration (NOEC) or effective concentration at 10% (EC10) should be utilized. However, NOEC and EC10 could overestimate the toxicity risk, as these concentrations vary according to the observed phenotype and the experimental system used. On the contrary, LC50 is based on mortality, so that concentration in the test system should be similar when using the same organisms. Further, in this study, the effective concentration (EC) was calculated based on the frequency of malformations in hatching larvae. However, it remains uncertain whether these malformations would directly translate into effects at the population or ecosystem level. Mortality, in contrast, directly affects population maintenance and is therefore expected to be of ecological relevance. LC50 was therefore used in this study.

Statistical and data analysis

R software (version 4.3.0) was used for statistical analysis (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The differences in mortality, abnormality rates, and hatching times in embryos and larvae between the exposure and control groups were examined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Dunnett’s test (p < 0.05, p < 0.01). The effective concentration at 50% (EC50) and LC50 were estimated using the probit method (EcoTox, version 3.01.01).

Results and discussion

Oxy-PAH distributions

The distribution of oxy-PAHs in sediments collected from Tokyo Bay is presented in Table 1. The total concentrations obtained of Σoxy-PAHs ranged from 10.2 to 1299 ng/g dry weight. The highest concentrations of those were observed at St. 3, followed by stations 2 and 4, respectively. Notably, relatively high concentrations of BA were detected at stations 2 and 3 (St. 2: 126 ng/g dry weight; St. 3: 454 ng/g dry weight), and AQ at St. 4 (118 ng/g dry weight). Oxy-PAH detected at all sites was only BAQ. Meanwhile, MAQ was below the detection limit at all the sites. A comparison of concentrations by the number of aromatic rings at each site revealed that, except for stations 10, 14, and 16, the typical order was 4-aromatic rings > 3-rings > 2-rings. This trend of increasing oxy-PAHs concentrations with an increasing number of aromatic rings had been previously observed by Kong et al. (2023). In the remaining sites, the order was 3-rings > 4-rings > 2-rings, with AQ exhibiting the highest concentrations. Few studies have measured oxy-PAHs in sediments, and even when measured, the target substances varied among research groups. This complicates comparisons of oxy-PAHs concentrations across studies. Therefore, we compared the individual oxy-PAHs commonly measured in these studies. BAQ concentrations in coastal sediments have been reported in a few studies, such as in the Persian Gulf (not detected (N.D.) to 0.5 ng/g dry weight) (Bateni et al., 2022), and Taiwan Strait (< 0.01 to 3.74 ng/g dry weight) (Zeng et al., 2021). In comparison, the BAQ detected in sediments from Tokyo Bay was significantly higher, ranging from 5.03 to 213 ng/g dry weight (Table 1). The AQ concentrations in this study ranged from N.D. to 152 ng/g dry weight (Table 1), compared with < 0.06 to 48.9 ng/g in the Taiwan Strait (Zeng et al., 2021), 0.19–5.4 ng/g in the Persian Gulf (Bateni et al., 2022), and 130–3030 ng/g dry weight at Todos os Santos Bay in Brazil (Sola et al., 2022). In addition, Sola et al. (2022) also measured NaQ and BA at Todos os Santos Bay. The BA at all their sites was below the detection limit, whereas NaQ concentrations were higher (210–620 ng/g dry weight) than those in Tokyo Bay (Table 1).

PAH distributions

Concentrations of the 16 PAHs ranged from 1.57 to 43,689 ng/g dry weight (Table 2). The highest concentration (43,689 ng/g dry weight) was observed at St. 3, which also exhibited the highest concentrations of oxy-PAHs. According to the classification of Baumard et al. (1998), which evaluated the PAH pollutions, the total concentrations of 16 PAHs can be categorized as low (0–100 ng/g), moderate (100–1000 ng/g), high (1000–5000 ng/g), and very high (> 5000 ng/g). Based on these classifications, five sites in Tokyo Bay were categorized as low, seven sites as moderate, four sites as high, and two sites as very high. Sources of PAHs

PAHs are often characterized by comparing their ratios with various substances to determine their sources (e.g., Idowu et al., 2020). In this study, we examined the origins of PAHs in Tokyo Bay sediments by employing the following ratio: [PAHs with low molecular weight (2–3 aromatic rings)]/[PAHs with heavy molecular weight PAH (4–6 aromatic rings)] (LMW/HMW) (Rocher et al. (2004), Wang et al. (2006)); Anthracene/(Anthracene + Phenanthrene) (Yunker et al. (2002), Zhang et al. (2017)); Fluoranthene/(Fluoranthene + Pyrene) (Yunker et al. (2002), Zhang et al.(2017)); and Benz(a)anthracene/(Benz(a)anthracene + Chrysene) (Zhang et al. (2017)) (Table S2).

The LMW/HMW ratio can be classified as originating from pyrogenic sources when the ratio is < 1, and from petrogenic sources when the ratio is > 1. In this study, it was below 1 across most of the sampling sites, indicating that the PAHs detected in sediments were predominantly of pyrogenic origin. No detectable LMW compounds were found at stations 7, 9, and 15. The Anthracene/(Anthracene + Phenanthrene) ratio indicates a petrogenic source when the value is < 0.1, and those > 0.1 indicate combustion. The ratio values for most of the stations in this study are > 0.1, except for stations 11 and 12. The ratio of Fluoranthene/(Fluoranthene + Pyrene) suggests a petrogenic source when the value is < 0.4, petroleum combustion when it ranges from 0.4 to 0.5, and wood, grass, and/or coal combustion when it exceeds 0.5. In this study, no sites in Tokyo Bay exhibited a ratio below 0.4, whereas five sites (stations 1–5) fell within the 0.4 to 0.5 range and 10 sites (stations 8 and 10–18) exceeded 0.5. The Benz(a)anthracene/(Benz(a)anthracene + Chrysene) ratio indicates pyrogenic sources at values > 0.35. This result was observed for most stations in this study, except for stations 1, 6, 7, 9, and 15, where no detectable compounds were found.

These indicators suggest that a majority of the PAHs detected in sediments of Tokyo Bay originated from pyrogenic sources. The highest concentration was observed at St. 3, located within a canal area surrounded by numerous industrial facilities. This suggests local accumulation of combustion-derived PAHs, likely originating from exhaust emissions associated with nearby factories, vessel traffic within the canal, and road traffic such as trucks in the surrounding industrial area. Overall, Tokyo Bay encompasses an extensive industrial area, particularly the Keihin (around Kawasaki city) and Keiyo (around Kisarazu city) regions, which dominate the shorelines of the eastern and western sections of the bay. Additionally, several highways with heavy traffic are located along its west side. These industrial and vehicular activities likely contribute to the observed PAHs contaminations in sediments.

PAHs and oxy-PAHs correlation with total carbon

In Tokyo Bay, the total carbon (TC) content ranged from 0.20% to 5.86% (Table S1). Several studies have reported significant correlations between carbon content and PAHs (e.g., Li et al., 2012; Wu et al., 2012). Therefore, we also examined the relationship between Σ16PAHs and TC in this study using the Pearson correlation analysis. Although no significant correlation was found between the untransformed Σ16PAHs concentration and TC content (r = 0.40, p > 0.05, Fig. S1a), a strong positive correlation emerged after log transformation of PAHs concentrations (r = 0.80, p < 0.001, Fig. S1b). This result suggests PAHs concentrations may increase exponentially with increasing TC. The exponential relationship between PAHs concentrations and carbon contents in sediments has also been reported by Viguri et al. (2002).

We further examined whether oxy-PAHs and PAHs exhibit similar relationships with carbon content. Significant positive correlations were found between Σoxy-PAHs concentrations and TC contents both before (r = 0.70, p < 0.01) and after log transformation (r = 0.86, p < 0.001). Similar to PAHs, which showed a strong correlation with TC contents, Σoxy-PAHs were also showed a strong correlation with TC contents. Further analysis of individual oxy-PAHs according to the number of aromatic rings was conducted, since several compounds were undetected in more than half of them. The results observed significant correlations with TC content and 2-ring compounds (NaQ and BP) (r = 0.80, p < 0.001), 3-ring compounds (FluO, Xat, AntO, AQ, and Dant) (r = 0.83, p < 0.001), and 4-ring compounds (BA, BAQ, and ChrQ) (r = 0.59, p < 0.05). In addition, 4-ring compounds showed a stronger positive correlation with TC content after log transformation (r = 0.85, p < 0.001). Although 4-ring oxy-PAHs accounted for the majority of total concentrations, the relationship with TC was not based on compounds with specific ring numbers. Although oxy-PAHs are generally more hydrophilic than their parent PAHs (Idowu et al., 2019), they appear to have a similar relationship with TC in sediments. These findings suggest that the distribution of oxy-PAHs in sediments is influenced by carbon content, as observed for PAHs. However, the relationship between oxy-PAHs and TC remains poorly understood and requires further investigation.

Oxy-PAHs association with parent PAHs

Oxy-PAHs are known to be produced not only by the incomplete combustion of fossil fuels and diesel exhaust emissions, but also through photooxidation, chemical oxidation, and biological transformation of their parent PAHs (e.g., Qiao et al., 2022; Nocun & Schantz, 2013). However, reports on oxy-PAHs in sediments remain limited, and their relationship and relative abundance between oxy-PAHs and parent PAHs in sediments remain poorly understood. So, we examined the relationships between parent PAHs and oxy-PAHs in sediment. A comparative analysis of their concentrations may provide valuable insights into compound-specific sedimentary associations between oxy-PAHs and their parent PAHs.

Each PAH was paired with its corresponding oxy-PAH that shared the same aromatic ring structure (naphthalene (NP) and NaQ, fluorene (Flu) and FluO, anthracene (Ant) and AQ, benz(a)anthracene (BaA) and BAQ, and chrysene (Chr) and ChrQ) and their sediment concentrations were compared using Pearson’s correlation analysis. Significant positive correlations were observed for Flu and FluO (r = 0.69, p < 0.01), Ant and AQ (r = 0.52, p < 0.05), BaA and BAQ (r = 0.86, p < 0.001), Chr and ChrQ (r = 0.97, p < 0.001). On the other hand, NP and NaQ showed on significant relationship (r = − 0.28, p > 0.05). These correlations suggested that these oxy-PAHs are closely associated with their parent PAHs within sediments despite site-specific variations. Zeng et al. (2021) reported that no positive correlation between Ant and AQ in the Taiwan Strait, differing from the finding of this study. Such inconsistencies may result from surrounding industries and ocean currents. Among the measured oxy-PAHs, the octanol–water partition coefficients (XLogP3; obtained from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/)) ranged from 3.2 to 4.3, except for NaQ, which exhibited the lowest value of 1.7. This low hydrophobicity suggests that even if NaQ derived from NP, it may not remain in sediments and may instead readily diffuse into the overlying seawater. The lack of a significant correlation between concentrations of NP and NaQ can be attributed to this behavior.

Effect and lethal concentrations of oxy-PAHs in embryos

Traditionally, toxicity tests of chemical substances predominantly utilize freshwater species such as Japanese medaka, zebrafish, and freshwater amphipods (Hayella azteca) (e.g., OECD, 2025). However, the sensitivity to chemical substances may notably differ between freshwater and marine species (Leung et al., 2001). Consequently, the evaluation of marine organisms is more appropriate for assessing the effects of chemicals in marine environments. In the present study, we employed the embryos of Java medaka (Oryzias javanicus), a marine species frequently used as a test fish, to evaluate chemical toxicity (e.g., Imai et al., 2005).

The equilibrium partitioning method is frequently employed to assess the chemical risks in sediments, with risks often predicted based on toxicity values obtained from water exposure (Di Toro et al., 1991). However, because the behavior of chemicals can vary in a complicated manner depending on the chemical characteristics of the aqueous and sediment phases in a real environment, accurate evaluation is not always possible. Therefore, in this study, embryos were directly exposed to oxy-PAHs spiked in sediments to evaluate their toxicity.

The effects of exposing embryos to sediments spiked with NaQ and BAQ are shown in Table 3. In the control group, less than 10% of the embryos exhibited slight mortality, and no larvae with malformations were observed. For NaQ, concentrations exceeding 1.02 µg/g resulted in greater than 20% mortality at the embryonic stage. Notably, all individuals in the 4.43 µg/g groups died within 5 days of starting the exposure. The abnormalities in hatched larvae particularly increased in the 1.02 µg/g group, inducing irregular head shapes and spinal curvature (Fig. S2b). In the 0.23 µg/g exposure group, which did not exhibit mortality and malformation of larvae, but a significant hatching delay was observed (p < 0.01). BAQ exposure reduced embryo hatching rate in a concentration-dependent manner. In particular, the number of hatched larvae remarkably decreased at concentrations exceeding 4.04 µg/g, and complete mortality was observed in the 31.6 µg/g exposure group. During embryonic development following BAQ exposure, adverse effects such as blood clots, sunken yolks, and tubular heart formation were observed. Furthermore, greater than 30% of larvae exposed to concentrations exceeding 4.04 µg/g exhibited malformations, including tubular heart, spinal curvature, abnormal head formation, and yolk-sac edema (Fig. S2c), despite being successfully hatched. Toxicities caused by the exposure of aquatic organisms to NaQ and BAQ have been reported, especially in freshwater organisms. Kawano et al. (2017) exposed NaQ and BAQ to Japanese medaka (Oryzias latipes) by introducing these compounds in the test water. They observed significant embryonic mortality and altered axial curvature. In particular, NaQ (> 74.3 µg/L) exposure resulted in an unabsorbed yolk sac and an undeveloped caudal fin, whereas BAQ (> 22.4 µg/L) led to incomplete cephalic region development, pericardial edema, tubular heart formation, altered axial curvature, and defective tail formation in hatching larvae (Kawano et al., 2017). In the study of Elie et al. (2015), the metabolomic analysis of zebrafish (Danio rerio) embryos exposed to BAQ (1033 µg/L) revealed disruptions in protein biosynthesis, mitochondrial function, neural development, vascular development, and cardiac function. In summary, the findings of the present study demonstrate that NaQ and BAQ are toxic to Java medaka, consistent with observations in other organisms where toxicity has been reported. Le Bihanic et al. (2015) exposed Japanese medaka to artificial sediments spiked with BaA, the parent PAH of BAQ, and reported a Lowest Observed Effect Concentration (LOEC) value of 0.92 µg/g based on developmental defects and other results. The LOEC value for BAQ in this study was 4.04 µg/g, so BAQ showed lower toxicity than BaA, although the fish species differed. Kawano et al. (2017) compared the toxicity of NaQ with previously reported values for NP, its parent PAH, and concluded that NaQ is equally or more toxic than NP. This finding suggests that NaQ may also show comparable or greater toxicity in sediments.

In the present study, as the frequency of anomalies in hatched larvae for BAQ and NaQ was less than 50%, EC50 estimation was not possible. In particular, for BAQ, only two larvae hatched at 10.4 µg/g, making the calculation of EC50 impossible. The LC50 values were 1.63 µg/g for NaQ and 3.28 µg/g for BAQ.

Oxy-PAHs risk assessment using PNECsed

Although previous studies as well as the present study have gradually elucidated the distribution of oxy-PAHs in sediments, their harmful effects on ecosystems remain inadequately understood. The RQ method is regarded as the most suitable approach for evaluating environmental risks to aquatic organisms (Liu et al., 2018). In this study, the RQ was estimated by comparing the measured concentrations of oxy-PAHs in sediments from Tokyo Bay and the PNEC derived from toxicity tests with NaQ or BAQ spiked into sediments. The PNEC and RQ values are listed in Table 4 and Table S3. The RQ index exceeded 1, suggesting that the concentrations of NaQ and BAQ adversely affect the organisms that inhabit sediments in Tokyo Bay. In the present study, 11 sites exhibited NaQ with RQ > 1 (Fig. 3a). BAQ with RQ > 1 was observed at 13 sites, predominantly on the western side of the bay where concentrations were higher than those on the eastern side (Fig. 3b). The western area of the bay contains a large industrial district and is influenced by the Tama River, which flows through Tokyo—Japan’s largest metropolitan area. In addition to the pollutant loads from the district, the river may transport traffic exhaust-related oxy-PAHs into this part of the bay. In contrast, the eastern area also contains a large industrial zone, although its industrial characteristics likely differ from those of the western area. Population density is also lower in the eastern area of the bay than in the western area. The high BAQ risk observed in the western area may therefore be attributed to differences in emission sources between the eastern and western sides of Tokyo Bay. BAQ has an XlogP3 value at 3.9, making it more hydrophobic than NaQ. We expect that BAQ didn’t spread in seawater but remained in high residual concentrations in the sediment of the western side, resulting in a higher RQ. Conversely, BAQ sites with an RQ < 1 were distributed in low TC areas (0.20–1.11, Table S1). Although the RQ values for other oxy-PAHs may also be > 1 at many locations within Tokyo Bay, the results of this study suggested that the risk posed by oxy-PAHs in sediments may depend on both the source of discharge and the amount of carbon.

As shown in Section “PAHs and oxy-PAHs correlation with total carbon”, sediments with higher TC contents tend to accumulate greater hydrophobic contamination, resulting in higher RQs when using measured field concentrations. However, the partitioning of these compounds between the sediment solids and pore water remains poorly understood. For example, the bioavailability and toxicity of PAHs in sediments may be influenced by the amount of organic matter and the Kow of the compounds (Perrichon et al., 2014). Therefore, in field sediments with variable carbon contents, the relationship between the environmental concentration of chemical substances and their actual biological effects is likely to be complex. Although residual oxy-PAHs in Tokyo Bay sediments may pose ecological risks, further investigations into solid–water partitioning and bioavailability are required for accurate risk assessments.

Conclusion

In this study, we examined the distribution of oxy-PAHs in Tokyo Bay sediments and evaluated their potential risks to benthic organisms. Concentrations of some oxy-PAHs tended to be relatively higher than those reported in previous studies, and these concentrations appeared to correlate with the carbon content and parent PAHs concentrations in sediments. The risk assessment based on toxicity tests using spiked sediments suggests that oxy-PAHs pose potential risks that are likely widespread to benthic organisms in Tokyo Bay. These results demonstrate the importance of monitoring oxy-PAHs in coastal sediments, providing new insights into the risks posed by chemicals to benthic organisms.
