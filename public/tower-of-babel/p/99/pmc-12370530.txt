Achieving a rapid and continuous learning health system requires socio-technical harmonization of research and operational IT
Authors: Cong Liu, Casey N. Ta, Elisabeth S. DiMaria, Rachel C. Shelton, Karthik Natarajan, Soumitra Sengupta, James J. Cimino, Chunhua Weng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This perspective identifies challenges in integrating clinical research and care delivery in a rapid and continuous Learning Health System (LHS), focusing on Information technology (IT) systems and workforce harmonization. Based on our experiences, we analyzed differences in funding mechanisms, governance, regulatory frameworks, priorities, workforce culture, technology architecture, risk tolerance, and data standardization. We highlight significant barriers to the LHS implementation caused by IT misalignment between clinical research and patient care and propose strategies to overcome these challenges.

Introduction

Imagine the following scenario (Fig. 1): an ideal Electronic Health Record (EHR) system holding every patient’s genotype: researchers build a breast-cancer polygenic risk-score model, operational IT turns it into portal or SMS alerts for risk-based MRI screening, and the resulting data fuel the next model update. Although the concept of “Learning Health System” (LHS) is involving1, the example above is a typic one we would like to discuss in this perspective, as it involves three phases: learning—using data to generate knowledge (or prediction model in the case above) through large-scale observational studies; implementation—applying that knowledge into clinical workflows using implementation science; and assessment—evaluating the effectiveness of these implementations (often through programmatic trials). The literature documents hurdles in data quality2–6, governance, workforce7,8, and effectiveness assessment9–11 for a rapid, digital, continuous, and automated LHS cycle. A definition of research IT, operational IT, and their interaction in an LHS cycle

Conventional research IT offers researcher-clinicians standardized computing, storage, software, and security, overlapping operational IT to ensure uniformity and stability. Yet, it is increasingly customized. For instance, while a REDCap virtual machine is institution-managed, project-specific syncing or custom plugins land with research IT specialists. Virtualized and cloud tools further blur responsibility boundaries. In this perspective, research IT refers to project-specific informatics teams and systems focused on conducting observational studies, developing innovative analytics or AI models, and iterating quickly with minimal documentation or downtime restraints. Operational IT denotes enterprise clinical IT teams and institutional-level clinical research computing teams that run standardized, vendor-supported systems, enforce stringent security and regulatory controls, and deliver 24/7 reliability for patient care, billing, and institution-wide IT infrastructure. Because modern clinical IT systems often consist of multiple components and commercial solutions, operational IT teams frequently work closely with industry partners (e.g., Epic).

Let’s use a hypothetical example (i.e., breast PRS in Fig. 1) to illustrate how these two interact within the context of an LHS. During the learning phase, enterprise clinical IT collaborates closely with the institutional clinical research computing team to create periodic snapshots of the EHR (e.g., Epic), which are transferred to a data warehouse or data lake, using enterprise-level technology solutions (e.g., Snowflake, Azure Synapse, or Databricks). An Extract-Transform-Load (ETL) team under operational IT converts this data into standardized formats (e.g., OMOP or i2b2). Genotype (though not yet widely integrated), may aslo be collected (e.g. via Epic Genomic Module). From there, individual research teams, including IT specialists and data scientists, work on specific subsets of the data relevant to their research topic (e.g., breast cancer PRS). A copy of this subset can be migrated to a PHI-compliant computing server “owned by” the research project, or researchers may “rent” institutional computing services to conduct large-scale observational studies. Researchers may also integrate additional research-specific data, such as research-generated genotype data in the example above. A cohort of breast cancer cases and controls will be defined using billing codes, and genotype data will be used to train and validate a PRS model for breast cancer prediction. Once the optimal PRS is developed and evaluated (often published in literature), it transitions to the implementation phase. While implementation involves multiple stakeholders, from an IT perspective, complex models like PRS prediction often require custom system architectures tailored to the specific project’s data sources, clinical workflows, and operational constraints. Close coordination is required to address several technical challenges: determining the model’s hosting environment (EHR-integrated, external server, or cloud platform), designing triggers for real-time or scheduled execution within clinical workflows, extracting data (e.g. genotype) from operational systems and plugging it to the model, and embedding risk predictions into clinical interfaces (e.g., alerts, dashboards, or notes). Some of these changes involve modifications not only to institution-owned or homegrown infrastructures but also to vendor-managed software (Epic Genomic Module), requiring collaboration with external vendors to enable such functionality. Additional considerations include provisioning computing resources with cost-efficiency, setting up monitoring, error handling, and recovery protocols, implementing version control and audit logging, and ensuring full compliance with security, privacy, and regulatory requirements. If (luckily) the PRS is successfully implemented, it moves into the assessment phase, where the effectiveness of these interventions is often evaluated using implementation science frameworks, such as RE-AIM12. To assess performance, outcome data (e.g., breast cancer incidence, patient engagement, and changes in medical recommendations) should be defined by frontline healthcare workers (HCWs), extracted from the EHR or data lake by the operational team, and analyzed by researchers. In some cases, these assessments are part of non-research quality improvement activities, which are generally managed by the Quality Improvement (QI) office, which is governed at the institutional level. It’s important to note that operational IT or QI leaders (e.g., C-suite officers) may also take on a research role. In such cases, they and their team may also function as project-specific research IT. Finally, the PRS model will be periodically retrained and refined based on newly collected data, completing the continuous learning cycle central to an LHS.

Perspective distinctions between research and operational health IT

Funding

One of the biggest differences we observed is the centralization of operational IT compared to the decentralized nature of research IT. Investment in centralized operational IT infrastructure is substantial, with minimal changes over time. Once implemented, these systems require ongoing maintenance, often with involvement of industrial vendors, and have a low tolerance for downtime, necessitating a significant and continuous funding stream (e.g., through clinical revenue or collective indirect costs from various external grants) and governance by institutional committees. In contrast, research IT systems are decentralized, typically funded by external grants (i.e., direct costs) and ‘owned’ by Principal Investigators (PIs). While operational IT represents a permanent infrastructure investment, research IT is often a temporary development. From an LHS perspective, continuous learning and refinement are essential. Therefore, relying solely on external funding to initiate an LHS can be problematic without institutional commitment to long-term support. However, depending exclusively on institutional-level support may stifle innovation, as individual researchers may be better positioned to identify research problems more accurately than decision-makers focused on broader priorities.

Regulatory and governance

Research is governed by Institutional Review Boards (IRBs), whose current regulations reflect historical abuses of research ethics13. An LHS approach often requires changes to clinical workflows and system architecture when applying research-generated evidence or models, impacting those not involved in the research. While pragmatic trials are becoming more common, a full LHS typically involves both observational studies and prospective trials, which are not usually covered under a single IRB protocol. Traditional IRBs ensure the proper conduct of research, but permissions to update patient records, modify system architecture, and change clinical workflows fall under various institution-wide clinical committees, IT surveillance groups, administrators, legal compliance officers, and, in some cases, require negotiations with vendors managing the commercial solutions. As a result, no single entity can make a final decision, and the entire approval process can become slow, requiring multiple overlapping approvals from committees that meet infrequently. This complexity hinders researchers, particularly those without decision-making authority, from achieving the rapid refinement and delivery needed for an effective LHS.

Technology and workforces

Research IT has traditionally operated locally, often sharing networks with educational or general-purpose infrastructure, while much of the operational infrastructure has transitioned to private cloud environments, which are sometimes encapsulated within their own private subnets. In addition, LHS initiatives, particularly those involving data science or AI, often require compute-intensive resources like GPUs; these setups will be considered by operational IT by factoring in the financial considerations. Beyond these technical differences, there is a cultural divide between the two workforces that can create friction. While the mission of operational IT is to provide reliable and consistent support to healthcare workers, researchers are primarily incentivized to publish their findings, whether as new knowledge or open-source tools. Given the iterative and experimental nature of research, frequent changes in the computing environment are common. Cutting-edge algorithms are often designed to optimize performance without the immediate need for integration into real-world clinical workflows. Software engineering practices14 such as issue tracking (including third-party), cost-efficiency optimization, comprehensive testing, scalability assessments, documentation management, and module reusability, are typically not prioritized in research. As a result, operational IT teams, accustomed to industry-standard and commercial software development, and often working closely with vendors, may become frustrated by the lack of standardized coding practices and technical documentation in research outputs. Conversely, researchers may feel uncomfortable with complex ticketing systems and the extensive documentation requirements. This disconnect—spanning technology, workforce skills, and culture—further complicates efforts to integrate the two IT systems for LHS projects.

Data standard

Another key difference lies in the adoption of data standards15. Although HL7v2 and FHIR are endorsed for health data exchange, elusive clinical data are difficult for research uses. While common data models like OMOP, i2b2, and PCORnet help streamline research, data standard mappings often involve institution-wide efforts managed by the ETL team. Without proper ETL knowledge, downstream researchers can make incorrect assumptions, leading to flawed conclusions. Furthermore, vocabulary usage can differ as well. research may require more granularity than clinical care (e.g., quantitative pain scales), or less (e.g., a checkbox for ‘history of cancer’). As an example, while the Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) is widely used in genomic research16, its adoption in EHRs is limited. Conversely, billing vocabularies like ICD and CPT may introduce bias and limit specificity in research. In LHS projects, these differences can create challenges when translating research findings or trained machine learning models into clinical applications, often requiring ‘reverse’ ETL efforts. In addition, the ‘continuous learning’ pipeline can also be disrupted when clinical data schemas are updated.

Barriers and recommendations to bridge the gap between research and operational IT for LHS projects

Harmonize the funding mechanism

The concept of “a rapid LHS” is not new to the tech industry. Tech giants have long employed data scientists, software engineers, and A/B testing to rapidly iterate products17. While tech companies are driven by profit, LHS projects in healthcare differ primarily due to distinct funding mechanisms. For LHS initiatives to thrive, institutions need to provide sustainable, long-term support for operational IT. This could explain why operational IT leadership is often reluctant to invest in research-initiated LHS projects, funded by external agencies, seeing them as “just another research IT endeavor.” To meet the IOM’s goal of achieving the LHS on a national scale3, federal funding agencies should establish a clear funding pathway. This includes continuous financial support, particularly in FTE budgeting for operational IT, similar to mechanisms like the Clinical and Translational Science Awards (CTSA)17. Institutions aiming to participate must develop comprehensive investment plans for LHS-specific operational IT infrastructure and workforce, demonstrating the potential benefits and return on investment.

Emphasize the assessment phase

While researchers focus on the “learning” phase and operational IT on the “implementation” phase, the “assessment” phase is often overlooked. We argued that an LHS should be initiated from the assessment phase. This initiation point facilitates the organic integration of research and operational ITs by healthcare workers (HCWs), who frequently assume the dual roles of clinician and researcher. While many LHS initiatives nominate HCW “champions” as beta testers, it could be more beneficial to have HCWs as co-designers of the evaluation framework rather than late-stage testers.” Such metrics, negotiated in partnership rather than imposed after the fact, keep the evaluation grounded in daily clinical realities (e.g., workflow burden, model explainability, health benefits, and patient satisfaction) rather than in publication priorities (on population-level performance)18,19. Successful examples include bedside nurses who worked iteratively to refine an Epic-integrated ambient-AI documentation app20, ICU nurses who turned a hand-drawn infection-control handover sheet into an EHR module built by the hospital’s IT team21, and physicians who reviewed false alarms each week and fed their notes into a reinforcement-learning loop that trimmed alert fatigue while keeping research updates aligned with live monitoring22. To foster meaningful HCW participation and counter publication-driven bias in purely research-funded LHS assessments, this work should be budgeted as a joint QI and research initiative, granting HCWs protected, compensated time (e.g., extra hours needed for AI-literacy, bias-recognition, and safe-override training) and formal credit in annual reviews. This likely explains why many successful LHS programs emerge as QI initiatives led by senior academic leaders who assume both clinical and scientific roles.

Establish a specialized regulatory framework

Not all LHSs are rapid. Simple problems can be swiftly identified, validated, and implemented with straightforward solutions. In contrast, complex issues, such as employing checklists to reduce medical errors23, require rigorous planning and meticulous execution. However, current regulatory bodies, focused on preventing disruptions in the healthcare system, lack clear guidelines to differentiate between these two scenarios. Establishing a specialized regulatory framework can help address this gap and facilitate the rapid implementation of solutions in appropriate contexts. Some institutions have treated rapid LHS projects as quality improvement (QI) initiatives24,25. In these cases, they are not classified as research by IRBs even if they involve (pragmatic) trials. Consequently, patients and clinicians are not typically able to opt out of participation, and no explicit consent is collected. However, the procedures and standards for determining whether an LHS qualifies as a QI initiative are unclear. In reality, the aim of those QI-LHS projects is often among the highest strategic priorities (e.g., sepsis prediction) of the institution2, driven top-down by organizational leadership to achieve rapid implementation progress. For smaller research teams, implementing LHS projects as QIs may prove infeasible without prior buy-in from leadership. Additionally, the “continuous learning” aspect of these QI-LHS initiatives is minimal, so that only innovations with a high likelihood of success or benefits will be considered. A universal effort to establish cross-institutional, transparent, and actionable criteria for defining QI initiatives, similar to the criteria for defining “what is a human subject research”, is needed. Furthermore, since a rapid LHS interacts with both research and operational systems, it is essential to include individuals with expertise in both areas on relevant review committees to streamline the review process.

Provide a testbed

Like any IT system, an LHS requires rigorous testing before going live. However, the nature of the LHS necessitates real-world utility testing. This is akin to the assertion by Soden et al. that some ethical issues can only be understood through real-world application26. Thus, determining how to test the system with minimal disruption to the health system while gaining real-world insights is crucial. A pressing need is to bridge the data standards between research and operations, with the ETL team playing a crucial role. Unfortunately, it is almost impossible for a single ETL team to close various data standardization gaps. Automation should be maximized to reduce the burden on operational IT while avoiding information loss. Initiatives like OMOP2FHIR and Clinical Data Interoperability Services in REDCap have emerged to facilitate clinical and research data exchange27,28. With BulkFHIR implemented, research IT should consider developing within the FHIR framework to enhance compatibility and standardize testing procedures for the LHS29. We argued that testbeds could help accelerate LHS development by focusing initial development on areas where data gaps are relatively small, enabling more rapid deployment while minimizing disruption. Some institutions have established a ‘virtual LHS testbed’ for individual research teams to conduct LHS-related research. This sandboxed system ideally would replicate the entire production system but without impacting the current operational environment30. It could be activated for randomization exclusively among participants who have consented to the study, without affecting non-participants25. From an operational perspective, using sandboxes to implement LHS innovations can significantly reduce adoption costs in production environments. However, the costs of maintaining these sandboxes can be discouraging. Given that several testing EHR instances are already developed and maintained for clinical training31, a potential solution is to repurpose those testing EHRs for the LHS software testing.

Engaging industry partners

EHR and digital-health vendors are indispensable to any rapid learning health system (LHS), and hospitals running Epic or Oracle Health cannot deploy new models without native hooks into those platforms. Luckily, vendor programs such as Epic Launchpad and Oracle-Cerner’s SMART-on-FHIR/CDS-Hooks sandboxes already provide controlled environments where research code can be hardened into production-grade extensions. However, Operational IT must act as the bridge, alerting researchers to these sandboxes, aligning build standards, and coordinating releases. A joint governance board that includes vendors, operational IT leads, and research PIs should (i) vet model provenance, (ii) define telemetry for post-deployment drift detection, and (iii) set licensing terms that reward both sides. To keep vendors engaged, incentive mechanisms should treat LHS participation as a product line, not a courtesy: streaming de-identified data to vendors for real-world-evidence services with revenue sharing, outcome-based subscription fees, co-owned IP rights that let vendors resell algorithms, fast-lane “LHS-Ready” certification with discounted app-store fees, and embedded DevOps “tiger teams” split between the vendor and hospital. These levers offer vendors predictable income, brand advantage, and early access to cutting-edge models. More importantly, industry partnerships are essential to address data standardization gaps, which may be the single biggest blocker for rapid learning health system (LHS) cycles. Even a successful academic-driven data standard like OHDSI’s OMOP CDM reflects strong academic-industry collaboration, ensuring broad adoption by connecting research needs with operational IT capabilities. While FHIR is an HL7 standard originating from academic/clinical communities, big tech now plays a large role in its cloud implementation and promotion (FHIR API on Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure, AWS). In genomics, standards like GA4GH APIs (e.g., htsget, refget, beacon) involve active collaboration between academic researchers and industry partners (such as Illumina, Google, Microsoft, and pharma companies), ensuring that large-scale sequencing data can be shared, accessed, and interpreted across institutions. More and more emerging standards (e.g., ONNX for AI models, model context protocol by Claudie) are increasingly shaped by industry leaders. Engaging industry allows LHS efforts to align with widely adopted technical frameworks on both the operational and research sides, leverage scalable infrastructure, and ensure interoperability across systems that academic institutions alone cannot control.

Bridge the cultures and skills of the two workforces

All these recommendations require a team with extensive knowledge of operational IT systems and workflows. Most academic health centers already offer training platforms with courses on topics like the IRB review process, HIPAA compliance, and Human Subjects Research. Adding courses focused on LHS topics—such as operational IT regulations and workflows—would significantly lower the entry barriers for research IT teams to develop LHS projects that are compatible with clinical workflows. Although many governance protocols are currently institution-specific, as LHSs mature and standardized practices are adopted, it will become easier to create a common curriculum on a national scale. This would allow individuals to transfer their skills between institutions, further accelerating the LHS adoption. Training is not only essential for researchers implementing LHS projects but may be even more crucial for regulatory personnel to assess risk levels accurately. The current approach, which relies heavily on monthly committee meetings with busy experts, could be streamlined by providing IT system training to IRB committee members, enabling a more efficient, one-stop review process. Additionally, agile practices32 should be applied throughout the entire lifecycle of LHS projects, with operational and research IT teams working closely together using techniques like pair programming, test-driven development, refactoring, and continuous integration to ensure rapid and iterative development. Institutions might consider establishing specialized Project Management Offices (PMOs) staffed with experienced professionals to serve as central hubs for coordinating collaboration among stakeholders, creating a cohesive ‘algorithm journey map’ from project inception2, involving coordinating efforts, aligning goals, unifying tech stacks, and outlining the roadmap.

Conclusions

Implementation is recognized as complex and challenging, particularly in the context of integrating constantly evolving (i.e., learning) IT systems into clinical workflow in a way that is both clinically valid and logistically feasible. This perspective has delineated the distinctions between operational and research IT systems and offered recommendations for bridging these gaps and addressing key challenges. We encourage a broader discussion on implementing a more effective, smart and sustainable LHS.
