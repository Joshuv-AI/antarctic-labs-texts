Nanomedicine in immunotherapy of urinary system tumors: advances, synergistic strategies, and translational challenges
Authors: Wenpeng Huang, Ayushi Chaudhary, Zhaonan Sun, Cynthia S Reuben, Sanjita Balaji, Amanda R Catey, Song Luo, Weibo Cai, Jessica C Hsu, Jian Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrological malignancies impose a substantial global health burden, underscoring the urgent need for more effective therapeutic strategies. Although immunotherapy has transformed cancer management, its benefit in urological tumors remains inconsistent, often limited by tumor-intrinsic resistance and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. In this setting, nanomedicine has emerged as a promising platform that extends beyond conventional drug delivery to actively modulate antitumor immune responses through engineered nano-bio interactions. This review presents a mechanistically organized overview of nanomedicine-enabled immunotherapeutic strategies for urological cancers, focusing on prostate cancer, renal cell carcinoma, and bladder cancer. We examine how advanced nanoplatforms regulate antitumor immunity through targeted delivery, externally activatable photodynamic immunotherapy, tumor immune microenvironment reprogramming, immunotherapy sensitization and combination strategies, and neoantigen–based vaccination. We also discuss key translational barriers—including delivery heterogeneity, biosafety, manufacturing scalability, and patient stratification—and outline design considerations for developing clinically translatable nano-based immunotherapeutic. Overall, this review highlights the potential of nanomedicine to overcome current limitations in cancer immunotherapy and to support the development of more effective, personalized treatments for urological malignancies.Graphical abstract Keywords: Nanodynamic therapy, Immunotherapy, Drug delivery, Kidney cancer, Prostate cancer, Bladder cancer

Introduction

Malignancies of the urinary system—most notably kidney, bladder, and prostate cancers (PCa)—are among the most prevalent cancers globally [1, 2]. Standard treatment options for urologic cancers include surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, hormone therapy, and immunotherapy; however, achieving durable disease control remains a major challenge [3]. Surgical resection can be curative but often fails to eradicate microscopic or occult lesions, increasing the risk of local recurrence and disease progression [4, 5]. Immunotherapy, encompassing immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs), cellular therapies, and cancer vaccines, has reshaped cancer treatment by harnessing the host immune system and has produced remarkable clinical successes across multiple solid tumors [6–14]. Nevertheless, immunotherapy outcomes remain highly variable. Therapeutic efficacy is often limited by tumor immune evasion [15, 16], systemic immune-related adverse events (irAEs), low objective response rates, suboptimal tumor targeting, and interpatient heterogeneity [17]. Consequently, overcoming immune resistance, reducing systemic toxicity, and achieving durable and effective antitumor immunity remain key unmet needs, especially for urological cancers, underscoring the need for innovative therapeutic strategies.

Nanomedicine has shown promise in addressing the challenges in disease prevention, detection, and treatment. In cancer immunotherapy, nanomedicine offers advantages by providing precise spatiotemporal control of immunotherapeutic delivery, coordinated modulation of tumor and immune compartments, and integration of multimodal treatment strategies [18–20]. Owing to their nanoscale size (typically 1–1000 nm), high surface reactivity, and tunable physicochemical properties, nanoparticles can be efficiently internalized by both tumor cells and immune cells [21]. Beyond direct tumor targeting, nanoplatforms can be engineered to selectively interact with the tumor microenvironment (TME), thereby enhancing antitumor immune responses while mitigating irAEs and promoting durable immune memory [21, 22]. Unlike conventional systemic therapies, nanomedicine can be designed to preferentially accumulate in tumors and respond dynamically to local microenvironmental cues—such as hypoxia, acidic pH, redox imbalance, or aberrant enzymatic activity—allowing localized and controlled release of immunomodulators, cytotoxic agents, or nucleic acids. Thus, nanomedicine-based approaches are being integrated into immunotherapy for urological cancers, improving drug bioavailability, enabling precision medicine, and reducing off-target toxicity [23, 24]. The convergence of nanotechnology with immunotherapy and conventional treatments further creates opportunities for synergistic and amplified antitumor efficacy.

Over the past five years, rapid advances in materials science, synthetic biology, and immunology have accelerated the development of a broad array of nanomedicine-based immunotherapeutic strategies for malignancies of the urinary system. These approaches encompass immune reprogramming through the induction of regulated cell death modalities—including immunogenic cell death, ferroptosis, pyroptosis, cuproptosis, and disulfidptosis—as well as the enhancement of tumor sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) via vascular normalization, metabolic modulation, and epigenetic regulation. In parallel, antitumor immune responses have been further strengthened through the use of nanovaccines, mRNA-based delivery platforms, and engineered nano-interfaces for immune cell modulation. Importantly, many of these nanoplatforms are designed to integrate therapeutic and immunoregulatory functions within a single, unified system, thereby enabling synergistic and self-reinforcing effects that are difficult to achieve with conventional free-drug combinations.

In this review, we present a comprehensive and mechanistically organized overview of nanomedicine-enabled immunotherapeutic strategies for urinary system malignancies, with a focus on PCa, renal cell carcinoma, and bladder cancer. We examine how nanoplatforms coordinate and amplify antitumor immune responses through targeted delivery, externally triggered photodynamic immunotherapy, tumor immune microenvironment remodeling, immunotherapy sensitization and synergy, and neoantigen–driven vaccination (Fig. 1). We further evaluate the translational potential of nanoparticle-based immunotherapies, discuss key barriers to clinical implementation, and highlight the emerging role of nanomedicine in advancing both immuno-oncology research and future clinical translation (Fig. 2).

Immunological landscape of urological tumors and mechanisms of nanomedicine-enabled immunotherapy

Urological cancers comprise a heterogeneous group of malignancies, including PCa, renal cell carcinoma (RCC), and bladder cancer, arising from the urinary and male reproductive systems [26]. These cancers exhibit distinct molecular, pathological, and immune characteristics, contributing to variable therapeutic responses. Clinical management remains challenging due to tumor heterogeneity, late-stage diagnosis, and the emergence of treatment resistance. Despite advances in surgery and systemic therapies, achieving durable clinical responses continues to be a major unmet need [27, 28].

Basic mechanisms of cancer immune response

Early studies on tumor-immune interactions established that cancer cells can hijack endogenous immune regulatory pathways to evade immune surveillance, thereby promoting tumor persistence and progression. Malignant cells exploit immune checkpoint pathways as a dominant mechanism of immune escape, highlighting the upregulation of inhibitory ligands targeting effector T cells. The programmed cell death-1 (PD-1) receptor and its ligand PD-L1 acts as a central inhibitory axis, demonstrating that PD-L1 expression on tumor and stromal cells induces T-cell exhaustion and suppresses cytotoxic activity upon engagement with PD-1 on activated T cells [29]. In parallel, the cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated antigen-4 (CTLA-4) acts as a negative regulator of T-cell priming, showing that CTLA-4 competes with costimulatory receptors for ligand binding during antigen presentation, thereby limiting the amplitude of adaptive immune responses [30].

Beyond checkpoint signaling, tumors evade immune recognition through loss of antigenicity, including downregulation of tumor-associated antigens and impairment of antigen presentation machinery such as major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) molecules, leading to ineffective T-cell recognition [31]. Further studies revealed that tumors actively remodel their microenvironment by recruiting immunosuppressive cell populations, including regulatory T cells (Tregs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and anti-inflammatory M2-polarized macrophages, which collectively inhibit effector immune functions and promote immune tolerance [32]. These suppressive immune populations secrete inhibitory cytokines such as interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), which directly suppress cytotoxic lymphocyte activity while further impairing antigen presentation and dendritic cell maturation [32]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that immune checkpoint engagement, loss of tumor antigenicity, recruitment of regulatory immune cells, and sustained production of suppressive cytokines constitute a major barrier to effective anticancer immunity and therapeutic intervention.

Immunosuppressive characteristics of the TME

TME is characterized by a complex set of physicochemical and biological conditions that collectively impair effective antitumor immune responses and facilitate tumor growth and progression. The central hallmark of the TME is profound hypoxia resulting from rapid tumor proliferation and aberrant vasculature, which not only limits oxygen availability but also reshapes cellular metabolism, enhancing immunosuppressive signaling and stromal barrier formation [33].

Hypoxia is frequently accompanied by the development of an acidic extracellular milieu, driven by elevated glycolytic flux and the accumulation of lactic acid, which lowers extracellular pH. The resulting decrease in extracellular pH adversely affects immune surveillance by impairing effector T-cell activity while simultaneously favoring immunosuppressive cellular phenotypes [34].

In addition, the abnormal architecture of tumor blood vessels leads to increased vascular permeability and inefficient lymphatic drainage. These structural abnormalities contribute to the generation of abnormally high interstitial fluid pressure (IFP) within the tumor, which limits the effective penetration of immune cells and therapeutic agents into the tumor core [35]. At the molecular level, several immunomodulatory factors further reinforce this suppressive microenvironment. Interleukin-10 (IL-10), a key immunosuppressive cytokine, contributes significantly to immune suppression within the TME by inhibiting pro-inflammatory cytokine production from macrophages and dendritic cells, thereby preventing effective T-cell activation and fostering an anti-inflammatory milieu [36]. In addition, IL-10 attenuates the cytotoxic functions of NK cells and CD8+ T cells which facilitates tumor immune escape [37, 38]. Similarly, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), although primarily associated with angiogenesis, also promotes immunosuppression by disrupting dendritic cell maturation and impairing antigen presentation and subsequent T-cell priming, thus undermining effective antitumor immunity [39, 40].

Another critical structural component of the TME involves the activity of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), which remodel the extracellular matrix (ECM) through the secretion of collagens, fibronectin, and matrix-crosslinking enzymes. This remodeling serves as a physical barrier that limits the ability of immune effector cells, particularly T lymphocytes, to infiltrate the tumor tissue, thereby contributing to immune exclusion and resistance to immunotherapy.

Consequently, the combined effects of low pH, hypoxia, elevated interstitial pressure, impaired immune infiltration, and dense ECM not only constrain effector immune cells access but also fosters a milieu that actively suppresses immune activation and promotes tumor immune evasion (Fig. 3). Collectively, these characteristics form a highly immunosuppressive TME that significantly compromises the effectiveness of conventional therapies as well as novel immunotherapeutic approaches.

Distinct immune microenvironments in prostate, renal, and bladder cancers

In immuno-oncology, tumors are often described as either “hot” or “cold” based on their immune microenvironment. “Hot” tumors are characterized by abundant infiltration of activated immune cells, especially cytotoxic T lymphocytes, and an inflamed microenvironment with higher levels of neoantigens that facilitate immune recognition. In contrast, “cold” tumors exhibit low effector immune cell infiltration, impaired antigen presentation, and an immunosuppressive TME, which contributes to immune evasion and poor responsiveness to immunotherapies [41]. PCa is typically immunologically “cold,” with low levels of effector immune cell infiltration and limited neoantigen burden, resulting in an inherently suppressive tumor immune microenvironment that confers resistance to most ICIs monotherapies, which can be reinforced by tumor-intrinsic signaling programs that shape antigen presentation, immune recruitment, and immune suppression. Recent studies implicate oncogenic transcriptional circuits (e.g., YAP1-centered signaling) in suppressing antitumor immunity and modulating responsiveness to ICB. In addition, alterations in DNA damage processing and innate immune sensing (e.g., cGAS–STING–linked programs in specific genetic contexts) can influence whether tumors adopt inflamed or non-inflamed immune states. Together, these mechanisms provide a strong rationale for nanomedicine strategies that deliver innate immune agonists, induce immunogenic cell death, or reprogram suppressive myeloid and Treg niches to convert cold tumors into inflamed, treatment-responsive states [42–45]. In this context, the limited presence of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, together with the predominance of regulatory and suppressive elements within the prostate TME, contributes to poor clinical responses to immunotherapeutic interventions [46].

In contrast, RCC demonstrates a relatively immune-inflamed phenotype, often marked by substantial infiltration of cytotoxic T cells and other immune effectors, which have been associated with enhanced responsiveness to ICB and integration of immunotherapies into frontline clinical management. Molecular profiling studies of RCC show high levels of T-cell infiltration and increased expression of antigen-presentation machinery, indicating that RCC is an immunogenic tumor. These features are consistent with the clinical efficacy of ICIs targeting PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 in both metastatic and adjuvant treatment settings [47].

Bladder cancer, particularly urothelial carcinoma, shows a moderate level of immune cell infiltration with distinct immune subtypes, which supports the clinical use of both immunotherapy monotherapy and combination treatment strategies. Notably, a subset of bladder tumors displays a highly inflamed microenvironment with abundant tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and elevated expression of immune checkpoints, which correlates with improved responses to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade and supports the emergence of novel combination strategies to enhance antitumor immunity [48].

Bladder cancer also presents a unique ecological feature due to the chronic exposure of the urothelium to urine. Urinary physicochemical stressors such as osmolarity, pH, nitrogenous metabolites, and urobiome-derived products can shape the local immune environment by modulating myeloid polarization, T-cell functional fitness, and inflammatory signaling. These features contribute to immune heterogeneity and carry important translational implications. Although intravesical therapies are limited by dilution and washout, the urinary environment also offers opportunities for nanomedicine platforms that specifically exploit urine-associated biochemistry (e.g., urease-responsive systems) to enhance immune activation and therapeutic retention.

Immunotherapeutic strategies in urologic cancers

Immunotherapy has emerged as a key treatment modality for urologic cancers by harnessing durable antitumor immune responses. ICIs show the most consistent clinical benefit, while vaccines, cell-based therapies, cytokines, immune stimulators, and rational combination strategies continue to broaden and enhance therapeutic outcomes (Fig. 4). These immunotherapeutic approaches are discussed in detail in the following subsections (Sect. 2.2.1–2.2.5).

Immune checkpoint inhibitors

Immune checkpoint inhibition has emerged as a central immunotherapeutic strategy in urologic malignancies by reversing tumor-mediated immune suppression. Bellmunt et al. demonstrated in the KEYNOTE-045 study that pembrolizumab significantly improved overall survival compared with chemotherapy in platinum-treated urothelial carcinoma, establishing PD-1 blockade as an effective therapeutic approach [49, 50]. Ferreiro-Pantín et al. reported in the analysis of the phase II IMvigor210 trial that atezolizumab induced durable clinical responses in advanced urothelial carcinoma, supporting the clinical relevance of PD-L1 inhibition in bladder cancer [51, 52]. In advanced clear-cell RCC, the phase III CheckMate 025 trial showed that nivolumab significantly improved overall survival and response rates compared with everolimus, with longer median OS and fewer severe adverse events, confirming the clinical benefit of PD-1 blockade [53]. The phase II TITAN-RCC trial showed that nivolumab with optional ipilimumab boosts produced encouraging response rates in intermediate- and poor-risk metastatic clear-cell RCC, supporting combination immunotherapy in selected patients [54]. The CheckMate 214 study demonstrated that nivolumab plus ipilimumab significantly improved survival compared with sunitinib in intermediate- and poor-risk RCC, underscoring the benefit of dual PD-1/CTLA-4 blockade [55]. A phase III trial of ipilimumab in castration-resistant PCa did not improve survival overall, although certain patients with better prognostic factors appeared to benefit compared with placebo [56]. In RCC, phase I studies of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 drugs reported meaningful disease control and durable responses up to about 50% in heavily pretreated metastatic patients [57]. In bladder cancer, a phase II trial found that giving ipilimumab before radical cystectomy was safe and well tolerated. Immune-related side effects such as colitis and dermatitis were common but generally manageable [58]. These studies underscore the clinical success of immune-checkpoint inhibitors in urologic cancers and establish combination checkpoint blockade as a promising strategy to overcome resistance and improve therapeutic durability (Table 1).

Cancer vaccines

Cancer vaccines enhance antigen-specific immune recognition to drive durable cellular immunity and have been widely investigated in urologic cancers due to well-characterized tumor antigens and typically slow disease progression. The following subsections discuss major categories of cancer vaccine strategies.

DNA vaccines are particularly promising in PCa, as plasmid-based constructs encoding tumor antigens can safely elicit durable antigen-specific T-cell responses. McNeel et al. [59] evaluated pTVG-HP, a DNA vaccine encoding prostatic acid phosphatase (PAP), demonstrating induction of PAP-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell immunity and delayed disease progression in early-stage clinical trials. McNeel et al. also reported that pTVG-HP vaccination generates a Th1-biased (T helper 1 cells, subtype of CD4+ T cells) immune response in preclinical models, providing mechanistic support for DNA-based vaccination strategies targeting prostate tumor antigens [60].

Peptide/adjuvant-based vaccines use defined epitopes to selectively activate tumor-specific T cells. Notably, NY-ESO-1 targeted vaccines combine this highly immunogenic antigen with delivery systems and innate immune activating adjuvants to enhance T-cell priming [61]. A first-in-human phase I study of the CHP–NY-ESO-1 vaccine with MIS416 in advanced solid tumors, including bladder and castration-resistant PCa, showed acceptable safety but limited antigen-specific immune responses, underscoring the need for improved adjuvant and combination approaches [62]. NY-ESO-1 is expressed in a significant fraction of urologic cancers, including ~ 40% of refractory urothelial tumors and 15–40% of advanced PCas, supporting its use as a target for peptide- or protein-based vaccines that activate both CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses [62].

Viral vector-based vaccines enhance antigen delivery and immune activation by efficiently targeting antigen-presenting cells and inducing strong cytotoxic T-cell responses. The ETBX platform uses modified Ad5 (E1-, E2b-) adenoviral vectors to express multiple tumor antigens, improving antigen presentation and T-cell activation [62]. A phase I trial showed that combined ETBX vaccines targeting CEA, MUC1, and brachyury safely induced antigen-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses in patients with advanced solid tumors, demonstrating the immunogenicity of this multivalent platform [63]. Although not urologic-specific, related adenoviral vaccines incorporating PSA with MUC1 and brachyury have shown safety and immune activation in metastatic castration-resistant PCa.

Cell-based immunotherapies

Adoptive cell therapies such as CAR-T and CAR-NK cells can overcome immune suppression in urologic cancers, with CAR-NK offering antigen-specific killing and a reduced risk of severe cytokine release. In PCa, prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA), prostate stem cell antigen (PSCA), and six-transmembrane epithelial antigen of the prostate 1 (STEAP1) are highly enriched tumor surface antigens with limited normal-tissue expression, making them attractive CAR targets. Narayan et al. reported results from a Phase I trial (NCT03089203) of PSMA-targeted CAR-T cells in mCRPC, demonstrating manageable safety, PSA declines, and preliminary antitumor activity [64]. Morgenroth et al. demonstrated that PSCA-directed CAR-T cells effectively lyse PSCA-expressing PCa cells in preclinical models, supporting PSCA as a promising target in advanced PCa [65]. Follow-up studies demonstrated that PSCA-targeted CAR-T cells elicit strong antigen-dependent cytokine release and tumor cell killing in vitro, often using PCa models engineered to express PSCA due to low endogenous expression in standard cell lines [66]. The STEAP family (STEAP 1–4) comprises six-transmembrane proteins involved in metal metabolism and tumor progression [67]. STEAP1 is overexpressed in PCa, particularly mCRPC, an advanced stage resistant to androgen-deprivation therapy, while showing minimal expression in normal tissues [68]. Recent advances include second-generation STEAP1-targeted CAR-T cells demonstrating strong antitumor activity in preclinical models, even at low antigen density [69]. These findings have enabled a first-in-human phase I/II clinical trial (NCT06236139) evaluating STEAP1 CAR-T therapy in combination with enzalutamide in patients with mCRPC to assess safety and antitumor efficacy [70]. STEAP2 (STAMP1) is highly expressed in prostate tissue and upregulated in PCa, and the STEAP2-targeted CAR-T candidate AZD0754 showed TGF-β-resistant cytotoxicity in vitro and strong, dose-dependent antitumor efficacy in PCa xenografts [71]. NECTIN4-directed CAR-T cells have shown potent cytotoxicity against urothelial carcinoma cells with varying NECTIN4 expression, supporting their use in bladder cancer immunotherapy [72]. PSCA-CAR-T and B7H3-CAR-T cells have also demonstrated efficacy in bladder cancer model systems [73].

In PCa, anti-PSMA CAR-engineered NK-92 cells have shown strong and antigen-specific cytotoxicity against PSMA-expressing tumor cells in preclinical models [74]. In RCC, Zhang et al. demonstrated that EGFR-specific third-generation CAR-NK-92 cells selectively kill RCC cells in vitro, and that combination with cabozantinib further enhances efficacy by increasing EGFR expression and reducing PD-L1, leading to synergistic antitumor effects in RCC xenograft models [75]. Additionally, CD70 has emerged as an immune-relevant biomarker and therapeutic target. Early-phase clinical studies of CD70-directed CAR-T therapies have reported encouraging results, including durable responses in certain patients [76]. Ongoing clinical trials further underscore the translational potential of CD70-targeted cellular immunotherapy in advanced RCC [77].

In bladder cancer, CD44v6-targeted CAR-NK cells showed selective binding and enhanced cytotoxicity in preclinical models, while tumor-reactive, predominantly CD3+/CD8+ TILs capable of IFN-γ release were successfully expanded from tumors and lymph node metastases, with 4-1BB stimulation further improving TIL expansion and supporting the translational potential of autologous TIL therapy [78].

Cytokine and immune-stimulator-based therapies

Cytokine and immune stimulator therapies enhance antitumor immunity by activating effector cells and reshaping the TME in urologic cancers; notably, high-dose IL-2 showed durable responses in a subset of metastatic RCC patients, establishing it as a foundational cytokine therapy [79]. Using murine bladder cancer models, O’Donnell et al. demonstrated that interleukin-12 (IL-12) effectively suppressed tumor growth and generated protective immune memory upon rechallenge, indicating activation of durable adaptive immune responses [80]. Complementary findings by Eto et al. showed that systemic IL-12 administration inhibited MBT-2 tumor progression and induced tumor-specific cytotoxic T lymphocyte activity, supporting a role for IL-12 in antigen-specific immune priming [81]. Collectively, these studies establish IL-12 as a strong driver of Th1-skewed immunity and cytotoxic T cell-mediated tumor control in preclinical bladder cancer models.

More recent efforts have focused on combinatorial immune stimulation. In PCa models, Esteves et al. reported that combined treatment with IL-15 and the STING (Stimulator of Interferon Genes) agonist ADU-S100 resulted in enhanced activation of NK and CD8+ T cells, elevated IFN-γ production, and superior tumor regression compared with single-agent therapy [82]. STING signaling is activated when cGAS senses cytosolic DNA and produces cGAMP, triggering type I interferon responses; NK cell–mediated tumor control requires STING in host immune cells and tumor-intrinsic cGAS, with STING activation mainly inducing IFN-β in myeloid and B cells rather than directly in NK cells [83]. Nicolai et al. showed that STING-activating cyclic dinucleotides can induce strong NK cell–dependent tumor rejection, even without CD8+ T cells, via type I interferon–driven IL-15/IL-15Rα upregulation in dendritic cells [84].

Combination immunotherapy approaches

Combining immunotherapy with radiotherapy can overcome resistance in urologic cancers by inducing immunogenic cell death and enhancing antigen presentation, thereby strengthening systemic antitumor immunity in advanced bladder and PCas [85]. Nivolumab administered in combination with gemcitabine and cisplatin demonstrated superior efficacy over gemcitabine-cisplatin monotherapy in previously untreated advanced urothelial carcinoma [86]. KEYNOTE-361 and IMvigor130 showed no significant survival benefit from adding ICIs to gemcitabine-platinum chemotherapy, with modest effects largely linked to maintenance immunotherapy. Subgroup signals in cisplatin-treated patients align with CheckMate 901, but overall evidence does not support limiting benefit to cisplatin regimens, especially given the success of avelumab maintenance [87]. Nanomedicine approaches have been proposed to further augment these combinations. In PCa models, multifunctional nanoplatforms have been developed to combine tumor-directed therapies with immune modulation. Polymeric, liposomal, and MOF-based systems enable co-delivery of photothermal/photodynamic agents and immunostimulants or chemotherapeutics, resulting in synergistic antitumor effects. Such integrated nano-designs consistently outperform single-modality approaches and highlight their promise for PCa therapy.

Nanoplatforms and functional designs in immunotherapy

Nanoparticles possess intrinsic and engineerable properties that enable them to modulate antitumor immunity beyond serving as passive drug carriers. Organic nanoplatforms, such as liposomes and polymeric nanoparticles, enhance immune activation primarily through improved payload protection, cellular uptake, and lymphoid trafficking. By facilitating co-delivery of antigens and immune agonists (e.g., STING or TLR ligands), these systems promote dendritic cell maturation, antigen presentation, and downstream CD8+ T-cell responses while reducing systemic toxicity [88–98] (Table 2).

Example nanoplatforms designed to enhance antitumor immunity and overcome immune evasion in urologic cancers

Polymeric nanoparticles such as biodegradable systems like PLGA, further enable sustained release and intracellular delivery of immunostimulatory molecules, enhancing costimulatory signaling and MHC-I presentation. Their tunable size, surface charge, and composition allow rational design to bias immune activation toward cytotoxic T-cell-mediated antitumor immunity.

Inorganic nanomaterials such as metallic and metal-oxide nanoparticles can induce immunogenic cell death, generate danger-associated molecular patterns, and reprogram suppressive immune populations, such as polarizing M2 tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) toward pro-inflammatory M1 phenotypes. For example, iron oxide nanoparticles, such as clinically approved ferumoxytol and ferucarbotran, can induce macrophage polarization from an M2 to an M1 phenotype by triggering intracellular iron-driven redox signaling through Fenton-type reactions, engaging toll-like receptor-4 (TLR-4), and upregulating the interferon regulatory factor 5 (IRF5) pathway [99, 100]. Some inorganic systems also restore tumor immunogenicity by enhancing antigen release or upregulating MHC expression, thereby overcoming immune evasion. For instance, manganese-based nanoparticles can activate the cGAS-STING pathway through the release of Mn2+ ions, which bind to and stimulate cGAS, thereby promoting antitumor immune response [101, 102]. These nanoparticles can also increase dendritic cell maturation by upregulating the expression of MHC molecules and costimulatory markers such as CD80 and CD86, ultimately improving antigen presentation and T cell activation [103–105]. Similarly, copper-based nanoparticles can catalyze Fenton-like reactions, producing a surge of reactive oxygen species (ROS) that help to activate the cGAS-STING pathway [106]. In addition, the excess copper ions delivered to the mitochondria of cancer cells induce cuproptosis, leading to toxic aggregation of dihydrolipoamide S‑acetyltransferase (DLAT) and consequent proteotoxic stress, turning “cold” tumors “hot” for more effective immunotherapy [107–109].

Mesoporous and hybrid nanostructures integrate physical retention, tissue permeation, and immune modulation. These platforms can prolong local drug exposure, disrupt epithelial barriers, and reshape the tumor immune microenvironment through cytokine modulation and macrophage reprogramming.

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) and biomimetic nanovesicles represent emerging immunoregulatory systems that combine precise molecular loading with microenvironment-responsive behavior. These platforms can induce immunogenic cell death, block immunosuppressive signaling pathways (e.g., TGF-β), or remodel immune cell phenotypes, thereby restoring sensitivity to immunotherapy.

Lastly, nucleic acid-based nanostructures, such as spherical nucleic acids, demonstrate how nanoscale architecture itself governs immune outcomes. By controlling the spatial organization of antigens and adjuvants, these systems enhance dendritic cell cross-priming and generate robust, antigen-specific cytotoxic T-cell responses. Collectively, these nanoplatforms demonstrate how material composition, structure, and surface functionality can be utilized to reshape the TME.

Nanomedicine-based immunotherapy for RCC

RCC is intrinsically immunogenic and characterized by extensive crosstalk between angiogenic and immune signaling pathways. However, the clinical efficacy of immunotherapy in a substantial proportion of patients is often limited by hypoxia-induced immune evasion, abnormal tumor vasculature, and progressive exhaustion of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells [110, 111]. These biological characteristics make RCC particularly amenable to nanomedicine-mediated reprogramming of the tumor immune microenvironment and to the rational development of combination immunotherapeutic strategies.

Nanomedicine-mediated immune reprogramming

RCC remains a prevalent and aggressive malignancy of the urinary system, with a particularly poor prognosis at advanced or metastatic stages, where five-year survival rates fall below 10% [112]. Despite the clinical adoption of targeted agents and ICB, therapeutic benefitoccurs in only a minority of patients, as the overall response rate to ICB in RCC remains modest (~ 20%). This limited efficacy is largely attributable to insufficient intrinsic tumor immunogenicity and poor infiltration of cytotoxic T-cell within the TME [113]. To overcome these barriers, Wu et al. [114] engineered a biomimetic copper-based nanozyme (Cu2O-OMV) capable of potentiating ICB therapy by converting immunologically “cold” RCC tumors into “hot” tumors through the coordinated induction of immunogenic cell death. By integrating Cu2O nanoparticles with bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), this nanoplatform activated copper-dependent cuproptosis and lipopolysaccharide (LPS)–mediated noncanonical pyroptosis. In murine RCC models, Cu2O-OMV markedly inhibited tumor progression and, when combined with anti-PD-L1 therapy, strongly suppressed both primary and distant lesions by eliciting robust systemic antitumor immune responses (Fig. 5A–L).

Hypoxia-inducible factor-2α (HIF-2α) is a central oncogenic regulator in RCC, strongly associated with immune evasion, upregulation of PD-L1, and therapeutic resistance [115]. The clinical approval of belzutifan, a selective HIF-2α inhibitor, offers a compelling pharmacological approach to disrupt hypoxia-driven signaling, facilitating its incorporation into multifunctional nanotherapeutic designs [116]. Building on this rationale, Zhu et al. [117] engineered a hypoxia-responsive sonodynamic nanoplatform (APm/Ce6/HIF)designed to overcome hypoxia- and autophagy-mediated immunotherapy resistance in RCC. This system co-loads the sonosensitizer chlorin e6 with belzutifan, enabling targeted, hypoxia-triggered release within the TME. In vivo, ultrasound-activated APm/Ce6/HIF induced pronounced tumor growth suppression and synergistically potentiated PD-1 blockade, highlighting hypoxia-targeted sonodynamic nanotherapy as a promising strategyto enhance RCC responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibition.

Manganese ions have recently emerged as potent immunostimulatory agents through activation of the cyclic GMP–AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS–STING) pathway and induction of tumor cell pyroptosis; however, their clinical translation remains limited by systemic toxicity and poor tumor selectivity [118, 119]. Hyaluronic acid (HA)–mediated CD44 targeting has been established as an effective approach for tumor- and macrophage-specific drug delivery, yet its potential for delivering immunomodulatory manganese remains largely underexplored [120]. In this context, Du et al. [121] designed a multifunctional manganese–polydopamine nano-immunomodulator (PDA-Mn-HA NPs) to potentiate immunotherapy in RCC by simultaneously targeting TAMs and malignant cells. Surface functionalization of manganese-chelated polydopamine nanoparticles with HA enabled selective accumulation within RCC lesions and TAMs via CD44-dependent uptake, with TME–responsive Mn2+ release. Mechanistically, PDA-Mn-HA NPs drove pronounced M1 macrophage polarization and amplified the production of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines through activation of the ROS–STING–p38/MAPK signaling cascade, promoting CD8+ T-cell recruitment and intratumoral infiltration. In murine RCC models, treatment with PDA-Mn-HA NPs markedly inhibited tumor progression, increased intratumoral CD86+ macrophages and CD8+ T cells, and exhibited a favorable biosafety profile (Fig. 5M–W).

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy has achieved limited success in solid malignancies, largely due to inadequate tumor infiltration and the profoundly immunosuppressive TME. In contrast, macrophages inherently exhibit efficient tumor homing and phagocytic activity, rendering chimeric antigen receptor macrophages (CAR-Ms) an attractive alternative therapeutic modality. Nonetheless, CAR-Ms remain vulnerable to phenotypic reprogramming within the TME, shifting from an initial tumoricidal M1-like state toward an M2-like, tumor-supportive phenotype. To address this limitation, Jing et al. [122] developed an in situ macrophage-engineering strategy designed to sustain proinflammatory polarization and durable antitumor activity of CAR-Ms. Specifically, the authors constructed a synthetic chimeric IL-2 receptor–TLR4 signaling receptor (CSR) capable of converting exogenous IL-2 stimulation into canonical TLR4–NF-κB inflammatory signaling, reinforcing M1-like macrophage identity in the process. Circular RNAs encoding both an anti-CA9 CAR and the CSR were delivered to macrophages using optimized mannose-modified lipid nanoparticles, while an injectable hydrogel enabled localized, sustained co-delivery of IL-2 and circRNA nanoparticles. This integrated platform markedly augmented antigen-specific phagocytosis, amplified proinflammatory cytokine production, and enhanced downstream activation of CD8+ T-cell responses. In orthotopic and postoperative renal cancer models, localized administration of the CAR-mLNP/CSR-mLNP/IL-2 hydrogel robustly suppressed tumor growth, curtailed metastatic dissemination, prevented recurrence, and significantly prolonged survival without detectable systemic toxicity, highlighting a versatile and clinically translatable strategy for enhancing CAR-M immunotherapy in solid tumors.

Nanomedicine-mediated immunotherapy sensitization and synergistic amplification

Although ICIs–based regimens have reshaped the therapeutic landscape of metastatic RCC, durable clinical benefit remains elusive, and treatment-associated toxicities are common, with over 75% of patients experiencing clinically significant adverse events and nearly 30% ultimately developing therapeutic resistance [123]. To address these limitations, Au et al. [124] engineered a biologically targeted, multifunctional nano-immunotherapy platform for clear cell RCC (ccRCC) by constructing human carbonic anhydrase IX (hCA-9)–directed PEG-PLGA nanoparticles coated with agonistic anti-4-1BB and anti-CD27 antibodies. This rational design enables spatially restricted and temporally coordinated activation of adaptive (CD8+ T cells) and innate (natural killer cells) immune responses directly within the TME. The resulting hCA-9–targeted multifunctional immunotherapy nanoparticles (MINPs) elicited markedly stronger immune activation and tumor-selective cytotoxicity than bifunctional nanoparticles or comparable combinations of free antibodies, both in vitro and in vivo. In immunocompetent murine models and human peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC)–humanized ccRCC xenografts, MINPs robustly inhibited tumor growth, induced durable antitumor immunity, and maintained therapeutic efficacy even when immune cells were derived from aged donors, partially overcoming immunosenescence. Notably, nanoparticle-anchored costimulatory signaling substantially mitigated systemic immune-related toxicities, including hepatotoxicity, highlighting tumor-targeted, multi-axis nano-immunotherapy as a safer and more effective treatment strategy for ccRCC (Fig. 6A–H).

Advanced renal carcinoma is characterized by profound angiogenic dysregulation, producing chaotic, hyperpermeable, and functionally compromised vasculature that severely impedes both efficient drug delivery and cytotoxic T-cell infiltration [125]. To overcome these barriers, Wu et al. [126] developed a bispecific peptide precursor (pre-GP1)integrating an NRP-1–blocking motif, a PD-L1–binding motif, and a self-assembly domain, enabling deep intratumoral penetration followed by receptor-triggered in situ self-assembly into fibrous supramolecular networks within the TME. This conformational transition substantially enhanced binding avidity and tumor retention, leading to effective pruning of aberrant neovasculature, increased pericyte coverage, and restored vascular normalization. As a consequence, pre-GP1 markedly promoted CD8+ T-cell infiltration, reduced regulatory T-cell populations, and increased interferon-γ secretion, thereby reshaping the immunosuppressive tumor milieu. Across multiple murine renal carcinoma models, including large and orthotopic tumors, pre-GP1 achieved superior tumor growth suppression and markedly reduced pulmonary metastasis compared with clinically deployed PD-L1 and anti-VEGF antibody combinations, while showing attenuated hepatic and renal toxicities. Collectively, this work highlights receptor-responsive supramolecular therapeutics as an emerging precision oncology paradigm.

Zinc is an essential trace element critical for redox homeostasis and immune regulation [127]. Zeng et al. [128] demonstrated that orally administered zinc gluconate can spontaneously associate with plasma proteins in vivo to form zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs), which selectively accumulate in renal tumors and provide both drug delivery and immunomodulatory functions. These endogenously generated ZnO NPs, with a core diameter of approximately 40–60 nm, exhibited prolonged and tumor-selective retention in papillary RCC models by exploiting constitutive overexpression of metallothionein-1X (MT-1X) in renal tumor cells. Upon daunorubicin loading, ZnO NPs markedly enhanced intratumoral drug exposure and antitumor efficacy in both murine and rabbit renal tumor models while maintaining a favorable biosafety profile. Nevertheless, human evidence remains limited to ex vivo tumor specimens rather than rigorously controlled clinical intervention studies, and the long-term consequences of chronic zinc supplementation on systemic metal homeostasis require further investigation. Notably, the key innovation of this workis the demonstration of a self-amplifying, tumor-specific nanoparticle accumulation mechanism derived from nutritional supplementation, eliminating the need for exogenously engineered nanomaterials while achieving effective tumor targeting and immune modulation.

Nanomedicine-mediated tumor neoantigen vaccines

DNA vaccines hold considerable promise for eliciting cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–mediated antitumor immunity; however, their clinical translation has been limited by insufficient intrinsic immunogenicity [129]. Chai et al. [130] engineered a chitosan nanoparticle–based DNA vaccine co-encoding the renal carcinoma–associated antigen carbonic anhydrase IX (CAIX) and the transcription factor L-Myc, demonstrating that L-Myc serves as a potent immunological adjuvant that enhances dendritic cell (DC)–dependent antitumor immune responses. In murine hCAIX-Renca subcutaneous and lung metastasis models, co-immunization with CS-pL-Myc/pCAIX markedly inhibited tumor progression, reduced metastatic burden, and substantially prolonged survival compared with CAIX vaccination alone. These findings suggest that L-Myc–based adjuvant strategies may be broadly applicable across solid tumors and merit further exploration in combination with ICB and for promoting long-term memory T-cell responses.

Most contemporary neoepitope vaccine platforms rely on long peptides or RNA constructs that preferentially activate CD4+ T cells and only indirectly elicit cytotoxic CD8+ T-cell responses [131, 132]. To directly address this limitation, He et al. [133] developed a multivalent short-peptide vaccination approach to systematically identify and validate functional CD8+ T-cell neoepitopes in RCC within the murine RENCA model. By integrating whole-exome and transcriptomic sequencing with MHC class I binding prediction, the authors screened 20 candidate 9-mer neoepitopes formulated as immunogenic nanoparticles using a cobalt porphyrin–based CPQ liposomal adjuvant. Strikingly, only a single mutated epitope, Nesprin-2 L4492R (Nes2LR), elicited robust neoepitope-specific CD8+ T-cell responses and produced potent antitumor activity. Vaccination with CPQ/Nes2LR achieved complete tumor growth suppression or eradication in both prophylactic and therapeutic settings, including subcutaneous, orthotopic renal, and experimental lung metastasis models, and induced durable immunological memory. Notably, this short-peptide vaccine retained efficacy at antigen doses 100–1000-fold lower than those required with conventional adjuvants such as poly(I: C) or alum, while exhibiting strong synergy with PD-1 and CTLA-4 ICB.

Nanomedicine-based immunotherapy for PCa

PCa is one of the most prevalent malignancies affecting men worldwide [134]. Due to its pronounced immune inertia, monotherapy-based immunotherapeutic approaches have yielded limited clinical benefit, underscoring the need for strategies that actively initiate and sustain antitumor immune responses. In this context, nanomedicine platforms offers a promising translational approach by inducing immunogenic cell death, reprogramming the myeloid-dominated immunosuppressive TME, and enhancing the efficacy of ICB.

Nanomedicine-mediated immune reprogramming

The TME of PCa is enriched with several TAMs, with the immunosuppressive M2 phenotype being predominant. These macrophages promote both tumor growth and metastasis and secrete a variety of pro-tumor and immunosuppressive factors weakening immunotherapy efficacy. Sun et al. [135] engineered an M2 macrophage–targeted immunotherapeutic nanoplatform designed to remodel this suppressive environment by selectively reprogramming TAMs. In this platform an amphiphilic cationic β-cyclodextrin–based nanoparticle was functionalized with the M2 macrophage–selective peptide M2pep and enclosed CSF-1R siRNA. This designachieved preferential uptake by M2-polarized macrophages. Targeted delivery induced robust CSF-1R gene silencing, promoting a phenotypic shift of TAMs from an M2 to an M1 state. This shift was accompanied by an enhanced production of immunostimulatory cytokines (e.g., IL-12) and suppression of immunoregulatory mediators (e.g., IL-6). Macrophage reprogramming enhanced cytotoxic T-cell infiltration while reducing regulatory T-cell populations. As a result, PCa cells in coculture assays underwent pronounced apoptosis and substantial tumor growth suppression in a TRAMP-C1 mouse model reducing tumor volume and mass by approximately 76%. Additionally, the group [136] designed a sialic acid–decorated, cyclodextrin-based nanoparticle platform to selectively deliver CSF-1R siRNA to M2-polarized macrophages. The platform targeted the CD169 (Siglec-1) receptor, which is abundantly expressed on immunosuppressive TAMs. Compared to non-targeted formulations, these nanoparticles exhibited markedly enhanced uptake in both human and murine M2 macrophages, improving CSF-1R silencing and promoting M1-like polarization. This phenotypic reprogramming was accompanied by increased expression of pro-inflammatory mediators (IL-12, TNF-α, CXCL-10) and downregulation of M2-associated markers (IL-6, IL-10, Arg-1, CD163). In Transwell co-culture systems, the reprogrammed macrophages substantially increased apoptosis in PCa cells, demonstrating that immune microenvironment remodeling alone can trigger potent antitumor activity.

Lin et al. [137] demonstrated that nanoparticle-mediated delivery of PTEN mRNA effectively restores tumor suppressor function, reprograms the immunosuppressive TME, and sensitizes PTEN-deficient tumors to ICB. Systemic administration of PTEN mRNA restored functional PTEN expression in PTEN-null PCa models, leading to attenuation of PI3K/Akt signaling and activation of autophagic pathways. This treatment, induced immunogenic cell death, as indicated by calreticulin surface exposure and release of HMGB1 and ATP.

Ferroptosis—an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation—has emerged as a potent form of immunogenic cell death that promotes dendritic cell maturation, activates cytotoxic T lymphocytes, and reshapes macrophage polarization [138, 139]. Building on this concept, Wang et al. [140] developed a multifunctional nanozyme-based immunomodulator (ZnFe2O4@Pt@PEG-GOx, ZFPG) to address the highly immunosuppressive TME and the intrinsic resistance of PCa to immunotherapy. The ZFPG nanoplatform integrates magnetic targeting, ultrasound responsiveness, and five distinct enzyme-mimetic activities. Together, these features enable cascade enzyodynamic reactions that simultaneously deplete intratumoral glucose and glutathione, potentiate H2O2 utilization, and trigger sustained reactive oxygen species generation. In vivo, magnetic guidance markedly enhances tumor accumulation of ZFPG, while ultrasound reinforcement further amplifies its therapeutic performance. These effects culminate in increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration, effective suppression of MDSCs, and significant inhibition of lung metastasis.

Boron neutron capture therapy (BNCT) is a binary radiotherapeutic approach predicated on the selective accumulation of 10B within tumor tissues, followed by thermal neutron irradiation that generates high–linear energy transfer (LET) 4He and 7Li particles, inducing highly localized tumor cell destruction. Yang et al. [141] developed a multifunctional boron delivery system by encapsulating 10B-doped carbon dots within lyophilized PCa cells (L-RM1@10B-CDs), effectively integrating BNCT with antitumor immunotherapy. By leveraging the micron-scale architecture and inherent tumor-homing capacity of whole tumor cells, this platform substantially prolonged intratumoral 10B retention while simultaneously enabling the delivery of a full collection of native tumor antigens without requiring epitope preselection. Following thermal neutron irradiation, L-RM1@10B-CDs–mediated BNCT elicited pronounced tumor growth inhibition through high-LET–induced DNA double-strand breaks, along with robust ferroptotic cell death characterized by excessive reactive oxygen species generation, Fe2+ accumulation, glutathione depletion, and lipid peroxidation. Notably, BNCT treatment triggered marked immune activation, with increased CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocyte infiltration and elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IFN-γ, effectively transforming the TME from immunologically “cold” to “hot”.

In a complementarily approach, Zhang et al. [142] reported a polyamine metabolism–targetted nanotherapeutic strategy to reverse immunosuppression in PCa inducing synergistic oxidative and carbonyl stress. The authors developed a PSMA–targeted mesoporous silica nanoparticle co-encapsulating spermidine, polyamine oxidase, and the glutathione synthesis inhibitor buthionine sulfoximine (BSO). This enabled sustained production of hydrogen peroxide and acrolein while impairing endogenous antioxidant defenses. This amplified redox stress triggered profound mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA damage, lipid peroxidation, and disruption of the GPX4–GSH axis, ultimately leading to robust immunogenic cell death marked by calreticulin exposure and the release of HMGB1 and ATP. In orthotopic and metastatic PCa models, this approach significantly promoted dendritic cell maturation, increased intratumoral CD8+ T-cell infiltration and TNF-α production, suppressed primary tumor growth and lung metastasis, and established durable antitumor immune memory.

When Zinc ions (Zn2+) released from zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8), they can trigger caspase-1/GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis, a highly immunogenic form of regulated cell death [143]. Based on this mechanism, Li et al. [144] developed a dual-functional chemo-immunotherapeutic nanoplatform encapsulating the immunogenic cell death (ICD)–inducing chemotherapeutic agent mitoxantrone (MIT) within ZIF-8. Besides serving as a pH-responsive carrier that promotes tumor-localized accumulation of MIT while minimizing off-target immune toxicity, ZIF-8 enhanced ICD through Zn2+-mediated activation of the caspase-1/GSDMD pyroptotic pathway. This synergistic effect increased reactive oxygen species production, calreticulin surface exposure, and HMGB1 release, leading torobust activation of antitumor immune responses.

In a distinct but complementary approach, Tao et al. [145] developed a peptide-functionalized gold nanostar (P-AuNS) for PCa that induces lysosome-dependent degradation of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), rather than relying on conventional ICB. The PD-L1-binding peptide enabled selective recognition of cell-surface PD-L1 and facilitated receptor-mediated endocytosis, directing PD-L1 to lysosomes for proteolytic degradation without altering its transcriptional regulation. Functionally, P-AuNS effectively disrupted PD-1/PD-L1 signaling and restored T-cell effector function, leading to a significant increase in Jurkat T-cell proliferation and interferon-γ secretion in PCa co-culture systems. In syngeneic RM-1 tumor–bearing micesystemic administration of P-AuNS resulted in pronounced tumor growth inhibition and reduced intratumoral PD-L1 and Ki-67 expression.

Although Cisplatin remains a cornerstone chemotherapeutic for advanced malignancies, PCa displays intrinsic resistance driven by rapid drug hydrolysis, dose-limiting systemic toxicity, and, most critically, glutathione S-transferase (GST)–mediated conjugation with glutathione that intracellularly neutralizes cisplatin. Zhao et al. [146] engineered a reactive oxygen species (ROS)–responsive polyphotosensitizer-based nanoparticle (NP2) incorporating Michael addition acceptors to cisplatin resistance and the immunologically “cold” phenotype of PCa. Using 808-nm near-infrared irradiation, NP2 generated abundant ROS, inducing nanoparticle disassembly and release of a platinum (IV) cisplatin prodrug, while the Michael acceptors covalently engaged intracellular GST, suppressing GST activity and reducing glutathione-mediated cisplatin inactivation. This coordinated mechanism markedly increased intracellular platinum retention, intensified DNA damage, and promoted tumor cell apoptosis. Simultaneously, photodynamic activation elicited robust type II immunogenic cell death, marked by ATP secretion, calreticulin surface exposure, and HMGB1 translocation. These events facilitated dendritic cell maturation, enhanced intratumoral CD8+ T-cell infiltration, promoted macrophage repolarization from an M2 to M1, and reduced regulatory T-cell accumulation.

Metastatic PCa remains a principal cause of cancer-related mortality, with liver metastases constituting an especially aggressive and therapeutically refractory disease manifestation. Growing evidence suggests that metastatic tumors exhibit pronounced “transcriptional addiction,” in which tumor growth and survival depend on a limited set of transcription factors that function as master regulators of oncogenic signaling networks. Cui et al. [147] engineered a Selective Cell in ORgan Targeting (SCORT) nanoparticle platform to enable systemic delivery of an RNA-targeting CRISPR/Cas13d (CasRx) system that silences the classically “undruggable” transcription factor HoxB13 in metastatic PCa. By integrating an optimized ionizable lipid composition with reduced polyethylene glycol content and an E3 RNA aptamer targetting transferrin receptor 1, SCORT nanoparticles preferentially accumulated in liver mCRPC cells after intravenous administration. SCORT-mediated co-delivery of CasRx mRNA and HoxB13-specific guide RNAs achieved efficient and selective HoxB13 depletion in both androgen receptor–positive and –negative CRPC models, resulting in pronounced suppression of hepatic metastases and a prolonged of survival. Collectively, these findings establish SCORT–Cas13d nanotherapy as a precise and effective strategy for targeting oncogenic transcription factors in metastatic PCa, while underscoring the translational promise of RNA-level CRISPR interventions for treating transcription factor–driven malignancies.

Nanomedicine-mediated immunotherapy sensitization and synergistic amplification

Castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) represents a highly refractory and lethal stage of disease, characterized by rapid progression, multidrug resistance, a high metastasic potential [148]. To address these challenges, Cheng et al. [149] developed a red blood cell membrane–camouflaged biomimetic nanovesicle (FiFe@RBM) that co-delivers the fatty acid synthase (FASN) inhibitor IPI-9119 and iron oxide nanoparticles to overcome therapeutic resistance in CRPC. By suppressing FASN-driven de novo lipogenesis, FiFe@RBM rewires lipid metabolism, leading to substantial accumulation of polyunsaturated fatty acids and PUFA-enriched phospholipids, sensitizing tumor cells to iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. In parallel, the released iron ions catalyze reactive oxygen species generation, inhibit GPX4 activity, disrupt mitochondrial function, and suppress the AKT–mTOR signaling axis, collectively amplifying ferroptotic and apoptotic cell death. Due to its superparamagnetic properties, FiFe@RBM further enables magnetic resonance imaging–guided magnetic hyperthermia therapy, which enhances tumor ablation under an alternating magnetic field. Notably, in PC-3 xenograft models, FiFe@RBM subtantially suppressed of primary CRPC tumors but also completely abrogated liver metastasis, an effect associated with pronounced activation and infiltration of natural killer cells.

Although external magnetic fields can enhance nanoparticle accumulation, their efficacy in deep-seated tumors is limited by rapid field attenuation. To address this limitation, Wang et al. [150] reported a magnetically assisted nanotherapeutic strategy for PCa that addresses both inadequate intratumoral drug delivery and resistance to apoptosis- and immunotherapy-based treatements. In this approach, PEGylated manganese–zinc ferrite nanocrystals (PMZFNs) were administered intravenously and guided by intratumorally implanted micromagnets, establishing a sustained internal magnetic field that markedly improved nanoparticle accumulation and retention within deep prostate tumors.

Nanotechnology, particularly cell membrane–biomimetic nanoparticles, has emerged as a powerful strategy to enhance drug delivery efficiency, tumor-specific targeting, and immunological compatibility. Deng et al. [151] developed a multifunctional nanotherapeutic approach targeting Aurora-A kinase (AURKA) to overcome therapeutic resistance in CRPC. Analysis of the TCGA-PRAD cohort showed that AURKA is significantly overexpressed in PCa and is associated with advanced disease stage, poor therapeutic response, and shortened progression-free survival. Building on these findings, the authors engineered a PSMA CAR-T cell membrane–camouflaged mesoporous gold–silica nanoparticle co-loaded with alisertib and docetaxel (CM-AMS@AD), integrating chemotherapy, photothermal therapy, and immune modulation within a single platform. Inhumanized CRPC xenograft models CM-AMS@AD with near-infrared irradiation achieved pronounced tumor growth inhibition, increased DNA damage, promoted dendritic cell maturation, and significantly elevatedthe intratumoral CD8+/CD4+ T-cell ratio.

Although ICIs have achieved notable clinical success, their therapeutic efficacy in PCa—particularly in bone metastases—remains limited. Furthermore, antibody-based PD-L1 blockade is frequently associated with immune-related toxicities and the emergence of resistance driven by target mutations. To address these challenges, Mu et al. [152] developed a biomimetic nanoplatform for targeted chemoimmunotherapy of PCa bone metastases by co-delivering doxorubicin (DOX) and PD-L1 siRNA via mesenchymal stem cell membrane–coated polydopamine nanoparticles (PDA–DOX/siPD-L1@SCM). In a PC-3 bone metastasis mouse model, PDA–DOX/siPD-L1@SCM markedly suppressed tumor growth, alleviated osteolytic bone destruction, increased tumor cell apoptosis, and reduced Ki67-positive proliferating cells compared with monotherapy or non-targeted controls, while also exhibiting weakened systemic toxicity. Liao et al. [153] engineered a genetically defined nanoplatform that synergistically combines immunogenic cell death (ICD) induction with ICB to enahnce antitumor immunity. In this system, curcumin-loaded PLGA nanoparticles were coated with cancer cell membrane–derived nanovesicles overexpressing programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), thus enabling efficient tumor targeting and antibody-free inhibition of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis (Fig. 7A–I).

Growing evidence highlights the pivotal role of epigenetic dysregulation—particularly EZH2 overexpression and aberrant H3K27 and H3K9 methylation—in driving tumor aggressiveness and immune evasion in PCa [154, 155]. Zhang et al. [156] engineered an outer membrane vesicle–based nanocarrier displaying programmed death-1 (PD-1) for targeted delivery of curcumol, aiming to modulate tumor immunity and epigenetic abnormalities. By leveraging PD-1/PD-L1 interactions, the OMV–PD-1 platform achieved enhanced tumor localization and immune engagement, while the encapsulated curcumol effectively suppressed EZH2 expression and diminished histone H3K27 and H3K9 trimethylation (Fig. 7J–P). To address the challenges of limited tumor penetration and suboptimal immunotherapeutic efficacy of conventional nanomedicines, Xu et al. [157] developed a tumor acidity–activated polymer/calcium phosphate hybrid nanomotor (PCaPmotor). This nanomotor exploits calcium phosphate dissolution in the acidic TME to generate thermal energy, enabling autonomous propulsion, deep intratumoral penetration, and site-specific release of an anti–PD-L1 antibody. Concurrent delivery of the CDK12 inhibitor THZ531 via the PCaPmotor enhanced tumor immunogenicity by suppressing RNA polymerase II phosphorylation, inducing DNA damage, apoptosis, and immunogenic cell death, provoking lysosomal dysfunction, and restoring MHC-I expression. In PCa models, the PCaP@THZ/αPD-L1 nanomotor demonstrated superior antitumor efficacy and prolonged survival compared to free-drug combinations or single-agent nanotherapies, revealing a robust and synergistic immunotherapeutic effect.

In PCa, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling axis is frequently hyperactivated and has been closely linked to P-glycoprotein upregulation and myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC)–mediated immunosuppression. To simultaneously overcome chemoresistance and immune evasion, Yin et al. [158] developed a prostate-specific nanomedicine strategy targeting this pathway. They engineered glutathione-responsive nanoparticles with PSMA ligands to selectively deliver the dual PI3K/mTOR inhibitor BEZ235 to prostate tumors. These nanoparticles displayed enhanced tumor accumulation and sustained intratumoral drug release, resulting in effective suppression of PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling in both tumor cells and MDSCs. In vivo, PSMA-NP/BEZ significantly inhibited tumor growth and exhibited pronounced synergistic efficacy when combined with paclitaxel or anti–PD-1 therapy. The latter combination induced durable antitumor immune memory and delayed tumor recurrence.

In a complementary vascular-targeted immunotherapeutic approach, Gasparri et al. [159] developed an isoDGR-tagged gold nanoparticle for selective delivery of interleukin-12 (IL-12) to the tumor vasculature, addressing the dose-limiting systemic toxicity that has historically hindered IL-12 clinical translation. By exploiting the high affinity of isoDGR for αvβ3 integrin expressed on tumor endothelial cells, this nanoplatform achieved precise vascular targeting and localized cytokine activity. As a result, antitumor efficacy was attained at picogram-level IL-12 doses—corresponding to a 70–800-fold enhancement in therapeutic potency relative to free IL-12—without detectable systemic toxicity. Notably, low-dose isoDGR–IL-12 nanogold synergized with adoptive T-cell therapy in an autochthonous PCa model, enhancing tumor-specific T-cell effector function and inducing robust tumor regression.

Photodynamic therapy–enhanced immunotherapy

Previous investigations have established that immunogenic cell death (ICD) enhances tumor immunogenicity through the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). However, achieving efficient ICD induction in PCa remains a challenge. Wang et al. [160] developed a transformable supramolecular peptide nanoplatform (TEP-FFG-CRApY) capable of eliciting cascade-amplified immunogenic ferroptosis to surmount the intrinsic immune resistance of PCa. The peptide initially self-assembles into nanoparticles that enable systemic administration and preferential tumor accumulation, then undergoes alkaline phosphatase–mediated transformation into nanofibers within tumor lysosomes. This intralysosomal remodeling induces lysosomal membrane permeabilization and releases endogenous Fe2+. Simultaneously, the nanofibers selectively target and photoinactivate glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), causing uncontrolled lipid peroxidation and potent ferroptotic cell death. Activated T cells secrete interferon-γ, which suppresses f SLC7A11 and SLC3A2 expression, further sensitizing tumor cells to ferroptosis and reinforcing a self-propagating positive feedback loop between ferroptosis and antitumor immunity. In vivo, this strategy produced marked inhibition of primary tumor growth, prolonged survival, pronounced abscopal effects, and effective suppression of pulmonary metastases.

Black phosphorus (BP) nanosheets have emerged as promising agents for photothermal therapy and immunomodulation, yet their clinical translation is limited by pronounced oxidative instability [161]. Selenium incorporation has been shown tosignificantly improve BP stability while providing inherent anticancer and immunoregulatory activities [162]. Building on this rationale, Yan et al. [163] engineered a biomimetic photothermal–immunotherapeutic nanoplatform consisting of selenium-doped black phosphorus nanosheets cloaked with homologous PCa cell membranes (Se@BPNSs@RM-1) to address the immunologically “cold” nature of PCa. Selenium doping enhanced both the structural robustness and photothermal conversion efficiency of BP, while membrane camouflage imparted homologous tumor targeting and prolonged intratumoral retention. Under 808 nm laser irradiation, Se@BPNSs@RM-1 produced localized hyperthermia, inducingdirect tumor cell eradication and potent immunogenic cell death, as evidenced by augmented calreticulin exposure, release of HMGB1 and ATP, elevated intracellular reactive oxygen species, and G0/G1 cell-cycle arrest.

Liu et al. [164] reported a biomimetic immunomodulatory nanoplatform based on cyclic RGD-functionalized, manganese-doped eumelanin nanocomposites (RMnMels) for synergistic hyperthermia-augmented immunotherapy in PCa. Using the intrinsic antioxidative capacity of eumelanin, RMnMels effectively neutralized excessive reactive oxygen species within the TME. This activity drove TAMs repolarization from a pro-tumoral M2-like phenotype toward an antitumoral M1-like state, as demonstrated by increased iNOS, TNF-α, IL-1β, and CD86 expression and concomitant suppression of M2 markers in vitro and in vivo. Upon near-infrared irradiation, RMnMels further generated localized hyperthermia, leading to direct tumor ablation and immunogenic cell death accompanied by enhanced tumor-associated antigen release. This combined immunoregulatory and photothermal activity effectively remodeled the immunosuppressive TME, producing pronounced inhibition of primary tumor growth and substantial reduction of distant metastases in PC3 PCa models after a single administration and single laser exposure, while maintaining a favorable biosafety profile.

Although focal therapies such as laser ablation provide organ-sparing benefits, their durability and risk of tumor recurrence remain inadequately defined. To address these limitations, Lin et al. [165] engineered a dual-functional nanoplatform that couples photothermal therapy (PTT) with immunostimulation for PCa. Indocyanine green (ICG) and the Toll-like receptor 7/8 agonist resiquimod (R848) was co-encapsulated within biodegradable poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles. Following near-infrared laser irradiation, the PLGA–ICG–R848 system enabled efficient photothermal tumor eradication while simultaneously triggering localized immune activation in situ. In a murine RM9 subcutaneous PCa model, intratumoral injection combined with laser exposure led to pronounced tumor growth suppression and a significant expansion of splenic natural killer cell populations, indicating systemic antitumor immune engagement.

Liu et al. [166] designed a PCa–targeted nanoplatform that integrates second near-infrared (NIR-II) photothermal therapy with epitranscriptomic modulation to enhance antitumor immunotherapy. Using 1208 nm laser irradiation, this nanoplatform exhibited high photothermal conversion efficiency and elicited robust immunogenic cell death, while simultaneously increasing global N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA methylation and accelerating the degradation of oncogenic transcripts, including PD-L1 and MYC. In a bilateral prostate tumor model, this synergistic strategy achieved near-complete elimination of both primary and distant lesions, substantially promoted dendritic cell maturation and CD8+ T-cell infiltration, elevated systemic interferon-γ levels, and generated a pronounced abscopal response without observable systemic toxicity.

Nanomedicine-mediated tumor neoantigen vaccines

PCa is characterized by low intrinsic immunogenicity. Despite the development of therapeutic vaccines such as Sipuleucel-T and PROSTVAC, which have advanced intolate-stage clinical trials, their clinical efficacy has remained modest, particularly with improvements in progression-free survival [167–169]. Spherical nucleic acids (SNAs) are a versatile nanotechnology platform that enhances cellular uptake of nucleic acids and potently activates innate immune signaling, notably through Toll-like receptor 9 engagement by CpG oligonucleotides [170, 171]. Qin et al. [88] designed immunostimulatory spherical nucleic acids (IS-SNAs) as a therapeutic vaccine strategy for PCa by co-delivering CpG oligonucleotides with clinically relevant prostate tumor-associated peptide antigens, including prostate-specific antigen (PSA), PSMA, and PAP. Their work demonstrated that the spatial organization of antigens within the SNA architecture is a decisive factor in governing vaccine performance. In particular, HM-SNAs markedly improved the coordinated delivery of antigens and CpG to dendritic cells, enhanced dendritic cell activation and antigen cross-presentation, and induced robust antigen-specific CD8+ T-cell responses, characterized by elevated interferon-γ production, increased polyfunctional effector profiles, and heightened cytotoxic activity. In syngeneic murine PCa models, HM-SNAs significantly delayed tumor progression and increased intratumoral CD8+ T-cell infiltration, while EM-SNAs or linear CpG/peptide mixtures provided minimal therapeutic benefit.

mRNA vaccines are a highly promising cancer immunotherapy modality due to their favorable safety profile, scalability, and flexibility in encoding tumor antigens. However, their clinical translation has been constrained by insufficient magnitude and durability of cytotoxic T-cell responses. To address this limitation, Islam et al. [172] developed an adjuvant-pulsed mRNA nanovaccine that couples efficient antigen delivery with potent innate immune stimulation to amplify antitumor immunity. In this approach, a chemically modified mRNA encoding ovalbumin was co-encapsulated with a palmitic acid–conjugated Toll-like receptor 7/8 agonist (C16-R848) within a polymer–lipid hybrid nanoparticle. This design achieved highly efficient dendritic cell transfection (> 95%) while retaining full adjuvant bioactivity. Compared with adjuvant-free mRNA nanoparticles, the C16-R848–pulsed formulation markedly enhanced MHC class I antigen presentation, expanded antigen-specific CD8+ T-cell populations, and facilitated their effective recruitment into the TME. In syngeneic murine models of PCa, this nanovaccine elicited strong prophylactic and therapeutic antitumor effects, suppressing tumor growth by approximately 84% in preventive vaccination settings and by ~ 60% in established tumors, all without detectable systemic toxicity.

CD70, a transiently expressed co-stimulatory ligand that signals through CD27 on T cells, has gained recognition as a potent amplifier of antitumor immune responses [173–175]. Leveraging this axis, Cao et al. [176] developed a lipid nanoparticle (LNP)–based mRNA vaccine that co-delivers the tumor-associated antigen 5T4 along with the immune co-stimulatory molecule CD70 to reinforce antitumor immunity in PCa models. Co-administration of 5T4 and CD70 mRNA-LNPs significantly improved both humoral and cellular immune responses compared with 5T4 mRNA alone. This was reflected by an increase in 5T4-specific IgG titers, elevated interferon-γ and tumor necrosis factor-α secretion by CD8+ T cells, augmented cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–mediated tumor cell killing, and robust activation of natural killer cells capable of mediating antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (Fig. 8A–G). In a complementary approach, Li et al. [177] developed a patient-tailored nanovaccine by coating R837-loaded poly(2-oxazoline) nanoparticles with surgically resected PCa cell membranes, integrating individualized tumor-associated antigen repertoires with strong innate immune stimulation. This biomimetic nanovaccine efficiently trafficked to draining lymph nodes and was preferentially internalized by plasmacytoid dendritic cells, where Toll-like receptor 7 activation drove pronounced pDC maturation and robust secretion of type I interferon (IFN-α) as well as pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and IL-12 (Fig. 8H–R).

Nanomedicine-based immunotherapy for bladder cancer

Bladder cancer (BC) is among the most frequently diagnosed malignancies of the genitourinary system [178]. Due to its immunogenicity, direct luminal accessibility, and clinical success of intravesical immunotherapy, bladder cancer occupies is essential to the immunotherapeutic landscape of urologic cancers. High recurrence rates and heterogeneity in immune responses call for nanomedicine-enabled local drug delivery, controlled immune activation, and combination strategies to overcome therapeutic resistance. Nanoparticle-based approaches for nonsurgical bladder cancer treatment have shown promise by prolonging drug residence time and enhancing interactions with diseased tissues [179, 180].

Targeted delivery–mediated immunotherapy

Effective ICIs therapy requires infiltration and activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes, which are frequently compromised by tumor-derived immunosuppressive cytokines and the accumulation of regulatory T cells. Astragaloside IV, a major bioactive constituent of Astragali Radix, exerts immunomodulatory effects, including inhibition of STAT3/NF-κB signaling and modulation of T-cell recruitment. Its clinical applicability are limited by poor aqueous solubility and low systemic bioavailability. Kong et al. [181] developed rod-shaped PPA@aPD-L1 nanomicelles with an average diameter of 200 nm, achieving a high astragaloside IV encapsulation efficiency (90.92%) and a substantial anti-PD-L1 antibody conjugation efficiency (60.24%). In vivo fluorescence imaging revealed preferential accumulation and prolonged retention of PPA@aPD-L1 within bladder tumors. Using an orthotopic MB-49 bladder cancer mouse model, PPA@aPD-L1 suppressed tumor growth, reducing tumor volume and weight and a tumor growth inhibition (TGI) rate of 84.6%, outperforming PPA monotherapy and the combination of PPA and free anti-PD-L1 antibody. This work demonstrates that astragaloside IV increases PD-L1 blockade by reprogramming the tumor immune microenvironment via an antibody-functionalized nanocarrier.

The cyclic GMP–AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS–STING) pathway is developmentally crucial for antitumor immunotherapies [182, 183]. Peng et al. [184] introduced an innovative intravesical immunotherapeutic approach for bladder cancer based on a self-propelled, in situ–polymerized nanoplatform that activates the cGAS–STING signaling. Through the infusion of dopamine hydrochloride, Mn2+, cyclic GMP–AMP (cGAMP), and urease into the bladder, Mn-cGAMP@polydopamine–urease (DMCU) nanoparticles were generated in situ via dopamine polymerization under mildly alkaline conditions. In an orthotopic MB49 bladder cancer mouse model, intravesical administration of DMCU reduced tumor burden, 100% survival, upregulated systemic and local proinflammatory cytokines, and enhanced immune cell infiltration, while maintaining a biosafety profile. This study highlights how the synergistic integration of in situ polymerization, self-propelled nanotechnology, and targeted STING pathway activation can avoid the limitations of conventional intravesical therapeutic strategies.

Photodynamic therapy–enhanced immunotherapy

Advanced malignancies frequently display poor responsiveness to conventional therapies and ICIs, due to inadequate immune cell infiltration and the establishment of a immunosuppressive tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) [185]. Although immunotherapy can elicit clinical responses, its therapeutic benefit is confined to a limited subset of patients. Evidence indicates that dendritic cell (DC) maturation is key for the initiation of effective antitumor immunity, and that Toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists, stimulator of interferon genes (STING) agonists, and immunogenic cell death (ICD) are capable of promoting DC activation. Their clinical translation is blocked by systemic toxicity, weak delivery efficiency, and insufficient activation of immune signaling pathways [186, 187]. Xue et al. [188] developed PDR nanoparticles including a pheophorbide-a–doxorubicin conjugate (PhD) for combined phototherapy and chemotherapy, with encapsulation of the TLR7 agonist R837 to provide immunostimulatory activity. STING pathway activation was raised IFN-β and CXCL10 expression, along with increased phosphorylation of TBK1 and IRF3 following phototherapy. In vivo studies show pronounced tumor accumulation of PDR nanoparticles and improved pharmacokinetics of R837, reflected by a 6.6-fold increase in the area under the curve and an extension of the half-life from 0.7 to 1.7 h. In bilateral bladder tumor models, PDR nanoparticles together with laser irradiation effectively removed primary tumors and inhibited the growth of distant lesions, with the most antitumor responses observed upon combination with α-PD-1 therapy. This work shows importance of immune activation in achieving cancer immunotherapy and provides a framework for the design of multifunctional, tumor-responsive nanomedicines. Future works may focus on extending this platform to additional tumor types, developing laser-independent or deeper-penetrating activation strategies, incorporating supplementary immunomodulators, and evaluating combinatorial regimens with other ICIs or radiotherapy (Fig. 9A–J).

Nanozymes paired with peroxidase-like catalytic activity and photothermal functionality have increasing interest as alternatives to natural enzymes, due to their superior stability, structural tunability, and multifunctionality. Wu et al. [189] established a light-activated nanozyme-based oxidative stress amplification strategy (Pd@CPT) to address the shortage of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) within the TME that limits the success of nanozyme-mediated catalytic therapies. In this system, a palladium (Pd) nanozyme exhibiting peroxidase-, superoxide dismutase–, and glutathione oxidase–like activities was co-encapsulated with camptothecin (CPT) within a polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) matrix, allow spatiotemporally controlled catalytic activation under 808 nm near-infrared irradiation. Chuang et al. [190] assembled iron-containing glycol chitosan–polypyrrole nanozymes (GCS-I-PPy NZs) that demonstrated favorable biocompatibility, X-ray–induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, driving M1 phenotype polarization. Following X-ray exposure, GCS-I-PPy NZs increased intratumoral ROS levels, enhanced X-ray–induced photodynamic therapy (XPDT) success, and reduced the immunosuppressive TME. In vivo evaluation in an MB49 bladder cancer model showed that combined treatment with GCS-I-PPy NZs and X-ray irradiation inhibited tumor progression compared to other models, accompanied by increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration, upregulated CD86 expression, and reduced CD206 expression. The same group [191] developed a biomimetic photoimmunotherapeutic nanoplatform by integrating extracellular vesicles derived from M1 macrophages with molybdenum disulfide nanosheets (M1 EVs@MoS2) for bladder cancer therapy. The M1 macrophage–derived vesicle coating enhanced MoS₂ dispersion, tumor-homing capability, and biocompatibility, while preserving infrared photothermal and photocatalytic properties potentency. Near-infrared irradiation, M1 EVs@MoS2 achieved efficient tumor destruction via hyperthermia and ROS generation, while remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment by promoting M1 macrophage polarization, facilitating dendritic cell maturation, and increasing the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and natural killer cells.

Deng et al. [192] engineered a T-cell membrane–camouflaged nanoplatform (Tim3@PHSM@IC) that synergistically integrates photothermal therapy (PTT) with cuproptosis to strengthen bladder cancer immunotherapy. This construct comprised hollow mesoporous silica nanoparticles co-loaded with the photothermal agent IR820 and the cuproptosis inducer CuET, later coated with polydopamine and covered with membranes derived from Tim-3–overexpressing T cells. Upon near-infrared irradiation, Tim3@PHSM@IC generated pronounced photothermal effects and triggered copper-dependent cuproptosis, resulting in immunogenic cell death marked by extracellular ATP release, calreticulin exposure, and HMGB1 secretion. Multiple murine bladder cancer modelsshowed this strategy inhibited both primary and distant tumor growth, promoted dendritic cell maturation and CD8+ T-cell infiltration, reduced immunosuppressive myeloid cell populations, and shifted the TME toward an immunostimulatory phenotype. These findings indicate that photothermal-activated cuproptosis combined with immune checkpoint modulation forms a promising strategy for overcoming resistance to immunotherapy in bladder cancer.

Chin et al. [193] engineered a multifunctional, cluster-structured nanoplatform composed of iron oxide and chlorophyll–iron complexes (Fe3O4@Chl/Fe CNPs) for intravesical bladder cancer therapy, integrating photodynamic therapy (PDT), chemodynamic therapy (CDT), ferroptosis induction, and immune modulation within a single system. Upon red-light irradiation, the chlorophyll–iron component generated singlet oxygen to initiate PDT, while the iron species catalyzed Fenton-like reactions that produced hydroxyl radicals, depleted intracellular glutathione, and triggered lipid peroxidation–driven ferroptosis. In an orthotopic bladder cancer mouse model, treatment with Fe3O4@Chl/Fe CNPs resulted in tumor growth inhibition and prolonged survival, emphasizing the promise of ferroptosis-coupled photodynamic immunotherapy as a localized, immune-activating nanomedicine strategy for bladder cancer.

Nanomedicine-mediated immune reprogramming

Pyroptosis is a immunogenic mode of programmed cell death driven by gasdermin family proteins, characterized by the release of proinflammatory cytokines and tumor-associated antigens that can amplify antitumor immune responses. Effective induction of pyroptosis in vivo remains challenging due to drug dilution and rapid systemic clearance [194, 195]. Tian et al. [196] developed a biomimetic immunotherapeutic strategy for bladder cancer based on a platelet-camouflaged nanoplatform (nano-Erda@PLT) designed to trigger tumor pyroptosis while reshaping the immunosuppressive TME. Erdafitinib was nanoformulated and encapsulated within platelet membranes, enabling prolonged blood circulation and preferential tumor accumulation through platelet-mediated targeting. Pyroptosis induced by nano-Erda@PLT promoted dendritic cell maturation and significantly enhanced cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, converting an immune-suppressive microenvironment into an immunostimulatory niche. In murine bladder cancer models, nano-Erda@PLT achieved tumor growth inhibition (88.31 ± 4.22%) and extended overall survival compared with free erdafitinib, while maintaining a favorable biosafety profile. Sonodynamic therapy (SDT) is as a noninvasive therapeutic approach capable of inducing reactive oxygen species (ROS)–mediated tumor cell death and augmenting tumor immunogenicity. To overcome fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) inhibition and immunotherapy resistance, Yang et al. [197] engineered a ROS-responsive pseudo-semiconducting polymer (PSP) that is both a sonosensitizer and an oxidation-degradable carrier. Erdafitinib was co-encapsulated with PSP into nanoparticles (NP-PE) using an oxidation-sensitive polymer matrix. Upon ultrasound irradiation, NP-PE–mediated SDT synergized with anti–PD-L1 therapy, leading to enhancement of antitumor immune responses in otherwise immune-cold tumors.

Wang et al. [198] developed an ultrasound-responsive nanoplatform (SPCP/CCP@Bay) to increase disulfidptosis-driven immunotherapy in bladder cancer by coupling metabolic intervention with sonodynamic therapy. This multifunctional nanocomposite co-encapsulates a glucose transporter 1 (Glut1) inhibitor (Bay-876), a cystine-containing polymer, and a degradable sonosensitizing polymer, enabling preferential tumor accumulation and ultrasound-triggered reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. Upon ultrasound activation, SPCP/CCP@Bay concurrently inhibits glucose uptake, disrupts NADP+/NADPH redox homeostasis, and promotes intracellular cystine accumulation, activating the RAC1/WAVE2 signaling cascade, inducing F-actin contraction, and triggering disulfidptosis. Disulfidptosis-oriented nanomedicine is a promising strategy for synergistic cancer immunotherapy. Wu et al. [199] developed a mesoporous polydopamine (PDA)-based composite nanorod platform to enhance immunotherapeutic efficacy in bladder cancer through the integration of drug delivery, TME modulation, and phototherapy. The nanorods, composed of MnO2@PDA and functionalized with acid-responsive 2,3-dimethylmaleic anhydride and wheat germ agglutinin, demonstrated prolonged bladder retention, pH-triggered surface charge reversal, and enhanced cellular internalization. Co-delivery of anti–PD-L1 antibodies synergized with phototherapy to induce tumor cell apoptosis, increase intratumoral infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, and upregulate proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-2, IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ, in vivo. The synergistic integration of nanozyme-mediated TME remodeling with phototherapy-augmented immunotherapy is key to the work by activating antitumor immune responses while overcoming the limitations of bladder cancer drug delivery and PD-L1 monotherapy.

Intratumoral copper accumulation has been shown to correlate positively with PD-L1 expression, showing copper metabolism regulates immune checkpoints Guo et al. [200] developed a reactive oxygen species (ROS)–responsive nanoplatform (NP@ESCu) to induce copper-dependent cell death (cuproptosis) while potentiating ICB in bladder cancer. NP@ESCu reprogrammed the tumor immune microenvironment by promoting dendritic cell maturation, enhancing CD8+ T-cell infiltration, increasing the M1/M2 macrophage ratio, and reducing the accumulation of MDSCs, transforming immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors. Copper-mediated upregulation of PD-L1 rendered tumors more susceptible to αPD-L1 therapy, and combined treatment with NP@ESCu and αPD-L1 produced superior tumor suppression outperforming either monotherapy. This study provides the first in vivo evidence that nanomedicine-induced cuproptosis boosts cancer immunotherapy (Fig. 10A–I).

Cheng et al. [201] reported a bioactive, light-responsive gold nanohybrid (Au/2ATP@PGlyco) capable of macrophage reprogramming through metabolic modulation and photodynamic stimulation. The nanoplatform integrates a galactose-functionalized, polyaniline-based glycopolymer shell with an Au–S (2-aminothiophenol) interfacial architecture, enabling selective targeting of M2-like macrophages via galactose receptors while enhancing plasmon-mediated charge transfer to boost singlet oxygen generation under low-power 660 nm irradiation. This strategy achieved an exceptionally high M2-to-M1 polarization efficiency (up to ~ 97% under optimized conditions). Reprogrammed macrophages displayed augmented phagocytic capacity and secreted elevated levels of TNF-α and IL-12, resulting in pronounced apoptosis of bladder cancer cells in coculture models. This establishes a framework for integrating metabolic biostimulation with photodynamic immune modulation for control over macrophage phenotypes, with future studies warranted to validate its therapeutic potential in vivo (Fig. 10J–N).

Resiquimod, a toll-like receptor 7/8 (TLR7/8) agonist, can repolarize immunosuppressive M2 macrophages toward a proinflammatory M1 phenotype; however, its clinical translation is delayed by systemic toxicity and inefficient tumor accumulation [202]. Geng et al. [203] engineered a reactive oxygen species (ROS)–responsive resiquimod (R848) prodrug nanoparticle system designed to alleviate macrophage-mediated immunosuppression and potentiate ICIs therapy in bladder cancer. In murine MB49 bladder cancer models, this nanotherapeutic strategy achieved pronounced tumor growth inhibition of up to 90% and improved survival, while combination treatment with anti–PD-1 further increased antitumor efficacy, reaching approximately 98% tumor inhibition. Comparable immunomodulatory and antitumor effects were validated in patient-derived bladder cancer organoid models, highlighting it’s tranlational potential. Shang et al. [204] developed a thermosensitive lipid nanoparticle system co-encapsulating the TLR7/8 agonist resiquimod (R848) and a photothermal agent (DTPA) overcoming the immunosuppressive TME and limited responsiveness to ICB in bladder cancer. Upon 635 nm laser irradiation, R848/DTPA@DSPE-PEG nanoparticles generated localized hyperthermia and reactive oxygen species, inducing immunogenic cell death showed increased expression of calreticulin (CRT), high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), and heat shock protein 70 (HSP70), while simultaneously enabling pH- and laser-triggered release of R848. This promoted dendritic cell maturation and reprogrammed TAMs toward an M1-like phenotype, increasing CD8+ T-cell infiltration and M2 macrophages and MDSCs in subcutaneous and orthotopic bladder cancer models. This work underscores macrophage-focused immune reprogramming in overcoming ICI resistance and highlights the potential of externally triggerable nanomedicines for precise modulation of tumor immunity.

Nanomedicine-mediated immunotherapy sensitization and synergistic amplification

Intravesical Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG), a live-attenuated mycobacterial immunotherapy, is the standard treatment for high-risk non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC). In certain settings, BCG has been combined with intravesical chemotherapeutic agents (e.g., mitomycin C) to improve local control; however, overall efficacy remains limited by drug washout, insufficient bladder retention, and local or systemic toxicities [205]. Combination regimens incorporating BCG and intravesical chemotherapy show partial clinical benefit, but overall efficacy is constrained by rapid drug washout, inadequate bladder retention, and dose-limiting local or systemic toxicities [206, 207]. Liu et al. [208] developed and employed a robust biotin–streptavidin anchoring strategy to immobilize doxorubicin (DOX)–loaded poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles onto the surface of live BCG, thereby generating a DOX@BCG hybrid formulation. This construct suppressed tumor progression and prolonged survival in orthotopic bladder cancer models. DOX@BCG integrates BCG-elicited innate and adaptive immune activation with DOX-induced immunogenic tumor cell death, leading to sustained antitumor immunity within the bladder TME. Future studies may focus on loading alternative chemotherapeutic or immunomodulatory cargos onto BCG, optimizing nanoparticle composition to achieve controlled drug release, and combining this platform with ICIs. This work supports the development of next generation intravesical combination therapies aimed at overcoming BCG failure and improving outcomes in high-risk NMIBC.

Samaddar et al. [209] reported a biomimetic, non-bacterial immunotherapeutic strategy that recapitulates key adhesion mechanisms of Bacillus Calmette–Guérin. The authors engineered RWFV peptide–functionalized, pH-responsive lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) designed to target the fibronectin-rich bladder TME and deliver CpG oligonucleotides to immune cells. These targeted LNPs elicited potent toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9)–mediated immune activation, as evidenced by upregulated expression of co-stimulatory molecules (CD83, CD86, and MHC II) and enhanced TNF-α secretion, without detectable cytotoxicity. This study presents a grounded nanoplatform that emulates essential BCG functions and highlights stimulus-responsive, targeted LNPs as a promising alternative strategy for bladder cancer immunotherapy.

Translational and clinical challenges, regulatory considerations, and future perspectives

Despite substantial preclinical progress, the clinical translation of nanomedicine-enabled immunotherapy for malignancies of the urinary system is hindered by several interrelated challenges. First, PCa, RCC, and bladder cancer display profoundly distinct immune landscapes, metabolic programs, and stromal architectures, all of which dynamically evolve during disease development and in response to therapy. Most nanomedicine platforms are developed and validated using simplified or single-model systems, which fail to fully capture intratumoral heterogeneity, immune cell plasticity, and therapy-induced adaptive resistance. Consequently, there is a growing need to evaluate using more physiologically relevant models, including orthotopic, immunocompetent, large-animal systems, to better assess clinical applicability and translatability.

Second, achieving efficient, robust, and reproducible tumor delivery remains a bottleneck for translation. Although targeted delivery strategies are conceptually appealing, their effectiveness is often limited by heterogeneous target expression, ligand–receptor saturation, and unintended off-target accumulation. Moreover, many advanced nanoplatforms depend on complex surface functionalization or multicomponent architectures, which can affect physicochemical stability, scalability, and batch-to-batch reproducibility. In addition, rapid systemic clearance following intravenous administration often leads to reduced retention at the tumor site, which limits effective interactions with target tissues and ultimately diminishes therapeutic efficacy.

Third, the safety and immunotoxicity profiles of nanomedicine-enabled immunotherapies remain incompletely understood. Many nanoplatforms are intentionally designed to amplify immune activation through mechanisms such as immunogenic cell death induction, innate immune stimulation, or cytokine signaling. While these properties underpin their therapeutic efficacy, excessive or poorly controlled immune activation can result in systemic inflammation, cytokine release syndrome, or immune-related adverse events. Compounding these concerns, the in vivo fate of nanoparticles—including their metabolism, biodistribution, and clearance—has not been fully elucidated [210]. The lack of comprehensive long-term biosafety data, particularly regarding immune memory disruption, off-target macrophage activation, and the accumulation of metallic or polymeric components, further lowers regulatory confidence and poses a significant barrier to clinical translation.

Fourth, the structural and functional complexity of multifunctional nanoplatforms presents major challenges for scalable manufacturing and regulatory translation. Many high-performance nano-systems combine multiple therapeutic modalities—such as phototherapy, catalytic therapy, gene delivery, and immune checkpoint modulation—within a single construct. While mechanistically appealing, these designs often require complex synthesis, precise stoichiometric control, and stringent quality assurance, complicating large-scale production under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) conditions. Regulatory pathways for such hybrid systems remain poorly defined, and translating laboratory-scale success to industrial manufacturing presents significant technical, economic, and logistical hurdles. Integrating computational modeling with systematic experimental validation may help streamline design and improve translatability [211].

Finally, patient stratification and biomarker-guided treatment selection remain underdeveloped. Immunotherapeutic responses are highly patient-specific, yet most nanomedicine studies assume immune homogeneity and apply broadly uniform treatment strategies without accounting for baseline immune status, molecular heterogeneity, or tumor burden. Clinical translation is further constrained by uncertainties in trial design, including optimal dosing, treatment duration, and response evaluation. Accordingly, although preclinical results are promising, clinical efficacy remains difficult to predict, and clinical trials are often costly and time-consuming [211].

Despite these challenges, nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems have significantly improved the efficacy of nonsurgical treatments for urinary system-related malignancies. A key future goal is to develop nanomedicine platforms that can adapt to disease states and respond to the local immune environment. Designing nanoplatforms that respond to hypoxia, redox imbalance, enzymatic activity, or immune signaling cues may allow more precise spatial and temporal control of immune modulation. In particular, stimuli-responsive systems—including photodynamic, photothermal, sonodynamic, and radiation-activated nanoplatforms—allow on-demand immune activation with high spatial selectivity, thereby enhancing intratumoral immunogenicity while minimizing systemic toxicity. Integration with molecular imaging techniques may further enable real-time therapeutic monitoring, dose optimization, and adaptive treatment adjustment.

Nanomedicine-based tumor neoantigen vaccines are expected to play an increasingly prominent role in personalized immunotherapy. Advances in high-throughput sequencing, epitope prediction, and mRNA nanotechnology now enable the rapid generation of patient-specific vaccines capable of eliciting robust cytotoxic T-cell responses. Future efforts should focus on enhancing antigen cross-presentation, producing durable immune memory, and combining nanovaccines with ICB or TME reprogramming. Simplifying and standardizing nanoplatform design will be essential for eventual clinical translation, with emphasis on modular, scalable, and manufacturable systems that balance multifunctionality with regulatory feasibility. Ultimately, personalized nanomedicine offers the potential to deliver safe, effective, and highly specific cancer immunotherapies, advancing precision medicine beyond one-size-fits all treatment paradigms [212–215].

Conclusion

Urologic malignancies impose a substantial and growing global health burden, with incidence rates continuing to rise in many regions. This review highlights how nanomedicine has moved beyond passive drug delivery to actively shape tumor–immune interactions and enhance cancer immunotherapy through multiple mechanisms. Despite encouraging preclinical advances, clinical translation remains limited. Key barriers include heterogeneous tumor delivery, insufficient long-term safety evaluation, challenges in manufacturing and scalability, and the lack of robust biomarker-guided patient stratification. Addressing these obstacles will require a strategic shift toward simplified, scalable, and immune–responsive nanomedicine platforms, integrated with molecular imaging, systems immunology, and precision oncology approaches. With multidisciplinary collaboration, nanomedicine holds promise to overcome current limitations in immunotherapy and enable more precise, durable, and personalized treatments for urinary system cancers.
