Frost damage segmentation in grapevine organs using YOLOv11s with ASPP and dynamic confidence thresholding
Authors: Kaan Arık, Erdal Büyükbıçakcı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Climate change, particularly increasing frequency and intensity of spring frost events, poses a serious threat to viticulture by reducing yield and product quality. This study proposes an image processing and machine learning-based framework for early, rapid, and accurate segmentation of frost damage in vineyards using YOLOv11s enhanced with Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling (ASPP). A unique dataset called FGVL dataset from Sultana seedless grape vineyards in Manisa, Türkiye, following a severe frost event in April 2025. FGVL includes 418 frost-damaged grapes, 510 frost-damaged leaves, 395 healthy grapes, and 698 healthy leaves, all manually annotated by experts under natural field conditions. By integrating ASPP into YOLOv11s, proposed model improved multi-scale contextual feature extraction and achieved mAP@50 of 0.7686, demonstrating stronger performance in instance segmentation of small, overlapping, and visually similar grapevine organs. In addition, Dynamic Confidence Thresholding (DCT) strategy was introduced to improve prediction reliability in dense and visually complex vineyard scenes. Despite challenges such as background clutter, object overlap, and small target structures, model maintained stable performance with low computational demand, requiring only 6.45 GB of GPU memory. Proposed framework offers an accurate, efficient, and practically deployable early recognition system for frost damage assessment in viticulture.

Introduction

Climate change is a growing concern worldwide, with rising temperatures in atmosphere and oceans leading to significant changes in ecosystems1. Not only altering average temperatures, this situation also is having a significant negative impact on agricultural production by changing rainfall patterns, wind patterns, and frequency of extreme weather events2. Sensitive crops, such as grapevines, are particularly at high risk in terms of changes in phenological cycles, yield declines, and quality deterioration3. Rising average temperatures due to climate change are causing plants to awaken early and become vulnerable to sudden cold spells4. This situation is increasing frequency and intensity of spring frosts, particularly in Europe and North America, leading to serious economic losses in agricultural production. For example, frost events that occurred across Europe in April 2021 caused significant damage to many agricultural products, particularly vineyards and fruit trees, with approximately 80% of France’s wine and fruit production regions affected5. In Turkey, irregular and significant increase in frost events between 2016 and 2024 has seriously affected plants in Marmara, Aegean, and Central Anatolia regions.

Figure 1 shows how a specific climate-related event, snow-covered or cold days, has changed over time and how it might change in future6. Black line, based on observations from 1950 to 2020, shows that this event has generally remained at a steady level, even though it has fluctuated from year to year. Projections after 2020 indicate a scenario-dependent decline in these days, sharper under high emissions, implying fewer and shorter events in Southern Europe and Turkey7,8. As of 2023, total area of vineyards worldwide (for table grapes, wine grapes, and raisins) reached approximately 7.2 million hectares, with Turkey ranking as world’s fifth-largest grape producer, with 410,000 hectares of vineyards9. Grape sector has major economic significance worldwide, with annual trade exceeding $30 billion and generating roughly $420 million in added value in Turkey10.Fig. 1European Environment Agency’s Predictions Frost-Related Events from 1950 To 2100.

Sultana seedless grape (also as know “Vitis vinifera”) is a medium, thin-skinned, elliptical grape variety with a light green-yellow color, known internationally as “Sultana”11. Widely cultivated in Turkey, particularly in Aegean provinces such as Manisa, Izmir, and Denizli, Sultana can yield 15–18 t/ha and benefits from Turkey’s diverse Mediterranean–continental microclimates, enabling early harvest in late August–early September12,13. However, climate change is altering flowering and fruit ripening timings, increasing risk of frost, and it is projected that over next 30 years, frequency and intensity of frost events will increase further, particularly in Northern Hemisphere. In this region, sudden cold spells following rising temperatures in spring months are causing significant damage to vineyards. Frost damage harms cell walls of grapes, causes water loss and metabolic imbalance, and leads to irregular increases in soluble solids (sugar) content and significant deviations in breakdown of organic acids14. This situation directly negatively affects chemical composition of grapes and, consequently, quality of wine and dried grapes by disrupting acidity-sugar balance, particularly through changes in tartaric and malic acid levels. Phenological shifts caused by climate change have led to an average of 9–13 days earlier budburst and flowering periods in vineyards, increasing risk of late spring frosts15.

In this context, early and accurate recognition of frost damage using image processing methods has become critically important for separating damaged products and protecting agricultural yields. Against this background, we contextualize vineyard production in Turkey and worldwide, highlight Turkey’s geographical suitability for grape cultivation, and summarize agronomic and economic relevance of Sultana seedless variety, before synthesizing prior evidence on how climate-change-driven phenological shifts increase frost vulnerability.

Related works

Modern precision agriculture increasingly leverages image processing for scalable, high-accuracy monitoring16, where satellite and UAV imagery combined with vegetation indices (e.g., NDVI, EVI) enables early detection of stress signals such as disease emergence, water stress, and nutrient deficiency17,18. Deep learning-based Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) analysis, further supports automated recognition and pixel-level delineation of crop conditions (e.g., fruit classification and leaf damage segmentation), facilitating timely and spatially targeted management interventions (fertilizer, pesticide, irrigation)19. Traditionally, frost damage detection was performed through visual inspection and field measurements in vineyards. However, in recent years, image processing techniques have increasingly been used for early detection of frost formation and automatic assessment of post-frost damage20. A comprehensive review from 2023 examined over 80 image-based studies on frost detection, revealing that a wide range of approaches, from statistical methods to deep learning, have been successfully applied21. These studies utilize various sources, including satellite images, thermal recordings taken by drones, and RGB camera data from ground22.

Satellite-based frost damage monitoring is an effective method for determining frost risk and damage in vineyards23. Another study investigated impact of late frost in April 2020 on vineyards in Ningxia region of China using a practical remote sensing monitoring framework24. Gaofen-1, Landsat-8, and Sentinel-2 data were combined to map vineyards and frost damage for 20–25 April 2020 using thermal/ESTARFM-derived temperatures and Sentinel-2 vegetation indices for post-frost change detection25,26. Following a frost event in Lodi vineyards of California in 2023, a study conducted by Sentinel-2 NDVI change analysis to detect damage27. Yield losses in primary and secondary shoots due to frost were confirmed by field measurements, and results demonstrated that satellite-derived data can show distribution of frost-induced damage28.

When we review drone-based solutions, drones play an important role in frost detection and protection efforts by providing more detailed and flexible imaging capabilities at vineyard scale29. Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAVs) to equipped with thermal cameras, including systems integrating acoustic tomography and long-wave thermal sensing, can map nocturnal vineyard microclimates and track frost dynamics over large areas by overlaying temperature fields on GPS-based maps30–32. These studies have demonstrated that drones can be effectively used, particularly for early detection of frost formation and optimization of preventive heating/circulation strategies. Beyond detection, drones have also been deployed for active frost protection, as demonstrated by vineyards’ 2021 field trial using an unmanned helicopter for frost-prevention flights while concurrently enabling post-event identification of weakened, high-risk areas via remote image analysis33. Ground-based image processing and deep learning also utilize images captured by cameras closer to the ground at vineyard scale, along with deep learning algorithms, to assess frost damage34. Object detection and segmentation models are applied to automatically identify damage in plant organs such as grape clusters and leaves. Pinheiro et al. (2023) trained three deep-learning detectors on 10,010 RGB images and reported YOLOv5 as the best performer for cluster detection and condition classification (≈72% mAP50, 92% F1)35. There are studies in literature that use Mask R-CNN-based segmentation to detect leaf diseases in vineyards36. Recent progress in deep learning–based instance and semantic segmentation suggest strong potential for delineating frost-dried shoots or leaf regions, as evidenced by successful vine disease detection from UAV multispectral (visible–infrared) imagery using optimized image registration37. Additionally, advanced sensor technologies, such as hyperspectral imaging, show promise in early detection of frost stress38. The greatest challenge in working with hyperspectral data is need for large, labeled datasets across hundreds of channels at different wavelengths. To address this issue, in 2023, Ruan and colleagues developed a model capable of identifying frost stress with very few examples using meta-learning (few-shot learning) techniques39. Through a meta-learning approach, model achieved 89.1% accuracy and 93.1% recall with limited data, enabling it to accurately distinguish whether plants were under frost stress. It even achieved approximately 70% accuracy in classifying frost severity.

Image processing-based approaches, such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), have revolutionized field of image processing by enabling automatic extraction of complex spatial features from raw pixel-level data40. From image classification to segmentation, face recognition to medical image analysis, CNNs have established a foundational infrastructure that is insensitive to time but highly detailed in spatial relationships41. YOLO (You Only Look Once) family takes CNN-based architecture a step further by solving real-time object detection problem in a single pass (single-shot)42. Unlike traditional two-stage pipelines, YOLO performs grid-based class and localization prediction in a single shot, enabling millisecond-level, real-time monitoring and intervention on platforms such as drones and agricultural robots for tasks including fruit cluster detection, pest classification, and damage assessment43. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and variants such as LSTM/GRU can complement image-based models by capturing temporal dynamics in video and time-series signals (e.g., sequential CNN features) to support spatio-temporal prediction of vineyard growth cycles, frost risk, and harvest timing, enabling real-time monitoring, early warning, and dynamic decision-support in agriculture44,45. Despite growing interest in agricultural monitoring, a comprehensive, open-access dataset specifically labeled for grape frost damage—combining visible light-band satellite data and thermal remote sensing imagery46—is still absent from literature. In this context, our dataset offers a novel contribution by combining to grape frost damage detection research by combining high-resolution RGB images and masks showing frost damage at pixel level under same structure.

Primary objective of this study is to develop an image processing and machine learning-based system that enables early, rapid, and accurate segmentation of frost damage in vineyards. In this context, a novel dataset comprising images of frost-affected leaves and grape clusters collected from vineyards was constructed and annotated according to the presence and severity of frost damage. The proposed model was designed to perform pixel-level segmentation of both healthy and frost-damaged leaves and grape clusters. Ultimately, main goal is to integrate developed system into agricultural production processes so that it can be used as a decision support tool for farmers to detect frost damage, thereby becoming a low-cost and applicable technology.

Materials and methods

Section "Materials and methods" details the overall methodological pipeline adopted in this study, from dataset acquisition to model evaluation. It first describes geographical location and vineyard properties associated with FGVL dataset, followed by image pre-processing and augmentation steps performed prior to training. Finally, section outlines model training and parameter tuning procedures, together with evaluation metrics used to quantify model performance.

Vineyard properties of FGVL (frost grapevine and leaf dataset)

Our dataset was collected from a vineyard located in Sarıgöl District, Manisa, where Sultana seedless grape variety is intensively cultivated. Vineyard is planted in a north–south direction at coordinates 38°19′23.3" N 28°42′39.6"E, at an altitude of 194 m. These geographical advantages, particularly moisture balance created by water body and wind protection provided by hills, act as a natural buffer against climatic fluctuations in region. However, vineyard’s location in valley poses a risk of cold air masses settling during winter and spring months, making it particularly susceptible to frost events.

A severe frost event occurred on nights of 9–10 April 2025, with temperatures dropping to approximately 4°C (39°F) during the frost period. Meteorological records indicated a relative humidity of 87% and a westward wind speed of approximately 19 km/h (12 mph), collectively resulting in advection-type frost. The vineyard’s location in a valley at low elevation facilitated cold air settling, creating conditions conducive to severe frost damage despite moderating microclimate effects of nearby water bodies and hills. A comprehensive damage assessment conducted on 12 April 2025 revealed that nearly 95% of the 6.5-hectare vineyard area was affected, with 80% of shoots per vine and 90% of grape clusters sustaining damage. High-resolution images were subsequently captured during daylight hours under natural lighting conditions, with close-up focus applied wherever possible to document the effects of frost damage on leaves, shoots, and grape clusters — particularly tissue loss, discoloration, and signs of desiccation. Although image acquisition was performed three days after frost event, damage symptoms remained fresh and clearly visible, providing an ideal timeframe for training instance segmentation algorithms and representing realistic field conditions suitable for applied machine learning tasks in agricultural monitoring.

The homogeneous land use in the area — predominantly vineyards with only sparse olive trees on distant mountain slopes — likely contributed to the uniform and widespread nature of the observed damage. Our dataset confirms that frost damage in April is an exceptionally rare occurrence in this region, with no similarly severe cold wave recorded in recent decades. As shown in Fig. 2, the frost event caused visually evident damage across grape clusters and leaves, underscoring both the intensity of the cold wave and the unusual nature of this early spring episode. Given the rarity of such events, open-access visual datasets documenting spring frost damage in vineyards remain extremely limited, if not entirely absent from the literature, making the present dataset particularly valuable for academic studies and practical applications such as precision viticulture, climate anomaly detection, and crop protection. The documentation of this rare meteorological episode and the absence of comparable examples in existing databases contribute significantly to the scientific originality of this study, which addresses an important gap in the visual documentation and analysis of early spring frost damage in vineyards. Image pre-processing and data augmentation

The raw dataset, totaling 3.2 GB, was compressed to 236 MB using the Python-based Pillow (PIL) library at 80% quality ratio, resulting in approximately 93% reduction in storage size. All images in the dataset were standardized to a resolution of 1512 × 2016 pixels, 96 DPI, and 24-bit color depth. Auto-orientation was applied to ensure correct image alignment based on the EXIF metadata embedded in images captured via mobile devices or digital cameras, thereby preventing the model from incorrectly associating rotated or sideways instances of leaves and grape clusters with distinct object classes47. This process reduces model’s direction-dependent learning and increases data consistency, improving both classification and segmentation accuracy48. Resize is rescaling of all images to a fixed size (640 × 640 pixels) that homogenizes input data during training. Images with different resolutions increase computational cost and memory consumption of model, as larger input images require networks to process four times as many pixels when size is doubled. It can also create a learning imbalance as feature tensors must be of same size for consistent model training49.

640 × 640 resolution is commonly adopted as a standard input size across many YOLO models. This standardization facilitates faster convergence, more efficient GPU memory utilization, and improved comparability across experiments. Within this framework, YOLO suppresses influence of irrelevant background content and redundant visual details, allowing network to focus more effectively on primary labeled target, particularly in multi-object and visually cluttered scenes50. Nevertheless, due to intrinsic structure of grape clusters and leaves, vineyard images inevitably contain a certain level of visual complexity, including overlap patterns, non-uniform boundaries, texture heterogeneity, and partial occlusions, which should be regarded as natural object characteristics rather than entirely avoidable noise.

Figure 3 illustrates the data augmentation process in which various transformation techniques were applied to enhance the generalization ability of the model against patterns with different variations. In this context, rotating images by 90 °C enabled the model to recognize direction-sensitive objects, such as leaves and grape clusters, from multiple angles. Furthermore, horizontal and vertical flip operations allowed symmetrical and direction-independent objects to be modeled from different perspectives, enabling the model to learn a wider range of appearances from a limited number of examples. The applied shear operation further improved model robustness against perspective shifts encountered in real-world conditions by distorting the geometric structure of objects at a defined angle. Collectively, these transformations increased the variation of segmentation masks while reducing the risk of overfitting in object detection and classification tasks, thereby improving overall accuracy metrics.Fig. 3Sample Data Augmentation Techniques Applied to Dataset. Primary goal of these augmentation techniques is to artificially diversify dataset on which model is trained, making it robust to real-world image conditions. For example, horizontal flipping (Flip) helps model learn symmetrical variations in object orientation. Random shearing (− 15° to + 15°) introduces geometric distortions, simulating perspective changes51. 90° rotation and small-angle rotations (± 15°) ensure robustness against variations caused by different camera angles or misaligned frames52. Cropping random portions of image (0–20%) teaches model to handle partial occlusion and framing inconsistencies. Adjusting image brightness (− 18 to + 18%) enables model to perform well under different lighting conditions53. Gaussian blur (σ = 0.5–2) simulates image degradation due to lens imperfections or motion, helping model generalize better to low-quality or noisy images54

Hyperparameter tuning and model evaluation metrics

Throughout research process, YOLOv11 was adopted for instance segmentation and all compared models were initialized with weights pre-trained on the COCO dataset and subsequently fine-tuned on the custom dataset developed for this study. The FGVL data were split into training, validation, and test sets at ratios of 70%, 20%, and 10%, respectively.

Hyperparameters used for model development are presented in Table 3. The model was trained for 25 epochs with a batch size of 16 and a learning rate of 0.01, using “AdamW” optimizer. During training, default loss functions implemented in YOLO were employed, comprising CIoU loss for bounding box regression, binary cross-entropy loss for classification, and corresponding pixel-wise loss components for mask segmentation 55. Model performance was evaluated for the instance segmentation task using mean Average Precision at IoU = 0.50 (mAP@50), mean Average Precision across multiple IoU thresholds (mAP@50:95), Precision, Recall, and False Classification Rate (FCR)56–60. While mAP, Precision, and Recall provide a comprehensive assessment of localization quality and classification accuracy, FCR complements these metrics by quantifying the proportion of misclassified instances relative to all predictions for a given class, offering a more granular view of per-class confusion. This test set contained scenes with multiple objects per frame, substantial visual clutter, and high inter-class similarity, thereby providing a challenging benchmark for assessing the model’s generalization capability under realistic conditions.

In segmentation tasks, deep learning-based algorithms generate pixel-level outputs in form of masks representing regions of interest, and model performance is assessed by comparing these predicted masks with corresponding ground truth annotations. One of the most widely used metrics in this context is Intersection over Union (IoU), which measures the overlap between predicted and reference masks. By comparing IoU value against a predefined threshold, the quality of segmentation can be systematically evaluated, enabling the computation of core performance measures including Precision, Recall, and F1-score, which collectively reflect Accuracy and robustness of segmentation model. Dynamic confidence thresholding

Although confidence thresholding is commonly treated as a fixed post-processing parameter in standard YOLO inference pipelines, this study introduces a Dynamic Confidence Thresholding tailored to dense, visually complex, and overlap-prone nature of vineyard frost damage imagery as seen in Fig. 5. While adaptive confidence thresholding has been explored in adjacent areas of computer vision, its use in YOLOv11s + ASPP instance segmentation for frost-damaged grapevine organs remains limited. Figure 5 illustrates the end-to-end inference pipeline of the proposed YOLOv11s + ASPP model with dynamic confidence thresholding. The input scene, which contains visually challenging conditions such as dense overlap, frost damage, thin branches, and young inflorescence, is first processed by the backbone to generate raw, unfiltered predictions with associated confidence scores. These predictions are then passed through an adaptive gate control mechanism that adjusts the confidence threshold based on local scene context — applying a lower threshold (e.g., 0.28) in dense, high-noise regions and a higher threshold (e.g., 0.68) in clearer areas. The adaptive prediction refinement stage subsequently suppresses false detections while preserving difficult true instances, ultimately producing final clear masks with class-specific labels for healthy-leaf, frozen-leaf, frozen-grape, and healthy-grape categories.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, FGVL dataset was collected from a single vineyard, a single grape cultivar, and a single severe frost event, which may limit the generalizability of the findings across different cultivars, regions, seasons, and imaging conditions. Second, although the dataset reflects realistic field complexity, the visual similarity between healthy and frost-affected organs, together with overlap, clutter, and annotation ambiguity, may have affected class-wise segmentation performance. Third, although reliability was further examined using False Classification Rate (FCR), broader validation under truly unseen cross-domain field conditions is still needed to more comprehensively assess operational robustness. Finally, while the proposed framework showed promising deployment characteristics, some edge-device results were estimation-based, and broader validation under diverse real-world operational settings is still needed.

Proposed model architecture

This section presents the architectural design of the proposed hybrid model, which integrates YOLOv11 with Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling (ASPP) to enhance instance segmentation performance in visually complex vineyard scenes. Section "YOLOv11 and multi-scale contextual enhancement" describes the YOLOv11 backbone and its multi-scale feature extraction capabilities, while Section "ASPP (atrous spatial pyramid pooling)" details the ASPP module and its role in enriching contextual representations across multiple receptive field scales. Together, these components form a unified architecture tailored to address the challenges of overlapping instances, fine-grained class distinctions, and varying object scales inherent in grape cluster segmentation.

YOLOv11 and multi-scale contextual enhancement

YOLOv11 is a robust member of YOLO family that builds on strengths of earlier versions while introducing architectural refinements to improve efficiency and accuracy61. In particular, model incorporates more effective building blocks and improved feature extraction and fusion mechanisms in backbone and neck, enabling stronger multi-scale representation with lower computational cost61–64. These improvements enhance detection and segmentation of objects of different sizes, especially in visually complex scenes, while maintaining real-time processing capability that characterizes single-stage YOLO-based frameworks in Fig. 6.Fig. 6YOLOv11 Segmentation Architecture: Backbone, Neck, and Detection Head.

ASPP (atrous spatial pyramid pooling)

Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling (ASPP) is a widely used module for multi-scale feature extraction in deep learning-based image segmentation. Originally introduced in the DeepLab family, it extends conventional convolutional processing through parallel Atrous Convolution branches with different dilation rates. This design enables the network to capture contextual information at multiple receptive field sizes, improving representation of both fine details and broader spatial structures. As a result, ASPP enhances segmentation performance by allowing the model to better distinguish object boundaries and recognize targets of different sizes65,66.

In the proposed architecture, the standard PAFPN neck of YOLOv11s is replaced with an ASPP-based neck module to enrich multi-scale contextual representations. As illustrated in Fig. 7, multi-scale feature maps extracted by the backbone at three levels — P3 (80 × 80 × 256), P4 (40 × 40 × 512), and P5 (20 × 20 × 1024) — are fed into the ASPP neck, which processes them through four parallel Atrous Convolution branches with dilation rates of 1, 6, 12, and 13. Each branch captures complementary spatial information, ranging from local texture patterns to wider contextual cues, and their outputs are concatenated to generate fused feature representations of shape 40 × 40 × 512. These enriched features are subsequently passed to the detection and segmentation head, which produces bounding box coordinates, class predictions across four categories, and mask coefficients together with prototype masks for final instance segmentation output.Fig. 7Yolov11s + ASPP Instance Segmentation Architecture for Vineyard Frost Recognition.

Results

Real-world vineyard scenes present significant challenges, for instance segmentation, including dense object arrangements, occlusions, frost damage, and high inter-class visual similarity between grape clusters and leaves at different phenological stages. These conditions make it difficult for models to clearly separate target instances from complex backgrounds, motivating the need for robust architectures and carefully curated training data. Our FGVL dataset was specifically prepared for instance segmentation, with each object annotated at the pixel level to enable precise separation of individual instances. Since instance segmentation inherently subsumes object localization and class prediction while extending them to pixel-level delineation, it provides a more comprehensive evaluation framework than bounding box-based detection alone. Accordingly, this study reports exclusively instance segmentation performance, as it encompasses and surpasses the information conveyed by detection-only metrics.

Conclusion and discussion

Under real-world conditions, dense co-occurrence of grape clusters and leaves within the same frame introduces substantial visual complexity for both object detection and pixel-level segmentation. Close proximity, overlap, and similar color-texture characteristics make it difficult for the network to extract discriminative features and accurately separate adjacent objects. This challenge becomes even more pronounced at higher IoU thresholds, where precise mask delineation is required. For this reason, extremely high mAP@50 values are rarely achieved in real-field datasets, particularly in scenes with dense object arrangements, frequent overlap, and environmentally induced visual variability. In such settings, stable, consistent, and practically meaningful performance is often more important than a single high metric value. This is especially true in domains such as agriculture, healthcare, and industrial inspection, where image quality is affected by illumination changes, shadows, viewpoint variation, and acquisition conditions. Therefore, beyond mAP@50, models that maintain robust performance across varying conditions and can support expert decision-making should be preferred 67–69. In addition, practical considerations such as real-time inference capability, processing efficiency, resource consumption, and ease of system integration also play a critical role in model selection.

Accordingly, segmentation performance in real-world image analysis should be interpreted in relation to task complexity, dataset characteristics, and scene difficulty rather than by numerical thresholds alone. In domains such as agriculture70, healthcare71, and robotic vision72, performance is often affected by class imbalance, annotation uncertainty, irregular object boundaries, and variations in illumination, viewpoint, and resolution. Natural images also contain occlusion, background clutter, and strong visual similarity between classes, all of which increase the difficulty of precise segmentation. Under such challenging conditions, performance levels that may appear moderate in controlled benchmarks may still represent practically reliable and meaningful results. Therefore, model effectiveness should be assessed not only through metrics such as mAP@50, but also in terms of robustness, consistency, and applicability under realistic operating conditions. When assessing machine learning models in such environments, both internal and external validity should be considered73. Since real-world data are influenced by multiple environmental and acquisition-related factors, achieving very high accuracy is inherently more difficult than in controlled and homogeneous datasets74. Nevertheless, models trained and evaluated under these conditions provide more meaningful evidence of generalization than systems tested only on cleaner and less variable data75,76.Agricultural imaging problems such as frost damage involve highly complex visual patterns defined not only by shape, but also by color tone, texture, and localized regional variation77. This makes the task challenging not only for computational models but also for human experts78. As reported in the literature, annotation consistency in such tasks is rarely perfect, and different experts may interpret the same image differently79. Therefore, a certain level of model error is expected and should be interpreted within the inherent ambiguity of the problem. Under these conditions, performance approaching human-level consistency may represent a more meaningful benchmark than absolute accuracy alone80.

A possible explanation for the limited improvement observed with the other enhancement modules is that most of them primarily emphasize channel or spatial reweighting rather than explicitly addressing the multi-scale and overlap-intensive nature of frost damage imagery. In vineyard scenes, where small organs, irregular boundaries, and dense occlusions frequently occur together, feature refinement alone may be insufficient to resolve visually ambiguous regions. Some modules may even amplify locally dominant but less informative responses, leading to only marginal gains or unstable behavior across classes. This suggests that the main challenge in the present task is not merely feature emphasis, but the ability to capture context across different receptive fields in a structurally coherent manner. Within this context, the YOLOv11s + ASPP + DCT framework developed in this study delivered a strong balance between predictive performance and computational efficiency for frost damage segmentation, in agreement with prior literature emphasizing the importance of real-time capability and efficiency-aware model design81,82. The main performance gain of the proposed framework stems from the ASPP module, which enriches multi-scale contextual representation and improves the separation of visually similar and partially overlapping classes such as frozen leaves, healthy leaves, and frozen grapes. In addition, the proposed Dynamic Confidence Thresholding strategy further strengthened the system by adaptively filtering predictions in dense and overlap-prone scenes, where a fixed confidence threshold would either suppress borderline true instances or increase false positives. In this sense, ASPP improved what the network could represent, while DCT improved how those representations were translated into operational predictions. Together, these two components contributed to the more stable and practically meaningful behavior observed under difficult vineyard conditions.

One of the main strengths of this study lies in the FGVL dataset, which was collected under natural illumination and real field conditions. Unlike many previous studies that rely on controlled or artificial imaging environments83, the present work addressed segmentation in scenes characterized by illumination variability, complex ground surfaces, object overlap, and class ambiguity. Under these conditions, the YOLOv11s + ASPP architecture outperformed several attention-based alternatives reported in the literature, including CBAM, SE, and ECA, and did so while maintaining a compact model structure and moderate hardware demand84,85. Further deployment analysis showed that the model remained feasible not only on server-grade hardware but also on edge-oriented platforms, supporting its practical use in smart agriculture workflows86,87. From a broader perspective, frost damage segmentation is both a highly challenging and agriculturally important problem, since frost events often occur during the most sensitive developmental stages and require rapid, reliable assessment. The scarcity of open-access datasets specifically focused on vineyard frost damage further increases the originality and practical relevance of the present study.

Future research should focus on constructing larger and more diverse datasets that include wider variation in illumination, seasonal conditions, camera type, and spatial resolution, particularly for environmentally complex agricultural domains. In addition, for visually ambiguous and interpretation-dependent tasks such as frost damage assessment, hybrid decision-support systems that combine artificial intelligence with expert knowledge may further improve reliability and transparency. This direction is in line with broader hybrid modeling and control-based perspectives developed for renewable resource management, where system dynamics and decision policies are jointly analyzed88. Explainable artificial intelligence methods may also help identify the image regions driving model predictions, thereby increasing user trust and supporting feedback-guided refinement. A particularly promising direction would be to extend the present FGVL dataset by combining ground-level RGB frost damage observations with hyperspectral satellite imagery, enabling a new multi-source research framework for frost monitoring.
