ABSTRACT
BackgroundUpadacitinib is an oral selective Janus kinase inhibitor approved to treat moderate-to-severe atopic dermatitis (AD) in adults and adolescents; long-term efficacy and safety data beyond 1 year are needed.ObjectiveThe aim was to evaluate the long-term efficacy and safety of upadacitinib treatment through 140 weeks in patients with moderate-to-severe AD.MethodsMeasure Up 1 (MeUp1; NCT03569293), Measure Up 2 (MeUp2; NCT03607422), and AD Up (NCT03568318) are ongoing, phase 3, randomized clinical trials evaluating upadacitinib 15 mg (UPA15) and 30 mg (UPA30) in adults and adolescents with moderate-to-severe AD. This interim analysis evaluated efficacy and safety through week 140. At baseline, patients were randomized 1:1:1 to receive once-daily UPA15, UPA30, or placebo alone (MeUp1/2) or with concomitant topical corticosteroids (AD Up). At week 16, patients initially randomized to placebo were rerandomized 1:1 to UPA15 or UPA30. Skin and itch efficacy assessments included achievement of ≥ 75%/≥ 90%/100% improvement from baseline in Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI 75/90/100), validated Investigator Global Assessment for AD score of clear/almost clear (vIGA-AD 0/1), and ≥ 4-point improvement from baseline in Worst Pruritus Numerical Rating Scale (∆WP-NRS≥4). Safety assessments included incidence of treatment-emergent adverse events.ResultsA total of 2782 patients were randomized in MeUp1/2 or AD Up. Efficacy response rates, including optimal outcomes such as EASI 90 and WP-NRS score of 0/1, were sustained through week 140 in all three studies. At week 140, EASI 75 was achieved by 85.5%/90.5% (UPA15/UPA30; integrated MeUp1/2) and 81.5%/90.0% (UPA15/UPA30; AD Up) of patients, and vIGA-AD 0/1 was achieved by 56.6%/64.4% (UPA15/UPA30; integrated MeUp1/2) and 52.0%/56.8% (UPA15/UPA30; AD Up) of patients. Over 60% of patients across all three studies achieved ∆WP-NRS≥4 at week 140. Pooled safety data across all three studies demonstrated safety profiles consistent with 16-week and 52-week analyses.ConclusionsUPA15 and UPA30 with and without topical corticosteroids demonstrated robust, durable efficacy and a favorable safety profile through 140 weeks in adults and adolescents with moderate-to-severe AD.Trial RegistrationMeasure Up 1 (NCT03569293; https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03569293), Measure Up 2 (NCT03607422; https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03607422), and AD Up (NCT03568318; https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03568318).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40257-025-00975-3.

Key Points

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic, recurrent, immune-mediated, inflammatory skin disease characterized by typical eczematous morphology, intense itch, and impaired quality of life [1, 2]. Patients with AD may require long-term treatment, and systemic therapies are often needed for patients with refractory disease. Current treatment options for moderate-to-severe AD include traditional systemic immunosuppressive therapies (e.g., cyclosporine, methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine) and advanced systemic therapies, including injectable biologics (e.g., dupilumab, lebrikizumab, tralokinumab, nemolizumab) and oral Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors (e.g., abrocitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib) [3, 4]; however, the long-term efficacy and safety of advanced systemic therapies used to treat AD are not well understood, and more long-term data are needed.

Upadacitinib is an oral selective JAK inhibitor [5, 6] approved for the treatment of moderate-to-severe AD in adults and adolescents [7]. In multiple phase 3 studies (Measure Up 1 [MeUp1], Measure Up 2 [MeUp2], and AD Up), upadacitinib 15 mg (UPA15) and 30 mg (UPA30) demonstrated greater efficacy for skin clearance and itch improvement endpoints with and without concomitant topical corticosteroids (TCSs) compared with placebo over 16 weeks of treatment in adults and adolescents with moderate-to-severe AD [8, 9]. Furthermore, rapid efficacy was demonstrated as early as day 2 for itch improvement and week 2 for skin clearance [8, 9]. Efficacy of UPA15 and UPA30 was shown to be sustained over 52 weeks of treatment in MeUp1, MeUp2, and AD Up, with favorable safety profiles and no new safety findings compared with 16-week analyses [10, 11].

Here, we report long-term efficacy and safety results of UPA15 and UPA30 from a prespecified 140-week interim analysis of the ongoing MeUp1, MeUp2, and AD Up phase 3 studies.

Methods

Study Design, Patients, and Treatment

MeUp1 (NCT03569293), MeUp2 (NCT03607422), and AD Up (NCT03568318) are ongoing, randomized, double-blind, phase 3 studies evaluating the efficacy and safety of upadacitinib for the treatment of AD for up to 10 years [8, 9]; each clinical trial contains a main study that includes adults and adolescents, along with an adolescent substudy. Eligible patients were adolescents (aged 12–17 years; body weight ≥ 40 kg) and adults (aged 18–75 years) with diagnosed moderate-to-severe AD who were candidates for systemic therapy; detailed eligibility criteria have been previously reported [8, 9].

Patients were randomized 1:1:1 at baseline to receive once-daily oral UPA15, UPA30, or placebo as monotherapy (MeUp1 and MeUp2) or with concomitant TCS (AD Up) (Supplementary Fig. 1; see the electronic supplementary material). At week 16, patients randomized to placebo at baseline were rerandomized 1:1 to either UPA15 or UPA30 (crossover cohorts). Concomitant TCS therapy was required in the AD Up study through week 52 in patients with active lesions and optional per the investigator’s discretion thereafter [9]; TCS use was prohibited in the MeUp1 and MeUp2 studies until week 16 and optional as rescue therapy thereafter [8].

For MeUp1 and MeUp2, study visits were scheduled at screening, baseline, weeks 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 32, 40, and 52, followed by visits every 12 weeks through week 124, and a visit at week 140. For AD Up, study visits were scheduled at screening, baseline, weeks 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 32, 40, and 52, followed by visits every 12 weeks through week 124, and a visit at week 140.

The study was conducted in accordance with the protocol, International Council for Harmonisation guidelines, and applicable regulations, guidelines, and ethical principles originating from the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by an institutional review board, and all patients provided written informed consent before screening or undergoing study-specific procedures.

Assessments

This prespecified interim analysis evaluated efficacy and safety of UPA15 and UPA30 through 140 weeks of treatment: at least 2 years of follow-up with upadacitinib plus an additional 16 weeks to account for the crossover of patients in placebo cohorts. The data cutoff date was the point at which all patients had completed the week 140 study visit.

Efficacy assessments for skin clearance included the proportion of patients achieving ≥ 75%/≥ 90%/100% improvement from baseline in Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI 75/90/100), absolute EASI ≤ 3 or ≤ 7 (recommended by the Aiming High in Eczema/Atopic Dermatitis [AHEAD] global consensus as optimal and moderate treatment targets for skin clearance, respectively [12]), and clear or almost clear with ≥ 2 grades of improvement from baseline on the validated Investigator Global Assessment for AD (vIGA-AD 0/1) [13]. Itch improvement was evaluated using the Worst Pruritus Numerical Rating Scale (WP-NRS), with response assessed as the proportion of patients achieving ≥ 4-point improvement from baseline (among patients with WP-NRS scores ≥ 4 at baseline; ∆WP-NRS≥4) or no/minimal itch (WP-NRS score of 0/1, among patients with WP-NRS scores > 1 at baseline). Additional efficacy assessments included percent change from baseline in EASI, percent change or absolute change from baseline in WP-NRS, and proportion of patients simultaneously achieving EASI 90 and WP-NRS 0/1.

Maintenance of response was assessed among patients who simultaneously achieved EASI 75 and vIGA-AD 0/1 at week 16; the proportion of patients who experienced loss of response after week 16 (defined as ≥ 50% reduction of the EASI improvement from baseline at week 16 and vIGA-AD ≥ 2) through week 140 was also assessed. The proportion of patients who achieved EASI 75 at week 16 and maintained EASI 75 at week 140 and the proportion of patients who did not achieve EASI 75 at week 16 and subsequently achieved EASI 75 at week 140 were also assessed; a similar analysis was performed for EASI 90 outcomes. TCS and topical calcineurin inhibitor (TCI) use for AD treatment [9] was evaluated among all patients at each study visit from baseline through week 140.

Safety assessments included monitoring of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), serious TEAEs, TEAEs leading to discontinuation of study drug, deaths, and incidence of adverse events of special interest (AESIs). TEAEs were defined as adverse events that began or worsened in severity after the first dose of study drug or within 30 days after the last dose. All TEAEs that were reported for a patient were counted as events.

Statistical Analysis

Efficacy analyses were conducted for all patients randomized at baseline for each clinical trial, including patients from the main study and the adolescent substudy, or using integrated data from MeUp1 and MeUp2. Long-term efficacy data were reported as observed cases without imputation for missing data. Categorical endpoints were summarized using frequency counts and percentages; 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were based on the normal approximation to the binomial distribution. For continuous endpoints, within-group least squares mean and 95% CIs were calculated using analysis of covariance models. No statistical comparisons were made between treatment groups.

Safety analyses were conducted in all patients who received one or more doses of upadacitinib. TEAEs were summarized using Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities version 25.1 Preferred Terms. Safety data were evaluated for each study or pooled for all three studies and reported as exposure-adjusted event rate (events per 100 patient-years [E/100 PY]) or exposure-adjusted incidence rate (number of patients with ≥ 1 E/100 PY [n/100 PY]) to adjust for potential differences in study drug exposure.

Results

Patients

A total of 2782 adult and adolescent patients were randomized at baseline and included in this 140-week interim analysis: 902 in MeUp1 (Supplementary Fig. 2a), 912 in MeUp2 (Supplementary Fig. 2b), and 968 in AD Up (Supplementary Fig. 2c; see the electronic supplementary material). Baseline demographics and patient characteristics were well balanced across treatment groups (Supplementary Table 1) [8–11]. At the data cutoff date for this analysis, the median (range) duration of study drug exposure in years among all patients who received upadacitinib was as follows: UPA15, MeUp1: 3.5 (0.02–4.7); UPA30, MeUp1: 3.5 (0.04–4.6); UPA15, MeUp2: 3.4 (0.003–4.8); UPA30, MeUp2: 3.4 (0.003–4.9); UPA15, AD Up: 3.3 (0.04–4.7); and UPA30, AD Up: 3.5 (0.09–4.7).

Efficacy

Skin Clearance

Overall, efficacy response rates for skin clearance at week 16, including the coprimary endpoints EASI 75 and vIGA-AD 0/1, were sustained long term through week 140 among all patients randomized to upadacitinib at baseline. Across all three studies, the vast majority of patients achieved EASI 75 at week 140 (UPA15, 81.5–88.8%; UPA30, 90.0–90.7%) (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 4a, and Supplementary Fig. 5a; see the electronic supplementary material). Achievement of vIGA-AD 0/1 at week 140 across studies was 49.2–63.4% and 56.8–65.5% with UPA15 and UPA30, respectively (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 4b, and Supplementary Fig. 5b). Itch Improvements

A substantial proportion of patients achieved ∆WP-NRS≥4 at week 16, and response rates were sustained long term. At week 140 across studies, 61.4–68.0% and 70.5–75.2% of patients achieved ∆WP-NRS≥4 with UPA15 and UPA30, respectively (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 3h, Supplementary Fig. 4h, and Supplementary Fig. 5h; see the electronic supplementary material). WP-NRS 0/1 response rates were also sustained long-term through week 140 (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 3i, Supplementary Fig. 4i, and Supplementary Fig 5i). TCS/TCI Use

In the MeUp1 and MeUp2 studies, the proportion of patients who did not receive any topical therapies for AD from baseline through week 140 was as follows: UPA15, MeUp1: 58.6%; UPA30, MeUp1: 58.1%; UPA15, MeUp2: 45.8%; and UPA30, MeUp2: 58.9% (Supplementary Table 3; see the electronic supplementary material). Topical therapy use in the MeUp1 and MeUp2 studies increased slowly over 140 weeks but remained below 50% at each study visit (Supplementary Fig. 9a–c). At week 140, the proportion of patients receiving one or more topical therapy in the prior 4 weeks was as follows: UPA15, MeUp1: 33.5%; UPA30, MeUp1: 30.5%; UPA15, MeUp2: 41.9%; and UPA30, MeUp2: 27.7%.

In the AD Up study, in which a topical therapy regimen was required for patients with active lesions from baseline to week 52 [9, 11], the proportion of patients who did not receive any topical therapies from weeks 16–140 was 26.3% with UPA15 and 30.2% with UPA30 (Supplementary Table 3). In the AD Up study, the proportion of patients receiving topical therapies at each study visit remained relatively stable from weeks 16–140, ranging between 40% and 56% across treatment arms (Supplementary Fig. 9d).

Safety

Overall, upadacitinib was safe and well tolerated, with a similar safety profile through 140 weeks as previously reported in the placebo-controlled 16-week analyses [8, 9] and the long-term 52-week analyses [10, 11]; no new safety findings emerged with longer-term upadacitinib treatment. The safety profile of upadacitinib through week 140 was generally consistent for the three individual studies; safety results from the individual studies through week 140 are reported in Supplementary Table 4 (see the electronic supplementary material), and AESIs from the individual studies through week 140 are reported in Supplementary Table 5.

In an integrated analysis of patient data for the three studies through to the week 140 cutoff date, the incidence of any TEAE, serious adverse events, and TEAEs leading to study drug discontinuation was similar across upadacitinib treatment arms (Table 2). The most common TEAEs were coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), nasopharyngitis, acne, upper respiratory tract infection, worsening of AD, and blood creatine phosphokinase increased. Rates of serious infections were generally low; slightly higher rates of serious infections and opportunistic infections were observed with UPA30 than with UPA15. The types of serious infections reported are consistent with those observed in prior reports of studies of upadacitinib for AD, and none were reported at an incidence rate > 0.2 E/100 PY in either treatment group. Serious infections reported at a frequency of 0.2 E/100 PY in either treatment group were COVID-19, COVID-19 pneumonia, eczema herpeticum, herpes zoster, and pneumonia. Most opportunistic events observed were cases of eczema herpeticum. Dose-dependent increases in eczema herpeticum were observed (UPA15, 1.6 E/100 PY; UPA30, 2.1 E/100 PY). The incidence of a bacterial skin infection event was 0.3 E/100 PY with both UPA15 and UPA30. Furthermore, dose-dependent increases in the rates of herpes zoster, hepatic disorder, neutropenia, and elevation of creatine phosphokinase levels were observed; most of these events were nonserious and did not lead to study drug discontinuation. Hepatic disorders were predominantly elevations in transaminase levels. Rates of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), venous thromboembolic events (VTE), nonmelanoma skin cancer (NMSC), and malignancy excluding NMSC were low and consistent with those observed in the general population of patients with AD [14–19]. There were seven deaths (three with UPA15; four with UPA30); all deaths occurred in adults, and none were considered possibly related to the study drug (Supplementary Table 6). Discussion

Through 140 weeks of treatment in three phase 3, randomized controlled trials, UPA15 and UPA30 provided durable long-term efficacy with skin clearance and itch improvement consistent with 16- and 52-week outcomes. Response rates observed at week 16 were sustained through week 140, and most patients who achieved EASI 75 or EASI 90 responses at week 16 maintained those responses at week 140. UPA15 and UPA30 were well tolerated through 140 weeks with no new safety findings and consistent with the known safety profile of upadacitinib. Given the need for long-term efficacy and safety data for advanced systemic therapies such as upadacitinib (therapies that are strongly recommended by American Academy of Dermatology guidelines for management of patients with moderate-to-severe AD [20]), these findings highlight the potential for upadacitinib as a safe and effective long-term therapeutic option as a monotherapy or with concomitant TCS.

AHEAD guidelines define optimal treatment targets for skin and itch outcomes, including EASI 90 and WP-NRS 0/1; simultaneous achievement of EASI 90 and WP-NRS 0/1 is considered minimal disease activity (MDA) [12]. Patients have also reported complete skin clearance and itch cessation among their most important treatment goals [21]. In clinical trial and real-world settings, a substantial proportion of patients achieved long-term MDA and quality-of-life improvements (including improved sleep) with upadacitinib treatment [22, 23]; achieving MDA or optimal treatment targets has also been associated with greater improvements in patient-reported outcomes [24, 25]. Together, these findings highlight the breadth of improvements that patients may achieve and maintain long term with upadacitinib for AD.

These 140-week findings represent some of the longest-term efficacy and safety findings compared with other advanced systemic therapies for AD; upadacitinib has been approved for multiple indications and has demonstrated an established safety profile through nearly 10 years of clinical experience [26–29]. Long-term efficacy and safety data for other advanced AD treatments are limited. Positive results have been reported in long-term extension studies with abrocitinib for 48 weeks [30], nemolizumab for 68 weeks [31], baricitinib for 104 weeks [32], tralokinumab for 2 years [33], and dupilumab for 5 years [34]. Although no comparative analyses of long-term outcomes with advanced systemic therapies for AD have been performed to date, results from two head-to-head studies have shown superior efficacy with upadacitinib over dupilumab through 16 weeks [35, 36]. In addition, multiple network meta-analyses reported the highest efficacy rates with UPA30 among all advanced systemic therapies for AD [37–39].

Results were generally consistent among the MeUp1, MeUp2, and AD Up studies, except for differences in topical therapy use based on the study designs. Topical therapies were prohibited until week 16 and optional as rescue therapy thereafter in the monotherapy trials (MeUp1 and MeUp2), while concomitant TCS was required for the AD Up study through week 52 in patients with active lesions and optional per the investigator’s discretion thereafter. Interestingly, although topical therapy was optional between week 52 and week 140 in all three studies, a higher proportion of patients in the AD Up study (~ 53–55% at week 140) remained on topical therapies long term than in the MeUp1 and MeUp2 studies (~ 28–42% at week 140), suggesting that the initial treatment regimen may influence long-term topical therapy use. Specifically, incorporating topical therapies when initiating advanced systemic therapies may result in a greater likelihood of continued topical therapy utilization than regimens initiating advanced systemic therapies as monotherapy, despite clinical response; this may result in unnecessary topical therapy in some upadacitinib-treated patients. Nevertheless, topical therapy use did not seem to affect the long-term efficacy or safety of upadacitinib.

Safety data in this long-term integrated analysis through 140 weeks of treatment are consistent with the known safety profile for upadacitinib in previous reports. Overall, rates of TEAEs were slightly higher with UPA30 compared with UPA15, which is consistent with previous safety analyses in AD [28]. The most common TEAEs were similar to those reported in the placebo-controlled 16-week analyses and long-term 52-week safety analyses of MeUp1, MeUp2, and AD Up [8–11]. Rates of AESIs, including MACE, VTE, and malignancy excluding NMSC, remained low and were generally consistent with previously reported 52-week data for upadacitinib treatment in AD [40]. Incidence of MACE, VTE, NMSC, and malignancy excluding NMSC was within the range of background rates in the general population of patients with AD [14–19].

Limitations of this analysis include lack of a placebo or other comparator group after week 16; however, upadacitinib dose groups remained blinded through week 140 (and will remain blinded through the end of the studies). There were no statistical comparisons between treatment groups after week 16. Additionally, findings from the clinical trial populations may not be fully generalizable to the real-world population of patients with AD. Furthermore, observed cases analyses may be biased toward long-term responders, which should be considered when interpreting long-term maintenance among week 16 responders and nonresponders. Patient-reported outcomes (other than WP-NRS) and longer-term safety data were not included but will be reported in future publications. Patients were not allowed to change upadacitinib doses during the MeUp1, MeUp2, or AD Up studies, but the effects of upadacitinib and changes in dosage are being evaluated in other studies (Level Up, NCT05601882 [36], and Flex Up, NCT05507580).

Conclusions

Overall, these interim 140-week results from the MeUp1, MeUp2, and AD Up phase 3 studies demonstrate a favorable long-term benefit–risk profile for UPA15 and UPA30 with and without concomitant TCS in adults and adolescents with moderate-to-severe AD.
