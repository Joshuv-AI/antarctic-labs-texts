GLP-1 receptor agonist targets an AMPKα1-HIF1α-PFKFB3 metabolic vulnerability and enhances Lenvatinib response in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Takayuki Noma, Yuan Li, Yuma Wada, Yuji Morine, Tetsuya Ikemoto, Yu Saito, Mitsuo Shimada, Ajay Goel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), widely used for diabetes and obesity, have recently attracted attention for potential anti-tumor effects, although their mechanisms remain unclear. Given their role in metabolic regulation, we hypothesized that GLP-1RAs may target cancer-specific metabolic vulnerabilities. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is characterized by metabolic reprogramming and enhanced glycolysis, which contribute to therapeutic resistance. Here, we investigated the anti-tumor effects of a GLP-1RA in HCC, focusing on its ability to modulate glycolytic pathways and enhance sensitivity to Lenvatinib. Lenvatinib-resistant HCC cells exhibited suppressed AMPKα1 and increased HIF-1α and PFKFB3 expression, promoting glycolytic adaptation. GLP-1RA treatment restored AMPKα1 activity while suppressing HIF-1α/PFKFB3 signaling, thereby reducing glycolytic activity and enhancing apoptosis. Combination treatment with GLP-1RA and Lenvatinib significantly inhibited tumor cell growth in resistant models. These findings suggest that the AMPKα1/HIF-1α/PFKFB3 axis represents a metabolic vulnerability in Lenvatinib-resistant HCC and support GLP-1RA-based combination strategies.Subject terms: Cancer, Cell biology, Oncology

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most common cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide1,2. Due to the lack of overt symptoms and the challenges with its early detection, more than half of HCC cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage, often making curative treatment options such as surgical resection and liver transplantation infeasible3–5.

Lenvatinib, a multikinase inhibitor targeting vascular endothelial growth factor receptors, has been shown to extend overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with HCC6,7. Over the past decade, the treatment of HCC has made significant progress from localized to systemic therapy, and more recently, the emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has further significantly altered the landscape of systemic treatment in this malignancy8,9. Nonetheless, even in the era of widespread excitement about immunotherapy, Lenvatinib remains widely used among HCC patients due to its superior, established effectiveness. Analyses using inverse probability of treatment weighting and large-scale international propensity score matching indicated that, in patients with non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) associated HCC, Lenvatinib provides a significant survival benefit compared to ICI therapy10–12. Despite its efficacy, Lenvatinib’s overall response rate remains approximately 25%, and resistance to therapy remains a major challenge.

The mechanisms underlying this resistance are complex, involving multiple pathways such as epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) activation, WNT-related signaling, abnormal drug transport, and epigenetic regulation13–22. In addition to these mechanisms, emerging evidence suggests that metabolic reprogramming plays a critical role in therapeutic resistance. Enhanced glycolysis and activation of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) signaling have been shown to promote adaptive survival under therapeutic stress, particularly in response to anti-angiogenic therapies23–27. However, the specific contribution of metabolic reprogramming to Lenvatinib resistance in HCC remains poorly understood.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) is an incretin hormone essential for maintaining glucose homeostasis through stimulation of insulin secretion, inhibition of glucagon release, delayed gastric emptying, and appetite regulation28–31. Its receptor, GLP-1R, is expressed not only in pancreatic β cells but also in numerous peripheral tissues, underscoring its diverse physiological functions beyond glucose metabolism30,32–34. Agonists of GLP-1R (GLP-1RAs) are synthetic protein-based analogs that mimic endogenous GLP-1 activity. Initially developed as antidiabetic drugs, GLP-1RAs have demonstrated additional clinical benefits in weight reduction, the management of NAFLD/NASH, cardiovascular risk reduction, and even the potential treatment of neurodegenerative diseases35–44.

More recently, accumulating evidence has suggested that GLP-1RAs may exert antitumor effects through modulation of cellular metabolism, suppression of proliferation, and induction of apoptosis in multiple cancer types, including HCC45–50. Given the central role of metabolic reprogramming in both HCC progression and therapeutic resistance, these findings raise the possibility that GLP-1RAs may target cancer-specific metabolic vulnerabilities.

In this study, we investigated whether GLP-1RA can overcome Lenvatinib resistance in HCC by targeting metabolic reprogramming. Specifically, we aimed to elucidate the role of the AMPKα1/HIF-1α/PFKFB3 axis in mediating glycolytic adaptation in resistant cells and to determine whether modulation of this pathway enhances sensitivity to Lenvatinib. Understanding these mechanisms may provide a rationale for metabolically targeted combination strategies in HCC.

Results

Successful establishment of Lenvatinib-resistant HCC cell lines

To investigate the potential synergistic effect of GLP-1RA with Lenvatinib, we first assessed Lenvatinib resistance in various HCC cell lines. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values for Lenvatinib were determined as follows: Huh7 (IC50: 6.4 μM), PLC (IC50: 28 μM), SNU398 (IC50: 25 μM), HLE (IC50: 26 μM), and HLF (IC50: 22 μM) (Fig. 1A). Among these, we selected Huh7, which exhibited the lowest IC50, and PLC, which displayed the highest IC50, to establish Lenvatinib-resistant cell lines. Parental Huh7 (pHuh7) and PLC-PRF-5 (pPLC) cells were continuously exposed to gradually increasing concentrations of Lenvatinib over several months, leading to the successful generation of Lenvatinib-resistant Huh7 (rHuh7) and PLC-PRF-5 (rPLC) cell lines. Notably, the IC50 values of Lenvatinib were significantly higher in the resistant cell lines compared to their respective parental cells. Specifically, rHuh7 exhibited a 7-fold increase in resistance compared to pHuh7 (IC50: 57 μM vs. 6.4 μM, Fig. 1B), while rPLC showed a 2-fold increase in resistance compared to pPLC (IC50: 66 μM vs. 28 μM, Fig. 1C). When treated with Lenvatinib at the IC50 values of their respective parental cell lines, the resistant clones exhibited minimal impact on colony formation capacity (Fig. 1D, E).

Furthermore, apoptosis analysis revealed that the rates in parental cells were 17.6% (Huh7) and 14.4% (PLC). In contrast, the resistant cells demonstrated significantly lower apoptosis rates of 5.4% (Huh7) and 7.4% (PLC) (Fig. 1F, G), confirming their strong resistance to Lenvatinib. Using these two acquired Lenvatinib-resistant cell lines, we conducted a series of experiments to assess the potential of GLP-1RA to overcome Lenvatinib resistance in HCC.

These data confirm robust, quantifiable Lenvatinib resistance in Huh7 and PLC models, providing a tractable system to test GLP-1RA.

GLP-1RA is significantly more effective in Lenvatinib-resistant HCC cells

To identify potential therapeutic targets for GLP-1RA in HCC, we first examined GLP-1R expression in HCC tissues. Notably, GLP-1R expression was nearly undetectable in normal liver tissues but was significantly upregulated in HCC tissues (p < 0.001; Fig. 2A). Next, we investigated the expression pattern of GLP-1R in HCC cells. qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses revealed that GLP-1R expression was significantly higher in Lenvatinib-resistant HCC cells compared to their parental counterparts (p < 0.01; Fig. 2B, C). Immunofluorescence (IF) staining further confirmed this increased protein expression in resistant cell lines (Fig. 2D). Based on these findings, we hypothesized that GLP-1RA may be more effective in targeting tumor cells, particularly those that have acquired resistance to Lenvatinib.

Resistant cells exhibited lower IC50 values for GLP-1RA compared with parental cells, indicating increased sensitivity.

To further test this hypothesis, we compared the IC50 values of GLP-1RA between parental and resistant cell lines. Interestingly, GLP-1RA exhibited significantly greater efficacy in resistant cells, as evidenced by lower IC50 values in resistant lines compared to their parental counterparts (Huh7: IC50 40 μM vs. 26 μM, PLC: IC50 42 μM vs. 30 μM; Fig. 2E).

These results indicate that higher GLP-1R expression in resistant cells coincides with increased sensitivity to GLP-1RA, suggesting that GLP-1R upregulation marks a metabolically distinct subset of Lenvatinib-resistant HCC amenable to GLP-1RA-mediated modulation.

Synergistic efficacy of GLP-1RA and Lenvatinib in Lenvatinib-resistant HCC cells

To investigate the potential antitumor activity of GLP-1RA, we examined its synergistic interaction with Lenvatinib in Lenvatinib-resistant HCC cells. Interestingly, GLP-1RA exhibited a measurable synergistic interaction with Lenvatinib, particularly in resistant cell lines. The most effective combinations were observed at Lenvatinib concentrations of 10 μM and 20 μM, with GLP-1RA at 20 μM, yielding the highest inhibition rates (Fig. 3A–D). Quantitative analysis of synergy further supported these findings, with the combination therapy yielding Bliss synergy scores of 6.62 in Huh7-resistant cells and 4.54 in PLC-resistant cells (Fig. 3C, D). Furthermore, colony formation assays confirmed significant suppression of colony formation when GLP-1RA and Lenvatinib were combined compared with monotherapy treatments (Huh7: p < 0.001; PLC: p < 0.001; Fig. 3E).

Three-dimensional synergy maps demonstrated synergistic interactions between GLP-1RA and Lenvatinib, with mean Bliss scores of 6.62 and 4.54, respectively. Combination treatment reduced colony-forming ability compared with single-agent treatment.

To further characterize the effects of the combination therapy, we performed invasion and wound-healing assays. The combination treatment significantly reduced invasive capacity and migratory potential compared with control and monotherapy groups (Huh7: p < 0.001; PLC: p < 0.001; Fig. 4A, B), indicating enhanced suppression of metastatic phenotypes.

Combination treatment increased apoptosis compared with single-agent treatment. D Western blot analysis of apoptosis-related proteins in resistant Huh7 and PLC cells. Combination treatment increased cleaved PARP, cleaved Caspase-3, and BAX expression compared with single-agent treatment.

To assess apoptosis, flow cytometry analysis revealed that combined treatment induced significantly higher apoptotic rates than either agent alone. In Huh7-resistant cells, apoptosis increased from 9.6% in controls to 11.3% with Lenvatinib, 25.6% with GLP-1RA, and 37.7% with the combination (p < 0.001; Fig. 4C). Similarly, in PLC-resistant cells, apoptosis increased from ~10% in control and Lenvatinib groups to 22.1% with GLP-1RA and 29.1% with the combination (p < 0.001; Fig. 4C). Western blot analysis further confirmed activation of apoptotic signaling, as evidenced by increased expression of cleaved PARP, cleaved Caspase-3, and BAX (Fig. 4D). Importantly, the increase in apoptosis induced by the combination treatment was partially attenuated by the pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK, supporting the involvement of caspase-dependent apoptotic pathways (Supplementary Fig. S1A and B).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that GLP-1RA enhances the antitumor efficacy of Lenvatinib in resistant HCC cells by suppressing proliferation, invasion, and migration, while promoting caspase-dependent apoptosis.

GLP-1RA modulates glycolytic metabolism and enhances Lenvatinib efficacy via the AMPKα1/HIF-1α/PFKFB3 axis in resistant HCC cells

To elucidate the underlying mechanism of GLP-1RA’s antitumor activity, we explored its impact on cellular metabolism. Given the well-established efficacy of GLP-1RA in metabolic diseases such as NASH and NAFLD, we hypothesized that it may exert anti-tumor effects in HCC cells through metabolic regulation. To test this hypothesis, we first assessed glycolytic activity in Lenvatinib-resistant cells. Extracellular lactate levels were significantly increased in resistant cells compared with parental cells (p < 0.01; Fig. 5A), indicating enhanced aerobic glycolysis associated with HIF-1α–driven metabolic reprogramming. Consistently, extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) analysis confirmed that resistant cells exhibited increased glycolytic flux (p < 0.01; Fig. 5B, C). GLP-1RA monotherapy reduced lactate production compared with control, and combination treatment with Lenvatinib also suppressed the elevated lactate levels observed in resistant cells (p < 0.01; Fig. 5D). Similarly, ECAR analysis showed that both GLP-1RA monotherapy and combination treatment attenuated glycolytic flux (p < 0.01; Fig. 5E, F).

Collectively, these findings suggest that GLP-1RA modulates glycolytic metabolism and may contribute to the attenuation of the Warburg-like phenotype in Lenvatinib-resistant HCC cells.

GLP-1RA induces metabolic remodeling through the AMPKα1/HIF-1α/PFKFB3 pathway

To gain a broader understanding of glycolytic regulation, we subsequently conducted a comprehensive qRT-PCR analysis of genes involved in glycolysis. Several glycolytic enzymes and regulators were significantly upregulated in resistant cells compared with parental counterparts (Supplementary Fig. S2). Significantly, treatment with GLP-1RA, either alone or in combination with Lenvatinib, suppressed the expression of some of these upregulated glycolytic genes. More specifically, GLP-1RA significantly inhibited HIF-1α and PFKFB3 through the modulation of AMPKα1, thereby highlighting its potential ability to normalize glycolytic gene expression profiles (p < 0.01; Fig. 5G, Supplementary Fig. S3). These findings were further validated by Western blot analysis, which demonstrated increased AMPKα1 expression and decreased HIF-1α and PFKFB3 expression following GLP-1RA treatment (Fig. 5H).

Since protein localization can critically influence function, we next investigated the subcellular localization of HIF-1α and PFKFB3. HIF-1α was predominantly localized in the nucleus, where it functions as a transcription factor to regulate hypoxia-related genes. In contrast, PFKFB3 was primarily localized in the cytoplasm, where it enhances glycolysis by producing fructose-2,6-bisphosphate (Fig. 6A). Interestingly, both HIF-1α nuclear localization and PFKFB3 cytoplasmic expression were significantly suppressed by GLP-1RA, particularly in combination with Lenvatinib, as demonstrated by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 6B, C).

Finally, to confirm that HIF-1α directly regulates PFKFB3 expression and to further verify the effects of GLP-1RA on these pathways, we used the HIF-1α activator FG-4592 (50 μM) and the HIF-1α inhibitor BAY 87-2243 (10 μM) and assessed their effects by Western blot analysis. Activation of HIF-1α by FG-4592 led to upregulation of PFKFB3, whereas inhibition of HIF-1α by BAY 87-2243 significantly suppressed PFKFB3 expression (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, the combination of GLP-1RA and Lenvatinib yielded phenotypic effects resembling those observed with the HIF-1α inhibitor, including reduced HIF-1α and PFKFB3 expression. In addition, co-treatment attenuated the upregulation of HIF-1α and PFKFB3 induced by an HIF-1α activator, which is consistent with a model in which GLP-1RA can modulate the HIF-1α/PFKFB3 axis. These findings provide supportive evidence that GLP-1RA may influence metabolic programs in Lenvatinib-resistant HCC cells by suppressing components of the HIF-1α/PFKFB3 pathway. To further validate the involvement of AMPK signaling, pharmacological inhibition of AMPK significantly attenuated the effects of GLP-1RA on HIF-1α/PFKFB3 expression and glycolytic activity (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Together, these results are consistent with a mechanism in which GLP-1RA is associated with activation of AMPKα1 and reduced expression of HIF-1α and PFKFB3, accompanied by attenuation of glycolytic activity in Lenvatinib-resistant HCC models. This metabolic modulation was associated with enhanced Lenvatinib response in vitro, suggesting that targeting metabolic dependencies may represent a rationale for combination strategies in metabolically reprogrammed HCC.

Clinical significance of HIF-1α and PFKFB3 in HCC

To further investigate the clinical significance of HIF-1α and PFKFB3 expression in HCC prognosis, we analyzed their expression levels in both our clinical cohort and the TCGA dataset. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses revealed that high expression of HIF-1α and PFKFB3 in our clinical samples was significantly associated with poor overall survival (HIF-1α: p < 0.001, PFKFB3: p = 0.004; Fig. 7A–C). These findings were further validated in the TCGA cohort, where high HIF-1α and PFKFB3 expression were similarly associated with worse overall survival (HIF-1α: p = 0.006; PFKFB3: p = 0.004; Fig. 7B–D). Correlation analysis revealed a significant positive association between HIF-1α and PFKFB3 expression across clinical HCC samples (Pearson r = 0.55, p < 0.001; Fig. 7E), underscoring their cooperative role in regulating glycolysis. Moreover, comparison between DM and non-DM HCC patients revealed significantly higher expressions of both HIF-1α and PFKFB3 in DM patients (HIF-1α: p < 0.001, PFKFB3: p = 0.009; Fig. 7F).

High expression of both markers was associated with poorer overall survival. Given that HIF-1α and PFKFB3 were more highly expressed in HCC tissues from patients with diabetes, our data raise the hypothesis that metabolically dysregulated, HIF-1α/PFKFB3-high HCC, frequently seen in diabetes and MASLD, may constitute a subset particularly dependent on this glycolytic axis.

Discussion

GLP-1RA, originally developed for diabetes, has demonstrated therapeutic potential in metabolic diseases and cancer. In this study, we explored the antitumor effects of GLP-1RA in HCC cells, assessed its ability to enhance Lenvatinib activity, and investigated the underlying metabolic mechanisms. Our study showed that GLP-1R expression was significantly increased in HCC, particularly in Lenvatinib-resistant cells, suggesting that GLP-1RA exposure may be more relevant in metabolically dysregulated HCC. Furthermore, HCC is known as a malignant tumor in which metabolic remodeling is extensively involved, and metabolic abnormalities play an essential role in tumor progression and chemotherapy resistance51–53. Glycolysis, which was significantly elevated in Lenvatinib-resistant cell lines, was partially attenuated by GLP-1RA treatment. GLP-1RA has already been reported to be an effective treatment for metabolic disorders such as NASH and NAFLD38–40, and it was suggested that it exerts its antitumor effects through metabolic remodeling.

Furthermore, a targeted transcriptional analysis of glycolysis-related genes identified AMPKα1/HIF-1α/PFKFB3 as a key metabolic axis modulated by GLP-1RA in resistant cells. While this analysis was not based on unbiased genome-wide or transcriptomic profiling, it enabled focused evaluation of metabolically relevant pathways directly linked to our hypothesis. This metabolic change was associated with reduced glycolytic readouts and greater sensitivity to Lenvatinib in vitro in resistant cells.

Mechanistically, AMPK functions as a central energy sensor that maintains cellular metabolic balance by inhibiting anabolic pathways and promoting catabolic processes under metabolic stress54–56. GLP-1RA may modulate the dysregulated AMPKα1 signaling observed in Lenvatinib-resistant HCC cells, thereby suppressing HIF-1α stabilization and, in turn, reducing the transcription of key glycolytic enzymes, such as PFKFB3. This AMPK-dependent regulatory cascade may contribute to the observed metabolic changes and attenuation of the glycolytic (Warburg-like) phenotype in Lenvatinib-resistant HCC cells. These findings suggest that GLP-1RA acts not only as an anti-tumor agent but also as a metabolic modulator, restoring energy homeostasis disrupted in resistant cancer cells. Several studies have reported that the HIF-1α/PFKFB3 pathway plays a central role in metabolic remodeling in solid tumors57–60, highlighting the importance of these targets in glycolysis. Overall, these results are consistent with the possibility that GLP-1RA reduces glycolytic activation associated with Lenvatinib resistance. In this context, lactate was selected as a key metabolic readout because it represents the terminal product of aerobic glycolysis and reflects HIF-1α–dependent metabolic reprogramming, a hallmark of the Warburg effect in cancer cells. In addition, ECAR was evaluated as a complementary and dynamic measure of glycolytic flux, further supporting the suppression of glycolysis by GLP-1RA. Although GLP-1R expression was elevated in Lenvatinib-resistant cells, whether GLP-1R overexpression alone is sufficient to confer resistance remains unclear. Importantly, our findings suggest that GLP-1R upregulation may reflect a metabolically reprogrammed state rather than acting as a direct driver of resistance. Future gain-of-function studies will be necessary to determine the causal role of GLP-1R in the acquisition of Lenvatinib resistance.

In addition, our clinical sample analysis revealed that HCC tissues from DM patients had significantly higher expression of HIF-1α and PFKFB3 compared with non-DM HCC tissues. It has been reported that a high glucose environment enhances the Warburg effect in cancer by promoting glycolysis, increasing the glycolysis-related genes, and contributing to tumor malignancy61–63. Clinically, GLP-1RAs such as liraglutide and semaglutide are already widely prescribed and have well-established safety profiles. This pharmacological familiarity provides an opportunity for drug repurposing in oncology64,65. Given the high prevalence of diabetes and MASLD among HCC patients, incorporating GLP-1RA into existing therapeutic regimens may offer a metabolically targeted adjunctive strategy for selected patients. However, the potential clinical benefit of such combinations remains to be determined.

We acknowledge that the GLP-1RA concentrations used in our in vitro experiments exceed clinically achievable plasma levels. Our intent, however, was not to mimic systemic exposure but to explore the maximal capacity of GLP-1RA to modulate glycolytic pathways and Lenvatinib response in a controlled mechanistic setting. Similar supra-physiological ranges (5–20 μM) are widely employed in mechanistic studies of liraglutide and other GLP-1R agonists in HepG2 and cancer cell models to define direct anti-tumor and metabolic effects66–69. Thus, the current work should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating pharmacology, and future studies are needed to evaluate whether comparable pathway modulation occurs at clinically relevant exposures in vivo. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as proof-of-concept evidence demonstrating the potential of GLP-1RA to target metabolic vulnerabilities associated with Lenvatinib resistance.

We acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, all functional experiments were performed in in vitro models of acquired Lenvatinib resistance under controlled conditions, without validation in orthotopic, patient-derived xenograft, or immunocompetent in vivo models. This represents a limitation in terms of translational relevance, particularly because GLP-1 receptor agonists exert systemic endocrine effects and therapeutic responses to Lenvatinib are influenced by tumor microenvironment interactions. However, the primary objective of the present study was to identify metabolic vulnerabilities and delineate the underlying mechanisms of GLP-1RA action at the cellular level. Future studies using orthotopic and patient-derived xenograft models will be essential to determine whether GLP-1RA enhances Lenvatinib efficacy in vivo and to further evaluate the impact of systemic metabolism and tumor microenvironment on therapeutic response. Second, we did not directly demonstrate GLP-1R dependence or canonical cAMP/PKA signalling; therefore, off-target or receptor-independent effects cannot be excluded. Third, although supra-physiological concentrations were applied to explore mechanistic capacity, these doses do not reflect clinically achievable exposures. Future studies will be required to determine whether similar metabolic modulation can be achieved under physiologically relevant conditions. In addition, further investigation using isotope-labeled metabolic flux analysis and metabolomic profiling will be important to comprehensively characterize the downstream metabolic effects of GLP-1RA.

Despite these limitations, our findings provide proof-of-concept evidence supporting metabolic targeting as a strategy to overcome Lenvatinib resistance. These findings may inform the development of metabolically targeted combination strategies in HCC and support further translational investigation.

Taken together, our findings identify the AMPKα1/HIF-1α/PFKFB3 pathway as a metabolic vulnerability in Lenvatinib-resistant HCC and suggest that GLP-1RA-mediated metabolic modulation may enhance Lenvatinib response.

METHODS

Patient cohort

This study included an analysis of a cohort of fresh-frozen tumor and matched normal tissue specimens from 83 HCC patients enrolled at Tokushima University, Japan. Patient characteristics are detailed in Supplementary Table S1. All patients underwent surgical resection, and tumors were histologically confirmed as HCC. Tumors were classified according to the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) TNM Classification of Malignant Tumors, version 7. GLP-1R expression levels were assessed in these clinical specimens using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) to compare tumor and adjacent normal tissues. To evaluate the clinical relevance of metabolic regulators, publicly available HCC datasets from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) were used to analyze the association between HIF-1α and PFKFB3 expression and overall survival using Kaplan–Meier analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 23228). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to inclusion in the study.

Cell culture and materials

Human HCC cell lines (Huh-7, PLC-PRF-5, SNU398, HLE, and HLF), obtained from the RIKEN BioResource Center Cell Bank (Tsukuba, Japan), were utilized in this study. All cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Carlsbad, CA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO₂. Liraglutide (SML3925; GLP-1RA) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), and Lenvatinib (S1164) was obtained from Selleckchem (Houston, TX, USA). Both compounds were dissolved in DMSO to prepare stock solutions, which were then further diluted in culture medium to achieve the desired experimental concentrations. Liraglutide concentrations were selected based on previous mechanistic in vitro studies examining direct anti-tumor effects of GLP-1R agonists in liver and cancer cells66–69, which commonly employ supra-physiological ranges to explore maximal pathway modulation.

Lenvatinib-resistant cell line establishment

Lenvatinib-resistant cells were established by continuous culture of the Huh-7 and PLC-PRF-5 cell lines in Lenvatinib-enriched medium. The parental cell lines were treated with increasing doses of Lenvatinib, with 0.5 µM administered weekly for 10 months. Finally, resistant cells that could grow stably in medium containing 20 µM Lenvatinib were selected and used for further experiments to evaluate the antitumor effects of GLP-1RA.

Cell viability assays

For these assays, 5 ×10³ cells per well were seeded into 96-well plates under each culture condition. After overnight incubation to promote cell adherence to the plates, all cell lines were treated for 48 h with Lenvatinib, GLP-1RA, or their combination. Following completion of the treatments, Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) were performed using the CCK-8 Reagent (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Inc., Kumamoto, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Synergy scores were calculated using SynergyFinder 3.0, a freely accessible tool for interactive analysis and visualization of combination response outcomes70.

Invasion assays

For the invasion assay, cells (2.5 × 104 cells) treated with Lenvatinib, GLP-1RA, and their combination for 48 hours were grown in 24-well transwell chambers (8-μm pore size) coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). After 48 hours, invaded cells were fixed, stained with Diff-Quick, and counted.

Wound healing assays

For the wound-healing assay, cells treated with Lenvatinib, GLP-1RA, or their combination were seeded into 6-well plates and grown to 80% confluency. Wounds were made by scraping 200 µl of monolayer cells with a pipette tip. Twenty-four hours after wounding, treated and control cells were examined under a microscope.

Colony formation assays

For the colony formation assay, 5 ×102 cells treated with Lenvatinib, GLP-1RA, and their combinations for 48 hours were seeded into 6-well plates. Seven to ten days later, the number of colonies with 50 or more cells was counted, and relative changes were measured.

Apoptosis assays

For the Annexin V binding assay, cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with Lenvatinib, GLP-1RA, and their combinations for 48 hours. For inhibitor experiments, cells were pretreated with the pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK (20 μM, Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX, USA) for 1 hour at the indicated concentration prior to drug exposure. Apoptotic cell fractions were then measured using the Muse Cell Analyzer (Millipore Corp, Billerica, MA) with the Annexin V and Dead Cell Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Metabolic assays using XFe96 extracellular flux analyzer

Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) measurements were performed using the Seahorse XFe96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer and the Seahorse XF Glycolytic Rate Assay Kit (Agilent Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All materials and reagents were obtained from Agilent Technologies. HCC cells were seeded at a density of 1.6 × 10⁴ cells per well in XF96 cell culture microplates (pre-coated with Cell-Tak, Corning) using DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cells were incubated overnight at 37°C in a non-CO₂ incubator to allow for proper adherence. One hour before the assay, HCC cells were washed and incubated in XF DMEM assay medium (phenol red-free) supplemented with 2 mM glutamine, 10 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate, and 5 mM HEPES. ECAR was measured at the basal level (basal glycolysis + mitochondrial acidification) and in response to the sequential addition of rotenone/antimycin A (Rot/AA, mitochondrial electron transport chain inhibitors, inducing compensatory glycolysis) and 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG, a glucose analog, inhibiting glycolysis post-acidification). The metabolic assay was conducted following standardized protocols to ensure reproducibility and accuracy. For ECAR analysis, the mean ECAR value during the peak period (20–40 min) was used as an indicator of glycolytic activity. For AMPK inhibition studies, cells were pretreated with the AMPK inhibitor Compound C (Dorsomorphin; 10 μM, Selleck Chemicals) for 1 hour prior to treatment with GLP-1RA, Lenvatinib, or their combination. Both ECAR and lactate production were subsequently assessed under these conditions.

Lactate assays

Lactate levels in the cell culture medium were quantified using the Lactate Assay Kit (Fluorometric) (Cell Biolabs, Inc.), following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. Cell culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 5 minutes to remove insoluble particles. The clarified supernatant was transferred to a new tube for analysis. Prior to the assay, medium samples were diluted 50-fold in PBS. Then, 50 µL of each diluted sample was loaded in triplicate into a 96-well microplate, along with lactate standards prepared at various concentrations in 1X Assay Buffer. The reaction mixture, containing lactate oxidase, horseradish peroxidase (HRP), and a fluorometric probe, was added to each well. After incubation at 37°C for 30 minutes, fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader with excitation at 530–570 nm and emission at 590–600 nm. Lactate concentrations were determined using a standard curve generated from known lactate concentrations.

Immunofluorescence staining

Huh7 and PLC cell lines were used to assess the protein expression levels of GLP-1R, HIF1α, and PFKFB3. Cells were cultured on coverslips in appropriate culture medium and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes at room temperature. After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), permeabilization was performed using 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 minutes. To block nonspecific binding, cells were incubated with blocking buffer (5% bovine serum albumin [BSA] in PBS) for 1 hour at room temperature. Primary antibodies were diluted in blocking buffer according to the manufacturer’s instructions and incubated with the cells overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Coverslips were mounted using antifade mounting medium and sealed with nail polish. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope equipped with appropriate filter sets.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cancer cells treated with Lenvatinib, GLP-1RA, or their combination for 24 hours using the Qiagen miRNeasy Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from the extracted RNA using a high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), QuantStudio 6/7 Flex RT-PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), and qRT-PCR was performed using SensiFAST SYBR Lo-ROX Kit (Bioline, London, UK). The housekeeping gene β-Actin was used as an internal control. Gene expression levels were quantified using the delta Ct method, which calculates the difference in Ct values between target and control genes. To compare expression levels relative to a reference group, the delta-delta Ct (ΔΔCt) method was subsequently applied. The primer sequences used in this study are described in Supplementary Table S271,72.

Protein isolation and Western blotting

Western blotting was performed according to previous studies73–75. Total protein was extracted from HCC cell lines treated with Lenvatinib, GLP-1RA, or a combination of the two for 48 hours. Cells were collected using a plastic scraper and washed three times with cold PBS. Total cellular proteins were extracted using ice-cold RIPA buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Nuclear and cytoplasmic protein fractions were extracted using a nuclear extraction kit (Active Motif, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted proteins were denatured in 4× Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) containing 5% 2-mercaptoethanol. Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein samples (60 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE using 7.5% or 10% Mini-PROTEAN TGXTM Precast Gels (BIO-RAD, Hercules, CA, USA), transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, and then transferred to 0.45 μm. The membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich) in Tris-buffered saline (Bio-Rad) supplemented with 0.1% Tween-20 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were allowed to stand overnight at 4°C anti-primary antibody. After three washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody for 1 hour at room temperature. Blots were visualized using Gel Imaging Systems (BIO-RAD) with an HRP-based chemiluminescence kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A list of antibodies used is provided in Supplementary Table S3; β-actin, Lamin A/C, and α-Tubulin were used as a loading control.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between two groups, statistical tests were selected according to the data type. In cell-based experiments, Welch’s t-test was applied to account for unequal variances under the assumption of normal distribution. For analyses of clinical specimens, the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test was used due to potential departures from normality. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. For survival analyses, Kaplan–Meier curves were generated to estimate overall survival. Group differences were assessed using the log-rank test, and hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the Cox proportional hazards model. For correlation analyses, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was employed to evaluate linear associations between continuous variables. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1).
