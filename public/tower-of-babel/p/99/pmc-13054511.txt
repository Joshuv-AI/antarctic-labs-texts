Extended Transoral Resection of Anterior Commissure Tumors in a Canine Model: Impact on Laryngeal Framework, Glottis, and Vocal Function
Authors: Zhongyi Wu, Li Wang, Yuheng Lu, Liuxiang Huang, Jingmei Yao, Dongxiao Nong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and ObjectivesEvaluate the feasibility and functional outcomes of extended transoral resection for anterior commissure (AC) tumors in canines, focusing on laryngeal framework, airway, and vocal function.MethodsTwelve dogs underwent endoscopic partial thyroid cartilage resection, randomized into: Group A (AC + adjacent cartilage), Group B (AC + left vocal cord anterior 1/3), Group C (AC + bilateral vocal cord anterior 1/3). Outcomes assessed included laryngoscopic healing, CT‐measured laminal angle (LA) and glottal area (GA), weight, and GRBAS vocal scores.ResultsThe wound healed and epithelized in 4 weeks. Postoperative LA significantly decreased in all groups (p ≤ 0.010), with the largest reduction in Group C (p ≤ 0.030 vs. A/B). LA reduction ratio correlated with resected midline height (RMH) (r = 0.546, p = 0.041) rather than resection factors of width and area. GA remained stable. Group C showed severe vocal impairment (GRBAS 8.67 ± 0.58 vs. 5.63 ± 0.48 in A, p = 0.008). Weight transiently decreased at day 7 but recovered by day 35.ConclusionExtended transoral resection effectively removes AC lesions while preserving airway dimensions. However, extensive resection causes significant vocal dysfunction, highlighting the balance between oncologic radicality and function preservation. The potential LA‐RMH correlation reveals the stabilizing characteristics of cartilaginous structures, offering preclinical insights for optimizing AC cancer surgery.Keywords: Glottic carcinoma, laryngeal anterior commissure, microsurgery, thyroid cartilage

Introduction

The American Cancer Society (2022) reported approximately 12,470 annual global cases of laryngeal cancer, with about 60% originating from the glottis, making it the most common type among the subtypes of laryngeal cancer [1]. Anatomically, the glottic region extends from the superior plane of the laryngeal vestibule to a plane 1 cm below the vocal cords, encompassing both the vocal cords and the glottis. The anterior commissure (AC) is defined as the region where the anterior ends of the bilateral vocal cords meet. This end is linked by the Broyles ligament and attached to the thyroid cartilage (TC) lamina, making the AC a vulnerable area [2, 3, 4]. Due to its unique anatomy, tumors can invade the potential space in the anterior laryngeal region and the TC through the AC, and may even penetrate the cartilage to affect extra‐laryngeal tissues. Some studies have suggested that involvement of the AC is a risk factor for poor prognosis in glottic cancer [5, 6, 7].

Transoral laser microsurgery (TLM) is an effective treatment for glottic cancer, offering distinct advantages over traditional open surgery, including shorter hospital stays, better preservation of the larynx, and superior functional outcomes [8, 9]. Promising results have been reported in the exploration of TLM for early‐stage glottic cancer with AC involvement [6, 10, 11]. On the other hand, Vilaseca et al. reported a 13.6% complication rate in TLM‐treated patients, with postoperative bleeding being the most life‐threatening complication [12]. The study identified vocal cord fixation and focal cartilage infiltration as independent negative prognostic factors for organ preservation. Similarly, Tulli et al. emphasized that AC involvement was associated with higher recurrence rates and poorer oncological outcomes, underscoring the need for careful patient selection and treatment planning [7]. TLM presents challenges, including limited surgical field exposure and technical difficulties in en bloc resection. Nevertheless, some studies have pointed out that TLM is feasible for tumors requiring cartilage resection in stage T3, under appropriate conditions [13].

A study proposed a transoral laser microsurgery plan for AC involvement in canines [14], recommending that partial laryngectomy with different extents of cartilage resection offers potential value. Nonetheless, the challenges associated with the laryngeal framework after an extended resection of the TC can lead to deformation or even collapse of the larynx, along with alterations in the size of the airway. These issues involve not only perioperative risks but also the postoperative quality of life. The present study proposed an extended resection surgical plan for glottic cancer, performing transoral partial TC resection with varying extents in a canine model. In addition to postoperative observations of wound healing, respiration and phonation quality, CT imaging was used to evaluate the surgical impact on the TC framework and airway. Our previous study involving human subjects revealed that multi‐slice spiral CT imaging and three‐dimensional reconstruction offer satisfying visualization of laryngeal cartilages [15], enabling the measurement of objective parameters using various landmarks. Based on the analysis of preoperative and postoperative CT measurements, the impact of different resection extents on the TC and airway changes, as well as the underlying mechanisms, were discussed.

Material and Methods

Twelve healthy Corgi dogs (both male and female), weighing between 8 and 13 kg, were provided by the Animal Experiment Center of Guangxi Medical University. A comprehensive health assessment was conducted before the experiment. The study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guangxi Medical University, Approval Number: 2025‐E0683. To minimize pain to the greatest extent, pain management for all surgical dogs strictly complied with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines.

Grouping and Surgical Process

All experimental canines underwent general anesthesia, using combined anesthesia of 5% sodium pentobarbital, 0.25 mL/kg and sertraline hydrochloride (0.4 mg/kg) without intratracheal intubation. The animals were randomly divided into three groups: Group A: resection of AC + adjacent cartilage, Group B: resection of AC + left vocal cord anterior 1/3 + adjacent cartilage, and Group C: resection of AC + bilateral vocal cord anterior 1/3 + adjacent cartilage (Figure 1A–C).

Different surgical resection plans for groups (A–C) are respectively illustrated in subfigures (A–C). Sketches (D–F) show measurements of thyroid cartilage (TC) using reconstructed CT before and after surgery. (D) The original area (OA) of TC laminae (blue area), The original width (OW) of TC (green dashed line), the original midline height (OMH) of TC (blue line); (E) resected area (RA) (blue mesh), resected width (RW) (green dashed line), resected midline height (RMH) (blue line); (F) the glottic area (GA) (blue area) and laminal angle (LA) of TC (angle between redlines).

A Stativ S3 surgical microscope (Zeiss, Germany), a self‐retaining laryngoscope, and a laparoscopic electrocautery hook (3 mm in diameter) connected to a high‐frequency electrosurgical generator were used for laryngeal surgeries. For Group A, a rectangular cartilaginous window was made in the AC area, with the upper margin being a 1.5 cm horizontal incision approximately 0.5 cm above the AC. Longitudinal incisions were made on both sides, starting from the upper incision and extending to the inferior margin of the TC. The resection range of Groups B and C corresponded to their settings, with the horizontal incision extended to the ipsilateral or bilateral side, respectively (Figure 1B,C). Without a lower border, the cartilage defect was more like a door rather than a window. The cutting depth was determined by ensuring the cartilage was just perforated. To ensure a better view and control of the cartilage resection range, the soft tissues attached to the target cartilage were removed more extensively. The entire resected specimen included the target cartilage and the overlying soft tissues of vocal folds, ventricular folds, muscles, and connective tissues.

General Condition, Glottic Observation, and Vocal Evaluation

The feeding conditions for each experimental dog were consistent. For the first five postoperative days, a full liquid diet was provided, with a regular diet introduced thereafter. The weights of the experimental dogs were measured at different times: preoperatively and then repeated on the 3rd, 7th, 14th, 21st, 28th, and 35th days after surgery. Endoscopic glottic observation and picture capture were performed preoperatively and immediately postoperatively, as well as at 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 weeks following the surgery. Additionally, a vocal assessment was conducted before the surgery and on the 40th postoperative day. Phonation was elicited using food stimuli. To ensure unbiased evaluation, two trained assessors, standardized on a simplified GRBAS scale (comprising Grade, Roughness, and Breathiness), independently rated the phonation samples under blinded conditions. The samples were presented in random order, with no access to surgical or timepoint information, and were identified only by randomized codes. Every parameter was scored from 0 to 3, with 0: Normal; 1: Mildly impaired; 2: Moderately impaired; 3: Severely impaired [16]. A score increment of 0.5 was adopted as the minimal unit to ensure measurement precision. The final score for each sample was calculated as the mean of the two assessors' ratings.

CT Scan and Imaging Data Processing

CT scans of the neck (Siemens 64‐slice spiral CT scanner) were conducted preoperatively and postoperatively on the 40th day under general anesthesia. After the postoperative CT scan, laryngeal specimens were surgically excised via total laryngectomy, followed by humane euthanasia with an overdose of pentobarbital sodium. The CT images were processed using the Mimics Research 21.0 system for glottic area (GA) measurement and 3D reconstruction of the TC. CT axial slices, acquired in the prone position, exhibited an angular deviation from the glottal plane. GA was thus measured after re‐slicing to align slices with the glottis, defined by bilateral vocal processes and AC (Figures 1F and 2, left). For 3D reconstruction of the TC, an appropriate gray‐level threshold was set to outline the cartilage's overall shape. Image noise was manually removed to optimize the contour. An intelligent smoothing algorithm was applied to optimize the reconstructed model (Figure 2, right). The 3D image was then analyzed using the 3‐matic Research 13.0 system. Original area (OA) or resected area (RA) of the TC laminae was measured using a mark tool (Figure 1D,E). Original width (OW) and resected width (RW) of the TC were measured through the full inferior margin of the TC and the inferior width of the resected TC window (Figure 1D,E). The original midline height (OMH) and resected midline height (RMH) of the lamina were measured through the anterior midline from the superior thyroid notch to the postoperative cartilage cut edge or the preoperative lower edge of the TC (Figure 1E). Laminal angle of the TC was measured using an angle tool based on cartilaginous landmarks of the bilateral superior TC tubercles and the superior thyroid notch (Figure 1F). The original laminal angle (OLA) was defined as the preoperative angle, and the reduction of laminal angle (RLA) was derived by subtracting the postoperative measurement from the preoperative value. To ensure measurement precision, two blinded assessors independently performed all morphometric analyses on three‐dimensional CT reconstructions using standardized anatomical landmarks (as previously described). Each parameter was measured in triplicate under identical observation angle and magnification conditions, with final values representing the mean of six independent measurements (inter‐observer ×3 replicates).

Red arrows: Various extents of cartilage missing after operation; Blue arrows: The glottis; Green arrow: The ventricle of the larynx.

Statistical Methods

Data, presented as mean ± SD, were analyzed and processed using SPSS Statistics version 26.0. Paired t‐tests were used to compare preoperative and postoperative values. For inter‐group differences, one‐way ANOVA or repeated measures ANOVA was used if the data followed a normal distribution. Pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni or Tambane's T2 tests. Correlation analyses were performed to assess the relationships between RLA/OLA and three parameters: RA/OA, RMH/OMH, and RW/OW. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Post hoc power was calculated using G*Power version 3.1.9.7.

Result

The surgical procedures were completed in all animals without intraoperative complications. Postoperative endoscopic and computed tomography (CT) imaging data were successfully obtained from all canines.

Preoperative and postoperative endoscopic images are presented in Figure 3. The immediate postoperative observation, conducted through both microscope and endoscope, demonstrated that the resection met the protocol, as the size of the TC defect increased in the order of Group A, Group B, and Group C. During the initial postoperative phase (Week 1), all experimental groups exhibited characteristic edema development at the surgical sites. Marked vascular congestion and soft tissue swelling were observed in both the defect areas and adjacent tissues, with Group B and C demonstrating significantly greater edema severity compared to Group A. Discussion

The AC is a vulnerable area in laryngeal carcinoma, with tumors often invading the TC. Previous studies have highlighted the characteristics of tumors with AC involvement. Rucci et al. mentioned that the probability of a primary tumor at the AC is very low, and most cases are due to the spread of surrounding lesions, including both sides of the vocal cords and adjacent supra and subglottic tissues [2]. Additionally, Hirvikoski and Ulusan found that tumors originating in the supraglottic region are more invasive than those from the glottis. They suggested that tumors spreading from the supraglottic area to the AC are more likely to invade the cartilage [17, 18]. The use of narrow‐band imaging (NBI) and high‐definition video laryngoscopy has improved the visualization of tumor margins and enhanced the precision of TLM [19], however, it is unhelpful in evaluating cartilage invasion.

In diagnostic imaging, Barbosa et al. investigated a gross radiological CT classification system for AC involvement (GRACI), which did not provide any statistically significant benefit for early‐stage tumors and failed to demonstrate thyroid cartilage invasion [20]. Comparative studies about preoperative diagnosis of glottic tumors with AC involvement have shown that MRI exhibits superior accuracy over CT in T‐staging of laryngeal carcinomas involving the AC. Still, it is noteworthy that MRI possesses relatively lower specificity, with diagnostic outcomes potentially compromised by temporal factors related to imaging acquisition and physiological motion artifacts from respiration and deglutition [21]. Furthermore, some studies indicate that preoperative imaging frequently demonstrates limited reliability in detecting cartilage involvement, often necessitating intraoperative tumor re‐localization based on surgical findings [12, 13]. Regardless of the imaging modalities employed, the precise determination of tumor invasion depth remains a significant diagnostic challenge in clinical practice.

Given the AC's susceptibility to tumor invasion and limitations in preoperative imaging, extended resection of cartilage surrounding the AC is recommended when AC involvement is suspected, as incomplete excision during TLM increases recurrence risk. Supracricoid partial laryngectomy with cricohyoidopexy or with adjacent tissue flaps [22, 23] offers advantages of oncologically adequate resection margins involving the AC and functional restoration. While conventional open techniques address tumor extirpation scenarios unachievable through transoral approaches, their invasive nature incurs significant morbidity and prolonged recovery. Conversely, aggressive transoral resection poses not only technical challenges but also uncertainty regarding laryngeal framework stability and the feasibility of functional reconstruction. These unresolved considerations highlight the need for refined surgical strategies in AC management. Shapshay et al. proposed an endoscopic CO2 laser combined with open surgery for window partial resection of the TC in one block, as well as two different techniques for vocal cord reconstruction using the sternohyoid muscle. The resection of cartilage in one piece and postoperative window repair both showed excellent outcomes [4]. Subsequent clinical validation of this technique confirmed its therapeutic efficacy [24]. The feasibility of this technique warrants further debate, as it necessitates the integration of TLM‐based tumor resection with transcervical reconstruction, which are approaches with inherently divergent surgical philosophies. An animal study, reported by Sun, proposed an endoscopic CO2 laser technique for excision of vocal cords and soft tissues in the AC region, along with cartilage windowing at different depths in the AC [14]. The results indicated that this approach led to minimal damage and preservation of the TC framework. Notably, this canine model corresponds to restricted tumoral invasion to the AC, with the resected cartilage exhibiting characteristics akin to a minor perforation rather than an extensive fenestration. As the defect caused by extended AC resection is not amenable to transoral procedures of reconstruction, the postoperative implications for laryngeal anatomies and functions require further investigation through animal surgical models. The present study proposed a surgical technique that necessitates an anterior partial resection of the TC, encompassing the inferior margin of the TC to accurately replicate clinical scenarios involving neoplastic infiltration of vocal fold structures, supraglottic and subglottic tissues, as well as AC involvement. Moreover, this technique transcends the conventional concept of creating a window with a limited size at the lower junction of TC laminae; instead, it enlarges a window into a sizable door that addresses the oncological requirements for three‐dimensional tumor clearance not only in depth but also in vertical and horizontal planes.

The application of electrosurgical devices in transoral laryngeal surgery presents a distinct alternative to the mainstream option of laser microsurgery. Both cutting devices have their own advantages and disadvantages. In terms of resection precision and minimal damage to surrounding tissues, lasers show a distinct advantage due to the smaller cutting point [25, 26, 27]. From a practical surgical perspective, the laser's non‐contact modality, eliminating instrument interference, offers distinct advantages in providing superior surgical field visualization. On the other hand, the electrocautery hook provides surgeons with distinct tactile feedback during tissue dissection, particularly when penetrating cartilaginous structures. For hemostatic effect, electrocautery demonstrates superior coagulation efficacy compared to laser resection. The successful completion of all surgical procedures in canine models, characterized by minimal intraoperative bleeding and negligible complications, substantiates the technical feasibility and safety profile of this technique. Nevertheless, difficulties can happen during transoral TC resection in patients, owing to the anatomical differences between canine and human laryngeal structures, especially in TC calcification patterns. Future efforts should focus on combining electrocautery and laser techniques, along with specialized tools, to improve surgical outcomes.

Endoscopic evaluation of postoperative wound healing demonstrated remarkable concordance with the findings from Sun's canine laser surgery experiments [14]. The implementation of weekly endoscopic observation enabled monitoring of inflammatory resolution and edema regression patterns. During the initial two‐week postoperative period, groups B and C, characterized by more extensive resection volumes, exhibited more remarkable inflammatory responses compared to group A. Nevertheless, all experimental groups subsequently achieved satisfactory wound healing outcomes with complete mucosal re‐epithelialization by the fourth postoperative week, without complications of glottic stenosis or respiratory compromise. These align with the typical postoperative course observed in most humans.

Body weight monitoring demonstrated no statistically significant intergroup differences, suggesting that the extent of surgical resection did not substantially affect food intake or body weight. However, a marked reduction in body weight was observed during the initial postoperative week. Feeding behavior and laryngoscopic examination revealed that this weight loss was primarily attributable to transient feeding difficulties arising from acute inflammatory responses at the surgical site in the early postoperative phase. Aside from this temporary reduction in nutritional intake, the canine model exhibited favorable tolerance and adaptive capacity to transoral laryngeal surgical intervention.

In accordance with established clinical voice assessment standards, we performed perceptual voice analysis using the GRBAS scale to comprehensively evaluate vocal function [28, 29]. Quantitative analysis of postoperative vocalization revealed significant intergroup differences. Group A demonstrated superior preservation of vocal quality compared to Group C, a finding attributable to the relatively limited surgical defect in Group A. The moderate extent of tissue defect in Group B did not yield statistically different voice quality as compared to the other two groups, which demands more precise and objective voice analysis in future studies.

Anatomically, the TC can be conceptualized as comprising two symmetrical laminae that converge anteriorly to form a roof‐like structure. The structural integrity of this configuration is principally maintained by the roof ridge at the junction of the two laminae, with supplementary stabilization provided by the cricothyroid joints and the thyrohyoid ligaments connecting the superior cornua to the hyoid bone. Under physiological conditions, the stability of the TC framework is crucial for upper airway patency and laryngeal muscular attachment. Given the inherent limitations of ex vivo larynxes, including the absence of vascular perfusion, neural innervation, and cervical tissue support, discrepancies in laryngeal framework configuration and glottic morphology inevitably exist compared to in vivo conditions. Accordingly, this study deliberately abstained from direct morphometric measures in excised larynx specimens. Instead, CT imaging, which presents superior visualization of cartilaginous structures compared to MRI, was selected to obtain accurate, three‐dimensional data of laryngeal anatomical structures in vivo.

Quantitative analysis of reconstructed CT imaging data enabled precise morphometric evaluation of the TC framework and glottic dimensions. Partial TC resection induced a significant change in LA in each group, with varying degrees of RLA observed across experimental groups. These findings suggest a biomechanical linkage between TC structural deformation and resection dimensions (RMH, RW, and RA) as well as the preserved roof ridge (calculated as OMH minus RMH). Correlation analysis revealed a strong positive relationship between the relative LA reduction (RLA/OLA) and the resected MH ratio (RMH/OMH) (r = 0.57, p = 0.033). In contrast, no significant correlations were detected between RLA/OLA and either the resected area ratio (RA/OA) or the resected width ratio (RW/ORW). Regardless of the significance of the vertical factor (RMH), the nonsignificant width factor (RW), a contributor to the total resected area, attenuated the area‐dependent relationship. These results collectively indicate that the preserved MH is the only key factor maintaining postoperative TC structure and the cartilaginous roof stability. Our data strongly suggest RMH as a critical predictor of postoperative laryngeal framework stability. While the current study's exploratory design (n = 18, post hoc power = 0.576) may limit immediate clinical extrapolation, the mechanistic plausibility underscores the need for confirmatory animal studies and clinical trials.

Although postoperative TC framework weakening and deformation occurred, they did not cause complete collapse. Nonetheless, these changes potentially impact the glottis, the narrowest and most vulnerable segment of the upper airway. Interestingly, the GA remained unchanged despite cartilage resection and resultant roof weakening. Postoperative CT imaging revealed an irregular glottic configuration with significant AC widening across all groups. This AC enlargement appears to compensate for the bilaterally depressed glottis secondary to TC roof deformation, suggesting a complex interplay between surgical defects, TC deformation, and healing processes in maintaining postoperative glottal patency.

Clinical studies on thyroid cartilage framework alterations or deformities primarily involve open partial laryngectomy for laryngeal cancer, traumatic injuries, or congenital malformations [30, 31]. Particularly, supracricoid partial laryngectomy significantly modifies laryngeal framework morphology, typically requiring patients to undergo tracheotomy followed by prolonged postoperative rehabilitation of respiratory, voice and deglutition functions [32]. In contrast, TLM for laryngeal cancer does not routinely include tracheotomy, with most patients achieving immediate postoperative oral intake. This experimental model particularly emphasizes the necessity of evaluating postoperative respiratory and swallowing functions and their underlying mechanisms following extensive transoral resection adjacent to the AC. Despite significant anatomical and physiological differences between canines and humans, our findings suggest potential clinical benefits, such as enhanced tumor clearance, airway safety, and functional preservation. These advantages could lead to refinements in TLM protocols, particularly in expanding surgical indications and optimizing techniques.

Conclusion

A novel transoral microlaryngoscopic technique for extended TC resection adjacent to the AC was investigated using a canine model. Postoperative evaluation confirmed the anticipated preservation of deglutition and phonatory function. CT images revealed that the structural integrity of the TC framework was associated with longitudinal (height) rather than transverse (width) resection dimensions, whereas glottic dimensions remained stable despite modifications to the cartilaginous framework. These preclinical findings provide valuable insights for optimizing surgical resection strategies in glottic carcinoma involving the AC.
