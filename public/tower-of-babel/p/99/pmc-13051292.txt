Chest Port Placement Through a Recently Placed Superior Vena Cava Stent Using a Hemostatic Vascular Sheath: A Case Report
Authors: Jonathon Schutt, Ravi Malpani, Alexandra Rhodes, Christopher Yeisley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 57-year-old man with extensive-stage small cell lung cancer presented with superior vena cava (SVC) syndrome caused by a right upper lobe mass and underwent stenting from the SVC to the left brachiocephalic vein. Because ongoing chemotherapy required durable central venous access, chest port placement was subsequently attempted after stent placement. However, advancing a conventional peel-away sheath across the recently placed stent was considered undesirable because of the risk of stent deformation or displacement. A chest wall port was successfully placed by advancing only the guidewire and catheter through the stent interstices using an 8-Fr hemostatic vascular sheath instead of a peel-away sheath. Imaging confirmed appropriate catheter course and tip position within the stented segment. This case illustrates a feasible and practical alternative for central venous access following malignant SVC stenting.Keywords: Interventional radiology, Superior vena cava syndrome, Port catheter, Central venous access, Stents

INTRODUCTION

Superior vena cava (SVC) syndrome results from impaired venous return caused by obstruction of the SVC, most frequently secondary to thoracic malignancy. Lung cancer accounts for approximately 70% of malignant cases, with small cell lung cancer comprising approximately one-quarter of these cases [1]. Patients typically present with facial and upper extremity swelling, venous distension, and dyspnea, with symptom severity depending on the extent of collateral venous development and the rapidity of obstruction [1,2]. Historically, treatment relied on systemic chemotherapy or radiation therapy; however, these modalities often provided delayed symptom relief and were less effective in cases of severe or complete obstruction [1].

Endovascular stenting has become the standard treatment for malignant SVC obstruction because it provides rapid symptom relief with high technical and clinical success rates. Technical success has been reported in more than 95% of cases, and symptom relief in more than 90% [2]. Self-expanding bare-metal stents are most commonly used owing to their flexibility and conformability, whereas covered stents may offer improved long-term patency in selected cases but carry risks of migration and collateral occlusion [2,3].

Reliable central venous access remains essential in these patients for administering systemic therapy. However, chest port placement after SVC stenting may be technically challenging because advancement of a peel-away sheath through the stented segment may cause stent deformation, displacement, or embolization [4]. Preservation or repositioning of pre-existing central venous catheters during SVC stenting has been described [3]. In contrast, initial port placement after stenting has rarely been reported. Alternative techniques employing a hemostatic vascular sheath may help minimize these risks of stent-related complications [4]. Here, we describe successful chest port placement by passing only the wire and catheter through the stent interstices after malignant SVC stenting. This case report was exempt from institutional review board review, and informed consent was obtained from the patient.

CASE

A 57-year-old man with a history of tobacco use presented with symptoms suggestive of SVC syndrome. Chest computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a right upper lobe mass invading the SVC with multiple hepatic metastases, consistent with extensive-stage small cell lung cancer (Fig. 1).

Contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography demonstrating a right upper lobe mass invading the superior vena cava.

Endovascular stenting was performed. Venography revealed near-occlusive lesion, and an 18-mm self-expanding Abre stent (Medtronic) was deployed from the SVC to the left brachiocephalic vein. This trajectory was selected on the basis of the vessel diameters (SVC, 24 mm; right brachiocephalic vein, 22 mm; left brachiocephalic vein, 16 mm). The patient’s symptoms improved promptly after stent placement.

The patient began chemotherapy with cisplatin and etoposide, although the etoposide dose was reduced due to elevated liver enzyme levels. After minimal tumor response and cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity were observed, carboplatin was substituted in subsequent cycles. Durable venous access was required for ongoing therapy.

A left-sided approach was avoided due to unfavorable angulation relative to the distal end of the stent, and a right chest wall port was selected. The right internal jugular vein was accessed using the Seldinger technique, and a 0.035-inch Amplatz Super Stiff guidewire (Boston Scientific) was carefully navigated through the stent interstices into the inferior vena cava. An 8-Fr×11-cm vascular access sheath was then inserted through the internal jugular access site and used as a hemostatic sheath. In contrast to peel-away systems, this approach helped prevent air entry during catheter exchange. After creation of the port pocket, the metal tunneler was passed in a retrograde fashion from the pocket to the neck dermatotomy, allowing the catheter attachment end to emerge at the neck site. An 8-Fr PowerPort ClearVUE catheter (BD) was then advanced over the guidewire through the sheath into the inferior vena cava (Fig. 2). The sheath was removed over the wire and catheter, after which the guidewire was removed and the catheter was clamped with hemostats. The catheter was then attached to the tunneler and pulled into the port pocket. The procedure was then completed in the standard fashion, with the catheter withdrawn to the appropriate length, trimmed at the pocket, and connected to the port reservoir.

Intra-procedural fluoroscopic image demonstrating a guidewire and port catheter advanced through the pre-existing central venous stent, with the guidewire extending into the inferior vena cava.

Only the 0.035-inch guidewire and the port catheter traversed the open-cell stent interstices; no dilators or large sheaths crossed the stent. Venography confirmed brisk flow through the stent (Fig. 3). Post-procedural fluoroscopic imaging and noncontrast CT demonstrated appropriate catheter course and tip position (Figs. 4, 5). The procedure was uncomplicated, and the patient resumed systemic therapy without device-related complications. At 9 months of follow-up, imaging demonstrated stable stent configuration and intact catheter position without device-related complications.

Superior vena cava venography during port placement showing contrast flow through the open-cell stent.

Post-procedural fluoroscopic image demonstrating the final position of the right chest port catheter traversing the pre-existing central venous stent, with the catheter tip projecting over the expected cavoatrial junction.

Noncontrast chest computed tomography showing the port catheter within the superior vena cava stent, confirming successful passage through the stent interstices.

DISCUSSION

Malignant SVC syndrome most commonly arises from thoracic malignancies, particularly bronchogenic carcinoma and lymphoma, with small cell lung cancer accounting for approximately one-quarter of cases [1]. Clinical severity depends on the level and rapidity of obstruction and the extent of collateral venous development; obstructions at or across the azygos vein ostium may cause more severe symptoms because a major collateral pathway is compromised [1,2]. Because survival remains limited in many patients with malignant SVC syndrome, rapid symptom relief and reliable venous access are important components of management [1].

Endovascular stenting is currently accepted as the first-line treatment for malignant SVC syndrome, offering immediate symptom relief and compatibility with systemic therapy [1,2]. Stent configuration should be individualized according to the pattern of venous involvement. Unilateral stenting is technically simpler and effective in most cases, while bilateral or kissing stents may be considered in patients with extensive brachiocephalic vein involvement but are technically more demanding and potentially associated with higher risk and procedural complexity [3]. In the present patient, extension into the left brachiocephalic vein provided the most favorable anatomy for stent placement.

After stenting, central venous access is often required for chemotherapy administration. Previous reports have described preservation or repositioning of pre-existing central venous catheters during SVC stenting [3]. In contrast, initial chest port placement after stenting has rarely been described. In this setting, advancement of a peel-away sheath through the stented segment may risk stent deformation, displacement, or embolization. In a large series, major embolic events during venous access were reported during catheter insertion through peel-away sheaths [4]. Hemostatic vascular sheaths may reduce this risk by avoiding direct exposure of the intravascular tract during catheter insertion [4].

In the present case, the sheath remained proximal to the stent, and only the guidewire and catheter traversed the open-cell stent interstices. This wire-and-catheter only technique may minimize radial stress on the stent and reduce the risk of stent deformation. Post-procedural imaging confirmed preserved stent configuration and appropriate catheter position.

The choice of stent design was also relevant to the technical strategy in this case study. The Abre stent is an open-cell nitinol venous stent with relatively large interstices and a conformable structure, making it suitable for long and irregular venous lesions. Venovo (BD) shares a similar open-cell venous stent design, whereas the WALLSTENT Endoprosthesis stent (Boston Scientific) has a braided closed-cell architecture with greater scaffolding and compression resistance. These structural differences may influence how each stent tolerates mechanical stress from catheter-based devices. In the present case, because the implanted stent was an open-cell Abre stent, advancing a large peel-away sheath and dilator assembly across the stent interstices was considered undesirable because of the potential risk of stent deformation or compromise. Therefore, a wire-and-catheter-only technique was deliberately selected to minimize stress on the stent struts while still permitting effective central venous access.

A theoretical concern with this approach is the possibility of a “scissoring” or friction effect between the metallic stent struts and the catheter, which could predispose the catheter to wear or fracture over time. This potential risk was weighed against alternative access options, including the left internal jugular, upper extremity, and femoral access, each of which carried less favorable anatomic or clinical considerations in this patient. At 9 months of follow-up, interval imaging demonstrated stable stent configuration and intact catheter position without fracture, kinking, or dysfunction, supporting the short- to mid-term feasibility of this strategy.

In summary, chest port placement through the SVC stent interstices using a hemostatic vascular sheath may be a feasible and practical option when conventional techniques are considered high risk. This approach may reduce stent manipulation and the risk of embolic complications while providing central venous access for systemic chemotherapy in selected patients with malignant SVC syndrome.
